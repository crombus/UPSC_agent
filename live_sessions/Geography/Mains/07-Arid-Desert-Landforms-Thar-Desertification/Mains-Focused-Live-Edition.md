## Lesson 1 — Aridity, desert and dryland boundaries

Progress: L1 / 18  |  Stage: Foundation  |  Subtopic: Definitions and global controls

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong local PDF 58 and Majid local PDF 59/204 for historical desert distribution.
Current anchor: UNCCD Article 1 supplies the treaty definition and aridity-index domain.
Scope: climatic and geomorphic meaning; climate-regime detail remains outside this lesson.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
PRECIPITATION / POTENTIAL EVAPOTRANSPIRATION

 <0.05             0.05----------0.65                         >0.65
 hyper-arid        UNCCD arid + semi-arid + dry sub-humid    increasingly humid
                  (polar and sub-polar regions excluded)

DESERT is a broader climatic/geomorphic term.
UNCCD DRYLAND is an operational reporting domain.
The two sets overlap, but they are not identical.
```

✅ Aridity is a persistent moisture deficit. Comparing precipitation with potential
evapotranspiration is more informative than defining deserts by heat or sand. ⚠️ Potential
evapotranspiration represents atmospheric demand; actual evaporation can remain small when little
water is available.

Five recurring controls help explain global aridity: subtropical subsidence, continentality, rain
shadow, cold-current stability and polar or high-altitude cold dryness. They overlap. The latitude
band near the subtropical highs explains an important desert family, not every desert.

The UNCCD defines desertification only within arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas. Its Article 1
range uses an annual precipitation-to-potential-evapotranspiration ratio of 0.05–0.65 and excludes
polar and sub-polar regions. Hyper-arid terrain below 0.05 is outside that operational range, though
it remains desert in ordinary physical geography.

**UPSC use:** begin with moisture balance, then match each control to a named case and qualify that
large deserts usually have interacting causes.

Revision notes:

- Aridity is a water-balance condition, not a temperature definition.
- Sand is a depositional material, not a defining property of desert.
- Subtropical subsidence explains the Sahara–Arabian family.
- Continentality helps explain Central Asian interiors.
- Rain shadow is central to Patagonia and many enclosed basins.
- Cold currents stabilise coastal air near the Atacama and Namib.
- UNCCD drylands cover the 0.05–0.65 aridity-index range.
- Desert and UNCCD dryland are overlapping, non-identical categories.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “deserts are hot sandy regions around 30 degrees latitude” an inadequate definition?

**Model answer:** It confuses one cause and one surface with the whole category. A desert is defined by persistent moisture deficit, so cold and high-altitude deserts also qualify. Sand requires a supply, transport and depositional setting; many desert surfaces are rock or gravel. Subtropical subsidence produces major deserts near 20–30 degrees, but continentality, rain shadow, cold currents and cold dryness create others outside that belt. The UNCCD further uses an operational dryland range of 0.05–0.65 for precipitation divided by potential evapotranspiration and excludes polar regions, so its reporting domain is not identical to every physical desert.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the subtropical belt as a universal law or treat the treaty boundary as the sole physical definition.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Account for the global distribution of deserts in about 150 words.

**Model (about 150 words):** Desert distribution reflects several routes to persistent moisture deficit. Descending air in subtropical highs suppresses convection and supports the Sahara, Arabian and Australian deserts. Deep continental interiors such as Central Asia receive moisture-depleted air, while mountain barriers create leeward rain shadows, as in Patagonia. Cold offshore currents stabilise coastal air, producing fog but little rain along the Atacama and Namib. Cold, moisture-poor air also creates polar and high-altitude deserts. These controls frequently overlap: a latitude belt is therefore a signature of one mechanism, not the definition of desert. Surface material must also be separated from climate, because rock and gravel are common and sand seas require sediment supply, transport energy and accommodation. A strong answer consequently begins with water balance, matches mechanisms to locations, and ends by rejecting single-cause explanations.

## Lesson 2 — Desert surfaces and the pavement problem

Progress: L2 / 18  |  Stage: Foundation  |  Subtopic: Erg, reg/serir, hamada and armour

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong local PDF 58–59 and Majid local PDF 59 for historical surface terms.
Current anchor: modern interpretation treats pavements and exposed rock surfaces as polygenetic.
Scope: surface genesis; detailed soil-profile processes appear in Lesson 9.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Surface | Material and form | Main controls |
|---|---|---|
| Erg | extensive sand cover and dunes | sand supply, transport and accommodation |
| Reg / serir | gravelly or stony plain | clast concentration, wash, deflation and soil processes |
| Hamada | exposed bedrock or rocky plateau | structure, weathering, runoff and removal of debris |
| Badlands | dissected weak material | episodic runoff, sparse cover and erodibility |
| Mountain desert | high-relief arid terrain | tectonics, mass movement, runoff and weathering |

The familiar dune photograph represents only one desert surface. An erg is depositional; a reg is
commonly residual or concentration-dominated; a hamada exposes bedrock. These surfaces may occur
within one desert because sediment calibre, relief, lithology and hydrology vary over short distances.

⚠️ Desert pavement cannot be reduced to “wind removed all fines.” A close clast cover may emerge
through interacting deflation, sheetwash, lateral reworking and upward migration of stones during
shrink-swell or heave. Dust can accumulate beneath an old pavement even while the surface remains
armoured. Once continuous, the clasts raise roughness and shelter finer material, reducing
entrainment; vehicle tracks, construction or concentrated trampling can breach that protection.

Badlands and mountain deserts demonstrate why wind-versus-water rankings fail. Runoff can dissect
weak sediment rapidly, while wind may redistribute exposed fines between events. Inherited structure
and relief remain essential.

**UPSC use:** classify the surface first, then state whether it records deposition, concentration,
exposure or dissection.

Revision notes:

- Erg means a sand sea, not desert in general.
- Reg/serir is a stony or gravelly plain.
- Hamada is an exposed rocky or bedrock surface.
- Desert pavement is a clast armour, not a deflation-only product.
- Sheetwash and soil movement may help concentrate surface stones.
- Pavement suppresses further entrainment while intact.
- Badlands are strongly associated with runoff on erodible material.
- One desert can contain several surface families.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two nearby tracts under the same winds show dunes in one and a gravel pavement in the other. Explain.

**Model answer:** The contrast can arise without different winds. The dune tract has an available sand supply and room for deposition, so transported grains accumulate. The pavement tract has lost or redistributed finer material while coarse clasts became concentrated by deflation, sheetwash and soil processes. Once packed, those clasts increase roughness and shelter underlying fines, creating conditional stability. Thus the dune field is principally a depositional store, whereas the pavement is an armoured surface with a more complex history. Disturbance will reshape an active dune, but breaking pavement can expose formerly protected sediment and sharply increase local erosion.

**Misconception to avoid:** A stony surface does not prove that wind alone created it or that erosion has permanently ended.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** “Most deserts are not seas of sand.” Explain the geomorphic significance.

**Model (about 150 words):** Desert surfaces reflect sediment and relief rather than a single climatic appearance. Ergs form only where sand supply, transport and accommodation coincide. Reg or serir surfaces contain gravel and stone concentrated by combinations of deflation, wash and soil movement; their clast cover may armour finer sediment. Hamadas expose bedrock where structure, weathering, runoff and removal of debris dominate. Badlands record episodic dissection of weak material, while mountain deserts retain strong tectonic and mass-movement controls. The distinction matters because each surface responds differently to disturbance. Mobile sand can migrate or bury land; intact pavement can suppress entrainment but becomes vulnerable when broken; exposed rock may remain supply-limited. Therefore “desertification” cannot be inferred from the presence of dunes, and a restoration measure appropriate to mobile sand may be irrelevant or damaging on a pavemented or rocky surface.

## Lesson 3 — Sediment availability and entrainment thresholds

Progress: L3 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: When wind begins to move grains

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Majid local PDF 59–60 and Khullar local PDF 120 for transport terminology.
Current anchor: threshold physics distinguishes initiation from maintenance of transport.
Scope: weathering supplies sediment; Topic 04 retains full weathering doctrine.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
EROSION REQUIRES BOTH:

AERODYNAMIC FORCING                ERODIBLE SURFACE
wind stress                  +     loose grains of suitable size
                                  limited moisture/cohesion
                                  sparse shelter/roughness
                                  sufficient fetch

fluid threshold  -> starts movement
impact threshold -> sustains movement after saltation begins
```

Wind strength and surface condition jointly control entrainment. A strong wind over welded rock may
move nothing; loose dry sand can move under a weaker event. Yet sediment availability is not always
the limiting factor: shelter, crusts, moisture, vegetation and aerodynamic roughness can raise the
threshold even where grains are abundant.

The fluid threshold is the stress needed for airflow to initiate movement from rest. Once saltating
grains strike the bed, impacts eject additional grains, allowing transport to continue at a lower
impact threshold. Fine silt and clay are light but cohesive, so they are often released from
aggregates by impact rather than lifted individually from an undisturbed surface. Coarse particles
require greater force because of their mass.

Tillage, trampling and vehicle movement can reduce aggregate strength or break crusts and pavements.
Conversely, vegetation, litter, stones and biological crusts dissipate momentum and trap particles.
Moisture commonly increases cohesion, but the response varies with salt crusting, clay mineralogy and
surface texture.

**UPSC use:** explain degradation as a threshold shift: land use changes the surface even when the
regional wind regime is unchanged.

Revision notes:

- Wind forcing and surface erodibility are co-controls.
- Fluid threshold initiates movement from rest.
- Impact threshold sustains an existing saltation cloud.
- Fine particles may resist direct lifting because of cohesion.
- Saltating impacts can release suspendible dust.
- Moisture, crusts and vegetation generally raise thresholds.
- Tillage and trampling may lower thresholds by disturbing structure.
- Sediment supply can be contemporary, inherited or water-delivered.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why may a ploughed field erode while adjacent grassland under the same wind does not?

**Model answer:** Ploughing changes the surface rather than the atmosphere. It removes canopy and litter shelter, disrupts root binding, breaks crusts and aggregates, and exposes loose grains to airflow. Grassland retains roughness and biological protection, so near-surface wind stress remains below the entrainment threshold. Once grains begin saltating on the field, impacts eject more particles and allow transport to continue at a lower threshold than was needed to start it. The contrast therefore reflects altered erodibility and feedbacks, not stronger wind over one field.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that fine dust is always easiest to lift; undisturbed fines may be highly cohesive.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Explain how human activity can intensify aeolian erosion without changing climate.

**Model (about 150 words):** Human activity can lower the surface threshold for entrainment. Vegetation removal reduces aerodynamic roughness, canopy interception and root binding. Repeated tillage or trampling breaks aggregates, crusts and desert pavement, exposing finer material that had been sheltered. Vehicle tracks and construction concentrate flow and create fresh sediment sources. Once the fluid threshold is crossed, saltating grains strike the bed and eject additional particles; transport can then continue at the lower impact threshold. This creates a rapid feedback in which disturbed surfaces produce more saltation and dust even under the pre-existing wind regime. The effect is not inevitable: controlled grazing, residue retention, roughness elements and recovery of native cover can raise thresholds again. The key analytical point is that climatic aridity supplies susceptibility, while land use determines whether a particular surface becomes erodible.

## Lesson 4 — Creep, saltation and suspension

Progress: L4 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Transport modes and sediment sorting

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Majid local PDF 60 and Khullar local PDF 120 for the three traditional modes.
Current anchor: mode boundaries overlap and vary by event and surface.
Scope: aeolian transport; fluvial transport remains outside this lesson.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
height
  ^          . . . . . . . . . .     SUSPENSION
  |       . . . . . . . . .
  |        _/¯\___/¯\___/¯\_          SALTATION
  |   o -> o -> o -> o                 CREEP / REPTATION
  +---------------------------------> distance

Impact links the modes:
saltating grain -> bed collision -> coarse grain nudged + fine aggregate disrupted
```

Creep or reptation describes rolling, sliding and very short hops of relatively coarse grains near
the bed. Saltation consists of repeated hops and commonly dominates sand transport. Suspension keeps
fine particles aloft while turbulent motion exceeds their settling tendency. These are useful modes,
not rigid grain-size boxes: density, aggregation, moisture, turbulence and surface roughness shift the
boundaries.

Saltation is mechanically important because impacts transfer momentum. They can move coarser grains,
disaggregate crusted material and release dust into suspension. A transport cloud grows after
initiation but is limited by feedback as moving grains extract momentum from near-surface airflow.

No universal statement can assign most sediment mass to one mode at every site. Sand-rich events may
be saltation-dominated; dust storms can carry immense suspended loads; coarse surfaces may show
substantial creep. Likewise, suspension may export material far away, but dust can also redeposit
within the region. Long-distance transport therefore creates redistribution at several scales rather
than guaranteed permanent loss from an entire dryland system.

**UPSC use:** link each mode to a different effect—surface movement, abrasion/ejection, or dust
export—and avoid absolute percentages.

Revision notes:

- Creep/reptation keeps coarse grains close to the bed.
- Saltation moves sand through repeated hops.
- Suspension carries fine particles in turbulent air.
- Mode boundaries overlap and vary by particle and event.
- Saltation impacts transfer momentum to other fractions.
- Dust often comes from disrupted aggregates.
- Moving grains slow near-surface airflow at high flux.
- No mode universally carries most load everywhere.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is saltation called the engine of aeolian transport without implying that it always carries most mass?

**Model answer:** Saltation is the engine because its impacts connect the transport modes. Hopping grains acquire momentum from airflow, strike the bed, nudge coarser particles into creep and break aggregates that release finer dust into suspension. This impact feedback also allows transport to continue below the wind stress needed to initiate it. “Engine” therefore describes mechanical influence, not a universal mass share. During a sand-rich event saltation may dominate flux, while a dust storm can carry a very large suspended load and a gravelly surface may emphasise creep.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not turn the three modes into fixed, non-overlapping size classes.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish the geomorphic roles of creep, saltation and suspension.

**Model (about 150 words):** Aeolian transport modes differ mainly in trajectory and landscape effect. Creep or reptation moves relatively coarse grains by rolling, sliding or very short hops along the bed, contributing to local redistribution and lag development. Saltation carries sand in repeated hops; impacts abrade exposed surfaces, transfer momentum to coarser grains and disrupt aggregates that release dust. Suspension carries fine particles while turbulence offsets settling, permitting regional or intercontinental transport and deposition as dust or loess. The boundaries are not fixed because moisture, aggregation, particle density and turbulence alter behaviour. Nor does one mode universally carry most mass. Their interaction matters most for land degradation: disturbance can initiate saltation, impacts generate suspendible fines, and the fine nutrient-bearing fraction may then leave the field. Controls must therefore combine surface protection, roughness and vegetation rather than target only airborne dust.

## Lesson 5 — Deflation, abrasion and qualified erosional forms

Progress: L5 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Hollows, ventifacts, yardangs and zeugen

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Majid local PDF 60–62 and Leong local PDF 59/61 for historical landform terms.
Current anchor: modern interpretation separates descriptive forms from single-agent origin stories.
Scope: regional residual relief is not reclassified as wind sculpture.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Form | Defensible interpretation | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Deflation hollow | lowered surface where loose material is removed | armour, moisture, bedrock or cement may limit depth |
| Ventifact | stone shaped or polished by wind-driven grains | facets need not encode a simple sequence of wind changes |
| Yardang | streamlined erosional ridge | abrasion, deflation, weathering, lithology and joints interact |
| Zeugen | legacy cap-and-pedestal descriptor | not every mushroom rock is aeolian or a zeugen |
| Inselberg / mesa / butte | resistant residual or structural relief | regional denudation, slope retreat and runoff are central |

Deflation removes available sediment; abrasion wears exposed material with moving grains. The two can
coincide but should not be merged. Deflation hollows do not universally descend to the water table:
bedrock, a coarse lag, cemented horizons, vegetation or moisture may halt further lowering.

Abrasion is often strongest near the ground because much sand flux occurs there, but the exact
vertical profile changes with grain size, roughness and wind. Ventifacts record aeolian wear; a
dreikanter has three main facets, yet facet geometry need not prove two reorientations or a simple
wind chronology.

Yardangs are aligned broadly with effective erosion and transport. Their scale and shape also reflect
rock resistance, bedding, joints, salt weathering and runoff. “Zeugen” is retained as a textbook
descriptor for resistant cap-and-pedestal forms, not as a universal genetic class. Natural arches and
mushroom forms may be polygenetic.

**UPSC use:** name the form, identify the evidence for wind, and state the competing structural or
weathering control.

Revision notes:

- Deflation removes; abrasion wears with transported tools.
- Deflation hollows have several possible limiting controls.
- Abrasion commonly concentrates near the surface.
- Ventifacts show wind wear, not a simple wind calendar.
- A dreikanter has three principal facets.
- Yardangs are streamlined but polygenetic.
- Zeugen is a qualified legacy descriptor.
- Mesas, buttes and inselbergs are not automatically aeolian.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it unsafe to classify every undercut desert rock as a zeugen?

**Model answer:** Similar profiles can arise through different combinations of rock resistance, bedding, weathering, runoff and wind-driven abrasion. The term zeugen is a legacy descriptor for cap-and-pedestal forms, not a reliable universal genetic category. Near-ground sand flux can contribute to undercutting, but a resistant cap over weaker material may also retreat through salt weathering, moisture changes and slope processes. A defensible classification therefore describes the geometry, identifies evidence of abrasion, and retains alternative controls rather than inferring wind-only origin from shape.

**Misconception to avoid:** A familiar textbook silhouette is not proof of one erosional agent.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Explain how aeolian erosional landforms can be used as evidence rather than as a catalogue.

**Model (about 150 words):** Aeolian forms become analytical when their geometry is linked to process and limitation. A deflation hollow indicates removal of loose material, but its floor may be controlled by armour, moisture, cement or bedrock rather than only the water table. Ventifacts demonstrate grain impact at the surface, while yardang alignment records a persistent effective erosion direction; their shape still depends on lithology, joints and weathering. Near-ground concentration of sand flux helps explain pedestal-like undercutting, but the legacy term zeugen should not be applied to every mushroom rock. Inselbergs, mesas and buttes mainly record structural resistance and regional denudation. Thus the examiner should see a hierarchy of evidence: wind wear is strongest where facets, polish, alignment and sediment tools agree, whereas form alone cannot establish agency. This approach replaces a vocabulary list with causal diagnosis.

## Lesson 6 — Complete dune taxonomy and dynamics

Progress: L6 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Form as evidence of wind, sand and vegetation

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong local PDF 62–63, Majid local PDF 62–63 and Khullar local PDF 55.
Current anchor: dune fields contain transitional, compound and inherited forms.
Scope: morphology is interpreted probabilistically, not as a one-to-one climatic code.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Dune type | Typical geometry | Common controls |
|---|---|---|
| Barchan | crescent; horns downwind | limited sand, mainly one transport direction |
| Transverse | ridges across net transport | abundant sand, low directional variability |
| Linear / seif | long ridge | bimodal or seasonally varying winds; oblique flank transport |
| Parabolic | U-shape; arms commonly upwind | vegetation/moisture anchoring and blowout growth |
| Star | several radiating arms | abundant sand and multidirectional winds |
| Reversing | opposed slip faces | seasonal reversal of effective transport |
| Nebkha / obstacle | mound around roughness element | shrubs or obstacles trap sand |
| Compound / composite | superposed or combined forms | changing regimes and multiple spatial scales |

```text
                 WIND =====>

             gentle stoss       crest      steep slip face
______________________/^^^^^^^^^\________________
 grains climb/hop up                 avalanche down

The FORM migrates as grains cycle through it.
Migration rate varies with sand flux, dune size, moisture and vegetation.
```

Barchan horns point downwind; parabolic arms commonly point upwind because vegetation or moisture
anchors them. This contrast is useful, but neither form proves a single land-use history. Linear
dunes are not simply ridges parallel to one constant wind; transport commonly approaches their two
flanks obliquely under bimodal or seasonal regimes. Star dunes mainly accumulate vertically but may
shift, elongate or reorganise.

Real dune fields preserve older climates and may be out of equilibrium with present winds.
Compound and composite dunes warn against treating a single planform as a calibrated measure of sand
supply or directionality. Vegetation can stabilise dunes locally; disturbance can remobilise them,
but fixed dunes are not necessarily restored ecosystems.

Khullar's Thar classification identifies obstacle, parabolic, longitudinal, transverse, barchan and
shrub-coppice forms. It establishes morphological diversity, not present mobility or migration rate.

Revision notes:

- Barchan horns point downwind.
- Transverse ridges need relatively abundant sand.
- Linear dunes commonly reflect bimodal or seasonal winds.
- Parabolic arms are generally vegetation-anchored and point upwind.
- Star dunes reflect multidirectional transport.
- Reversing dunes carry opposed slip faces.
- Nebkhas form around vegetation or obstacles.
- Compound forms record changing or scale-dependent regimes.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can dune shape guide interpretation but not uniquely determine environmental history?

**Model answer:** Dune shape responds to several controls at once: sand supply, directional variability of transport, vegetation, moisture, topography and inheritance from earlier conditions. A barchan often indicates limited sand under a mainly unidirectional regime, while a parabolic form commonly reflects anchored arms, but either may be modified after formation. Linear dunes can arise from seasonally varying or bimodal winds, and compound dunes preserve more than one scale of activity. Therefore morphology narrows plausible controls but does not provide a unique climatic or land-use history without chronology, sediment evidence and present wind observations.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that every parabolic dune proves degradation or every linear dune records one prevailing wind.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Classify major dune forms and explain why morphology must be interpreted cautiously.

**Model (about 150 words):** Barchans are crescentic dunes with downwind horns, commonly associated with limited sand and a dominant transport direction. With greater sand cover, transverse ridges may form across net transport. Linear or seif dunes are long ridges often maintained by bimodal or seasonally varying winds. Parabolic dunes have arms commonly anchored upwind by vegetation or moisture; star dunes develop multiple arms under multidirectional winds and abundant sand; reversing dunes carry opposed slip faces. Nebkhas accumulate around shrubs or obstacles, while compound and composite dunes combine forms at different scales. These associations are diagnostic rather than deterministic. Dunes may be inherited, partly stabilised, remobilised or out of equilibrium with present winds. Consequently, morphology should be combined with sediment supply, vegetation, chronology and observed transport before inferring environmental change.

## Lesson 7 — Loess, dust sinks and the Indian record

Progress: L7 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Dust accumulation and variable soil quality

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong local PDF 62–63 and Majid local PDF 62 for historical loess descriptions.
Current anchor: Zeeden et al. (2020) documents extensive Kashmir loess-palaeosol sequences.
Scope: loess as sediment and palaeoenvironmental archive; agriculture is treated only as an effect.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
SOURCE                     TRANSPORT                 SINK
glacial grinding  ----\
river floodplain  ------> airborne silt/dust -----> topographic or vegetated trap
desert surface    ----/                              |
                                                     v
                                             LOESS + palaeosols
                                     deposition alternates with stability/soil formation
```

Loess is predominantly silt-sized terrestrial sediment accumulated mainly from wind-blown dust.
Sources may be glacial, fluvial or desert-derived. Deposits are often porous and massive-looking,
but bedding, reworking and soil horizons can occur; lack of visible stratification is not an
infallible field test.

Fertility is equally non-universal. Fresh mineral material and carbonate may support productive
soils where moisture and management are favourable, yet loess can be nutrient-imbalanced, drought
prone, collapsible and extremely vulnerable to gullying. Agricultural value depends on mineralogy,
weathering, organic matter, moisture regime and erosion control.

✅ The Kashmir Valley contains almost 500 square kilometres of reported loess cover, commonly on
plateaus and terraces, with thickness from several metres to more than 20 metres. Intercalated
palaeosols record periods of landscape stability and soil formation. This directly rejects the old
claim that India lacks significant loess sequences. Upper-Ganga *bhur*, by contrast, is described in
Khullar as wind-blown sand and should not be relabelled loess.

Long-range suspension can remove valuable fines from a field, but dust may settle within the same
region or fertilise a distant sink. The scale of “loss” must therefore be stated.

Revision notes:

- Loess is mainly wind-accumulated silt.
- Dust sources may be glacial, fluvial or desert.
- Massive appearance is common but not universal.
- Reworking and palaeosols may interrupt loess sequences.
- Fertility varies with material, water and management.
- Loess can erode rapidly into gullies.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why are both “loess is always fertile” and “India has no loess” incorrect?

**Model answer:** Loess soil quality depends on mineralogy, carbonate, weathering, organic matter, moisture and erosion. Some loess regions are highly productive, while others are drought prone, collapsible or severely gullied, so fertility is not intrinsic. India also has substantial loess-palaeosol sequences in the Kashmir Valley, covering almost 500 square kilometres in published review evidence and locally exceeding 20 metres in thickness. These deposits are valuable palaeoclimate archives. They must be distinguished from upper-Ganga bhur, which Khullar describes as wind-blown sand rather than silt-sized loess.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat loess as a soil order or equate every wind-blown deposit with loess.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Explain how loess links source-area degradation with palaeoclimate reconstruction.

**Model (about 150 words):** Loess links an eroding source with a depositional archive. Wind entrains silt-sized material from deserts, floodplains or glacial outwash and carries it to topographic or vegetated traps. Removal of fine particles can reduce soil quality at the source, especially where clay, organic matter and nutrients are preferentially exported. At the sink, repeated dust accumulation produces loess; intervals of landscape stability permit soil formation, creating loess-palaeosol sequences. These sequences preserve changes in sediment supply, atmospheric circulation and moisture. The Kashmir Valley illustrates the Indian significance, with extensive loess cover and deposits locally thicker than 20 metres. However, neither transport nor fertility should be universalised: dust can redeposit regionally, loess may be reworked, and agricultural quality depends on mineralogy, climate and management. Loess is therefore both a geomorphic transfer and a palaeoenvironmental record.

## Lesson 8 — Episodic water in arid landscapes

Book context: Leong local PDF 64 and Majid local PDF 62–63 for the classic basin sequence.
Current anchor: modern doctrine rejects a universal ranking of wind against water.
Scope: arid-specific runoff and deposition; general river-process doctrine remains elsewhere.

```text
MOUNTAIN FRONT
      | intense runoff in a confined channel
      v
    WADI
      | channel exits relief; competence falls
      v
ALLUVIAL FAN -- neighbouring fans merge --> BAJADA
```

Rare rainfall can perform large geomorphic work when intensity exceeds infiltration. Yet runoff is
not automatically high in every desert: coarse sand may infiltrate rapidly, whereas steep rock,
clay-rich crusts and compacted surfaces shed water. Antecedent moisture, vegetation and storm
location matter.

A wadi is normally dry or intermittent but can carry sudden floodwater and sediment. Where a
confined channel leaves a mountain front, flow spreads and loses competence, building an alluvial
fan. Coalescing fans form a bajada. A pediment is a gently inclined bedrock surface at a mountain
foot, often covered by a thin veneer; its development may combine scarp retreat, weathering,
sheetflow and channel action. It should not be equated with the depositional fan above it.

A playa occupies a closed or poorly drained basin floor and may alternate among shallow water, mud,
salt crust and dry sediment. Solutes tend to concentrate because evaporation removes water, but the
salt budget is not always one-way: groundwater outflow, exceptional overflow, wind deflation,
dissolution and extraction can remove material.

**UPSC use:** derive the sequence from confinement, competence and drainage rather than memorising
names.

Revision notes:

- Arid floods are episodic, not absent.
- Runoff depends on intensity relative to infiltration.
- Wadis are normally dry or intermittent channels.
- Fans form where confinement and competence fall.
- Bajadas are coalesced fan aprons.
- Pediments are bedrock surfaces, not alluvial fans.
- Playas occupy closed or poorly drained basin floors.
- Salt concentration is strong but not universally irreversible.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a desert have both high infiltration and dangerous flash floods?

**Model answer:** Different surfaces respond differently. Loose coarse sand can absorb rainfall rapidly, but rock, steep slopes, clay-rich crusts, compacted tracks and already wet patches may shed water when rainfall intensity exceeds infiltration. Storms are also spatially concentrated, so runoff generated upstream can enter a dry channel where no rain fell locally. Converging flow then produces a rapid flood with high sediment competence. Thus high infiltration in one substrate does not remove catchment-scale flood risk.

**Misconception to avoid:** Sparse vegetation alone does not determine runoff; substrate and storm intensity are equally important.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Discuss the role of episodic water in arid landform development.

**Model (about 150 words):** Episodic water can produce concentrated geomorphic work in arid terrain, but its importance varies with storm intensity, infiltration, relief and substrate. Intense rainfall on rock, crusted soil or steep catchments generates rapid runoff into wadis. Floods scour confined channels and transport coarse debris. At a mountain front, loss of confinement and gradient reduces competence, creating alluvial fans; adjoining fans merge into bajadas. Bedrock pediments may develop beneath or upslope of these deposits through weathering, sheetflow, channel action and scarp retreat. In internally drained basins, temporary water spreads across playas, where evaporation commonly concentrates solutes. Coarse sandy surfaces may nevertheless absorb rainfall quickly, so not every desert storm produces high runoff. A strong answer therefore avoids ranking wind and water universally and instead identifies the surface, event magnitude, drainage condition and timescale controlling each landform.

## Lesson 9 — Arid soils, crusts and salinity

Progress: L9 / 18  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Variable profiles and fragile protection

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Majid local PDF 153/366 and Khullar local PDF 120–123 for historical soil notes.
Current anchor: USDA Soil Taxonomy has twelve orders; Aridisols are one order, not all desert soils.
Scope: soil-process links to land degradation; detailed pedology remains outside this lesson.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
surface: vegetation / crust / pavement
          ↓ storm infiltration        ↑ evaporation or capillary rise

rainfall amount and intensity + texture + drainage + groundwater + parent material + time
```

Arid soils do not experience one permanent upward water flow. Storm infiltration moves water
downward; evaporation and capillary rise may later move moisture and solutes upward. The net profile
depends on texture, drainage, groundwater depth, parent material and climate. Calcic, gypsic or salic
horizons are common in suitable settings, but their order and depth are not universal.

Aridisols are one of the current twelve USDA soil orders. Not every soil in a climatic desert is an
Aridisol, and older terms such as sierozem belong to other classificatory traditions. Low biomass
often limits organic inputs, but decomposition can also be moisture-limited; “organic-poor because
heat rapidly oxidises everything” is too simple.

Crusts have contrasting roles. Biological crusts and intact physical crusts can protect against wind,
whereas sodic dispersion and sealing can reduce infiltration and intensify runoff. Desert pavement
adds roughness and armour but can be breached. Salinisation may result from natural closed-basin
concentration, shallow saline groundwater, irrigation without adequate drainage or coastal influence.
Irrigation relocates and concentrates salts through a water balance; it does not always “import” them,
nor is drainage and leaching universally safe without attention to water quality and disposal.

Revision notes:

- Arid soil water moves both downward and upward.
- Profile development varies with texture, drainage and groundwater.
- Carbonate, gypsum and salts need not occur in one fixed sequence.
- USDA Soil Taxonomy currently has twelve orders.
- Aridisols do not include every desert soil.
- Biological crusts may protect the surface.
- Sodicity can disperse clay and seal pores.
- Salinity requires a site-specific salt and water budget.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “arid soils are chemically fertile but structurally weak” too broad?

**Model answer:** Arid soils vary in parent material, weathering, organic matter, salinity, sodicity, texture and drainage. Some retain abundant bases or weatherable minerals, but plant availability may be limited by water, salt, pH or nutrient imbalance. Structure also varies: biological crusts, stable aggregates or pavement may protect a surface, while sodic dispersion and low organic inputs can create fragility elsewhere. The useful conclusion is conditional—many arid soils contain mineral reserves yet require water- and salt-aware management—not a universal fertility label.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer soil capability from climate alone.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Explain how surface protection and salinity create contrasting dryland degradation pathways.

**Model (about 150 words):** Dryland surfaces may be protected by vegetation, biological crusts or a clast pavement that increases roughness and shelters finer particles. When grazing concentration, tillage, vehicles or construction breach that cover, entrainment thresholds fall and wind erosion accelerates. Salinity follows a different water-and-solute pathway. In closed basins, evaporation concentrates dissolved material; in irrigated commands, seepage or excess application can raise saline groundwater, after which capillary rise and evaporation move salts toward the root zone. Sodicity may disperse clay, seal the surface and increase runoff. The remedies therefore differ. Protecting roughness and native cover addresses aeolian susceptibility, whereas salinity management requires diagnosis of groundwater, drainage, water quality, crop tolerance and safe disposal of salts. Uniform plantation or irrigation cannot treat both pathways and may worsen one while improving the other.

Subtropical latitude creates high atmospheric demand; the region lies near the variable north-western margin of the summer monsoon; moisture transport and convergence fluctuate; continental position and regional subsidence reduce reliable rainfall; and the low, south-west–north-east Aravalli offers limited orographic enhancement for some flows. ARAVALLI runoff -> seasonal streams -> inland saline basins

Khullar describes the Luni as a seasonal stream flowing south-west toward the Rann. Flow and channel
continuity vary; it should not be converted into a perennial river reaching the Arabian Sea.
North of the Luni basin, inland drainage supports saline lakes including Sambhar, Didwana, Degana,
Kuchaman, Sargol and Khatu. The cited Sambhar dimensions are source-era seasonal observations, not a
current wetland assessment.

Closed and poorly drained basins tend to accumulate solutes because evaporation removes water, but a
real salt budget also includes groundwater exchange, exceptional overflow, wind removal, dissolution
and human extraction. Lake area can fluctuate sharply while salt storage follows a different path.
This is why seasonal shrinkage alone does not prove long-term desiccation.

The Rann of Kachchh belongs to the wider arid region and receives terrestrial drainage, yet it is a
seasonally inundated saline mudflat and marsh with marine influence. Calling it either unrelated to
the Thar or simply an extension of its dunes loses the crucial geomorphic distinction.

**UPSC use:** draw separate boxes for dune field, inland playa basin and tidal Rann.

- The Luni is seasonal and trends south-west.
- Inland drainage supports several saline basins.
- Sambhar area varies seasonally.
- Evaporation concentrates solutes but does not define the whole budget.
- Groundwater, overflow, deflation and extraction can remove salt.
- Seasonal contraction is not automatically long-term lake death.
- The Rann is a saline mudflat/marsh.
- The Rann is regionally linked but not a Thar dune field.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why must a closed-basin salt budget include removal pathways as well as evaporation?

**Model answer:** Evaporation removes water without dissolved salts, so concentration is a strong tendency. However, actual basins may lose solutes through groundwater outflow, exceptional surface overflow, wind deflation of exposed salts, dissolution followed by export, and commercial extraction. Inputs and storage also vary through time. The correct formulation is therefore that internal drainage favours net accumulation unless removal pathways offset it, not that salt mass must rise continuously under every condition.

**Misconception to avoid:** A simplified accounting identity is not a measured basin budget.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish the Thar dune system, inland saline basins and the Rann of Kachchh.

**Model (about 150 words):** The Thar's dune tracts are aeolian sand systems shaped by sediment supply, vegetation and changing transport directions. Inland saline basins east of the arid core receive episodic runoff but lack reliable external drainage; evaporation commonly concentrates solutes, producing playas or saline lakes such as Sambhar and Didwana. Their area may fluctuate seasonally without indicating a permanent trend. The Rann of Kachchh is different again: it is a low-lying, seasonally inundated saline mudflat and marsh influenced by both terrestrial drainage and the sea. It is regionally connected to the Thar and receives the Luni, but it is not another dune field. The distinction matters because each system has different hazards and management needs—sand mobility and cover in dunes, inflow and salt balance in inland basins, and inundation, salinity and tidal processes in the Rann.

## Lesson 15 — Desertification, drought and land degradation

Progress: L15 / 18  |  Stage: Applied  |  Subtopic: Definitions and interacting pathways

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Khullar local PDF 123/135 provides historical Indian framing.
Current anchor: UNCCD Article 1 and the current Environment-and-Ecology evidence base.
Scope: physical diagnosis; treaty institutions and reporting architecture remain supporting context.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
GENERAL LAND DEGRADATION
reduction or loss of land capability in any climatic setting

DESERTIFICATION under UNCCD
land degradation specifically in arid + semi-arid + dry sub-humid areas

DROUGHT
temporary precipitation/water deficit relative to normal

DESERT
climatic/geomorphic region; may include productive and degraded land
```

UNCCD Article 1 defines desertification as land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid
areas resulting from various factors, including climatic variations and human activities. The word
“including” does not require both causes in every case. General scientific and policy usage may use
land degradation across all climates; the Convention's own defined term is dryland-specific.

A desert can contain productive rangeland, oasis agriculture, irrigated fields, biological crusts
and vegetated dunes. Those surfaces can lose productivity. Therefore a blanket denial of
desertification within desert regions is too absolute. Hyper-arid bare terrain may have little biological productivity and may fall outside
the Convention's aridity-index range, but that is a boundary qualification, not a universal rule.

Four useful pathway families are aeolian erosion, water erosion, vegetation/biomass decline and
chemical degradation such as salinity or sodicity. They interact: cover loss lowers erosion
thresholds; sodic sealing can increase runoff; irrigation may increase biomass while worsening
salinity. Drought is temporary and can trigger degradation without being identical to it.

Revision notes:

- Desert is a region; desertification is a process.
- UNCCD desertification is dryland-specific.
- General land degradation can occur in any climate.
- Drought is a temporary departure from normal.
- Productive surfaces within deserts can degrade.
- Climatic and human factors may act separately or together.
- Aeolian, hydric, vegetative and chemical pathways interact.
- Sand movement is only one possible degradation mechanism.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can land far from an existing sand desert undergo desertification?

**Model answer:** Yes, if it lies within the UNCCD dryland domain and loses biological or economic productivity. Semi-arid or dry sub-humid farmland can degrade through water erosion, vegetation loss, salinity, sodicity or wind erosion without any nearby dune field. Conversely, the presence of sand does not itself prove desertification. The diagnosis depends on climatic class, baseline condition and productivity change. General land degradation also occurs outside drylands, but the Convention reserves “desertification” for arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas.

**Misconception to avoid:** Desertification is not a frontier of sand moving across a map.

### Mains practice

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish desertification from drought and general land degradation.

**Model (about 150 words):** Desertification is land degradation occurring within arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas. General land degradation is the broader reduction or loss of biological or economic productivity in any climatic setting. Drought is different again: it is a temporary precipitation or water deficit relative to local normal and may end without permanent capability loss. A drought can expose soil and trigger degradation, but the two terms are not synonymous. Nor does desertification mean that an existing sand desert physically advances. Productive rangeland, oasis, irrigated land or vegetated dunes within an arid region can lose cover, soil quality or yield through wind erosion, runoff, salinity or biomass decline. Diagnosis therefore requires climate class, baseline, process, period and evidence of productivity change. This terminology prevents both climate determinism and exaggerated claims about moving desert boundaries.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check A — process chain

**Question:** Build the chain from surface disturbance to off-site dust deposition and state where uncertainty enters.

**Model answer:** Disturbance removes cover, breaks crust or pavement and exposes erodible particles. If wind stress crosses the fluid threshold, grains begin saltating; impacts then sustain transport at a lower threshold, move coarser grains by creep and release fines into suspension. Fine particles may leave the field and deposit locally, regionally or at continental distance as dust or loess. Productivity can fall through selective loss of clay, organic matter and nutrients. Uncertainty enters at every scale: thresholds vary with moisture and aggregation, mode shares vary by event, and suspended material may redeposit within the dryland rather than leave it permanently.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe the pathway as uniform removal of a soil layer.

### Cumulative check B — landform diagnosis

**Question:** How should an examiner distinguish an erg, pavement, yardang and playa?

**Model answer:** An erg is a depositional sand sea requiring supply, transport and accommodation. A desert pavement is a clast armour produced through combinations of deflation, wash and soil movement; it suppresses entrainment while intact. A yardang is a streamlined erosional ridge aligned broadly with effective wind erosion, but controlled also by rock resistance, joints and weathering. A playa is a closed or poorly drained basin floor that alternates among water, mud, evaporite and dry sediment. The first is depositional, the second protective/concentration-dominated, the third erosional, and the fourth hydrological–evaporative.

**Misconception to avoid:** Similar arid appearance does not imply one agent or one genesis.

### Cumulative check C — Thar evidence

**Question:** Reconcile climatic aridity, dated dune science and modern land-use change in one argument.

**Model answer:** The Thar's regional aridity arises from interacting circulation, moisture, relief and high evaporative demand. Dated luminescence evidence shows repeated dune accumulation and reworking, with Holocene dynamics prominent in many preserved sections; provenance evidence identifies multiple local, fluvial and transboundary sediment sources. Modern irrigation, cultivation, grazing, infrastructure and energy development modify vegetation, mobility, salinity and dune preservation. These layers answer different questions: climate explains persistent dryness, chronology explains landscape assembly, and land use explains much contemporary condition. None justifies a single origin date or a natural-versus-human percentage for the whole Thar.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use present degradation to infer creation of the desert climate.

### Cumulative check D — evidence discipline

**Question:** Separate the four numbers 97.85, 83.69, 21.76 and 6.45 million hectares.

**Model answer:** The SAC Atlas maps 97.85 million hectares under all desertification/land-degradation classes for 2018–19. Within drylands, 83.69 million hectares is classified as desertification. # ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These prompts and models are original practice, not official UPSC solutions.

## 10-mark practice 1 — transport and productivity

**Question:** “Wind erosion removes a fraction of soil, not a uniform layer.” Explain in 150 words.

**Model answer (134 words):** Wind sorts sediment according to threshold, trajectory and settling tendency. Coarser grains remain near the surface or move by creep; sand commonly travels by saltation; sufficiently fine particles enter suspension after direct entrainment or impact release. The exported material is therefore selective. Clay, fine silt, organic matter and associated nutrients can be removed disproportionately, reducing aggregate stability and water-holding capacity even when total depth loss appears small. Saltating grains also abrade seedlings and crusts, while deposition may bury fields and roads. Yet the outcome is not universally permanent at regional scale because dust can redeposit nearby or farther downwind. Land degradation intensifies when vegetation removal, tillage, grazing concentration or pavement disturbance lowers the entrainment threshold. Effective control protects native cover and surface roughness and targets active source areas rather than treating airborne dust alone.

## 10-mark practice 2 — interpreting SAC evidence

**Question:** What qualifications must accompany India's 29.77% land-degradation figure? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (120 words):** The figure refers to the SAC-ISRO mapping of 2018–19 condition: 97.85 million hectares, or 29.77% of India's total geographical area, under all desertification and land-degradation classes. It is not a wind-erosion percentage, a Thar area, a 2026 measurement or an annual rate. Desertification restricted to drylands is 83.69 million hectares. Process shares differ: water erosion is 11.01%, vegetation degradation 9.15% and wind erosion 5.46% nationally. State patterns also diverge; Rajasthan records 62.06% of state area under DLD and 43.37% under wind erosion. Comparisons with 2003–05 show the national total rising while national wind erosion and Rajasthan's total decline. Therefore every use needs class, denominator, period, scale and method, and should not be converted into a single story of advancing desert.

## 15-mark practice 1 — Thar evolution

**Question:** Critically examine natural and human dimensions in the evolution of the Thar. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (207 words):** The Thar must be separated into climatic origin, landscape assembly and present land condition. Its persistent aridity is natural in the sense that high evaporative demand, variable monsoon moisture and convergence, continental position, regional circulation, low relief and the Aravalli's orientation interact over long timescales. Human activity does not create this monsoon-margin setting.

The landscape itself is also older and more complex than a single sand source. Luminescence work records recurrent dune accumulation, stabilisation and reactivation, with Holocene activity prominent in many sampled western-Thar sections. Provenance research in the transboundary desert identifies local material, palaeochannels, Himalayan tributaries, the Indus system and recycled deltaic stores. This evidence rejects both one birth date and one sediment source.

Human influence is nevertheless substantial for contemporary condition. Grazing concentration, cultivation, tillage, roads, mining and energy infrastructure alter cover and thresholds; irrigation can stabilise dunes and increase biomass but also raise waterlogging and salinity risks. Agricultural expansion may flatten inherited dunes and erase chronological archives.

The defensible conclusion is therefore neither “natural and untreatable” nor “largely man-made.” Climate explains regional aridity; geological and fluvial-aeolian processes explain inherited terrain; human decisions increasingly shape mobility, productivity and preservation. Quantitative apportionment requires comparable dated and historical evidence that is not available for the whole Thar.

## 15-mark practice 2 — process-matched restoration

**Question:** Evaluate why dryland restoration must be ecosystem- and process-specific. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (197 words):** Dryland degradation is multi-pathway, so a uniform measure can be ineffective or harmful. Active dune margins may need native roughness, shelter and carefully targeted stabilisation. Intact pavemented surfaces need protection from vehicle and grazing concentration, because disturbance exposes sheltered fines. Vegetation-degraded grassland requires grazing agreements, pasture recovery and native open-ecosystem restoration rather than automatic conversion to dense tree cover. Runoff erosion requires catchment diagnosis, while saline canal commands need groundwater, drainage, crop and salt-disposal planning.

This specificity also improves evidence. The SAC 2018–19 Atlas maps condition, and its 2024 vulnerability assessment supports prioritisation; neither by itself proves restoration success. Official 2026 reporting states that 21.76 million hectares were brought under restoration during 2011–2020 against a 26-million-hectare 2030 ambition. It shows programme scale but must be tested through vegetation composition, soil stability, hydrology, productivity and livelihoods.

COP17 and India's Open Natural Ecosystems guide strengthen the warning against tree-cover reductionism. Grassland, scrub, savanna and desert ecosystems possess their own biodiversity and pastoral functions. Restoration should first avoid new degradation, then reduce ongoing pressures and reverse damage where feasible. Success is consequently a measured recovery of function in the appropriate ecosystem, not merely area planted, money spent or land identified.

## 20-mark practice 1 — desert geomorphic systems

**Question:** Discuss how wind, episodic water and inherited geology jointly produce arid landforms. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (209 words):** Arid landscapes are polygenetic because low effective moisture exposes sediment without eliminating water, structure or weathering. Wind removes loose material by deflation, abrades exposed surfaces with transported grains and deposits sand and dust. Creep, saltation and suspension sort the load, creating dunes, lags and distant dust sinks. Yet yardangs, pedestal forms and ventifacts depend on lithology, joints, weathering and sediment tools as well as airflow; mesas and inselbergs mainly record structural resistance and regional denudation.

Episodic water performs concentrated work when rainfall intensity exceeds infiltration. Wadis scour confined channels; loss of confinement at mountain fronts builds alluvial fans and coalesced bajadas. Bedrock pediments reflect combinations of weathering, sheetflow, channel action and scarp retreat. In closed or poorly drained basins, playas alternate among shallow water, mud and evaporite surfaces.

Inherited geology controls relief, rock strength, sediment calibre and accommodation. Pavements may form through deflation, sheetwash and upward stone migration, while hamadas expose resistant bedrock. Dunes may recycle older fluvial or deltaic stores and remain out of equilibrium with present winds.

Therefore no universal ranking of wind and water is valid. The appropriate explanation identifies scale, substrate, event regime and timescale: wind may dominate sand redistribution, floods may dominate coarse sediment transfer, and geology determines what each agent can work upon.

## 20-mark practice 2 — evidence and governance

**Question:** Assess India's contemporary desertification evidence and restoration governance. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (205 words):** India's strongest condition evidence is the direct SAC-ISRO 2018–19 assessment: 97.85 million hectares, or 29.77% of national area, under desertification/land degradation, with 83.69 million hectares classified as desertification within drylands. Water erosion at 11.01% and vegetation degradation at 9.15% exceed national wind erosion at 5.46%. Rajasthan differs sharply: 62.06% of state area is mapped as DLD and wind erosion covers 43.37%. Such denominators prevent a simplistic national “advancing desert” narrative.

Monitoring must distinguish condition from vulnerability and intervention. The 2024 SAC vulnerability atlas develops prioritisation methods for selected districts, not a replacement national total. Official 2026 reporting states that 21.76 million hectares were brought under restoration during 2011–2020 against a 26-million-hectare ambition, while about 6.45 million hectares were identified under the Aravalli Green Wall. Neither figure alone proves ecological recovery.

Governance also requires legal precision. The Supreme Court's November 2025 Aravalli definition was kept in abeyance on 29 December, and expert review continued in 2026; it should not be mapped as settled law. Ecologically, COP17 and India's Open Natural Ecosystems guide support native grassland, scrub and pastoral restoration rather than blanket plantation.

The way forward is process-specific action, repeated condition monitoring, ecosystem-reference indicators, community tenure and transparent separation of targets, treated area and verified outcomes.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Aridity and distribution

- Desert is defined by persistent moisture deficit, not heat or sand.
- Major controls are subtropical subsidence, continentality, rain shadow, cold currents and cold dryness.
- Large deserts commonly combine controls.
- UNCCD drylands use an aridity-index range of 0.05–0.65 and exclude polar/sub-polar regions.
- Hyper-arid terrain below 0.05 lies outside that operational range.

## B. Surfaces and sediment

- Erg = depositional sand sea.
- Reg/serir = stony or gravelly surface.
- Hamada = exposed rocky or bedrock surface.
- Pavements can form through deflation, wash and upward clast migration.
- Pavement armour reduces entrainment while intact.
- Sediment can be local, inherited, fluvial or imported by wind.

## C. Aeolian transport and erosion

- Fluid threshold initiates movement; impact threshold sustains it.
- Creep/reptation, saltation and suspension are overlapping modes.
- Saltation impacts move coarse grains and release dust.
- No mode universally carries most mass in every event.
- Deflation removes; abrasion wears with moving grains.
- Yardangs are streamlined but polygenetic.
- Zeugen is a qualified legacy descriptor.
- Ventifact facets do not provide a simple wind chronology.

## D. Dunes and loess

- Barchan horns point downwind; parabolic arms commonly point upwind.
- Transverse dunes need broad sand cover and low directional variability.
- Linear dunes commonly involve bimodal or seasonal transport.
- Star dunes reflect multidirectional winds and abundant sand.
- Reversing, nebkha, compound and composite dunes complete the taxonomy.
- Loess is mainly wind-accumulated silt with variable fertility.
- Kashmir preserves extensive loess-palaeosol sequences.
- *Bhur* is wind-blown sand, not loess.

## E. Water, soils and salt

- Runoff depends on intensity, infiltration, slope and surface condition.
- Wadi → fan → bajada is a confinement-and-competence sequence.
- Pediment is a bedrock surface, not an alluvial fan.
- Playas occupy closed or poorly drained basin floors.
- Real salt budgets include both inputs and removal pathways.
- Arid soils experience episodic downward and upward water movement.
- Aridisols are one of twelve USDA soil orders.
- Salinity and sodicity require site-specific diagnosis.

## F. Thar geography and evolution

- The Thar is transboundary and boundary estimates vary.
- Aridity is multi-control and monsoon-margin in character.
- Marusthali, Bagar, thali, rohi and canal commands differ.
- The Rann is regionally linked but geomorphically a saline mudflat/marsh.
- Luminescence records repeated dune accumulation and reworking.
- Provenance includes local, palaeochannel, Himalayan/Indus and recycled stores.
- Climate, inherited landscape and current condition answer different questions.

## G. Exact current evidence

- India DLD, 2018–19: 97.85 mha / 29.77%.
- Desertification within drylands: 83.69 mha.
- National wind erosion: 17.94 mha / 5.46%.
- National water erosion: 11.01%; vegetation degradation: 9.15%.
- Rajasthan DLD: 21.237665 mha / 62.06% of state area.
- Rajasthan wind erosion: 43.37% of state area.
- Restoration reporting: 21.76 mha during 2011–2020.
- Aravalli programme: about 6.45 mha identified by September 2026.

## H. Governance and answer logic

- COP17 met in Ulaanbaatar, 17–28 August 2026.
- Open natural ecosystems require more than tree-count metrics.
- The November 2025 Aravalli definition was stayed on 29 December.
- Expert review continued in 2026.
- Match restoration to process and ecosystem.
- Keep condition, vulnerability, target, programme area and outcome separate.
- For 2026 Q6: mechanism + productivity + human amplification + controls + bound.
