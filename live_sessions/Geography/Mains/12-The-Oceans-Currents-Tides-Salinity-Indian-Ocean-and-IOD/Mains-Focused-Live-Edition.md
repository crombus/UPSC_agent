# The Oceans: Currents, Tides, Salinity / Indian Ocean and IOD - Mains-Focused Live Edition

The ocean looks like one blue surface. Underneath it, three things are always moving: the wind-driven skin on top, the density-driven deep circuit below, and the Moon's slow tug on the whole water column. This topic builds those three systems in order, then shows how the Indian Ocean breaks the usual rules and how El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole couple the ocean to the monsoon.

### Start with a picture of the store

```text
WHERE THE WATER IS (a store)           WHAT MOVES IT (flows)
Ocean          about 97% of all water  evaporation from sea  -> clouds
Ice and snow   most fresh water        rain on land          -> rivers
Groundwater    large fresh store       rivers                -> sea
Lakes, rivers  small fresh store       melting ice           -> sea
```

Most of Earth's water sits in one store, the ocean. Most of the fresh water sits in another, the ice. The lesson is about the first store: its size, its shape, and the boundary problem that makes its size a matter of definition.

### The intuition: an area needs an edge

Ask how big a country is and the answer depends on the coastline you draw. The ocean has the same problem. Its northern edges are continents, which are clear. Its southern edge is open water that merges into the Antarctic. Somewhere a line has to be drawn, and different conventions draw it in different places.

### The Southern Ocean: four candidate lines

- The Antarctic coast: the land edge, the most literal boundary.
- The Antarctic Convergence: a belt near 50 to 60 degrees south where cold polar water sinks beneath warmer subantarctic water. It is a real oceanographic boundary, not a line on a chart.
- A proposed limit near 60 degrees south, put forward for hydrographic charting.
- A limit near 55 degrees south, which one national charting authority used in the mid-twentieth century; it was not an international limit.

Each choice changes the reported area of the Southern Ocean and of the world ocean. None changes the water itself. The Convergence is the physically meaningful choice; the chart limit is the administratively meaningful one. Whether the proposal near 60 degrees south has been formally adopted is not verified here, so the lesson does not treat it as settled.

### The inventory

Five named ocean basins are recognised in common use: the Pacific, the Atlantic, the Indian, the Southern and the Arctic. In commonly used definitions the Pacific is the largest, the Atlantic second, and the Indian the smallest of the three great basins. The Arctic is smaller still. The commonly cited area of the Indian Ocean is about 70.6 million square kilometres. That figure includes the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf and leaves out the Southern Ocean, and other boundary choices give other values.

The Indian Ocean has one feature that the others do not share. Its northern side is closed by the Asian landmass. The Atlantic and Pacific extend north into the subpolar seas, where subpolar gyres and boundary currents close their circulation. In the Indian Ocean, Asia closes the north, so no such northern circuit exists. Lesson 13 turns this geometry into the monsoon reversal.

### Stocks, flows and the time it takes

A store is only useful with its flows. Water enters the ocean by rain, river discharge and melting ice, and leaves it by evaporation and by freezing at high latitudes. The residence time of water in a basin is the store divided by the flow through it. A large store with a small flow holds its water for a long time. This is why the deep ocean can hold heat and carbon for centuries while the surface changes in months.

### Limits and an objection

**Objection:** if the ocean is one body of water, dividing it into five is arbitrary.

**Reply:** the division is a convention for measurement and navigation, and it is useful because the basins have different geometry. The Indian Ocean's closed north side, the Atlantic's north-south orientation and the Pacific's width produce different circulation. The convention is arbitrary at the edges and meaningful in the interior.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: basin sizes and the Indian Ocean's position in the ranking.
- Mains: an answer on ocean extent should state the convention before quoting an area.
- Trap: treating a quoted ocean area as a measured fact rather than a boundary choice.

**Revision points**

1. Oceans hold about 97 per cent of Earth's water; most fresh water is in ice.
2. Ocean area depends on where the southern limit is drawn.
3. The Antarctic Convergence is a physical boundary; chart limits are conventions.
4. The five basins are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic.
5. The Indian Ocean is the smallest of the three great basins, at about 70.6 million square kilometres on the commonly cited definition.
6. The Indian Ocean's northern side is closed by Asia.
7. Residence time is the store divided by the flow.
8. A large store with a small flow changes slowly.
9. Ocean area is a convention at the edges and a measurement in the interior.
10. Always state the boundary before quoting an area.

Pause and retrieve: if the Southern Ocean limit moves north by five degrees, does the volume of sea water change?
*Answer pointer:* No. Only the area assigned to the Southern Ocean changes; the water is the same. The boundary choice changes the reported area, not the store.

The next lesson moves from the surface of the store to its floor, because the shape of the basin determines what can settle there and where the continental edge lies.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two reputable atlases give different areas for the Southern Ocean. What is the most likely reason, and why does the disagreement not change how much water the ocean holds?

**Model answer:** The atlases use different southern boundaries, so they cut the same water into different areas. The water itself is the same; only the assigned area changes. A physical boundary such as the Antarctic Convergence and a chart boundary can both be defensible, but they answer different questions.

**Misconception to avoid:** a different area is not evidence of a different ocean. The disagreement is about the edge of the map, not about the sea.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Why is the extent of an ocean partly a matter of convention? Use the Southern Ocean as your example. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Ocean extent is a convention where the ocean meets land or another ocean. The Southern Ocean has no continental edge, so the boundary must be drawn across open water. Options include the Antarctic coast, the Antarctic Convergence, and chart limits near 55 and 60 degrees south. The Convergence marks a real change in water masses, while chart limits serve navigation and charting. Each choice alters the reported area but not the water, so the answer should quote the boundary before any figure. The Indian Ocean's closed northern side shows why the basin's internal circulation depends on its geometry, which is the more important physical point.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Convention at the boundary, 2. Four candidate limits named, 3. Physical versus chart reasoning, 2. Area changes but water does not, 2. Link to the Indian Ocean's geometry, 1.

### Read the floor as a profile

```text
LAND                                         SEA
 mountains  ->  coast  -> shelf -> slope -> rise -> abyssal plain -> trench
   high        flat      shallow   steep    gentle     flat, deep     deepest
                          ~200 m                      3,000-6,000 m
                                        ridges and seamounts rise from the plain
```

The ocean floor has relief as large as the land's, but it is hidden. Once the profile is in view, every feature has a job: the shelf is where light reaches the bottom, the slope is where the continent ends, the plain is where fine sediment accumulates, and the trench is where one plate descends under another.

### The intuition: depth controls what happens

Light penetrates only the upper part of the water. Where the floor is shallow enough for light to reach it, plants and animals can use the bottom, and mixing can carry nutrients up. Where the floor is deep, life depends on what falls from above. So the shelf is the productive fringe and the abyss is the quiet interior. Lesson 8 returns to this.

### Features, one at a time

- **Continental shelf:** the shallow, gently sloping margin of the continent, usually ending near 200 metres. It is the zone where light reaches the floor.
- **Continental slope:** the steeper drop from the shelf edge toward the deep floor.
- **Continental rise:** the gentle wedge of sediment at the foot of the slope, where the slope meets the plain.
- **Abyssal plain:** the flat deep floor, commonly between about 3,000 and 6,000 metres, built by fine sediment settling over long periods.
- **Mid-ocean ridge:** a long chain of volcanic mountains where new floor forms.
- **Seamount:** an isolated submarine volcano that does not reach the surface.
- **Guyot:** a seamount with a flat top. The flat top is a clue: wave erosion planed it while it was near the surface, and later subsidence carried it down. It is not a basin filled with sediment.
- **Trench:** the deepest feature, formed where one plate sinks beneath another. The Mariana Trench reaches close to 11,000 metres on commonly used figures. The Japan Trench's maximum depth is commonly given as about 8,046 metres, far shallower than the deepest trenches, so depth alone does not make a trench.

### What settles, and why

Sediment on the floor falls into two broad groups. Terrigenous sediment comes from land: silt and clay carried by rivers and wind. Pelagic sediment forms in the open ocean from the remains of organisms and from dust. Near the continents the terrigenous share dominates. Far from them, pelagic deposits take over.

The depth of the floor changes which pelagic remains survive. Calcareous shells from plankton, such as foraminifera, dissolve in cold deep water below a certain depth, the carbonate compensation depth. Their remains form oozes above it and red clay below it. Siliceous shells survive in some regions. This is why the floor under a ship tells you where you are: a sample of carbonate ooze means shallow water, while red clay means deep water far from land.

### Seabed resources, physically

The deep floor holds three kinds of mineral deposit. Polymetallic nodules lie on the abyssal plain and grow very slowly, so they need slow sedimentation. Polymetallic sulphides form around hot vents on ridges. Cobalt-rich crusts form on the flanks of seamounts. Phosphorite is found on continental margins and shelves at shallower depths. These are physical facts. The legal regime for licensing exploration is a separate matter, and it is taught in the Indian Ocean lesson, Lesson 12.

### Comparison and objection

Land and sea relief are both measured with depth or height, but they behave differently. A mountain range erodes continuously under rain and ice. A deep floor erodes very little, so its features survive for very long periods. Guyots are the exception that proves the rule: they show that the sea floor can change shape, but only under specific conditions.

**Objection:** the deep floor is too far away to matter for India.

**Reply:** the floor controls the shelf, which controls the fishing grounds, the ports and the offshore resources that India depends on. The deep floor matters through the shelf it bounds.

### Trap and UPSC application

- Trap: a guyot is not a trench, a ridge or a sediment-filled basin.
- Trap: the shelf is not defined by a sharp edge; it is defined by a gradient break near 200 metres.
- Prelims: match each feature to its formation.
- Mains: explain why the shelf is the productive margin, and why nodules and sulphides are found in different places.

**Revision points**

1. The continental shelf usually ends near 200 metres.
2. Light reaches the floor on the shelf; the deep floor depends on what falls from above.
3. The slope is steeper; the rise is a gentle wedge at its foot.
4. The abyssal plain lies commonly between 3,000 and 6,000 metres.
5. A seamount is a submarine volcano; a guyot is a seamount with a planed flat top.
6. Trenches mark plates descending beneath others.
7. Terrigenous sediment dominates near land; pelagic sediment dominates far from it.
8. Carbonate remains dissolve below the carbonate compensation depth.
9. Red clay marks the deep, distant floor.
10. Nodules, sulphides and crusts occur in different settings.
11. Phosphorite lies on the shallower margins.

Predict before reading on: a sediment core is almost all calcareous ooze. Is it more likely from a shelf or from a deep floor below the compensation depth?
*Answer pointer:* A shelf or a shallower ridge crest, because calcareous remains are dissolved in the cold deep water below the compensation depth.

The next lesson takes the same logic to the water column itself: the depth of the floor sets how far the surface's heat reaches, and that leads into temperature.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a continental shelf more productive than the abyssal plain, even though both lie under the same ocean?

**Model answer:** The shelf is shallow enough for light to reach the floor and for mixing to bring nutrients up. The abyssal plain is deep and dark, and it depends on material that falls from the surface. Depth decides whether the bottom can use light and whether nutrients return to it, so the shelf is productive and the abyss is quiet.

**Misconception to avoid:** shelves are not productive because they are near land. Their productivity comes from light reaching a shallow floor and from mixing.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how the form of the ocean floor controls where sediment settles and where living resources concentrate. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer, 15 marks:** The floor forms a profile from shelf to deep plain. Near the continent, terrigenous silt and clay settle on the shelf and slope. On the shelf, light reaches the floor, so benthic life and fishing concentrate there. Deeper, the slope and rise carry sediment down, and the abyssal plain receives fine pelagic and dust material slowly. Calcareous remains dissolve below the compensation depth, so the deep floor is often red clay. Resources follow the same profile: nodules accumulate slowly on the plain, sulphides cluster on ridges where hot vents form, and phosphorite concentrates on shallower margins. The argument is that depth governs both sediment and life, so the same gradient explains the productive shelf and the mineral-bearing deep floor.

**Rubric (15 marks):** Profile described, 2. Terrigenous and pelagic distinction, 3. Carbonate dissolution and depth, 3. Shelf productivity mechanism, 3. Mineral placement by setting, 3. Integrating argument, 1.

## Lesson 3 - Why does the upper ocean warm and cool so slowly?

### Start with a thermal column

```text
SURFACE   mixed layer      warm, stirred by wind and waves, tens of metres to about 100 m
          thermocline      temperature falls fast with depth, roughly 100 m to 1,000 m
DEEP      deep water       cold and nearly uniform, below about 1,000 m
```

The mixed layer is the part of the ocean that the atmosphere touches daily. The thermocline is a steep temperature step beneath it. The deep water is cold and changes very little. Most of the ocean's volume lies in the deep layer, which is why the ocean as a whole warms slowly.

### The intuition: a deep bath

Imagine heating a shallow tray of soil and a deep tank of water with the same sunlight. The soil warms quickly because only its top few centimetres absorb the heat. The water warms slowly because its heat is spread through a large depth and carried down by mixing. The ocean is the deep tank. The ocean's annual temperature range is therefore small compared with land's.

### The doctrine: why water holds heat

Water has a high specific heat, so it needs a large input of energy to warm by a given amount. Its surface mixes under wind and waves, so the heat is distributed through a mixed layer tens of metres deep. Water is also partly transparent, so sunlight is absorbed over a depth rather than only at the surface. Together these explain the small annual range. Heat capacity is the key idea: to warm a column of water 50 metres deep by one degree takes many times the energy needed to warm a thin layer of dry soil by the same amount. The exact multiple depends on the soil and the depth, so the lesson uses the comparison, not a precise factor.

### The surface pattern

Sea surface temperature is highest in the tropics and falls toward the poles. The warmest waters sit in the western tropical oceans, forming warm pools. Along the equator the surface is warm but the thermocline is shallow in the east, which matters for Lesson 14. At mid-latitudes the seasonal swing is larger, because the mixed layer is shallower and the sun's angle changes more. Coastal and inland seas show larger annual ranges than the open ocean, because they are shallower and more exposed to land air.

### Limits of the picture

The mixed layer does not have a fixed depth. It deepens under strong winds and in winter, and it shallows in summer and in calm, sunny weather. A single temperature for "the ocean" is therefore meaningless without a depth and a season. The thermocline is also a barrier to mixing rather than a depth; it is the feature that limits how much heat reaches the deep layer.

### Comparison: the same latitude, two behaviours

The Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal lie at similar latitudes, yet they do not behave alike. The Bay receives large river discharge and heavy rain, which makes its upper layer fresher and more stratified. A stratified surface warms and cools differently from a well-mixed one. Lesson 5 explains why the freshwater layer changes the temperature response, and Lesson 12 returns to the Indian Ocean's seasonal behaviour.

**Objection:** if the ocean stores so much heat, why does the surface warm at all?

**Reply:** the surface warms because heat enters faster than mixing can remove it in the short term. Over decades the stored heat grows, which is why the upper ocean's warming is a long-term signal. The slow response is a reason to watch the deep store, not a reason to deny the surface change.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: vertical zones, the thermocline's role and the reason for the small annual range.
- Mains: explain why an ocean's temperature response is slow, and use the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal as a contrast.
- Trap: the ocean's temperature is not one number; state the depth and the season.

**Revision points**

1. The mixed layer is warm, wind-stirred and tens of metres to about 100 metres deep.
2. The thermocline is a steep temperature step beneath it.
3. The deep layer is cold and holds most of the ocean's volume.
4. Water's high specific heat makes the ocean's annual range small.
5. Heat is spread through depth by mixing and by partial transparency.
6. Sea surface temperature falls from the tropics toward the poles.
7. Warm pools sit in the western tropical oceans.
8. The equatorial thermocline is shallow in the east.
9. Shallow and enclosed seas show larger annual ranges.
10. The mixed layer deepens in winter and under strong wind.
11. The thermocline limits heat transfer to depth.
12. Always state depth and season with any temperature.

Retrieve without notes: two seas at the same latitude, one with a large river discharge and one without. Which shows the larger seasonal swing in surface temperature, and why?
*Answer pointer:* The river-fed sea, usually, because its fresher upper layer is more stratified and responds more to the seasonal air. The effect depends on the wind and the depth, so state those conditions.

The next lesson asks what makes the surface salty, and why the freshwater layer matters for density.

### Concept check

**Question:** An ocean's surface warms in summer but its deep water changes little within a year. Explain the difference using heat capacity and the thermocline.

**Model answer:** The surface absorbs sunlight and is mixed through a limited depth, so it warms and cools within the season. Heat must pass through the thermocline to reach the deep layer, and mixing carries it slowly. The deep water holds most of the volume, so a year's heat input changes it only slightly.

**Misconception to avoid:** the thermocline does not stop heat from entering the ocean. It limits how fast heat moves down into the deep layer.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Why does the ocean's temperature change more slowly than land's, and what does the thermocline do to that response? (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Water has a high specific heat and absorbs sunlight over a depth, so a large input is needed for a given warming. Wind and waves mix the surface into a mixed layer tens of metres deep, which spreads the heat. The thermocline separates this layer from the cold deep water, so heat moves downward only slowly through mixing. Together these processes produce a small annual range compared with land. Shallow and enclosed seas show larger swings because they have less volume and more exposure to the air. The answer should state the depth and season for any temperature claim.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Heat capacity, 2. Mixed layer and absorption depth, 2. Thermocline's role, 2. Comparison with land, 2. Depth and season qualification, 2.

## Lesson 4 - What makes sea water salty, and what changes it?

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Start with a bucket and a kettle

```text
  EVAPORATION (E)   water leaves, salt stays      -> salinity RISES
  PRECIPITATION (P) fresh water arrives           -> salinity FALLS
  RIVER RUNOFF (R)  fresh water arrives           -> salinity FALLS
  ICE FORMATION     fresh water locked in ice     -> salinity RISES locally
  ICE MELT          fresh water released          -> salinity FALLS locally
```

Boil a pan of sea water and the salt stays in the pan while the water leaves. The ocean behaves the same way on a large scale. Salinity is set by the balance between water leaving by evaporation and water arriving by rain and rivers.

### The intuition: salt is conserved

Salt does not evaporate and does not disappear into the air. Once it is in the ocean it stays there until it is removed by sea-spray, by precipitation of minerals on the seabed, or by burial in sediment, and those removal rates are very slow. So, as a budget approximation, salinity changes mainly because water is added or taken away, since the slow salt removals are small over a budget's time scale. If evaporation removes a tenth of the water and no salt leaves, the salinity rises by a ninth: 35 per mille becomes 35 divided by 0.9, which is about 38.9 per mille.

### Definition and units

Salinity is the mass of dissolved salts per kilogram of sea water. Expressed as grams per kilogram it is the per mille, written ‰ or ppt. The open ocean averages about 35 ‰, though the value is commonly quoted as 34.7 to 35. Scientists also use a dimensionless practical salinity scale. In this topic salinity is always written in per mille, never as a per cent, because writing 35 per mille as 3.5 per cent understates it tenfold, a slip that is easy to make and hard to spot.

### Composition

Most of the dissolved salt is sodium chloride, with chloride and sodium together making up most of the mass. Magnesium, sulphate, calcium and potassium make up the rest. Because the proportions of the major ions are nearly constant across the ocean, measuring one property, such as electrical conductivity, is enough to estimate the total salinity. This is why salinity can be measured quickly at sea.

### The budget, with India in view

The Arabian Sea is saltier than the Bay of Bengal at similar latitudes. The reason is the budget. The Arabian Sea is hot and dry, has high evaporation, and receives little river water, so evaporation exceeds rain and runoff. The Bay receives the Ganga-Brahmaputra discharge and heavy monsoon rain, so runoff and precipitation outweigh evaporation in much of the basin. Only the direction of the difference is needed; the exact values vary by place and season.

### The Red Sea

The Red Sea is the clearest test of the budget. It is hot and arid, with high evaporation, very little precipitation and almost no river inflow. It exchanges water with the Indian Ocean through Bab-el-Mandeb. Its surface salinity is high, commonly reported at about 40 ‰ in parts of the northern basin, though the figure varies with place and season.

The exchange through Bab-el-Mandeb is shaped by the strait's depth as well as its width. Surface water flows in and dense, salty water flows out at depth, so the Red Sea keeps its salt. A useful comparison is the Strait of Gibraltar, about 14 kilometres wide at its narrowest, and Bab-el-Mandeb, commonly about 26 kilometres at its narrowest, though other figures near 16 kilometres appear. Bab-el-Mandeb is therefore the wider of the two. The Red Sea's salinity comes from its budget, not from a narrow connection.

### The opposite case: the Baltic

The Baltic Sea sits at the other end of the salinity range. Large river inflow and a narrow connection to the North Sea keep its surface water far fresher than the open ocean's 35 per mille. The contrast is the same budget logic, with inputs exceeding losses.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** temperature must be the main control, since hot seas are salty.

**Reply:** temperature affects evaporation, so it enters the budget, but it is not the control itself. The Bay of Bengal is warm and fresh; the Red Sea is warm and salty; the cold polar seas are cold and fresh. The budget explains all three.

### Limits

Salinity cannot be read from temperature alone, and it cannot be read from a single location. A value means little without the season, the depth and the surrounding basin. Ice formation adds a complication: when sea ice forms, it leaves salt behind in the water below, which raises local salinity. Lesson 5 shows how that salt changes density.

### Answer use and traps

Name the budget terms and a regional case. Avoid writing salinity as a per cent, and avoid attributing it to temperature alone.

**Revision points**

1. Salinity is the mass of dissolved salt per kilogram of sea water, in per mille.
2. The open ocean averages about 35 ‰.
3. Salt is conserved; salinity changes because water is added or removed.
4. Evaporation raises salinity; rain, runoff and ice melt lower it; ice formation raises it locally.
5. The major ions are nearly constant in proportion, so conductivity measures salinity.
6. The Arabian Sea is saltier than the Bay of Bengal because of the budget.
7. The Ganga-Brahmaputra discharge and monsoon rain dilute the Bay.
8. The Red Sea is salty because evaporation far exceeds its inputs.
9. Bab-el-Mandeb exchanges water at depth as well as at the surface.
10. Gibraltar is about 14 kilometres wide at its narrowest; Bab-el-Mandeb is wider.
11. Temperature matters through evaporation, not as a direct control.

Stop and reason: a seawater sample has 35 grams of salt per kilogram. Evaporation removes ten per cent of the water and no salt leaves. What is the new salinity, and in which direction did it move?
*Answer pointer:* About 38.9 per mille, upward. The water mass fell to 0.9 kg with the same 35 g of salt.

The next lesson shows what the salinity does to density, and why the layers do not mix freely.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two seas lie at the same latitude. One is salty and one is fresh. Give the budget explanation without relying on temperature.

**Model answer:** Salinity is set by the balance of evaporation against precipitation and runoff. A sea where evaporation strongly exceeds rain and river input becomes salty because salt is conserved while water leaves. A sea with heavy rain or large rivers is diluted. The difference lies in the budget, and the same logic explains the Red Sea and the Bay of Bengal.

**Misconception to avoid:** a warm sea is not salty by heat alone. Heat increases evaporation, but the balance of water inputs and outputs decides the salinity.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why the Red Sea is saltier than the open Indian Ocean, and what the strait at Bab-el-Mandeb contributes. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Salinity follows the budget. The Red Sea loses large amounts of water by evaporation under hot, dry air, while precipitation and river inflow are negligible. Because salt is conserved, the remaining water becomes saltier. Exchange through Bab-el-Mandeb admits surface water and releases dense, salty water at depth, so the sea keeps its salt. The strait's depth and the exchange pattern matter as much as its width. The answer should name evaporation, the missing inputs, the conserved salt and the exchange, and should avoid attributing the salinity to temperature alone.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Salt conservation, 2. Evaporation and missing inputs, 3. Exchange at depth, 3. Temperature correctly placed, 1. Units in per mille, 1.

### Start with two bottles

```text
            COLD                      WARM
   SALTY    densest - sinks            denser than fresh, lighter than cold salt
   FRESH    lighter                    lightest - floats

   Stable layering: lightest on top, densest at the bottom
```

Take two bottles of sea water. One is cold and salty, the other warm and fresh. The cold salty bottle is heavier and sinks. The warm fresh bottle floats. Density is the quantity that decides whether water sinks, floats or mixes, and it depends on both temperature and salinity.

### The intuition: density is a joint quantity

Density rises when temperature falls and when salinity rises. Temperature changes density by a large amount in cold water and only a little in warm water. Salinity changes density steadily. Because the two act together, a water body can be dense because it is cold, because it is salty, or both. Density does not reverse seasonally in the large-scale pattern, although the surface temperature and salinity do change with the seasons.

### The doctrine: stratification

A stratified ocean has layers of different density. The layer boundaries have names. A thermocline is a temperature-driven step, a halocline is a salinity-driven step, and a pycnocline is any density step, usually caused by both. A stratified column is stable when density increases with depth. A column is stable exactly when density increases with depth. Warm water over cold water is therefore stable unless salt makes the upper layer denser; only then does the column overturn. In practice this means a strong pycnocline resists vertical mixing and keeps the surface separate from the deep water.

### Water masses: an identity, not a flow

A current is motion: water moving from one place to another. A water mass is an identity. It is a body of water that has formed at the surface with a particular combination of temperature and salinity, sunk or spread to a level where its density matches its surroundings, and carried its signature along with it. Water masses are picked out on a temperature-salinity diagram, where each mass sits as a cluster of points. Two different currents can carry the same water mass, and one current can carry several.

### Worked case: the Mediterranean outflow

Evaporation over the Mediterranean exceeds the rain and runoff it receives. The sea is therefore saltier and denser than the Atlantic at the same depth. Through the Strait of Gibraltar, relatively fresh Atlantic surface water flows in, while the dense Mediterranean water flows out at depth. The outflow sinks along the continental slope and settles at intermediate depth in the Atlantic, carrying its warm, salty signature. The exchange works because density, not surface motion, decides where each water goes.

Limits: the Gibraltar exchange is a clean example, but the depth of the outflow depends on the surrounding Atlantic water, so the layer is a neutral-buoyancy level rather than a fixed depth.

### The Bay of Bengal's freshwater cap

Large river discharge and heavy rain spread a thin layer of fresh water over the Bay's surface. The fresh layer is lighter than the water beneath it, so the upper ocean is strongly stratified. Stratification has three consequences. It keeps the warm surface separate from the cold, nutrient-rich water below, so nutrients are not easily lifted. It reduces the mixing that would otherwise cool the surface, so the surface stays warm. And it can make the upper layer more responsive to storms, because the stratified water warms and then stirs differently. The exact effect on cyclones is a matter of ongoing research, so the lesson states the mechanism only.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** density is simply temperature, so salinity is a footnote.

**Reply:** in the cold high latitudes temperature dominates, but in the tropics and in enclosed seas salinity can decide the density. The Mediterranean outflow shows that a salty, warm water can sink below a fresher, cooler one. Density needs both.

**Residual:** the mixing rate inside a stratified layer depends on wind, waves and turbulence that are not fully resolved here.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: stratification terms, the sign of density change and the identity of a water mass.
- Mains: explain how salinity and temperature jointly govern sinking, and use the Mediterranean outflow as the test case.
- Trap: confusing a current with a water mass, or treating a surface salinity figure as the density of the whole column.

**Revision points**

1. Density rises when temperature falls or salinity rises.
2. In cold water, temperature changes density strongly; salinity changes it steadily.
3. A thermocline is a temperature step; a halocline is a salinity step; a pycnocline is a density step.
4. A stable column has density increasing with depth.
5. A denser layer over a lighter one is the unstable arrangement; warm, salty water over cold, fresh water is unstable only when its salt makes the upper layer the denser one.
6. A water mass is an identity defined by temperature and salinity.
7. A current is motion; a water mass is what the motion carries.
8. Water masses are plotted on a temperature-salinity diagram.
9. The Mediterranean outflow sinks to intermediate depth in the Atlantic.
10. The Bay's freshwater cap strongly stratifies its upper layer.
11. Stratification limits vertical mixing and nutrient supply.
12. Density does not reverse seasonally at large scale; surface values do.

Quick test: a cold, salty water mass and a warm, fresh one meet. Which sinks, and what does the answer depend on?
*Answer pointer:* The cold, salty mass sinks, because its density is higher. The answer depends on both temperature and salinity, not on either alone.

The next lesson leaves the water column and asks what makes the surface move, because density explains the deep circuit but not the surface currents.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a warm, salty water body sink beneath a cooler, fresher one, and what does this imply about using temperature alone?

**Model answer:** Density depends on both temperature and salinity. A warm but very salty water can be denser than a cooler, fresher water, so it sinks. Using temperature alone would predict the reverse and misplace the water mass.

**Misconception to avoid:** the warmest water is not always the lightest. Salinity can outweigh temperature in density.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain the difference between a current and a water mass, and show how density links the two. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer, 15 marks:** A current is water in motion, moved mainly by wind and pressure differences at the surface and by density differences at depth. A water mass is a body of water defined by a particular temperature and salinity, formed at the surface and carried away with its signature. Density links them because it decides whether a water mass sinks or floats and where it comes to rest. The Mediterranean outflow illustrates the link: evaporation makes Mediterranean water dense, so it sinks into the Atlantic at intermediate depth, carrying its warm, salty signature far from its origin. A current can carry several water masses, and one water mass can be carried by several currents. The answer should therefore keep the motion and the identity separate, and use density as the bridge between them.

### Start with a spinning table

```text
  WIND blows across the surface  -> drags water (wind stress)
         |
  EARTH'S ROTATION               -> deflects the moving water
    NH: deflection to the RIGHT of motion
    SH: deflection to the LEFT of motion
    Equator: no deflection
         |
  EKMAN SPIRAL                   -> surface moves to the right (NH) of the wind
         |
  NET TRANSPORT                  -> 90 degrees to the right (NH), left (SH) of the wind
```

Put a bowl of water on a turntable and drag a finger across the surface. The water does not go straight. The turntable's rotation bends the motion. The ocean does the same on a much larger scale. Lesson 6 is about that bending and what it does to the wind-driven surface.

### The intuition: the wind pushes, the Earth bends

Wind stress is the friction the air exerts on the sea surface. It sets the surface water moving, but the surface does not move downwind. The Coriolis effect, which is the apparent deflection caused by the Earth's rotation, bends the motion. Its strength depends on latitude. It is zero at the equator, and it is strongest at the poles.

### The Coriolis parameter

The Coriolis parameter is f = 2Ω sin φ, where Ω is the Earth's rotation rate and φ is latitude. It is positive in the Northern Hemisphere and negative in the Southern, which is the mathematical form of the right-hand and left-hand deflections. At the equator f is zero, so the deflection vanishes. This is why the equator is a special place for the ocean and why several rules in this lesson change sign there.

### The Ekman layer: why the surface does not follow the wind

In 1905 the oceanographer Vagn Walfrid Ekman worked out the idealised theory of a wind-driven layer. In his simplest model, each thin layer of water is turned a little more than the layer above it by the Coriolis effect and by friction. The result is a spiral: the surface moves at a substantial angle to the wind, and each deeper layer moves more slowly and turns further. The net movement of the whole layer, called the Ekman transport, is at 90 degrees to the wind: to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern.

The depth of the wind-driven layer is the Ekman depth. A simple scaling gives D = π√(2A/|f|), where A is the eddy viscosity, a measure of how strongly turbulence mixes momentum. With a typical mid-latitude value of A near 0.01 square metres per second and f at 30 degrees, D is about 50 metres. The assumptions are idealisations, so the lesson gives the scale, not a precise depth.

### Geostrophy: why currents follow the contours

Away from the surface layer and the coast, the balance is between the Coriolis force and the pressure gradient. Water flows along the contours of sea-surface height, not across them, with high water on the right in the Northern Hemisphere. This balance is called geostrophic. A gyre is a circulation in which the sea surface is raised in the centre and the water flows around it.

### The gyres

Each ocean basin has large subtropical gyres. In the Northern Hemisphere they turn clockwise, and in the Southern they turn anticlockwise. The sense follows from the Coriolis deflection: a wind-driven circulation is bent into a loop, and the bend is to the right in the north and to the left in the south.

Western intensification explains a feature the simple picture misses. The gyre's western side carries a narrow, fast, warm current, such as the Gulf Stream, while the eastern side is broad and slow. The reason is that the Coriolis parameter changes with latitude. Its change, called the beta effect, allows the western boundary to carry the return flow of the gyre in a narrow, intense jet. The Sverdrup relation expresses the interior balance: the north-south transport in the open ocean is set by the curl of the wind stress divided by the beta effect. The relation is an approximation; friction and eddies shape the real boundary currents.

### Worked example: the Gulf Stream

Its core carries warm water northward along the North American coast at surface speeds of about 2 metres per second in commonly cited measurements. Warm water on the western side of the basin is carried poleward, and this is why the North Atlantic's eastern coasts, such as those of north-western Europe, are milder than their western counterparts at the same latitude. The example has a limit: the warming depends on the air above and the distance from the coast, so the current is one factor among several.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** the wind blows, so the surface should go with it.

**Reply:** the Coriolis effect and friction turn the surface layer, so the water moves across the wind. The turning is the reason gyres exist at all. Without rotation, the surface would simply drift downwind.

**Residual:** real gyres are shaped by coastlines, eddies and friction that the idealised theory leaves out.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: the deflection rule in each hemisphere, the gyre sense and the position of the warm western currents.
- Mains: explain the gyres as wind stress plus rotation plus coastline, and mention western intensification as the reason the western currents are strongest.
- Trap: stating that the wind alone drives the gyre, or that gyres rotate the same way in both hemispheres.

**Revision points**

1. Wind stress is the friction of the air on the surface.
2. The Coriolis parameter is 2Ω sin φ; it is zero at the equator.
3. Motion is deflected to the right in the north and to the left in the south.
4. The Ekman spiral turns the surface layer away from the wind.
5. Net Ekman transport is 90 degrees to the right (north) or left (south) of the wind.
6. The Ekman depth is a few tens of metres, about 50 metres at mid-latitudes on a typical assumption.
7. Geostrophic flow runs along sea-surface height contours.
8. Northern subtropical gyres turn clockwise; southern ones anticlockwise.
9. Western intensification makes the western boundary currents narrow and fast.
10. The beta effect is the change of Coriolis with latitude.
11. The Gulf Stream is the classic western boundary current.
12. Every gyre explanation needs wind, rotation and boundaries together.

Try it on paper: in the Southern Hemisphere, a steady westerly wind blows over the surface. In which direction is the net Ekman transport?
*Answer pointer:* To the left of the wind, which means towards the north when the wind blows eastward, because the Southern Hemisphere deflects left. Check the direction by drawing the wind arrow and rotating it 90 degrees to the left.

The next lesson follows these circulations to the basin edges, where the warm and cold limbs of the gyres meet the continents.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a steady wind not push the surface water straight downwind, and why does the Southern Hemisphere's gyre spin the opposite way to the northern one?

**Model answer:** Friction transmits the wind's momentum downward, but the Coriolis effect turns each layer more than the one above, so the surface moves at an angle and the net transport is at 90 degrees to the wind. The deflection is to the right in the north and to the left in the south, so the gyres spin in opposite senses.

**Misconception to avoid:** the wind does not carry the water in its own direction. It sets the water in motion, and rotation bends that motion.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how the wind and the Earth's rotation together produce a subtropical gyre, and why its western side is stronger than its eastern side. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer, 15 marks:** Wind stress drags the surface, and the Coriolis effect deflects the motion to the right in the north and to the left in the south. The Ekman spiral turns the surface layer away from the wind, so the net transport is at 90 degrees to the wind. Winds that curl across a basin therefore pile water up in its centre, and the resulting sea-surface slope drives geostrophic flow around the centre, clockwise in the north and anticlockwise in the south. The western side is stronger because the Coriolis parameter changes with latitude. That change, the beta effect, concentrates the return flow into a narrow, fast western boundary current such as the Gulf Stream, while the eastern side is broad and slow. Coastlines and friction shape the real gyre, so the idealised account is a first approximation.

### Start with a gyre cut open

```text
SCHEMATIC CROSS-SECTION ACROSS ONE SUBTROPICAL GYRE (west at left, east at right, not to scale)

 WEST side of basin                                     EAST side of basin
 narrow, fast, warm                                     broad, slow, cool
 western boundary current  <== return flow of the gyre   eastern boundary current
 carries water POLEWARD                                 carries water EQUATORWARD
 e.g. Gulf Stream, Kuroshio                             e.g. Canary, California
        |                                                       |
 interior: wind-driven, slow, set by wind-stress curl and the beta effect
```

Lesson 6 gave you the gyre as a loop. This lesson opens the loop and asks what each side carries. The two sides of a basin do not behave alike: one is narrow and strong, the other broad and weak. The contrast is the point of the lesson.

### Two edges of one loop

Picture a slow, large wheel of water with the wind pushing its top. The wheel's interior turns gently. Its western rim, where the water must return from the tropics to the poles and back, is squeezed into a fast, narrow stream. Its eastern rim is wide and slow. A western boundary current is therefore the gyre's return flow forced into a narrow channel. An eastern boundary current is the gentle outer limb.

### The doctrine: four circuits, one rule

Each ocean basin has a subtropical gyre. In the Northern Hemisphere it turns clockwise, and in the Southern it turns anticlockwise. Along the western edge the warm water runs poleward, and along the eastern edge the cold water runs equatorward. This is the rule of boundary currents, and it holds in every basin.

- **North Atlantic:** the Gulf Stream carries warm water northward off North America and continues as the North Atlantic Current. The Canary Current runs cold and equatorward off North-west Africa.
- **North Pacific:** the Kuroshio carries warm water northward off East Asia. The California Current runs cold and equatorward off North America.
- **South Atlantic:** the Brazil Current carries warm water poleward on the western side. The Benguela Current runs cold and equatorward off south-west Africa.
- **South Pacific:** the East Australian Current is a warm western limb, and the Humboldt, or Peru, Current runs cold along South America's western coast.
- **Indian Ocean:** the Agulhas and Mozambique currents carry warm water along the western side, and the West Australian Current runs cold and equatorward along the eastern side.

Two points need care. First, "warm" and "cold" are relative to the surrounding water; the labels describe the contrast, not an absolute temperature. Second, the subpolar loops at higher latitudes have their own names. In the North Pacific, the cyclonic loop in the Gulf of Alaska is called the Alaska Gyre by some writers and treated as part of the subpolar gyre by others. The disagreement is about the name and the boundary of a convention, not about the water. An answer that quotes one name should say so.

### The Southern Ocean: the circuit with no closed basin

The Southern Ocean has no subtropical gyre of the usual kind, because the wind-driven circulation runs all the way around the globe at high southern latitudes. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current, or ACC, is the result. It flows eastward, driven by the strong westerly winds of the roaring forties and furious fifties, and it is the one current that links all three major basins.

A common error must not be repeated here: there is land south of 40 degrees south, including Tasmania, New Zealand, Patagonia and Antarctica itself. What is true is narrower: the ACC is not blocked by a continent along its full circuit, and it passes through gaps such as the Drake Passage between South America and the Antarctic Peninsula. The absence of a closed continental ring is what lets the current encircle the globe.

### Why the western side is stronger: the depth view

The western intensification of Lesson 6 is an interior-plus-boundary story, and it is worth seeing it at depth. Below the Ekman layer of about 50 metres, the interior of a gyre is in geostrophic balance, so the flow runs along sea-surface height contours. The interior's north-south transport is set by the Sverdrup balance, which links the north-south transport to the curl of the wind stress divided by the beta effect. The curl, the rotation of the wind's pattern, is what drives the interior; the beta effect, the change of the Coriolis parameter with latitude, is what turns that interior transport into a return flow concentrated on the western side.

The argument has three steps. The wind curl pumps water toward the gyre's centre. Geostrophy then makes the centre a hill and the edges slopes. The beta effect pushes the return flow west, where friction and the boundary close the loop into a narrow jet.

### Comparison: the Atlantic and the Pacific

The Atlantic is narrow and north-south in orientation, so its warm western current crosses fewer longitudes before it meets cold water. The Pacific is much wider, so its circuits are longer and its subpolar loop is more clearly marked. Both basins have a northern subpolar circuit, whose gyre and boundary currents close the circulation with the subtropical gyre. The Indian Ocean does not, and that is the subject of Lesson 12. The contrast shows that basin geometry, not only the wind, sets the circuit.

### Example: the Indian Ocean's western limb, with its limit

India's nearest analogue is the western limb of the Indian Ocean. The East African Coastal Current and the Somali Current flow along the western side of the basin, and they change direction with the monsoon. A year-round, Gulf Stream-type western current does not exist there. The example teaches the rule's exception: the western-limb logic is present, but the seasonal reversal of the winds breaks the loop that a permanent gyre would need. Lesson 13 returns to this.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** if the western current is so strong, the winds must be strongest on the western side.

**Reply:** the winds are broadly similar across the basin. The strength comes from the beta effect, which concentrates the return flow on the western side however uniform the wind.

**Residual:** eddies, friction, the coastline and the detachment of the Gulf Stream from the coast all change the real boundary current, and the idealised theory does not capture them.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: which current is warm and which is cold, and which limb is poleward.
- Mains: explain the rule of boundary currents with a basin-specific example, and say why the western side is stronger.
- Trap: writing that there is no land south of 40 degrees south; the ACC is open where the gaps are, not everywhere.

**Revision points**

1. Each subtropical gyre has a narrow, fast western boundary current carrying warm water poleward.
2. The eastern boundary current carries cold water equatorward.
3. Warm and cold are relative terms.
4. The Gulf Stream and Kuroshio are western limbs; the Canary, California, Benguela and Humboldt are eastern limbs.
5. The Brazil and East Australian currents are southern western limbs.
6. The Alaska Gyre name is a convention; some writers merge it into the subpolar gyre.
7. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current flows eastward around the globe at high southern latitudes.
8. Land does exist south of 40 degrees south; the ACC is not blocked along its whole circuit.
9. Below the Ekman layer the interior is geostrophic and set by wind-stress curl and beta.

11. The Indian Ocean's western limb reverses with the monsoon, so no permanent western current forms there.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the western side of a subtropical gyre carry a narrow, fast current while the eastern side is broad and slow, even when the wind is nearly uniform across the basin?

The beta effect pushes the return flow of the gyre onto the western boundary, where it is concentrated into a narrow jet. The strength is therefore a result of rotation and geometry, not of stronger winds on the western side.

**Misconception to avoid:** a strong western current does not mean stronger winds. The boundary is where the return flow is forced to concentrate.

### Mains practice

Because the Coriolis parameter changes with latitude, the beta effect pushes that return flow onto the western side, where it becomes a narrow, fast current such as the Gulf Stream or Kuroshio. The eastern side carries the outer, broad limb, which is slower and cooler, such as the California or Canary currents. The difference is therefore one of dynamics, not of wind strength. Friction and eddies shape the real currents, so the explanation is an approximation of the interior and the boundary.

### Start with a surface that is being emptied

```text
COASTAL UPWELLING, NORTHERN HEMISPHERE (eastern boundary of an ocean: land to the east, ocean to the west)

  equatorward wind along the coast (blowing south)
        |
  Ekman transport: 90 degrees to the RIGHT of the wind = OFFSHORE (west)
        |
  surface water pushed away from the coast
        |
  cold, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it   <- UPWELLING
        |
  nutrients + sunlight in the surface layer -> phytoplankton bloom -> fish
```

The chain begins with a wind and ends with a fish, and every link must hold. Each step has a condition that can fail.

### A surface drawn down from below

The sea surface is like a shelf in a crowded kitchen. Plankton need light (the top layer) and nutrients (which are mostly deep). Upwelling is the process that empties the top shelf and refills it from below. Where the wind removes surface water faster than rain or inflow replaces it, deep water rises. Deep water is cold and carries nitrate and phosphate that surface water has used up.

### The doctrine: three mechanisms, one common input

Upwelling is the rise of deep water to the surface. Three settings supply the nutrients that feed the fishery. Each setting has its own driver.

| Setting | Driver | Where it occurs | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coastal (eastern boundary) upwelling | Alongshore wind, Ekman transport offshore | Eastern margins: Peru, California, Canary, Benguela | Needs a suitable wind and a coast |
| Equatorial divergence | Easterly trades; water moves away from the equator | A narrow band around the equator | Explained by equatorial dynamics, not by simple Ekman theory |
| Convergence and frontal mixing | Warm and cold currents meet and mix | Where a warm and cold current meet, often over broad shelves | Needs a sharp contrast and enough light |

Mechanism one follows directly from Lesson 6. Winds blowing parallel to a coast set up an Ekman transport that is 90 degrees to the wind. In the Northern Hemisphere, an equatorward wind along an eastern coast drives the transport offshore, and the water that replaces it comes from below. The figure above is the same rule as Lesson 6, applied to a coast.

Mechanism two needs a correction that the earlier lessons flagged. Ekman transport is 90 degrees to the wind, and geostrophic balance holds, only where the Coriolis parameter is non-zero. The Coriolis parameter vanishes at the equator. Ekman and geostrophic balance therefore break down there, and equatorial upwelling is explained by a different dynamics involving equatorial waves. What remains true is the simple result: easterly trades drive water away from the equator on both sides and draw deeper water up, but the exact balance is not the mid-latitude one. The equator is a special place in the ocean, and a student should not apply the mid-latitude Ekman rule without saying so.

Mechanism three is convergence and frontal mixing. Where a warm current and a cold current meet, the boundary between them is sharp, and mixing across it brings nutrients into the sunlit layer. Major fishing grounds in the middle latitudes often sit on broad shelves where such fronts form. The Grand Banks, where cold Labrador water meets the warm waters of the Gulf Stream system, and the junction of the Oyashio and Kuroshio off north-eastern Japan are the classic examples.

### The Namib: upwelling that makes a desert

Off south-west Africa, the Benguela upwelling cools the coast, and the cold surface water suppresses evaporation and rain. The Namib desert lies along that coast. The chain is: upwelling cools the air above it, cool air holds little moisture, fog forms over the cold water, and little rain falls on the land. The Namib is therefore a case where an ocean process shapes the land.

The limit is important. The Kalahari lies inland, away from the cold coast. Its dryness owes much less to the sea, and it cannot be explained by upwelling. A student who calls all dry southern African land "upwelling desert" has made the error that the Namib example is designed to expose.

### The India case, with its limit

The Arabian Sea coast of India and the Somali coast show seasonal upwelling during the south-west monsoon. The monsoon winds drive coastal upwelling, nutrients rise, and fisheries respond. The limit is timing. The upwelling is strong in one season and weak in others, so productivity off the Indian west coast is seasonal rather than year-round. Lesson 13 turns this into the monsoon reversal.

### Failure: the deepened thermocline

Upwelling supplies nutrients only if the water that rises is nutrient-rich. In the tropical Pacific, the thermocline, the steep temperature step described in Lesson 3, sits shallow in the east under normal conditions. When it deepens, the water that the wind lifts is warm and nutrient-poor. The wind may still blow, but the fishery collapses because the supply has changed. Lesson 14 explains why the thermocline deepens; here the point is only that the same wind produces a different result.

### Comparison: why the three settings differ

All three settings deliver nutrients, but they differ in their control. Coastal upwelling depends on the wind parallel to a coast. Equatorial divergence depends on the trades and the equatorial dynamics. Frontal mixing depends on the contrast between currents. The fishery is therefore a joint product of wind, geometry and water mass, and a change in any of them can remove it.

### Objection and reply

**A tempting claim:** cold water must simply contain more fish.

**Reply:** cold water carries nutrients because it is deep, and the nutrients fuel the plankton that feed the food chain. Cold water without the supply of nutrients is poor, and warm water with a strong nutrient supply can be productive. The cause is the nutrient supply, and cold is only the sign that it may be present.

**Residual:** a productive season does not guarantee a catch. Markets, regulation, fishing effort and species movement also decide landings, and the oceanographic explanation covers only the base of the food chain.

**Revision points**

1. Upwelling raises deep, cold, nutrient-rich water into the sunlit layer.
2. Coastal upwelling is driven by alongshore wind through offshore Ekman transport.
3. The Ekman rule, and geostrophy, fail where the Coriolis parameter vanishes, at the equator.
4. Equatorial divergence is a distinct mechanism with its own dynamics.
5. Frontal mixing at a warm-cold junction is the third fishing setting.
6. The Namib desert is shaped by the cold Benguela coast through fog and suppressed rain.
7. The Kalahari is inland, and its aridity is not an upwelling result.
8. Upwelling supplies nutrients; it does not guarantee a fishery.
9. A deepened thermocline makes the upwelled water warm and nutrient-poor.
10. The Arabian Sea coast upwells seasonally during the south-west monsoon.
11. Productivity is set by the nutrient supply, not by the temperature alone.

Pause and predict: a coast has a steady wind blowing parallel to it and productivity falls sharply after the thermocline deepens. Which two links in the chain have failed, and which one would you check first?
*Answer pointer:* The wind may still blow, so the failing links are the nutrient supply (the water lifted is warm and poor) and the thermocline's depth. Check the thermocline first, because it decides what the wind lifts.

The next lesson moves below the wind. The deep circuit is driven by density, not by the wind, and it can be disturbed by changes at the surface that no wind can reverse.

The argument is that the wind creates the opportunity but the water column decides the yield.

### Start with a sinking pool

```text
NORTH ATLANTIC (schematic, not to scale)
  warm, salty surface water flows NORTH  (Gulf Stream / North Atlantic Current)
        |
  loses heat to cold air; gains density; evaporation has salted it
        |
  dense water SINKS in the subpolar seas (deep-water formation)
        |
  deep water flows SOUTH at depth (North Atlantic Deep Water)
        |
  rises slowly through mixing across the world ocean, returns to the surface
  (the loop is closed by upwelling that is diffuse, and mostly in the Southern Ocean)
```

The deep circuit is not a surface current. Its motion is slow, measured in centuries for a full loop, and it is driven by differences of density. This is the thermohaline circulation: thermo for temperature, haline for salinity.

### Why deep water moves at all

Density is set by temperature and salinity (Lesson 5). Cold water is denser than warm water, and salty water is denser than fresh. The North Atlantic is cold in winter and salty from evaporation, so its surface water becomes heavy enough to sink. Once it sinks, it cannot easily return until mixing lifts it again. The circuit runs on that one fact.

### The doctrine: formation, flow, upwelling

The overturning has three parts. The first is formation: dense water sinks in a few high-latitude regions, notably the subpolar North Atlantic, where North Atlantic Deep Water forms, and around Antarctica, where Antarctic Bottom Water forms and spreads along the seafloor. The second is the deep flow: the water moves south at depth and along the bottom of the basins. The third is return: the water rises again, most of it diffusely across the ocean and much of it in the Southern Ocean, where the westerly winds bring deep water to the surface.

The overturning's Atlantic limb is called the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC. It is the Atlantic part of the thermohaline circuit. It carries warm water north near the surface, which contributes to the milder climate of north-western Europe, and returns cold water south at depth.

### Two engines, not one

A common statement says that the AMOC is the only engine of heat transport in the Atlantic. That is wrong, and it must not be repeated. The overturning is powered by two sources. The density contrast from cooling and evaporation drives the sinking. But the wind drives much of the circulation as well. The Southern Ocean's westerly winds pump deep water upward and drive exchange between basins, and mixing from tides and winds across the deep ocean lifts water back to the surface. Density sets where the water goes down, and the wind and mixing keep the loop moving.

### Sensitivity: freshwater and a feedback

A large freshwater input at high latitudes, from melting ice or heavy rain, lowers the density of the surface water. If the surface becomes too light to sink, deep-water formation slows. This is the mechanism behind the worry about a weakened AMOC.

A positive feedback can make this process stronger once it starts. If sinking slows, less warm salty water reaches the subpolar seas, the surface becomes fresher there, and sinking slows further. This is the salt-advection feedback, and simple models of it show that the overturning can have more than one stable state. But a feedback can amplify and switch a state. It does not, by itself, explain a seasonal reversal such as the monsoon currents, which are wind-driven and taught in Lesson 13. Keep the two ideas apart.

### The limits of the picture

The sensitivity of the AMOC is an open research question. Observations of its strength have been reported and contested, and the timing and size of any future weakening are uncertain. Models differ in how much they weaken the circulation and in what happens to rainfall afterward. Some models link a large AMOC weakening to a southward shift of the tropical rain belt, and in some of them the Indian monsoon responds weakly or with a delay. The Indian monsoon response is model-dependent, not a settled one-way forecast. A claim that the overturning is slowing, or that a particular monsoon outcome will follow, needs a dated and attributed reference.

### Comparison: the Indian Ocean and the Atlantic

The North Atlantic is one of the main sites of deep-water formation. The North Indian Ocean has little. The Bay of Bengal's freshwater cap, described in Lesson 5, keeps the upper layer stratified and light, so cooling does not easily produce deep sinking there. Thus the Indian Ocean's deep exchange depends more on the Southern Ocean than on the north. This is an India-centric example with a limit: the Bay's stratification explains the weak northern sinking, but the basin's deep water still arrives from the south, and the statement is a comparison, not a measured rate.

### Objection and reply

**Doubt:** the deep circuit is too slow to matter for the monsoon.

**Reply:** its timescale is long, but its heat and salt are stored and released over decades. A change in the overturning alters the distribution of heat that the atmosphere sees, so slow change can still reach the rain belts through the atmosphere.

**Residual:** how much of a given climate change would come through the overturning, and how much through other routes, is not settled.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: the formation sites, the sense of the deep flow and the difference between a current and the overturning.
- Mains: explain thermohaline circulation with its two engines, and treat the monsoon link as model-dependent.
- Trap: calling AMOC the only engine, or using a positive feedback to explain a seasonal reversal.

**Revision points**

1. The thermohaline circulation is driven by differences of density from temperature and salinity.
2. Dense water sinks in a few high-latitude regions.
3. North Atlantic Deep Water forms in the subpolar North Atlantic; Antarctic Bottom Water forms around Antarctica.
4. The deep flow is slow, over centuries for a full loop.
5. The AMOC is the Atlantic limb of the overturning.
6. Wind and mixing are engines alongside density.
7. Freshwater input can lower surface density and slow sinking.
8. A salt-advection feedback can amplify a slowdown and give more than one stable state.
9. A feedback does not explain a seasonal monsoon reversal.
10. AMOC strength and change are open research questions.
11. The Indian monsoon response to a weaker AMOC is model-dependent.
12. The Bay's freshwater cap keeps the northern Indian Ocean from sinking easily.

Check yourself: name one process that sinks water in the North Atlantic, and one process that lifts it back to the surface.
*Answer pointer:* Cooling and evaporation make the water dense enough to sink in the subpolar seas. Diffuse mixing and Southern Ocean upwelling lift it back, with the winds and tides keeping the loop moving.

The next lesson moves from the deep circuit to the sea's daily and monthly rhythm. The tides are driven by the Moon and the Sun, not by density, and they set the level of the water on every coast.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a slowdown of the North Atlantic overturning be triggered by freshwater, even when the wind over the basin does not change, and why is that not the same as a seasonal monsoon reversal?

**Model answer:** Sinking depends on density, and freshwater lowers the density of the surface water in the subpolar seas, so less of it sinks. The wind need not change for this to happen. A monsoon reversal is a seasonal change of wind direction that reverses the surface currents, and it is a different process with a different driver, so the two should not be treated as the same kind of switch.

**Misconception to avoid:** a feedback that amplifies a state does not explain every change of state. The driver must be identified for each case.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why the thermohaline overturning is not driven by density alone, and assess the statement that a freshwater input could slow it. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The overturning begins with density: cooling and evaporation make surface water in the subpolar seas dense enough to sink. But winds, especially the Southern Ocean westerlies, and mixing from tides and winds also drive the circulation, so density alone is not the whole engine. A large freshwater input lowers surface density and can slow sinking, and a salt-advection feedback can then amplify the slowdown. The statement is therefore plausible but conditional: the size and timing of any slowdown are open, and its monsoon effects are model-dependent.

## Lesson 10 - Why are tides large in one place and small in another?

Progress: 10 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Tide-generating forces, the arithmetic of the Moon and Sun, and tidal types

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not run
CA found: none verified (search service unavailable, 24 October 2026)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Start with two bulges, not one

```text
TIDE-GENERATING FORCE (schematic, not to scale)

   Moon side:   water is pulled MOST strongly  -> bulge toward the Moon
   Earth core:  pulled LESS than the near water -> the Earth moves away from it on that side
   Far side:    pulled LEAST strongly           -> water is left behind -> bulge AWAY from the Moon

   Result: two bulges, opposite each other, while the Earth turns beneath them
   One coast passes through a bulge about every 12 hours 25 minutes (a lunar day is about 24 hours 50 minutes)
```

The most common picture of a tide is one wave sweeping in from the sea. The real tide is a difference in pull. The Moon pulls the near side of the Earth harder than the centre, and the centre harder than the far side. That difference, not the total pull, stretches the water into two bulges.

### Tides are a difference, not a push

Gravity falls off with distance. The water nearest the Moon is pulled hardest, the solid Earth is pulled less, and the water on the far side is pulled least. Subtract the average pull of the whole Earth, and what is left points outward at the near and far sides. Water responds to that residual stretch, which is why tides are controlled by the gradient of gravity, not by its size.

### The arithmetic: Moon against Sun

The Sun is far heavier than the Moon, yet the Moon raises the larger tide. The tidal effect of a body depends on its mass divided by the cube of its distance, because the difference of pulls falls off with the cube of distance. Write the ratio of the two effects:

```text
Moon / Sun ratio = (M_moon / M_sun) x (d_sun / d_moon) cubed

  M_moon / M_sun   about 7.3 x 10^22 / 2.0 x 10^30  (roughly 3.7 x 10^-8)
  d_sun / d_moon   about 1.50 x 10^11 / 3.84 x 10^8 (roughly 390)
  390 cubed        roughly 5.9 x 10^7

  ratio = 3.7 x 10^-8 x 5.9 x 10^7  =  about 2.2
```

The Moon's tide-raising effect is therefore about 2.2 times the Sun's. The Sun is heavier, but it is so far away that its tidal stretch is smaller. The arithmetic is derived from mass and distance, and it gives the ratio as a rounded value. Nothing in the lesson needs a more precise figure.

### Spring and neap tides, derived

Spring tides occur when the Sun and Moon pull together, at New Moon and Full Moon. Neap tides occur when they pull at right angles, at the quarter Moons. The cycle repeats every half of the time it takes the Sun-Moon angle to come back to the same alignment.

```text
Synodic month (New Moon to New Moon)   about 29.5 days
Sun-Moon angle changes by 360 degrees in 29.5 days -> about 12.2 degrees a day
Alignment repeats every 180 degrees (New Moon, then Full Moon)
Time between springs = 180 / 12.2  =  about 14.8 days
```

Spring tides therefore come about every fortnight, and so do neap tides. The name "spring" refers to the water springing up, not to the spring season. The timing has nothing to do with the seasons.

### Tidal types: one, two or a mix

The number of high tides a day depends on the place, not on a universal rule. Three types are recognised:

| Type | Pattern | Where it is typical |
|---|---|---|
| Semidiurnal | Two highs and two lows a lunar day, of similar height | Many open coasts, including much of the Atlantic coast of Europe |
| Mixed | Two highs and two lows a day, but of unequal height | Many coasts with an uneven pattern, including parts of the Indian coasts |
| Diurnal | One high and one low a lunar day | Some enclosed or resonant seas and some coasts |

The statement that all coasts have two high tides a day is wrong. A diurnal coast has one high tide each lunar day, and a mixed coast has two highs of unequal height. The lunar day is about 24 hours 50 minutes, so each successive tide is about 50 minutes later on the clock.

### Why the local range differs

The tide-generating force is almost the same everywhere on the globe, but the tidal range differs widely. The range is the height between high and low water. It depends on the shape of the basin and the coastline, and on how long the water takes to slosh back and forth. A funnel-shaped estuary can amplify the rise, while an open coast with a short shelf may show little. The Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch on India's west coast have large ranges, while many open coasts have small ones. The reason is local geometry, not a difference in the Moon's pull.

### Limit and objection

**Objection:** if the Moon pulls the water, the tide should rise at the same time everywhere on the Moon's meridian.

**Reply:** the tide wave is a response of the basin, not a direct pull. It takes time to travel and is shaped by the coastline, so the timing and height differ from place to place.

**Residual:** the exact height of a tide at a given hour depends on many constituents that combine the Moon, the Sun and the latitude. The simple two-body picture gives the principle, not the local prediction.

### Answer use and traps

Explain tides as a difference of pulls and the local range by basin. Avoid calling spring tides seasonal and assuming two highs everywhere.

**Revision points**

1. Tides come from the difference in gravity across the Earth, not from the total pull.
2. The two bulges sit on opposite sides of the Earth.
3. The tide-generating effect scales with mass divided by the cube of distance.
4. The Moon's tidal effect is about 2.2 times the Sun's.
5. Spring tides occur at New and Full Moon, and neap tides at the quarter Moons.
6. The spring-neap cycle repeats about every 14.8 days.
7. Spring tides are not tied to the season.
8. Semidiurnal, mixed and diurnal are the three tidal types.
9. A diurnal coast has one high tide per lunar day.
10. A lunar day is about 24 hours 50 minutes.
11. The local range is set by basin geometry and resonance.
12. The Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch show large ranges, from local shape.

Stop and retrieve: a coast has one high tide each lunar day, and the Sun and Moon are at right angles this week. Is the tide type diurnal or semidiurnal, and is the range large or small?
*Answer pointer:* Diurnal, because there is one high tide per lunar day. The Sun and Moon at right angles give neap tides, so the range is small this week.

The next lesson asks what the tide does for human use. The same water that gives a predictable energy supply also limits navigation, siting and the sediment that reaches the coast.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student says that because the Sun is far heavier than the Moon, the Sun must raise the larger tide. Correct the claim with the arithmetic, and say what the spring-neap cycle tells you about the two effects.

**Model answer:** The tidal effect depends on mass divided by the cube of distance, and the Sun's distance is so great that its tidal effect is smaller, about 0.46 of the Moon's, which is the same as the Moon being about 2.2 times more effective. Spring and neap tides show both effects at work: they add when the Sun and Moon align and partly cancel when they are at right angles.

**Misconception to avoid:** mass alone does not decide the tide. The difference in pull across the Earth, which depends on distance, decides it.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why the Moon, not the Sun, dominates the tide, and why the tidal range still varies from coast to coast. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The tide is produced by the difference in gravity across the Earth. That difference falls with the cube of distance, so the Moon, which is much closer, has about 2.2 times the Sun's tidal effect despite its smaller mass. Spring tides form when the two align and neap tides when they are at right angles, with a cycle of about 14.8 days. The number of highs a day also varies: semidiurnal, mixed and diurnal coasts all occur. The local range is set by basin shape and the time water takes to respond, so two coasts under the same pull can have very different tides.

## Lesson 11 - What makes tidal energy possible, and what limits it?

Book context: not available
CA search: not run
CA found: none verified (search service unavailable, 24 October 2026)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Start with a basin filling and emptying

```text
TIDAL BARRAGE, ONE CYCLE (schematic cross-section of an estuary)

  FLOOD (rising sea)        a sluice holds the basin behind a barrier
  basin level  LOW          water passes through turbines as the sea rises (power option)
  sea level    HIGH

  EBB (falling sea)         the sea falls, leaving the basin high
  basin level  HIGH         water flows back out through turbines (power on ebb)
  sea level    LOW

  Energy per cycle is set by the water stored in the basin and the height it falls through.
```

A tidal power plant works like a hydroelectric dam with the water supplied twice a day. The sea rises and falls by the tidal range, and the basin behind a barrier can be filled or emptied through turbines. The energy comes from the Moon's and the Sun's work in lifting and lowering the water.

### The intuition: the energy of a shifted pool

Lift a pool of water through a height and it stores energy. The stored energy is the mass times the height times the gravitational pull. A barrage captures the energy of a basin whose level is pushed up or down by the tide. The useful energy is therefore set by how much water moves and by how far it falls.

### The energy relation

For a basin of area A and a tidal range h, the water moved in one filling or emptying has a potential energy of about one half of rho times g times A times h squared, where rho is the density of water and g is gravitational acceleration. The energy grows with the square of the range. A coast with twice the range can store about four times the energy in the same basin. Power is the energy divided by the time, and because the tide turns twice a day, it is intermittent.

Three conclusions follow from the relation. The range matters more than the area, because it is squared. Large basins help, because the stored volume grows with area. And the output is not constant, because the tidal range itself rises and falls over the fortnight.

### Methods: three, with trade-offs

| Method | How it works | Main advantage | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrage across an estuary | Basin filled and emptied through turbines | Uses the largest stored volume | Heavy capital cost; alters tidal flow, sediment and fish passage |
| Tidal stream turbine | Rotors placed in fast tidal currents | No large dam | Needs strong currents; siting and maintenance are hard |
| Tidal lagoon | An artificial enclosure built to hold water | Flexible timing | Large construction; ecological effects on the shore |

The method chosen depends on the coast's range, its currents and the sediment that the works would disturb. There is no single best method.

### Tidal energy in the wider set of sources

Tidal energy is one of several natural energy sources that the ocean offers. It is not the only one, and it is not the only renewable source at sea. Waves, ocean thermal gradients and offshore wind are also available. Tidal energy has a particular advantage: its timing can be predicted years ahead from the Moon's and the Sun's orbits. Its disadvantage is that it is intermittent in a daily and fortnightly rhythm. The statement that it is the only natural energy source at sea is therefore wrong.

### India, with its limit

India's west coast has large-range inlets, the Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch, which are often cited for tidal study. The limit is that a large tidal range does not by itself give a viable project. The site must also have a basin of suitable shape, a sediment regime the works will not choke, and a fish and shoreline cost that the local economy can bear. No output figure is taught here.

### Constraints: a resource that also limits use

Tides are as much a constraint as a resource. Navigation depends on the high and low water of each day, and ports are timed to the tide. Estuaries with large ranges also carry strong currents and tidal bores, which affect shipping and settlement. The same tides that give a predictable energy supply also set the timing of the sediment that reaches the coast, so a barrage changes the sediment supply that the tide carries.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** tidal energy is free and inexhaustible, so every large-range coast should use it.

**Reply:** the fuel is free, but the capital, the sediment and the ecological cost are not. A large range helps, but only where the basin and the costs are acceptable.

**Residual:** the ecological effect of any given barrage or turbine array depends on the species and the sediment of that estuary, and it is not settled in general.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: the methods of tidal power and the sign of its variation over the fortnight.
- Mains: explain the energy relation, name the constraints, and assess a proposal with the site test.
- Trap: calling tidal energy the only natural energy source at sea, or quoting a power output without a site.

**Revision points**

1. Tidal power captures the water moved by the tidal range.
2. Stored energy in a basin scales with the range squared and the basin area.
3. A barrage uses the largest volume but is the most costly to build.
4. Tidal stream turbines need strong currents, not a dam.
5. A tidal lagoon is an artificial enclosure with flexible timing.
6. Tidal energy is predictable but intermittent in a daily and fortnightly rhythm.
7. It is one of several natural energy sources at sea, not the only one.
8. The Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch are large-range sites.
9. A large range does not make a project viable on its own.
10. Barrages alter flow, sediment and fish passage.
11. Tides set port timing and navigation.
12. A barrage changes the sediment supply, not only the tide.

Predict, then check: a barrage is proposed for an estuary with the same range as its neighbour, but a basin twice as large. Which factor changes the stored energy more, the range or the area, and by how much?

### Concept check

**Question:** Two estuaries have the same tidal range, but one has a basin four times as large. Which stores more energy per cycle, and what other constraint could still prevent a project from being built?

**Model answer:** The larger basin stores more energy, in proportion to its area, so the energy is about four times greater with the same range. The constraint that could still block the project is the sediment, the ecology or the cost of the barrage, because the stored energy does not decide whether those can be borne.

**Misconception to avoid:** the amount of energy available is not the same as the amount a project can use. Ecology, sediment and cost set the feasible share.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Why is tidal power predictable yet intermittent, and what does that mean for its place in a national energy supply? (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Tidal power is predictable because the tide follows the Moon's and Sun's orbits, which can be computed well in advance. It is intermittent because the tide turns twice a day and the range varies over the fortnightly cycle, so the output rises and falls. The energy relation explains the pattern: the energy stored depends on the range squared and the basin area. Its place in a supply is therefore as a predictable but variable contributor, to be combined with other sources. A project also needs a site where the basin, sediment and ecology are acceptable.

```text
   Madagascar sits in the west; the Indonesian seas link to the Pacific in the east
   ---------------------------------------------
   SOUTH: open to the Southern Ocean; subtropical circulation runs freely here
```

Lesson 1 said that the Indian Ocean's northern side is closed by Asia, so its surface circulation cannot close in the north. This lesson turns that geometry into an argument, then applies it to the seabed, which lies beyond the basin's coasts, and to the instruments that observe it.

### A loop with nowhere to close

Picture a gyre as a loop that must be closed. In the Atlantic and the Pacific, the loop closes through the subpolar gyres and boundary currents to the north. In the Indian Ocean it cannot, because Asia blocks the northern side. The wind that drives the loop has nowhere to return the water except across the equator and into the monsoon system. So the north develops a different pattern, and the south keeps a more regular subtropical gyre.

### The argument, in steps

The argument has three premises. First, a permanent subtropical gyre needs a return path on its northern side, which in the Atlantic and Pacific is supplied by the subpolar circulation. Second, the Indian Ocean's northern side is closed by Asia, so no such return path exists. Third, the monsoon winds reverse twice a year, so even the open parts of the north do not stay in one pattern. The conclusion is that the north Indian Ocean has no permanent gyre, while the south keeps one. The conclusion is not a claim that the wind is weak; it is the consequence of geometry combined with a seasonal wind.

The south has a more regular subtropical gyre. Its western limb carries warm water along the coast of Africa, through the Agulhas Current, which flows past the tip of Africa. Some Indian Ocean water leaks into the Atlantic by this route, called Agulhas leakage. A second connection is the Indonesian throughflow, which carries water from the Pacific into the Indian Ocean through the Indonesian seas.

### The Red Sea and the Gulf: links back to salinity

Lesson 4 described the Red Sea's budget. The Indian Ocean's north-west corner contains the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, both hot and with high evaporation, and both salty. The strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, commonly about 26 kilometres wide at its narrowest, connects the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. The figure varies between reports, and the salinity follows from the budget, not from the strait's width.

### The seabed beyond the coasts: contracts and the Area

Lesson 2 described three kinds of seabed mineral. Polymetallic nodules lie on abyssal plains, polymetallic sulphides form at ridges and vents, and cobalt-rich crusts occur on seamount flanks. Who may explore them depends on where they lie.

Beyond national jurisdiction, the seabed is called the Area, and the International Seabed Authority, or ISA, licenses exploration. Contractors must be sponsored by a state that is party to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, and the ISA contracts with government and private bodies. The contracts cover exploration, not exploitation, and for the minerals named, the ISA reports contracts for nodules in the Clarion-Clipperton zone, the Central Indian Ocean Basin and the Western Pacific; sulphides on several ridges including ridges in the Indian Ocean; and crusts in the Western Pacific. Contracts run for 15 years, and contractors must report annually and propose training for developing countries.

India holds a contract in this regime. The ISA signed a sulphide contract with the Government of India on 26 September 2016, for the polymetallic sulphides of the ridges the ISA lists for that mineral, which include the South West Indian Ridge and the Central Indian Ridge. The record does not name the exact ridge for India, and this topic does not supply one.

The limit is that an exploration contract is not a right to mine. Exploitation requires a separate regime that is not yet in force, and the legal and environmental questions that follow belong to the environment and international-relations topics. A 2021 Prelims item on seabed mineral licensing bears on this regime; its key is not recorded here.

### The observing system: three instruments

The ocean's interior and surface are watched by three kinds of instrument, and each measures a different thing.

```text
ARGO FLOAT, ONE CYCLE (about 10 days)
  parked at about 1,000 m, drifting with the current
  sinks to about 2,000 m, then rises to the surface
  measures conductivity, temperature and pressure on the way up
  -> salinity and density profiles from the upper 2,000 m
  at the surface, a GPS fix shows where the float drifted

SATELLITE ALTIMETRY (radar)
  radar measures the time for a pulse to return from the surface
  time x speed of light / 2 = distance to the sea surface
  sea-surface height = satellite orbit height minus that distance, after corrections
  height slopes -> geostrophic currents, as derived in Lesson 6

MOORED ARRAY (buoys in the Indian Ocean)
  surface buoys and subsurface sensors fixed in place
  record air and sea-surface conditions, and subsurface currents
```

The Argo float drifts at a depth of about 1,000 metres, where it avoids the surface's wind-driven motion. Every ten days it descends to about 2,000 metres and rises again, measuring conductivity, temperature and pressure as it goes. Salinity and density are derived from those readings. The cycle gives a profile of the upper ocean that ship surveys cannot match in coverage.

Altimetry measures the sea surface from space. The principle is simple timing: the radar pulse travels down and back, and half the round trip, multiplied by the speed of light, gives the distance. Subtract that distance from the satellite's known height and the sea-surface height follows. A slope in sea-surface height, as derived in Lesson 6, gives the geostrophic current. The method fails where the Coriolis parameter approaches zero, so near the equator the geostrophic conversion is not reliable, and the equator needs its own methods.

The Indian Ocean moored array, known as RAMA, is a set of buoys in the basin. It records surface meteorology, sea-surface temperature, conductivity and subsurface currents. The array was planned as 46 moorings, 38 at the surface and 8 below it. Reported figures give 22 buoys in place by the end of 2008 and 30 by July 2011, with full completion delayed by piracy off Somalia. The array is therefore partly built in the reported record, and its coverage should be stated with its date.

### Ocean heat content, in brief

Surface temperature can change within days, but the ocean stores heat in its depth. Heat content is the temperature summed over a depth range, weighted by water density and specific heat. A column that is warm to a few hundred metres holds far more heat than a warm surface film, and that stored heat changes slowly. This is why profiles matter: a profile from a float such as Argo gives the vertical sum that a surface sensor cannot. Whether such a store helps a seasonal forecast is a research question, and its value depends on the depth, the region and the season.

### Comparison: what each instrument can and cannot see

Argo gives depth and salinity profiles over broad areas, but it does not see the surface's detail in a single day. Altimetry gives the surface slope everywhere, but not the vertical structure. Moorings give a time series at a point, but not the basin. The three together are needed to test a hypothesis about the basin. No single instrument decides an Indian Ocean claim.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** the Indian Ocean has no permanent gyre, so its currents do not matter for climate.

**Reply:** the absence of a permanent gyre makes the monsoon currents more important to climate, not less. Their seasonal pattern links the ocean to the rainfall of the subcontinent, which Lesson 13 develops.

**Residual:** the exact depth and extent of Indian Ocean observing coverage in a given year depends on the state of the buoys and funding, and that status must be dated.

### Start with a current that changes direction

```text
NORTH INDIAN OCEAN, SEASON BY SEASON (schematic, not to scale)

 SUMMER MONSOON (roughly June to September)
   south-west winds blow across the basin
   -> SOUTH-WEST MONSOON DRIFT (surface flow across the northern basin)
   -> Somali current flows NORTH along East Africa; coastal upwelling off Somalia

 WINTER MONSOON (roughly December to February)
   north-east winds blow across the basin
   -> NORTH-EAST MONSOON DRIFT (surface flow reverses)
   -> the East African coastal flow turns SOUTH

 Same ocean, same place, opposite surface flow, set by the season of the wind.
```

Most ocean basins have a surface pattern that stays put. The north Indian Ocean does not. Its surface flow changes direction with the monsoon, and this is the reason it is taught apart from the Atlantic and Pacific.

### Why the wind sets the direction

Lesson 6 showed that wind stress and rotation produce a surface drift, and Lesson 12 showed that Asia closes the north. Put those together. In summer the south-west winds push the surface water across the basin. In winter the north-east winds push it back. The current follows the wind's season because the wind is the driver. There is no permanent loop in the north to hold a fixed direction.

### The doctrine: drifts and the coastal current

The north Indian Ocean carries two named drifts. The South-West Monsoon Drift runs during the summer monsoon and the North-East Monsoon Drift during the winter. Along East Africa, the Somali current flows northward in summer and turns southward in winter. The seasonal pattern is therefore a reversal of the boundary flow, with the basin's interior drift changing with it.

The south of the basin behaves differently. Lesson 12 showed that it keeps a more regular subtropical circulation, with the Agulhas leakage and the Indonesian throughflow. The contrast is the lesson's point: the south follows the gyre logic, and the north follows the monsoon logic.

### The mechanism, step by step

The argument has four steps. First, the monsoon winds reverse twice a year, because the land heats and cools more quickly than the sea. Second, the winds drive the surface drifts, through the same Ekman transport as before, so the drift follows the wind. Third, a reversal of the wind's direction reverses the drift, because there is no permanent gyre in the north to resist it. Fourth, the coastal wind along the Somali and Arabian coasts drives upwelling in summer, which cools the surface there, so the reversal also changes the sea-surface temperature pattern.

Step four is the link to Lesson 8. The south-west wind runs parallel to the Somali coast in summer, so the offshore Ekman transport lifts cold, nutrient-rich water. This is the seasonal upwelling that supports the summer fishery on the Somali and Arabian coasts.

### The monsoon link: a two-way coupling

The surface currents and the sea-surface temperature are linked to the monsoon, but the link is not a simple cause. The south-west winds cool the sea surface by upwelling, and they carry moisture across the basin. The sea-surface temperature pattern in turn helps the atmosphere decide where rain forms. The coupling runs in both directions, which is why a forecaster treats the ocean as a partner in the monsoon rather than as a passive background.

The honest statement is that the ocean is a modulator of the monsoon. It shapes the moisture supply and the surface temperature pattern, but the atmosphere's own dynamics and the monsoon's internal variability do much of the work. A claim that the currents alone decide the rainfall is too strong.

### A trap to avoid: the reversal is wind-driven

Some answers attribute the reversal to a feedback, such as a warm-water loop that keeps itself going. That is the wrong mechanism. Feedbacks amplify and sustain an anomaly, as Lesson 9 showed for the overturning. The seasonal reversal is produced by the reversing wind, and a feedback does not explain why the wind reverses. Use the wind first, then the feedback only as an amplifier if one is shown.

### Comparison: the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal

The two northern seas respond differently to the same monsoon. The Arabian Sea is more saline, through evaporation, and it upwells strongly on its western coast in summer. The Bay of Bengal is diluted by the Ganga-Brahmaputra discharge and heavy rain, and its upper layer is strongly stratified (Lesson 5). The Bay's stratification limits the nutrient supply that the Arabian Sea's upwelling provides. The same wind therefore gives a productive western sea and a more stratified eastern one.

### Objection and reply

**The strongest rival view:** if the wind drives the current so directly, the current is just a copy of the wind and tells us nothing new.

**Reply:** the current tells us where the water goes and what it carries, which the wind does not. The upwelling, the warm and cold water, and the nutrients are all set by the current's path, so the current is a second record of the same forcing.

**Residual:** the exact timing of the onset and withdrawal of the drifts varies from year to year, and that variability is not fully explained here.

- Mains: explain the reversal as a wind-driven response in a basin without a permanent northern gyre, and link it to the summer upwelling.
- Trap: attributing the reversal to a feedback, or describing the Indian Ocean's northern currents as a fixed gyre.
- Verified PYQ: the 2022 GS-I question on the forces that influence ocean currents and their role in fishing is taught across Lessons 6, 8 and 13. Its directive and marks are set out in the verified linkage section.

2. The South-West Monsoon Drift runs in summer, the North-East Monsoon Drift in winter.
3. The Somali current flows north in summer and turns south in winter.
4. The reversal is wind-driven, not a feedback.
5. The south keeps a more regular subtropical circulation.
6. Summer south-west winds upwell cold water off Somalia and the Arabian coast.
7. Upwelling is the link between the drift and the fishery.
8. The ocean and the monsoon are coupled in both directions.

### Start with the normal Pacific

```text
NORMAL (neutral) TROPICAL PACIFIC (schematic, not to scale)

  WEST (Indonesia)                                   EAST (off South America)
  warm pool, deep warm layer                         cool surface, shallow thermocline
  rising air, heavy rain                             sinking air, dry coast
          ^                                                  |
          |_______ surface easterly trade winds ____________ |
          |                                                  upwelling of cold water
          return flow aloft from east to west                |

  Trade winds push warm water west; cold water rises in the east to replace it.
  Rising air in the west and sinking air in the east close the loop: the Walker circulation.
```

Lesson 3 said that the equatorial thermocline is shallow in the east, and Lesson 8 said that upwelling in the east fails when that thermocline deepens. This lesson explains both. It starts from the normal state, because an anomaly is defined against it.

### A see-saw along the equator

The trade winds push the warm surface water to the west, so the west is warm and deep, and the east is cool because cold water rises to replace the water that the wind has moved. The air above the warm west rises, and the air above the cool east sinks. The rising and sinking air are joined by a return flow aloft. That east-west loop is the Walker circulation.

### The doctrine: the coupled feedback

The coupling is the heart of El Niño. Suppose the trade winds weaken. Less water is pushed west, so the warm pool spreads east, and the east becomes warmer. The east-west temperature contrast weakens, and the atmosphere responds with a weaker pressure and wind difference, so the trades weaken further. This is a positive feedback in the sense of Lesson 9: it amplifies a small start into a basin-scale anomaly.

The Pacific's El Niño (warm phase) and La Niña (cold phase) are the two states of this see-saw. The ocean and the atmosphere are two parts of one system, which is why the combined term El Niño-Southern Oscillation is used. The phases are named for the ocean's temperature, and the Southern Oscillation names the atmosphere's see-saw in pressure between the east and the west.

### The threshold, as commonly defined

El Niño and La Niña are monitored in a box in the central-eastern equatorial Pacific: 5 degrees north to 5 degrees south, and 120 degrees west to 170 degrees west. The commonly used threshold is a sustained sea-surface temperature anomaly of 0.5 degrees Celsius above normal for a warm event, or below normal for a cold one, measured in this box. The duration rule that turns a single warm month into an event is set by the monitoring agency and is not restated here.

### Why the thermocline deepens: waves on the equator

The equator is a special place. Near it, the Coriolis parameter goes to zero, so Ekman transport and geostrophy do not hold in the usual way, and the ocean responds through waves. Two wave types matter here. Kelvin waves travel eastward along the equator, confined close to it. Rossby waves travel westward, away from the equator.

The delayed oscillator is the simplest account of the Pacific's reversal, and it is taught here at an advanced but light level. When the trades weaken, a Kelvin wave carries a warm, deep-thermocline signal eastward along the equator. It arrives at South America and deepens the thermocline there, which is the upwelling failure of Lesson 8. Part of its energy reflects from the eastern boundary as Rossby waves that travel westward, and on reaching the western Pacific they reflect back as eastward Kelvin waves. Those later signals shallow the thermocline in the east and help the anomaly turn around. The time lag between the first and the return signal is the delay in the name.

The feedback in the previous section explains why the event grows. The delayed oscillator explains why it eventually turns. A student who uses the feedback alone to explain the reversal has used half the mechanism.

### Comparison: why El Niño is not the same as a cold phase

The two phases are not mirror images. El Niño weakens the Walker circulation and shifts the rising limb toward the central and eastern Pacific, which changes rainfall far from the equator. La Niña strengthens the Walker circulation and pushes the rising limb west. The difference matters for India, because the rainfall response is driven by where the rising air sits, not by the warmth of the ocean alone.

### The rainfall pattern

In the warm phase rainfall shifts eastward across the Pacific: the South American side tends toward flooding, while Indonesia and Australia tend toward drought. In the cold phase the normal pattern is reinforced, wetter in the west and drier in the east. These are the commonly described patterns; their size differs from event to event.

### India: a tendency, not a rule

El Niño years are on average associated with a weaker Indian summer monsoon, and La Niña years with a stronger one. The link is statistical. Strong monsoons have occurred in El Niño years, and weak ones in neutral years, because the Indian Ocean's own state, the Pacific warming's location, and the monsoon's internal variability all intervene. Lesson 15 adds the Indian Ocean Dipole as one of those modifiers.

The trap is the deterministic sentence that El Niño causes drought in India. El Niño shifts the probability of a weak monsoon. It does not decide it. The answer that names the probability, names the offsetting factors, and states the limit is the stronger answer.

### Objection and reply

**A reader will ask:** if El Niño is a Pacific event, it cannot matter for India.

**Reply:** the Walker circulation is a single equatorial loop that spans the Pacific, the Maritime Continent and the Indian Ocean's eastern side. Its rising and sinking limbs change the atmospheric circulation over the Indian subcontinent, so the link is through the atmosphere.

**Residual:** the size of the link varies from event to event, and the same Pacific state can give different monsoons when the Indian Ocean differs.

### UPSC application

- Prelims: the phases of ENSO, the Walker circulation's normal state and the threshold box.
- Mains: explain the coupled feedback, the reversal by the delayed oscillator, and the probabilistic link to the monsoon.
- Trap: writing that El Niño causes drought in India, or using the feedback alone to explain the reversal.

3. El Niño weakens the trades and spreads the warm pool east.
4. The Bjerknes-type feedback amplifies the anomaly.

The Indian Ocean Dipole is a see-saw inside one ocean. It is not El Niño in the Indian Ocean, and the two can reinforce or offset each other. The question for this lesson is how the see-saw develops and how it fits with the other modes.

### A tug of war inside one basin

The equatorial winds in the Indian Ocean can push warm surface water toward one side. If they push it west, the west warms and the east cools. The cooling in the east then changes the convection there, and the rising air moves. That pattern is the dipole. Its sign is set by which side is warm.

### The doctrine: definition and seasonal development

The index is the sea-surface temperature contrast between the western and eastern equatorial Indian Ocean. A positive IOD has a warmer west and a cooler east. A negative IOD has the reverse. The boxes used to define the index are set out in the technical documentation of the index and are not restated here, because the exact boundaries need a dated official reference before they can be quoted.

The dipole develops with the seasons. It typically begins to build during the middle of the year, peaks in the autumn, and decays by the end of the year. Its events commonly last about six months. The autumn peak is one reason the index matters for East Africa's short rains, which fall from October to December. This phase-locking to the seasons is the reason a dipole forecast is tied to a particular time of year.

### The mechanism: a Bjerknes-type feedback

The dipole grows by a feedback similar to the Pacific's. Easterly wind anomalies along the equator push warm water west. In the east the shallow thermocline lets cold water rise, so the east cools. The cooling strengthens the east-west contrast, which strengthens the easterly anomaly, which pushes more warm water west. The feedback is positive, so it amplifies the start. The seasonal decay comes from the seasonal cycle of the winds and the thermocline, and this is why the dipole ends by the year's close.

### The Indian link, with its limit

A positive IOD tends to favour rainfall over the western basin and over India, and it tends to bring drier conditions over Indonesia and Australia. A negative IOD tends toward the reverse. These are tendencies, and the monsoon forecast uses the IOD alongside ENSO. A positive IOD can offset the drying of an El Niño, and a negative IOD can compound it. Single-index reasoning fails for that reason.

### The other modes: different timescales

The ocean-atmosphere system has several modes, and each works on its own timescale. The table places the main ones.

| Mode | What it is | Timescale | Where its effect is felt |
|---|---|---|---|
| El Niño-Southern Oscillation | Coupled Pacific warm and cold phases, maximum warming in the eastern equatorial Pacific | Years | Global rainfall and temperature patterns |
| El Niño Modoki | Warming concentrated in the central equatorial Pacific, cooler water on both sides | Years | A different longitude of heating, so a different remote response |
| Indian Ocean Dipole | West minus east sea-surface temperature in the equatorial Indian Ocean | Seasons to a year | Indian and Indonesian rainfall |
| Madden-Julian Oscillation | An eastward pulse of enhanced convection, followed by suppressed convection | Weeks to a couple of months | Active and break spells within a monsoon season |
| Pacific Decadal Oscillation | A long-lived North Pacific sea-surface pattern alternating between phases | Decades | The background against which ENSO events occur |

The Madden-Julian Oscillation deserves a mechanism. It is a pulse of convection that moves eastward through the Indian Ocean and the Maritime Continent and then into the Pacific. Its convection heats the air and drives circulation that then triggers convection further east, so the pulse propagates. This is a simplified account of a coupled process that is still studied, given for its main logic. The MJO explains wet and dry spells inside a single monsoon season, which ENSO and the IOD cannot. El Niño Modoki is a different spatial pattern, not a weak El Niño: the heating sits in a different place, so the remote circulation and the rainfall response differ. The Pacific Decadal Oscillation sets the background state, and it does not replace the El Niño cycle.

The organising insight is timescale. Each mode works on a different clock, and they superimpose. A single index never predicts an outcome, and the honest statement is always probabilistic.

### Marine heatwaves: a general concept

A marine heatwave is a prolonged spell of unusually high sea-surface temperature relative to the region's own normal seasonal range. The commonly used definition, from a 2016 study, sets the threshold at the 90th percentile of a 30-year baseline and requires the anomaly to last at least five days. The baseline is local, so a warm day at one place can be normal at another.

The mechanism combines three parts: a warming background ocean, weak winds and reduced mixing that stop cooler subsurface water reaching the surface, and persistent high pressure that suppresses cloud and increases the sun's heating. An ENSO phase or a current anomaly can provide the initial heat. The consequences include coral bleaching, shifts in fish and plankton, losses of kelp and seagrass, and extra energy for tropical cyclones crossing the warm water. Any link between tropical Indian Ocean heatwaves and India's rainfall is an association to be tested against the record, not a rule, and an answer should say so.

### Coral risk in India's seas

Heat stress is the route by which marine heatwaves reach India's reefs. The reefs of Lakshadweep and the Gulf of Mannar are the systems that a persistent warm anomaly in the Indian Ocean would test first, because bleaching follows sustained heat over shallow reef water. The link is a risk, not a forecast: whether a given warm spell bleaches a given reef depends on its duration, its depth and whether the heat ends soon. The same warm anomaly that stresses reefs can also add energy to the cyclones that reach the coast, so reef and coastal risks can arise together.

### Ocean change: oxygen, acidity and heat together

Marine heatwaves are one of several changes now under way in the ocean. Three others act on the same food chain, and they reinforce one another.

Dead zones are stretches of water with very little dissolved oxygen, called hypoxia, where most animals cannot survive. The worst cases are anoxic, with no oxygen at all. The chain runs from nutrient runoff of fertiliser and sewage, to eutrophication, an algal bloom, the death of the algae, and decay by bacteria that consume oxygen. Coastal dead zones are usually shallow and seasonal. Stratification makes them worse, because a warm, light surface layer caps the colder water below, and warmer water holds less oxygen to begin with.

An oxygen minimum zone is a mid-depth layer of low oxygen that forms where productivity is high and the water is poorly ventilated. The Arabian Sea has one of the strongest tropical examples. Its driver is largely natural, but nutrient inputs and warming may intensify it. The natural and human causes should not be treated as mutually exclusive. Low oxygen removes fixed nitrogen through denitrification and anammox, which can release nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. It also favours Noctiluca, a hypoxia-tolerant organism whose green blooms can replace diatoms, with consequences for fisheries and the food chain.

Acidification happens because the ocean absorbs part of the carbon dioxide in the air. The gas reacts with sea water, lowers its pH and reduces the carbonate available to shell-forming organisms. Corals, molluscs and some plankton then find it harder to build calcium carbonate skeletons. Acidification is slow and global, while a heatwave is short and regional.

The point of this section is that heat, oxygen, acidity and nutrient supply are linked. A warmer, more stratified surface holds less oxygen and brings fewer nutrients up, so the changes compound. A claim about any one of them should state the mechanism and its direction, and should not rely on a remembered figure for pH, oxygen or catch.

### The answer on ocean-monsoon coupling

A question that asks about the ocean's role in the monsoon has a predictable structure. Decode the directive first: an "examine" asks for an evaluation, a "discuss" for an account with a limit, and a "critically" asks for an argument with an objection. Then build the answer in four blocks: the mechanism of the ocean, the monsoon link, the modes that modify it, and the probabilistic verdict. Each claim needs a named mechanism and a named case, and each number needs its date.

The strongest answer names the timescale of each mode. It shows that a seasonal dipole, an interannual Pacific cycle and an intraseasonal pulse act together, and it treats the forecast as a probability. It avoids the claim that any one index determines the rainfall.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** if the modes are so many, the ocean's role in the monsoon is too complicated to say anything useful.

**Reply:** the timescales are what make it useful. A seasonal forecast uses the IOD and ENSO, and an intraseasonal forecast uses the MJO. Separating the clocks lets each be used for its own question.

**Residual:** the interaction of the modes in a particular year is not fully predictable, and the verdict must remain probabilistic.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student says that the Indian Ocean Dipole and El Niño are the same phenomenon in two oceans. Correct the claim and explain how the two can reinforce or offset each other.

**Model answer:** The IOD is a separate coupled mode of the Indian Ocean, measured as the west-minus-east temperature contrast in that ocean. El Niño is a Pacific mode. They have different boxes and different mechanisms, although both use a positive feedback. A positive IOD can offset El Niño's drying tendency over India, and a negative IOD can compound it, so the two are separate modes that interact.

**Misconception to avoid:** a shared feedback does not make two modes the same. Identify the ocean and the box first.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Examine whether the Indian Ocean Dipole, the Madden-Julian Oscillation and El Niño can be used together to forecast a monsoon season. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer, 15 marks:** The question is an examination, so the answer should argue a graded position. The three modes work on different timescales: El Niño over years, the IOD over a season that builds in mid-year and peaks in autumn, and the MJO over weeks inside a season. Each has its own mechanism, a coupled feedback for the first two and an eastward convective pulse for the third. Used together, the seasonal indices set the probability of a season's tendency, and the MJO indicates the timing of wet and dry spells within it. The limit is that the interactions are not fully predictable, and a positive IOD can offset El Niño's drying while a negative one can compound it. The verdict is that the three modes give a probabilistic seasonal outlook and a within-season indicator, but not a deterministic forecast of any month's rainfall.

**Rubric (15 marks):** Three timescales distinguished, 3. Mechanism for each mode, 3. Joint seasonal use, 2. MJO for within-season timing, 2. Offset and compound interaction, 2. Probabilistic verdict with limit, 3.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks join lessons that the lesson-level checks did not connect.

### Cumulative check A - sinking, stability and the deep loop (Lessons 5 and 9)

**Question:** A warm, salty patch sits above a cold, fresh layer in an enclosed basin. Does it overturn, and what determines whether its overturn matters for the deep circuit?

**Model answer:** Stability depends on density increasing with depth. Warm over cold is unstable only if the salt makes the upper layer the denser one, and then the patch overturns locally. That local overturn is a small-scale process. The deep circuit is set by sinking in high latitudes, where cooling and evaporation make surface water dense enough to descend across a whole basin, so a local patch does not decide it.

**Misconception to avoid:** a local overturn is not the thermohaline circulation.

### Cumulative check B - western limbs across basins (Lessons 6, 7 and 13)

**Question:** The Gulf Stream is a steady western current, but the Indian Ocean's western limb changes with the monsoon. Explain the contrast using the closure of the gyre.

**Model answer:** The Atlantic's subtropical gyre closes through the subpolar circulation, so its western boundary current is steady. In the Indian Ocean, Asia closes the north, so the monsoon winds set the flow, and the western limb reverses with the season. The south keeps a steadier subtropical circulation with the Agulhas current.

**Misconception to avoid:** a seasonal current does not mean weak winds; the reversal is the wind's direct effect.

### Cumulative check C - tides and the observing system (Lessons 6, 10 and 12)

**Question:** Why must the tide be removed before satellite sea-surface height is used to infer a geostrophic current?

**Model answer:** The tide raises and lowers the sea surface on periods of about half a lunar day and a fortnight. Its height can be larger than the slope that a current produces, and an uncorrected tidal height would look like a current slope. The tide must therefore be modelled and removed before the slope is read as geostrophic flow.

**Misconception to avoid:** the tide is not a steady current; its oscillating height has to be removed before a current is inferred.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These tasks are new in type: a critique, a test that separates two explanations, and an evaluation of a proposition.

**Practice question (10 marks, 150 words):** A student writes: "The Indian Ocean has no permanent northern gyre, so the monsoon has no effect on its surface water." Critique the statement.

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The first half is correct: Asia closes the north, so no permanent gyre forms there. The second half does not follow. The monsoon winds reverse twice a year and drive the surface drifts through Ekman transport, so the monsoon has a strong effect on the surface water, even though the effect is seasonal and reverses. Summer south-west winds also upwell cold water along the western coasts, which changes the sea-surface temperature and the nutrient supply. The statement confuses a missing permanent pattern with a missing effect. A fair critique keeps the geometric point and rejects the inference.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Accepts the geometric premise, 2. Identifies the non-sequitur, 3. Monsoon drift and reversal explained, 2. Upwelling effect named, 2. Verdict stated, 1.

**Practice question (15 marks, 250 words):** Two reports on a coastal fishery collapse disagree: one blames a warm year, the other a change in the wind. Set out the test that separates the two explanations, state what each result would show, and give a verdict on how far the data settle the question.

**Model answer, 15 marks:** The two explanations predict different things. A warm-year explanation predicts a surface temperature anomaly with the wind unchanged. A wind explanation predicts a change in the alongshore wind and so in the offshore Ekman transport, with temperature following. The first test is the wind record in the weeks before the collapse, compared with the same season in earlier years. The second is the depth of the thermocline and the nutrient content of the surface water, which show whether the upwelled water changed. If the wind is normal and the thermocline has deepened, the deepening explanation gains weight. If the wind weakened and the thermocline did not change, the wind explanation gains weight. Both can occur together, and then each contributed in an unsettled proportion. The data settle the direction of the failure more firmly than its size, which needs longer records and the exclusion of other causes such as fishing effort.

**Rubric (15 marks):** Two predictions stated, 3. Wind test specified, 3. Thermocline and nutrient test, 3. Decision rule, 2. Combined case, 2. Verdict on size and other causes, 2.

**Practice question (20 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the proposition that India's coastal fisheries are more vulnerable to ocean warming and marine heatwaves than to changes in upwelling.

**Model answer, 20 marks:** The proposition has force but needs a qualified verdict. Warming and marine heatwaves act through heat stress, bleaching, shifts in species distribution and the energy available to cyclones, and a heatwave is defined against a local baseline, so its effect can be sharp within one season. Upwelling change works through the nutrient supply, and on the Indian west coast that supply is strongly seasonal, so a change in its timing can be as damaging as a change in its size. The strongest objection is that the two are linked: stratification from warming reduces the nutrient supply that upwelling brings, so the vulnerabilities cannot be ranked cleanly. The reply is to rank them by season and by species. Heatwaves are the more acute threat in the short term, and upwelling change the more persistent threat to the seasonal base of the food chain. The residual is that the size of either change in a given year is not settled, so the verdict stays conditional.

**Rubric (20 marks):** Heat mechanism, 3. Upwelling mechanism and seasonality, 3. Linkage through stratification, 3. Objection and reply, 3. Ranking by season and species, 3. Residual and conditional verdict, 3. Structure and clarity, 2.
