# Climate Classification: Koppen / India Climatic Regions - Mains-Focused Live Edition

A hot afternoon is weather. A place that is hot for most of the year, and dry in a predictable season, has a climate. Climate classification is the discipline of turning long-run temperature and rainfall into named classes that can be mapped, compared and used for planning. This topic follows that discipline in one direction: first the logic of the classes, then the codes, then India, then the rival schemes, and finally the question of why the lines on a map are not permanent.

The three ideas that run through all twelve lessons are these. A classification is a set of thresholds, not a description of cause. Every threshold uses a statistic that has been averaged over a standard period. And the place where a boundary falls depends on the statistic chosen, which is why different schemes give different maps for the same country.

Read the chart from the top. The single afternoon is a weather event. It enters a classification only after it has been averaged into a normal and compared with a threshold.

### Plain intuition: a single day cannot name a climate

Suppose a town records 40 C on one May afternoon. That tells you the atmosphere was hot on that day. It does not tell you that the town has a hot climate, because one day can be an unusual heat spell in an otherwise mild month. A climate is the pattern that comes back year after year: the usual range of temperature in each month and the usual amount of rain in each season.

That is why every classification works on normals. A normal is the average of a weather element over a standard period. The current World Meteorological Organization standard uses a 30-year period, and the period commonly cited for Indian stations is 1991 to 2020, not re-verified here. The period is a convention. It is chosen so that the average is stable enough to compare places, but recent enough to describe the present.

### The doctrine: a classification is a choice of measure

A classification is not a neutral picture of the world. It is a set of rules that decides which statistic matters. Three choices appear in almost every scheme:

1. **The temperature statistic.** Some schemes use the coldest month, some the warmest month, some the number of months above a line, and some the annual mean.
2. **The precipitation statistic.** Some use the annual total, some the rain in the wettest and driest months, and some the proportion of rain that falls in summer.
3. **The moisture statistic.** Some compare rainfall with evaporation or with potential evapotranspiration, which is the water a surface would lose if water were available.

Each choice draws a different line. A place can be on one side of a boundary under one measure and on the other side under another. This is the first reason why no climate map is the only correct map.

### Scale: the same climate, four different units

Map-scale terms matter here, and examinations often blur them. A **station normal** is the average from one observatory. A **grid cell** is the average over a square of the earth's surface that has been interpolated from many stations, so its value reflects network density as well as the place. A **climatic region** is a zone drawn on a map by a rule, and its edges are approximations. A **biome** is a vegetation and fauna unit, which may follow climate but also soils, fire and human use. An **agro-climatic zone** is a planning unit that combines climate with soil and cropping advice. Two of these can share a name and still cover different areas.

### Causal chain and its limits

```text
observed daily values
    -> monthly means
    -> normals over a standard period
    -> thresholds applied to the normals
    -> class assigned to a station or grid cell
    -> boundaries drawn between classes
```

The chain holds only if the normal is representative. An average can hide variability. Two places can share an annual rainfall total of 1,200 mm, yet one receives it in four monsoon months and the other spreads it through the year. The total is the same. The climate is not. That is the limit of any scheme that reads only one number.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If climate is the pattern, why not use the record of a single recent decade, which is more up to date?

**Reply:** A short record has a large sampling error. A few unusually wet or dry years can move a station across a threshold. A standard normal trades some recency for stability, and the comparison between places depends on that stability.

**Residual:** No normal period is perfect. A classification that changes its baseline changes its boundaries, and that matters for planning.

### India application

Take a station on the Deccan interior. A single monsoon failure can produce a dry year, and a newspaper will call the season a drought. A classification asks a different question: what is the usual rainfall, and how is it distributed across the year? The dry-season logic of Lesson 5 and the threshold logic of Lesson 4 both depend on this shift from the event to the normal.

Limit: the normal for a station with a short record, or for a mountain station with sparse observations, is less secure than the normal for a long-run urban observatory. Read the map's density of stations before reading its boundaries.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography and for the phenomena that shape it, including climatic regions. Examination answers in this area need the distinction between weather and climate stated plainly in the first sentence.

### Revision points

1. Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place and time; climate is the typical pattern over a long period.
2. A normal is an average over a standard period.
3. The current WMO normal period is 30 years; the Indian period commonly cited is 1991 to 2020 (not re-verified here).
4. Every classification chooses a temperature statistic, a rainfall statistic and, often, a moisture statistic.
5. A station normal, a grid cell, a climatic region, a biome and an agro-climatic zone are different units.
6. A grid cell reflects station density as well as the place.
7. Two places with the same annual total can have different climates because the seasonality differs.
8. A threshold applied to a normal can move when the normal moves.
9. A single extreme day is weather and is not used to name a class.

### Stop and predict

> **Misconception check.** A friend says a 40 C afternoon in May proves the station's climate has turned hot. What is the flaw?
>
> *Answer pointer:* One afternoon is weather. The class rests on the normal, and a single day moves the average only slightly.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Why must a climate map rest on long-period normals rather than on the record of a single season? Illustrate with one example of how the same annual rainfall can lead to different classes.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** A seasonal record describes what happened in one period, and a climate class must describe what usually happens. Normals average the weather over a standard period, so that a single extreme year does not decide a boundary. The average is not complete, however. Two stations can receive the same annual rainfall of about 1,200 mm, yet one may get almost all of it in the monsoon months while the other spreads it across the year. Their totals match, but their seasonal patterns differ, and a scheme that tests seasonality will class them differently. A map therefore needs both a stable normal and a seasonal statistic. The normal supplies stability; the seasonal test supplies the distinction that a total cannot give.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for defining a normal as a standard-period average; 2 for explaining why a single season is unstable; 2 for the example of equal totals with different seasonality; 2 for stating that seasonality needs a separate test; 1 for a clear conclusion on the need for both statistics; 1 for accurate use of climate and weather.

### Bridge to Lesson 2

Once a classification is built on normals, the next question is who chose the thresholds and on what basis. Lesson 2 takes up the scheme that set the tradition, and explains why its thresholds were tied to vegetation rather than to atmospheric causes.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two stations have the same mean annual temperature and the same annual rainfall. Why might a classification still assign them different codes, and what does this show about the reading of a single total?

**Model answer:** The codes depend on how rain is distributed through the year and on the temperature of the coldest and warmest months, not only on annual totals. A station with rain concentrated in one season and a dry season elsewhere can differ in code from a station with evenly spread rain, even when the totals match. This shows that a total alone cannot classify a climate, because the seasonal pattern carries information that the total omits.

**Misconception to avoid:** The belief that a classification reads the annual average alone. It reads seasonal distribution and monthly extremes as well, so two identical totals can receive different classes.

### Visual first: three ways to draw a climate boundary

```text
                 WHAT THE SCHEME USES AS INPUT
        +------------------+------------------+------------------+
        | Observed         | Water balance    | Air masses and   |
        | temperature and  | (rain against    | circulation      |
        | rain thresholds  | evaporation)     | (causes)         |

        | KOPPEN           | THORNTHWAITE     | GENETIC          |
        | empirical,       | moisture index   | causal schemes   |

        +------------------+------------------+------------------+
   Reading across: the first column records outcomes, the last records causes.
```

### Plain intuition: classify by what the plants can do

Imagine you must name the world's climates without a laboratory. You could look at where forests grow, where grasslands stop and where deserts begin, and then ask what temperature and rain those places receive. Koppen worked this way. He chose thresholds of temperature and rainfall that lined up with the limits of major vegetation types. A class is then a named range of conditions in which a particular kind of plant cover tends to appear.

The advantage is that the scheme can be applied to any station that has temperature and rain records. The cost is that the scheme records the result of climate without explaining its cause. Keep that trade-off in view throughout.

### The doctrine: an empirical threshold scheme

Köppen's scheme rests on three premises, and each can be questioned:

1. **Vegetation premise.** The boundaries of natural vegetation are a good proxy for the boundaries of climatic conditions that matter to life.
2. **Threshold premise.** The conditions can be captured by fixed numerical cut-offs in monthly temperature and precipitation.
3. **Letter premise.** The seasonality of rain and the warmth of the seasons can be coded in a short string of letters attached to a main group.

The premises explain why the scheme works as a map and why it fails as an explanation. Thresholds give reproducibility: two analysts with the same records reach the same class. They do not give causes: the scheme does not say why a monsoon arrives or why a mountain is cold.

### Editions and revisions

Köppen's scheme was not fixed in one publication. It was revised over several decades, and later revisions were made with other workers, including Geiger. The version used in most modern maps is therefore the product of more than one edition. This topic gives no precise date for each revision, because the dates available for this topic have not been confirmed against a primary text. What can be stated is that the scheme was revised and that modern maps use updated versions.

### Argument tree: why the scheme is still standard

```text
Claim: the scheme remains the usual reference
  |
  +-- Because it is reproducible from station data
  |     -> two analysts, same records, same class
  |
  +-- Because it is globally comparable
  |     -> the same code means the same kind of climate anywhere
  |
  +-- Because it maps easily
        -> a code can be drawn as a boundary on a map

Objection: it is not the most accurate scheme
Reply: accuracy and operational use are different tests; the scheme wins on the second
Residual: a causal account is still needed to explain the boundaries
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The scheme draws sharp lines across gradual gradients. A station a few kilometres on either side of a boundary is given a different code, though its climate is almost identical.

**Reply:** This is true, and it is the scheme's most serious practical limit. The reply is that a map is a summary, and the boundary is a choice made for comparison. The honest response is to read a boundary as a transition belt rather than as a wall.

**Second objection:** The same code can cover different air-mass regimes. A monsoon coast and a Mediterranean coast can both be warm and wet, yet owe their rain to different circulations.

**Reply:** Yes. That is why the scheme is complemented, not replaced, by causal explanations of circulation. The scheme tells you the outcome; the circulation lesson tells you the mechanism.

### India application

India contains a clear test of the vegetation premise. The windward slope of the Western Ghats and the leeward Deccan interior lie at about the same latitude. Their temperatures differ by much less than their rainfall does, and they are assigned different groups. The contrast is produced by relief and the direction of the moisture-bearing wind, not by latitude. A scheme built on rainfall thresholds captures this immediately, which is one reason why it is taught for India.

Limit: the contrast is large at the scale of the Ghats and the Deccan, but a single boundary through a foothill can separate two stations whose vegetation is nearly the same. Scale matters, and Lesson 12 returns to it.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography. A climate classification answer should state the scheme's basis in its first lines, then explain its strengths and limits.

### Revision points

1. Koppen's scheme is empirical: it uses observed temperature and precipitation thresholds.
2. It is vegetation-linked: its thresholds were chosen to track vegetation boundaries.
3. Its strength is reproducibility, comparability and map use.
4. Its weakness is that it records outcomes without explaining causes.
5. Its boundaries are sharp; natural transitions are gradual.
6. The same code can cover different circulation regimes.
7. The scheme was revised over several decades; modern maps use updated versions.
8. Thornthwaite's scheme uses a water balance; genetic schemes use air masses and circulation.
9. The three bases answer different questions and are complements, not rivals, in a complete account.

### Retrieve before the next lesson

**Map task.** On a sketch of India, the Western Ghats and the Deccan interior sit at about the same latitude. Name the factor that separates their rain, and the premise the contrast supports.

*Answer pointer:* Relief and wind direction; the contrast supports the threshold premise.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why Koppen's classification is called empirical, and state what that label gains and what it costs.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** An empirical classification is built from observed temperature and rainfall rather than from a theory of causes. Its thresholds are set so that the resulting classes match the limits of major vegetation, which gives the scheme a visible link to life on the ground. The gain is reproducibility: two analysts with the same station records reach the same code, and the codes can be compared across continents. The cost is explanation. The scheme records that a place is a monsoon or a desert but does not show why the monsoon reverses or why a desert forms. Its sharp boundaries also cross gradual natural transitions. A complete account therefore pairs the empirical map with a causal account of circulation.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for defining empirical as observation-based thresholds; 2 for the vegetation link; 2 for the gain in reproducibility and comparability; 2 for the cost of causal silence; 1 for the sharp-boundary limit; 1 for the complementary causal account.

### Bridge to Lesson 3

Having seen that the scheme works through thresholds, the next question is which thresholds it uses for temperature. Lesson 3 sets out the five temperature groups as a ladder, and it shows where the boundary between two of them is a matter of convention rather than of nature.

### Concept check

**Question:** A map shows a sharp line between a monsoon group and a dry group. Why is the line a convention, and how would you tell a student that it is not a physical wall?

**Model answer:** The line is the place where a station's rainfall crosses a stated threshold. A threshold is chosen so the class matches vegetation and comparison needs, so the line is a summary of a gradient. In reality rainfall changes gradually across a region, and a station a few kilometres either side of the line may have almost the same climate. The student should read the line as the centre of a transition belt.

**Misconception to avoid:** The belief that a boundary on a Koppen map records a physical discontinuity. The line is a cut-off applied to a gradual gradient.

### Visual first: the thermal ladder

```text
  warmest month  ->  top rung            coldest month  ->  reading
  ----------------------------------------------------------------

  coldest month >= 18 C                       ->  A  (no cool season)
  18 C > coldest month > 0 C (or -3 C)        ->  C  (mild winter)
  coldest month <= 0 C (or -3 C)              ->  D  (cold winter)
  warmest month < 10 C                        ->  E  (no warm season)

  Ladder reading from the top:
      18 C  -----  A / C boundary (coldest month)
       0 C  -----  C / D boundary (convention: 0 C or -3 C)
      10 C  -----  warm-month threshold for C and D
```

Read the ladder from the coldest month upward. The E test uses the warmest month, not the coldest.

### Plain intuition: the coldest month sets the winter, the warmest sets the summer

A place's climate in this scheme is judged by two extremes. The coldest month tells you how severe its winter is. The warmest month tells you whether it has a true summer at all. A tropical lowland never falls below 18 C in its coldest month, so it is A. A high plateau never rises above 10 C in its warmest month, so it is E. Between these extremes, the coldest month decides between C and D.

### The doctrine: four temperature groups

**Group A (tropical).** The coolest month is at or above 18 C. The climate has no cool season. Rainfall then decides the second letter.

**Group C (mild winter).** The coldest month is below 18 C but above the C/D line, and the warmest month is above 10 C.

**Group D (cold winter).** The coldest month is at or below the C/D line, and the warmest month is above 10 C.

**Group E (no warm season).** The warmest month is below 10 C.

**Group B (dry)** is a separate test, taught in Lesson 4. It is not a temperature group.

### Highland tracts and the H suffix

Some descriptions add a highland group, H, to Koppen's scheme. The usual description of the scheme has five main groups, A to E, and uses H only as a suffix in the dry group to mark high altitude; the letter n marks frequent fog. This topic follows the five-group form and notes that the two descriptions differ on H, without settling the question.

### Common codes in groups A and C

Af is the rainforest code, with no dry month. Cf is a warm-temperate code with no dry season. Cw is a warm-temperate code with a dry winter. These show the second letter at work in groups A and C, and Lessons 5 to 8 apply them to India.

### The C/D convention: state it, do not settle it

The C/D line appears in two forms in common use. The first sets the coldest-month line at -3 C, which some descriptions give as the original Koppen threshold. The second sets it at 0 C, which many modern maps use. The usual descriptions give both values and do not settle the choice.

This topic therefore does not assert that one value is correct. It shows both, and it tells you where the choice matters: for a station whose coldest month sits between -3 C and 0 C, the code changes between C and D depending on the convention. Any examination answer should state which convention is in use.

### Derivation: a station read in four steps

Take an illustrative station with monthly mean temperatures that are not real observations. Its coldest month is 8 C and its warmest month is 27 C.

1. Is the coldest month at or above 18 C? No, it is 8 C. Not A.
2. Is the warmest month below 10 C? No, it is 27 C. Not E.
3. Is the coldest month at or below the C/D line? With the 0 C convention, 8 C is above 0 C, so not D. With the -3 C convention it is also not D.
4. The remaining group is C.

The station is therefore C under both conventions. Now change only the coldest month to -1 C. Under the 0 C convention it becomes D. Under the -3 C convention it remains C. The number is the same, the code differs, and that is the convention at work.

The thermal ladder below reads the temperatures from the coldest month upward. It describes temperatures only, and it is not the decision order. The order used in this topic is: first the E test (warmest month below 10 C), then the dry test, then the A, C and D tests. The dry test comes before A, C and D because those tests assume a climate that is not dry. Some descriptions test the dry group first and others the E group first; this topic tests E first, and both place the dry test before A, C and D.

Worked check, a hot desert: mean annual temperature 28 C, 10 per cent of rain in summer, annual rain 150 mm, coldest month 22 C, warmest month 35 C. The E test fails, since 35 C is not below 10 C. The dry threshold is 20 x 28 + 0 = 560 mm; 150 mm is below 560 mm, and half the threshold is 280 mm, so the station is BW. Its mean annual temperature is above 18 C, so the code is BWh. If the A test were applied first, the coldest month of 22 C would wrongly class the station as A. That is why the dry test must come first.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
temperature of each month
   -> coldest month  (decides A, C or D)
   -> warmest month  (decides E, and the 10 C test for C and D)
   -> class
```

The chain uses only temperature, and it omits the cause of the temperature. A cold winter in a high plateau and a cold winter in a polar latitude both fall in group D or E, though one is a mountain and the other a polar sea. The group names the outcome, not the cause.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The 18 C line is arbitrary. Why should a tropical climate begin at that value, rather than at 17 or 19?

**Reply:** The threshold is a convention chosen to match where vegetation and the tropical character of the climate change. It is not a law of nature. The honest response is to treat it as a conventional cut-off that works well for comparison, and to say so.

**Residual:** A station near 18 C in its coldest month has an unstable code. A small change in the record moves it across the line.

### India application

Much of India's lowland has a coldest month above 18 C and falls in group A. The Ganga plain is the large lowland exception: its coldest month is below 18 C, so it is group C. The Ganga plain in January has a coldest month above zero but below 18 C, and so it falls in group C. Only the high Himalaya, where the coldest month falls below the C/D line and the warmest month stays low, reaches group D or E. The India-wide picture is therefore dominated by A and C at the lowland scale, with D and E confined to altitude. This contrast is developed in Lesson 8.

Limit: a single lowland station can have a cold January in an unusual year. A classification uses the normal, not one winter.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The temperature groups are the first thing a good answer must state, because every later code uses them. A question on the basis of a classification will ask for the thresholds, and a question on India's climates will ask which groups occur.

### Revision points

1. Group A: the coolest month is at or above 18 C.
2. Group E: the warmest month is below 10 C.
3. Group C and Group D are separated by the coldest month, relative to the C/D line.
4. The C/D line is either -3 C or 0 C, depending on the convention in use.
5. Group C and Group D both require the warmest month above 10 C.
6. Group B is a dryness test, not a temperature group.
7. The thermal ladder reads the coldest month for A, C and D, and the warmest month for E.
8. A station near a threshold has an unstable code.
9. Much of India's lowland is A; the Ganga plain is C; high altitude carries D and E.

### Check the map

**Predict, then check.** A station's coldest month is -4 C and its warmest is 22 C. Predict its group under the -3 C convention and under the 0 C convention.

*Answer pointer:* D under both. The convention changes the result only for coldest months between -3 C and 0 C.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Why is the boundary between Groups C and D a matter of convention, and what should an examination answer say about it?

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Groups C and D are separated by the coldest month. Written as a threshold, the line is set at -3 C in one convention and at 0 C in another, and the material used here gives both values without establishing that one is correct. A station whose coldest month lies between those values changes code with the convention, though its temperature has not changed. An examination answer should therefore name the convention it uses and explain that the line is a choice made for comparison. It should not present either value as a physical law. Stating the convention shows the examiner that the classification is understood as a tool and not as a fact of nature.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for identifying the coldest month as the separating test; 2 for stating both candidate values; 2 for the station example in which only the convention changes the code; 2 for the principle that the line is conventional; 1 for the advice to name the convention used; 1 for a clear, non-absolute conclusion.

### Bridge to Lesson 4

Groups A, C, D and E describe temperature alone. But a region can be warm and still be dry, and a temperature ladder cannot see dryness. Lesson 4 gives the rainfall test that identifies Group B, and shows why it depends on temperature as well as rain.

### Concept check

**Question:** A station has a coldest month of 12 C and a warmest month of 30 C. Which group does it fall in, and why does the coldest month alone not settle the answer?

**Model answer:** Group C. The coldest month is below 18 C and above the C/D line, and the warmest month is well above 10 C, so the station has a mild winter and a warm summer. The coldest month alone does not settle the answer because the test for Group A and the test for Group E use other conditions. The station is C only after both the 18 C test and the warm-month test have been applied, and after the dryness test has been passed.

**Misconception to avoid:** The belief that the coldest month alone decides every group. The warmest month and the dryness test also apply, and a station is not classed until they have been applied.

### Visual first: a balance, not a line

```text
  THE DRY TEST IS A COMPARISON

  rain in the year (mm)   ?   threshold (mm) = 20 x T + c
                              T = mean annual temperature (C)
                              c = 280, 140 or 0 (rain seasonality)

      P < threshold          -> dry (B)
      P < 50% of threshold   -> BW  (desert)
      50% <= P < threshold   -> BS  (steppe)
      P >= threshold         -> not dry; classify by the other groups

  Temperature raises the threshold: 20 mm more per degree C.
```

The diagram reads from the top. The threshold is not a fixed rainfall value. It rises with the temperature of the place, and that is the central idea of the group.

### Plain intuition: hot places need more rain to be non-dry

A dry climate is not a place with little rain in an absolute sense. It is a place where the rain available is smaller than what the air can take up as evaporation. A hot place evaporates more water than a cold one, so it needs more rain to avoid being dry. The threshold therefore grows with temperature.

This is why a temperature group cannot classify dryness, and why a rainfall cut-off cannot either. A cut-off of 500 mm would call a cold region dry that receives very little evaporation, and would miss a hot region that evaporates far more than 500 mm.

### The doctrine: the threshold and its seasonal term

The threshold is built in three steps.

1. Take the mean annual temperature T in degrees Celsius and multiply it by 20.
2. Add a seasonal term. Add 280 if 70 per cent or more of the annual rain falls in the summer half of the year. Add 140 if 30 to 70 per cent falls then. Add 0 if less than 30 per cent falls then. (The summer half is April to September in the northern hemisphere and October to March in the southern.)
3. The result is the threshold in millimetres of annual precipitation.

The dry tests are then:

- **B (dry):** annual rainfall P is below the threshold.
- **BW (desert):** P is below 50 per cent of the threshold.
- **BS (steppe):** P is at least 50 per cent of the threshold and below the threshold.

The rule below follows the published Koppen-Geiger arithmetic as summarised in the usual description of the scheme. A primary Koppen-Geiger text should be checked before the rule is quoted in an examination answer.

### Worked stations (illustrative figures, not observations)

**Station 1.** Mean annual temperature 26 C. Eighty per cent of the rain falls in the summer half.
- Threshold: 20 x 26 + 280 = 520 + 280 = 800 mm.
- BW: P below 400 mm. BS: P from 400 mm up to 800 mm. Not dry: P 800 mm or more.
- If P = 350 mm, the station is BW.
- If P = 600 mm, the station is BS.
- If P = 900 mm, the station is not dry.

**Station 2.** Mean annual temperature 10 C. Ten per cent of the rain falls in the summer half.
- Threshold: 20 x 10 + 0 = 200 mm.
- BW: P below 100 mm. BS: P from 100 mm up to 200 mm.
- Winter-wet places with a cool mean have a low threshold, so their dryness line is lower.

**Station 3, the warming test.** Keep the station 1 seasonality, but raise T from 26 C to 27 C.
- Threshold rises from 800 mm to 20 x 27 + 280 = 820 mm.
- A station with 810 mm, not dry at 26 C, becomes dry at 27 C.
- The same rainfall now falls inside the dry class. No rain has changed, and the class has moved.

### Limits of the rule

The rule depends on the mean annual temperature, which hides the seasonal range. It also depends on the seasonal split, which is defined by hemisphere. A station on the boundary of the 30 and 70 per cent ranges is sensitive to a small change in its record. The rule is also a classification of the normal, not of a single year, so a drought year in a wet station does not make it dry.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** This is needlessly complicated. Why not classify dry places by rainfall alone?

**Reply:** Because evaporation rises with heat. A rainfall-only test would classify a cold place with low rain as dry, although little water is lost there, and would miss a hot place where the same rain disappears quickly. The temperature term is what makes the test about water balance.

**Residual:** The three seasonal ranges are conventions. A station near 30 or 70 per cent of summer rain needs its record checked before the seasonal term is chosen.

### India application

The dry belt of India shows the threshold at work. Western Rajasthan and the Kachchh are hot, so their thresholds are high, and their rainfall is well below them. The Deccan interior is less hot, and its rain-shadow position keeps its rainfall low. Both can be dry, yet they are dry for different reasons, and their codes differ in the third letter and in the 50 per cent test. The codes are taken up in Lesson 7.

Limit: the examples above are illustrative. Station values must come from a station record, and the threshold must be computed from that record's own temperature and seasonal split.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography, and a dry-climate answer must show the water-balance logic. A question on desertification or on arid landforms belongs to the landform and ecology topics, and is not answered here.

### Revision points

1. A dry climate is defined by a balance between rain and evaporation, approximated by temperature.
2. The threshold is 20 times the mean annual temperature in degrees Celsius plus 280, 140 or 0 mm.
3. The seasonal term depends on the share of annual rain that falls in the summer half.
4. B is dry when annual rain is below the threshold.
5. BW is below 50 per cent of the threshold; BS is between 50 and 100 per cent.
6. Warming raises the threshold by 20 mm per degree Celsius, so the dry class can widen without any loss of rain.
7. Hot places need more rain to be non-dry.
8. B is a relation between rain and heat, not a temperature level.
9. The rule applies to a normal, not to a single year.

### Work one number

**Worked number.** A station has mean annual temperature 20 C, 75 per cent of its rain in the summer half, and 300 mm of rain a year. Compute the threshold, half the threshold, and the code.

*Answer pointer:* Threshold 680 mm; half is 340 mm; 300 mm is below 340 mm, so the code is BW (BWh in standard form).

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** A place warms by one degree Celsius while its rainfall stays the same. Explain, with the threshold rule, why its climate class can change.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Under the standard rule, a dry class is set by a threshold equal to 20 times the mean annual temperature plus a seasonal term. Warming by one degree raises that threshold by 20 millimetres, so the rain that once made the place non-dry is now below the line. The rainfall has not changed, but the water the air can take up has increased, and the rule measures dryness against that demand. A place near its threshold can therefore move from non-dry to dry, or from BS to BW, through temperature alone. This is why boundaries of dry classes are sensitive to warming and why a rainfall decline is not the only route to an expanding arid zone.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for stating the threshold as 20 times temperature plus a seasonal term; 2 for computing or describing the 20 mm rise per degree; 2 for explaining that the rain is unchanged but the demand is greater; 2 for the reclassification from non-dry to dry or BS to BW; 1 for the warning about boundary sensitivity; 1 for linking the logic to an expanding arid zone.

A dry station is now identified, but the code also needs its seasonal and temperature letters. Lesson 5 reads the full code letter by letter, and shows how the third letters h and k carry the temperature of the dry class.

### Concept check

**Question:** A station has a mean annual temperature of 15 C, a seasonal term of 0, and an annual rainfall of 300 mm. Is it dry? Show the threshold and the comparison.

**Model answer:** The threshold is 20 x 15 + 0 = 300 mm. The station is dry only if its rainfall is below 300 mm. Its rainfall equals the threshold, so it is not dry, and the dry tests do not apply. The comparison is strict: the station must fall below the line to be B.

**Misconception to avoid:** The belief that a station with rainfall equal to the threshold is dry. The test is a strict inequality, and a station exactly at the line is classed by the other groups.

### Visual first: a code is three slots

```text
   C     w     a
   |     |     |
   |     |     +-- THIRD LETTER: temperature of the warmest month,
   |     |         or of the year in the dry group (a, b, c, d; h, k)
   |     |
   |     +-------- SECOND LETTER: the seasonality of rain
   |               (f, m, w, s)
   |
   +-------------- MAIN GROUP: the temperature class from Lesson 3
                   (A, B, C, D, E)

   Cwa = C group  + dry winter  + hot summer
   Dfc = D group  + no dry season + cool short summer
   BWh = B group  + desert      + hot annual mean
```

Read every code from left to right. The main group comes first, the rain pattern second, and the temperature pattern third. The grammar is what lets one code describe three different facts.

### Plain intuition: the letters describe a year, not a place

A code is a short description of a typical year. The second letter asks when the rain falls: all year, in the summer, in the winter, or in a monsoon burst. The third letter asks how warm the year is. The main letter has already told you the broad temperature class. Learn the grammar and you can read any code, even one you have never seen.

### The second letters, defined

**f: no dry season.** Every month has enough rain that the climate does not dry out.

**m: monsoon.** The driest month is below 60 mm, but not below the monsoon line, which is 100 minus the annual rainfall in millimetres divided by 25. The rain is intense in one season, with a short dry spell.

**w: dry winter.** The wettest summer month receives at least ten times the rain of the driest winter month.

**s: dry summer.** The wettest winter month receives at least three times the rain of the driest summer month, and the driest summer month is below 40 mm.

The letter w here means dry winter. Students often read it as wet, because the same letter is a common shorthand for wetness in other contexts. In this grammar, w is the dry season, and s is the dry summer.

### Derivation: reading two codes from monthly rain

Take an illustrative station whose wettest summer month receives 300 mm and whose driest winter month receives 25 mm.
- Ten times 25 mm is 250 mm. The wettest summer month, 300 mm, is at least 250 mm.
- The second letter is **w**: a dry winter.

Now take a second illustrative station whose wettest winter month receives 120 mm and whose driest summer month receives 30 mm.
- Three times 30 mm is 90 mm. The wettest winter month, 120 mm, is at least 90 mm.
- The driest summer month, 30 mm, is below 40 mm.
- The second letter is **s**: a dry summer.

Both examples are illustrative figures, not observations. The rule is the thing to learn.

### The third letters, defined

**a:** the warmest month is at least 22 C. This marks a hot summer.

**b:** no month is above 22 C, and at least four months are above 10 C. This marks a warm summer that is not as hot as an a summer.

**c:** only one to three months are above 10 C. This marks a short cool summer.

**d:** the coldest month is below -38 C, and one to three months are above 10 C. This marks a very severe winter.

For the dry group, the third letters carry the annual temperature instead:

**h:** the mean annual temperature is above 18 C. This is a hot dry climate, as in BWh and BSh.

**k:** the mean annual temperature is below 18 C. This is a cold dry climate, as in BWk and BSk.

A caution about the h and k definitions. Some older textbook tables print them the other way round. The definition used here, h for an annual mean above 18 C, is the standard one, and it is the reading the Indian codes in the next lessons assume.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
rain by month  ->  second letter (when the rain falls)
temperature by month  ->  third letter (how warm the year or summer is)
main group (Lesson 3)  ->  the broad temperature class
combined  ->  a code that describes a typical year
```

The grammar describes seasonality and temperature. It does not describe the total amount of rain beyond the dry tests, and it does not describe the cause of the rain. A code tells you that the dry season is in winter, not why the monsoon retreats in winter.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The codes are too dense. A student should learn the names of the climates, not a grammar.

**Reply:** The names hide the reasoning. The grammar shows which statistic produced each name, so a student can check a code against data and can see why two places with the same name may differ. The grammar is the content.

**Residual:** The letters are conventions. Two schemes can use the same letter for slightly different tests, so the test must be stated, not assumed.

### India application

Indian codes use several second letters. The west coast and the north-east receive rain in every month of the monsoon and have no dry season of the usual kind, which the grammar records as f or m. The peninsular interior has a clear dry winter, which the grammar records as w. The grammar explains why one country can contain so many codes: the seasonality changes sharply over a short distance. Those contrasts are taken up in Lessons 6 and 7.

Limit: the test values depend on the station record. A code assigned from a single year, or from a station with a short record, may change when the normal is recomputed.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography, and a code is the shortest form of that description. A question that asks you to classify a region wants the main group, the second letter and the third letter stated in that order, with the test used for each.

### Revision points

1. A code has three slots: main group, second letter and third letter.
2. The second letter describes when the rain falls.
3. f means no dry season; m means a monsoon with a short dry spell.
4. w means a dry winter: the wettest summer month has at least ten times the driest winter month.
5. s means a dry summer: the wettest winter month has at least three times the driest summer month, and the driest summer month is below 40 mm.
6. The third letters a, b, c and d describe summer warmth and winter severity.
7. In the dry group, h and k describe whether the annual mean is above or below 18 C.
8. w is dry winter, not wet.
9. The grammar describes seasonality and temperature, not cause.

**Complete the sentence.** A station has its second letter s when its wettest winter month is at least ___ times its driest summer month, and its driest summer month is below ___ mm.

*Answer pointer:* three; 40.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain how the letters of a Koppen code encode seasonality and temperature, and show why the letter w cannot be read as wetness.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** A Koppen code has three slots. The main letter gives the broad temperature class. The second letter gives the seasonal rain pattern: f for no dry season, m for a monsoon with a short dry spell, w for a dry winter, and s for a dry summer. The third letter gives the temperature pattern, with a, b, c and d describing summer warmth and winter severity, and h and k describing the annual mean in the dry group. The letter w means dry winter because it is defined by the ratio between the wettest summer month and the driest winter month. A reader who takes w as wet will misread the climate of every Indian peninsular station and will confuse it with the monsoon coast, which has a different code.

A student who reads s as wet would expect summer rain and would place the climate on the wrong seasonal side.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading the second letter as a wetness grade rather than as the season in which the dry period falls.

### Plain intuition: the main letter is shared, the seasons are not

Every station here has a coolest month at or above 18 C, so every one is in Group A. The main letter cannot separate them. The second letter does, and it turns on two tests: the monsoon line, which decides whether a place has a monsoon pattern, and the dry test, which decides whether the place is dry at all.

### The monsoon line: the m test, worked

The m test has two parts. The driest month must be below 60 mm, and it must be at least the monsoon line, which is 100 minus the annual rainfall in millimetres divided by 25. The line falls as rain rises. Near 1,000 mm the line is 60 mm, and at or below 1,000 mm no driest month can satisfy both parts, so the m test is closed there.

Worked station W (illustrative): annual rain 3,000 mm; driest month 40 mm.
- Monsoon line = 100 - 3,000 / 25 = 100 - 120 = -20 mm.
- The driest month, 40 mm, is below 60 mm and at least -20 mm. The m test passes: **Am**.

Worked station D1 (illustrative): annual rain 1,000 mm; driest month 5 mm; wettest summer month 240 mm.
- Monsoon line = 100 - 40 = 60 mm. The driest month would have to be at least 60 mm and below 60 mm. No value does that, so the m test cannot pass.
- The w test applies. The wettest summer month, 240 mm, is at least ten times the driest winter month, 5 mm (50 mm). The second letter is **w**, and the code is **Aw**.

These figures are illustrative. The rule is what carries into any station record.

### The dry test decides the leeward plateau

Take a leeward plateau station with mean annual temperature 26 C and 80 per cent of its rain in the summer half. The dry threshold is 20 x 26 + 280 = 800 mm (Lesson 4).
- Station D1, with 1,000 mm, is above the threshold. It is not dry, so it is Aw, as worked above.
- Station D2 (illustrative), with 600 mm, is below the threshold and above half of it (400 mm). It is dry, classed BS, and labelled BShw in Indian usage (Lesson 7).

One leeward plateau can therefore carry Aw in one place and BShw in another. The dry test, not the leeward position alone, decides. Lesson 7, the register and the coverage matrix apply the same rule.

### Am and Amw: a labelling difference, stated

Two treatments label the monsoon tracts differently. One places Am on the windward Western Ghats and the far north-east. Another uses Amw, with a w for a dry-winter note, for the west coast. Both are in the monsoon family, and the m test decides the monsoon code. This topic uses Am for the monsoon tracts and treats Amw as an optional Indian label.

### Af and the terminology control

Af is the rainforest code: the driest month is at least 60 mm, so there is no dry month. Whether any Indian tract qualifies as Af is taken up in the equatorial topic. Af is not a label for the monsoon coast, which has a short dry season or a dry winter. Keep Af, Am and Aw separate in every answer.

### The east coast label

The label As is used for the Tamil Nadu and Andhra coast. Its rain comes mostly in winter from the retreating north-east monsoon, a pattern the standard s test does not express cleanly. As is therefore an Indian convention, and an answer should say so.

### Causal chain and land use

```text
south-west monsoon wind from the sea
  -> rises over the Western Ghats: heavy rain on the windward slope (Am)
  -> dry air descends on the leeward plateau (Aw, or BShw where dry)
  -> north-east monsoon brings winter rain to the east coast (As label)
```

The land use follows the code. Am areas carry evergreen and semi-evergreen forest, plantation crops and heavy leaching, with laterite soils. Aw areas carry rain-fed cereals and millets, and their rain varies widely from year to year.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Am and Aw are both tropical, so one label would be simpler.

**Reply:** They differ in total and in seasonal pattern. A single label would hide the difference between a coast that is wet for most of the year and a plateau that dries sharply in winter.

**Residual:** The Indian labels for the coasts do not all match the standard symbols, and the monsoon-line test is sensitive to the normal chosen.

### Visual first: the rain shadow and the dry test

  moist air  ---->  Western Ghats  ---> Deccan interior  ---------> Thar and Kachchh
  rain on the       air descends,       dry test decides:             dry test:

### Plain intuition: why the lee is dry

Air that rises over a barrier loses much of its moisture on the windward slope. On the far side it descends. Descending air warms and its relative humidity falls, so it takes up water rather than releasing it. The leeward interior is therefore dry although it lies in the monsoon zone.

### The doctrine: the dry labels

B is the dry group, and the dry test from Lesson 4 applies. BShw is the semi-arid steppe: annual rain at least half the threshold and below the threshold. BWhw is the hot desert: annual rain below half the threshold. The h means mean annual temperature above 18 C. The trailing w in these Indian labels marks the winter-dry pattern; the standard symbols are BSh and BWh.

The land use follows the class. Drought-prone dryland farming and pastoralism go with the steppe. Irrigated pockets, with a high risk of salinisation, go with the desert.

### Worked stations (illustrative)

Take a leeward station with mean annual temperature 26 C and 80 per cent of its rain in the summer half. The threshold is 800 mm and half of it is 400 mm.
- 1,000 mm: above the threshold, not dry: Aw (Lesson 6).
- 600 mm: below the threshold and at least 400 mm: BS, labelled BShw.
- 250 mm: below 400 mm: BW, labelled BWhw.

The rain figures are illustrative. A station record decides the actual class.

### Limits

The thresholds come from the normal, so a station whose rain sits near 400 mm or 800 mm is sensitive to its record. The code describes the normal and not any single drought year.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** A dry place is simply a place with little rain. Why bring in temperature at all?

**Reply:** A hot place evaporates more, so it needs more rain to avoid being dry. Two places with the same rainfall can differ in dryness if their temperatures differ, and the threshold makes the comparison fair.

**Residual:** A station near half its threshold sits on the BW and BS boundary, and a small change in its record changes its code.

### India application: drought and planning

A drought year in a steppe station does not change its code, because the code describes the normal. A sustained fall in the normal, or a rise in temperature that lifts the threshold, is what moves a boundary. Lesson 12 returns to that distinction.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the salient features of India's physical geography. A dry-climate answer names the threshold test and then the cause of the dryness.

### Revision points

1. A rain shadow forms on the lee of a barrier when moist air loses its rain on the windward slope.
2. The dry test compares annual rain with 20 times the mean annual temperature plus 280, 140 or 0 mm.
3. BShw is annual rain between half the threshold and the threshold; BWhw is below half.
4. The trailing w in BShw and BWhw is an Indian label; the standard symbols are BSh and BWh.
5. A drought year does not change the code; a shifted normal or a higher threshold does.
6. The rain-shadow contrast is a relief effect, not a latitude effect.
7. Steppe and desert are a difference of degree in rain against the threshold.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain how a rain shadow can leave the Deccan interior dry while the same monsoon wets the coast, and show how the dry test separates steppe from desert.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Monsoon air rising over the Western Ghats loses most of its moisture on the windward slope. Descending on the leeward side, it warms and its relative humidity falls, so it takes up water rather than releasing it, and the plateau becomes dry. The dry test compares annual rain with a threshold of 20 times the mean annual temperature plus a seasonal term. Rain below the threshold makes the place dry; rain at least half the threshold makes it steppe, and rain below half makes it desert. At 26 C with 80 per cent summer rain the threshold is 800 mm, so 600 mm is steppe and 250 mm is desert. The code records the rainfall outcome; the rain shadow explains it.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the uplift and loss of rain on the windward slope; 2 for the descent, warming and falling humidity on the lee; 2 for the threshold as the classifying test; 2 for the half-threshold split; 1 for the worked numbers; 1 for the statement that the code omits the cause.

### Visual first: altitude stacks the codes

```text
  ALTITUDE (rising)                         CODE (commonly used)
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
  Ganga plain                               C: Cwa (standard); Cwg (Indian label)
  Hills and foothills                       C or D, by the coldest month
  High Himalaya, cold belts                 D: coldest month at or below the line
  Highest tracts, warmest month 0-10 C      ET (tundra)
  Highest tracts, all months below 0 C      EF (ice cap)
  --------------------------------------------------------------------

```

### Plain intuition: altitude does what latitude cannot

Air cools as it rises, by roughly 6 to 7 C per kilometre. A place at the foot of the Himalaya and a place a few hundred kilometres north can share a latitude band, yet the one two kilometres higher is much colder. Altitude is therefore the main control on India's cold codes, and the lowland scheme cannot be carried into the mountains unchanged.

### The Ganga plain: Cwa and Cwg

The Ganga plain has a monsoon summer, a cool dry winter, and a coldest month above the C/D line. Its standard code is Cwa, a humid subtropical climate with a dry winter. Cwg is an Indian label in which the g marks the Ganges-type annual pattern. This topic uses Cwa as the code and states Cwg as the label in use. No winter temperature is given here for the plain, and the code stands without one.

### Tundra and ice cap: ET and EF

Both codes belong to the E group, whose warmest month is below 10 C. ET (tundra) applies where the warmest month lies between 0 and 10 C. EF (ice cap) applies where all twelve months are below 0 C. A station whose warmest month is 8 C is ET; a station whose warmest month is -1 C is EF. Indian treatments place the high Himalaya and its cold belts in this family and say that the code varies by map convention, so this topic does not assign a numeric range to E in India.

### Dfc

Dfc is a cold humid code in the D group: no dry season, and a coldest month at or below the C/D line. Its use in India is not established here, and foothill stations rarely reach that line. This topic therefore does not assign Dfc to India.

### Worked altitude example (illustrative)

Suppose a station at sea level has a coldest month of 16 C. A station two kilometres higher on the same slope, at a lapse rate of 6.5 C per kilometre, is about 13 C colder, so its coldest month is about 3 C. That is above the C/D line under both conventions, so it stays C. A station four kilometres higher is about -10 C in its coldest month, which is below both lines, so it is D.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
rising altitude
  -> cooler monthly means (lapse rate)
  -> coldest month crosses the C/D line
  -> warmest month low enough for E (ET, or EF where all months are below 0 C)
```

The chain uses averages. A sheltered valley can be warmer than a ridge at the same height, and slope aspect and rain shift the local lapse.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Why not draw the Himalaya as one cold zone?

**Reply:** The altitude bands differ in forest, horticulture and pasture, and planning uses them separately. Lesson 9 shows another scheme that merges the highlands into one group.

**Residual:** The bands are not fixed lines on the ground, and their edges depend on local lapse rates.

### India application

The Ganga plain carries the rice-wheat double-cropping core, and its dry winter is what makes a rabi crop possible. The Himalaya is India's clearest case of climate changing over a few tens of kilometres. The codes describe normals, so a high station with a short record may have a normal that is not yet stable.

The syllabus asks for the salient features of the world's physical geography and for India's distinctive regions. An answer on the Himalaya gives the altitudinal stack, names the lapse-rate mechanism, and states the code for each band.

### Revision points

1. Altitude controls India's cold codes more than latitude does.
2. The environmental lapse rate is roughly 6 to 7 C per kilometre.
3. The Ganga plain is Cwa in the standard set; Cwg is an Indian label.
4. The g in Cwg marks the Ganges-type pattern; it is a convention.
5. ET is a warmest month of 0 to 10 C; EF is all months below 0 C.

**Practice prompt:** Explain why altitude, not latitude, controls the cold codes of the Himalaya, and why one cold class would hide planning differences.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Air cools as it rises, at roughly 6 to 7 C per kilometre, so a place two kilometres higher can be far colder than one at the same latitude on the plain. The coldest month decides between the C and D groups, and it crosses the C/D line at altitude. The warmest month decides the E group, and at the highest elevations it falls below 10 C, giving ET where the warmest month is between 0 and 10 C and EF where all months are below 0 C. ### Visual first: twelve months, counted

```text
  A MID-LATITUDE STATION, MONTH BY MONTH (illustrative)
  month:    J F M A M J J A S O N D
  >= 10 C:  . . . * * * * * * * . .     count of months at or above 10 C = 7

  TREWARTHA MID-LATITUDE RULE (count of such months)
    8 or more months  -> C  (subtropical)
    4 to 7 months     -> D  (temperate)
    1 to 3 months     -> E  (boreal)
```

Each mark records a month at or above 10 C. The scheme asks how many months carry that warmth, not what the coldest month is.

### Plain intuition: how long is the growing season?

Koppen asks how cold the coldest month is. Trewartha asks a different question: how many months are warm enough for growth? Two places can share a coldest month and differ sharply in the length of their warm season. A crop, a forest or a grassland responds to the length of the warm season as much as to the depth of winter. The count captures that length directly.

### The doctrine: a modification of Koppen

Glenn Trewartha first published his scheme in 1966. He described it as a modification of Koppen's system. Its main change is in the middle latitudes. Tropical and polar climates stay close to Koppen's, but the middle latitudes are split by the count of months at or above 10 C:

- **C (subtropical):** 8 or more months at or above 10 C.
- **D (temperate):** 4 to 7 months.
- **E (boreal):** 1 to 3 months.

The tropical group is A, the dry group is B, the polar group is F. The group letters differ from Koppen's: in Trewartha, E is boreal and F is polar. Mixing the two alphabets is a common error. A student who writes E for polar is describing Koppen's scheme while naming Trewartha's letters.

### Version difference: the H group

Some descriptions describe Trewartha's scheme as including a highland group, H. The usual description used here lists six groups, A to F, with no H, and gives 1966 as the first publication. This topic follows the six-group form and treats highland tracts in words.

### Derivation: the same region in two schemes

Take two illustrative stations, each with a coldest month and a count of warm months.

- **Station P:** coldest month -5 C, nine months at or above 10 C. Under Koppen, -5 C is below both convention lines, so the station is D under either convention. Under Trewartha's rule, nine months make it C. The schemes disagree under both conventions.
- **Station Q:** coldest month 1 C, six months at or above 10 C. Under Koppen's coldest-month test it is C, since 1 C is above the line. Under Trewartha it is D, because six months falls in the 4 to 7 range.

The disagreement is in the method. Koppen reads the winter, Trewartha reads the length of the warm season. Neither is an error; each answers a different question.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
monthly temperatures
  -> count of months at or above 10 C
  -> range (8 or more, 4 to 7, 1 to 3)
  -> middle-latitude group (C, D or E)
```

The count is sensitive to the 10 C line. A station with a warm season that sits just under 10 C for most months changes group with a small warming. The count also ignores how far above 10 C the warm months are.

### Omitted: the dry test

This topic does not use Trewartha's dry test. The printed form of it in common descriptions is garbled, so the test is omitted rather than reconstructed. Lesson 4 gives the Koppen dry test, which is fully specified.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Counting months is cruder than reading the coldest month. A scheme that uses a count throws away information.

**Reply:** It throws away the depth of winter, but it keeps the length of growth. For agriculture and for vegetation, the length of the warm season is often the more useful number. Each scheme keeps a different piece of information.

**Residual:** The count's threshold of 10 C is itself a convention, and two stations near it can be classed differently.

### India application: the same city, two codes

A code belongs to a scheme. The same city can carry different letters in two schemes, so a city's name is not itself a code.


### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for India's distinctive climatic regions and for the comparison of approaches. A comparison answer should name the axis of each scheme: Koppen on the coldest month and the rain pattern, Trewartha on the count of warm months, and Thornthwaite on the water balance (Lesson 10).

### Revision points

1. Glenn Trewartha first published his scheme in 1966, as a modification of Koppen's.
2. Trewartha's middle-latitude test counts months at or above 10 C.
3. C has 8 or more such months; D has 4 to 7; E has 1 to 3.
4. In Trewartha, E is boreal and F is polar; in Koppen, E is polar. Do not mix the letters.
5. Some descriptions add an H highland group; the usual description has no H.
6. The count gives the length of the growing season; the coldest month gives the depth of winter.
7. The count depends on the 10 C line, so stations near it are unstable.
8. Trewartha's dry test is omitted in this topic.

### Spot the error

| Monthly row for one station | Coldest 3 C; months at or above 10 C: 5 |
|---|---|
| Question | Give the Koppen group and the Trewartha group. |

*Answer pointer:* Koppen C (3 C is above the line); Trewartha D (5 months falls in the 4 to 7 range).

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Compare Koppen's and Trewartha's treatment of the middle latitudes, and say what each gains by its choice of statistic.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Both schemes classify middle-latitude climates, but they read different features. Koppen separates groups by the coldest month, relative to a convention line near 0 C or -3 C, and by the warmest month. Trewartha counts the months at or above 10 C and assigns C, D or E according to whether there are 8 or more, 4 to 7, or 1 to 3. Koppen's method reads the depth of winter; Trewartha's reads the length of the warm season, which governs growth for crops and forests. Stations near the 10 C line are unstable under Trewartha, and stations near the convention line are unstable under Koppen. A comparison that names the axis of each scheme shows why the same station can carry different groups without either scheme being wrong.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for Koppen's coldest-month axis; 2 for Trewartha's count axis; 2 for the range boundaries (8 or more; 4 to 7; 1 to 3); 2 for the gain each scheme offers; 1 for the instability near each threshold; 1 for the conclusion that the disagreement is about the question asked.

### Bridge to Lesson 10

**Question:** Why can two schemes disagree about the group of a mid-latitude station without either being mistaken? Name the feature each scheme reads.

**Model answer:** Each scheme reads a different feature of the same record. Koppen reads the coldest month against a convention line and the warmest month against 10 C. Trewartha reads the number of months at or above 10 C. The schemes answer different questions, so they can assign different groups to the same station without either being wrong for its own purpose.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that one scheme's group is the true group and the other's is an error. A group is a result of a method, and two methods can give two valid results.

## Lesson 10 - Thornthwaite: the 1931 P/E index and the 1948 moisture index

CA found: none verified (no search run, 24 October 2026)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: two versions, two bases

```text
  THORNTHWAITE 1931                      THORNTHWAITE 1948
  basis: vegetation-linked indices       basis: water balance
  +---------------------------------+    +------------------------------------+
  | P/E ratio, summed over 12 months|    | PET: water the surface could lose  |
  | humidity provinces A to E       |    | D = deficit, S = surplus           |
  | seasonal letters r, s, w, d     |    | Ia = D / PET x 100                 |
  | temperature efficiency A' to F' |    | Ih = S / PET x 100                 |
  +---------------------------------+    | Im = Ih - 0.6 x Ia                 |
                                         +------------------------------------+
  Name the version before the basis: "moisture-index based" is true of 1948.
```

The two versions share a name and a family. They differ in their bases, so the version must be named before any claim about the basis.

### Plain intuition: rain is only useful if it is not lost again

Rain counts for a plant only if the air does not take it back. Evaporation and transpiration remove water from the soil and leaves, and the amount removed depends on heat and wind. A place with a large rainfall total can still be short of usable water if the air's demand is high. Thornthwaite's schemes measure this balance, rather than rainfall alone.

### The 1931 scheme: P/E and temperature efficiency

In 1931 Thornthwaite compared each month's precipitation with the evaporation of that month, as a ratio P/E. The P/E index is the sum of the twelve monthly ratios, not a single annual ratio. A high index means a wet place; a low index means a dry one.

The humidity provinces use approximate boundaries. A is the wettest, with an index of about 128 or more; B is humid, about 64 to 128; C is subhumid, about 32 to 64; D is semi-arid, about 16 to 32; and E is arid, under about 16. Printings differ slightly at the boundaries. Seasonal letters attach to the province: r for adequate rain in every season, s for deficiency in summer, w for deficiency in winter, and d for deficiency in every season.

The temperature efficiency index is a separate sum, built from the monthly temperatures, and it gives the thermal provinces A' (tropical) to F' (frost). Its boundary values are also approximate in the description used.

### The 1948 scheme: potential evapotranspiration and the moisture index

In 1948 Thornthwaite replaced the vegetation-linked basis with a water balance. He used potential evapotranspiration, PET, which is the water a surface would lose if it were supplied with all the water it could use. Each station then has a deficit D, the water short of PET, and a surplus S, the water in excess of it.

The moisture index is built from two percentages:

- Ia = (D / PET) x 100, the aridity index.
- Ih = (S / PET) x 100, the humidity index.
- Im = Ih - 0.6 x Ia, the moisture index.

The 0.6 weight reflects that a surplus stored in the soil can be used later to meet a deficit. It is a convention of the scheme and is stated as given.

Ranges of Im then give the moisture types. The humid range runs from about 20 to 100, the subhumid from about -20 to 20, the semi-arid from about -40 to -20, and the arid from about -60 to -40. Range boundaries differ slightly across printings and are stated approximately here.

### Worked examples (illustrative figures)

**Station 1, a dry season.** PET is 1,000 mm and the deficit D is 300 mm; there is no surplus, so S = 0.
- Ia = 300 / 1,000 x 100 = 30.
- Ih = 0 / 1,000 x 100 = 0.
- Im = 0 - 0.6 x 30 = -18.
- A value of -18 falls in the subhumid-dry range, from -20 to 0.

**Station 2, a wet season.** PET is 800 mm, the deficit is 0, and the surplus S is 240 mm.
- Ia = 0.
- Ih = 240 / 800 x 100 = 30.
- Im = 30 - 0 = 30.
- A value of 30 falls in the humid range, B1.

The two stations differ only in their balance of water, and the moisture index separates them as the 1931 P/E index could not do as cleanly.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
temperature and daylight  ->  PET (the demand for water)
rain against PET          ->  deficit D and surplus S
D and S                   ->  Ia, Ih and Im
Im                        ->  moisture range
```

The chain depends on estimating PET. The estimate depends on the temperature model used, so two estimates can differ. The index is therefore only as good as the PET it is built on. The scheme also describes an average year, not the variability of the rain.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Thornthwaite's PET is an estimate, not a measurement. A scheme built on an estimate is no more objective than one built on temperature thresholds.

**Reply:** That is a fair limit. The reply is that the estimate uses the physical demand for water, which the temperature thresholds do not. The scheme's advantage is that it answers the question of usable water, and the estimate is the price of that answer.

**Residual:** The 1948 scheme is stronger for water-balance questions than for the question of how a climate is named on a map. Both uses should be stated.

### India application

The rabi season makes the water-balance logic concrete. The northern plain's winter is dry and relatively cool. Evaporation demand is lower in winter, so the water balance is less severe than in summer, even though rain is scarce. Western disturbances give some light winter rain. The climate premise is that winter demand falls with temperature; the crop choice and irrigation scheduling that follow belong to the agriculture topic.

Limit: a water balance for a single station is an average. The rainfall of a given winter can differ widely from the normal.

### Syllabus, PYQ boundary and answer use

The syllabus asks for the distribution of natural resources and for the factors behind agricultural regions. In an answer, state the version of Thornthwaite used, name its basis, and then give the index.

### Revision points

1. Thornthwaite's 1931 scheme is vegetation-linked and uses the P/E index.
2. The 1931 P/E index is the sum of twelve monthly P/E ratios.
3. The 1931 temperature efficiency index gives the thermal provinces A' to F'.
4. The 1948 scheme uses potential evapotranspiration and a water balance.
5. The 1948 moisture index is Im = Ih - 0.6 x Ia.
6. Ia and Ih are the deficit and surplus each expressed as a percentage of PET.
7. The 0.6 weight reflects that stored surplus can offset a later deficit.
8. A moisture index separates places with the same rain but different demand.
9. Name the version before the basis in any answer.

### Answer in one line

*Answer pointer:* Im = 40; it sits on the boundary between the B1 and B2 ranges, so state which side the printing uses.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why a water-balance index can separate two places that receive the same rainfall, and say what the index does not show.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Rainfall is not the same as usable water. What a plant gains depends on how much water the air and the surface can take back, which is the potential evapotranspiration. Thornthwaite's 1948 index compares the monthly rain with that demand, and expresses the deficit and surplus as percentages of PET. Two places with the same rainfall can differ sharply in demand: the hotter, windier place has a larger PET, so it is more deficient and receives a lower moisture index. The index does not show the rainfall in any particular year, the sensitivity of the PET estimate to the temperature model, or the soil that stores the surplus. Those limits should be stated in any use of the index for planning.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the distinction between rainfall and usable water; 2 for PET as the demand; 2 for the deficit and surplus as percentages; 2 for the same rain giving different indices through demand; 1 for the PET estimate limit; 1 for the soil storage limit.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two stations have the same annual rainfall. One has a deficit and the other a surplus. Which has the higher moisture index, and why does the answer depend on PET?

**Model answer:** The station with the surplus has the higher moisture index, because a surplus raises Ih and a deficit raises Ia, which lowers Im. The answer depends on PET because both Ia and Ih are expressed as percentages of PET. A higher demand makes the same rain produce a larger deficit, so the index falls even with equal rainfall.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating equal rainfall as equal moisture. The index measures the balance of rain against demand, and demand differs between places.

  ISOLINE     JOINS PLACES WITH EQUAL     USE
  isotherm    temperature                 heat belts
  isohyet     rainfall                    rain belts

### Plain intuition: an isoline is a contour of climate

An isotherm joins places with the same temperature, and an isohyet joins places with the same rainfall. Read a set of isohyets across a country and you see its rain belts at a glance. Two warnings follow. An isohyet is rainfall, not humidity, and an isotherm is temperature, not heat stored in the ground. The lines summarise station data, so their position depends on how many stations feed them.

### Reading January isotherms

The textbook explanation of the January pattern over north India is that the land cools faster than the sea in winter, so the isotherms bend southward over the land. The pattern is a seasonal field, and it has not been re-verified against station data here. Where isohyets crowd together, rainfall changes steeply over a short distance; along the Western Ghats the crowding marks the sharp contrast between the windward slope and the leeward plateau.

### The regional schemes: names and purpose

- **Stamp and Kendrew:** a regional division of India by temperature and rainfall, credited to two authors. The date is not settled here, because different descriptions give different years. This topic does not reproduce its regions, because the region-by-region figures could not be checked against an available source.
- **R.L. Singh (1971):** ten climatic divisions, presented after and modifying the Stamp and Kendrew work, based on the temperature of the hottest and coldest months and on annual rainfall.

Both schemes are regional descriptions for comparison and planning. Their names are not Koppen codes.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Hand-drawn regions are only judgements, so they are not real.

**Reply:** The objection is partly right, since a boundary between two stations is a choice. The reply is that a regional scheme is valuable when its basis is stated, because another worker can then check the choice.

**Residual:** A regional division cannot be reproduced from a station list without the author's choices, so it describes India and does not classify it in the Koppen sense.

3. January isotherms bend over the land in winter, by the textbook account.
4. Crowded isohyets mark steep rainfall gradients, as along the Western Ghats.
5. Stamp and Kendrew gave a regional division of India by temperature and rainfall.
6. The Stamp and Kendrew date is not settled in this topic.
7. R.L. Singh's 1971 scheme has ten divisions based on the hottest and coldest months and annual rainfall.
8. Both regional schemes are descriptions, not Koppen codes.

> **Map task.** On a rainfall sketch the isohyets crowd together along the Western Ghats. Which side is the windward side, and what does the crowding show?
>

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain what an isohyet and an isotherm each measure, and why a climate map is clearer with both than with either alone.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** An isohyet joins places with equal rainfall and shows where rain belts lie and how steeply it changes. An isotherm joins places with equal temperature and shows heat belts and their seasonal bending. Each line answers one question, and neither describes humidity or the water that the ground holds. Read together, they show whether a region is both warm and wet, or warm and dry, and where the transitions lie. A reader who uses only isohyets misses the heat that drives evaporation, and a reader who uses only isotherms misses the rain that a crop receives. The two lines are complementary, and each map should state the station data it summarises.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for defining the isohyet; 2 for defining the isotherm; 2 for what each shows about belts; 2 for the warm-and-wet reading from both together; 1 for the warning about humidity; 1 for the caveat on station data.

### Concept check

**Question:** A reader says an isohyet of 200 cm shows a place's humidity. Correct the reader.

### Visual first: a boundary follows its statistic

```text
  A CLASS BOUNDARY IS A THRESHOLD APPLIED TO A NORMAL

  normal (rain, temperature) --> threshold --> class --> map line
          ^                                              |
          |                                              |
   changes slowly (decades)              moves when the normal moves

  A year of drought changes the record, not the normal.
  A sustained shift in the normal moves the line.
```

The chain runs from the normal to the line. An annual swing moves the record but not the normal, so the line stays where it was.

### Plain intuition: a line on a map is a rule, and rules can be restated

If a class is defined by a threshold, the class moves when the threshold is crossed by the underlying values. The threshold itself need not change. In Lesson 4, the dry threshold rose by 20 mm for every degree of warming, so a station with 810 mm of rain was not dry at 26 C and was dry at 27 C. Nothing about the rain changed. The line moved because the statistic it measures moved.

This is the sense in which a classification is a measurement. Its boundary is an instrument, and an instrument reads the place as it currently is.

### Baselines: the normal is a choice

The normal period is a convention. Two normal periods give two maps. The World Meteorological Organization standard normal is a 30-year period, and the Indian period commonly cited is 1991 to 2020, not re-verified here. An earlier period, such as 1961 to 1990, gives a different normal and possibly a different class for a station near a threshold. A comparison of two maps must use the same baseline, or it compares the baselines rather than the climates.

The implication for claims is direct. A statement that a class has moved needs the two baselines, the threshold, and the dataset. A statement without them is a headline, not a finding.

### Worked check: a threshold move (illustrative)

Take a station with mean annual temperature 26 C and 80 per cent of its rain in the summer half. Its threshold is 800 mm, and its rain under the earlier baseline is 810 mm, so it is not dry.

Under a later baseline the mean temperature is 27 C. The threshold becomes 820 mm. The rain, 810 mm, is now below the threshold, so the station is dry.

The move comes entirely from the temperature statistic. The same rain fell, and the class changed. The example is illustrative, and its size would have to be taken from a real station normal before it could be reported.

### Causal chain and limits

```text
warming or changed rainfall
  -> normal shifts (over decades, not in one year)
  -> threshold crossed for stations near the line
  -> class boundary moves on the map
```

The chain is slow and station-specific. A boundary moves only where the normal lies near the threshold. Stations far from the line keep their class.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If boundaries can move, the Koppen map is not reliable, and it should be redrawn every year.

**Reply:** The map is reliable as a description of a stated baseline. It is not a forecast. The honest response is to state the baseline and to recompute the boundary when the normal has genuinely changed, not every year.

**Residual:** Stations near a threshold are unstable, and a boundary drawn through them is uncertain. The uncertainty should be stated on the map.

### The hazard atlas and the climate class

A climate class and a hazard map answer different questions. A climate class describes the typical year. A hazard map describes the frequency and intensity of extreme events. Risk comes from the hazard, the exposure of people and assets to it, and their vulnerability. A climate class is not a risk map.

The Indian Meteorological Department publishes a Hazard Atlas of India. Its site lists hazards including wind, extreme rainfall, lightning, dust storms, hailstorms, fog, drought, cyclones and thunderstorms, and it links pages on heat, cold, flood and snow. The English title shown on the site is Hazard Atlas of India, and that is the title used here. The atlas is a hazard product, and it is not a Koppen classification.

### Hazard, exposure, vulnerability

```text
HAZARD          frequency and intensity of an event (a cyclone, a heatwave)
EXPOSURE        people, crops and assets in the event's path
VULNERABILITY   susceptibility to harm, given exposure
CAPACITY        coping and recovery ability, which reduces risk
RISK            rises with hazard, exposure and vulnerability; falls with capacity
```

Risk rises with hazard, exposure and vulnerability, and falls with coping capacity. The factors can be mapped separately. A district can share a climate class with a neighbour and still carry a different risk.

### India application

Consider the western Thar. Its class is dry, and its temperature has risen. A climate analysis may say that its dry threshold has moved. A hazard analysis asks a different question: how often do extreme heat or dust events occur, and how many people are exposed? The two analyses use different data and should not be merged. The planning consequence is that drought, crop and water decisions rest on the climate normal, while heat and dust warnings rest on the hazard product.

Limit: any claim about the expansion of a dry class in a named district needs a named dataset, baseline and scenario. This topic does not quote a national percentage of area changing class, because no such figure is verified here.

### Related questions and method

Several related questions sit in the environment, ecology and essay topics: on desertification, on climate change and sea-level rise affecting island nations, and on climate-resilient technologies. Those topics take up the subject matter. For this topic the transferable point is method. A boundary movement is a statistical result that needs a baseline, a threshold and a dataset. An essay that uses a climate boundary as evidence must state those three things.

### Revision points

1. A class boundary is a threshold applied to a normal.
2. A normal changes slowly; a single year changes only the record.
3. A sustained shift in the normal moves the line for stations near the threshold.
4. Two baselines give two maps; compare like with like.
5. The current WMO normal period is 30 years; the most recent Indian normal used here is 1991 to 2020.
6. A climate class describes the typical year; a hazard product describes extreme events.
7. Risk rises with hazard, exposure and vulnerability, and falls with coping capacity.
8. The Indian Meteorological Department's Hazard Atlas of India is a hazard product.
9. No national percentage of area changing class is quoted without a named study.

### Read the table

**Misconception-led.** Two districts share a climate class, and a reader concludes they face the same disaster risk. Which factor would prove the reader wrong?

*Answer pointer:* Exposure. A shared class says nothing about how many people and assets lie in a hazard's path.

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why a district's climate class and its disaster risk can differ, and what would have to be shown before a boundary shift is reported as evidence of rising risk.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** A climate class describes the typical year, drawn from a threshold applied to a normal. Disaster risk rises with the hazard, the exposure of people and assets, and their susceptibility to harm, and falls with coping capacity. Two districts can share a class and face very different risk if one lies in the path of cyclones and holds many people, and the other does not. Before a boundary shift is reported as evidence of rising risk, three things must be shown: the baseline and threshold that define the class, the dataset and period used, and the hazard data that link the change to exposure. A moving line alone shows a changed normal. It does not show that more people are harmed.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the class as threshold applied to a normal; 2 for risk as hazard, exposure and susceptibility, reduced by capacity; 2 for the example of shared class and different risk; 2 for the baseline, threshold and dataset requirement; 1 for the hazard link; 1 for the caution that a moving line is not harm.

### Bridge to the final sections

The final sections consolidate the teaching and introduce no new doctrine.

### Concept check

**Question:** A station's class changes after a rise in mean temperature, although its rainfall is unchanged. Explain the mechanism, and say what additional evidence would be needed before reporting that the change reflects a rise in risk.

**Model answer:** The dry threshold rises with mean annual temperature, so the same rainfall can fall below the new threshold. That changes the class, though the rain has not changed. A reported change in risk needs the hazard data showing the event frequency, the exposure data showing the people and assets in the path, and the vulnerability data showing their capacity to cope. The class change alone shows a changed normal only.

**Misconception to avoid:** Taking a change in climate class as direct evidence of a change in disaster risk. The class and the risk are different measures, and the link between them must be shown.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These questions are new task types and are not repeats of the lesson prompts. Each model is original. No model answers a verified question.

### Question 1 (10 marks, 150 words or fewer): decode an unfamiliar code

**Prompt:** A climate is coded Cfb in a standard scheme. Decode each letter, state what the code means for the seasonality of rain, and explain why the same letters could not describe a monsoon coast.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** The main letter C places the climate in the group whose coldest month lies between the convention line and 18 C, with a warmest month above 10 C. The second letter f means there is no dry season, so rain falls in every month. The third letter b means no month is above 22 C and at least four months exceed 10 C, which describes a warm summer that is not very hot. A monsoon coast would not carry f, because its rain is concentrated in a season with a short dry spell, which the monsoon letter m records. Its main letter might also differ if its coldest month were above 18 C, which would make it an A-group code. The letters are a sequence of tests, and a different test result gives a different letter.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the main letter's group and thermal test; 2 for f as no dry season; 2 for b as the warm-summer test; 2 for distinguishing a monsoon coast by m or by its A-group main letter; 2 for the principle that letters are sequential tests.

### Question 2 (10 marks, 150 words or fewer): correct a student's answer

**Prompt:** A student writes that Thornthwaite's classification is a temperature scheme and that the moisture index is a feature of all three of Thornthwaite's versions. Correct both statements with precision.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Both statements need correction. Thornthwaite's 1931 scheme is not a temperature scheme alone: it uses a P/E index, the sum of twelve monthly ratios of precipitation to evaporation, as well as a temperature efficiency index. The 1948 scheme is the one based on a water balance, using potential evapotranspiration and a moisture index, Im equals Ih minus 0.6 times Ia. The moisture index is therefore a feature of the 1948 scheme, not of the 1931 one. The correct statement is that the 1931 scheme is vegetation-linked and uses both indices, and that the 1948 scheme is a water-balance scheme. The version must be named before the basis is stated.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for correcting the temperature-only claim; 2 for the 1931 P/E index as twelve summed ratios; 2 for the 1948 water balance and PET; 2 for the moisture-index formula attached to 1948; 2 for the instruction to name the version first.

### Question 3 (10 marks, 150 words or fewer): comment on an isotherm pattern

**Prompt:** In a January map, the isotherms over the north Indian plain bend southward over the land, while the isotherms over the adjoining sea run more nearly east to west. Comment on what the pattern shows and why it arises.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** The pattern shows that the north Indian land is colder than the sea at the same latitude in January. Isotherms bend towards the cooler region, so they are pushed south over the land, where the winter cold is strongest. The reason is the land-sea contrast of winter: land loses heat faster than the sea, which holds its warmth, so the land becomes a cold pool. The pattern is a textbook description of the January field, and its size depends on the year and on the isotherms selected. A comment should therefore state the pattern, the land-sea reason, and the caveat that the isotherms are a summary of station data.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for stating the bend as a cold land pool; 2 for the comparison with the sea at the same latitude; 2 for the land-sea heat-loss reason; 2 for the observation that the pattern is a seasonal field; 2 for the caveat that the isotherms summarise station data.

### Question 4 (15 marks, 250 words or fewer): choose a basis for a purpose

**Prompt:** A state planner must zone a district for crop choice and irrigation scheduling. Discuss which classification basis, Koppen's threshold codes, Trewartha's count of warm months, Thornthwaite's water balance, or a regional division of the Stamp-Kendrew or Singh type, would best serve this purpose, and state the limit of your choice.

**Model (250 words or fewer):** The planner's purpose is to decide what can be grown and when water must be supplied, so the basis must measure usable water and the length of the growing season. Koppen's threshold codes describe the kind of climate and the seasonality of rain, which is useful for a first description, but they do not measure water lost to evaporation. Trewartha's count of warm months captures the length of the growing season well, since crops respond to how many months are warm enough. Thornthwaite's 1948 water balance comes closest to the purpose, because it compares the district's rain with its evaporation demand and yields a deficit and a surplus that show when irrigation is needed. A regional division by hottest and coldest months and rainfall, as in the Singh scheme, supports a planning vocabulary for the district but gives no water balance. The best choice is therefore the water balance for scheduling, with Trewartha's count to define the growing season and Koppen's code to describe the regional context. The limit is that the water balance depends on an estimate of evaporation demand, and the estimate is only as good as its temperature model. The planner must also use station records for the district, since the normal of one station may not represent the whole district. A zoning built on the water balance should be checked against observed crop failures before it is adopted.

**Rubric (15 marks):** 3 marks for stating the planning purpose as usable water and growing season; 3 for Koppen's strength and limit for this purpose; 3 for Trewartha's count and its fit with growing season; 3 for Thornthwaite's water balance and irrigation fit, with the PET limit; 2 for the combined recommendation; 1 for the district-representativeness caveat.

### Question 6 (20 marks, 250 words or fewer): evaluate a policy use

**Prompt:** A state government proposes to treat a shift in a district's dry climate class as the trigger for a drought-preparedness programme. Evaluate this proposal. In your answer, consider what the class shift measures, what it does not measure, and what evidence a programme should require.

**Model (250 words or fewer):** The proposal treats a change in the dry class as a trigger, which assumes that a class shift is a reliable measure of the change in drought exposure that a programme must address. The class shift has real value. Under the threshold rule, a rise in temperature raises the threshold and can move a district across the dry line without any loss of rain, so the class captures a change in the balance between rain and demand. That is a genuine signal of a shifted normal. The shift does not, however, measure drought. A class describes the typical year, while a drought is an extreme of rainfall or of soil moisture in a particular season. A district can remain in the same class and suffer a severe drought, and it can change class with no change in the frequency of drought. The evidence a programme should require is therefore threefold. First, the baseline and threshold that define the class, with the dataset and period named. Second, hazard data on the frequency and intensity of drought, which the climate class does not provide. Third, exposure and vulnerability data, showing how many people, crops and water systems would be affected and how well they cope. A programme triggered by the class shift alone would be late for some districts and premature for others. A better trigger combines the normal-based class with a drought-hazard measure and a vulnerability index, so that resources follow risk rather than the line on the map.

**Rubric (20 marks):** 4 marks for what the class shift measures (normal balance and threshold); 4 for what it does not measure (extreme drought frequency and exposure); 4 for the evidence required (baseline, hazard, exposure and vulnerability); 4 for weighing the proposal's strength and failure; 3 for a constructive alternative trigger; 1 for the statement of limits.

### Question 7 (20 marks, 250 words or fewer): critically discuss a planning replacement

**Prompt:** Critically discuss whether Indian planning should replace Koppen's empirical threshold codes with a causal classification based on air masses and circulation.

**Model (250 words or fewer):** The case for replacement rests on the central weakness of the empirical codes: they record outcomes without explaining them. A causal classification would identify the air masses and circulation that produce the monsoon, the western disturbances, and the rain shadow, and it would explain why the Western Ghats and the Deccan interior differ at the same latitude. Such an account could also anticipate how a changed circulation alters the regions, which a threshold code cannot do. The case against replacement is strong for planning. Causal classifications are harder to reproduce from routine station records, and their boundaries are less standard, which makes comparison across districts and across time difficult. Threshold codes are reproducible, comparable and map easily, and a district planner needs exactly those properties to set a baseline. The two schemes answer different questions, so replacement misreads their purpose. A balanced conclusion is complementary use. Planning should keep the threshold codes as the descriptive and comparative frame, supply a causal account for the regions where the mechanism matters, such as the Ghats and the north-east, and use a water-balance measure for irrigation. Replacement would trade a reproducible baseline for an explanation that cannot yet be applied at the scale of a district plan.

**Rubric (20 marks):** 4 marks for the causal case (explanation of outcomes and of change); 4 for the strength of threshold codes (reproducibility, comparability); 4 for the practical limits of causal schemes in planning; 3 for the difference in purpose between the schemes; 3 for the complementary conclusion; 2 for the scope of the conclusion.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

**Classification.** A class is a set of thresholds applied to normals, and every scheme chooses its measure. **Koppen.** Empirical thresholds tied to vegetation: reproducible, comparable and map-ready, but silent on cause and sharp at boundaries. **Groups.** A: coolest month at least 18 C. E: warmest month below 10 C. C and D: coldest month against the -3 C or 0 C convention. H is a suffix for high altitude, not a main group. **Dry test.** Threshold 20T + 280, 140 or 0 mm; below half is BW, half to the threshold is BS. Warming raises the threshold. **Letters.** Second letters f, m, w, s; the m test uses the monsoon line 100 - P/25. Third letters a, b, c, d; h and k in the dry group. **India.** Am for windward monsoon tracts; Aw for the dry-winter plateau, where rain is above the threshold; BShw or BWhw where the dry test is met; Cwa for the Ganga plain; ET and EF at altitude. **Trewartha.** Six groups A to F; C, D and E by months at or above 10 C; first published 1966. **Thornthwaite.** 1931 P/E and temperature indices; 1948 PET and Im = Ih - 0.6 Ia. **Boundaries and risk.** Lines move when normals move; risk rises with hazard, exposure and vulnerability and falls with capacity. **Open.** The C/D convention; the Indian labels Amw, As, BShw, BWhw and Cwg; the dates and figures of the regional schemes.
