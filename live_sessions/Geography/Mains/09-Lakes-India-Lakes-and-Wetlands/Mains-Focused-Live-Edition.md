# Lakes and Wetlands - Mains-Focused Live Edition

A lake looks like the simplest landform a geographer can hold: a hollow that holds water. Yet the same hollow records how it was made, how long it will last, what its water is like, and what human decisions will do to it. These thirteen lessons build that story in order. The first two give you the two ideas everything else depends on: the water budget and the outlet. The next four ask how lakes are made, and why origin still matters decades later. Lessons seven to ten ask how lakes change and die, through hydrology on one side and sediment and nutrients on the other. The last three bring the work to India, to the wetland framework that governs many Indian lakes, and to the answer-writing skills that turn the material into a Mains answer.

```text
                       v              v                  v

                  |         LAKE WATER STORE  (volume V)        |

                 EVAPORATION    OUTLET RIVER      ABSTRACTION + SEEPAGE

  Change in storage  =  (inflows)  -  (outflows)
```

Read this diagram as a bank account. Water arrives from three directions and leaves by three or four routes. The level rises only when arrivals exceed departures. Almost every question about a lake, whether it is shrinking, becoming salty or drying out, is a question about one line of this account.

The intuition comes before the vocabulary. Picture a bucket with a tap running into it and a hole in its side. If the tap is stronger than the hole, the level rises. If the hole is stronger, the level falls, and the falling level changes how much water the hole can drain. A lake behaves the same way, with a far larger bucket and many more holes.

The lesson uses four terms. A **lake** is a body of standing water in a land hollow, deep and extensive enough that open water covers the centre for most of the year. Stretches of river, sea arms and aquifers are not lakes, even when they hold water. A **wetland** is land saturated or flooded for some part of the year and supporting water-tolerant plants. The Ramsar Convention's definition of a wetland is wide enough to include lakes, so a lake is one kind of wetland rather than its opposite; a marsh is distinguished by shallow, vegetated water and a lake by its open deep water. A **reservoir** is a lake made by a dam to store water. A **lagoon** is a shallow coastal water body cut off from the sea by a bar or spit.

The budget is written in full: change in storage equals rainfall on the lake, plus river inflow, plus groundwater inflow, minus evaporation, minus outflow, minus abstraction, minus seepage. Each term has its own controls. Rainfall and evaporation are climatic. River inflow depends on the upstream catchment. Groundwater depends on the geology around the basin. Abstraction is a human decision. The budget tells you which term is driving a change, which is why it is the first tool for diagnosing any lake.

Residence time turns the budget into a timescale. If a lake holds a volume V and loses an outflow Q per year, its mean residence time is τ = V/Q. A lake holding 1 cubic kilometre that loses 0.5 cubic kilometres per year has a residence time of two years: on average, a parcel of its water stays about two years before leaving. Residence times of small lakes are a few years or less, while the largest lakes run to decades and, in the case of Lake Superior, roughly two centuries on commonly quoted figures. Rivers typically flush in days. A long residence time buffers change, so a dry year barely shows. A short residence time tracks inflow closely. The same number helps predict five things: how fast a lake salts up, how fast it becomes eutrophic, how quickly it recovers once a pressure is removed, how sensitive it is to abstraction, and how well it buffers floods.

The formula assumes steady conditions and well-mixed water. Real lakes have short-circuiting, where inflow streams cross the lake to the outlet without mixing with the rest. The number is therefore an average for judging buffering, not an exact age for each drop of water.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Wular Lake in Jammu and Kashmir is a large lake fed by the Jhelum River and smaller streams, so its level is driven largely by river inflow and the flood pulse of the valley. A year of poor river flow lowers it visibly. The limit is that this lesson uses Wular only to show the structure of the budget. Its present level needs current monitoring data, which is not used here.

**Objection and reply.** A student might say a lake is simply a big pond. The reply is that size is not the criterion. What separates a lake is its residence time and its capacity to hold deep, long-lived water layers, the basis of the limnology in Lesson 7. A pond with a short residence time behaves very differently even at similar size.

**The temporary-lake sentence.** Textbooks differ on whether lakes are "often" or "always" temporary. The defensible statement is this: every lake is geologically transient, because it will eventually fill with sediment, drain through an eroding outlet, or turn into a marsh. But the timescale runs from years to millions of years. An ox-bow lake can disappear within decades, while Lake Baikal's basin is estimated at 25 to 30 million years old. So "lakes are temporary" is true on a geological clock and misleading on a human one. Use the qualified statement in answers.

**UPSC application.** The syllabus asks for changes in critical geographical features, including water bodies, and their effects. That demand starts with the budget. The 2018 Prelims item on human-caused shrinkage of two named lakes (Lesson 9) begins here.

- Prelims: separate lake, wetland, reservoir and lagoon by criterion, not by appearance.
- Mains: state the budget term that changed first, then the consequence.
- Trap: treating a lake as a static object, or calling every lake permanent.

Mini recap:

```text
Lake = open water in a hollow
Budget = inflows - outflows (change in storage)
Residence time = storage / outflow
Long residence -> buffered level; short residence -> fast response
Every lake is geologically temporary; timescales differ widely
```

Pause and retrieve: a lake with a residence time of 30 years loses 10 per cent of its inflow for one year. Why does its level barely move?
*Answer pointer:* Its store is large relative to its outflow, so one year's shortfall is a small fraction of what is stored and is absorbed by the buffer.

Revision notes:

- A lake is a deep, open-water hollow; a wetland is saturated land with water-tolerant life; a reservoir is a dammed lake; a lagoon is a coastal bar-enclosed water body.
- Change in storage equals inflows minus outflows; read every lake question through one of these terms.
- Residence time is storage divided by outflow, expressed in time.
- Small lakes renew their water in years; the largest take decades or longer to flush.
- Long residence time buffers change; short residence time tracks inflow.
- The formula is an average and assumes steady, mixed conditions.
- All lakes are geologically temporary; many are short-lived, some persist for millions of years.
- Wular is a structural example of a river-fed valley lake; its current level needs current data.

Next, the budget tells you how much water a lake keeps. Lesson 2 asks what happens to what the water carries, which depends on whether the lake has a way out.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two lakes receive the same drop in river inflow. Lake A has a residence time of about one year and Lake B about twenty years. Which one's level falls faster, and why?

**Model answer:** Lake A falls faster. Its residence time is short, so the water it holds is replaced quickly, and a lost inflow is not buffered by stored water. Lake B holds a large volume relative to its outflow, so it draws down slowly and absorbs the shortfall over many years. The mechanism is the storage-to-outflow ratio, not the inflow change alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the level depends on rainfall alone. Outflow, evaporation, abstraction and residence time decide how a given change in inflow shows up in the level.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Distinguish a lake, a wetland and a reservoir, using the water budget as the criterion. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** A lake is a deep, open-water hollow whose level is set by the balance of inflow and outflow. A wetland is land saturated or flooded for part of the year, supporting water-tolerant life; it may be shallow, seasonal or vegetated, and the Ramsar definition is wide enough to include some lakes. A reservoir is a lake created by a dam, whose budget is managed by releases and abstraction rather than only by natural outflow. The budget criterion separates them: residence time and the share of abstraction in the budget differ. An Indian example is Wular, a river-fed natural lake, against a dam-impounded reservoir whose outflow is set by operators.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Lake definition with the hollow and open water, 2 marks. Wetland definition with saturation and vegetation, 2 marks. Reservoir definition with the dam and managed outflow, 2 marks. Budget criterion stated as residence time or abstraction, 2 marks. Correct India example used with its role, 2 marks.

```text
  EXORHEIC BASIN                           ENDORHEIC BASIN

  mountain --> river --> lake --> SEA      mountain --> river --> lake --> ( no outlet )
                         |                                    |
                    outflow carries                     water leaves ONLY by
                    water AND salt out                  evaporation or seepage
                                                        salt is left behind
```

Lesson 1 showed that water arrives and departs. This lesson asks what happens when the departure route is missing, and what happens to the salts that rivers carry.

Think of two sinks. Both take water in. The first has a plughole, so water and any dissolved matter drain away. The second has no plughole, so water can only leave by evaporating into the air, and whatever it dissolved stays behind. Over time the second sink becomes concentrated. That is the intuition for everything below.

The lesson introduces four terms. An **exorheic** basin drains to the sea through an outlet, so its lakes are flow-through and their salts leave with the water. An **endorheic** basin has no outlet to the sea, and water leaves only by evaporation or seepage. A **playa** is the flat, often temporary floor of a closed basin that fills after rain and dries to a crust of salts and mud. A **salt lake** is a lake with a high dissolved-salt concentration, usually because it is endorheic in a dry climate.

**Outlet status is a matter of geometry.** Whether a basin drains depends on whether water can spill over a sill on its rim, not on climate alone. A basin whose rim is lower on one side drains that way. A sill can be buried by sediment or lowered by erosion, so the outlet status of a basin can change over long periods. Climate then decides what a closed basin does with its water.

**The salt budget.** Let the lake hold a mass of salt M and a volume V, so the concentration is C = M/V. Salts enter with river inflow and leave only with outflow. In an exorheic lake the outflow carries salt out, so M stays small and C stays low. In an endorheic lake the only losses are evaporation and seepage. Evaporation removes pure water, so V falls while M stays the same, and C rises every year. Suppose a closed lake holds 100 units of salt in 1,000 units of water, giving a concentration of 0.1. If evaporation removes 200 units of water and no salt leaves, the concentration becomes 100 divided by 800, which is 0.125. Each year of this process raises it again, unless the salts are removed by crystallisation on the basin floor or by seepage.

**Endorheic does not always mean saline.** Lake Chad is endorheic, yet its water seeps toward a depression in the north, which keeps its salinity low. Salinity depends on the balance between evaporation and seepage, not on outlet status alone. Pangong Tso in eastern Ladakh is an endorheic brackish lake, and only part of it lies in India. A closed basin can therefore be brackish, and its national position needs care on maps.

**Shallow closed basins have a shape effect.** In a shallow closed basin, a small fall in depth can expose a large area of bed. The area lost per metre of level fall is set by the floor gradient: the flatter the floor, the more area each metre exposes. A flat-floored closed basin can look catastrophic in imagery while losing comparatively little water. Lesson 9 returns to this point.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Sambhar, a major salt lake of Rajasthan, sits in an arid, inland-drainage basin. Its salinity follows the logic above: rivers bring in salts, the lake has no outlet to the sea, and evaporation concentrates the brine. The limit is that Sambhar's salinity, brine chemistry and salt harvest change over time, and this lesson gives the mechanism rather than measured values.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that salt lakes are simply lakes near the sea that have absorbed sea water. The reply is that a salt lake gets its salt from its own catchment, and sea water plays no necessary role. A coastal lagoon can be saline because of the sea, but an inland salt lake is saline because its basin is closed.

**Unresolved residual.** Salinity in a closed basin can shift with rainfall, groundwater and abstraction, so a salt lake may move between states over decades. Do not assign a fixed category to any named lake without current data.

**UPSC application.**

- Prelims: the 2021 item on saline lakes of Rajasthan (naming Didwana, Kuchaman, Sargol and Khatu) tests whether you can place a salt lake in an arid, inland-drainage setting and explain why it is saline. No answer is given here.
- Prelims: the 2022 item on a West African lake that dried into desert belongs to World Regional Geography, but its closed-basin logic is the one taught here.
- Trap: calling every lake without an outlet "dead" or "stagnant". It loses water constantly, which is why it is sensitive.
- Answer use: state the outlet, then the salt budget, then the climatic control.

Mini recap:

```text
Sill on the rim -> outlet status (geometry)
Outlet to sea  -> salts leave with outflow -> low salinity
No outlet      -> losses = evaporation + seepage -> salts concentrate
Seepage can keep an endorheic lake fresh (Lake Chad)
Shallow closed basin -> small depth change, large area change
```

Pause and retrieve: a closed lake holds 200 units of salt in 2,000 units of water. Evaporation removes 500 units of water and no salt leaves. What is the new concentration?
*Answer pointer:* 200 divided by 1,500 is about 0.133, up from 0.1.

Next, the outlet tells you what happens to water and salt. Lesson 3 asks how the basin itself was made, since origin sets the lake's depth and form before any water arrives.

Revision notes:

- An exorheic lake drains to the sea, and its outflow carries salts away.
- An endorheic lake has no sea outlet; water leaves only by evaporation or seepage.
- Outlet status is set by a sill on the basin rim; climate decides what a closed basin does with its water.
- Salts enter with inflow and leave only with outflow, so closed basins concentrate salts.
- A playa is a closed basin floor that fills and dries, leaving salt and mud.
- Endorheic does not always mean saline; seepage can keep Lake Chad fresh.
- Salt lakes are typically in arid, inland-drainage basins.
- Sambhar in Rajasthan is a salt lake of an arid, closed basin.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does an endorheic lake in a dry climate tend to grow saltier over time, even if its inflow stays constant?

**Model answer:** Its water leaves only by evaporation or seepage, which removes pure water and leaves the dissolved salts behind. Salts keep entering with inflow, so the concentration rises each year unless the salts are removed by crystallisation or seepage. A dry climate increases evaporation and therefore the rate of concentration. A constant inflow is not enough to stop the rise.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume a closed lake has stopped losing water. It loses water constantly through evaporation, which is precisely why its salts concentrate.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why closed-basin lakes are more sensitive to upstream water use than lakes with an outlet. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** An outlet lake loses water and dissolved matter through a river, so its level and salinity are partly buffered. A closed-basin lake has no such route. Its inflow must balance evaporation and seepage, so any reduction in inflow lowers the level directly, and the remaining water concentrates salts. Because evaporation is high in arid basins, the balance is tight. Upstream abstraction for irrigation therefore reaches the lake without any buffering. Lake Chad illustrates the sensitivity: it is shallow and endorheic, and its inflow depends on upstream rivers. The argument is strongest when stated as a budget: the closed basin's buffer is its inflow, and that is the term controlled upstream.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Outlet and closed-basin contrast stated, 2 marks. Salt budget explained with evaporation and concentration, 2 marks. Inflow as the controlling term with upstream abstraction, 2 marks. Aridity or evaporation role, 2 marks. Named example used with a limit, 2 marks.

```text
  HOW A HOLLOW IS MADE                      WHAT THE HOLLOW LOOKS LIKE

  Earth movement  --> basin (warp, fault, rift)  --> deep, long-lived lake
  Glacier erosion --> cirque / scoured hollow    --> small, steep-sided tarn
  Ice blocks melt --> hollow in outwash          --> shallow kettle lake
  Moraine damming --> blocked valley             --> young, fragile lake
```

Every lake has a basin, and the basin has an origin. This lesson covers the two origins that produce the deepest and most striking lakes: earth movement and ice. The origin matters because it sets the lake's shape, depth and lifespan before any human action begins.

The intuition is the difference between digging a hole and filling one. A tectonic basin is a large part of the crust that has been pulled down or split apart, and it can stay open for millions of years. A glacial hollow is excavated by ice over a much shorter time, and it is often shaped by the ice itself. The first kind is structural. The second is erosional and, for lakes, usually small.

**Tectonic lakes.** A tectonic lake forms when earth movement warps, sags, faults or rifts the crust, creating a basin. Four basin shapes carry different signatures. A downwarp is a broad, shallow sag. A single fault gives a long, steep-sided basin along one edge. A half-graben is a tilted block with a deep edge. A full graben is a symmetric valley bounded by a fault on each side. Rift lakes are typically of the last two kinds.

**Examples of tectonic depth.** Lake Baikal in Siberia is a rift lake with a maximum depth of about 1,642 metres, the deepest lake in the world, and an age estimated at 25 to 30 million years. Lake Tanganyika, in the East African Rift, is second in depth at about 1,470 metres; its central basin began forming some 9 to 12 million years ago. The Caspian Sea is the largest inland body of water by area. It is a downwarped inland basin that became landlocked about 5.5 million years ago through tectonic uplift and a fall in sea level.

**Glacial lakes.** A glacial lake owes its hollow to ice. A **tarn** is a small mountain lake filling a cirque, the bowl-shaped hollow carved by a glacier on a mountainside. A tarn's rim can be a moraine, a heap of glacial debris, and that rim can act as a natural dam; such dams are not guaranteed to be stable. A **kettle lake** forms differently: a retreating glacier leaves blocks of ice buried in outwash sediment, and when the buried ice melts, the ground above sinks into a depression that water can fill. Kettle lakes tend to fill with sediment over time. A **moraine-dammed** lake forms when glacial debris blocks a valley, and these lakes are often young and vulnerable to breaching.

Glacial erosion also produces three further forms. Ribbon lakes lie in overdeepened valleys, rock-hollow lakes occupy bedrock depressions, and drift-deposition lakes sit behind outwash or ridge deposits. Glacial erosion can enlarge an older hollow rather than make one from nothing: the Great Lakes of North America occupy basins inherited from structure and deepened by Pleistocene ice. The hazard that moraine-dammed lakes can pose, the glacial lake outburst flood, is handled in the glaciation and hazard topic (Topic 06), not here.

The argument has a clear structure. Premise one: a lake is a basin filled with water. Premise two: the basin's origin sets its size, depth and persistence. Premise three: earth-movement basins persist longer and reach greater depth than ice-made hollows. Conclusion: origin predicts depth and vulnerability, so a deep lake is not automatically glacial. A tarn is shallow for its catchment, and a rift lake is deep whether or not ice ever touched it.

**The Himalayan and Kashmir family.** The Himalaya contain lakes of several origins, and the rule is not "all Himalayan lakes are glacial". Gangabal and Sheshnag in the Kashmir valley are high-altitude lakes in glacial settings. Kailash-Kund is listed among high Himalayan lakes of glacial character, though its exact setting is not fixed here. Sat Tal, near Bhimtal in Kumaun, is a group of interconnected lakes whose origin is complex, so it should be treated as a multi-process case rather than given a single glacial label. Naini, in Kumaun, sits in a tectonic basin later reshaped by landslides. Wular, in Bandipora district of the Kashmir valley, is tectonic and fed by the Jhelum, so it belongs with the Himalayan and Kashmir lakes by setting and with the tectonic lakes by origin. Sanasar, in Ramban district of the Jammu region, is a high-altitude lake whose origin is uncertain, so a glacial attribution should not be made without checking it.

**Unresolved residual.** Himalayan lakes can combine faulting, landslide blockage and glacial shaping, and the balance between them cannot always be read from a map.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that high altitude means glacial. The reply is that altitude is evidence of climate, not of origin. A tectonic basin can lie high, and a glacial hollow can lie in a low valley. The test is the form of the basin and its geological setting, not its height.

**UPSC application.**

- Prelims: the 2018 item on human-caused shrinkage of Baikal and the Aral Sea (Lesson 9) and the 2023 item linking rivers and lakes including Wular test the basin origin of named lakes.
- Trap: labelling every Himalayan lake glacial, or assuming a deep lake is always glacial.
- Answer use: state the origin, then the depth and persistence it implies.

Mini recap:

```text
Earth movement -> tectonic basin -> deep, persistent (Baikal, Tanganyika)
Ice -> cirque tarn, kettle, moraine-dammed, ribbon -> smaller, more fragile
Altitude is not origin; test the form and setting
GLOF hazard -> Topic 06
```

Pause and retrieve: a high lake is 1,200 metres deep, and a second high lake is 3 metres deep and sits in a cirque. Which is the tectonic basin, and what does the depth tell you?
*Answer pointer:* The 1,200-metre lake is the structural basin, because structural basins are the usual origin of such depth; the cirque tarn is glacial, and altitude alone would not separate them.

Next, origin sets the basin. Lesson 4 turns to lakes whose hollow is made by volcanoes, meteorite impacts or dissolution, where a round outline is especially misleading.

- Earth movement creates tectonic basins by warping, sagging, faulting or rifting.
- Four tectonic shapes: downwarp, single fault, half-graben, full graben.
- Rift lakes such as Baikal and Tanganyika are among the deepest in the world.
- Baikal: about 1,642 m deep, age estimated at 25 to 30 million years.
- Tanganyika: about 1,470 m deep, the second deepest lake.
- The Caspian is the largest inland body of water by area and became landlocked by tectonic uplift.

- The Great Lakes are tectonic basins deepened by Pleistocene ice.

- Sanasar lies in Ramban district, Jammu region; its origin is uncertain.

### Concept check

**Question:** A high Himalayan lake is very deep, while a second is shallow and sits in a cirque. What do these two features tell you about their origins?

**Model answer:** Great depth points to a structural basin, which earth movement creates and which can persist for long periods. A shallow lake in a cirque points to glacial excavation of a mountain hollow. The depth-and-form evidence is strongest when both are read together: the deep lake's basin is structural, and the shallow tarn's hollow is glacial. Altitude alone would mislead, because both lakes could be high.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat altitude as proof of a glacial origin. High lakes can be tectonic, and deep lakes can be structural.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** "Origin determines the life and depth of a lake." Examine this statement with reference to tectonic and glacial lakes. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The statement is largely correct because origin sets the basin's form before any human effect begins. Tectonic basins are structural. Rift lakes such as Baikal and Tanganyika reach great depths and have persisted for millions of years, which is why they are among the deepest lakes. Glacial hollows are excavated over shorter periods and are usually smaller, with tarns in cirques and kettle lakes formed from melted buried ice. Their persistence depends on sediment and drainage, so they are more vulnerable to filling. The qualification is that origin is not the whole story: later landslides, sedimentation and human storage can change any lake. The Himalayan case shows this, since Naini is tectonic but has been reshaped by slope processes.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Origin-to-form causal link stated, 2 marks. Tectonic depth and persistence with a named lake, 2 marks. Glacial hollow and small size with a named form, 2 marks. Qualification that later processes modify origin, 2 marks. Himalayan or Naini example used correctly, 2 marks.

Three of the lakes in this lesson have a round outline, and that is the trap. A round hollow can be made by a volcanic vent, by the collapse of a magma chamber, by a meteorite strike, or by water dissolving rock. The shape alone does not decide the origin. This lesson teaches the tests that separate them.

The intuition is that a round hole needs a cause. Something pushed material out, something collapsed into a void, something hit the ground from above, or something dissolved the ground away. Each produces a round hollow, and each leaves different evidence in the rock.

**Diagnostics.** A **crater** is built by material ejected from a vent, leaving a constructional rim. A **caldera** forms when the roof subsides after magma is withdrawn, so its size reflects the chamber beneath. An **impact** crater is made by something arriving from outside, with no volcanic edifice, an overturned rim and shock features in the rock. A lake's name does not fix its class: Crater Lake in Oregon, despite its name, occupies a caldera.

**Volcanic lakes.** A volcanic crater lake fills a small crater at the top of a vent. A caldera is much larger, often many kilometres across. Lake Toba in Sumatra is a caldera lake and a standard example of a large volcanic basin. The eruption itself belongs to Topic 03. One hazard does cross over: water in a crater lake can mix with ash to form a lahar, a destructive mudflow that travels far down valleys long after the eruption, and that is treated in Topic 03.

**Impact lakes and Lonar.** An impact crater lake sits in a bowl excavated by a meteorite. **Lonar Lake** in Buldhana district of Maharashtra is the Indian case. The crater lies on Deccan basalt, whose host rock dates to about 66 million years, but the crater itself is much younger. Dating the crater is contested: one earlier estimate is about 52 thousand years, while later argon-argon dating gave about 570 thousand years, which is disputed because inherited argon may distort the result. Geologists first thought Lonar was volcanic, and the impact origin was established later. The lake is saline and highly alkaline, with reported pH values of about 9 to 10.5, because it lies in an endorheic basin. It is a National Geological Monument and a Ramsar site; reports give two different 2020 dates for its Ramsar designation, so quote the year.

**Solution and deflation hollows.** A solution lake forms in karst, where water dissolves limestone and leaves hollows that fill with water. Karst landforms are covered in Topic 08. A deflation lake forms when wind scoops a hollow in a desert floor, and it often becomes a salty playa where the drainage is closed. Unfamiliar names need the genesis test in Lesson 6 rather than a default assumption.

**Argument.** Premise one: a volcanic lake needs a vent or caldera, which should be linked to lava or ash deposits. Premise two: an impact lake needs shock features in the rock, which a vent does not produce. Premise three: a karst lake needs soluble bedrock, and a deflation hollow needs a dry, wind-exposed floor. Conclusion: a round lake is an origin claim to be tested against the rock and setting, not read from the map.

**Objection and reply.** A student might say that a crater lake is always volcanic. The reply is that "crater" is a shape term, and impact craters are common on the surface. The test is the rock: volcanic evidence is lava and ash, impact evidence is shocked rock. Lonar is the Indian reminder that a crater can be impact-made.

**UPSC application.**

- Classify lakes by process, not outline. A demand to classify lakes by origin is answered by naming the process and an example for each class: tectonic, glacial, volcanic, impact, solution, deflation, depositional and artificial.
- Trap: calling Lonar a volcanic lake, or the lake's name proving its class.
- Answer use: name the origin, then the example, then the evidence that supports it.

Mini recap:

```text
Round outline -> not enough
Volcanic: vent (crater) or collapsed chamber (caldera) -> lava and ash evidence
Impact: excavated bowl, shocked rock (Lonar, in Deccan basalt)
Solution: karst -> soluble bedrock (Topic 08)
Deflation: wind-scooped playa -> closed drainage, salt
```

Pause and retrieve: two round lakes sit in a basalt plateau. One has an overturned rim and shock features. The other has a constructional rim of ejected material. Which is which, and what settles it?
*Answer pointer:* The overturned rim with shock features marks an impact; the constructional rim of ejected material marks a volcanic crater. Rock evidence, not the outline, settles it.

Next, the same tests apply to lakes built by deposition, where a river or the sea does the sealing and the lake is often temporary.

```text
  river flows      neck narrows     river cuts through     abandoned loop
  around loop  ->  by erosion   ->  the neck         ->   is sealed by silt
```

A depositional lake is made by sediment, not by a hollow that is dug out. Something deposited material and cut water off from a channel or the sea, and water collected behind the deposit. This group is common in Indian coastal and river settings, and it is also the group most prone to filling.

The intuition is a dam built by the river and the sea themselves. A river bends, the bend tightens, the river breaks through the neck, and the loop is left behind. The sea builds a sand bar across a bay and encloses a lagoon behind it. In both cases the deposit does the work.

**Step 1: the meander tightens.** A river meander becomes more curved over time. Deposition on the inside of the bend and erosion on the outside bring the two sides of the loop together.

**Step 2: the neck breaks.** The narrow neck is breached, a straighter channel develops, and the abandoned loop is cut off from the main flow.

**Step 3: the loop is sealed.** Sediment blocks the loop's ends, and the sealed loop is an **ox-bow lake**. A step often missed comes next: the loop first becomes a backwater, fed only at high flow, and flood reconnection can keep the lake alive for longer or hasten its filling. The lake then fills with silt until it becomes a bog and dries. An ox-bow's lifespan depends on the flood regime, not only on the cut-off.

**Kanwar Jheel: an Indian ox-bow.** Kanwar Jheel, also known as Kabartal, lies in Begusarai district of Bihar, about 22 kilometres north-west of Begusarai town. It is a residual ox-bow lake formed by meandering of the Burhi Gandak, a Ganga tributary. The reference gives its area as 2,620 hectares, though another figure in the same reference gives 67.5 square kilometres, so the scale is uncertain. The wetland floods during the monsoon. It was designated a Ramsar site on 21 July 2020 according to the reference's infobox, and the Ministry is reported to have named it Bihar's first Ramsar site in November 2020. The 2023 Prelims item on rivers feeding lakes names Kanwar, and the item's Kanwar is this lake, a river-cut loop on a Ganga tributary in Bihar.

**Lagoons.** A **lagoon** is a shallow coastal water body separated from the open sea by a bar or spit. It is brackish where river water mixes with sea water, and its condition depends on the size and position of its mouth. A **barrier lake** forms when deposits or other obstacles block a drainage line, so water backs up behind the barrier. Lakes dammed by landslides or debris are often unconsolidated and lack a stable spillway, so they can breach suddenly once the dam erodes, which makes them short-lived.

**Chilika.** Chilika in Odisha is the best Indian lagoon. A barrier beach, the Rejhansa, formed by northerly currents in the Bay of Bengal, lies on its eastern side, and the lake is a shallow, bar-built estuary with large mudflats. Reference accounts describe it as the largest brackish lagoon with estuarine characteristics on the east coast of the subcontinent, and as Asia's largest brackish-water lagoon. It was among the first two Indian Ramsar sites, designated on 1 October 1981 together with Keoladeo National Park. Its Irrawaddy dolphin population is commonly described as the only known one in India. Its condition depends on mouth management, sediment, fisheries and freshwater flow, which can pull in different directions, so there is no single optimum for salinity.

**Pulicat and Kolleru.** Pulicat is an east-coast lagoon between Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, formed behind sand bars, and it shares Chilika's depositional logic. Kolleru, by contrast, lies inland between the Krishna and Godavari deltas and is a freshwater wetland, not a coastal lagoon. Placing the two correctly is the lesson: a coastal bar makes a lagoon, and an inland delta setting does not.

**Objection and reply.** A student may say a lagoon is simply a lake next to the sea. The reply is that the distinction is genetic: the lagoon is cut off by a deposit and mixes with sea water, so its salinity and ecology differ from a freshwater lake. Its management also differs, because the sea mouth is a control point.

**Unresolved residual.** Lagoon salinity varies seasonally and with monsoon inflow, so one salinity figure cannot describe a lagoon all year.

**UPSC application.**

- Prelims: the 2023 item on rivers feeding Wular, Kolleru and Kanwar tests the link between a lake and its drainage. Kanwar is the oxbow case taught above.
- Prelims: the adjacent 2023 item on repeated sea-level change forming extensive marshlands belongs to Topic 10 (Coastal Landforms), where coastal depositional morphology is taught.
- Trap: calling Chilika a tectonic freshwater lake. It is a depositional brackish lagoon.
- Answer use: name the depositional process, then the salinity and conservation consequence.

```text
Kanwar Jheel: residual ox-bow of the Burhi Gandak (Begusarai, Bihar)
```

Some lakes are built by people. This lesson asks how to recognise them, and how to deal with a name you have never seen. The method is a sequence of questions about what made the hollow, not about what the lake looks like now.

The intuition is that a human-made lake has a builder. A dam, a bund or an embankment is a deliberate structure, and its purpose is usually storage, irrigation, power or flood control. A natural lake has no builder: its hollow was made by earth movement, ice, volcanoes, meteorites, dissolution, wind or deposition.

An **artificial lake** is a water body created or substantially enlarged by human construction. A **reservoir** is an artificial lake behind a dam, storing water for release. A **tank** is a smaller artificial water body, often built to hold rain or runoff for irrigation, drinking or community use. The distinction matters because a reservoir's level is managed by release decisions, while a natural lake's level is set mainly by the water budget of Lesson 1.

**The test in steps.** First, look for a structure: a dam, barrage or embankment that impounds water. Second, check the setting: a village tank or a river dam. Third, check the geology: a cut-off meander, a coastal bar, a crater or a cirque. Fourth, ask whether the basin drains, and over what sill. A closed hollow with no sill and no outlet is a different case from a dammed one. If no structure exists and the geology shows a natural hollow, the lake is natural.

**Further clues.** Natural lakes differ from built ones in the form of their basin and their setting, and the structure of a reservoir, typically a straight dam line across a valley, is the clearest clue. Human and biological lakes also exist: mining voids, ornamental ponds and beaver ponds all hold water without a natural tectonic, glacial or volcanic origin. The test is designed for names you do not recognise, because it asks about process, not memory.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Hirakud Reservoir in Odisha is a large dam-formed reservoir on the Mahanadi. Its genesis is clear: the dam is the structure, and the lake exists because of it. The limit is that a reservoir's storage and outflow depend on operating rules and sediment inflow, so its current volume needs current operating data.

**Tanks.** The tank tradition in south India is a set of human-built water bodies that store rain and runoff. Some are very old, and many have been silted or encroached on. The lesson uses the tank as a category of artificial lake, not as a claim about any specific named tank.

**Objection and reply.** A student may say that all lakes are now affected by people, so the distinction is meaningless. The reply is that the question is origin. Human effects change the rate and form of a natural lake, but its basin still has a natural origin, while an artificial lake's basin has a built origin. The genesis test separates them even when human effects are present.

**Unresolved residual.** Some lakes were natural and were later enlarged by dams or embankments, so the test can give a mixed origin. In such cases, name both origins and say which controls the present level.

**UPSC application.**

- Trap: classifying a lake as natural because it is old or large.
- Answer use: apply the genesis test, name the structure or the geology, then state the consequence for the level.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available

CA found: none verified (search service unavailable, 23 October 2026)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
  SUMMER PROFILE OF A DEEP TEMPERATE LAKE            DEPTH (metres)

  WARM, LIGHT SURFACE WATER    EPILIMNION   (mixed by wind)     0
  --------------- THERMOCLINE (steepest temperature change) ----
  COLD, DENSE BOTTOM WATER     HYPOLIMNION  (cut off from air)  ...
  O2 falls with depth:  high at surface -> low near bottom
```

Limnology is the study of lakes as physical and biological systems. Lesson 1 told you how much water a lake holds and how long it keeps it. Limnology tells you what happens inside: how the water arranges itself into layers, how the layers mix, where the oxygen goes, and how nutrients drive productivity.

**Step 1: density sets the layers.** Water is densest at about 4 degrees Celsius, so warm water floats above colder water. A temperate lake's profile forms three layers. The **epilimnion** is the warm, wind-mixed surface layer. The **hypolimnion** is the colder, denser bottom layer, largely separated from the surface during stratified periods. Between them lies the **metalimnion**, or **thermocline**.

**Step 2: the thermocline is where the change is steepest.** The thermocline is defined by where temperature changes fastest, not by a fixed temperature. The density barrier can also come from salinity. A salinity-driven boundary is a **chemocline**, and a lake held permanently stratified by salt is **meromictic**.

**Step 3: the seasons break the layers.** Turnover is the seasonal breakdown and re-mixing of the layers. In temperate lakes the cycle is typically twofold: in spring the surface warms through the density maximum and the lake mixes, and in autumn cooling and wind break the summer layering and the lake mixes again. Lakes that mix twice a year are **dimictic**. Shallow lakes that mix often are **polymictic**. Lakes that never fully mix are **meromictic**, usually because salt keeps the bottom denser than the top. Many Indian lakes are shallow and mix often, so polymictic regimes are common outside the deep, high lakes. This is an inference from depth and climate, and it varies lake by lake.

**Step 4: summer starves the bottom.** Premise one: in summer the lid of warm water keeps the bottom water cut off from the air. Premise two: bottom organisms and decaying matter keep consuming oxygen. Premise three: nothing supplies oxygen to the bottom layer until mixing returns. Conclusion: the bottom of a stratified lake can become anoxic. Anoxia can also release phosphorus from the sediment, so a lake can stay degraded for some time after external loads are cut. When autumn mixing returns the water, it can recirculate those nutrients and cause an oxygen dip. Temperature is a causal control as well: warm water holds less dissolved oxygen and its organisms respire faster, so a warm spell can push a stratified bottom into anoxia sooner.

**Trophic state.** Trophic state is a lake's biological productivity, set mainly by nutrients. The usual classification has four categories. An **oligotrophic** lake is clear and low in nutrients, with plentiful oxygen near the surface and scarce oxygen at depth. A **mesotrophic** lake is intermediate, often clear with submerged plants.

A **eutrophic** lake is nutrient-rich, with heavy algal or plant growth. A **hypereutrophic** lake is extremely rich, with frequent severe algal blooms and low transparency.

Three indicators measure it: chlorophyll a, which tracks algal biomass most directly; total phosphorus, often the limiting nutrient in fresh water; and Secchi depth, the cheapest measure of clarity. The Carlson index combines them, and it works best where rooted plants and non-algal turbidity are minor.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Chilika's brackish, mixed, shallow water is a case where salinity and wind, not a thermocline, drive the oxygen regime. The limit is that this lesson gives the mechanism for stratified lakes and claims no measured thermal profile for any named Indian lake.

**Invasive species as a confounder.** A fish kill or a weed mat has several possible causes. Invasive plants can shade the water, consume oxygen as they decay, and change the community that a lake supports, so they can look like a nutrient problem or a drought problem. An answer that attributes a decline to nutrients should first rule in or out invasive growth and the other causes in Lesson 8.

**Objection and reply.** A student may say a lake is simply a bowl of water and needs no vertical analysis. The reply is that vertical structure controls oxygen, and oxygen controls fish and most of the biology. A single average measurement can hide a dead bottom layer.

**Unresolved residual.** The trophic category depends on the indicator chosen. Chlorophyll, phosphorus and clarity can disagree, so an answer should say which indicator it uses.

**UPSC application.**

- Limnology is rarely examined as a standalone fact. It underpins the eutrophication answers of Lesson 8 and the death mechanisms of Lessons 9 and 10.
- Trap: speaking of "stratification" without naming which layer lacks oxygen and why.
- Answer use: name the layer, the mixing state, the oxygen consequence and the nutrient consequence.

Mini recap:

```text
Warm surface floats; cold bottom is cut off in summer
Bottom O2 consumed, not replaced -> anoxia; phosphorus released from sediment
Autumn turnover -> O2 restored, nutrients recirculated
Trophic state: oligotrophic -> mesotrophic -> eutrophic -> hypereutrophic
Invasive growth can mimic nutrient stress: rule it in or out
```

Pause and retrieve: a lake is oxygen-poor at depth in late summer and oxygen-normal after autumn. What does turnover do, and why might nutrients spike briefly?
*Answer pointer:* Turnover mixes oxygenated surface water down and redistributes bottom water; the same mixing returns nutrients stored at depth to the whole water column.

- Water is densest near 4 degrees Celsius, so warm water floats above cold water.
- The epilimnion is the warm, wind-mixed surface layer.
- The thermocline is the zone of steepest temperature change, defined by gradient, not a fixed temperature.
- A salinity-driven boundary is a chemocline; a salt-stratified lake is meromictic.

**Question:** A deep temperate lake shows low oxygen near the bottom in late summer but normal oxygen throughout after autumn. Explain the sequence of events.

**Model answer:** In summer the warm epilimnion sits over a cold hypolimnion, and mixing is cut off. Oxygen in the bottom water is consumed by organisms and decaying matter and is not replaced, so oxygen falls. In autumn cooling and wind break the stratification and the lake mixes, bringing oxygenated surface water down and distributing the bottom water. That re-mixing restores oxygen throughout the lake and can briefly release stored nutrients.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume oxygen is evenly distributed in a lake. Its distribution is set by the layers and by the mixing that breaks them.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how stratification and turnover create seasonal oxygen stress in a lake, and what this implies for a lake's trophic state. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Stratification forms in summer when warm surface water floats above cold dense water, separating the bottom from the air. Organisms and decaying matter keep consuming oxygen in the hypolimnion, and nothing replaces it until mixing returns. Turnover in autumn breaks the layers and redistributes oxygen, but it can also recirculate nutrients that built up at depth. Trophic state, the lake's productivity, depends on nutrients and clarity. A nutrient-rich lake will have a larger oxygen demand from algal decay, so the stress is stronger. The measurement must name the indicator used, since chlorophyll, phosphorus and clarity may disagree. The conclusion is that oxygen stress is a seasonal, layered process that becomes severe when nutrients are high.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Summer stratification mechanism, 2 marks. Bottom oxygen demand and no resupply, 2 marks. Turnover as redistribution and nutrient recycling, 2 marks. Trophic state linked to oxygen demand, 2 marks. Indicator qualification, 2 marks.

```text
  TWO WAYS A LAKE GETS OLDER

  NATURAL SUCCESSION                   CULTURAL EUTROPHICATION
  (geological timescale)               (decades or less, human-driven)

  slow sediment + nutrient build-up     sewage, fertiliser runoff, detergents
          |                                     |
          v                                     v
  shallowing, marsh, then land          algal bloom -> shade -> decay -> O2 loss
                                                |
                                                v
                                        fish kill, marsh, loss of lake
```

Lakes age in two ways that are easy to confuse. Both fill the basin and both reduce the open water, but the speed and cause differ. Natural succession is slow, geological ageing. Cultural eutrophication is fast, human-driven enrichment. An answer that treats them as one process misses the key diagnostic question: what drove the change? A third process, water-budget shrinkage, reduces open water by losing inflow, and Lesson 9 takes it up. Succession removes space, eutrophication removes oxygen, and shrinkage removes water.

The intuition is the difference between a cup filling slowly with dust and a cup filling quickly with food scraps. Both fill the cup, but the second one rots. Eutrophication is the rotting. It happens when nutrients, mainly nitrogen and phosphorus, arrive faster than the lake can handle.

**Natural succession** is the slow ageing of a lake: sediment and nutrients accumulate over geological time, the lake shallows, plants spread, and the water body eventually becomes marsh and then dry land. It runs in the same direction as the sedimentary death of Lesson 10, but without human acceleration.

**Cultural eutrophication, step by step.** Excess phosphorus and nitrogen enter the water. Algae and some plants grow rapidly and bloom. Dense surface algae shade submerged plants. When the algae die, decomposers consume dissolved oxygen. Oxygen falls, and fish and other animals suffocate. The lake loses its ecology and, over time, its open water. Some bloom-forming algae also release toxins, so the damage can reach livestock and people.

**Where the nutrients come from.** Sewage arrives at a single pipe, and even conventional biological treatment can leave substantial nitrogen. Fertiliser, manure and septic leakage arrive spread across a catchment in runoff. Phosphorus binds to soil particles, so erosion carries it into the lake, and disturbed soils raise nutrient input to surface waters. Phosphate detergents were a major contributor in some countries until they were phased out, so the mix of inputs changes over time. Invasive growth and encroachment can add to the same symptoms, as Lesson 7 showed.

**Why urban lakes are the worst case.** Urban lakes combine several pressures. They receive concentrated sewage and stormwater. Their catchments are sealed by roads and buildings, so runoff arrives fast and dirty. Their inflow channels are often encroached or blocked, and their function as flood buffers is lost. Lake degradation and urban flooding are therefore two symptoms of one failure. The urban-settlement topic (Topic 28) covers the wider urban flooding and water-stress problem; this lesson keeps to the lake side.

**Restoration order.** The order matters, and it has five steps. First, diagnose the catchment and the hydrology. Second, intercept and treat the inflow, because if the nutrient input keeps arriving nothing else holds. Third, remove the nutrient-rich sediment that acts as a store of phosphorus. Fourth, restore the catchment buffer and the inflow channels. Fifth, add biological measures such as aeration or de-weeding. Aeration or weed removal without stopping the nutrient input is a recurring and expensive failure, because the nutrients return.

**Current anchor.** Sukhna Lake in Chandigarh was announced on 15 October 2026 as India's 103rd Ramsar site. One listing dates its designation to 16 March 2026, so quote it as the announcement date. A Ramsar designation recognises ecological value and creates obligations for wise use. It does not, by itself, stop sewage or runoff. Designation and restoration are different steps, and the restoration order still applies.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that eutrophication is just natural ageing sped up. The reply is that the mechanism and the timescale differ. Natural ageing is slow sediment build-up. Cultural eutrophication is driven by nutrient loads that the lake's natural outflow and buffers cannot handle, and it can cause fish kills within a single season.

**Unresolved residual.** Lakes differ in nutrient sensitivity. Some recover quickly once loads are cut, while others stay degraded because nutrients stored in their sediments keep being released.

**UPSC application.**

- The 2021 Mains GS-I question on the environmental implications of reclaiming water bodies for urban land (10 marks) is a policy question that Environment and Ecology covers, with its biosphere and Ramsar material. Use this lesson for the mechanism and restoration order, and that treatment for the reclamation argument.
- Trap: presenting aeration as a cure.
- Answer use: separate natural from cultural ageing, name the nutrient input, then order the restoration steps.

Mini recap:

```text
Natural succession: slow sediment build-up -> marsh -> land
Cultural eutrophication: N and P -> bloom -> shade -> decay -> O2 loss -> fish kill
Urban lake: sealed catchment + sewage + encroached inflow
Restore: diagnose -> intercept inflow -> remove sediment -> buffers -> biology
```

Pause and retrieve: an urban lake is aerated every summer, but its blooms and fish kills return. Which step has been skipped?
*Answer pointer:* Interception of the inflow: the nutrient input is still arriving, so aeration treats the symptom while the cause continues.

Revision notes:

- Natural succession is slow geological ageing of a lake basin.
- Cultural eutrophication is fast nutrient enrichment driven by human activity.
- Nitrogen and phosphorus drive algal blooms; phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in fresh water.
- Algal shading, decay and oxygen loss lead to fish kills.
- Sewage enters at one pipe; fertiliser runoff spreads across the catchment.
- Urban lakes combine sewage, sealed catchments, encroachment and lost flood buffering.

### Concept check

**Question:** An urban lake is aerated every summer, but its algal blooms and fish kills return each year. What does this tell you about the restoration order?

**Model answer:** Aeration treats the symptom, low oxygen, while the cause is the continuing nutrient load. Until sewage and runoff are intercepted and the nutrient-rich sediment is managed, the bloom re-forms and the oxygen demand returns. The correct order is to diagnose the catchment, stop the inflow of nutrients, remove stored nutrients, and restore the buffers first, with biological measures used as a supplement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat oxygenation or weed removal as the first step of a restoration. Without stopping the nutrient input, the same failure returns.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Distinguish natural succession from cultural eutrophication, and state the order of restoration for an urban lake. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Natural succession is a slow geological process in which sediment and nutrients accumulate, the lake shallows and becomes marsh and land. Cultural eutrophication is much faster and human-driven: sewage and fertiliser runoff add nitrogen and phosphorus, algae bloom and shade the bottom, decay consumes oxygen, and fish die. An urban lake suffers both pressures through sealed catchments and encroached inflows. Designating a lake as a wetland recognises its value but does not replace these steps.

```text
  MODE A: HYDROLOGICAL DEATH

  inflow falls (diversion, drought, abstraction)
        |
        v
  outflow + evaporation > inflow  --> level falls
        |
        v
  area shrinks --> salinity rises --> fishery collapses

        v
  exposed bed --> salt and dust storms --> health and farm losses

```

Lesson 1 gave the budget. This lesson shows what happens when the budget fails on the inflow side. This mode of death is usually driven by human decisions upstream, and its signature is that the lake shrinks and concentrates its salts.

The intuition is a bath with the tap turned off. The plug still lets water out, evaporation still takes water, and nothing refills it. The level falls, the water that remains becomes stronger, and the exposed edges change the land around. The cause is not in the bath; it is in the tap.

**The mechanism in five steps.** First, inflow falls: rivers are diverted for irrigation, rainfall declines, or groundwater is pumped. Second, outflow and evaporation now exceed inflow, so the level falls. Third, the area shrinks and the salinity of what remains rises. Fourth, the native fishery and vegetation collapse, and the exposed lake bed turns into a dust and salt hazard. Fifth, the water body may split, and the climatic moderation that a large water mass provided weakens.

**The Aral Sea.** The Aral Sea was fed by two rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya. From the early 1960s both were diverted to irrigate cotton in a desert basin. Reference figures put irrigation withdrawals at roughly double their 1960 level by 2000, with leaking canals adding losses.

The effects followed in order. The sea split in 1987 into a North and South Aral. Approximate reference figures put salinity at about 10 grams per litre rising to about 30 grams per litre by 1990, which ended the native fishery and stranded former ports far inland. The exposed bed carried salt and agrochemical residues as dust.

The northern remnant recovered in part after a dike completed in 2005 retained inflow, and its salinity fell toward about 8 grams per litre. The lesson does not quote area-loss percentages.

The analytical value of the case is the northern remnant. Partial recovery shows that the outcome was a policy choice about inflow, not an inevitability. That is the stronger conclusion, and an answer should carry it.

**The Lake Chad case.** Lake Chad is a shallow, endorheic lake. Its feeders, chiefly the Chari and Logone rivers, supply most of its inflow, and that inflow depends on rainfall in the Sahel and on upstream water use. Its shrinkage has several causes at once, and separating them is the analytical task.

Step one: drought. A multi-decade drought from the late 1960s reduced rainfall over the basin. Step two: abstraction. Irrigation diverted water from the feeders, so inflow fell even in years when rain was not the main limit. Step three: population and land use. Land degradation and population pressure added demand on water and land. Step four: the lake's response. Its open water fell sharply between 1960 and 1980, and later averages show the decline had slowed. Those dated figures describe the past, not the present.

Chad is the standard case in which shrinkage becomes a livelihood and security question, since more than thirty million people depend on the lake according to reference accounts. It is a multi-cause case, not a second Aral. Rainfall, abstraction and population must be separated before any conclusion is drawn.

**Endorheic sensitivity.** The general principle links Lesson 2 to this one. An endorheic lake has no outlet to buffer a loss of inflow, so reduced inflow shows immediately as level fall and salinity rise. Recovery therefore requires restoring inflow. Local measures can reduce losses, but the water must come from upstream.

**Objection and reply.** A student may say that drought alone explains shrinkage. The reply is that drought lowers inflow, but the scale of the Aral loss came from diversion, while Chad's loss came from drought and abstraction together. The cause must be separated site by site. The unresolved residual is that climate variability can make an already stressed lake collapse faster.

**The concept of shallow-lake change.** A wide, shallow lake and a deep lake can lose the same volume of water. The deep lake's level falls more for that volume, because it has less area to spread the loss. The shallow lake's level falls less, but its area can collapse faster per metre of level fall, because its floor is flat and each metre exposes a wide bed. Both effects matter, and Lesson 9's concept check asks which is which.

- Prelims: the 2018 item asked about shrinkage of the Aral Sea and Lake Baikal due to human activities. The Aral mechanism is taught here. Baikal is a deep tectonic lake, so any answer on its shrinkage must check its water budget rather than assume it.
- Prelims: the 2022 item on a West African lake drying into desert tests the closed-basin logic taught here.
- Trap: attributing all shrinkage to climate, or treating Chad as a second Aral.
- Answer use: name the budget term that failed, then the causal chain, then the policy lever.

```text
Endorheic: no buffer, so restore inflow
Aral: diversion-led; Chad: drought + abstraction + population
```

**Question:** Two lakes each lose 100 units of water in a year. One is deep with a steep, narrow basin, and the other is a wide, shallow lake with a flat floor. Which loses the larger share of its storage, and which loses area faster per metre of level fall?

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
  LIVE STORAGE vs DEAD STORAGE IN A RESERVOIR

  full level  ----------------------------  top of usable water
              |  LIVE / ACTIVE STORAGE     |  usable for release,
              |  (flood, power, supply)    |  flood control
  ------------+----------------------------+-----------------------
              |  DEAD / INACTIVE STORAGE   |  cannot drain by gravity;
              |  (silt settles here)       |  only pumped
  bed         +----------------------------+
```

Lesson 9 described a lake that dies because its inflow fails. This lesson describes a lake that dies because it fills. Sediment arrives with every flood, settles in the basin, and gradually takes the space that held water. The mechanism is slower than inflow failure, and in reservoirs it can be decisive.

The intuition is a tank that accumulates sludge at the bottom. The tank still holds water, but the usable volume shrinks each year. Sedimentary death does not remove the water. It removes the space, and with it the usable part of the water.

**Four terms.** **Siltation** is the settling of fine sediment in a basin, which reduces storage over time. **Dead storage** is the part of a reservoir's water that cannot drain by gravity through the outlet works, spillway or power intake, so it can only be pumped. It allows sediment to settle and gives fish a refuge when levels fall. **Live** or **active storage** is the usable part, released for flood control, power, navigation and downstream supply. **Trap efficiency** is the fraction of incoming sediment that a reservoir retains rather than passing downstream. Its value depends on the reservoir's shape, inflow and retention time, and no figure is given here.

**The argument, step by step.** Premise one: sediment arrives with inflow, and reservoirs trap a large share of it. Premise two: the trapped sediment settles in the basin, mainly near the inflow and at the bottom. Premise three: settled sediment reduces the volume the reservoir can hold above its outlet. Conclusion: siltation shrinks live storage over time, so usable capacity declines even when the dam stands.

**Which clock decides.** Every lake runs both the hydrological and the sedimentary clock at once, so the question is which is decisive. This is an analytical hypothesis, not a law, and its reason is worth stating. In a closed basin the hydrological clock has no brake, because nothing flows out and any loss of inflow goes straight to the level. In an open basin, outflow carries some water and some sediment away, so the sedimentary clock is more often the decisive one. The hypothesis fails where an open basin also loses inflow sharply, so it must be tested case by case.

**Lake versus reservoir.** The same process fills natural lakes more slowly. A natural lake's sediment accumulates until the basin is filled, shallowed or drained, which is the same direction as natural succession in Lesson 8, but without the nutrient driver. Dead storage in a reservoir is a built feature that anticipates this filling: the lower volume is designed to hold silt. The distinction matters because a reservoir's storage problem is an engineering and operating problem as well as a geographical one.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Indian reservoirs and tanks are widely affected by siltation, and many tanks have lost capacity through sediment and encroachment. The pattern is well established. The lesson gives the mechanism rather than any storage-loss figure, because such figures must come from current surveys.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a silted reservoir is still useful. The reply is that usable storage, not total water, is what matters for flood control and supply. A reservoir with much of its live storage filled cannot regulate floods as designed. The unresolved residual is that dredging, sediment management and upstream soil conservation all reduce siltation, but each has costs and limited reach.

**UPSC application.**

- Prelims: the 2022 item on major reservoirs and their states belongs to Contemporary Geographical Issues (Topic 36). Use this lesson for the mechanism and Topic 36 for the inventory and for reservoir submergence.
- Mains: a question on lake or reservoir shrinkage must name siltation as a second mode of death, separate from hydrological failure.
- Trap: calling all shrinkage hydrological.
- Answer use: name the sediment inflow, the settling zone, the lost storage and the operational consequence.

Mini recap:

```text
Sediment arrives -> settles in basin -> live storage shrinks
Dead storage: can only be pumped; holds silt and gives fish refuge
Trap efficiency: share of sediment retained
Siltation reduces usable capacity while the dam stands
Which clock decides: closed basin -> hydrological; open basin -> usually sedimentary (hypothesis)
```

**Family five: phumdi lakes.** Loktak Lake in Manipur is a freshwater lake whose surface holds phumdis, floating masses of vegetation, soil and decomposing organic matter. Keibul Lamjao National Park sits on the largest phumdi, and it is widely described as the world's only floating national park. It is the last natural refuge of the sangai, the Manipur brow-antlered deer, a subspecies of Eld's deer and Manipur's state animal. Loktak is stressed by the Ithai barrage and pollution, and the phumdis are thinning, which threatens the deer. Loktak appears on the Montreux Record, which Lesson 12 explains.


Wetlands sit at the boundary between geography and environmental governance. This lesson draws that boundary. Geography explains a wetland's form, water and origin. Environment and Ecology explains its biodiversity, legal regime and policy tools. Mixing them creates weak answers.

The intuition is a passport and a warning list. Being listed as a Ramsar site recognises that a wetland matters internationally. Being on the Montreux Record is an alert that the wetland's ecological character is under threat. The two statuses are different, and a site can carry both.

**Step 1: the Convention.** The Ramsar Convention was adopted at Ramsar, Iran, and signed on 2 February 1971; it entered into force on 21 December 1975. It is an intergovernmental treaty on the conservation and wise use of wetlands. A **Ramsar site** is a wetland formally designated under the Convention for its international importance. Designation is made against listing criteria, which cover representative or rare wetland types and support for threatened species and biodiversity.

**Step 2: wise use.** **Wise use** means maintaining the ecological character of a wetland while allowing sustainable human use. It does not mean strict no-use preservation.

**Step 3: the Montreux Record.** The Montreux Record was established in 1990 at the Montreux conference and adopted in 1996 at Brisbane. It lists Ramsar sites where ecological change has occurred, is occurring or is likely, because of development, pollution or other interference. Listing is an advisory signal, not delisting.

**Step 4: what makes a wetland a wetland.** Two physical drivers define wetland class. **Hydroperiod** is the duration and timing of inundation, and it sets the vegetation and soils. A lake that shallows and whose vegetation spreads loses open water, and its hydroperiod increasingly makes it a wetland. **Connectivity** is the surface and groundwater link between a wetland and rivers, the sea or other wetlands, and it controls how water and nutrients move through the system. Wetlands commonly provide four hydrological functions: storage of flood water, groundwater recharge, attenuation of flood peaks, and retention of sediment and nutrients. Connectivity decides how far each function reaches.

**The argument.** Premise one: a site becomes a Ramsar site when a state nominates it and it meets listing criteria. Premise two: a site's ecological character can change after designation. Premise three: the Montreux Record lists sites whose character has changed or may change, so it reports a threat and does not revoke the designation. Conclusion: a Montreux listing does not remove a site from the Ramsar List.

**India-centric examples and their limit.** Loktak Lake is a Ramsar site designated on 23 March 1990, and it appears on the Montreux Record, with a listing reported from 1993. Keoladeo National Park is also reported on the Montreux Record. The limit is that this lesson makes no claim about other Indian sites on the Record. Loktak and Keoladeo are the two Indian entries most often reported; for any other site, the Record's current list decides.

**Two pairs of terms to keep apart.** A Ramsar listing is an international designation of a wetland, while a National Park or sanctuary notification is a domestic legal status; one place can carry both. The Montreux Record is a list of ecological concern rather than a funding list, though funding may follow a listing. The Ramsar listing itself creates no domestic legal power.

**Wetlands Rules 2017.** India's domestic framework is the Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017. It provides for a National Wetlands Committee and state and union-territory wetland authorities. Its procedures and text are regulatory material. Lesson 12 names the instrument as a pointer, and the detail belongs to Environment and Ecology.

**Three statuses, one distinction.** A wetland can be notified under the 2017 Rules, which is a legal step. It can be designated as a Ramsar site, which is an international recognition. It can have a funded, implemented management plan, which is the only status that shows protection in practice. Funding for such plans is handled under the National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems, a route that Environment and Ecology treats in detail.

**The handoff.** Geography covers the lake or wetland's origin, its water budget, its hydroperiod, its connectivity and its landscape role. Environment covers biodiversity, the regulatory route, wise-use policy and restoration instruments. The 2018 Mains demand on Ramsar wise use with Indian examples, the 2023 Mains demand on the National Wetland Conservation Programme and India's Ramsar sites, the 2019 and 2022 Prelims demands on Ramsar and wetlands, and the 2026 Prelims demand on the Amur falcon and a community conservation site are all Environment-led. This lesson uses only the geographical boundary. The 2019 Mains question on mangrove depletion and the 2026 Prelims mangrove item belong to Environment and Ecology's coastal material and are cross-referenced only.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that Ramsar status means no human use is allowed. The reply is that wise use allows sustainable use, and designation recognises value and creates obligations rather than a ban. A second objection is that Ramsar protection is automatic in India. The reply is that domestic protection depends on national law and rules, which is why the Wetlands Rules matter.

**Unresolved residual.** How well a site's ecological character is maintained depends on governance and funding, which a geographical lesson cannot assess.

**UPSC application.**

- Use geography for the wetland's form and water, then hand over the policy to Environment and Ecology.
- Trap: writing a wetland answer that is only about Ramsar procedure.
- Answer use: define the wetland geographically, state its hydroperiod and connectivity, then name the governance layer and the Environment cross-reference.


## Lesson 13 - Answer architecture: directives, the 15-mark spine and mapwork

Progress: L13 / 13  |  Stage: Advanced  |  Subtopic: Decoding directives, building the argument and placing lakes on the map

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: none attempted for this synthesis lesson

```text
  FROM DIRECTIVE TO ANSWER

  "Discuss" / "Examine"   -> mechanism, evidence, two cases, qualification
  "Classify"              -> process first, then an example for each class
  "Explain why"           -> cause -> consequence -> limit
  "Distinguish"           -> criterion -> contrast -> example for each side

  15-MARK SPINE:  thesis -> mechanism -> two contrasting cases -> effect chain
                  -> India register -> balance -> graded conclusion
```

A lake answer is judged on whether it does what the question asks. Lessons 1 to 12 gave you the content. This lesson turns it into structure, because a well-taught fact placed in the wrong answer scores poorly.

The intuition is that the directive is the instruction about what to build. "Classify" asks for a sorting principle with examples. "Explain why" asks for a causal chain. "Examine" asks for an evaluation with evidence and a conclusion. Read the verb before writing a line of content.

**Decoding the directive.** Four common directives recur for this topic. For "classify lakes by origin", the skill is naming the process that forms the basin, for each class, with an example; a list of names without process fails. For "discuss the shrinkage of inland water bodies and its effects", the skill is the budget-failure mechanism plus effects on livelihood, ecology, climate and security; narrating one lake's story fails. For "why are lakes described as temporary?", the skill is the two natural terminations, infilling and outlet erosion, plus human acceleration, with the qualified statement of Lesson 1. For "examine the ecological value of wetlands and lagoons", the skill is services such as fisheries, flood buffering, groundwater recharge and biodiversity, with trade-offs, and it must not confuse a lagoon with a freshwater lake.

**The 15-mark spine.** The spine is a reusable argument structure, shown here for the shrinkage question. Its thesis states that shrinkage of major inland water bodies is rarely climatic alone; it is largely a diversion-and-abstraction outcome amplified by climate variability.

The next moves carry the argument. The mechanism explains a closed basin's water budget and why evaporation makes such a basin sensitive to inflow loss. Two contrasting cases follow: the Aral Sea as diversion-dominated and Lake Chad as drought plus abstraction plus population pressure.

The effect chain runs from area loss to salinity change, to fishery and biodiversity collapse, to exposed bed and dust, to lost climatic moderation, to livelihood loss, and where institutions are weak, to conflict. The Indian register then notes that lagoons and wetlands face sedimentation, encroachment, aquaculture conversion, catchment change and nutrient loading.

The balance says partial recovery is possible where inflow is restored, as with the Aral's northern remnant, and that not every lagoon is degrading. The conclusion is graded: inland water bodies are basin-scale outcomes, so protecting them is an upstream allocation decision.

**The two-evidence rule.** Every substantive claim needs two kinds of support: a named case and a mechanism. A claim that upstream irrigation can destroy a fishery is supported by the Aral mechanism. A claim that a lagoon's ecology depends on its mouth is supported by Chilika's salinity regime. A claim with a named case but no mechanism is a story, and a mechanism with no case is an assertion. Use both.

**Mapwork.** Place each lake with its family and region. In India, the Kashmir and Himalayan group comes first: Wular in Bandipora district of the Kashmir valley; Gangabal and Sheshnag in the Kashmir valley; Sanasar in Ramban district of the Jammu region; Naini in Kumaun, Uttarakhand.

The lowland and coastal group follows: Kanwar Jheel in Begusarai district, Bihar; Chilika in Odisha; Pulicat between Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu; Kolleru inland between the Krishna and Godavari deltas; Vembanad on the Kerala coast; Sasthamkotta in Kollam, Kerala.

The arid and peninsular group includes Lonar in Buldhana, Maharashtra; Sambhar in Rajasthan; and Pangong Tso in eastern Ladakh, an endorheic brackish lake only partly in India. Loktak in Manipur sits apart as a phumdi lake.

Outside India: Baikal in Siberia; Tanganyika in the East African Rift; the Caspian between Europe and Asia; Lake Chad at the junction of four countries; the Aral Sea in Central Asia; Lake Turkana in Kenya and Ethiopia. A misplaced lake signals a wrong argument.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a long list of lakes is enough for a lake answer. The reply is that the examiner wants the mechanism, the case and the consequence. A list without a mechanism is description, and description does not earn analytical marks.

**Unresolved residual.** Mapwork is only as good as the locations checked. Some Indian origins and figures in this set are reported rather than settled, and the answer should say so.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check A - budget to salt

**Question:** A lake has a short residence time, no outlet and a dry climate. Trace what happens when its inflow falls by half, from budget to salinity to ecology.

**Model answer:** Short residence time means the lake follows its inflow, so a halved inflow lowers the level quickly. With no outlet, the only losses are evaporation and seepage, and these now exceed inflow. As the level falls, the remaining water becomes more concentrated, so salinity rises. The shallower water warms and loses oxygen more easily, and the fishery and vegetation decline. Each step follows from the one before, and the closed basin removes any buffer.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not separate salinity from the budget. Salinity is the budget's consequence in a closed basin.

### Cumulative check B - origin to form

**Question:** Two round lakes are found in different settings. One sits in a high cirque and the other in a basalt crater. How would you decide their origins, and which would you expect to be deeper?

**Model answer:** The cirque lake is glacial, a tarn carved by a glacier, and its evidence is glacial form and setting. The basalt crater is an impact lake, and its evidence is shocked rock and an excavated bowl. Neither round outline settles the origin. Usually the basalt impact crater is the deeper of the two: it is an excavated bowl, while the cirque tarn is a small glacial hollow and is usually shallower. This is a general expectation with a reason, not a measurement for any named lake, and infill can reduce any basin's depth over time.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that a round lake is either glacial or volcanic. The rock and setting decide.

### Cumulative check C - artificial, natural and mixed

**Question:** A water body is held behind a dam that was built across a natural lake. How do you classify it, and what does the classification tell you about its level?

**Model answer:** It is a mixed case. The natural lake has a natural origin, and the dam is the built structure that now controls its level. The present level is governed by release rules, abstraction and sediment accumulation, rather than only by the natural budget. Both origins should be named, and the level analysis should follow the built control.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not force a mixed case into one category. Name both origins and say which controls the level.

### Cumulative check D - evidence discipline

**Question:** A student writes that "a lake shrank because of climate change" and cites no other factor. Identify the weakness and state the evidence needed to strengthen the claim.

**Model answer:** The claim names one cause without a mechanism or a case, so it fails the two-evidence rule. Shrinkage can come from diversion, abstraction, drought, sediment or nutrient loading, and these must be separated by site and period. The student needs a budget that names the failing term, a named case showing that term, and a second case or a qualification showing where climate is or is not dominant. Without that, the claim is an assertion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not attribute lake change to one cause. Separate the terms of the budget before concluding.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark practice 1 - evaluate a policy claim

**Question:** A state government argues that designating a lake as a Ramsar site will restore its water. Evaluate this claim.

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The claim confuses recognition with restoration. A Ramsar designation recognises international importance and creates an obligation of wise use, but it does not by itself add inflow, cut nutrient loads or remove sediment. A lake's water comes from its budget, so restoring water requires restoring inflow, controlling abstraction or reducing evaporation losses. The designation can help: it draws attention, reporting and resources, and it may support the funded plan that delivers the fix. But if the failing term is upstream diversion, a designation leaves that term untouched. The claim is therefore partly right about attention and wrong about mechanism.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Recognition versus restoration distinguished, 3 marks. Water budget as the route to restoration, 2 marks. Designation's real function named, 2 marks. Failing-term diagnosis, 2 marks. Verdict stated with its split, 1 mark.

## 10-mark practice 2 - the water budget

**Question:** Explain why a lake with a short residence time responds faster to a change in inflow than one with a long residence time.

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Residence time is storage divided by outflow. A lake with a short residence time has a small store relative to its outflow, so its water is replaced quickly and a lost inflow is not buffered. Its level follows inflow closely. A lake with a long residence time holds a large store relative to its outflow, so a shortfall draws the store down slowly and the level changes gradually. The contrast is in the buffer. The formula is an average under steady, well-mixed conditions, so the answer should note that real lakes have uneven flow paths.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Residence time defined, 2 marks. Short residence fast response, 2 marks. Long residence buffer, 2 marks. Buffer explained as stored water, 2 marks. Averaging qualification, 2 marks.

## 15-mark practice 1 - map, date and classify

**Question:** Place Wular, Kanwar Jheel, Chilika and Loktak on an outline map of India, state the origin of each, and explain why a Ramsar date must accompany any count of sites.

**Model answer, 15 marks:** On the map, Wular lies in Bandipora district of the Kashmir valley in Jammu and Kashmir. Kanwar Jheel lies in Begusarai district of Bihar, on the Gangetic plain. Chilika lies on the Odisha coast. Loktak lies in Manipur, in the northeast. Their origins follow the families. Wular is a tectonic lake in a valley setting, fed by the Jhelum. Kanwar Jheel is a residual ox-bow of the Burhi Gandak, a river loop cut off by deposition. Chilika is a brackish lagoon enclosed by the Rejhansa barrier beach, a depositional lake. Loktak is a freshwater lake whose phumdis are floating vegetation-and-soil mats, which is a biological and tectonic-basin case rather than a glacial one. The map must place each lake in its region and show that the families are scattered by process, not by state. The Ramsar date matters because designations accumulate: Chilika and Keoladeo were designated in 1981, Loktak in 1990, Kanwar Jheel in 2020, and a reference list dates the latest additions to October 2026. A count without its date mixes sites designated in different years, and cannot be compared. The qualification is that some reported dates, such as Kanwar Jheel's, rest on reference sources and should be confirmed before quoting.

**Rubric (15 marks):** Four lakes placed with state or district, 4 marks. Origin of each lake, 4 marks. Families scattered by process, 2 marks. Dated Ramsar designations used, 2 marks. Why a count needs a date, 2 marks. Qualification on reported dates, 1 mark.

## 15-mark practice 2 - compare and trace

**Question:** Compare a deep rift lake with a shallow wind-scooped playa, trace how each responds to a sustained 20 per cent loss of inflow, and state which one's shrinkage is more reversible.

**Model answer, 15 marks:** A deep rift lake such as Baikal or Tanganyika has a large volume and a steep, narrow basin, so a sustained 20 per cent inflow loss lowers its level slowly and its area only a little. Its residence time is long, so the store buffers the loss for years before the level shows much change. A shallow playa is a closed basin floor with a wide, flat bed. A 20 per cent inflow loss there lowers its level by little, but its area can collapse quickly per metre of fall, because each metre exposes a broad bed. Its salinity rises as evaporation concentrates its brine, and the dust and salt from its exposed floor are the main effect. The trace differs: the rift lake's effects arrive slowly and stay in the water, while the playa's effects arrive quickly and spread as dust. On reversibility, the working hypothesis is tied to inflow. If inflow returns, the rift lake's basin still holds its volume, so its level can recover. The playa's concentrated brine and exposed crust can persist after inflow returns, so its ecological recovery is slower. This is a hypothesis about the mechanism, not a measured rate, and it depends on how long the inflow loss lasted and on the basin's history.

**Rubric (15 marks):** Rift-lake response traced with buffer, 3 marks. Playa response traced with area and salinity, 3 marks. Contrast in speed and form of effect, 3 marks. Reversibility judged with a reason for each, 3 marks. Qualification tied to inflow and history, 3 marks.

## 20-mark practice 1 - integrate two lessons

**Question:** Integrate the lessons on stratification and on eutrophication to explain how nutrient loading becomes a fish kill in a deep lake, and why the restoration order must start with the catchment.

**Model answer, 20 marks:** Nutrient loading from sewage and fertiliser runoff enters a lake's surface water, where phosphorus and nitrogen drive algal growth. Dense algae shade submerged plants and, when they die, decomposers consume oxygen. In a deep lake, summer stratification is the amplifier. The warm epilimnion sits over a cold hypolimnion that is cut off from the air, so the oxygen consumed by decaying algae at depth cannot be replaced until autumn mixing returns. The bottom water becomes anoxic, and anoxia releases phosphorus from the sediment, feeding the next bloom. Fish confined to oxygen-poor layers cannot escape and suffocate, which is the fish kill. The sequence therefore runs from catchment loading, through surface productivity, to the layers that trap the oxygen deficit. The restoration order follows the chain. Aeration treats the bottom-layer oxygen, but if the catchment still delivers nutrients, the sediment keeps releasing phosphorus and the bloom returns. So interception and treatment of the inflow come first, then removal of the nutrient-rich sediment, then restored buffers, and only then biological measures. The qualification is that stratification matters most in deep lakes; a shallow, polymictic lake mixes often and may show oxygen stress without a stable layer, so the amplifier is depth and mixing, not stratification alone.

**Rubric (20 marks):** Nutrient source and algal chain, 3 marks. Shading and decay mechanism, 3 marks. Stratification as the amplifier, 4 marks. Anoxia and sediment phosphorus feedback, 3 marks. Fish kill as the endpoint, 2 marks. Restoration order justified by the chain, 3 marks. Shallow-lake qualification, 2 marks.

## 20-mark practice 2 - lake change, governance and the Indian register

**Question:** Examine the causes of degradation in India's lakes and wetlands, and evaluate the case for treating a Ramsar designation as an instrument of protection.

**Model answer, 20 marks:** Degradation in Indian lakes has several mechanisms that must be separated. Hydrological failure occurs when inflow falls through diversion or abstraction, lowering levels and concentrating salts in closed basins. Sedimentary filling reduces storage, as siltation fills the space that water occupies, and dead storage is designed to hold settled silt. Cultural eutrophication arises when sewage and fertiliser runoff enrich the water, so algal blooms shade the bottom, decay consumes oxygen and fish die. Urban lakes add sealed catchments, encroachment and lost flood buffering. Each mechanism has its own control: inflow, sediment, nutrient load and catchment management. Ramsar designation must be judged against this register. It recognises international importance and creates an obligation of wise use. It does not by itself cut nutrients, restore inflow or remove sediment. The Montreux Record is a sharper signal for sites whose character is changing, but it is an alert, not an enforcement tool. The strongest case for designation is that it draws attention, reporting and resources; its weakness is that protection depends on domestic law and on measures that address the failing term. The conclusion is that designation is a necessary signal but not a sufficient instrument. An effective response diagnoses the failing budget term and applies the matching control, using designation to secure attention and resources.

**Rubric (20 marks):** Hydrological mechanism, 3 marks. Sedimentary mechanism and dead storage, 3 marks. Eutrophication chain, 3 marks. Urban pressures, 2 marks. Each mechanism matched to its control, 3 marks. Ramsar as signal, not instrument, 3 marks. Montreux as alert, 1 mark. Graded conclusion, 2 marks.

# REMEDIATION

### Misconception 1 - the level depends only on rainfall

Learners often believe the level of a lake follows rainfall. The budget shows that outflow, evaporation, abstraction and residence time decide how a change in inflow shows up. Repair: draw the budget and name at least three terms before attributing a change to one cause.

### Misconception 2 - endorheic means dead or stagnant

A closed lake is not stagnant. It loses water constantly through evaporation and seepage, which concentrates salts and makes it sensitive to inflow change. Repair: say what the lake loses, and why the loss matters for salinity.

### Misconception 3 - a round lake is volcanic

Shape does not decide origin. Volcanic, impact, solution, deflation and glacial hollows can all be round. Repair: state the rock or setting evidence that would separate them, and use Lonar as the reminder.

### Misconception 4 - Chilika is a freshwater tectonic lake

Chilika is a brackish, barrier-enclosed depositional lagoon in Odisha, with its ecology controlled by the mouth. Repair: state its origin, its salinity cause and its management lever.

### Misconception 5 - a Montreux listing removes Ramsar status

A Montreux listing is a sub-list alert within the Ramsar List. The designation remains. Repair: state the two statuses separately and their different meanings.

### Misconception 6 - Ramsar designation protects the wetland by itself

Designation recognises value and creates an obligation of wise use. It does not cut nutrients, restore inflow or remove sediment. Repair: name the failing mechanism and the control that addresses it, then mention designation as a signal.

### Misconception 7 - all Himalayan lakes are glacial, and Wular is Peninsular

Himalayan and Kashmir lakes include tectonic, landslide-modified and glacial forms, and Wular is a tectonic Kashmir valley lake. Repair: classify by origin and setting, then state any uncertainty, as with Sanasar.

### Misconception 8 - the Ramsar count is a fixed fact

The count rises as designations accumulate. Repair: always give the count with its date and treat recent additions as moving.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

- **Budget and residence time:** storage change = inflows minus outflows; residence time = storage divided by outflow; lakes, wetlands, reservoirs and lagoons are separated by criterion.
- **Outlet and salinity:** a sill on the basin rim sets outlet status; salinity = salt mass over volume, raised by evaporation in closed basins; endorheic need not mean saline.
- **Origin:** tectonic (four basin shapes; Baikal about 1,642 m; Tanganyika about 1,470 m; Caspian a downwarped basin and largest by area); glacial (tarn, kettle, ribbon, rock-hollow, moraine-dammed, drift; Great Lakes deepened by ice); volcanic (crater, caldera); impact (Lonar, in Deccan basalt); depositional (ox-bow, lagoon, barrier, landslide-dammed); deflation (wind-scooped playa); artificial (reservoir, tank).
- **Indian cases:** Wular (tectonic, Bandipora, Kashmir valley); Naini (tectonic, landslide-modified); Sanasar (Ramban, origin uncertain); Gangabal and Sheshnag (Kashmir valley, glacial settings); Kailash-Kund and Sat Tal (high Himalayan, origin complex); Chilika (brackish barrier lagoon, Ramsar 1 October 1981); Pulicat (east-coast lagoon); Kolleru (inland freshwater); Kanwar Jheel (Burhi Gandak oxbow, Bihar); Sambhar (arid salt lake); Lonar (saline impact lake); Loktak (phumdis, Keibul Lamjao, sangai).
- **Limnology:** water densest near 4 degrees Celsius; epilimnion, thermocline and hypolimnion; thermocline is the steepest gradient; chemocline and meromixis are salt-driven; turnover mixes the layers; anoxia can release stored phosphorus; invasive growth can mimic nutrient stress.
- **Death and change:** hydrological death is inflow failure (Aral: diversion-led; Chad: drought, abstraction and population together); sedimentary death is siltation, which reduces live storage, and dead storage cannot drain by gravity. Which clock decides is an analytical hypothesis.
- **Eutrophication and restoration:** natural succession is slow; cultural eutrophication runs nutrients, bloom, shade, decay, oxygen loss and fish kill; restore by diagnosis, inflow interception, sediment removal, buffers, then biology.
- **Ramsar and governance:** the Convention was signed at Ramsar, Iran, on 2 February 1971; wise use permits sustainable use; criteria, hydroperiod and connectivity define class and reach; the Montreux Record (1990) is an advisory alert; Loktak designated 23 March 1990; the count is dated (103 as reported, latest designations 15 October 2026); governance under the Wetlands Rules, 2017, belongs to Environment and Ecology.
- **Answer use:** decode the directive; use the 15-mark spine for shrinkage; name a mechanism and a case for every claim; place each lake by family and region.
