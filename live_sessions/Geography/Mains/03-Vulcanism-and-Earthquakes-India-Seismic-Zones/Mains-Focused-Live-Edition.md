Two curves and three ways to make them cross. Everything in Lessons 2 to 7 is a consequence of which crossing happened, and of what the resulting melt was made of.

### 1. The question this lesson answers, and why it must come before any terminology

⚠️ Most treatments of vulcanism open with a list of volcano types. That is the wrong end of the causal chain, because a volcano type is an *outcome* and the examiner's better questions are about *mechanism*. The prior question is simpler and far more productive: **the Earth's interior is hot, but it is overwhelmingly solid — so why does any melt exist at all, and why does it exist in particular places?** An answer that can state the three routes to melting can derive the global distribution of volcanoes in Lesson 5 instead of memorising it.

⚠️ A second reason for this ordering is terminological hygiene. **Vulcanism is a process; a volcano is a landform; volcanic hazard is an outcome in a populated place.** Collapsing the three is the error that produces the one-sided disaster narrative which Lesson 7's demand explicitly penalises.

### 2. The heat budget

⚠️ Temperature generally rises downward, but the near-surface geothermal gradient varies regionally and cannot be extrapolated linearly through the mantle.

✅ *Vulcanism* derives from Vulcan and the island of Vulcano. ⚠️ Water vapour is normally the dominant volcanic gas; CO₂, SO₂ and other species vary by system. Gas content affects eruption dynamics and atmospheric impact.

⚠️ Earth's internal heat includes residual heat from accretion/differentiation and heat generated by radioactive decay. Their relative contribution is model-dependent.

### 3. Why hot rock is not molten rock — the point most answers skip

⚠️ A rock's melting temperature depends on pressure and composition. Under most mantle conditions the **geotherm** remains below the **solidus**, so the mantle is predominantly solid despite its temperature.

⚠️ Three principal routes bring mantle or crustal material across its solidus:

| Route | What changes | Where it operates | The volcanism it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Decompression melting** | Pressure falls during ascent | Ridges, rifts and some upwellings | Commonly basaltic; volume/style remain setting-specific |
| ⚠️ **Flux melting** | Slab-derived water lowers the mantle-wedge solidus | Subduction zones | Primary melt is commonly basaltic; differentiation, mixing and crustal interaction broaden arc compositions |
| ⚠️ **Heat-transfer/anatectic melting** | Hot magma or crustal thickening raises temperature | Crust above intrusions or in thickened belts | Commonly more silicic, but body-specific |

❗ The Himalaya is a continent-continent collision orogen, not an Andean-style active volcanic arc. After intervening oceanic lithosphere was consumed, Indian continental lithosphere underthrust Eurasia along the Main Himalayan Thrust. This generates major seismicity without a continuous subduction arc. Regional crustal melting or post-collisional magmatism is not thereby excluded.

### 4. From melt to landform: the vocabulary, separated once and kept separate

⚠️ A volcano is a surface landform produced where magma, gases and fragments reach the surface through vents or fissures. Storage and transport may involve several interconnected melt bodies rather than one simple chamber-and-pipe system.

| ✅ Term | What it denotes | The error it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **Magma** | Molten rock **below** the surface, with its dissolved gases still in solution | Using "lava" for a subsurface body, which makes every intrusive form in Lesson 2 unintelligible |
| **Lava** | Magma erupted at the surface | Treating subsurface magma and erupted material as identical |
| **Tephra** | Fragmental volcanic material ejected through the air | Calling every fragment “ash”; ash is a size class |
| **Vent / conduit / pipe** | The opening and the pathway connecting chamber to surface | Confusing the *opening* with the *crater*, which is the depression around it |
| **Magma chamber** | The subsurface reservoir where melt accumulates and evolves | Imagining a continuous column of liquid from the mantle to the summit |

⚠️ One further distinction earns marks and costs nothing: **the melt that leaves a chamber is not the melt that entered it.** Crystals settle out, the residual liquid becomes progressively richer in silica, and the chamber therefore evolves. That is why a single volcano can erupt effusively at one stage and explosively at another, and why "a volcano has a fixed eruption type" is false.

### 5. Viscosity and gas: the controlling interaction

⚠️ Mafic magma is generally hotter and less polymerised than felsic magma and commonly has lower viscosity. Felsic magma is commonly more viscous. These are tendencies, not eruption predictions.

⚠️ Silicate polymerisation raises viscosity, but temperature, crystals, dissolved volatiles, ascent rate and conduit geometry also matter. High viscosity can impede gas escape; explosivity depends on how gas exsolves and is retained, not on silica alone.

```text
   MAFIC / HOTTER / FEWER CRYSTALS  <---- interacting controls ---->  FELSIC / COOLER / CRYSTAL-RICH
   commonly LOWER viscosity                                          commonly HIGHER viscosity
   gas may escape efficiently                                        gas may be retained
        |                                                                  |
        v                                                                  v
   commonly EFFUSIVE                                                 greater explosive potential
   shields, flows, plateaux                                          domes, composite cones, calderas
```

### 6. Vulcanism as a process against volcanic hazard as an outcome

⚠️ One more separation, because the 2021 demand turns on it. **Vulcanism is a planetary heat-transfer process.** It builds crust, releases volatiles and creates landforms. A volcanic process can be hazardous without people present, but **risk and disaster depend on exposure and vulnerability**. The same eruption can remain a remote hazard or become a catastrophe in a dense valley.

❗ This is not a pious observation; it is the structural reason why Lesson 7 can argue a *balance* rather than list disasters, and why the examiner's phrase "environmental impact" must be read as including soil, geothermal energy, minerals and landscape, not only ash and death.

### 7. 🇮🇳 India hook — the Deccan is a plateau, not a cone, and the reason is in this lesson

🇮🇳 The **Deccan Volcanic Province** is a flood-basalt province built largely by repeated fissure-fed mafic flows rather than one central cone.

⚠️ Plateau-scale flow fields support low-viscosity mafic emplacement. They do not by themselves prove a plume or one melting route; source interpretation requires geochronology, geochemistry and geophysics.

❗ The relevant point here is emplacement form: repeated mafic flows built a volcanic plateau. Detailed Deccan stratigraphy requires its own evidence.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| | What it explains | What it does not explain |
|---|---|---|
| The geotherm-solidus relation | Why a hot planet is a solid planet, and why melting needs an event | Where the heat itself comes from — that is the partition between primordial and radiogenic heat, left unquantified here |
| The three melting routes | Why volcanoes occur where they occur, including away from boundaries | Why a particular volcano erupts in a particular year; eruption *timing* is a monitoring question, not a mechanism question |
| Magma rheology and gas | How composition, temperature, crystals and volatiles influence flow and fragmentation | Exact eruption timing or size |

**Strongest objection.** This is petrology, not geography. A General Studies candidate has no use for polymerised tetrahedra.

**Reply.** Rheology connects magma properties to flow distance, fragmentation and landform. The answer must preserve multiple controls rather than reducing eruption behaviour to silica alone.

⚠️ **Residual.** The three melting routes are the standard account and are not seriously contested, but the **relative contribution** of each to global melt production is model-dependent, and the depth and nature of mantle thermal anomalies remain actively debated — which is exactly the unresolved residual that Lesson 5 develops into an argument tree.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Volcanoes occur because the interior of the Earth is molten, and the molten rock forces its way out through weak points in the crust." Identify the errors and give a corrected two-sentence statement that would earn marks in a ten-mark answer.

**Model answer:** The mantle is hot but predominantly solid because its geotherm usually remains
below the solidus. Melt forms locally through decompression, volatile addition or heat transfer.
Fractures guide ascent but do not create melt. Buoyant partial melt may rise and evolve before
eruption; composition, temperature, crystals, volatiles, ascent and conduit conditions then influence
whether activity is effusive or explosive.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "hot" and "molten" as interchangeable merely because the interior is hot enough to flow slowly. Solids near their melting point deform over geological time without being liquid, and the distinction is what makes decompression melting at ridges and plume heads an explanation rather than a restatement.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Partial melting, not internal heat, is what makes a volcano possible." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Internal heat is necessary but insufficient for melting because the mantle solidus generally rises with pressure and most mantle remains solid. Melt forms where decompression, volatile addition or heat transfer carries rock across its solidus. Rising mantle can decompress beneath ridges, rifts or thermal anomalies; water released from a descending oceanic slab can lower the wedge solidus; and hot intrusions can partially melt cooler crust. The resulting partial melt is commonly buoyant, while composition, crystals, temperature and dissolved gases influence viscosity and eruption behaviour. Continental collision illustrates the distinction between rupture and melt: India’s underthrusting beneath Eurasia produces intense Himalayan seismicity, but the belt lacks the continuous oceanic-slab dehydration system that sustains an Andean volcanic arc. Local melting or magmatism is not impossible, so “no continuous arc volcanism” is more accurate than “volcanically silent.”

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 2 for explaining why the mantle is mostly solid; 3 for all three melting mechanisms and settings; 2 for partial-melt buoyancy; 2 for multi-variable rheology; 1 for the qualified Himalayan contrast. Deduct 2 for describing the mantle as molten and 1 for claiming collision makes melting impossible.

### 1. Why an intrusive lesson exists in a topic about eruptions

⚠️ Vulcanism includes subsurface emplacement as well as eruption. Much magma crystallises at depth; later uplift and erosion may expose intrusive bodies as landforms.

- ⚠️ Intrusive bodies can preserve part of an eruption’s supply system. Dyke swarms may record feeder fractures, but linking an individual dyke to a flow requires field, age and geochemical evidence.
- ⚠️ **Intrusions can become relief by inversion.** After exposure, preferential erosion of softer country rock may leave a resistant body as a wall, ridge, dome or upland. Present relief records resistance contrast and exhumation, not emplacement force alone.
- ⚠️ Intrusive names are useful only when attached to geometry, host relation and relative timing.

### 2. The sorting test, applied: concordant against discordant

| Form | Relation | Shape and emplacement |
|---|---|---|
| **Sill** | Concordant | Sheet along bedding or foliation |
| **Dyke** | Discordant | Sheet cutting pre-existing structure; may feed flows or sills |
| **Laccolith** | Concordant | Flat-based, domed intrusion that arches its roof |
| **Lopolith** | Concordant | Basin- or saucer-shaped intrusion |
| **Phacolith** | Concordant | Lens occupying a fold crest or trough |
| **Batholith** | Discordant | Very large, commonly composite plutonic body with no exposed base |

⚠️ Laccoliths dome their roofs; lopoliths are basin-shaped. Geometry must be established from mapped contacts rather than inferred from present relief alone.

⚠️ A phacolith records control by fold geometry. Its relation to folding must be tested with cross-cutting and contact evidence; shape alone does not prove strict contemporaneity.

### 3. Grain size, depth and the texture rule — the reason "plutonic" is not a synonym for "big"

⚠️ Crystal size generally increases as cooling slows. Depth matters, but body size, geometry, host temperature, composition and volatiles also affect texture.

❗ A single body can be coarse at its centre and fine at a chilled margin, so texture describes a sample rather than an entire province. A coarse plutonic rock at the surface also records emplacement at depth followed by exhumation.

### 4. From intrusion to landscape: inversion of relief, worked through

```text

   soft country rock           softer material is        resistant intrusion
   ####################        stripped preferentially   stands PROUD
   ####[ DYKE ]########           ####  ||  ####              ||
   ####[      ]########             ##  ||  ##                ||  <- WALL
   ####################               \ || /              ____||____
   dyke is BURIED and                  \||/              /          \
   topographically invisible            ||              /  country rock
                                                       /   lowered around it
```

⚠️ A less resistant intrusion may weather into a depression rather than positive relief. The correct formulation is therefore **resistance contrast**, not “igneous rock always makes high ground.”

### 5. The intrusive forms as evidence rather than as vocabulary

⚠️ A list of six forms is worth very little in a Mains answer. The same six forms, used as **evidence**, are worth a great deal, and three uses are available.

- ⚠️ **Magma mobility:** long thin sheets imply lateral propagation; doming records pressure against overburden.
- ⚠️ **Structural timing:** cross-cutting, folding and contact relations establish relative sequence.
- ⚠️ **Former volcanic systems:** plugs and radial dykes may preserve an eroded feeder architecture, but field relations must confirm it.

### 6. 🇮🇳 India hook — Deccan dyke swarms

🇮🇳 Deccan dyke swarms record important feeder pathways and regional extension, but not every dyke fed every preserved flow. Correlation requires geochemistry, age and field relations.

❗ Intrusive and extrusive bodies can be parts of one magmatic system, but a feeder relation must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| Members | Sill, laccolith, lopolith, phacolith | Dyke, batholith |

| Typical landscape expression on exhumation | Benches, domed uplands, basin-centred masses | Walls and ridges; eroded uplands over a batholith |

**Strongest objection.** These are names on a diagram. No Mains question has ever asked for a lopolith.

**Reply.** The forms matter when geometry is used to infer emplacement, host control and relative timing. A Mains answer needs one or two relevant forms, not a gazetteer.

⚠️ **Residual.** Intrusive geometry constrains magma viscosity and structural timing, but **not** absolute age: a dyke that cuts a fold is younger than the fold by an unknown interval. No radiometric date for an Indian intrusive body is inferred from geometry alone.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus:** "Salient features of world's physical geography" and the volcanic-activity element of the geophysical-phenomena clause (Mains GS-I, direct scope).
- **Verified linkage:** supplies subsurface context for volcanism and the **2018** plume demand. Intrusive bodies are not classified as products erupted at the surface; the **2024** objective product demand is treated independently in Lesson 3, without a key or verdict.
- **Probable framing:** intrusive forms appear in a Mains script as **one clause inside a larger answer**, most often as the feeder relation behind a named province. They are almost never the demand.
- **Traps:** saying the intrusion "pushed the land up" instead of invoking differential erosion; reversing the laccolith and lopolith geometries; treating "plutonic" as a synonym for large; assigning a coarse texture to a chilled dyke margin; quoting an Indian intrusive body's age or volume from memory.
- **Answer use:** Deccan dykes and flood basalts illustrate intrusive–extrusive linkage, subject to field and geochemical correlation.

Revision notes:

- Much magma crystallises below ground; exposed intrusions may become landforms through **relief inversion**.
- **The sorting test:** does the body **follow** bedding (**concordant**) or **cut across** it (**discordant**)?
- **Concordant:** **sill** (sheet along bedding) · **laccolith** (commonly flat-based with domed roof) · **lopolith** (broad basin/saucer shape) · **phacolith** (fold-hinge lens).
- **Discordant:** **dyke** (cuts structure; may feed flows or sills) · **batholith** (very large, commonly composite plutonic body with no exposed base).
- **Laccolith:** domed roof; **lopolith:** basin-shaped.
- A phacolith follows fold geometry; timing needs contact/cross-cutting evidence.
- Slower cooling generally gives larger crystals; depth is one of several controls.
- A coarse plutonic rock at the surface records **emplacement at depth plus exhumation**.
- ⚠️ Relief comes from **differential erosion of a resistance contrast**; the intrusion did not rise, the surroundings came down. A less resistant dyke weathers out as a **trench**.
- Evidential uses: magma mobility · structural timing · eroded feeder architecture.
- 🇮🇳 Deccan dyke swarms preserve feeder/fracture evidence; individual correlations require proof.
- In a 150-word answer, deploy only the forms relevant to the mechanism.

### Concept check

**Question:** A dyke stands as a wall several metres above the surrounding country, and in the same district a sill forms a flat bench. A candidate concludes that "the dyke was intruded with greater force than the sill." Explain why the conclusion does not follow, and state what the two landforms actually record.

**Model answer:** Present relief reflects differential erosion, not emplacement force. A dyke is
discordant because it cuts host structure; a sill is concordant because it follows bedding or
foliation. If either body resists erosion better than its host it may stand as positive relief; if
less resistant it may form a depression. The wall and bench therefore record geometry, host control
and resistance contrast, not the pressure of intrusion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume present relief directly records emplacement force. Intrusions can deform or fracture their hosts, but an exposed ridge or dome commonly reflects later differential erosion; establish the relation from contacts and structure.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Distinguish concordant from discordant intrusive bodies, and explain what each type reveals about the magma and the rocks it entered. *(Distinguish and explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Intrusive bodies are classified by their contact with host structure. A sill follows bedding or foliation, whereas a dyke cuts across it. Laccoliths are concordant, commonly with a domed roof; lopoliths are broad basin- or saucer-shaped bodies; phacoliths occupy fold-related hinge zones, but their timing relative to folding must be established from contacts and cross-cutting evidence. Batholiths are large composite plutonic bodies whose full depth is not exposed. Geometry therefore reveals emplacement pathways and relative sequence, not a compulsory magma composition or absolute age. Later erosion may expose a resistant intrusion as a ridge or dome, while a less resistant body may form a depression. A defensible answer links each name to contact geometry, host deformation and erosion rather than treating the present landform as the original intrusive shape.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 3 for the concordant/discordant criterion; 3 for sill, laccolith, lopolith and phacolith geometry; 2 for dyke and batholith; 1 for erosion/relief inversion; 1 for the relative-age limitation.

### 1. Product inventory

Pumice and scoria are vesicular products, but density and composition vary. A **pyroclastic density
current** is a hot, ground-hugging particle–gas current, not a drifting cloud. A **lahar** needs water
and loose volcanic debris, not a contemporaneous eruption.

### 2. Fissure versus central vent

Vent geometry helps explain form, but central vents can build shields as well as explosive cones,

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the same volcano produce an effusive lava flow in one phase and an explosive ash-rich phase in another?

**Model answer:** Eruption style changes when magma composition, temperature, crystal content,
volatile content, ascent rate or conduit conditions change. Lower-viscosity magma may allow gas to
escape and erupt mainly as lava. Cooling, crystallisation, magma mixing or rapid ascent can increase
viscosity or gas overpressure, causing fragmentation, ash columns and pyroclastic density currents.
The volcano therefore has no permanent eruption type; the mechanism and products must be described
for the dated episode.

**Misconception to avoid:** Silica alone does not determine explosivity, and arc location alone does
not prove a named eruption style.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain why eruption products are diagnostic of mechanism.
*(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Eruption products record how magma moved, degassed and fragmented.
Lava flows indicate coherent melt reaching the surface; their geometry reflects rheology, effusion
rate, slope and vent arrangement. Tephra—ash, lapilli, blocks and bombs—records fragmentation and
atmospheric transport. Pumice and scoria preserve vesiculation. Pyroclastic density-current deposits
record rapid, ground-hugging particle–gas transport, while lahars record later remobilisation by
water. Gas and aerosol evidence links the eruption to local toxicity and possible atmospheric
effects. Product assemblages therefore reveal process more reliably than a single named eruption
style, but interpretation still needs field relationships, grain-size, chemistry and chronology.

### 1. Reading the family as a two-variable grid, not a list

⚠️ Vent geometry and magma rheology are major controls, but eruption rate, duration, edifice failure and erosion also modify form.

| | **Central vent** | **Fissure** |

### 2. The crater-against-caldera distinction, with its mechanism

⚠️ Candidates lose this mark by defining both as "a depression at the top of a volcano". The distinction is **causal**, not dimensional.

- ⚠️ A **crater** is a vent-centred depression produced by eruption, excavation or small-scale collapse; diagnose it from its relation to the vent and deposits.
- ⚠️ A **caldera** is a structural collapse depression formed after substantial magma withdrawal; it may later contain a lake.

❗ Caldera formation does not end a volcanic system; later vents or cones can develop within the depression. Avoid copying historic height and casualty tables into a landform answer.

### 4. Active, dormant and extinct — the correction that must be made explicitly

⚠️ Active, quiescent/dormant and extinct are **revisable evidence-based assessments**, not stages every volcano passes through. Historic silence alone cannot establish extinction; use a dated volcanological authority for a named volcano.

### 1. The three settings — the organising frame for the whole distribution question

⚠️ Most volcanism occurs at subduction or spreading boundaries; some occurs within plates. Three settings organise the comparison:

| # | ✅ Setting | ✅ Mechanism of melting (Lesson 1) | ✅ Named cases |
|---:|---|---|---|

❗ Settings 1 and 2 are boundary phenomena; hot spots may be intraplate. The plume hypothesis is one leading explanation, not a universal remainder category.

### 2. Defining a mantle plume, precisely enough to answer "define and explain"

⚠️ In the plume hypothesis, anomalously buoyant mantle rises beneath the lithosphere. A broad head and narrower tail are model components, not directly observed universal anatomy.

- ⚠️ A broad **head** may contribute to large-volume magmatism; a narrower **tail** may sustain longer-lived volcanism.

⚠️ Relative plate/hot-spot motion can produce age-progressive chains. Hot spots can move, plume conduits can bend and lithospheric structure can redirect melt, so the frame is approximate.

### 3. The diagnostic signature — how you would recognise a plume if you were handed a map

| Feature | ⚠️ Boundary volcanism | ⚠️ Plume / hot-spot volcanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Ridge axis; trench-arc systems | Can be in a **plate interior**, far from any boundary |
| **Lava character** | Commonly basaltic at ridges; broad compositional range at arcs | Commonly basaltic, not universally |
| **Eruption style** | Setting- and magma-dependent | Often effusive, with case-specific exceptions |
| **Spatial signature** | Belt follows boundary | A coherent age trend may support relative hot-spot motion |

❗ Alignment alone proves nothing. A coherent age progression is stronger evidence, but multiple vents may remain active and the trend need not be perfectly monotonic.

### 4. The role in plate tectonics — the half of the 2018 demand that candidates lose

⚠️ The 2018 directive is *define and explain its role in plate tectonics*, which means the answer cannot stop at anatomy. Three roles are available and all three are defensible.

- ⚠️ **Relative reference frame.** Age progression can constrain relative plate/hot-spot motion, subject to hot-spot mobility.
- ⚠️ **Chain bends.** A bend may reflect changed plate motion, hot-spot motion or both; independent evidence is needed.
- ⚠️ **Break-up association.** Some large igneous provinces coincide with rifting, but temporal association does not by itself establish plume causation.

❗ A plume is not a complete theory of plate motion. Plate dynamics involve slab pull, ridge push, gravitational potential and mantle flow.

### 5. The objection, the reply, and the residual that must be conceded

⚠️ The plume hypothesis is leading but contested; shallow decompression related to lithospheric cracking or edge-driven convection can also generate intra-plate melt.

- **Objection.** Intra-plate volcanism can be produced without any deep plume: **lithospheric cracking** under far-field stress, and convection driven by the edges of thick continental lithosphere, both generate melt at shallow depth. If a shallower mechanism suffices, a deep plume is unnecessary.
- **Reply.** Plume support strengthens when independent geochemical, geochronological, geophysical and topographic evidence converges. No single map pattern is decisive.
- ⚠️ **Unresolved residual.** Plume source depth, width, longevity and even the number of genuine plumes remain **model-dependent and disputed**. Therefore no fixed plume depth, chain age-progression rate or eruption volume is supplied from memory; stating that uncertainty demonstrates method rather than evasion.

### 6. 🇮🇳 India hook — plume-based reconstructions of Indian flood basalts

⚠️ Deccan–Réunion and Rajmahal–Kerguelen links are widely used reconstructions. Flood-basalt emplacement is established; a specific plume-head cause remains a tested interpretation.

❗ Do not use these associations as proof that every large igneous province records a plume head. State the hypothesis and the alternative lithospheric controls.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| | What the three-setting frame explains | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary settings 1 and 2 | The overwhelming majority of the world's volcanoes, and why arc volcanism is explosive while ridge volcanism is effusive | Nothing about volcanism in plate interiors |
| Hot spot, setting 3 | Some intra-plate volcanism and relative motion recorded by age trends | Whether a deep plume, shallow process or combination feeds it |
| All three together | The **global pattern** of volcanism as a derived result rather than a memorised map | The **timing** of any individual eruption, which no distribution theory predicts |

**Strongest objection.** The plume hypothesis is contested, so teaching it risks teaching something that may be superseded.

**Reply.** The opposite is true, and this is a transferable examination point. ⚠️ A hypothesis that is **standard, powerful and openly contested** is the most valuable kind of content a candidate can hold, because it permits an answer that **states a position, supplies its evidence, names its objection and concedes its residual** — which is the structure every "critically examine" and "discuss" directive rewards. ❗ What would be indefensible is to teach the hypothesis as settled fact, or to omit it because it is debated. The correct move is the argument tree.

⚠️ **Residual.** The three-setting frame is a **classification of settings, not a prediction of events**: it tells you where volcanoes are likely to be, never when one will erupt. ❗ Lesson 8's parallel statement about earthquake prediction is the same discipline applied to the seismic strand.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two volcanic chains of similar length and similar alignment are mapped. In the first, every volcano has erupted within the past two thousand years. **Model answer:** Simultaneously active volcanoes suggest a boundary belt. An age-progressive chain
supports relative plate–hot-spot motion, but does not alone prove a deep plume. Direction and rate
can be estimated from age and distance only after allowing for hot-spot motion, conduit deflection
and age uncertainty. A flood-basalt province is supporting, not decisive, evidence. Alignment alone
cannot distinguish the two origins.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use linearity as the test. Both boundary belts and hot-spot chains are linear, and reading alignment as evidence of a plume is the error that produces a plume wherever volcanoes form a line. **Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Define a mantle plume and explain why hot-spot chains are more useful to plate tectonics than arc volcanoes are. *(Define and explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** A mantle plume is a hypothesis of buoyant upwelling associated with a thermal anomaly. If volcanism becomes progressively older away from the active centre, relative motion between lithosphere and melting anomaly can help reconstruct plate direction and rate. Such a chain is useful because it supplies evidence independent of magnetic stripes and plate-boundary geometry. However, hot spots may move, age progressions are not universal, and some intraplate volcanism may arise from lithospheric extension or shallow mantle heterogeneity. Deccan–Réunion and Rajmahal–Kerguelen are therefore reconstructions supported to varying degrees by age, geochemical and plate-motion evidence, not proof by map alignment alone. ### 1. What the directive actually asks, and the trap in the word "characteristics"

⚠️ The 2020 demand asks for the geophysical characteristics of the Circum-Pacific Zone. A count of volcanoes is only a statistic; the answer must explain architecture—trench, plate interface, inclined seismicity, volcanic arc and their variable associated features.

⚠️ The second trap sits in the word *zone*. The Circum-Pacific is not a single boundary; it is a **ring of convergent margins of several different kinds** — oceanic-oceanic in the western Pacific, oceanic-continental along the Americas, with transform segments interrupting it. ❗ An answer that treats it as one uniform subduction line loses the comparative mark that distinguishes the Andes from the Japanese arc.

### 2. The architecture, element by element

| Element | ⚠️ What it is | ⚠️ Why it is a geophysical characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| **Trench** | The deepest bathymetric expression of the plate boundary, where the oceanic plate begins to descend | It fixes the surface trace of the boundary, and its depth records the age and density of the descending plate |
| **Fore-arc / accretionary wedge where developed** | Sediment may be accreted, subducted or bypassed depending on margin type | Distinguishes accretionary from erosive margins |
| **Volcanic arc** | The line of volcanoes above the point where the slab reaches melting-relevant depth | **Flux melting** (Lesson 1) makes the magma volatile-rich, so the arc is **explosive and more silicic** than a ridge |
| **Wadati-Benioff zone** | An **inclined zone of earthquake foci** dipping away from the trench beneath the arc | It helps delineate the descending slab; focal depth generally increases landward, with segment-specific limits |
| **Back-arc basin where developed** | Extension behind some arcs | Convergence does not require compression everywhere in the overriding plate |

⚠️ **The focal-depth gradient deserves emphasis, because it is the evidence and not the inference.** Earthquake foci beneath an arc are **shallow near the trench, intermediate beneath the fore-arc, and deep beneath and behind the arc**, defining a dipping surface that can be contoured. ❗ Nothing else in geophysics images a descending slab so directly, and an answer that names the inclined seismic zone and explains what it is evidence *for* has done the work the directive asks.

⚠️ **Why the belt is also tsunamigenic** — the link that Lessons 11 and 12 will make quantitative. Megathrust slip on the shallow part of the plate interface produces **vertical displacement of the sea floor beneath deep water**, which is exactly the condition for generating a tsunami. ❗ The same architecture that produces the volcanoes produces the tsunami source; they are not two facts about one place, they are two outputs of one mechanism.

⚠️ Arc magmatism and hydrothermal circulation can form important mineral systems, especially porphyry and epithermal deposits. Occurrence is deposit-specific; a generic arc does not guarantee every metal.

### 3. Distribution evidence without obsolete counts

The Circum-Pacific margin contains the world's most extensive concentration of subduction-related
volcanic arcs and great earthquakes. Volcano and earthquake percentages vary with definitions,
catalogue period and network sensitivity; mechanism is more reliable than memorised shares.

Three snapshots and one caption. The sequence is worth drawing in an answer because it makes two definitions — focus and epicentre — fall out of the mechanism instead of being asserted.

### 1. Elastic rebound and source vocabulary

⚠️ A tectonic earthquake is the sudden release of stored elastic strain when a fault ruptures or slips. Seismic waves radiate from the source region; a rupture may extend over an area rather than one mathematical point.

⚠️ Strain accumulates slowly and releases rapidly. Existing faults commonly reactivate, but earthquakes can also reveal previously unmapped structures.

✅ The **hypocentre/focus** is the subsurface source location used in an earthquake solution; the **epicentre** is vertically above it. Rupture dimensions, directivity and depth influence shaking.

### 2. The four causal classes — including the one candidates omit

| Class | Mechanism | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Tectonic** | Fault loading and rupture from regional plate/intraplate stress | Dominates global seismic moment release |
| **Volcanic** | Magma/fluid movement, pressure change and fracture | Seismic unrest is one monitoring signal, never a sufficient eruption forecast |
| **Collapse/explosion** | Mine collapse, blasting or explosions | Usually local and much smaller than major tectonic events |
| **Induced/triggered** | Reservoirs, fluid injection/extraction or mining alter stress/pore pressure | Human action may advance failure on a pre-stressed fault; attribution is case-specific |

❗ Volcano monitoring combines seismicity with deformation, gas, thermal and visual observations. Seismic unrest alone neither guarantees nor times an eruption.

⚠️ Reservoir loading and pore-pressure diffusion can alter stress and effective normal stress. Use **reservoir-triggered/associated**, not a claim that the dam created all strain.

### 3. Primary and secondary consequences

Primary effects include shaking, permanent displacement and surface rupture. Secondary effects include
landslides, liquefaction, fire, dam/lifeline failure and tsunami. Fukushima's reactor-core damage
followed tsunami-driven loss of power and cooling, not direct ground shaking alone.

⚠️ **Three of these six are secondary hazards**, i.e. the earthquake triggers something else that does the damage: fire, landslide and tsunami. ❗ An answer that separates **primary ground motion** from **secondary triggered hazards** is immediately more structured than one that lists them together — and it sets up Lesson 13, where ground failure is developed in full, and Lesson 11, where the tsunami branch is taken.

⚠️ Shaking can alter groundwater levels, spring discharge and permeability, but such changes are not reliable deterministic precursors.

### 4. Catalogue discipline

Recorded-event counts depend on network coverage, magnitude threshold and processing. Historical
casualty registers from old textbooks are excluded; use named modern agency/event sources when a
number is analytically necessary.

### 5. Prediction, forecasting and early warning

- **Deterministic prediction:** a reliable advance time, place and size is not available.
- **Forecasting:** probabilities can be estimated for long-term hazard and aftershock sequences.
- **Earthquake early warning:** detects rupture after it begins and may provide seconds before stronger
  shaking reaches more distant sites. In India it remained **pilot-phase** as of MoES/PIB Release
  2298817 on 13 August 2026.
- **Tsunami warning:** evaluates earthquake and sea-level observations after a source event; it is a
  separate system taught at Lesson 12.

### 6. 🇮🇳 India hook — Koyna 1967 as reservoir-associated seismicity

🇮🇳 Koyna 1967 is widely studied as reservoir-triggered/reservoir-associated seismicity. Impoundment
began earlier in the decade, but temporal association alone does not prove a single mechanism.

- ⚠️ The case demonstrates that stable continental regions are not aseismic.
- ⚠️ Loading and pore-pressure diffusion may advance failure on existing faults.
- ⚠️ Attribution requires spatial, temporal, depth and reservoir-level evidence; use “associated” or

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| Typical location | Plate boundaries and intraplate faults | Volcanic plumbing systems | Near reservoirs, injection/extraction or mines |
| Timing | Not deterministically predictable | May accompany unrest before/during/after eruption | May correlate with operation, subject to proof |

**Strongest objection.** The anthropogenic class is marginal. Human activity cannot move a plate, so listing it alongside plate tectonics inflates its importance.

**Reply.** Induced seismicity is a small share of global energy release but is policy-sensitive and

⚠️ **Residual.** Attribution of any event to human activity is **statistical and contested**; the strongest cases combine timing, location and depth with an engineering operation. Use “associated” or “triggered”, not automatically “proved”.

The trace is the evidence and the three circles are the method. Draw both and the location of an earthquake stops being a mystery and becomes an arithmetic exercise.

### 1. Distinguishing hazard use from internal-structure use

❗ Seismic waves serve two distinct analytical purposes. In crustal structure they reveal internal state and boundaries; here they are treated as ground-shaking agents and as location tools. The physics is identical; the question asked is different.

⚠️ This lesson therefore **assumes** the internal structure, states the assumption once, and spends its space on the three things the hazard topic needs: **what each wave does to the ground**, **how the record is read**, and **how an event is located**. ❗ The shadow zones appear here only in the single sentence that explains why a distant station may record no S arrival at all — because a candidate reading a seismogram must know that a missing phase can be a geometry effect rather than an instrument failure.

### 2. The four wave families

| ✅ Wave | ✅ Nature | ✅ Travels through | ⚠️ Particle motion | ⚠️ Damage role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P (primary)** | **Compressional**; fastest body wave | Solids and fluids | Push-pull along propagation | Damage contribution varies with source and site |
| **S (secondary)** | **Shear**; slower body wave | Materials with shear rigidity | Motion perpendicular to propagation | Often strong structural shearing |
| **Love** | Surface wave | The near-surface layer | **Horizontal, transverse** to the direction of travel | **High** — horizontal shearing is what most buildings resist worst |
| **Rayleigh** | Surface wave | The near-surface layer | **Rolling, elliptical and retrograde**, like a sea wave | **High** — long period, large amplitude, slow decay |

⚠️ Three consequences follow.

- ⚠️ **The order of arrival is fixed by physics, not by convention.** P is fastest because a compressional disturbance travels through any material, including one with no shear strength; S is slower because shear requires rigidity; surface waves are slowest because they are confined to the surface and are dispersive.
- Surface waves are often highly damaging because of amplitude, duration and period, but near-source
  body waves and rupture directivity can dominate at a particular site.
- S waves cannot propagate through a true fluid with zero shear modulus. Liquefied soil is instead a
  granular–water mixture with reduced effective stress and shear stiffness; it can still transmit
  attenuated shear motion and must not be equated with the liquid outer core.

⚠️ **Shadow zones, in one sentence.** P waves are strongly refracted at the core boundary and S waves do not traverse the liquid outer core, so a distant station may legitimately record P but no direct S; the S-wave shadow is larger.

### 3. The S-P interval — the derivation that makes location possible

⚠️ The method is simple enough to derive in an answer, and deriving it is worth more than asserting it.

```text
   Let the station be at distance d from the focus.
   Let the P-wave speed be vP and the S-wave speed be vS, with vP > vS.

   Travel time of P  =  d / vP
   Travel time of S  =  d / vS

   S MINUS P INTERVAL  =  d/vS - d/vP  =  d * ( 1/vS - 1/vP )
                                            \_____ a constant _____/
                                              for a given path

   ==> The S-P interval is DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL to distance.
   ==> Measure the interval on the trace, read off d. ONE NUMBER, ONE CIRCLE.
   ==> But a circle is not a point. Hence THREE OR MORE STATIONS.
```

⚠️ A single station and travel-time curve constrain distance. Three stations can estimate an
epicentre in the simplified circle construction; robust hypocentre/depth/origin-time solutions use
multiple stations and phases.

⚠️ Published locations depend on the velocity model and carry horizontal/depth uncertainty. Initial
solutions can change as more stations and phases are processed.

### 4. What the record cannot do

⚠️ Three limits, stated because they are the honest residual and because each is examinable.

- ❗ A seismogram records rupture after it begins. EEW can exploit faster communication and P-wave
  detection to provide seconds of lead time at sufficiently distant sites; warning time may be zero
  near the source.
- ❗ Multi-station wave records support estimates of location, depth, origin time and size; they do not determine damage. Loss also depends on site conditions, exposure and vulnerability.
- ❗ **Detection counts depend on network sensitivity and year.** A denser, more sensitive network can record more events without any change in Earth’s behaviour.

### 5. Concept foundation for the objective demand

⚠️ **2023 Prelims Q65** concerns P- and S-wave properties. No statement verdict or option mapping appears.

⚠️ Hold the properties as contrasts: P is compressional and normally faster; S is shear and requires rigidity; P particle motion is parallel to propagation while S motion is transverse; both are body waves. Love and Rayleigh waves travel near the surface, arrive after body waves in a typical record and are often highly damaging, but no wave type is universally dominant at every site.

**Question:** One station records P and S arrivals; a distant station at a core-crossing angular distance records P but no direct S. What can the first station determine, and what can explain the missing S?

**Model answer:** The P–S interval at one station constrains source distance through an assumed
travel-time model, but not direction. Several stations are required: three-circle triangulation is a
simplified epicentre method, while a full hypocentre/depth/origin-time solution uses multiple phases
and a velocity model. At a genuinely core-crossing angular distance, direct S is absent because the
liquid outer core has no shear rigidity. A missing phase can also reflect attenuation, instrument
response or low signal-to-noise, so geometry and data quality must both be checked.

**Misconception to avoid:** A missing phase may reflect geometry, source radiation, attenuation, data quality or instrument performance. Check the expected travel path and record quality before interpreting it; a station in the direct S-wave shadow is one valid geometry case.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain how the properties of seismic waves make it possible to locate an earthquake, and why that capability is institutional rather than instrumental. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Earthquake location uses differences between phase arrivals and a velocity model. P waves are compressional and normally arrive before the slower S waves. At one station, the S–P interval constrains source distance but not direction. Distances from three or more stations can be represented as circles on a surface map to estimate an epicentre. A full hypocentre solution is more demanding: an inversion uses arrival times at multiple stations to estimate latitude, longitude, depth and origin time simultaneously. Its quality depends on station geometry, phase picking and how closely the assumed velocity model represents the crust. Additional stations reduce ambiguity and support uncertainty estimates; three circles should not be described as automatically fixing depth. Capability therefore belongs to the network and its data flow, not to one seismograph. Published epicentres and depths remain model-dependent estimates.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 3 for the P-S speed and medium contrast stated with particle motion; 3 for the S-P interval derived or at least justified as proportional to distance; 2 for the three-station requirement explained as the consequence of distance-without-direction; 1 for focal depth included in the solution; 1 for the velocity-model uncertainty conceded. Deduct 2 for claiming a single station can locate an event and 2 for confusing early warning with prediction.

The ladder is the arithmetic; the isoseismal sketch is the geography. Keeping them apart is the single most reliable mark in any earthquake answer.

### 1. The logarithmic relation, derived rather than asserted

⚠️ For magnitude scales calibrated to the traditional relation, one unit corresponds to ten times
wave amplitude and roughly 31.6 times radiated energy. This is an approximate cross-scale teaching relation.

⚠️ The energy relation is approximately \(10^{1.5}\), or 31.6, per unit.

❗ A one-unit rise is **not** a doubling. **Two** units multiply amplitude by a hundred and energy by about a thousand; **three** units multiply energy by about thirty-one thousand. Event counts alone therefore do not describe the annual energy budget.

⚠️ **"Open ended" matters too.** There is no design maximum on the scale; the practical upper limit is set by **how much fault area can rupture in one event**, which is a property of the Earth rather than of the scale.

### 2. Why the local magnitude had to be replaced — the mandatory correction

❗ **This is the obsolete-terminology correction that this topic is required to make explicitly, and it must be made as a correction rather than as a silent substitution.**

⚠️ The scale popularly called "Richter" is properly the **local magnitude**, devised for a particular instrument, a particular region and a limited distance range. It works well for moderate earthquakes. It fails for great ones, and the failure has a name: **saturation**.

```text
   WHY A LOCAL MAGNITUDE SATURATES

   local magnitude is read from the AMPLITUDE of a particular
   wave at a particular PERIOD on a particular instrument
            |
            v
   a GREAT earthquake ruptures a very long fault over TENS OF SECONDS
   to several MINUTES, radiating most of its energy at LONG periods
            |
            v
   the short-period amplitude the scale measures STOPS GROWING
   even though the earthquake keeps getting bigger
            |
            v
   SATURATION: two events of very different true size return almost
   the SAME local magnitude  ==>  the scale under-reports great events

   THE REPLACEMENT: MOMENT MAGNITUDE (Mw), derived from SEISMIC MOMENT
         seismic moment = rigidity x RUPTURE AREA x average SLIP
            |
            v
   a PHYSICAL quantity, not an instrument reading; it does NOT saturate,
   and it is what great modern earthquakes are reported in
```

⚠️ Large modern earthquakes are normally reported in **moment magnitude (Mw)**. “Richter” refers
properly to local magnitude and is often used imprecisely as a media synonym.

❗ Name local magnitude as a real historical/regional measure, explain saturation, and use Mw for great events.

⚠️ Magnitudes are estimates and can be revised as more waveforms and finite-fault information arrive;
attach agency and date when precision matters.

### 3. Intensity — a different quantity, not a different scale for the same thing

⚠️ Magnitude estimates event size; intensity describes observed shaking/effects at a place.

| | **Magnitude** | **Intensity** |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | The **size of the event** — energy radiated | The **effect at a place** — what was felt and damaged |
| How many values per earthquake | **One** | **Many** — one for every location |
| How obtained | Instrumentally, from the wave record or from seismic moment | By **observation** of felt effects and damage |
| Scale in use | Local magnitude historically; **moment magnitude (Mw)** for great events | **Modified Mercalli (MM)**; **MSK** widely used in India |
| Nature of the scale | Continuous, open-ended, logarithmic | **Ordinal**, in Roman numerals, bounded in practice |
| Depends on soil and buildings? | **No** | **Yes, decisively** |
| Mapped as | A number | **Isoseismals** — contours of equal intensity |

❗ A smaller shallow event near vulnerable exposure can cause greater loss than a larger distant or deep event.

⚠️ Isoseismals are contours of equal observed intensity. Their form reflects source geometry,
directivity, attenuation, site conditions, building stock and reporting density—not soil alone.

⚠️ **Peak ground acceleration (PGA)** is an instrumental site-motion measure. It is site-specific like intensity but measured rather than descriptive. No PGA value is attributed to an Indian zone without the operative standard itself.

### 4. 🇮🇳 Indian reporting discipline

NCS rapidly publishes origin time, coordinates, depth and magnitude estimates and may revise them.
Felt reports or intensity maps describe effects at places. A single felt tremor does not justify a
recurrence claim or change the BIS zone map.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| | Local magnitude ("Richter") | Moment magnitude (Mw) | Intensity (MM / MSK) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basis | Amplitude at a fixed period on a specific instrument | **Seismic moment** — rigidity × rupture area × average slip | Observed effects on people, objects, structures and ground |
| Behaves well for | Moderate, regional earthquakes | **All sizes, including great events** | Any event, wherever it was felt |
| Fails because | **Saturates** for great earthquakes | Requires modelling, so it is **revised** | **Subjective and construction-dependent** |
| Values per event | One | One | Many |
| Present status | Historical scale; **popular generic label** | **The reporting standard for great events** | Essential for damage mapping and zonation |

**Strongest objection.** This is a vocabulary quibble. Everyone understands what "7.6 on the Richter scale" means, so correcting it is pedantry.

**Reply.** The correction is not about the word; it is about a **systematic measurement error**. ❗ A saturating scale **under-reports precisely the events that matter most** — the great ones — so the historical catalogue compiled on it is not merely imprecisely labelled, it is **biased at the top end**. ⚠️ Any argument about recurrence, about the comparative size of historical earthquakes, or about whether a region has experienced a great event therefore depends on which magnitude was used and when. ⚠️ And the second half of the correction is substantive too: treating magnitude as the measure of a disaster conceals the fact that **soil, depth and construction determine loss**, which is the error that produces an answer about earthquakes and not about earthquake risk.

⚠️ **Residual.** Moment magnitude is a **computed estimate** with uncertainty, and agencies may publish different values for the same event. Event magnitudes therefore require a named source and attribution.

**Question:** Two earthquakes occur in the same year. ### 1. What a tsunami is, and the three things it is not

⚠️ A tsunami is a **series of long-period water waves produced by rapid displacement of a large
volume of water**. The Japanese term means harbour wave.

❗ **Three negations do more work than the definition.**

- ❗ A tsunami is not a tidal wave; tides are gravitational and periodic.
- ❗ **A tsunami is not a wind wave.** ⚠️ Wind waves are generated at the sea surface by shear stress and involve motion in the upper part of the water column only; a tsunami involves **the entire water column from sea bed to surface**, which is why its energy is so enormous and why it is unaffected by local weather.
- ❗ A tsunami is a wave train; the first wave need not be the largest. Periods are commonly about
  **10–60 minutes**, not one universal 5–6-minute value.

### 2. Generation controls, not hard thresholds

| Control | Effect |
|---|---|
| Source geometry and slip | Vertical sea-floor deformation efficiently displaces water |
| Rupture area and displacement | Larger displaced volume generally produces greater far-field potential |
| Water depth and bathymetry | Control propagation, focusing and coastal amplification |
| Landslide/volcanic coupling | Can produce damaging local waves from events below common earthquake screens |
| Predominantly strike-slip rupture | Usually low efficiency, but vertical components or landslides can still generate waves |

❗ Large shallow megathrusts are the dominant basin-wide source because they can deform a large sea-floor area. Magnitude and depth are screening indicators, not necessary-and-sufficient conditions.

### 5. The physical signature at sea — why it passes unnoticed

⚠️ In the open ocean, long wavelength and small amplitude make the wave difficult for vessels to notice.

### 1. Why a tsunami is a shallow-water wave in the deep ocean

⚠️ A long wave behaves as a shallow-water wave when wavelength greatly exceeds depth. Then
**c≈√(gd)** is a useful first-order speed relation; dispersion, bathymetry and source complexity still matter.

⚠️ Deep-ocean speed can be several hundred kilometres per hour and amplitude may remain small. A
speed is meaningless without water depth.

### 2. Shoaling — why the wave grows, stated as a conservation argument

⚠️ As the wave enters shallower water:

- **Speed falls**, because c = √(gd) and d is falling.
- **Wavelength falls**, because the period is set at the source and does not change, so L = cT shrinks with c.
- **Amplitude commonly rises**, but refraction, focusing, reflection, friction and breaking make the
  amount site-specific. Green's-law reasoning is an approximation, not “nowhere to go but up”.

⚠️ Run-up is the maximum vertical elevation reached on land and can differ sharply along one coast.

⚠️ **Coastal form modulates the outcome.** A gently shelving, funnel-shaped or embayed coast concentrates and amplifies; a steep, deep-water coast reflects more and amplifies less. ❗ This is why **run-up varies enormously along a single coastline in a single event**, and it is the physical reason behind the correction in §5.

### 3. The 2004 event: the India-specific consequence set

USGS records the source earthquake as **Mw 9.1**. Indian consequences included deaths and
displacement; inundation, erosion and subsidence; salinisation of soils and groundwater; damage to
ports, transport, power, fisheries and coastal ecosystems; and long recovery. Exact casualty,
reef-loss, Car Nicobar population and reclamation figures from the old book tables are excluded
because they are inconsistent or lack current first-party verification.

### 4. The Indian warning system — detection capability, and where this topic stops

✅ [INCOIS](https://tsunami.incois.gov.in/TEWS/abouttsunamiready.jsp) states that ITEWC at
INCOIS Hyderabad has operated since October 2007, combines real-time seismic and sea-level
observations with numerical modelling, operates 24×7 and provides UNESCO-IOC Indian Ocean advisories. The
[architecture page](https://tsunami.incois.gov.in/TEWS/searlywarnings.jsp) documents BPR,
tide-gauge and communications components.

⚠️ **Warning capability must be read against travel time.** A tsunami in deep water can travel hundreds of kilometres per hour, so a system valuable for far-field sources may still leave very little response time for a near-field Andaman source. Travel time depends on bathymetry and source distance.

The physical distinction is: seismic stations detect the earthquake; BPRs/tsunami buoys confirm
deep-ocean disturbance; tide gauges observe coastal arrival; modelling converts source and
bathymetry into forecasts.

### 5. Natural warning signs and the limits of simple rules

⚠️ Run-up depends on source, bathymetry, coast shape, period and direction. No universal elevation
cut-off works. Strong/long coastal shaking, unusual roar or rapid sea-level change require response
to official/local evacuation guidance.

❗ Drawback does not precede every wave; absence of withdrawal is not reassurance.

### 1. The question this lesson answers

⚠️ Earthquake damage is commonly patchy even at similar source distance because ground and buildings differ. Site response, liquefaction, structural period and construction quality can therefore control loss as strongly as regional shaking.

### 2. The six ground effects, with their mechanisms

| ⚠️ Effect | ⚠️ Mechanism | ⚠️ Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Site amplification** | Impedance contrast, basin geometry and resonance alter frequency content and duration | Response varies by frequency and shaking level; no fixed multiplier |
| **Liquefaction** | Cyclic loading raises pore pressure and reduces effective stress/stiffness in susceptible saturated sands, silty sands and some low-plasticity silts | Settlement, tilt, flotation of buried structures and foundation/lifeline failure |
| **Lateral spreading** | Liquefied ground on a gentle slope or near a **free face** moves sideways | Roads, embankments, quay walls and pipelines **pulled apart** |
| **Resonance** | A building's **natural period matches** the dominant period of shaking at that site | **Certain building heights fail selectively** while their neighbours survive |
| **Slope failure and rockfall** | Shaking triggers mass movement on already marginal slopes | **The dominant damage mode in mountainous seismic belts** |
| **Surface rupture** | Fault displacement reaches the surface | Avoidance/setbacks are primary; specialised lifeline design can reduce but not eliminate damage |

❗ Direct fault rupture is primarily a siting/setback problem; flexible lifelines and specialised
foundations may mitigate consequences but cannot erase displacement.

⚠️ **Resonance is the effect that produces the most counter-intuitive damage pattern.** Soft, deep sediment favours **long-period** shaking; tall buildings have **long natural periods**. The result is that in a basin, **mid-rise or high-rise structures of a particular height range can fail selectively** while low-rise buildings beside them survive — the opposite of the intuition that taller means more vulnerable in general.

### 3. Geomorphology screens liquefaction susceptibility

⚠️ Floodplains, deltas, coastal plains, reclaimed land and old channels identify first-pass
susceptibility. Actual assessment requires groundwater, grain size/plasticity, density/penetration
tests, stratigraphy and scenario shaking.

### 6. The India-applied conclusion: what a zone does and does not mean

| A seismic zone **does** | A seismic zone **does not** |
|---|---|
| Express **expected intensity and design ground motion** for a region | Predict **when** an earthquake will occur anywhere in it |
| Set a **minimum design obligation** for new construction | Guarantee that a building meeting it will survive any event |
| Generalise over a **large area at small scale** | Represent **site conditions** — that is microzonation's work |
| Encode a **decision about acceptable risk** | State a purely physical fact about the rocks |
| Change when the **assessment basis or the evidence** changes | Change because a single tremor occurred |

❗ No single tremor validates a deterministic recurrence or zone change.

### 1. Directive decoding — what each word actually asks

The directive controls weighting and evidence.

| ✅ If the question says | ✅ It is really asking for | ✅ Do **not** |
|---|---|---|
| *"Define mantle plume and explain its role in plate tectonics"* | Plume hypothesis, evidence, relative motion and competing models | Present fixed hot spots or plume causation as settled |
| *"Explain the geophysical characteristics of the circum-Pacific belt"* | Trench/interface, inclined seismicity, arc and segment diversity | Merely count volcanoes |
| *"What are tsunamis? How and where are they formed?"* | Vertical sea-floor displacement → long-wavelength wave → shoaling amplification → coastal consequence, **plus the source geography** | Treat a tsunami as a large wind wave |
| *"Assess the environmental impact of volcanic eruptions"* | The **six pathways** and the **fertility counterpoint** | Write a one-sided disaster narrative |
| *"Why does the Himalaya lack an Andean-style volcanic arc?"* | Continental underthrusting after ocean closure; major seismicity without continuous arc volcanism | Claim collision eliminates all magmatism |
| **Mention** (extended here) | **Light identification**, then weight on the second half of the question | Narrate each named item at length |
| **Discuss** (extended here) | A **characterised object** with a position, evidence and a qualification | Produce an undifferentiated list |

⚠️ **Standing directive:** *Why is the Himalaya highly seismic but lacks a continuous Andean-style volcanic arc?* India’s continental lithosphere underthrusts Eurasia, shortening and thickening the crust while strain is released on the Main Himalayan Thrust system. After ocean closure, the belt no longer had the continuous dehydrating oceanic slab that sustains an Andean arc. This contrast does not make all local melting or magmatism impossible.

### 2. The reusable spines, with their weighting

⚠️ The four spines address the listed demands. A general-purpose **15-mark seismic-hazard spine** is:

- ⚠️ **(i) The tectonic source** — collision belt against subduction belt against intra-plate.
- ⚠️ **(ii) The ground-motion modifier** — depth, soil amplification on alluvium, liquefaction on saturated sediment.
- ⚠️ **(iii) The human modifier** — building stock, density, code compliance, preparedness.
- ❗ **(iv) Qualification:** deterministic prediction is unavailable; forecasting and pilot EEW have bounded roles.
- ⚠️ **(v) A conclusion that grades where risk is concentrated and why.**

Structure hazard answers through source, site response, exposure and vulnerability. This is a
conceptual interaction, not a literal product with independent “location”.

### 3. Two reusable evidence units

> **Claim:** volcanic terrain can combine hazard and livelihood benefits. **Evidence:** soils,
> geothermal resources and geoheritage coexist with lava, ash, PDC and lahar hazards.
> **Limitation:** benefits are site-specific and do not justify settlement in rapid-hazard corridors.

> **Claim:** Earth’s interior is known by inference. **Evidence:** direct S waves do not traverse the liquid outer core, producing a shadow zone. **Significance:** this constrains physical state. **Limitation:** wave evidence does not by itself establish detailed composition.

⚠️ **India-specific unit:**

> **Claim:** highest source hazard need not equal highest risk. **Evidence:** site response,

> must be demonstrated with mapped data, not blanket claims about an entire plain.

⚠️ A diagram in a Mains answer is worth drawing **only if it does work that the prose would otherwise have to do**. Four rules make that test operational.

- ❗ **One diagram per ten-mark answer; at most two per twenty-mark answer.** More than that is time taken from content.
- ❗ **Label it, and make at least two labels non-obvious.** A section labelled only "trench" and "volcano" has added nothing; the same section labelled with the **inclined seismic zone**, the **mantle wedge** and the **back-arc basin** has added three facts.
- ❗ **Draw the diagram that the directive needs**, not the one that is easiest. For a plume question, the age-progressive chain **with its age arrow** is the diagram; a cross-section of a volcano is not.
- ❗ **Refer to the diagram in the text at least once.** An unreferenced diagram reads as decoration; *"as the section shows, focal depth increases away from the trench"* converts it into evidence.

⚠️ **The four highest-value diagrams in this topic**, each earning its space: the **Wadati-Benioff section** for any belt question; the **hot-spot chain with an age arrow** for the plume question; the **shoaling profile** for the tsunami question; and the **site-response stack** for any damage or vulnerability question.

### 5. The consolidated trap register

| # | Trap | The correction |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | The mantle is molten | Hot but **solid**; pressure raises the melting point. Melt needs **decompression, flux or heat transfer** |
| 2 | Shield volcanoes and lava domes are the same | Shields are broad edifices of repeated mobile flows; domes are compact viscous extrusions |
| 3 | A caldera is a big crater | Crater is vent-centred; caldera records larger-scale collapse or excavation; structure decides |
| 4 | All volcanoes pass through active → dormant → extinct | **Evidence-based activity classes, not a one-way life cycle**; absence of a historic eruption is insufficient |
| 5 | The Deccan plateau requires vanished giant cones | **Fissure-fed flow fields** can build plateaux without giant central cones |
| 6 | Lonar is a volcanic crater | It is an **impact crater** in Deccan basalt; structure and deposits decide, not outline |
| 7 | A line of volcanoes implies a plume | ❗ The diagnostic is the **age gradient**, not the alignment |
| 8 | A plume chain by itself proves and drives plate motion | It can record **relative** motion; causation and driving forces need independent evidence |
| 9 | The Himalaya is an Andean-style volcanic arc | Continental underthrusting/collision: major seismicity without a continuous arc; do not exclude all magmatism |
| 10 | "Richter" is the current scale | **Local magnitude saturates**; great events are reported in **moment magnitude (Mw)** |
| 11 | One magnitude unit doubles the energy | **×10 amplitude, ≈ ×31.6 energy** (energy ∝ amplitude^1.5) |
| 12 | Magnitude and intensity are the same | **One magnitude, many intensities**; intensity depends on distance, depth, **soil and construction** |
| 13 | Focus and epicentre are the same point | Focus is **inside**; epicentre is **vertically above it** at the surface |

| 15 | A tsunami is a tidal wave | Long-period wave train from rapid water-volume displacement; tides are gravitational |
| 16 | Any undersea earthquake makes a tsunami | Rapid displaced-water volume and geometry matter; strike-slip is usually inefficient but landslides/vertical components can generate waves |
| 17 | The first wave is the biggest | It is a **train**; the first need not be the largest |
| 18 | Drawback always precedes a tsunami | ❗ It does **not**; respond to shaking and official warning |

### 6. What not to write, and one lesson in source criticism

❗ **Five things that cost marks in this topic and should not appear.**

- ❗ **A casualty, cost, area or explosivity figure quoted from memory.** Use a named, dated source or omit it.
- ❗ Extended response administration or cyclone dynamics where the demand is geophysical process.
- ❗ **A deterministic sentence about the future.** Reliable short-term time-place-magnitude prediction is unavailable.
- ❗ **An answer-key letter, truth marking or elimination cue for an objective question.** Teach the mechanism the question tests and leave the key alone.
- ❗ **An undated percentage presented as a universal constant** — volcano counts, earthquake counts and belt shares all drift with the detection network.

⚠️ **Source-criticism example.** A local table labels the 1737 Kolkata catastrophe as an earthquake with an extreme death toll, although it is a long-standing cyclone misattribution. The same table places the 2004 Banda Aceh event in January while its running tsunami text correctly gives 26 December 2004. Internal contradiction and conflict with modern evidence justify excluding the table rather than repeating it.

### Concept check

**Question:** A paper carries the instruction: "Mention the major volcanic eruptions of a recent year and discuss their regional environmental impact." A candidate writes 150 words, of which 110 narrate three eruptions in detail and 40 describe ash damage. Diagnose every failure and state the allocation that should have been used.

**Model answer:** The answer reverses directive weight. “Mention” requires brief identification;
“discuss regional environmental impact” requires most of the space. It also narrows impact to ash,
omitting lava, gases, PDCs, lahars, water/agriculture effects and longer-term landform or soil
change. Detailed narration invites unsupported numbers. A better allocation is roughly one quarter
for names, places and dates and three quarters for mechanism-led regional impacts, comparison and a
qualified conclusion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the first directive in a compound question as the main one. The weighting follows the **substance of what is asked**, not the order in which the verbs appear, and in this topic the heavier half is almost always the impact, consequence or characterisation clause rather than the identification clause.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "In General Studies Paper I geophysical phenomena, mechanism is the answer and example is the evidence." Examine with reference to any two listed demands. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Mechanism turns examples into evidence. In a plume answer, an
age-progressive chain can constrain relative plate–hot-spot motion, but hot-spot mobility and shallow
alternatives must be stated; Deccan–Réunion is a qualified reconstruction. In a tsunami answer,
rapid displacement of a large water volume explains generation, while magnitude and depth are only
screens. The USGS Mw 9.1 Sumatra-Andaman event supplies a dated example without requiring disputed
slip or casualty figures. Examples therefore test and locate the mechanism; they do not replace it.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 3 for the plume mechanism stated with the age-progression consequence; 3 for the tsunami mechanism stated with the horizontal-motion corollary; 2 for named examples attached to each as evidence rather than as substitutes; 1 for the reply that examples remain necessary; 1 for a directive-sensitive framing. Deduct 2 for listing examples without mechanisms and 2 for any unsourced quantitative claim.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Four checks. Three consolidate an arc; the fourth spans the topic, which is justified because the chain from a melting curve to a design code cannot be tested inside any single arc.

### Cumulative check A — the vulcanism arc, Lessons 1 to 7

**Question:** Build the chain from "pressure raises the melting point of rock" to "volcanic regions are among the most densely settled places on Earth", naming every intermediate step, and identify the point at which a physical statement becomes a human-geography one.

**Model answer:** Mantle rock is usually below its solidus; decompression, volatile addition or heat
transfer creates partial melt. Composition, temperature, crystals, volatiles and ascent then control
rheology, degassing, products and landform. Those products create hazards—lava, ash, PDCs, lahars
and gases—but may also provide parent material, geothermal resources and geoheritage. The argument
becomes human-geographical when exposure, livelihood benefits, monitoring and land use determine
whether volcanic terrain becomes an opportunity, a tolerable risk or a disaster.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the benefit argument as an addendum to the hazard argument. Both are consequences of the same lever, and an answer that derives hazard from viscosity but treats fertility as a separate fact has missed that ash and basalt are the same material in two timeframes.

### Cumulative check B — the seismic arc, Lessons 8 to 10

**Question:** Three statements are made about one earthquake: it was "caused by a reservoir", it "measured 7.6 on the Richter scale", and it "had an intensity of VIII". Correct or qualify each, and state what the three together can and cannot establish about the loss it produced.

**Model answer:** “Caused by a reservoir” should become reservoir-associated or triggered:
impoundment can alter load and pore pressure on a pre-stressed fault, but attribution is
case-specific. “Richter 7.6” should identify the actual magnitude scale; great events are generally
reported in Mw because local magnitude saturates. “Intensity VIII” must name the place because
intensity varies spatially. Together the statements describe source size, possible triggering and
local effects, but cannot establish total loss without exposure, vulnerability and site response.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read a single intensity value as a property of the earthquake. Intensity is a field over the ground, and quoting one value without its location is the measurement equivalent of quoting a temperature without saying where the thermometer was.

### Cumulative check C — the India-risk arc, Lessons 13 to 15

**Question:** Explain, in one connected argument, why India's most hazardous region is not necessarily its most at-risk region, and why that distinction decides what a seismic zone map can usefully do.

**Model answer:** Hazard is highest along major plate-boundary systems, but risk also reflects site
response, exposure and vulnerability. Parts of the Indo-Gangetic foreland may amplify selected
frequencies or contain liquefiable deposits while supporting dense exposure. A national zone map
generalises regional design hazard; it cannot represent neighbourhood-scale soil, groundwater,
topography or building stock. Microzonation refines those local effects but does not replace the
national map. Thus the most hazardous source region need not produce the greatest loss, and a zone
is neither a risk ranking nor an event prediction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat a low zone number as a statement about safety, or a high one as a statement about inevitability. The map expresses design obligation under a chosen hazard basis, which is why the obligation could change on 6 November 2025 and change back on 3 March 2026 while the rocks did not.

### Cumulative check D — the whole topic, Lessons 1 to 16

**Question:** State what this topic establishes about the relationship between a physical process and a human outcome, drawing on at least five lessons, and say precisely where the relationship stops holding.

**Model answer:** Physical processes constrain spatial possibilities rather than determine human
outcomes. Melting setting influences magma; source geometry controls earthquake and tsunami
generation; bathymetry and site conditions modify waves. Loss then depends on exposure,
vulnerability, construction and decisions. Geography can identify likely source belts, susceptible
coasts and ground conditions, but cannot derive exact event time or social loss from tectonics
alone. Zonation and warning systems are therefore translations of physical evidence into bounded
decisions under uncertainty.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not conclude from this that the physical analysis is weak. Narrowing a distribution is an enormous achievement — it is what makes zonation, microzonation, warning systems and land-use control possible at all — and the correct reading is that physical geography supplies necessary conditions and spatial structure, not sufficient conditions for an outcome.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ These are **original practice prompts**, not previous-year questions. Each rubric is question-specific.

## 10-mark practice (about 150 words)

**Practice question 1 [10] — mechanism and distribution.** "The distribution of the world's volcanoes can be derived rather than memorised." Explain. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** Volcano distribution follows melting conditions. Decompression melting
occurs at ridges, rifts and some upwellings; slab-derived volatiles promote mantle-wedge melting at
subduction arcs; heat transfer or crustal thickening can melt crust locally. Hot spots require plume
and lithospheric alternatives to be evaluated. The Himalaya lacks a continuous Andean-style arc
because it is a continent-collision/underthrusting system, though regional magmatism is not excluded.
Thus the map is derived from mechanisms, but each province still needs geochemical and geophysical
evidence.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 3 for three melting mechanisms and settings; 2 for boundary distribution; 2 for qualified hot-spot alternatives; 2 for the Himalayan collision contrast; 1 for multi-evidence qualification.

**Practice question 2 [10] — evidence and its limits.** Examine the claim that a seismograph can tell us where an earthquake occurred but not how much damage it did. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** P–S arrival differences constrain source distance through a velocity
model, but one station gives no direction. Three stations provide simplified epicentral
triangulation; robust hypocentre, depth and origin-time solutions use multiple phases and stations.
Waveforms also estimate event size. They cannot determine loss, which additionally depends on site
response, exposure and vulnerability. Intensity and damage observations therefore complement, rather
than duplicate, the seismogram.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 3 for S–P distance and one-station limitation; 2 for simplified epicentre versus robust hypocentre solution; 2 for waveform-based size; 2 for site/exposure/vulnerability; 1 for intensity observations. Deduct 2 for claiming one station fully locates an event.

## 15-mark practice (about 250 words)

**Practice question 3 [15] — comparison across two systems.** Compare the ways in which a volcanic eruption and a tsunami convert a geological event into a human catastrophe, and assess what the comparison reveals about hazard management. *(Compare and assess — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** Both become catastrophes when physical hazard intersects exposure and
vulnerability. Volcanic impact depends on rheology, gas, products, topography and proximity; PDCs,
lahars, ash and lava require different exclusion and monitoring strategies. Tsunami impact begins
with rapid water displacement and is transformed by travel distance, bathymetry, coastal geometry
and warning time. Magnitude/depth are screens, not universal thresholds. Volcano sources are usually
spatially known, whereas a tsunami may arrive from a distant source or a near-field Andaman source.
Hence volcanic risk emphasises local monitoring and hazard-zone land use; tsunami risk emphasises
rapid seismic and sea-level detection, modelling and communications. The same warning system offers
different usable time for far-field and near-field sources. Both still require exposure reduction.

**Rubric (15 marks):** 3 for volcanic mechanism; 3 for displaced-water mechanism with threshold
qualification; 3 for spatial contrast; 3 for monitoring/warning implications; 2 for India evidence;
1 for exposure qualification.

**Practice question 4 [15] — measurement and policy.** "In seismology, every number is an estimate with a date attached." Discuss, with reference to magnitude, recurrence and zonation. *(Discuss — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** Magnitude is estimated from finite data and may be revised; Mw avoids
the saturation problem of local magnitude for great events. Recurrence depends on catalogue
completeness, magnitude scale and observation interval, so no region becomes “due”. Zonation
translates estimated regional hazard into design categories and must carry an edition/status date.
Gazette 267696/2025 established a revision while retaining 2016 concurrently during transition;
Gazette 270643/2026 withdrew that notification, and MoES confirmed 2016 remained current. The
discipline is therefore agency, date, scale and uncertainty—not treating estimates as arbitrary.

**Rubric (15 marks):** 3 for magnitude uncertainty; 3 for catalogue limits; 3 for exact Gazette
sequence; 2 for current 2016 status; 2 for source/date discipline; 2 for qualification.

## 20-mark practice (about 250 words)

**Practice question 5 [20] — the full risk argument with its counter-case.** "Seismic risk in India is a geographical problem with an engineering solution." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** Seismic risk is geographical at source, regional and site scales.
Continental underthrusting and the MHT shape Himalayan hazard; Indo-Burma, Kachchh, Peninsular
faults and Andaman-Sunda have different mechanisms. Geomorphology screens possible amplification,
liquefaction and slope failure, while exposure varies spatially. Engineering reduces vulnerability
through ductile design, foundations, ground improvement and retrofitting, but cannot remove source
hazard or fully overcome direct fault rupture and unsuitable siting. Microzonation integrates
geological and geotechnical evidence; national zonation remains a regional design generalisation.
Thus engineering is essential but not sufficient: land use, maintenance, enforcement and social
capacity also shape loss.

**Rubric (20 marks):** 4 for plate/regional/site geography; 4 for engineering contribution; 4 for
siting and implementation limits; 3 for risk interaction; 3 for zonation/microzonation; 2 for verdict.

**Practice question 6 [20] — method and evidence across the topic.** "In the geophysical sciences, the strongest claims are those that specify what would not happen." Critically examine, drawing on both the volcanic and the seismic halves of this subject. *(Critically examine — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer (original):** Strong explanations generate risky, qualified expectations. Subduction
predicts trench–arc geometry and inclined seismicity; collision predicts major seismicity without a
continuous Andean-style arc, not an absolute absence of magmatism. Tsunami theory predicts low
efficiency for mainly horizontal rupture, while allowing vertical components and landslides.
Hot-spot age progression supports relative plate motion but does not uniquely prove a deep plume
because hot spots move and shallow alternatives exist. Negative claims must therefore be phrased as
tendencies and tested with independent evidence. Network-dependent counts and book-era percentages
are weak tests because definitions and detection change.

**Rubric (20 marks):** 4 for qualified collision prediction; 4 for tsunami-efficiency prediction;
4 for plume alternatives; 3 for independent tests; 3 for catalogue/count limits; 2 for verdict.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

Compressed for retrieval. This section changes form and does not repeat the lessons' explanatory prose. Sixteen headings, one per lesson.

## A. Heat, Melting and the Birth of Magma

The mantle is predominantly solid because the geotherm is generally below the solidus. Principal
melting routes are decompression, volatile addition and heat transfer/anatexis. Himalaya = continental
underthrusting and major seismicity without a continuous Andean-style arc; regional magmatism is not
excluded. Magma rheology depends on composition, temperature, crystals and volatiles; gas behaviour,
ascent and conduit conditions influence explosivity. Water vapour commonly dominates volcanic gas.
Deccan = fissure-fed mafic flood-basalt province; plateau form does not alone prove a plume.

## B. Intrusive Vulcanism and Plutonic Forms

Intrusions are concordant (sill, laccolith, lopolith, phacolith) or discordant (dyke, batholith).
Geometry records host control and relative timing; cross-cutting evidence is essential. Crystal size
reflects cooling rate, body size, depth, geometry and host temperature. Exhumed relief reflects
differential erosion. Deccan dyke swarms preserve feeder/fracture evidence, but individual flow
correlations require field, geochemical and age control.

## C. Extrusive Vulcanism: Style and Products

Eruption style is a continuum controlled by rheology, volatiles, crystals, ascent and conduit
conditions. Named styles are descriptive, not a controlling ladder. Products: lava · tephra · gases ·
PDC/lahar/remobilised material. Barren Island lies about 135 km north-east of Port Blair; its
Smithsonian record includes 1991 and later episodes. Do not infer a named style from arc setting.

## D. Volcanic Landforms

Landforms: cinder cone · composite volcano · lava dome · shield · plateau · crater · caldera.
Shield and lava dome are distinct. Crater is vent-centred; caldera is collapse after magma withdrawal.
Activity classes are revisable, not a life cycle. Deccan = fissure plateau; Lonar = impact crater in
basalt, identified by impact evidence rather than circular shape.

## E. Plate Settings, Hot Spots and Mantle Plumes

Settings: subduction · spreading/rifting · intra-plate hot spots. Plume head/tail is a debated model.
Age trends constrain relative plate–hot-spot motion; hot spots can move. Use geochemistry,
geochronology, geophysics and topography together. Deccan–Réunion and Rajmahal–Kerguelen are
qualified reconstructions, not proof.

## F. The Ring of Fire as an Integrated System

Circum-Pacific analysis: trench/interface · inclined Wadati-Benioff seismicity · volcanic/island arc;
accretionary forearc and back-arc only where developed; megathrust tsunami potential; deposit-specific
hydrothermal systems. Avoid old shares/counts. Andaman-Nicobar ridge is a tectonic/accretionary
outer arc; Barren lies in the volcanic arc; coral reefs do not make the islands simply coral-built.

## G. Volcanoes and Society

Pathways: sulphate aerosol · ash · PDC · lahar · gases · longer-term weathering/soil effects.
Benefits are case-specific: geothermal resources, materials, mineral systems and geoheritage. 2021
examples span several settings, but impact also depends on weather, terrain, duration and exposure.
Barren has low residential but non-zero wider exposure. UNESCO: Tungurahua Geopark, Ecuador.

## H. Elastic Rebound, Faults and the Four Causes

Earthquakes release stored elastic strain through fault rupture/slip. Classes: tectonic · volcanic
· collapse · induced/triggered activity. Koyna requires reservoir-associated
qualification. Primary effects are shaking/displacement; secondary effects include landslides,
liquefaction, fire, lifeline failure and tsunami. Deterministic prediction is unavailable;
forecasting is probabilistic; EEW detects rupture after it begins. Historic casualty and animal-
prediction narratives are excluded.

## I. Seismic Waves and Location

P = compressional, fastest body wave, solids/fluids; S = shear, solids with rigidity only. Love and
Rayleigh are surface waves and often damaging, not universally dominant. Liquefied soil retains
reduced shear stiffness and is not equivalent to the liquid outer core. P–S constrains model
distance; three circles simplify epicentral triangulation; robust hypocentres use multiple phases and
stations. NSN: 169→170→174 stations in 2026; Indian EEW remained pilot-phase.

## J. Magnitude, Intensity and the Move to Moment Magnitude

Teaching relation: +1 magnitude ≈×10 amplitude and ≈×31.6 energy. Local magnitude saturates;
large events use Mw from seismic moment. Magnitude estimates event size; intensity is spatial and
observational. Isoseismal geometry reflects source, path, site, buildings and reporting. NCS rapid
parameters may be revised; one tremor does not alter zonation.

## K. Tsunami Generation

Tsunami = long-period wave train from rapid displacement of a large water volume. Large shallow
megathrusts dominate basin-wide events, but magnitude/depth are screens rather than thresholds;
landslides, volcanic collapse and impacts can generate waves. Mainly strike-slip motion is usually
inefficient unless vertical components or landslides intervene. Period commonly 10–60 minutes.
USGS: 26 Dec 2004 **Mw 9.1 Sumatra-Andaman**; mainland coasts can face distant sources, while
Andaman-Nicobar also has near-field exposure.

## L. Propagation, Shoaling and Coastal Consequence

Long-wave speed is approximated by **c≈√(gd)**: about 713 km/h at 4,000 m, 80 at 50 m
and 36 at 10 m. Shoaling shortens/slows the wave, while focusing, friction, reflection and breaking
control local amplitude/run-up. USGS records 2004 as Mw 9.1. Consequences span human,
physiographic, soil/water, ecological and infrastructural dimensions; old casualty/reef/Car Nicobar
tables are excluded. INCOIS/ITEWC: Hyderabad, operational since Oct 2007, real-time seismic and
sea-level network, modelling, 24×7 operation and UNESCO-IOC advisories.

## M. Ground Behaviour, Amplification and Liquefaction

Site response includes amplification, liquefaction, lateral spreading, resonance, slope failure and
surface rupture. Liquefaction affects susceptible loose saturated sands, silty sands and some
low-plasticity silts; reduced effective stress is not literal conversion into water. Geomorphology
screens susceptibility, while boreholes, groundwater, density/plasticity and scenario motion establish
site hazard. Loss reflects hazard, exposure, vulnerability and site response. Indian evidence includes
1934 Bihar-Nepal and 2001 Kachchh liquefaction without old damage/casualty figures.

## N. India's Seismic Setting and Regional Gradient

Indian continental lithosphere underthrusts Eurasia; the **Main Himalayan Thrust** is the principal
regional décollement. MFT/MBT/MCT are related mapped structures, not substitutes. Regional source
systems: Himalayan MHT · Indo-Burma/NE · Kachchh · Peninsular inherited faults/local triggers ·
Andaman-Sunda. Koyna is reservoir-associated; Latur/Jabalpur are intraplate. Historical catalogues
are short and network-dependent; old recurrence matrices, coefficients, coastline statistics and
“Everest-Badrinath stable” labels are excluded. NSN: 169→170→174 in 2026; EEW pilot-phase.

## O. Zonation, Microzonation and the Design Code

Operative: **IS 1893 (Part 1):2016**, Zones II–V; Delhi is Zone IV, wider NCR/location checked
from the map. Gazette 267696/2025 established the 2025 revision with concurrent 2016 transition;
Gazette 270643/2026 withdrew that notification; MoES/PIB 2247533 confirms 2016 current. Gazettes
do not state Zone-VI boundaries, PGA, cost or coverage. Microzonation refines national zoning; NCS
city-status page is dated 20 Apr 2020. Risk reflects hazard, exposure, vulnerability and site
response—not a literal product with independent “location”.

## P. Mapwork and Answer Craft

Four spines: plume hypothesis/evidence/alternatives · Circum-Pacific architecture and diversity ·
brief 2021 eruption identification plus pathway-led impacts · tsunami displaced-water mechanism,
shoaling and dimensional consequences. Hazard answers: source → site response → exposure →
vulnerability → monitoring/zonation limits. Highest-value visuals: inclined seismic zone · qualified
hot-spot age trend · shoaling profile · site-response stack. Maps: global settings · Indian source
systems/current zones · Andaman-Sunda and USGS 2004 source. Never use book-era casualty/count tables,
objective keys, deterministic predictions or technical claims absent from the cited official document.

4. NSN counts are dated: 169, 170 and 174 on the stated 2026 release dates.
