# Geography 21 — Warm Temperate Eastern Margin (China Type) Climate — Live Session Edition

The central puzzle is simple: **why are the warm-temperate eastern margins of continents wet in summer, while western margins at similar latitudes often become dry?**

The answer is not “latitude” alone. It is the seasonal interaction of land and sea, wind direction, continental size, warm offshore water, relief and tropical cyclones. Once that mechanism is clear, the distribution, three regional sub-types, agriculture, hazards and Indian comparison become parts of one connected story.

### 1. Start with the plain-language idea

Imagine standing on the eastern edge of a large continent. In summer, the land becomes strongly heated. Moist air can arrive from the adjoining warm ocean, rise and rain over the coast and interior. In winter, the land cools and can send drier continental air outward.

That seasonal switch produces the family resemblance:

```text
hot or warm, humid summer
        +
cooler, generally drier winter
        +
summer rainfall maximum
```

✅ **Fact:** The warm temperate eastern margin climate is found on the **eastern margins of continents in warm-temperate latitudes, just outside the tropics**.

✅ **Fact:** It receives more rainfall than the Mediterranean climate at similar latitudes, and much of that rain falls in summer.

### 2. Why does it have several names?

| Name | What the name highlights | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm Temperate Eastern Margin** | Its global map position | Does not explain the seasonal mechanism |
| **China type** | Its best-developed Asian expression | Must not be mistaken for the climate of all China |
| **Temperate monsoon** | Its summer-onshore and winter-offshore tendency | The Gulf and Natal variants do not show an equally strong Asian monsoon reversal |
| **Humid subtropical** | Its warmth and adequate moisture in modern classification language | The exact Köppen code varies with winter dryness and local station data |

✅ **Fact:** In Köppen shorthand, **Cw** represents a warm-temperate climate with a dry winter, while **Cf** represents a warm-temperate climate without a dry season.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** G.C. Leong’s regional label and Köppen’s threshold code answer different questions. “Warm-temperate eastern margin” describes the wider family, while “China type” in strict regional use identifies its Asian subtype; a Köppen code classifies a particular long-period temperature and rainfall regime. These levels should be related, not mechanically equated.

### 3. Meet the three sub-types

| Sub-type | Main region | Regional character |
|---|---|---|
| **China type** | Central and north China with southern Japan | Most typical; **temperate monsoonal** |
| **Gulf type** | South-eastern United States, especially the Gulf-facing region | **Slight-monsoonal** |
| **Natal type** | Natal in South Africa, eastern Australia, and south-eastern South America | **Non-monsoonal** southern-hemisphere expression |

The names are regional, but the shared logic is climatic:

```text
EASTERN MARGIN
      ↓
SUMMER ACCESS TO WARM, MOIST MARITIME AIR
      ↓
NO MEDITERRANEAN-TYPE SUMMER DROUGHT
```

> 🔑 **Terminology rule for this session:** **Eastern-margin family** refers to all three regional expressions. **Asian China subtype** refers specifically to central/north China and southern Japan. The conventional short label “China type” is retained only where the regional subtype or established textbook name is intended.

### 4. The first major limitation

The label does **not** mean that every eastern coast at every latitude has this climate.

- Nearer the Equator, tropical monsoon or tropical marine climates dominate.
- Farther poleward, the cooler Laurentian eastern-margin climate appears.
- Mountain relief can replace the broad zonal pattern with altitude-controlled mosaics.
- A coastline’s orientation, ocean current and distance from a continental interior modify the result.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** The warm-temperate eastern-margin family is positional and seasonal; the Asian China subtype is neither a political map of all China nor a universal label for every humid eastern coast.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** “China type” and “humid subtropical” are simply two names for one fixed Köppen code.

**Reply:** The eastern-margin family is defined by broad position and seasonal behaviour, while the Asian China subtype is its strongest temperate-monsoonal expression. Köppen separately assigns codes from measured temperature and precipitation thresholds at a station, so even comparable eastern-margin settings need not share one code.

**Residual limitation:** Climatic boundaries are transitional, and different classification schemes may draw them differently. The regional label remains useful only when its scale and rainfall season are stated.

### 5. Why UPSC cares

The topic is a compact test of four geographical abilities:

1. locate a climate from a world map;
2. infer rainfall season from wind direction;
3. compare eastern and western continental margins;
4. connect climate to vegetation, agriculture and hazards without climatic determinism.

### UPSC traps

- ❌ “China type” means the whole of China.
  ✅ It identifies a warm-temperate eastern-margin climatic expression.
- ❌ All three variants are equally monsoonal.
  ✅ China is temperate monsoonal, Gulf slight-monsoonal and Natal non-monsoonal.
- ❌ Latitude by itself explains the distribution.
  ✅ Margin, circulation, continental size, currents and relief also matter.
- ❌ Humid subtropical and tropical monsoon are identical.
  ✅ They overlap in summer-rain logic but differ in latitude, winter character and circulation intensity.

### Mini recap

1. The climate occurs on warm-temperate eastern continental margins.
2. It is wetter than Mediterranean western margins at similar latitudes.
3. Summer is the principal rainy season.
4. China type is the most strongly temperate-monsoonal expression.
5. Gulf type is only slight-monsoonal.
6. Natal type is the non-monsoonal southern-hemisphere expression.
7. Regional labels and Köppen codes are related but not identical.
8. The climate name must never be expanded to all of China or all eastern coasts.

### Revision notes

1. **Identity:** warm temperate eastern margin.
2. **Alternative names:** China type; temperate monsoon.
3. **Position:** eastern continental margins just outside the tropics.
4. **Rain:** adequate, mainly summer; no Mediterranean summer drought.
5. **China variant:** central/north China and southern Japan.
6. **Gulf variant:** south-eastern United States.
7. **Natal variant:** South Africa, eastern Australia and south-eastern South America.
8. **Classification:** Cw stresses dry winter; Cf stresses no dry season.
9. **Map caution:** climatic region is not a political boundary.
10. **Core comparison:** eastern margin summer rain versus western margin summer drought.

### Lesson-local Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Why is the term “China type” useful but potentially misleading?

**Sample response:** The term is useful because the Asian China subtype is the best-developed temperate-monsoonal expression: summer heating draws moist Pacific air inland, rainfall has a summer maximum, and winter continental outflow is cooler and drier. It is misleading if treated as the climate of all China or as a precise synonym for the wider eastern-margin family. Related Gulf and Natal variants occur in other continents, but their monsoonal character and winter severity differ. The Gulf subtype is only slight-monsoonal, while the Natal subtype is non-monsoonal. Moreover, Köppen station codes may vary between dry-winter and no-dry-season forms. “China type” should therefore be used with its scale stated explicitly.

**Why this earns marks — quantified rubric (10 marks):** definition and scale of the label 2; East Asian mechanism 2; China–Gulf–Natal evidence 3; classification limitation 2; qualified verdict 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two places belonging to the warm-temperate eastern-margin family have different Köppen codes?

**Model answer:** The family label groups warm-temperate eastern-margin climates by broad location and seasonal behaviour, whereas a Köppen code depends on a station’s measured temperature and precipitation thresholds, especially whether the winter is genuinely dry; local relief, ocean influence and continentality can therefore produce different codes within the wider family.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that a regional climate name and an empirical station code must have a one-to-one relationship.

**Bridge to Lesson 2:** We now know where the climate occurs. The next question is causal: **what makes the same eastern margin wet in summer and drier in winter?**

## Lesson 2 — Building the seasonal mechanism

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — the two-season engine

```text
SUMMER
Large continent heats strongly
        ↓
Lower pressure develops over land
        ↓
Warm, moist maritime air moves ONSHORE
        ↓
Convergence + convection + relief uplift
        ↓
HEAVY SUMMER RAIN
        ↓
Occasional tropical-cyclone enhancement

WINTER
Large continent cools strongly
        ↓
Higher pressure develops over land
        ↓
Cooler, drier continental air moves OFFSHORE
        ↓
Reduced moisture supply over land
        ↓
COOLER AND GENERALLY DRIER WINTER
```

*The climate is created by seasonal reversal in the relative influence of continent and ocean.*

### 1. The intuitive engine: land changes temperature faster than water

Land heats and cools more rapidly than the ocean. A large continental interior therefore produces a stronger seasonal pressure contrast than a small or ocean-surrounded landmass.

✅ **Fact:** In the Asian China type, intense summer heating over the huge landmass creates low pressure and draws a tropical Pacific air stream inland.

✅ **Fact:** Winter is cooler and drier because continental influence strengthens and the prevailing low-level flow is more offshore.

This is why “temperate monsoon” is a useful phrase. Yet it needs qualification: the mechanism is **monsoonal in tendency**, not identical in every detail to the tropical Indian monsoon.

### 2. The complete summer sequence

#### Step A — thermal and pressure contrast

The continent warms, the lower atmosphere expands, and relative pressure over land falls compared with the adjoining ocean.

#### Step B — maritime inflow

Air flows from the oceanic side toward the continental low. Because the source region is warm ocean water, the air can carry abundant moisture.

#### Step C — uplift

Moisture does not automatically become rain. Air must rise and cool. Uplift can occur through:

- surface heating and convection;
- convergence in a low-pressure zone;
- ascent along hills and mountain slopes;
- organised rainbands and tropical cyclones.

#### Step D — condensation and rainfall

Rising moist air cools, water vapour condenses, and rainfall is concentrated in the warm season.

> 🔑 **Mechanism line:** Warm ocean water supplies moisture; seasonal onshore circulation transports it; convergence, convection and relief convert it into rain.

### 3. The complete winter sequence

The land cools more strongly than the sea. The pressure gradient weakens or reverses, and the continent becomes a source of cooler, drier air.

```text
continental cooling
      → higher pressure
      → outward / offshore flow
      → weaker maritime moisture supply
      → drier winter tendency
```

In East Asia, the vast size and cold interior of the continent make this winter contrast especially strong. In southern-hemisphere variants, surrounding ocean moderates the winter.

### 4. What does the warm current do?

✅ **Fact:** Warm currents carry warm water poleward and can warm adjacent coasts.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** A warm western-boundary current on the seaward fringe helps maintain a warm, moist maritime source. It **reinforces** the summer-rain regime but does not create it alone.

This distinction matters:

| Incomplete claim | Better causal statement |
|---|---|
| “The warm current causes rain.” | The warm current supports heat and moisture supply; circulation and uplift are still required. |
| “Low pressure causes rain.” | Low pressure organises convergence, but rainfall also needs moisture and ascent. |
| “Latitude causes the climate.” | Latitude sets the broad energy zone; continentality, wind direction, currents and relief shape the actual regime. |

### 5. Why Asia has the strongest expression

The Asian landmass is exceptionally large and mountainous. Its interior therefore develops pronounced seasonal thermal and pressure changes.

```text
LARGE LANDMASS
   ├─ strong summer heating → powerful maritime draw
   └─ strong winter cooling → pronounced continental outflow
```

The same logic also explains why the Gulf and Natal variants resemble the Asian China subtype but do not reproduce its seasonal reversal equally.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** “This is just a giant sea breeze.”

**Reply:** The sea-breeze analogy is a useful first step because it shows differential heating. It is incomplete because a continental-scale climate also depends on seasonal pressure-belt movement, regional circulation, landmass size, ocean conditions, relief and migrating weather systems.

**Residual limitation:** A broad mechanism does not predict every station. Mountain barriers, latitude, coastal orientation and year-to-year circulation variability create local differences.

### 7. UPSC application

A strong explanation should use a causal chain, not a list:

```text
seasonal thermal contrast
   → pressure contrast
   → wind-direction change
   → moisture transport
   → uplift
   → rainfall season
   → vegetation / agriculture / hazard
```

### UPSC traps

- ❌ Moist air automatically means rain.
  ✅ Moist air must rise, cool and condense.
- ❌ The ocean current alone creates the climate.
  ✅ It reinforces moisture and temperature conditions.
- ❌ Winter is identical in all three variants.
  ✅ Winter severity depends strongly on continental size and oceanic moderation.
- ❌ “Monsoon” means only heavy rainfall.
  ✅ It refers to a seasonal circulation change; rainfall is an outcome.

### Mini recap

1. Summer continental heating develops a landward pressure gradient.
2. Warm ocean air moves onshore.
3. Convergence, convection and relief produce ascent.
4. Summer becomes the main rainy season.
5. Winter continental cooling favours drier offshore flow.
6. Asia’s large landmass strengthens both seasonal contrasts.
7. Warm currents assist but do not independently cause rainfall.
8. The mechanism is more than a continental sea breeze.

### Revision notes

1. **Summer:** land low, maritime inflow, uplift, rain.
2. **Winter:** land high, continental outflow, reduced rain.
3. **Moisture source:** adjoining warm ocean.
4. **Rain conversion:** convergence + convection + orography.
5. **Asian intensity:** largest adjoining continental mass.
6. **Current role:** reinforcement, not sole cause.
7. **Monsoon meaning:** seasonal circulation change.
8. **Scale caution:** broad type does not erase local relief.
9. **Answer method:** mechanism before consequences.
10. **Limitation:** annual variability requires weather-scale analysis.

### Lesson-local Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Explain why the warm-temperate eastern margin has a summer rainfall maximum.

**Sample response:** The summer maximum arises from a seasonal shift from continental to maritime control. Strong heating of the adjoining landmass lowers surface pressure relative to the ocean and draws warm, moisture-bearing maritime air onshore. Convergence over the heated land, convection and uplift along relief cool this air and convert its moisture into rain. Warm offshore water sustains the moisture source, while tropical cyclones can add episodic heavy rainfall. In winter the land cools rapidly, higher pressure develops over the interior and cooler, drier continental air flows toward the coast, reducing the maritime moisture supply. The mechanism is strongest in East Asia because its vast landmass produces the greatest seasonal pressure contrast. Thus, the rainfall maximum follows from moisture transport plus uplift, not from latitude or ocean warmth alone.

**Why this earns marks — quantified rubric (10 marks):** seasonal pressure contrast 2; complete summer moisture-to-rain chain 3; winter reversal 2; East Asian evidence 1; modifiers, limitation and conclusion 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** If a warm ocean current lies offshore, why may an eastern margin still fail to receive heavy summer rain?

**Model answer:** A warm current can supply heat and moisture, but heavy rain also requires a landward circulation and sufficient uplift through convergence, convection or relief; without transport and ascent, oceanic moisture does not become continental rainfall.

**Misconception to avoid:** Never treat a moisture source as a complete rainfall mechanism.

**Bridge to Lesson 3:** The shared engine is now clear. We can therefore explain variation: **why is the Asian winter harsher, the Gulf variant only slight-monsoonal and the Natal variant non-monsoonal?**

### Visual first — one family, three continental settings

| Controlling axis | China type | Gulf type | Natal type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemisphere | Northern | Northern | Southern |
| Adjoining landmass | Vast Asia | Large North America | Narrower or more ocean-moderated southern lands |
| Seasonal reversal | Strongest | Partial / slight-monsoonal | Weak; non-monsoonal |
| Winter character | Cool to cold and comparatively dry | Milder; rain less sharply seasonal | Mildest and strongly maritime |
| Summer character | Hot, humid, rainy | Hot, humid, rainy | Warm, moist, rainy |
| Signature examples | Central/north China, southern Japan | SE United States and Gulf coast | Natal, NSW, SE South America |

*The variants differ chiefly because the adjoining continents do not heat, cool or supply air masses in the same way.*

### 1. The controlling variable: size and position of the landmass

The three sub-types share an eastern-margin summer-rain tendency. Their biggest difference is the strength of continental influence, especially in winter.

```text
larger, colder interior
      → stronger winter outflow
      → colder / drier winter

more surrounding ocean
      → weaker continental outflow
      → milder winter
```

✅ **Fact:** The Asian type has the harshest winters.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The southern-hemisphere variants are moderated by the greater oceanic character of the southern mid-latitudes and by smaller continental interiors.

### 2. China type — the most monsoonal expression

The large Asian continent creates the clearest seasonal contrast:

- intense summer heating;
- strong Pacific inflow;
- hot and humid rainy season;
- pronounced winter continental outflow;
- late-summer typhoon exposure.

✅ **Textbook station evidence:** A standard station comparison records an annual temperature range of about **28°F at Canton, 27°F at Swatow and 22°F at Hong Kong**, illustrating how the annual range decreases southward and coastward.

⚠️ **Data limitation:** These are historical textbook comparison figures, not current station normals. Use them to demonstrate the **direction of maritime moderation**, not as a contemporary climate bulletin.

### 3. Gulf type — similar outcome, weaker monsoonal reversal

The south-eastern United States receives abundant warm-season moisture from the Gulf of Mexico and western Atlantic. Yet the circulation is not a copy of the Asian monsoon.

Its character is **slight-monsoonal** because:

- summer maritime moisture is strong;
- warm-season convection and storms are important;
- winter is less uniformly dry than in the classic Asian expression;
- North American frontal systems remain influential.

> 🔑 **Distinction:** Similar rainfall seasonality does not prove identical atmospheric circulation.

### 4. Natal type — the non-monsoonal southern expression

The Natal variant includes:

- Natal/KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa;
- eastern Australia, especially New South Wales;
- south-eastern South America: southern Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay and northern Argentina.

It is called **non-monsoonal** because maritime influence is more persistent and the continental seasonal reversal is weaker. It still belongs to the family through its warm eastern-margin position and adequate summer rain.

### 5. India-centric analogy with a limit

India helps us understand the control variable:

- the Indian monsoon shows how a vast heated landmass can draw moist air inland;
- the North-East shows how Bay-of-Bengal moisture and relief can intensify summer rain;
- but India’s tropical monsoon system must not be relabelled wholesale as the warm-temperate China type.

The analogy explains the **mechanism**, not identity.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** “If the Natal type is non-monsoonal, it should not belong to the same climate family.”

**Reply:** A climate family can be defined by a shared temperature–rainfall outcome and map position even when the detailed circulation differs. The Natal type retains warm-temperate eastern-margin warmth, moisture and summer-rain tendency but lacks the strong Asian seasonal reversal.

**Residual limitation:** Regional labels simplify transitional zones. No sharp line separates one variant from all neighbouring climates.

### 7. UPSC application — convert description into explanation

Weak answer:

> China, Gulf and Natal are three sub-types found in different continents.

Strong answer:

> The three regions share a warm eastern-margin summer-rain regime, but winter severity declines as the adjoining continental mass becomes smaller and maritime moderation increases; hence the Asian type is strongly monsoonal, the Gulf type slight-monsoonal and the Natal type non-monsoonal.

### UPSC traps

- ❌ Natal type means only South Africa.
  ✅ It also includes eastern Australia and south-eastern South America.
- ❌ Gulf type is a complete Asian-style monsoon.
  ✅ It is only slight-monsoonal.
- ❌ The three variants have identical winters.
  ✅ Continental size changes winter severity.
- ❌ The historical station ranges are current normals.
  ✅ They are textbook evidence of the coastward moderation gradient.

### Mini recap

1. All three variants share warm eastern-margin summer rain.
2. China type has the strongest seasonal pressure reversal.
3. Gulf type is only slight-monsoonal.
4. Natal type is non-monsoonal.
5. Asia’s large landmass produces the strongest winter continentality.
6. Southern variants are more maritime.
7. Similar rainfall timing can emerge from somewhat different circulation mixes.
8. Regional transitions remain gradual rather than perfectly bounded.

### Revision notes

1. **China:** central/north China + southern Japan.
2. **Gulf:** SE United States/Gulf-facing region.
3. **Natal:** South Africa + NSW + SE South America.
4. **Main discriminator:** continental size and winter outflow.
5. **Strongest monsoon tendency:** China.
6. **Slight monsoon tendency:** Gulf.
7. **Non-monsoonal:** Natal.
8. **Maritime moderation:** strongest in southern variants.
9. **Station pattern:** annual range decreases southward/coastward.
10. **Exam sentence:** same family, unequal continentality.

### Lesson-local Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Account for the regional differences among the China, Gulf and Natal types.

**Sample response:** The three types share a warm-temperate eastern-margin position, adequate moisture and a summer-rain tendency, but the size and thermal behaviour of the adjoining landmass differ. The vast Asian continent develops a strong summer low and a strong winter high; therefore the China type is the most distinctly temperate-monsoonal, with hot wet summers and cooler, drier winters. The south-eastern United States receives abundant Gulf and Atlantic moisture, but frontal systems and less complete seasonal reversal make the Gulf type only slight-monsoonal. Natal, eastern Australia and south-eastern South America are more ocean-moderated and lack a powerful winter continental outflow, producing the mildest, non-monsoonal expression. Thus, latitude gives the broad thermal zone, while continental size and maritime influence explain the differences.

**Why this earns marks — quantified rubric (10 marks):** shared family frame 2; China explanation 2; Gulf explanation 2; Natal explanation 2; continentality-led comparison and verdict 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is winter severity a better discriminator among the three variants than summer warmth?

**Model answer:** All three receive warm-season maritime influence, but their winters depend on how large and cold the adjoining continental interior becomes; Asia supplies the strongest cold, dry outflow, while the more ocean-moderated southern variants have much milder winters.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not compare sub-types only by crop or place names when the causal difference is continentality.

**Bridge to Lesson 4:** Regional variation explains the background climate. We now add the high-impact weather process most associated with East Asia: **the western North Pacific typhoon.**

### Visual first — from warm ocean to coastal hazard

```text
WARM WESTERN NORTH PACIFIC
          ↓
pre-existing low-level disturbance
          ↓
moist inflow + deep convection
          ↓
condensation releases latent heat
          ↓
surface pressure falls and circulation strengthens
          ↓
favourable Coriolis effect + low disruptive wind shear
          ↓
TROPICAL CYCLONE / TYPHOON
          ↓
westward or north-westward steering under subtropical circulation
          ↓
South China Sea / East Asian coast exposure
          ↓
wind + extreme rain + surge + river and urban flooding
```

*A typhoon is a tropical cyclone in the western North Pacific; the regional name changes, not the basic physics.*

### 1. The background temperature and rainfall regime

The core seasonal pattern is:

| Season | Typical condition | Main control |
|---|---|---|
| Summer | Hot or warm, humid and rainy | Maritime inflow, convection, relief and storms |
| Late summer | Peak tropical-cyclone exposure in the textbook South China setting | Warm ocean and favourable storm tracks |
| Winter | Cooler and generally drier | Continental outflow, strongest in Asia |

✅ **Fact:** The standard China-type description places destructive typhoons especially in **July–September**, moving westward from the Pacific toward coasts around the South China Sea.

⚠️ **Qualification:** This is a high-yield regional seasonal emphasis, not a claim that the western North Pacific has no storms outside those months or that every storm moves due west.

#### Climograph diagnostic — read the seasons, not the place name


| Climograph clue | Eastern-margin family | Mediterranean type | Marine west coast/British type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall season | Summer maximum or strong warm-season concentration | Summer minimum; winter maximum | Rain in all months |
| Temperature range | Moderate to large in the Asian subtype; milder in Gulf/Natal variants | Moderate | Small, maritime |
| Safest diagnostic | Rain rises with summer heat | Rain falls as summer heat rises | No pronounced dry season and weak thermal range |

⚠️ **Limit:** This is a normalised diagnostic, not observed monthly data. Gulf and Natal variants may have a less pronounced winter minimum than the Asian subtype.

### 2. What a typhoon needs

A complete answer should separate **energy**, **organisation** and **steering**:

| Requirement | Function | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Warm ocean and moist boundary layer | Supplies heat and water vapour | Warm water alone is insufficient |
| Pre-existing disturbance and convergence | Starts organised ascent and rotation | Not every disturbance develops |
| Coriolis effect away from the Equator | Supports rotating circulation | Rotation is weak very near the Equator |
| Deep convection and latent-heat release | Warms the core and lowers pressure | Dry-air intrusion can disrupt it |
| Relatively low vertical wind shear | Allows the vertical vortex to remain aligned | Strong shear can weaken organisation |
| Upper-level outflow | Removes rising air efficiently | Poor outflow can cap intensification |

### 3. Why the track is commonly westward, then sometimes recurves

Many western North Pacific cyclones are steered westward or north-westward on the southern side of the subtropical ridge. Some later turn poleward and eastward when they reach a weakness in that ridge or enter the mid-latitude westerly flow.

```text
REGIONAL EXPOSURE CORRIDOR — NOT ONE MANDATORY STORM TRACK

Philippines → Taiwan → south/east China → Korea → Japan
```

This chain identifies the principal exposed island-and-coast arc. A particular typhoon may affect only one segment, cross several segments or recurve over the ocean.

> 🔑 **Trap:** Genesis, intensity and track are different questions. Warm water helps genesis and intensification; the larger steering flow controls track.

### 4. Current official evidence — frequency is not the same as energy

✅ **Fact:** The WMO supplement to the *State of the Climate 2025* reports **27 tropical cyclones in the Northwest Pacific in 2025**, above the long-term average.

✅ **Fact:** The same WMO source states that many were relatively weak, only one reached super-typhoon status, and basin accumulated cyclone energy was well below average.

✅ **Fact:** JMA’s RSMC Tokyo climatology gives a **1991–2020 annual normal of 25.1** tropical cyclones reaching tropical-storm intensity or higher in the basin; its 2025 table records 27.

⚠️ **Inference:** A season can have an above-normal storm count but below-normal total cyclone energy. Therefore, storm number, maximum intensity, duration, landfall and damage must not be used interchangeably.

### 5. Climate versus weather versus trend

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| “A typhoon struck this coast, so it must be the Asian China subtype.” | Incomplete: one event cannot classify a climate. |
| “The Asian China-subtype coast is typhoon-exposed.” | Valid as a regional hazard tendency. |
| “One powerful typhoon proves a long-term intensification trend.” | Invalid without a long series and attribution analysis. |
| “Warmer seas can raise the potential for stronger rainfall or intensification.” | Physically plausible, but event outcome also depends on shear, moisture, track and internal dynamics. |

### 6. The hazard is not only wind

```text
TYPHOON
 ├─ destructive wind
 ├─ storm surge and coastal inundation
 ├─ extreme rainfall
 ├─ river flooding
 ├─ landslides on steep terrain
 └─ cascading infrastructure and crop loss
```

Loss depends on exposure and vulnerability:

```text
HAZARD ≠ DISASTER

disaster risk
  = physical hazard
  × exposed population/assets
  × vulnerability
  ÷ preparedness and resilience
```

### 7. Objection and reply

**Objection:** “Typhoons are an external disturbance, so they should not be taught as part of the climate.”

**Reply:** A single storm is weather, but recurrent seasonal cyclone exposure contributes to the Asian China subtype’s rainfall variability, hazard profile and land-use decisions. It belongs in climate teaching as a recurring process, not as proof from one event.

**Residual limitation:** The climate type explains broad exposure. It does not determine the exact track, intensity or losses of any named storm.

For a cyclone question, write:

2. intensification mechanism;
3. steering and track;
4. hazards beyond wind;

6. caution against single-event trend claims.

- ❌ Typhoon is a different phenomenon from a tropical cyclone.
  ✅ It is the western North Pacific regional name.
- ❌ All typhoons form near the coast.
  ✅ They form over warm ocean and may later approach the coast.
- ❌ Storm count equals intensity.
  ✅ Count, ACE, peak intensity and impact are separate measures.
- ❌ Warm sea alone guarantees intensification.
  ✅ Shear, moisture, structure and outflow also matter.
- ❌ Damage measures only meteorological strength.
  ✅ Exposure, vulnerability and preparedness shape losses.

3. Rotation, organised convection, low shear and outflow are also required.

2. **Genesis:** warm moist ocean + disturbance + rotation.

4. **Track:** commonly west/north-west, possible recurvature.

### Lesson-local Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Explain the role of typhoons in the climatic and hazard geography of East Asia.

**Sample response:** Typhoons are western North Pacific tropical cyclones that form over warm ocean water when a pre-existing disturbance, moist convection, Coriolis-supported rotation, limited vertical wind shear and upper-level outflow combine. **Misconception to avoid:** Do not use storm count as a synonym for basin intensity, destructiveness or long-term trend.

### 1. Natural vegetation: what should grow before land clearance?

✅ **Fact:** Warm, wet summers support luxuriant forests.

⚠️ **Analytical synthesis:** Depending on latitude, winter cold, altitude, soil and disturbance, the natural cover ranges from warm-temperate evergreen broad-leaved forest to mixed forest.

The vegetation is not equatorial rainforest:

| Warm-temperate eastern margin | Equatorial rainforest |
|---|---|
| Clear seasonal temperature change | Uniformly hot all year |
| Summer rain maximum, winter contrast | Rain throughout the year |
| Evergreen–deciduous mixing possible | Multi-storeyed evergreen rainforest |

✅ **Regional example:** Eucalyptus forests are characteristic of the eastern Australian expression.

### 2. Why agriculture can be intensive

Climate supplies three linked advantages:

1. **warm growing season** — supports rapid crop growth;
2. **summer rainfall** — water arrives when heat and plant demand are high;
3. **long frost-free period** — lengthens the cultivation window.

Where soils, labour and water control permit, these advantages support intensive cultivation and even multiple cropping.

```text
summer heat + summer moisture
          ↓
high biological productivity
          ↓
long crop calendar
          ↓
intensive land use
```

### 3. The signature regional examples

| Region / subtype | Textbook land-use example | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| China type | Rice and tea | Warm humid growing season; rice uses moist lowlands, tea needs humid, well-drained slopes |
| Gulf type | Maize belt | Warm growing season and adequate moisture, qualified by soils and farming technology |
| Natal type, South Africa | Sugar-cane | Warm moist coastal conditions and long growing period |
| Eastern Australia | Eucalyptus forest | Native vegetation associated with the regional temperature and rainfall regime |

### 4. Rice and tea must not be placed in the same micro-environment

The broad climate can support both, but their site requirements differ:

| Crop | Favoured site within the humid region | Key limit |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | Level alluvial or deltaic lowland with water availability | Not every rice system is permanently flooded |
| Tea | Humid, acidic, well-drained slopes with abundant labour | High rainfall does not help if roots are waterlogged |

> 🔑 **Trap:** A climate may support several crops, but relief and drainage decide where each crop actually fits.

### 5. Why dense population is common but not climatically determined

Productive plains, navigable rivers, long cultivation histories and labour-intensive wet-rice systems can support high rural densities. Yet climate alone does not “cause” dense population.

Other necessary variables include:

- alluvial soil;
- irrigation and water control;
- historical settlement;
- labour supply;
- land tenure;
- transport and market access;
- public policy and technology.

⚠️ **Inference:** Climate creates a productive possibility; institutions and society decide how fully it is used and how gains and risks are distributed.

### 6. The flood–fertility paradox

Summer rain and rivers renew soil moisture and may deposit fertile sediment, but the same system can:

- inundate crops and settlements;
- erode banks;
- waterlog fields;
- damage roads and storage;
- intensify disease and livelihood disruption.

```text
river flooding
   ├─ possible benefits: moisture + silt renewal
   └─ possible costs: erosion + inundation + displacement
```

Whether a flood is beneficial or disastrous depends on depth, timing, sediment, channel behaviour, embankments, floodplain occupation and preparedness.

### 7. Objection and reply

**Objection:** “Because rice, tea, maize and sugar-cane occur in the region, climate determines agriculture.”

**Reply:** Climate sets a broad suitability envelope. Actual land use depends on soil, slope, drainage, irrigation, labour, technology, pests, prices and markets. The same climate can support different farming systems, and the same crop can occur under different climates where irrigation or technology compensates.

**Residual limitation:** Textbook crop associations are spatial clues, not current production rankings.

```text
climatic opportunity
   → crop or forest response
   → human intensification
   → exposure concentration
   → hazard and adaptation
```

This converts a descriptive climate question into an analytical geography answer.

- ❌ Climate determines farm output.
  ✅ It enables and constrains; human and edaphic factors mediate.

  ✅ Its effects depend on magnitude, timing, sediment and exposure.

  ✅ Rankings require dated official statistics.

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** “The agricultural advantage and hazard exposure of warm-temperate eastern-margin regions arise from the same climatic mechanism.” Explain.

### 1. China type versus Mediterranean type

This is the highest-value comparison because the climates can occur at broadly similar warm-temperate latitudes on opposite continental margins.

| Axis | China type | Mediterranean type |
|---|---|---|
| Continental margin | Eastern | Western |
| Summer | Hot/warm, humid and rainy | Hot, dry and sunny |
| Winter | Cooler, generally drier | Mild and wet |
| Rainfall maximum | Summer | Winter |
| Main summer control | Maritime inflow, convection, storms | Subtropical-high subsidence |
| Main winter control | Continental outflow | Equatorward-shifted westerlies and depressions |
| Vegetation | Warm-temperate evergreen/mixed forest | Sclerophyll scrub/woodland |
| Agriculture | Rice, tea and intensive summer-season farming | Vines, olives, citrus, winter cereals and irrigated summer horticulture |
| Major hazard | Typhoons and floods | Summer drought and wildfire |

> 🔑 **Answer line:** The eastern margin converts summer oceanic influence into rainfall; the western margin falls under summer subtropical subsidence and waits for winter westerlies.

### 2. China type versus tropical monsoon climate

| Axis | China type | Tropical monsoon |
|---|---|---|
| Broad latitude | Warm temperate/subtropical | Tropical |
| Winter temperature | Cool to mild; coldest in Asia | Generally warm |
| Wind reversal | Strong in Asian expression, weaker elsewhere | Defining, large-scale seasonal reversal |
| Rainfall | Summer maximum, variant-dependent winter dryness | Strongly summer-concentrated, often with a marked dry season |
| Examples | East Asia, SE USA, Natal-type regions | Indian subcontinent and other tropical monsoon margins |

They are related because both connect summer continental heating with maritime inflow. They differ because the eastern-margin family is a **temperate modification**: its Asian China subtype has cooler winters and the strongest reversal, while Gulf and Natal variants are less fully monsoonal.

### 3. China type versus tropical marine climate

The tropical marine climate receives onshore trade winds for much of the year and therefore has steadier rainfall. The warm-temperate eastern-margin family has stronger seasonal thermal contrast and a clearer summer maximum.

```text
tropical marine: persistent onshore trades → more even rainfall
China type: seasonal maritime dominance → summer maximum
```

### 4. China type versus British type

The British type occupies cooler western margins under onshore westerlies:

- mild winters and cool summers;
- small annual temperature range;
- rainfall throughout the year, often with autumn–winter maximum;
- deciduous forest and dairying/mixed farming.

The China type has hotter summers, larger annual range, stronger summer rain and greater tropical-cyclone exposure.

### 5. China type versus Laurentian type

Both are eastern-margin climates with a summer rainfall maximum, so the temperature regime is the decisive discriminator.

| Axis | China type | Laurentian type |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal class | Warm temperate | Cool temperate |
| Winter | Cool to cold, depending on variant | Severe, cold and snowy |
| Vegetation | Warm-temperate evergreen/mixed | Cool-temperate mixed/coniferous |
| Marine economy | Agriculture prominent | Shelf fisheries, timber, pulp and paper prominent |
| Current association | Warm-current seaward fringe | Warm–cold current convergence, fog and fisheries |
| Hemisphere | Both | Effectively Northern only |

### 6. Why classification by threshold is useful but incomplete

Köppen provides a compact descriptive code from temperature and precipitation. It helps distinguish:

- **Cw:** dry winter;
- **Cf:** no dry season;
- **Cs:** dry summer.

But the code does not explain why the regime exists. A genetic explanation must add pressure, winds, land–sea contrast, currents and relief.

**Objection:** “If Köppen already gives the code, mechanism is unnecessary.”

**Reply:** The code tells us the observed pattern. UPSC Mains asks why the pattern occurs and what it causes. Description and mechanism are complementary.

**Residual limitation:** Threshold codes simplify gradual climatic transitions, so a mapped boundary cannot replace station data or a circulation-based explanation.

### 7. India-centric comparison

India gives three useful contrasts:

- tropical monsoon India shows a stronger low-latitude seasonal reversal;
- the Bengal–Brahmaputra/North-East belt shows a humid eastern-margin analogy;
- Himalayan altitude produces temperate belts that cannot be classified by latitude alone.

For any “compare” directive:

1. identify the common basis;
2. choose causal axes;
3. compare each axis side by side;
4. explain the reason for difference;
5. end with the most decisive discriminator.

  ✅ Winter temperature and associated economy differ sharply.
- ❌ Köppen code explains causation.
  ✅ It describes a measured regime.
- ❌ British type has a large annual range.
  ✅ Maritime moderation keeps its range small.

1. Mediterranean is the opposite seasonal-rain regime.
2. Tropical monsoon is warmer and more fully defined by seasonal reversal.
3. Tropical marine rain is more evenly distributed.
4. British type is cooler, western-margin and maritime.
5. Laurentian is cooler, eastern-margin and strongly continental in winter.

### Lesson-local Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Why do eastern and western continental margins at similar warm-temperate latitudes have contrasting rainfall seasons?

**Sample response:** Their position relative to seasonal circulation differs. On warm-temperate western margins, the subtropical high shifts poleward in summer, causing subsidence, clear skies and drought. In winter it retreats, allowing onshore westerlies and travelling depressions to bring rain; this produces the Mediterranean regime. Eastern margins face a warm ocean during the heated season. Summer continental low pressure draws moist maritime air inland, while convection, relief and tropical cyclones generate rainfall. Winter cooling then favours drier continental outflow. Thus, the western margin has a dry summer and wet winter, whereas the eastern China-type margin has a wet summer and generally drier winter. Ocean currents and relief reinforce the contrast, but seasonal wind direction is the decisive link.

**Why this earns marks — quantified rubric (10 marks):** direct comparative thesis 1; western-margin mechanism 3; eastern-margin mechanism 3; current/relief qualification 1; decisive comparison and conclusion 2.

**Model answer:** Rainfall season is the safest discriminator: the China type receives its main rain in summer through maritime inflow, while the Mediterranean type has a pronounced summer drought and receives its principal rain from winter westerlies and depressions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not rely on latitude, warmth or horticulture alone, because those features can overlap.

Climate supplies heavy seasonal runoff, but the flood outcome also reflects:

- enormous sediment load from a young mountain system;
- braided and shifting channels;
- bank erosion;
- channel and floodplain geometry;
- intense occupation of exposed land;
- embankments and other interventions;
- forecasting, evacuation and local resilience.

Therefore:

```text
monsoon rain is a major driver
but
rainfall alone is not a complete flood explanation
```

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Practice 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain how a climograph can be used to identify the warm-temperate eastern-margin family and distinguish it from neighbouring Mediterranean and marine west coast climates. Answer in 150 words.

### Directive decoding

“Explain” requires a reading method plus diagnostic application. The answer must interpret the temperature and rainfall curves together rather than list climate names.

### Model answer

A climograph plots monthly temperature and precipitation, so identification should begin with **seasonal timing**, not rainfall total alone. The warm-temperate eastern-margin family shows a warm or hot summer, a cooler winter and rainfall that rises toward the warm season. The Asian China subtype usually displays the clearest summer maximum and a marked annual temperature cycle; Gulf and Natal variants may have a weaker winter rainfall minimum.

A Mediterranean climograph reverses this relation: temperature peaks while rainfall falls to a summer minimum, with the main rain in winter. A marine west coast climograph shows a small annual temperature range and precipitation in every month rather than a pronounced summer maximum.

Thus, the safest diagnosis combines temperature range, rainfall season and dry-season position. Station altitude, coastal exposure and subtype variation must qualify any schematic reading.

### Why this earns marks — quantified rubric (10 marks)

- explains how to read both climograph variables: 2;
- identifies the eastern-margin family signature: 3;
- distinguishes the Mediterranean pattern: 2;
- distinguishes the marine west coast pattern: 2;
- adds subtype/station qualification: 1.

## Practice 2 — 15 marks

**Question:** Compare the China, Gulf and Natal variants of the warm-temperate eastern-margin climate. Explain the physical basis of their differences. Answer in 250 words.

### Directive decoding

“Compare” requires common axes and reasons for difference. A place-name list will not satisfy the question.

### Model answer

The China, Gulf and Natal types form one climatic family because all occupy warm-temperate eastern continental margins, receive adequate warm-season maritime moisture and lack the pronounced Mediterranean summer drought. Their differences arise mainly from the size of the adjoining landmass and the strength of winter continental outflow.

**China type:** Central and north China with southern Japan provide the strongest temperate-monsoonal expression. The vast Asian interior heats intensely in summer, drawing moist Pacific air inland, and cools strongly in winter, sending drier continental air toward the coast. Summers are hot, humid and rainy; winters are the coldest and driest of the three. Late-summer typhoons add hazard and rainfall variability.

**Gulf type:** The south-eastern United States receives abundant moisture from the Gulf of Mexico and western Atlantic. Its summers are hot, humid and stormy, but winter precipitation and frontal systems remain more important than in the classic Asian case. It is therefore only slight-monsoonal.

**Natal type:** Natal/KwaZulu-Natal, eastern Australia and south-eastern South America are more ocean-moderated. Their smaller or narrower continental interiors produce weaker winter outflow and milder winters, making the expression non-monsoonal.

Thus, latitude establishes the broad warm-temperate setting, but continentality and maritime moderation grade the family from strongly monsoonal China through slight-monsoonal Gulf to non-monsoonal Natal.

- Gulf-type mechanism and evidence: 3;
- Natal-type mechanism and evidence: 3;
- continentality-led synthesis: 2;
- graded conclusion: 2.

## Practice 3 — 20 marks

**Question:** “The warm-temperate eastern-margin climate creates agricultural abundance and hydro-meteorological vulnerability through the same summer-maritime mechanism.” Discuss, with a bounded reference to India. Answer in 250 words.

### Directive decoding

“Discuss” requires explanation of the proposition, evidence, limitations and a reasoned verdict. The India reference must illustrate rather than erase climatic differences.

### Model answer

Warm-temperate eastern margins become productive because summer continental heating draws warm, moisture-bearing air from the adjoining ocean. Convergence, convection and relief convert that moisture into rainfall during the period of maximum heat and plant growth. A long frost-free season and adequate summer moisture support luxuriant forest and intensive agriculture: rice and tea in East Asia, maize in the Gulf-type region and sugar-cane in Natal.

The same pathway creates vulnerability. Warm western North Pacific water and favourable atmospheric conditions support typhoons, while heavy rain over densely cultivated coastal plains produces river floods, erosion, waterlogging and crop loss. WMO’s 2025 record illustrates the need for indicator discipline: the basin had 27 cyclones, above the long-term average, but below-average accumulated cyclone energy. Storm count is not a damage measure; exposure and preparedness matter.

India’s Bengal–Brahmaputra–North-East belt offers a bounded analogue. Bay-of-Bengal moisture and hill uplift sustain rice lowlands and tea slopes, yet intense rain combines with sediment-rich braided channels, bank erosion and floodplain occupation to create severe flood risk.

However, climate is not destiny. Soil, drainage, labour, irrigation and markets shape output, while planning, warnings and infrastructure shape loss. India’s tropical monsoon and highland mosaic also prevent exact China-type classification. The proposition is therefore valid as a shared causal framework, not as a deterministic or taxonomic equivalence.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Identity and map

- Warm-temperate eastern-margin climate, also called **China type** or **temperate monsoon** in its Asian core.
- Occurs on eastern continental margins just outside the tropics.
- **Eastern-margin family** covers the China, Gulf and Natal variants; **Asian China subtype** refers specifically to central/north China and southern Japan.
- More rain than Mediterranean western margins at similar latitudes.
- Summer rainfall maximum; no Mediterranean-type summer drought.
- Do not equate the regional label with all China, all humid subtropical climates or every eastern coast.

## Three regional expressions

| Variant | Location | Monsoonal character | Winter |
|---|---|---|---|
| China | Central/north China; southern Japan | Strongest, temperate monsoon | Coolest/coldest and driest |
| Gulf | SE United States | Slight-monsoonal | Milder; frontal influence remains |
| Natal | Natal, NSW, SE South America | Non-monsoonal | Mildest, most maritime |

> 🔑 **Recall line:** Continental size grades the family—Asia strongest, southern variants mildest.

## Seasonal mechanism

```text
SUMMER: land heating → land low → moist onshore flow
        → convergence/convection/relief → summer rain

WINTER: land cooling → land high → continental outflow
        → weaker maritime moisture → drier tendency
```

- Warm offshore water reinforces heat and moisture supply.
- It does not replace the need for transport and uplift.
- Asia’s large interior strengthens seasonal pressure reversal.

## Temperature and rainfall

- Hot/warm humid summers; cooler winters.
- Annual range decreases southward and coastward.
- Historical textbook evidence: Canton about 28°F, Swatow 27°F and Hong Kong 22°F annual range.
- Treat these as comparative textbook values, not current normals.
- Rainfall timing is a safer identifier than rainfall total alone.
- Climograph diagnostic: temperature and rainfall rise together toward summer; Mediterranean rain falls as summer heat rises, while marine west coast rain persists through the year with a smaller thermal range.

## Typhoons

- Typhoon = western North Pacific tropical cyclone.
- Textbook south-China emphasis: most frequent in late summer, especially July–September.
- Formation requires warm moist ocean, disturbance, rotation, convection, limited shear and outflow.
- Track commonly west/north-west under steering flow; some systems recurve.
- Exposure corridor: Philippines → Taiwan → south/east China → Korea → Japan; this is a regional arc, not one compulsory storm track.
- Hazards: wind, surge, extreme rain, river flood and landslide.
- WMO 2025: 27 Northwest Pacific cyclones; only one super typhoon; ACE below average.
- JMA 1991–2020 normal: 25.1 storms reaching tropical-storm intensity or higher annually.
- Count, ACE, peak intensity, landfall and loss are separate measures.

## Vegetation and land use

- Warm wet summers support luxuriant warm-temperate evergreen/mixed forest.
- NSW example: eucalyptus forest.
- China type: rice and tea.
- Gulf type: maize belt.
- Natal example: sugar-cane.
- Rice favours wet/alluvial lowlands; tea favours humid, acidic, drained slopes.
- Climate enables; soil, relief, water, labour, technology and markets mediate.

## Core comparisons

### China versus Mediterranean

- Eastern versus western margin.
- Summer rain versus summer drought.
- Winter drier tendency versus winter rain.
- Maritime inflow versus summer subtropical subsidence.
- Warm forest/rice–tea versus sclerophyll/vine–olive system.

### China versus tropical monsoon

- Warm-temperate modification versus tropical system.
- Cooler winter and regional non-monsoonal variants versus defining full reversal.

### China versus British

- Warm eastern, summer rain, larger range versus cool western, year-round rain, small range.

### China versus Laurentian

- Both eastern-margin and summer-rain.
- China is warmer and agricultural; Laurentian is colder with fisheries/forestry emphasis.

## Köppen and scale controls

- Cw = dry winter; Cf = no dry season; Cs = dry summer.
- Köppen describes measured temperature/rainfall; it does not independently explain causes.
- A regional label and station code are not one-to-one.
- Plain, hill, valley, climatic region and administrative unit must not be merged.

## India application

- Bengal–Brahmaputra–North-East is a **bounded functional analogue**.
- Bay-of-Bengal moisture + convergence + relief → heavy summer rain.
- Alluvial lowlands → rice.
- Humid drained slopes → tea.
- Floods reflect rain plus sediment, braiding, bank erosion and floodplain exposure.
- India’s tropical monsoon and altitude mosaics prevent exact China-type identity.
- ASDMA’s 27 June 2026 advisory relayed an IMD heavy-rainfall forecast and preparedness risks; it was not an observed rainfall, flood-loss or tea-output report.

## Optional regionalisation facts

- R.L. Singh Humid North-East: NE India except Tripura + Sikkim + NW West Bengal.
- Current MDoNER North Eastern Region: eight states, including both Sikkim and Tripura; do not substitute it for the historical climatic boundary.
- Rainfall above 200 cm; July 25–33°C; January 10–25°C.
- Khullar Bengal and Assam Plain: West Bengal plain + Brahmaputra valley.
- About 150 cm rainfall from Bay-of-Bengal branch; hot sub-humid to humid/perhumid.
- Moisture + alluvium supports rice.
- Trewartha includes H for undifferentiated highlands.
- Different boundaries explain different regional averages.

## Ten examiner traps

1. China type is not all China.
2. Natal type is not monsoonal.
3. Gulf type is not a complete Asian monsoon.
4. Rain maximum is summer, not winter.
5. Mediterranean is the opposite seasonal-rain regime.
6. Warm current alone does not cause rain.
7. Typhoons do not form only near the coast.
8. Storm count is not intensity or damage.
9. Climate does not determine one crop.
10. North-East analogy is not exact classification.
