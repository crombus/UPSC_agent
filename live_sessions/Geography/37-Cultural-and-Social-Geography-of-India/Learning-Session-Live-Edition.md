### Visual atlas — from trait to changing region

```text
HEARTH
  │ relocation / contact / media / trade / pilgrimage
  ▼
DIFFUSION ROUTES ──> CORE AREA ──> TRANSITION BELT ──> MIXED BORDERLAND
                          │                │
                          └──── migration ┴────> new regional identity

FORMAL region    = dominant mapped trait
FUNCTIONAL region= interaction around a node/network
VERNACULAR region= perceived and named by people
```

*Caption: a cultural region is produced through concentration and interaction; it is not a sealed container.*

### Diagnostic — three-region sort

Classify each as mainly formal, functional or vernacular: a language-majority belt; a pilgrimage circuit centred on linked sacred places; “the Deccan” as popularly imagined. Then name one reason every answer remains provisional.

### Teaching — the geographic question

✅ Cultural geography studies how language, religion, ethnicity, livelihood, settlement and identity vary over space. Social geography adds access, inequality, migration, caste/community, tribe, gender and state action. Geography does not assign an inherent character to a group. It explains how ecology, historical settlement, routes, markets, institutions and unequal power produce spatial patterns.

| Concept | Geographic test | India-facing example |
|---|---|---|
| Hearth | Where did a practice or identity have an early concentration? | A literary-language core or sacred centre |
| Diffusion | How did it spread: relocation, contact, hierarchy or network? | Migrants carrying food, worship and language into a metropolis |
| Core | Where is a trait relatively concentrated and institutionally reproduced? | A state-level language core |
| Transition zone | Where do traits overlap or change gradually? | Bilingual border districts |
| Cultural landscape | How is culture visible in built form and land use? | Sacred groves, pilgrimage streets, pastoral routes, fishing settlements |
| Place identity | How does attachment convert space into meaningful place? | Region names, local narratives and landscape symbols |

⚠️ Formal, functional and vernacular regions can occupy the same territory without matching. A state may contain a language core, a pilgrimage network crossing its boundary, and a vernacular sub-region whose limits are debated. Scale changes the result: a district that appears homogeneous may contain mixed towns, border villages and migrant neighbourhoods.

### Worked spatial application — one coastal city

Take Kochi only as a method demonstration. Port situation encourages long-distance contact; neighbourhoods and worship sites record successive connections; Malayalam provides a regional core; migrant work and commerce create multilingual spaces; metropolitan commuting forms a functional region larger than the municipal boundary. The valid inference is **layering**, not that every resident shares one history or identity.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Cultural geography asks where, why there and how the pattern changes.
2. Formal regions use a dominant trait.
3. Functional regions use flows and nodes.
4. Vernacular regions use perception and naming.
5. Hearth is an origin or early concentration, not necessarily today’s largest centre.
6. Diffusion may follow migration, hierarchy, contact or networks.
7. Cores grade into transition zones.
8. State and cultural boundaries rarely coincide perfectly.
9. Scale can reveal or hide diversity.
10. Spatial association never proves innate group character.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a cultural region better represented as a core–transition system than as a homogeneous block? Ceiling: 130 words.

**Model answer:** A cultural region usually has a core where a language, practice or identity is relatively concentrated and institutionally reproduced. Toward its margins, migration, intermarriage, trade, pilgrimage, state boundaries and neighbouring languages create overlap. The resulting transition belt may be bilingual, multi-religious or functionally tied to more than one centre. Even the core varies internally by class, caste, gender, livelihood and rural–urban setting. Representing the region as a block therefore mistakes a dominant pattern for uniformity. A core–transition model preserves the usefulness of regionalisation while showing mixed borderlands, changing boundaries and scale effects.

**Model word count:** 92 words; ceiling: 130.

### Retrieval — classify before locating

Place these on separate cards: language family, mother tongue, script, Eighth Schedule language, official language and majority language. Build a hierarchy instead of treating them as synonyms.

### Classification lab

| Category | What it means | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| Language family | Historical-linguistic relationship | Not an administrative status |
| Mother tongue | Self-reported language category in census tabulation | Not automatically a scheduled language |
| Script | Writing system | One script may serve several languages |
| Eighth Schedule | Constitutional list containing 22 languages | Does not guarantee district majority |
| Official language | Language authorised for specified governmental use | Different from “national language” |
| Linguistic core | Area of relative concentration | Does not erase minorities or dialect continua |

### Teaching — pattern with caution

✅ Census 2011 language tables, as synthesised in Advanced37, place broad mother-tongue shares at Indo-Aryan 78.05%, Dravidian 19.64%, Austro-Asiatic 1.11% and Tibeto-Burmese 1.01%. These are dated 2011 classifications; they are not a 2026 estimate. India has 22 Eighth Schedule languages.

Broad cores include Hindi varieties across the north-central belt, Bengali in West Bengal with strong Tripura presence, Marathi in Maharashtra, Gujarati in Gujarat, Punjabi in Punjab, Assamese in the Brahmaputra valley, and Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam in their respective peninsular cores. The Northeast and central-eastern uplands contain especially dense language mosaics. Labels such as “Hindi belt” are useful at national scale but conceal dialect continua, non-Hindi communities and major ecological and economic differences.

⚠️ Linguistic reorganisation aligned many state boundaries with dominant language regions, reducing one kind of mismatch while leaving minority pockets, border dialects and multilingual metropolitan areas. Language also diffuses through schooling, administration, media, work migration and urban networks. These processes can expand multilingual competence without automatically replacing home languages.

### Worked map application — border and metropolis

Compare a bilingual interstate border district with Bengaluru. In the border district, a gradient may reflect historical settlement and everyday cross-border exchange. In Bengaluru, languages arrive through labour markets, education and corporate networks; the pattern is nodal and neighbourhood-based rather than a simple boundary gradient. One requires a transition-zone map; the other requires a flow-and-node map.

### Revision register — 12 anchors

1. Family, mother tongue, script and official status differ.
2. India has 22 Eighth Schedule languages.
3. Census 2011 remains the completed national language baseline.
4. Indo-Aryan has the broadest national spread.
5. Dravidian cores dominate the peninsular south.
6. Austroasiatic pockets are important in central-eastern uplands.
7. Tibeto-Burman mosaics are prominent in Himalayan and north-eastern spaces.
8. A linguistic state is not linguistically uniform.
9. Border districts often form continua, not sharp breaks.
10. Metropolises are nodal multilingual regions.
11. Migration can add competence without total language replacement.
12. Attach year and table category to every numerical language claim.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain why India’s linguistic geography cannot be mapped accurately by colouring each state with one language. Ceiling: 140 words.

**Model answer:** State colouring captures dominant linguistic cores but suppresses three spatial realities. First, dialect and language continua cross administrative borders, creating bilingual transition districts. Second, hill, forest and frontier regions contain compact minority pockets whose distribution reflects relief, historical settlement and relative isolation. Third, migration creates multilingual metropolitan and industrial nodes that cannot be represented by an areal majority alone. Classification also matters: a language family, mother tongue, scheduled language and official language are different categories. A better map combines core areas, transition belts, minority pockets and urban flow nodes, with Census 2011 identified as the data year.

### Sacred-network visual

```text
LOCAL SACRED SITE -> pilgrimage route -> regional node -> inter-regional circuit
        │                  │                  │
 land/water meanings   seasonal flows     service settlement

FORMAL concentration map ≠ FUNCTIONAL pilgrimage map ≠ lived neighbourhood map
```

### Contrast diagnostic

Which map would answer each question: (a) where a religious community is concentrated, (b) how pilgrims move, (c) how sacred meaning shapes a riverfront, (d) where mixed neighbourhoods occur? Explain why one choropleth cannot answer all four.

### Worked map reading before theory

Varanasi illustrates a sacred landscape where river, ghats, routes, ritual timings and service occupations form a functional region. Ajmer links a shrine-city to wide pilgrimage flows. Amritsar combines a Sikh regional core with a major sacred node. Kerala’s coastal and urban settlements show long histories of maritime contact and religious plurality. These examples demonstrate different mechanisms; they do not imply uniform behaviour within any community.

### Teaching — distribution and interaction

✅ Census 2011 C-01 records national shares of Hindu 79.8%, Muslim 14.2%, Christian 2.3%, Sikh 1.7%, Buddhist 0.7% and Jain 0.4%. The spatial pattern is more useful than the national average: Hindu populations form the majority across most regions; Muslim concentrations are stronger in the Kashmir Valley, parts of Assam and West Bengal, Lakshadweep, Kerala pockets and many towns; Christian concentrations are especially marked in several north-eastern states and parts of Kerala; Sikh concentration has a Punjab core; Buddhist geographies include Himalayan pockets and Maharashtra’s neo-Buddhist belt; Jain populations show notable urban-commercial concentration in parts of western and northern India.

⚠️ Religious geography has three layers:

1. **Formal:** concentration measured in an area.
2. **Functional:** pilgrimage, institutions and ritual flows.
3. **Landscape:** material and symbolic relations with rivers, hills, groves, streets and buildings.

Ports, frontier towns and metropolitan areas often display layering created by trade and migration. “Syncretic” should describe an evidenced shared practice or landscape, not become a vague label. Census categories record self-identification at a date; they do not map intensity of belief, sect, everyday practice or neighbourhood interaction.

### Synthesis — concentration without essentialism

A valid answer may explain why concentration persists through family networks, institutions, migration histories and place attachment. It must not infer occupation, politics or social conduct from religious location. Regional averages also conceal rural–urban and intra-community differences.

### Revision register — 11 anchors

1. Use Census 2011 for completed all-India religion data.
2. National share and regional concentration answer different questions.
3. Formal religion regions use areal concentration.
4. Pilgrimage creates functional regions.
5. Sacred landscapes connect belief with material place.
6. Port and frontier histories often produce layered settlement.
7. A shrine’s influence can cross state and language boundaries.
8. Census categories do not measure intensity of belief.
9. National averages hide local mosaics.
10. Spatial concentration does not prove fixed social behaviour.
11. Every numerical statement needs community, geography and year.

### Concept check

**Question:** Distinguish a religious concentration region from a sacred functional region using Indian spatial reasoning. Ceiling: 130 words.

**Model answer:** A religious concentration region is defined by the relative share or numerical presence of a community within mapped administrative units, using a dated source such as Census 2011. A sacred functional region is organised by flows toward and among religious nodes: pilgrims, transport, lodging, ritual calendars, markets and linked sites. Punjab’s Sikh concentration illustrates an areal core, while a pilgrimage circuit may connect devotees from many states to a node and therefore cross linguistic and administrative boundaries. The two can overlap but need different maps. Neither map establishes uniform belief or behaviour within the population.

### Sorting challenge

Sort these into **social identity**, **constitutional/administrative category**, or **programme category**: tribe, ethnicity, Scheduled Tribe, Scheduled Area, Sixth Schedule area and PVTG. Identify which items may overlap but are not interchangeable.

### Teaching — association is historical, not natural

✅ Many Scheduled Tribe communities have historical concentrations in forested uplands, hills, plateaux, borderlands and islands. Majid Husain’s OCR-searchable volume describes relative isolation as a pattern; its older figures are not used here as current statistics. ⚠️ The geography must be explained through historical state penetration, land and forest regimes, market access, displacement, transport, ecological livelihoods and community agency—not through an assertion that communities “belong naturally” to isolation.

| Spatial frame | Broad area | Geographic logic |
|---|---|---|
| North-eastern region | Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, Meghalaya, hill tracts and parts of adjoining states | Rugged relief, borderlands, valley–hill contrasts and high linguistic diversity |
| Central tribal belt | Rajasthan–Gujarat through MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha and Maharashtra | Forest-mineral uplands, plateau corridors and uneven accessibility |
| Southern/agency pockets | Andhra–Telangana uplands, Nilgiri–Western Ghats systems | Hill/forest habitats linked to nearby plains and plantation or urban economies |
| Island pockets | Andaman & Nicobar Islands | Strong isolation gradients, small territories and distinct access constraints |

✅ Advanced37 lists Fifth Schedule/Scheduled Areas in Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan and Telangana; Sixth Schedule areas occur in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. These are different constitutional spatial mechanisms; legal doctrine belongs to Polity.

✅ The Ministry of Tribal Affairs’ dated list recognises 75 PVTGs across 18 states and one UT, Andaman & Nicobar Islands. ✅ PIB’s 12 August 2026 release directly records habitation-level mapping under PM JANMAN. ⚠️ Geography uses that anchor to show why dispersed habitation and difficult access matter; it does not evaluate scheme entitlements or performance.

Compare the central belt and Northeast. The central belt often intersects mineral extraction, forest dependence and east–west transport corridors; the Northeast contains multiple hill-valley and borderland systems with different language and settlement histories. “Tribal India” is therefore a mapping family, not one cultural region.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the forest–hill association of many tribal communities a historical-geographic pattern rather than an environmental destiny? Ceiling: 150 words.

**Model answer:** Forests, hills and plateaux often provided relative distance from intensive plains agriculture and expanding state systems, while supporting locally adapted cultivation, gathering, pastoral and forest-based livelihoods. Over time, land and forest laws, mining, roads, markets, displacement and administrative boundaries altered both access and settlement. The association therefore reflects ecology interacting with history and power, not an innate preference for isolation. It also varies: the Northeast, central belt, southern hills and islands contain different language, livelihood and accessibility systems. Present habitation maps should be read with transport, service access and land-use change, while community agency and internal diversity remain central.

### Access matrix — map relations, not stereotypes

| Spatial field | Question to map | Safe unit |
|---|---|---|

| Work | Where are occupations and labour markets located? | corridor/cluster |

| Mobility | Who can safely reach school, work or transit? | route/time |
| Voice | Which places are connected to institutions? | jurisdiction/network |

### Spatial diagnosis

Two districts have the same state-level Scheduled Caste share. One has dispersed rural settlement; the other has segregated hamlets and a nearby industrial town. List the additional maps needed before comparing access.

### Worked transect — village to industrial node

```text
upland hamlet -> village service centre -> bus route -> market town -> industrial node
 distance          service quality        fare/time      rental access
```

A group’s opportunity is not explained by its district share alone. Landholding, settlement location, road access, labour contractors, rental markets and educational proximity mediate outcomes. Geography can identify clustering and accessibility; it cannot infer individual capacity or culture from category.

### Teaching — classification and scale

✅ Census 2011 Primary Census Abstract and relevant caste/tribe tables provide dated administrative-area distributions. Scheduled Caste is a constitutional statistical category, while caste group, community, class and occupation are different concepts. ⚠️ State percentages conceal district, village, ward and rural–urban variation.

Social-spatial inequality can appear as:

- **segregation:** groups are unevenly distributed among neighbourhoods;
- **peripherality:** settlements lie farther from services or safe transport;
- **occupational clustering:** networks and labour-market structure concentrate workers;
- **resource asymmetry:** land, water or common-property access differs;
- **environmental burden:** hazardous or poorly serviced sites receive disadvantaged residents.

These patterns emerge from historical land relations, institutions, markets, discrimination and planning. Geography maps the resulting spatial structure; Indian Society and Social Justice examine social institutions and remedies in depth.

### Error test — correlation is not cause

If a map shows community concentration and low service access in the same districts, it establishes spatial association. Causal explanation requires route, settlement, fiscal, historical or institutional evidence. Avoid ecological fallacy: an area average does not describe every person.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Scheduled category, caste group, class and occupation differ.
2. Use Census 2011 as the completed all-India distribution baseline.
3. State averages conceal local settlement structure.
4. Map land, work, housing, services, mobility and voice.
5. Segregation and peripherality are different mechanisms.
6. Occupational clustering can reflect networks and barriers.
7. Accessibility needs distance, time, cost and safety.
8. Co-location is association, not causation.
9. Area averages cannot define individuals.
10. Geography cross-links, rather than replaces, social-institution analysis.

### Concept check

**Question:** Show how an accessibility map can add more value than a population-share map in studying caste/community inequality. Ceiling: 130 words.

**Model answer:** A population-share map shows where a category is concentrated but not whether residents can reach land, schools, health care, transport or labour markets. An accessibility map adds route distance, travel time, cost, service quality and safety, and can reveal peripheral hamlets or poorly connected urban neighbourhoods within a district whose average appears favourable. It also identifies mechanisms: settlement location, road networks, rental exclusion or service placement. The two maps should be combined, because accessibility without social distribution can hide unequal exposure. Neither permits assumptions about individuals; causal claims require historical and institutional evidence.

### Source–destination retrieval

### Teaching — relocation and network diffusion

Migration redistributes culture through **relocation diffusion**: people carry language, cuisine, worship, association and skills. Continuing communication, visits, remittances and return migration create translocal regions linking origin and destination. Chain migration can concentrate people from particular source districts in specific occupations or neighbourhoods, but networks evolve and do not fix identity.

Internal destinations include metropolitan regions, industrial belts, plantations, construction corridors, university towns and border markets. Source regions may develop absentee households, changed housing, new consumption landscapes and altered gendered work. Diaspora geography distinguishes:

- **stock:** people counted at a date in a destination;
- **flow:** movement during a period;
- **origin:** birthplace, citizenship or ancestry—definitions differ;
- **community landscape:** institutions and neighbourhoods built over time;
- **transnational link:** repeated social or economic connection.

### Worked resource chain


### Teaching — why controls reinforce

The Ganga basin is unusually dense because major controls point in the same direction. Level relief makes cultivation, settlement and transport comparatively easy. The basin is not uniform:

| Sub-region | Land–soil–water combination | Population implication |
|---|---|---|

The Northern Plain is one structural foredeep filled with alluvium, but not one agricultural or hydrological region. Bhabar, terai, bhangar and khadar distinguish micro-relief; west–east rainfall and irrigation gradients distinguish farming and risk. A uniform water policy would misdiagnose scarcity in the west and drainage excess in the east.

High density is not synonymous with poverty. It supports labour, markets and service viability. Pressure becomes damaging where holdings fragment, non-farm absorption is weak, aquifers decline or active floodplains are intensively occupied.

### Inequality surface

```text
NATIONAL average
   ↓ disaggregate
STATE average
   ↓
DISTRICT mosaic
   ↓

   ↓
HOUSEHOLD / INDIVIDUAL variation

```

### Scale diagnostic

A state ranks well on a social indicator while several peripheral districts and urban wards perform poorly. Name the two statistical fallacies created by treating the state mean as every locality and every resident.

### Teaching — four analytical controls

1. **Scale effect:** pattern changes when units become larger or smaller.
2. **Zoning effect:** different boundaries aggregate the same people differently.
3. **Ecological fallacy:** an area relationship is assigned to individuals.
4. **Spatial autocorrelation:** nearby places may resemble one another, so observations are not independent.

Segregation measures uneven distribution among units; accessibility measures ability to reach opportunities. A neighbourhood can be socially mixed yet poorly connected, or segregated yet centrally located. Exposure adds a third layer: flood, pollution, heat or insecure tenure may be spatially concentrated.

### Worked accessibility profile

Compare two urban wards with the same school count. Ward A has frequent transit and safe footpaths; Ward B is divided by a railway, has costly last-mile travel and insecure rental settlements. Facility count suggests equality; travel-time and barrier mapping reveals unequal effective access.

### Fallacy test

If districts with high migration also show multilingualism, the district correlation cannot prove that every migrant is multilingual or that migration is the only cause. Historical minorities, education and urbanisation may contribute. A defensible answer states association, proposes mechanisms and seeks finer evidence.

### Spatial-justice boundary

Geography owns distribution, segregation, accessibility, exposure and scale. It can identify which places are underserved and why distance or network structure matters. Rights, discrimination doctrine and programme design are cross-linked to their primary subjects.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Scale changes apparent patterns.
2. Boundaries influence aggregation.
3. Area averages do not define individuals.
4. Nearby places may be statistically related.
5. Segregation and accessibility differ.
6. Facility count differs from effective access.
7. Travel time can outperform straight-line distance.
8. Exposure should be mapped separately.
9. Correlation requires a tested mechanism.
10. Geography identifies place-based inequality without stereotyping people.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can scale and zoning alter conclusions about social-spatial inequality? Ceiling: 130 words.

**Model answer:** Large units average together prosperous and deprived localities, so a favourable state or district value can conceal peripheral villages or disadvantaged wards. Different boundary designs can also group the same population into apparently different clusters. Inferring an individual’s condition from the area average creates ecological fallacy. Analysis should therefore compare multiple scales, use stable or clearly documented boundaries, and combine distribution with travel-time, service and exposure maps. Fine-scale evidence is not automatically superior, because small numbers and privacy constraints matter; the correct scale depends on the mechanism being tested.

**Model word count:** 89 words; ceiling: 130.

- Muslim concentrations include the Kashmir Valley, parts of Assam/West Bengal, Lakshadweep, Kerala pockets and urban clusters.
- Buddhist geographies include Himalayan pockets and Maharashtra’s neo-Buddhist belt.
- Jain distributions include notable western/northern urban-commercial concentrations.
- Pilgrimage creates functional regions crossing administrative and language boundaries.
- Sacred landscapes link meaning to rivers, hills, groves, streets and built forms.
- Census identity cannot measure belief intensity or neighbourhood interaction.

## D. Tribe, ethnicity and habitat

- Tribe, ethnicity, Scheduled Tribe, Scheduled Area, Sixth Schedule area and PVTG are distinct categories.
- Major broad frames: Northeast; central belt; southern/agency hills; island pockets.
- Habitat association reflects ecology interacting with history, state systems, land/forest regimes, markets and agency.
- The central belt crosses Rajasthan–Gujarat–MP–Chhattisgarh–Jharkhand–Odisha–Maharashtra.
- Fifth Schedule/Scheduled Areas and Sixth Schedule areas are different spatial mechanisms.
- Sixth Schedule areas occur in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.
- Official dated frame: 75 PVTGs across 18 states and one UT.
- PIB, 12 August 2026, records habitation-level PVTG mapping under PM JANMAN.
- Geography owns access and settlement pattern; constitutional and welfare doctrine is delegated.

## E. Caste/community, gender and inequality

- Map land, work, housing, mobility, services, environmental exposure and political/institutional access.
- Segregation, peripherality and accessibility are distinct mechanisms.
- Scheduled categories do not equal class, occupation or cultural essence.
- Women’s spatial opportunities vary by class, caste/community, age, disability, household and region.
- Mobility includes travel time, cost, safety and care constraints.
- PLFS is a sample labour survey; rates do not directly map job quality or individual autonomy.
- Ecological fallacy assigns an area relationship to individuals.
- State or district averages can conceal village and ward mosaics.

## F. Migration and diaspora

- Migration produces relocation diffusion and destination cultural landscapes.
- Chain networks can link specific source districts with work and housing nodes.
- Translocal regions include remittance, return, visits, media and institutional flows.
- Stock, flow, birthplace, citizenship and ancestry are different measures.
- MEA’s Overseas Indians page supports dated destination stocks, not annual migration flows.
- Cultural pockets are porous nodes, not sealed replicas of origin regions.
- Diaspora policy belongs to IR; remittance macroeconomics belongs to Economy.

## G. Cultural ecology and settlement

- Environment structures opportunity and risk but does not dictate culture.
- Technology, tenure, knowledge, markets, state action and power mediate adaptation.
- Cultural landscapes include fields, terraces, tanks, sacred groves, pastoral routes, riverfronts, ports and neighbourhoods.
- Himalayan pastoral, dryland, coastal, forest-upland and island systems require different route/access maps.
- Traditional practices are dynamic and not automatically sustainable.
- Rainfall, relief, altitude, soil, isolation and historical continuity help explain vegetation and endemism.
- Environment owns species/conservation designation; Geography owns spatial controls and livelihood interfaces.

## H. Population and Ganga basin

- Distribution describes arrangement; density is a ratio.
- Arithmetic density uses total area; physiological density uses cultivable area; agricultural density uses agricultural population over cultivable area.
- India’s density surface reflects relief, soil, water, climate, history, economy and connectivity.
- Ganga-basin density results from reinforcing level relief, alluvium, rivers, aquifers, monsoon, settlement continuity and transport.
- Bhabar is porous; terai is wet; bhangar is older alluvium; khadar is young floodplain alluvium.
- Western plain water stress differs from eastern flooding and drainage stress.
- Active channel and deltaic tracts combine fertility with erosion, flood or salinity exposure.
- High density can support markets and labour while increasing land, water and flood pressure.
- Never use unsupported memorised density or basin-share figures.

## I. PYQ-ready findings

- **Cultural pockets:** origin–route–destination–landscape–interaction; continuity without isolation.
- **Purvaiya:** easterly local perception → monsoon timing → agrarian calendar → song, memory and migration; climate mediated by society.
- **Ganga basin:** reinforcing controls + internal hydrological variation + balanced consequence.
- Attach Census 2011 to language/religion/SC-ST distributions.
- Census 2027 schedule, digital architecture and caste enumeration are current facts; 2027 distribution results are unavailable at the cutoff.
