## Lesson 1: Migration definitions, stocks, flows and residence bases

Progress: 1 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: define the measured event before explaining it

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: canonical Basic owner + permitted Majid Husain OCR page 333
Official baseline: Census 2011 migration tables use place-of-birth and last-residence concepts
Trap: mobility, migration, commuting and temporary visit are not interchangeable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — measurement decision tree

```text
Did usual residence change across a meaningful boundary?
 ├─ No → commuting / travel / temporary presence may apply
 └─ Yes → MIGRATION
          ├─ within country → internal
          └─ across country border → international

Measured as:
STOCK at a date ≠ FLOW during a period
place of birth ≠ place of last usual residence
```

### Original diagnostic

A worker spends eight months each year at a construction site but retains a village household and repeatedly returns. Explain why a usual-residence census may classify this movement differently from a labour-flow survey.

### Teaching — the denominator decides the story

Migration is a change in usual residence across a meaningful geographic or administrative boundary. **Immigration** is entry into a country; **emigration** is exit. Internal movement can be intra-district, inter-district or interstate. International migrant, refugee, internally displaced person and seasonal worker are analytically distinct categories.

A **migrant stock** counts persons meeting a residence/origin criterion at a date. A **migration flow** counts moves during a period. Place of birth captures lifetime displacement from birthplace but misses later return and repeated moves. Place of last usual residence better records the immediately preceding residence but can miss circular mobility that never crosses a duration threshold.

Measurement requires five labels:

```text
WHO → residence rule → boundary → reference period → stock/flow
```

### Guided worked example — return migration

Person A was born in Village X, moved to City Y for ten years, and returned to X before enumeration. By place of birth, A may not appear as a migrant in X; by migration history, A is a return migrant; by last residence, the city-to-village move may be visible. No single table captures the full trajectory.

### Revision checklist

1. Migration requires a residence change, not every movement.
2. Commuting normally lacks a residence change.
3. Internal and international migration use different boundaries.
4. Stock is measured at a date; flow over a period.
5. Place of birth captures lifetime displacement.
6. Last usual residence captures the preceding residence.
7. Return migration can disappear under a birthplace comparison.
8. Circular mobility is undercount-prone.
9. Duration thresholds change classification.
10. Always state source, residence rule and reference period.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the same person be a non-migrant in one table, a return migrant in another and a temporary migrant in a third?

**Model ceiling:** 90 words.

**Model answer:** Each table may use a different reference concept. A birthplace comparison can classify a person living again at the birthplace as non-migrant, while a last-residence or migration-history table records the return from a city. A survey focused on repeated seasonal residence may classify the same person as temporarily or circularly mobile. The classifications are not necessarily contradictory; they answer different questions about origin, previous residence, duration and timing.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 90-word ceiling):** 1 mark for distinguishing residence criteria; 1 for explaining return-migrant classification; 1 for temporary/circular timing; 1 for reconciling rather than treating the tables as contradictory.

**Misconception to avoid:** A dataset's migrant count is not independent of its definition.

**Remediation:** Rewrite one biography under birthplace, last-residence and annual-flow rules.

## Lesson 2: Types, streams, duration and motive

Progress: 2 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: classify movement on several dimensions at once

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: canonical stream and circular-migration ownership
India caution: rural-rural volume cannot be inferred to mean labour movement alone
Boundary: legal refugee status requires its own applicable criteria
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — migration classification cube

| Dimension | Categories |
|---|---|
| boundary | intra-district, inter-district, interstate, international |
| settlement stream | rural-rural, rural-urban, urban-urban, urban-rural |
| duration | permanent, long-term, short-term, seasonal, circular |
| agency | voluntary, constrained, forced/displaced |
| motive | work, marriage, education, family, conflict, disaster, retirement |
| direction | outward, inward, return, counter-stream |

### Guided worked example — one move, many labels

A woman moves after marriage from a rural district in State A to a peri-urban settlement in State B and later takes paid work. The move is interstate, rural-urban, initially marriage-recorded and potentially economically consequential. The recorded “reason” does not erase later work or the household's strategy.

### Original diagnostic

Classify a brick-kiln worker who leaves a drought-prone village for six months each year, lives at the worksite and returns after the season. Which single label would hide most information?

### Teaching — stream is not motive

Rural-rural, rural-urban, urban-urban and urban-rural are spatial streams. They do not identify motive. Rural-rural movement can include marriage, agricultural labour or family movement. Rural-urban migration can be aspirational, distress-led, educational or network-assisted. Urban-urban movement often follows professional hierarchies, education or firm transfer. Urban-rural movement includes return, retirement, peri-urban relocation and shock reversal.

**Seasonal migration** follows recurring calendars. **Circular migration** is repeated movement between origin and destination without a final permanent shift. **Distress migration** is distinguished by triggers such as livelihood collapse, debt, disaster, conflict or displacement and by weak bargaining capacity—not merely by low income.

### Revision checklist

1. Boundary, stream, duration and motive are separate dimensions.
2. Rural-rural is not synonymous with marriage.
3. Rural-urban is not always permanent.
4. Urban-urban reflects city networks and careers.
5. Urban-rural includes return and peri-urban movement.
6. Seasonal movement follows recurring calendars.
7. Circular migration retains repeated origin links.
8. Distress is identified by trigger and constrained choice.
9. Recorded reason can conceal later economic activity.
10. Forced movement has distinct legal and analytical categories.
11. Classify a move with more than one label.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain why “marriage migrant” and “worker” need not be mutually exclusive categories.

**Model ceiling:** 80 words.

**Model answer:** Official tables often record one principal reason for the residence change, such as marriage. That reason describes the move's initial trigger, not the migrant's entire economic role before or after migration. A woman may enter paid work, unpaid family work or home-based production at the destination. Treating marriage as proof of economic inactivity therefore creates a measurement and gender bias.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 80-word ceiling):** 1 mark for separating reason-for-move from current activity; 1 for recognising unpaid/paid work overlap; 1 for gender-aware classification; 1 for rejecting mutually exclusive labels.

**Misconception to avoid:** One recorded motive is not a complete life-course description.

**Remediation:** Add “initial reason,” “current activity” and “later transitions” to every migration profile.

## Lesson 3: Ravenstein—distance, step migration, selectivity and counter-streams

Progress: 3 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: turn classical “laws” into testable tendencies

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Theory owner: canonical Ravenstein and distance-decay unit
Historical caution: nineteenth-century observations are not timeless laws
Application rule: test each tendency against networks, transport and gender
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — step and counter-stream map

```text
Village → small town → regional city → metropolis
   ↑          ↑             ↑             |
   └──────── return / counter-stream ─────┘

distance increases → typical flow volume tends to fall
exception: exceptional opportunity or strong network corridor
```

### Teaching — robust tendencies, bounded claims

Ravenstein proposed that much migration occurs over short distances, often in steps toward commercial and industrial centres, and that each major stream produces a counter-stream. Migration is selective: age, gender, occupation, resources and family position alter who moves and how far.

Distance decay survives because financial, information, social and emotional costs usually rise with distance. But kilometres are an imperfect cost measure. A direct train, shared language or established network can make a farther city effectively “closer” than a nearby but disconnected town.

Ravenstein's gender observations reflected a specific historical context. Contemporary streams vary: short-distance marriage movement may be female-heavy; construction corridors may be male-heavy; care, nursing, domestic work and skilled migration can be feminised.

### Original diagnostic

Flow from District A to a distant metropolis exceeds flow to a nearer medium town. Name three conditions that could override simple distance decay.

### Guided worked example — counter-stream

A large outward labour flow can generate return migration through retirement, job loss, accumulated savings, family care or economic shock. The counter-stream is rarely equal to the original stream and may contain different ages and skills. Its existence does not cancel net migration.

### Revision checklist

1. Ravenstein describes tendencies, not physical laws.
2. Most movement is often short-distance.
3. Step migration links settlement hierarchy.
4. Major streams can produce counter-streams.
5. Net flow equals stream minus counter-stream.
6. Migration is selective by age, gender and skill.
7. Distance represents multiple costs.
8. Transport can compress effective distance.
9. Networks can override distance decay.
10. Historical gender rules require re-testing.
11. Counter-streams can have different composition.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can Ravenstein remain useful when direct village-to-metropolis migration is common?

**Model ceiling:** 90 words.

**Model answer:** The step sequence need not occur physically for every migrant. Information, job contacts and temporary shelter supplied by earlier migrants can bridge intermediate places, allowing a direct move. Ravenstein remains useful through distance cost, selectivity, stream formation and counter-stream logic, while network theory explains why the step is socially compressed. The model should therefore be treated as a set of tendencies whose mechanism changes with transport and communication.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 90-word ceiling):** 1 mark for stating Ravenstein’s distance/step tendency; 1 for a direct-flow exception mechanism; 1 for network or transport compression; 1 for treating the law as a tendency rather than a rule.

**Misconception to avoid:** One exception does not invalidate every Ravenstein proposition.

**Remediation:** Pair each classical tendency with one modern modifier.

## Lesson 4: Lee's push-pull framework, obstacles and personal selectivity

Progress: 4 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: explain why identical conditions produce different decisions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Theory owner: canonical Lee framework
Reasoning rule: perceived factors matter, not only objective conditions
Trap: push and pull are not self-executing forces
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — Lee decision field

```text
ORIGIN (+ / - / neutral) ── INTERVENING OBSTACLES ── DESTINATION (+ / - / neutral)
             \                 cost, law, risk,                  /
              \               distance, family                 /
               └──── PERSONAL FACTORS / SELECTIVITY ──────────┘
                       age, gender, skill, information,
                       resources, aspirations, life stage
```

### Original diagnostic

Two households face the same crop failure and know of the same urban wage. One migrates; the other does not. Use Lee's framework to identify the missing explanatory categories.

### Guided worked example — obstacle changes the destination

A worker prefers City M for wages but lacks affordable travel and contacts there. A nearer City N offers lower wages but a cousin, cheap transport and known housing. The obstacle and personal network can redirect the move even when M has the stronger abstract pull.

### Teaching — decision, perception and immobility

Push factors include unemployment, land stress, discrimination, disaster or conflict. Pull factors include jobs, safety, education, services and social freedom. The same condition can be interpreted differently: urban informality may be opportunity for one skilled entrant and unacceptable risk for another.

Intervening obstacles include travel cost, border or documentation rules, recruitment debt, language, care responsibilities, housing and information gaps. Personal factors shape thresholds. Lee therefore explains both migration and **non-migration**. Those with the strongest need may be unable to move because migration requires money, networks or physical capability—sometimes called trapped or involuntarily immobile populations.

### Revision checklist

1. Origin and destination contain positive and negative factors.
2. Perception matters alongside objective conditions.
3. Push and pull can operate simultaneously.
4. Obstacles redirect as well as suppress flows.
5. Personal factors create selectivity.
6. Information is uneven.
7. Household members face different thresholds.
8. Care duties can prevent movement.
9. The poorest may be unable to migrate.
10. Lee explains non-migration too.
11. The framework is descriptive more than predictive.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can environmental stress increase migration among middle-resource households but reduce it among the poorest?

**Model ceiling:** 90 words.

**Model answer:** Stress raises the incentive to leave, but migration also requires travel money, information, health and destination contacts. Middle-resource households may finance a move as risk diversification. The poorest can lose assets and borrowing capacity, making the obstacle larger than the push. They may become involuntarily immobile even under greater exposure. Lee's framework captures this through origin pressures, intervening obstacles and household selectivity.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 90-word ceiling):** 1 mark for the resource threshold to move; 1 for stress as an incentive; 1 for poorest-household immobility; 1 for a non-linear, differentiated conclusion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Stronger distress does not always produce more mobility.

**Remediation:** Draw separate “need to move” and “capacity to move” axes.

## Lesson 5: Gravity, distance decay and intervening opportunities

Progress: 5 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: compare flow-volume models without mistaking approximation for cause

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Theory owner: gravity and Stouffer models
Math caution: population size and distance are first approximations
Map skill: corridor strength requires connectivity and opportunity evidence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — competing model equations

```text
GRAVITY IDEA:
Flow i→j ∝ (Population i × Population j) / Distance^k

STOUFFER IDEA:
Flow depends less on kilometres than on opportunities at destination
relative to acceptable opportunities encountered before it.
```

### Teaching — what each model sees

The gravity model predicts larger flows between populous places and smaller flows as distance rises. It is useful as a baseline and can be improved with travel time, cost or connectivity. It does not explain why two similar city pairs have different cultural or occupational corridors.

Stouffer's intervening-opportunities approach predicts that a nearer acceptable opportunity can intercept a migrant who might otherwise travel farther. Opportunity must be defined for the relevant person: a university, construction job, marriage network or specialised hospital cannot be treated as equivalent.

**Distance decay** can be weakened by highways, trains, recruiters, digital information and kinship. Administrative borders can increase effective distance even when kilometres are small.

### Guided worked example — city pair comparison

Metropolis A is farther and larger than Town B. Gravity favours A through size but penalises distance. Stouffer asks whether B offers an acceptable job before A. Network theory asks whether relatives reduce A's entry costs. A realistic explanation combines all three.

### Original diagnostic

Two equally distant cities have similar populations, yet one receives five times the migrants from a district. List the variables that a simple gravity equation omitted.

### Revision checklist

1. Gravity links flow to size and distance.
2. It is a baseline, not a complete causal theory.
3. Travel time may outperform straight-line distance.
4. Stouffer focuses on intervening opportunities.
5. Opportunity is migrant-specific.
6. Networks change effective cost.
7. Borders and documentation increase friction.
8. Connectivity can weaken distance decay.
9. Similar city size does not imply equal flow.
10. Calibrate models to stream and period.

### Concept check

**Question:** A new industrial town opens midway between a source district and a metropolis. Predict its possible effect using gravity and intervening-opportunity logic.

**Model ceiling:** 85 words.

**Model answer:** Its smaller population may make it a weaker destination under a simple gravity model, but its shorter distance reduces cost. If it offers jobs acceptable to the relevant migrants, Stouffer's logic predicts interception of part of the metropolitan flow. The effect will depend on wages, recruitment, housing, transport and existing networks. It may divert first-time migrants while established chain migrants continue to the metropolis.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 85-word ceiling):** 1 mark for gravity effects of size/distance; 1 for the intermediate job opportunity; 1 for job-fit or network conditions; 1 for a conditional interception-versus-transit prediction.

**Misconception to avoid:** A nearer opportunity intercepts flows only if migrants perceive it as acceptable.

**Remediation:** Compare destination size, distance, job fit and network strength in four columns.

Progress: 6 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: explain migration despite visible urban unemployment

Theory owner: expected-income framework
PYQ anchor: exact held-paper demand appears below before tailored teaching
No-leakage rule: attempt the exact question before opening the explanatory model
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — neutral destination sketch

```text
ORIGIN ●────────────○ Destination A
       └────────────────────◎ Destination B

Choose only after writing your own comparison criteria.
```

> **Attempt first — exact UPSC CSE Mains 2024, GS-I, Question 5 — 10 marks, 150 words:**
> “Why do large citites tend to attract more migrants than smaller towns? Discuss in the light of conditions in developing countries.”

**Printed-wording caution:** `[sic]` flags the spelling “citites” in the official printed question; it is not silently corrected. Draft an unaided thesis and outline before proceeding.

### Post-attempt teaching visual — expected-income choice

```text
Expected urban income
 = possible urban wage × probability of obtaining work
   - search, housing, travel and living costs

Move if expected urban return + non-income benefits
exceeds the rural / smaller-town alternative.
```

### Guided worked example — unemployment and rational movement

A metropolis has higher unemployment than a small town but many more sectors: construction, transport, retail, domestic work, manufacturing and services. A migrant may accept a period of search because the probability of finding *some* work and later moving between jobs is higher. The visible unemployed stock does not by itself make migration irrational.

### Teaching — why metropolitan breadth matters

Harris-Todaro shifts attention from observed wage to **expected income**. Large cities often provide:

1. diverse formal and informal entry points;
2. frequent job turnover and matching;
3. education, health and administrative services;
4. transport hubs and recruitment networks;
5. agglomeration that keeps generating specialised demand; and
6. established migrant communities that lower entry cost.

Smaller towns can intercept movement when they offer credible jobs, connectivity and services. Metropolitan rent, commuting, congestion, insecurity and discrimination offset the pull. The correct conclusion is concentration of opportunity, not an intrinsic preference for city size.

### Original diagnostic

Why is “metros pay more” an incomplete answer to metropolitan attraction in developing countries?

### Revision checklist

1. Expected income differs from observed wage.
2. Job probability matters.
3. Informal work can absorb searchers.
4. Sector diversity reduces destination risk.
5. Agglomeration enlarges the opportunity set.
6. Networks lower information and housing costs.
7. Services create non-wage pull.
8. Rent and commuting reduce net benefit.
9. Smaller towns can be intervening opportunities.
10. Urban unemployment does not make every move irrational.
11. Attraction reflects spatial concentration of opportunity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why might a migrant choose a costly metropolis over a nearer town offering one known job?

**Model ceiling:** 95 words.

**Model answer:** The metropolis may offer a wider portfolio of jobs, allowing search, switching and recovery if the first job fails. Kinship networks may provide temporary housing and information, while education or health services add non-wage benefits. The nearer town's single job can be attractive but fragile. The choice depends on expected lifetime opportunity minus rent, travel and insecurity, not distance or one wage alone. A strong guaranteed town job could nevertheless reverse the decision.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (5 marks; 95-word ceiling):** 1 mark for expected rather than observed income; 1 for job-portfolio breadth; 1 for migrant-network or service benefits; 1 for metropolitan costs; 1 for a condition under which the nearer town wins.

**Misconception to avoid:** Expected-income theory does not assume migrants always obtain formal jobs.

**Remediation:** Replace one wage comparison with a probability-weighted opportunity portfolio.

## Lesson 7: Zelinsky, network migration and cumulative causation

Progress: 7 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: explain why migration systems persist after the initial trigger changes

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Theory owner: mobility transition and network/cumulative causation
Model caution: development stages do not prescribe one universal mobility path
Corridor rule: explain initiation and continuation separately
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — corridor feedback loop

```text
initial opportunity / shock
          ↓
first migrants establish foothold
          ↓
information + housing + credit + job contacts
          ↓
lower cost and risk for later migrants
          ↓
larger origin-destination corridor
          ↓
occupational clustering and more network resources
```

### Original diagnostic

A wage gap between two regions narrows, but migration along their old corridor continues. Which theory explains persistence, and what does it fail to explain?

### Teaching — mobility changes with development

Zelinsky links dominant mobility forms to demographic and socioeconomic transition: frontier or rural movement, rural-urban migration, inter-urban mobility, commuting and circulation can change in relative importance. The model is broad and inherits stage-model limits; policy, conflict and technology can alter the sequence.

Network theory explains destination specificity. Earlier migrants provide information, first accommodation, credit and referrals. **Cumulative causation** means migration changes the conditions that generate later migration: source aspirations, land use, skills and household strategies evolve; destination occupations become associated with particular origin communities.

Networks reduce risk but can reproduce segmentation, recruitment debt, caste or gender barriers and confinement to low-wage niches. They explain continuation better than the first move.

### Guided worked example — occupational corridor

Workers from one cluster of villages dominate a destination occupation because recruiters and relatives repeatedly connect them to employers. Wage difference initiated movement; the network maintains it. A new entrant outside the network may face higher cost despite identical skill.

### Revision checklist

1. Mobility forms change with development.
2. Zelinsky is broad-brush, not deterministic.
3. Networks lower information costs.
4. Networks provide housing and job contacts.
5. Corridors become self-reinforcing.
6. Cumulative causation changes origin conditions.
7. Destination occupations can cluster by origin.
8. Networks can protect and exploit.
9. They explain continuation more than initiation.
10. A wage gap alone cannot explain route specificity.
11. Digital contact can strengthen offline corridors.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a migrant network simultaneously increase mobility and reduce occupational mobility?

**Model ceiling:** 90 words.

**Model answer:** A network lowers the cost of reaching a destination by supplying information, travel help, housing and an initial job. Yet the same referrals can channel newcomers into one occupation, employer group or neighbourhood. Limited contacts outside that niche may restrict wage growth and skill matching. Networks therefore increase geographic mobility while reproducing occupational segmentation. Their effect depends on whether contacts expand over time or remain closed and hierarchical.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (4 marks; 90-word ceiling):** 1 mark for lower migration costs; 1 for channelled recruitment; 1 for occupational lock-in; 1 for explaining simultaneous mobility expansion and limited upward mobility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Social capital is not uniformly empowering.

**Remediation:** Evaluate every network under access, protection, segmentation and exit.

## Lesson 8: Selectivity—gender, caste, class, age and care chains

Progress: 8 / 16 | Stage: Core | Skill: reveal hidden mobility within aggregate migration totals

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Canonical control: marriage reason does not erase women's work
Boundary: full gender/family transformation belongs to Indian Society
Data rule: disaggregate motive, stream, sex, age, class and current activity
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — selectivity matrix

| Dimension | Mobility-enabling factor | Mobility constraint / bias |
|---|---|---|
| age | working-age job demand | child/elder dependence |
| gender | care, nursing, domestic/service work; education | safety, norms, unpaid care |
| caste/community | trusted network and referral | occupational confinement/discrimination |
| class | finance, documents, digital information | inability to fund move |
| life stage | marriage, study, first job | caregiving and household claims |
| skill | wider destination set | credential non-recognition |

### Guided worked example — feminisation and care chain

A woman migrates from a smaller town to provide paid care in a metropolis. Her earnings support children at origin, while an older relative assumes unpaid care there. Paid care at destination is connected to a redistributed care burden at origin—a care chain. The outcome can increase income and agency while creating separation and unpaid-work pressure.

### Original diagnostic

Why might an official table show women moving mainly for marriage while a destination labour survey finds many migrant women workers?

### Teaching — who moves and who remains

Migration is selective because expected gains, costs and permissions differ. Young adults dominate many labour streams. Caste and community networks can reduce entry barriers while reproducing occupational niches. Class shapes the ability to finance long moves; the poorest may remain trapped or use short circular routes.

**Feminisation of migration** can mean a rising female share, greater independent work migration, or visibility of women within previously hidden streams. These meanings must not be collapsed. Marriage-recorded migrants may work after movement. Male out-migration can feminise agricultural responsibility at origin without transferring land rights or decision power.

### Revision checklist

1. Migration selects by age, gender, class and skill.
2. The poorest may lack mobility capacity.
3. Networks can reflect caste/community ties.
4. Recorded marriage reason does not prove non-work.
5. Current activity and initial reason differ.
6. Feminisation has multiple meanings.
7. Male out-migration reshapes origin care and farm work.
8. Care chains link paid and unpaid labour across places.
9. Agency and vulnerability can coexist.
10. Credential and language barriers affect skilled migrants.
11. Disaggregate aggregate totals.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain why male-selective labour migration can alter women's workload at origin without automatically empowering them.

**Model ceiling:** 90 words.

          ↓

          ↓

### Teaching — environment as threat multiplier

7. The poorest may be trapped.
8. Stated work motive can have an environmental trigger.
9. Return depends on livelihood and habitability.
10. Avoid unverified “climate refugee” legal claims.
11. Distinguish event attribution from migration attribution.
12. Policy can support safe mobility and in-place adaptation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “climate change caused migration” usually an incomplete explanation of a specific Indian labour corridor?

**Model ceiling:** 95 words.

**Model answer:** Climate stress may reduce farm income or habitability, but the observed corridor also depends on destination labour demand, transport, recruiters, household assets, debt, caste networks and policy support. The defensible claim is that environmental change can intensify an existing mobility system or alter timing and distress, not that one climatic variable mechanically caused every move.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (5 marks; 95-word ceiling):** 1 mark for climate as a stress multiplier; 1 for livelihood/market mediation; 1 for household resources or networks; 1 for policy/conflict context; 1 for avoiding single-cause attribution.

6. **data quality:** are circular and return flows visible?
7. **environmental safety:** are adaptation and relocation choices real?

Restriction can trap workers in low-opportunity regions. Balanced policy expands local options while making movement safer. Outcome indicators—successful access, reduced recruitment debt, housing security and stable employment—matter more than portal or scheme announcements.

### Decision debrief

Use models as a sequence: Lee identifies motive and barriers; gravity/Stouffer estimate spatial choice; Harris-Todaro explains expected urban return; networks explain corridor persistence; selectivity and distress analysis identify who bears risk. The synthesis is stronger than a list of theorists.

### Revision checklist

1. Match model to explanatory task.
2. Combine motive, volume, destination and persistence.
3. Protect voluntary mobility.
4. Reduce forced distress.
5. Measure access outcomes, not portals alone.
6. Differentiate permanent, circular and displaced groups.
7. Include origin and destination policy.
8. Address gender, caste and class barriers.
9. Make circular mobility visible in data.
10. Treat climate mobility as multi-causal.
11. Verify current status and year.
12. Conclude with corridor-specific sequencing.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “develop villages so people do not migrate” an inadequate migration-policy objective?

**Model ceiling:** 100 words.

**Model answer:** Migration can be a voluntary route to income, education, autonomy and risk diversification, not merely a development failure. Better origin opportunities may reduce distress while increasing skilled mobility because households gain the capacity to move. A no-migration objective can restrict freedom and ignore productive labour matching. Policy should expand genuine choices: improve origin livelihoods and intermediate towns, protect recruitment and work, ensure portable services, and support safe return or settlement. Success is reduced compulsion and vulnerability, not zero movement.

**Adjacent scoring rubric (5 marks; 100-word ceiling):** 1 mark for migration as opportunity; 1 for distress reduction; 1 for freedom/choice; 1 for portable protection at destination; 1 for replacing zero migration with vulnerability and capability outcomes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Development does not mechanically eliminate mobility.

**Remediation:** Replace “stop migration” with measurable freedom, protection and opportunity outcomes.

**Solution:** No. It is a stock under a birthplace criterion. Annual inflow needs a period flow measure.

**Solution:** The need to move can rise while money, health, information or networks needed to cross obstacles collapse.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Practice 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain why circular migration is difficult to measure and important for Indian regional planning. Answer in 150 words.

**Model solution:** Circular migration involves repeated movement between origin and destination without a final permanent shift. Census or survey rules based on usual residence and duration may classify a worker at only one location or as a non-migrant. Informal employment, changing worksites and contractor recruitment further weaken records; transport or registry data count trips or users rather than unique migrants.

Yet circular workers sustain construction, agriculture, kilns and urban services while retaining households and care obligations at origin. Their repeated absence affects schooling, health, farm labour and remittance use. At destinations they need housing, safe work, transport and portable entitlements.

Planning therefore requires triangulation of origin surveys, destination worker data, seasonal transport, worksites and administrative systems, with explicit denominators and privacy safeguards. Making circulation visible is necessary both for regional labour analysis and for services that follow people across territory.

**Model word count:** 138. **Ceiling:** 150 words.

**Rubric (10/10):** definition 2 + measurement problems 3 + planning significance 3 + data response 1 + conclusion 1.

## Practice 2 — 15 marks

**Question:** Evaluate the usefulness of major migration theories for explaining India's internal migration corridors. Answer in 250 words.

**Model solution:** No single migration theory explains motive, volume, destination choice and corridor persistence. Ravenstein remains useful for short-distance dominance, selectivity, step movement and counter-streams, but its historical generalisations understate contemporary institutions. Lee identifies origin pressures, destination attractions, intervening obstacles and personal factors; it explains different household decisions but is weakly predictive.

Gravity provides a first estimate from population size and distance, while Stouffer explains why an acceptable nearer opportunity can intercept a longer move. Both require job-specific opportunity and connectivity data. Harris-Todaro clarifies why rural-urban migration can continue despite visible unemployment: migrants compare expected income and probability across formal and informal work. It underweights marriage, care and constrained movement.

Network and cumulative-causation approaches best explain persistent district-to-city corridors. Earlier migrants reduce costs through information, housing, credit and referrals, although network theory does not identify the initial trigger. Zelinsky connects mobility forms to development and demographic transition but is too stage-based for India's simultaneous rural-rural, circular and inter-metropolitan flows.

The strongest explanation is layered: regional inequality or shock initiates movement; expected opportunity shapes the decision; gravity and intervening opportunities influence range; and networks stabilise the route. Gender, caste, class, climate stress and policy then determine who can use the corridor and on what terms.

**Model word count:** 202. **Ceiling:** 250 words.

**Rubric (15/15):** theory accuracy 5 + limits 3 + India application 3 + integrated synthesis 3 + conclusion 1.

## Practice 3 — 20 marks

**Question:** “Migration redistributes both opportunity and vulnerability between source and destination regions.” Analyse with reference to India. Answer in 250 words.

**Model solution:** Migration moves workers toward concentrated jobs and services while transferring income, care and demographic effects across regions. For source households, remittances can smooth consumption, finance education, housing and debt, and diversify agricultural risk. Return may bring skills and savings. Yet selective loss of young adults can increase care burdens, alter sex and age structure and create dependence on external labour markets.

Destinations gain labour for construction, manufacturing, logistics, domestic work and services. Migrants can rejuvenate ageing labour markets and enlarge demand. Costs are unevenly distributed: insecure rentals, congestion, informal worksites and pressure on transport or water fall heavily on migrants and low-income neighbourhoods. Cities often receive labour after origin households have borne much of its upbringing cost.

Circular and distress migrants face special vulnerability because usual-residence data may miss them and benefits remain territorially organised. Portability mechanisms such as ONORC address one access barrier, but programme availability does not prove seamless use. Gender, caste, class and networks shape recruitment, occupation and bargaining power.

The geography is therefore a corridor balance sheet, not a source-versus-destination morality tale. Policy should expand origin and intermediate-town opportunity while protecting movement through portable services, safe housing, fair recruitment and better data. The aim is to reduce forced distress and externalised costs without restricting voluntary mobility.

**Model word count:** 210. **Ceiling:** 250 words.

**Rubric (20/20):** thesis/mechanism 3 + source effects 4 + destination effects 4 + migrant/selectivity analysis 4 + governance/data 3 + balanced judgment 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Definition and measurement

- Migration is change of usual residence across a meaningful boundary.
- Commuting and temporary travel need not be migration.
- Stock at a date differs from flow over a period.
- Place of birth and last usual residence measure different histories.
- Return and circular migration are undercount-prone.

## 2. Types and streams

- Internal: intra-district, inter-district and interstate.
- International: immigration and emigration.
- Spatial streams: rural-rural, rural-urban, urban-urban and urban-rural.
- Duration: permanent, temporary, seasonal and circular.
- Distress is defined by trigger and constrained bargaining, not poverty alone.

## 3. Ravenstein and Lee

- Ravenstein: short distance, step movement, selectivity and counter-stream.
- Distance is effective cost, not kilometres alone.
- Lee: origin/destination factors, obstacles and personal selectivity.
- Strong need can coexist with involuntary immobility.

## 4. Quantitative and economic models

- Gravity: flow rises with size and falls with distance.
- Stouffer: nearer acceptable opportunities can intercept movement.
- Harris-Todaro: expected income includes job probability and costs.
- Urban unemployment does not make all migration irrational.

## 5. Network and mobility transition

- Networks lower information, housing and job-search costs.
- Cumulative causation makes corridors self-reinforcing.
- Networks can protect migrants and reproduce occupational segmentation.
- Zelinsky links mobility forms to development but is not a universal timetable.

## 6. Selectivity

- Age, gender, caste, class, skill and life stage shape mobility.
- Marriage reason does not erase women's work.
- Feminisation can mean share, independent work migration or visibility.
- Care chains connect paid work at destination with unpaid work at origin.

## 7. India data

- Census 2011 remains the latest completed all-India migration baseline.
- MoSPI 2020-21 estimated migration rate: India 28.9%, rural 26.5%, urban 34.9%.
- Last usual residence: same state 87.5%, another state 11.8%, another country 0.7%.
- Marriage was recorded for 86.8% of female migrants.
- Temporary visitors were estimated at 0.7% in that survey round.
- These are dated survey estimates, not Census 2021 results.
- Census 2027 is scheduled; no new migration results are used.

## 8. India corridors and effects

- Source belts and destination nodes form evolving corridors.
- Intrastate and rural-rural movement remain central.
- Remittances improve household welfare but do not guarantee local transformation.
- Sources may lose selected workers; destinations gain labour and service pressure.
- Return flow can carry skills, savings and changed aspirations.

## 9. Environment and mobility

- Hazards work through livelihoods, assets, networks and institutions.
- Movement can be adaptation, distress, displacement or circulation.
- Some exposed households become involuntarily immobile.
- Avoid deterministic “climate caused every move” claims.

## 10. Governance and answers

- Gross flow differs from net migration.
- Portability responds to mobility across territorial systems.
- ONORC is a ration-portability mechanism, not proof of universal access.
- Strengthen origin and intermediate-town opportunity while protecting mobility.
- Exact current status and figures require re-verification.
- A strong answer combines theory, corridor map, balance sheet and data caution.
