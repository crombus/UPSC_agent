## Lesson 1 — What counts as a primary activity?

Progress: 1 / 15 | Stage: Core Foundation | Skill: classification by relation to nature

📚 Book context: Core owner and economic-geography framework checked.
🔍 CA Search: `"2025 GS-I non-farm primary activities official UPSC paper"`
📰 CA Found: The official 2025 GS-I paper directly tests classification and physiographic linkage.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Classification tree — visual first

```text
ECONOMIC ACTIVITY
├─ PRIMARY: harvests/extracts directly from nature
│  ├─ farm: crop cultivation + animal husbandry
│  └─ non-farm: fishing + forestry/gathering + mining + quarrying
├─ SECONDARY: transforms material
├─ TERTIARY: provides services
└─ QUATERNARY/QUINARY: knowledge and high-order decisions

LOCATION QUESTION:
resource occurrence → access → extraction/harvest → first landing/collection point
```

### Original boundary diagnostic

Classify: catching marine fish, operating a fish-canning unit, collecting tendu leaves, making plywood, extracting limestone, making cement, grazing sheep and providing crop insurance.

The primary set is catching fish, collecting forest produce, extracting limestone and grazing sheep. Canning, plywood and cement are secondary transformations; insurance is tertiary. The test is the activity performed, not the commodity’s broad sector label.

### Concept teaching

Primary activities depend most directly on the biophysical environment. Agriculture uses climate, soil, water and land; fishing uses marine or inland ecosystems; forestry uses biological stocks; mining and quarrying use geological occurrence. Yet physical presence alone does not guarantee production. Accessibility, labour, capital, technology, transport, regulation and markets determine whether a potential resource becomes an economic activity.

Three distinctions prevent category errors:

1. **Occurrence versus use:** ore may occur without being mined.
2. **Harvest versus transformation:** logs are primary output; furniture is manufacturing.
3. **Farm versus non-farm:** livestock is farm primary; capture fishing is non-farm primary.

Geography asks where an activity occurs, why there, what scale it assumes and how human capability modifies—but cannot freely relocate—the resource base.

### Guided worked application

Consider a coastal district containing an estuary, harbour, seafood plant and tourism services.

| Element | Sector | Locational reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capture fishery | Primary | biological stock and coastal water |
| Fish landing | Primary-chain node | sheltered shore and road access |
| Freezing/canning | Secondary | proximity to perishable raw material |
| Transport/auction | Tertiary | market linkage |
| Marine-resource survey | Quaternary support | information and management |

The district is a system, but the answer must still classify each operation correctly.

### Revision register — 9 anchors

1. Primary means direct harvest or extraction from nature.
2. Crop farming and animal husbandry are farm primary activities.
3. Fishing, forestry, gathering, mining and quarrying are non-farm primary activities.
4. Manufacturing is never made primary merely because it uses natural raw material.
5. Physiography determines resource occurrence and accessibility.
6. Technology alters reach and cost, not geological or biological origin.
7. Location potential differs from realised production.
8. Classify the activity, not the final commodity chain.
9. Define before giving examples in a 10-mark answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** Distinguish non-farm primary activities from secondary activities and explain why physiography has unusually strong control over the former. *(10 marks; write no more than 130 words.)*

**Model answer:** Non-farm primary activities directly harvest or extract natural resources outside crop cultivation and animal husbandry. They include fishing, forestry and gathering, mining and quarrying. Secondary activities transform those outputs, as when fish are canned, timber becomes furniture or limestone becomes cement. Physiography strongly controls primary location because continental shelves, rivers, forests, ore bodies, rock outcrops and relief cannot be freely relocated. It fixes the map of resource possibility and affects access cost. Technology, transport, capital and regulation decide whether a resource is actually used and at what scale, but they do not create the underlying shelf, forest or mineral deposit. Thus physiography is a necessary location base, while human factors convert potential into production.

**Model word count:** 114 words; ceiling: 130.

**Task-specific scoring rubric: 10 marks exactly**

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|
| Defines and exemplifies non-farm primary activities | 3 |
| Separates extraction/harvest from transformation | 2 |
| Explains immobility of the physical resource base | 3 |
| Qualifies physiography with access and technology within 130 words | 2 |
| **Total** | **10** |

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling seafood processing, timber products or mineral-based manufacturing primary activities.

**Remediation:** Classify one commodity chain from extraction to retail and justify every sector shift.

## Lesson 2 — Controls, land use and productivity: do not confuse the measures

Progress: 2 / 15 | Stage: Core Foundation | Skill: factor-chain and denominator discipline

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Canonical control matrix and India data cautions checked.
🔍 CA Search: `"Agricultural Statistics at a Glance official land use irrigation India"`
📰 CA Found: The Agriculture Ministry maintains dated land-use and irrigation series; year and denominator must accompany every comparison.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Control-to-pattern matrix

| Control | Mechanism | Observable spatial pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | heat, frost, rainfall and growing season | crop belts and calendars |
| Relief | slope, altitude, drainage, mechanisation | terraces, valley farming, large plains farms |
| Soil | texture, chemistry, depth, moisture retention | crop suitability and input response |
| Water | reliability and timing | irrigated cores versus rainfed mosaics |
| Labour/tenure | available work and decision scale | labour-intensive or mechanised systems |
| Market/transport | perishability, price and freight | peri-urban horticulture and export corridors |
| Technology | seed, machinery, storage, cold chain | altered intensity and market radius |
| Institutions | access, procurement and risk allocation | path-dependent regional specialisation |

### Guided measure audit

```text
NET SOWN AREA = land sown at least once
GROSS CROPPED AREA = every sown occurrence counted
CROPPING INTENSITY = gross cropped area / net sown area × 100

PRODUCTION = total output
YIELD/PRODUCTIVITY = output per unit area
IRRIGATED AREA = area receiving irrigation
IRRIGATION POTENTIAL = designed capacity, not proof of delivery
```

### Original diagnostic

District A produces more wheat than District B. Can we conclude that A is more productive?

No. A may simply cultivate more area. Productivity needs output divided by area. Similarly, rising gross cropped area may reflect multiple cropping without any rise in net sown area.

### Teaching through interaction

No single factor determines an agricultural region. Fertile alluvium without water control may underperform; irrigation on saline or poorly drained land may create new constraints; market access can make a less physically ideal location profitable. The strongest explanation uses a chain:

`physical suitability → usable water/land → labour and technology → market access → institutional reinforcement → observed land use`.

Land use also expresses competition. Near cities, high land rent can displace grain toward vegetables, flowers, dairying or non-agricultural uses. On remote plains, extensive grain may remain viable because land is cheaper and mechanisation substitutes for labour.

### Worked comparison

| Observation | Weak inference | Correct test |
|---|---|---|
| high output | high yield | divide output by harvested area |
| high irrigation potential | assured water | compare created, utilised and delivered irrigation |
| more cropped area | more farmland | separate gross from net sown area |
| more intensive farming | more machines | intensity may be labour-, capital- or input-based |
| crop concentration | pure climate control | test markets, procurement and path dependence |

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Agriculture reflects interacting physical and human controls.
2. Climate defines broad possibility; water reliability often decides actual season.
3. Relief affects drainage, terraces, erosion and machinery.
4. Soil suitability is crop-specific and management-dependent.
5. Net sown area differs from gross cropped area.
6. Cropping intensity differs from yield.
7. Production differs from productivity.
8. Irrigation potential differs from water delivered.
9. Markets matter most for perishables and high-value products.
10. Always state year, unit, area base and provisional/final status.

### Concept check

**Question:** A state records rising crop production, stable yield and declining net sown area. Explain two geographically plausible ways this can occur. *(10 marks; write no more than 140 words.)*

**Model answer:** Production can rise despite stable yield and declining net sown area if land is cropped more than once. Expanded irrigation, residual-moisture use or short-duration varieties can increase gross cropped area and cropping intensity while the unique area sown contracts. A second possibility is compositional change: cultivation may shift toward crops with greater output mass per hectare, while lower-output land leaves agriculture. Spatial concentration on better-watered, better-connected tracts can maintain average yield even as marginal land is abandoned or converted. The result must not be described as a yield revolution. It reflects seasonal intensification, crop composition and relocation of cultivation. Verification requires net and gross area, harvested area by crop, cropping intensity and the same production unit for comparable years.

**Model word count:** 119 words; ceiling: 140.

**Task-specific scoring rubric: 10 marks exactly**

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|
| Uses multiple cropping/cropping intensity correctly | 3 |
| Supplies a second spatial or compositional mechanism | 3 |
| Rejects an unsupported yield inference | 2 |
| Names verification variables within 140 words | 2 |
| **Total** | **10** |

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating production, productivity and cropping intensity as synonyms.

**Remediation:** Build a four-column table for area, output, yield and cropping intensity before interpreting any trend.

## Lesson 3 — Subsistence, intensive, shifting and pastoral systems

Progress: 3 / 15 | Stage: Core Systems | Skill: classification by land, labour and mobility

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Core owner and Majid Husain PDF pp. 282–284 checked.
🔍 CA Search: `"FAO shifting cultivation pastoral mobility land use official"`
📰 CA Found: Current international work treats mobile pastoralism and shifting systems as context-dependent land-use adaptations, not as one undifferentiated “primitive” category.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Farming-system comparison table

| System | Land/labour relation | Output orientation | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intensive subsistence | small land, high labour/input per area | household plus local surplus | densely populated monsoon regions |
| Shifting cultivation | temporary plot and fallow rotation | mainly household/community | humid forest margins |
| Nomadic pastoralism | mobile herds over sparse forage | subsistence and exchange | arid, semi-arid and cold drylands |
| Transhumance | seasonal movement between fixed ranges | livestock products | mountains and adjoining plains |
| Settled subsistence | permanent fields, low external input | household/local | varied low-income rural regions |

### Teaching from the table

**Intensive** describes input or labour per unit land, not necessarily machinery. Wet-rice cultivation may be highly intensive because a small area receives abundant labour, water control and repeated cultivation. **Subsistence** describes the dominant consumption orientation, not zero exchange.

Shifting cultivation rotates fields rather than permanently moving entire settlements in every case. Its viability depends on fallow length, population pressure, soil recovery and forest ecology. Shortened fallows can transform a rotational system into degradation, but the label alone does not prove ecological collapse.

Pastoral systems respond to dispersed, seasonal forage. Mobility may be rational risk management where rainfall is variable and permanent cultivation is hazardous. Nomadism, transhumance and ranching must be separated: ranching is commercially organised on bounded holdings, while nomadic or transhumant systems organise movement differently.

### Original pressure diagnostic

If population doubles but available forest land does not, what happens to a shifting system?

The fallow cycle may shorten. Soil fertility and vegetation recovery can weaken, weeds and erosion can rise, and households may intensify, diversify or settle. The causal variable is the land-to-fallow relationship, not an automatic moral judgment about the cultivator.

### Guided landscape reading

Mountain village evidence: summer grazing at high elevation, winter return to lower valley, fixed houses and cropland. This is transhumance, not nomadism. Humid upland evidence: temporary cultivated patch, mixed crops and fallow regrowth. This supports shifting cultivation, but duration and tenure require field evidence.

### Revision register — 9 anchors

1. Intensive is an input-per-land concept.
2. Subsistence does not mean complete isolation from markets.
3. Wet-rice farming combines high labour with water control.
4. Shifting cultivation rotates plots and depends on fallow recovery.
5. Shortened fallow changes ecological outcome.
6. Pastoral mobility tracks variable forage and water.
7. Transhumance is seasonal movement between known ranges.
8. Ranching is commercial and territorially bounded.
9. Avoid evolutionary labels; explain adaptation and pressure.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can mobility be an economically and ecologically rational feature of pastoralism rather than a sign of agricultural backwardness? *(10 marks; write no more than 140 words.)*

**Model answer:** In arid, semi-arid and cold drylands, forage and water are sparse, seasonal and spatially uneven. Moving herds allows pastoralists to track temporary pasture, avoid exhausting one site and spread drought risk across several ecological zones. Transhumance can connect summer highland grazing with winter lowland shelter while preserving fixed settlements and cropland. Mobility also matches herd composition to changing vegetation and reduces the need to transport bulky fodder. It becomes vulnerable when routes are enclosed, water points are blocked or herd pressure exceeds recovery. Thus mobility is not inherently inefficient; it is a land-use strategy fitted to variable environments. Evaluation should compare grazing pressure, access institutions and recovery rates rather than assume that permanent settlement is automatically more productive or sustainable.

**Model word count:** 120 words; ceiling: 140.

**Task-specific scoring rubric: 10 marks exactly**

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|
| Links mobility to variable forage and water | 3 |
| Explains risk-spreading and seasonal complementarity | 3 |
| Adds a bounded ecological or access qualification | 2 |
| Rejects the backwardness assumption within 140 words | 2 |
| **Total** | **10** |

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating all livestock movement as nomadism or all mobility as inefficient.

**Remediation:** Distinguish nomadism, transhumance and ranching using settlement, movement and market orientation.

📚 Book context: Core owner and OCR book pp. 284, 289 and 292 checked.
🔍 CA Search: `"FAO agricultural production systems plantation mixed farming horticulture official"`
📰 CA Found: Current statistics classify products and land use, but textbook farming systems remain analytical types rather than fixed national labels.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Commercial-system spectrum

```text
LAND-EXTENSIVE                                      MARKET-INTENSIVE
commercial grain → ranching → mixed farming → dairy → market gardening
 large plains       grassland    crop-livestock    daily      perishable,
 mechanised         livestock    integration       market     high value

SPECIALISED TROPICAL EXPORT:
plantation = estate organisation + crop specialisation + processing/export link
```

### Guided comparison

Use four axes for every system: **climate/terrain, land–labour ratio, product/perishability, market organisation**.

Commercial grain farming occupies broad temperate plains where large fields and machinery offset low labour density. Mixed farming integrates crops and livestock: fodder feeds animals, manure can return nutrients and diversified output spreads risk. Dairy requires daily care and rapid market access unless chilling and processing extend its radius. Market gardening produces vegetables, fruit and flowers for dense urban demand; high land values are offset by high value per hectare.

Plantation agriculture is not simply “a farm growing one crop.” The classical type combines large estate organisation, specialised tropical cash crops, hired labour, processing and export links. Tea, coffee, rubber, cocoa, oil palm and sugarcane appear in different plantation regions, but exact current producer rankings require dated data.

Mediterranean agriculture links winter rain and summer drought with cereals, vines, olives, citrus, vegetables and irrigation. It is a seasonal portfolio, not merely fruit farming.

### Original system-matching diagnostic

Match:

1. Vast level fields, sparse population, wheat and machinery.
2. Small peri-urban holdings, vegetables, overnight transport.
3. Crops feed livestock; livestock supports diversified revenue.
4. Humid tropical estate with on-site processing and port link.

Answers: commercial grain; market gardening; mixed farming; plantation.

### Worked application: cold chain shock

A new refrigerated highway does not abolish perishability. It reduces the time-cost penalty, expands the feasible market radius and may shift dairy or horticulture outward to cheaper land. Reliability, electricity, grading and return freight still matter. Location changes from simple kilometres to effective time and logistics cost.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Commercial orientation concerns sale, not necessarily farm size.
2. Extensive grain uses large land and little labour per hectare.
3. Ranching differs from nomadic pastoralism.
4. Mixed farming integrates crops and livestock.
5. Dairy has high time sensitivity.
6. Market gardening serves dense urban demand.
7. Plantation combines specialisation, estate organisation and processing.
8. Mediterranean farming uses winter rain and summer irrigation strategically.
9. Cold chains stretch market radius but do not remove logistics.
10. Compare systems through the same axes.

### Concept check

**Question:** Compare commercial grain farming, mixed farming and market gardening through land–labour ratio, product character and market relation. *(15 marks; write no more than 190 words.)*

**Model answer:** Commercial grain farming occupies extensive, relatively level temperate plains. Holdings are large, labour input per hectare is low and machinery handles sowing and harvesting. Grain is storable, so farms can be distant from final consumers if rail or bulk transport is efficient. Mixed farming combines crops and livestock. Land supports grain and fodder, animals provide milk or meat, and diversified output spreads price and weather risk; it therefore needs continuing farm management rather than only seasonal field operations. Market gardening uses smaller, intensively managed holdings near major urban markets or fast logistics corridors. Vegetables, fruit and flowers have high value per hectare but are perishable, so time, cold chain and road reliability outweigh simple distance. The three systems are commercial, yet their spatial forms differ because storage life, value density, labour rhythm and market frequency create different transport and land-rent tolerances.

**Model word count:** 141 words; ceiling: 190.

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|

| Mixed-farming integration and risk logic | 4 |

## Lesson 5 — Von Thünen: rings are the diagram; rent logic is the model

Progress: 5 / 15 | Stage: Core Model | Skill: assumption–mechanism–qualification

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Canonical model-control section checked.
🔍 CA Search: `"peri urban agriculture cold chain transport cost location official"`
📰 CA Found: Contemporary logistics change effective distance, while perishability and land rent continue to structure peri-urban production.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Classical ring diagram

```text
              [ EXTENSIVE GRAZING ]
          [ GRAIN / FIELD CROPS       ]
       [ FOREST / CLASSICAL FUELWOOD    ]
    [ DAIRY, VEGETABLES, FLOWERS          ]
                 [ MARKET ]

Bid rent falls outward as transport cost rises.
High-value/perishable uses can pay more for inner land.
```

### Assumption discovery

The classical model assumes one isolated market, a uniform plain, equal fertility, identical transport in every direction, economically rational farmers and no major barriers. These assumptions deliberately hold physical variation constant so distance and transport cost can be isolated.

### Teaching the mechanism

For a product, location rent can be simplified as:

`market revenue − production cost − transport cost`.

Transport cost rises with distance and with the product’s weight, bulk, fragility or urgency. Perishable, frequently delivered or high-value products can bid for expensive inner land. Storable grain tolerates greater distance. Extensive grazing requires cheap land and therefore lies farther out in the stylised scheme.

The forest ring reflects the historical bulk and transport difficulty of fuelwood, not a timeless ecological law. Modern energy systems, roads and land regulation alter the visible order.

### Guided modernisation test

| Modern change | Ring effect | Logic retained? |
|---|---|---|
| refrigeration | expands dairy/horticulture radius | yes: time-cost is reduced, not erased |
| expressway | creates corridor distortion | yes: effective distance changes |
| multiple cities | overlapping rent surfaces | yes, literal concentricity weakens |
| contract farming | secures outlet farther away | partly; institutional access enters |
| zoning/green belt | interrupts highest-bid use | market outcome is institutionally modified |
| air freight | permits distant high-value exports | value density can outweigh distance |

### Original counterfactual diagnostic

If transport cost becomes zero but land quality remains uniform, what happens to the distance mechanism?

The transport-cost component disappears, so distance alone no longer produces rings. Perishability may still matter if time or reliability is not zero, and zoning, labour, water and multiple markets can create other patterns.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Von Thünen is an agricultural location model.
2. State assumptions before applying rings.
3. Transport cost and land rent are central.
4. Perishability increases proximity value.
5. High-value output can pay higher inner rent.
6. Storable, extensive uses tolerate distance.
7. The forest ring is historically contingent.
8. Cold chain changes effective distance.
9. Multiple markets and corridors distort concentric rings.
10. Defend the rent logic while qualifying the literal map.

### Concept check

**Question:** “Cold chains weaken Von Thünen’s rings without making his location logic obsolete.” Examine. *(15 marks; write no more than 190 words.)*

**Model answer:** Cold chains reduce spoilage and permit dairy, vegetables, fruit and flowers to travel farther, so the classical concentration of perishables next to one market can spread along refrigerated corridors. Expressways, air freight and multiple cities further replace neat concentric rings with overlapping and directional zones. Yet the model’s logic survives. Refrigeration has capital, electricity and reliability costs; travel time, handling loss and delivery frequency still affect net return. Producers compare market revenue with production, land and logistics costs, while high-value uses can outbid extensive uses for well-connected land. A failed cold chain sharply restores the perishability penalty. Von Thünen is therefore weak as a literal map of modern agriculture but strong as a baseline for analysing how effective distance, value density, land rent and infrastructure shape location. Technology stretches or redirects the rent surface rather than abolishing spatial cost.

**Model word count:** 139 words; ceiling: 190.

**Task-specific scoring rubric: 15 marks exactly**

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|
| States how cold chains alter literal rings | 4 |
| Retains rent, perishability and effective-distance logic | 4 |
| Adds modern distortions such as corridors or multiple markets | 3 |
| Gives an examined verdict within 190 words | 4 |
| **Total** | **15** |

**Misconception to avoid:** Either reproducing rings without assumptions or declaring the model useless because exact rings are rare.

**Remediation:** Apply the model twice to the same crop—once without and once with reliable refrigeration.

### Teaching: thresholds are ranges, not destinies

Crop geography emerges from minimum conditions, optimum conditions and management. Irrigation can compensate for rainfall shortage; drainage can correct water excess; breeding shifts temperature or maturity ranges; market incentives can place crops on ecologically costly land. Therefore “crop X grows only in soil Y” is usually too absolute.

Distribution answers need three scales:

1. **Global climatic belt:** monsoon lowlands, temperate grasslands, Mediterranean margins or humid tropics.
2. **Regional physical site:** plain, delta, slope, plateau or irrigated basin.
3. **Human reinforcement:** labour, processing, port, cold chain or procurement.

### Original crop-elimination diagnostic

Tea. The decisive combination is humidity plus drainage, slope and labour—not rainfall alone.

### Guided rubber map application

Rubber’s world distribution follows humid frost-free tropics, especially Southeast Asia, with additional belts in South Asia, Africa and Latin America. Environmental issues arise when plantation expansion replaces natural forest, simplifies habitat, exposes soil or increases chemical use. Existing plantations, mixed agroforestry and expansion into degraded land have different consequences; never attach one environmental verdict to every rubber-producing country.

### Teaching the fishing chain

Shallow sunlit shelves can support productive food webs and accessible fishing grounds. Upwelling brings nutrient-rich deep water toward the surface, while current convergence can enhance mixing. ### Worked spatial explanation

The first Green Revolution concentrated in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh because assured irrigation, level alluvial land, rural infrastructure, input delivery and procurement aligned. The technology was divisible in theory but spatially selective in practice. Rainfed plateaus and less connected regions lacked parts of the package.

### Original counterfactual diagnostic

Would distributing identical seed everywhere have produced identical results?

No. Seed performance depends on water control, nutrients, timing, credit, knowledge and outlet. The package—not one input—produced transformation.

### Teaching divergence and path dependence

Early surplus regions accumulated mandis, storage, machinery services, roads and political expectations. These lowered transaction costs and reinforced existing crops. Procurement geography became more concentrated than the mere geography of production.

Benefits included higher output, food availability and farm-linked activity. Costs included inter-regional divergence, intra-regional inequality, rice–wheat lock-in, groundwater stress, nutrient imbalance and displacement of pulses or millets to less favoured land.

The geographic reform logic is differentiated:

- water-stressed surplus cores: diversification, demand management and procurement realignment;
- rainfed regions: soil moisture, water harvesting, resilient crops and market access;
- eastern potential regions: drainage, flood-sensitive infrastructure, irrigation reliability and logistics.

Detailed MSP formulas, subsidies and fiscal design remain Economy ownership.

### Guided 2023 PYQ bridge

India’s transition from net food importer to exporter can be explained through irrigation, technology, research, multiple cropping, institutional procurement, infrastructure and diversified production. A geographic answer must also note spatial selectivity and avoid implying that export status equals universal food or nutrition security.

Regions already possessing irrigation, level land and procurement infrastructure therefore respond first. Output and cropping intensity rise, creating national surplus and reducing import dependence. Success then attracts storage, machinery services, roads and buyers, reinforcing the core through path dependence. Technology is not inherently unequal, but its preconditions and supporting institutions are spatially uneven. National gain should therefore be paired with differentiated policy: resource-sensitive diversification in stressed cores and water, extension, logistics and resilient crops in lagging regions.

### Risk pathway map

```text
CLIMATE HAZARD
heat / drought / flood / erratic onset / intense rain / frost
        ↓
CROP OR LIVESTOCK EXPOSURE
growth stage + soil + slope + water system + location
        ↓
SENSITIVITY
crop requirement + rooting + drainage + herd/feed dependence
        ↓
ADAPTIVE CAPACITY
irrigation reliability + seed + forecast + storage + diversification
        ↓

```

### Teaching owned mechanisms

Geography owns the spatial mechanism: changing temperature, rainfall timing and extremes alter crop suitability, sowing windows, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, erosion, flood exposure and pest habitats. It does not own detailed climate-finance or environmental-regulation architecture.

Sustainability is region-specific:

- irrigated dry regions: groundwater balance, salinity and crop water demand;
- humid slopes: erosion, drainage and plantation biodiversity;
- floodplains: crop calendar, drainage, sediment and flood-compatible use;
- rainfed plateaus: soil moisture, drought resilience and diversified production;
- peri-urban belts: land conversion, wastewater quality and market pressure.

### Original adaptation diagnostic

Is micro-irrigation universally sufficient?

No. It can improve application efficiency, but source sustainability, energy, maintenance, crop choice and farmer access determine basin-level outcome. Efficiency may even encourage area expansion unless total withdrawals are governed.

### Guided climate-smart location audit

For a water-stressed district shifting from millet to summer paddy:

1. compare crop water demand and rainfall season;
2. identify irrigation source and recharge;
3. test soil drainage/salinity;
4. map market/procurement incentive;
5. assess alternative crops and livelihood value;
6. set a measurable water and income criterion.

### Worked sustainability portfolio

No single measure fits all regions. Portfolios may combine water harvesting, drainage, crop-calendar shifts, drought/heat-tolerant varieties, mixed farming, agroforestry, soil cover, forecast use, storage and market diversification. The geographic test is whether the measure matches the dominant local constraint and avoids transferring risk elsewhere.

1. Hazard alone does not determine loss.
2. Growth stage changes crop sensitivity.
3. Heat affects evapotranspiration and reproductive stages.
4. Intense rain can coexist with seasonal water scarcity.
5. Floodplain suitability is not the same as flood immunity.
6. Groundwater stress is spatially concentrated.
7. Efficiency differs from total resource conservation.
8. Diversification spreads climatic and market risk.
9. Adaptation must match the regional constraint.
10. Geography evaluates land-use viability, not every policy instrument.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why must agricultural sustainability strategies be region-specific rather than organised around one national technology? *(15 marks; write no more than 200 words.)*

**Model answer:** Agricultural constraints differ by physical and economic region. A dry irrigated plain may face groundwater depletion and salinity; a humid plantation slope faces erosion and habitat simplification; a floodplain needs drainage and flood-compatible calendars; a rainfed plateau faces soil-moisture variability; a peri-urban belt faces land conversion and polluted water. One technology can therefore solve one constraint while worsening another. Micro-irrigation improves field application but cannot secure an overdrawn aquifer by itself. A high-yield crop may raise output yet increase drought exposure if its water demand conflicts with local supply. Region-specific strategy begins with climate, soil, relief, water balance, crop stage and market incentive. It then combines suitable crops, water management, soil cover, forecasts, storage, mixed farming or agroforestry. Success should be judged through resource balance, yield stability, income resilience and avoided risk transfer, not adoption counts alone.

**Question:** Distinguish intensive farming from extensive farming and show why neither term by itself establishes whether farming is subsistence or commercial. **Answer in no more than 150 words.**

**Model answer (actual count: 114 words; ceiling: 150):** Intensive and extensive describe the concentration of inputs relative to land. ## Practice 2 — 15 marks

**Question:** Examine the continuing relevance and principal limitations of Von Thünen’s model for contemporary agricultural location. **Answer in no more than 250 words.**

**Model answer (actual count: 185 words; ceiling: 250):** Von Thünen explains agricultural location through the interaction of market price, production cost, transport cost and land rent around an isolated market on a uniform plain. Perishable, frequently delivered and high-value products can pay high rent near the market, while storable grain and extensive grazing tolerate greater distance. This logic remains visible in peri-urban dairy, vegetables, flowers and in the tendency of bulky low-value products to avoid long transport.

Its literal rings are limited. Modern regions contain several markets, unequal soils, rivers, mountains, highways and political boundaries. Refrigeration, processing, air freight and digital coordination reduce spoilage and stretch market radius. Contracts, procurement, zoning, subsidies and global value chains can redirect location. Transport is corridor-based rather than equal in every direction.

Yet these changes do not abolish spatial cost; they change effective distance and the shape of the rent surface. Cold chains have capital and reliability costs, and land near accessible markets remains contested. The model is therefore most useful as a baseline mechanism and counterfactual, not as a descriptive map. Its continuing value lies in asking how technology and institutions modify distance, perishability and rent.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Definitions

- Primary activities directly harvest or extract natural resources.
- Farm primary activities include crop cultivation and animal husbandry.
- Non-farm primary activities include fishing, forestry/gathering, mining and quarrying.
- Secondary activities transform primary products.
- Intensive and extensive describe inputs relative to land.
- Subsistence and commercial describe output orientation and market relation.
- An agricultural region is a recurring crop–livestock–land-use–market combination.

## 2. Controls

- Climate controls heat, frost, rainfall and growing-season length.
- Relief affects drainage, erosion, terraces, altitude and mechanisation.
- Soil affects rooting, moisture and nutrient response.
- Water reliability changes seasons, intensity and risk.
- Labour and tenure shape feasible techniques.
- Transport, market size and perishability shape location.
- Technology changes effective distance and suitability.
- Institutions reinforce selected regional patterns.

## 3. Measure discipline

- Net sown area counts land sown at least once.
- Gross cropped area counts every sowing.
- Cropping intensity = gross cropped area / net sown area × 100.
- Production is total output.
- Yield/productivity is output per unit area.
- Irrigation potential is not proof of actual delivery.
- Every number needs source, year, unit, scale and status.

## 4. Farming systems

- Intensive subsistence: small holdings, high labour/input, commonly monsoon Asia.
- Shifting cultivation: temporary fields and fallow rotation; outcome depends on recovery time.
- Pastoralism: mobility follows seasonal forage and water.
- Commercial grain: large, mechanised, storable output, temperate plains.
- Mixed farming: crops and livestock integrated.
- Plantation: specialised estate crop, labour, processing and export linkage.
- Mediterranean: winter crops plus vines, olives, citrus and summer irrigation.
- Dairy/market gardening: perishability and urban access.

## 5. Von Thünen

- Assumptions: single market, uniform plain/fertility, equal transport and rational producers.
- Mechanism: revenue − production cost − transport cost generates location rent.
- Perishables/high-value output can bid for inner land.
- Extensive/storable uses tolerate distance.
- Cold chains, highways and air freight alter effective distance.
- Multiple markets, zoning, contracts and unequal terrain distort rings.
- Use as a baseline mechanism, not a literal modern map.

## 6. Crop ecology

- Rice: warm season and reliable water; lowland, irrigated and upland systems.
- Wheat: cool growth and drier ripening.
- Millets: rainfall variability and dryland suitability.
- Cotton: long warm frost-free season; not restricted to black soil.
- Jute: humid alluvial east and retting water.
- Tea: humid, acidic, well-drained slopes and labour.
- Coffee: shaded tropical uplands and drainage.
- Rubber: humid frost-free tropics; expansion context determines environmental cost.
- Avoid undated current rankings.

## 7. India’s agricultural mosaic

- Kharif follows monsoon onset; rabi uses cool weather, residual moisture and irrigation; zaid fills a short summer window.
- North-west: irrigated wheat–rice and procurement reinforcement.
- East: rice, alluvial lowlands, flood/delta contexts and variable irrigation.
- Deccan: cotton, soybean, millets, pulses and oilseeds under mixed rainfed/irrigated conditions.
- Plantation highlands: tea, coffee, rubber and spices.
- Urban/transport corridors create horticulture and dairy belts.
- One crop can occur in several systems; one region can contain several crops.

## 8. Non-farm primary activities

- Marine fishing follows shelves, upwelling, currents, harbours and stock condition.
- Inland fishing follows rivers, floodplains, wetlands, tanks and reservoirs.
- Forestry follows climate, altitude, soil, species and access.
- NTFP links central/north-eastern forest landscapes with livelihoods.
- Metallic ores commonly relate to old crystalline/shield regions.
- Coal, limestone and hydrocarbons relate to suitable sedimentary basins.
- Quarrying follows stone, sand and gravel near demand but can alter channels and slopes.
- Physiography maps potential; infrastructure and institutions map realised use.

## 9. Transformation and regional inequality

- High-yield transformation required seed, water, nutrients, credit, extension and procurement.
- Preconditions concentrated early gains in the north-west.
- Surplus generated infrastructure and path dependence.
- Rainfed areas received weaker gains.
- Crop lock-in, groundwater stress and soil imbalance emerged in successful cores.
- Geography supports region-specific diversification and capability building.
- Detailed MSP/subsidy/WTO analysis belongs to Economy.

## 10. Data and currentness

- Agriculture Census measures operational holdings and structural characteristics.
- Retain completed-round and ongoing-round status exactly.
- Seasonal area and advance estimates are provisional.
- Final output cannot be inferred from an earlier advance estimate.
- PM Dhan-Dhaanya’s geographic use is the targeting of 100 districts through lagging indicators.
- Selection and launch are not impact.
- FAOSTAT series carry national reporting and estimation metadata.

## 11. Sustainability

- Hazard + exposure + sensitivity + adaptive capacity produce risk.
- Irrigated dry regions face depletion and salinity.
- Humid slopes face erosion and habitat simplification.
- Floodplains need drainage and compatible calendars.
- Rainfed plateaus need soil-moisture resilience and diversification.
- Efficiency does not automatically reduce total withdrawal.
- Judge measures by resource balance, yield stability, income resilience and avoided risk transfer.

## 12. Answer-writing spine

> 🔑 Mnemonic: **L-C-M-Q** = Locate → Controls → Mechanism → Qualification.

- Define categories before examples.
- Compare through identical axes.
- Describe map pattern before causation.
- Use a named Indian and world illustration.
- State assumptions before criticising a model.
- Attach year/status to data.
- End with a geographic verdict.
