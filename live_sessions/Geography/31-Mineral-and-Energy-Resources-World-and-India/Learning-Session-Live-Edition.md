### Resource-status funnel — visual first

```text
GEOLOGICAL OCCURRENCE
mineral concentration exists
        ↓ knowledge, grade and continuity
MINERAL RESOURCE
reasonable prospects of eventual use
        ↓ technology + price + law + feasibility + date
RESERVE
economically recoverable subset under stated conditions
        ↓ extraction and processing
PRODUCTION

A reserve can change without geology changing.
```

### Original classification diagnostic

Classify iron ore, copper, limestone, coal, petroleum, uranium, solar radiation and hydropower.

- Ferrous metallic: iron ore, manganese, chromite.
- Non-ferrous metallic: copper, bauxite, lead, zinc.
- Non-metallic: limestone, mica, gypsum, phosphates.
- Mineral fuels/energy minerals: coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium.
- Renewable energy resources: solar, wind, flowing water, tidal and geothermal flows.

Hydropower is an energy resource but not a mineral. Petroleum is a mineral resource in economic geography though it is not a metal.

### Teaching the economic filter

Geology fixes occurrence, but a deposit becomes economically usable only after grade, depth, continuity, overburden, technology, transport, energy, water, regulation and price are considered. Recycling extends material circulation but does not make a finite geological stock renewable.

Four distinctions matter:

1. **Ore versus mineral:** ore is a concentration that can be economically worked under stated conditions.
2. **Resource versus reserve:** reserve is the recoverable subset.
3. **Stock versus flow:** coal is finite stock; wind is renewable flow.
4. **Installed capacity versus generation:** equipment rating differs from actual electricity produced.

### Guided worked application

A low-grade copper occurrence lies near a port and power source; a higher-grade deposit lies deep in remote mountains. The higher-grade body is not automatically the better reserve. Depth, waste removal, road/rail, water, electricity and environmental limits can reverse economic ranking.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Occurrence is geological presence.
2. Resource includes reasonable eventual-use potential.
3. Reserve is recoverable under stated conditions and date.
4. Ore is an economic-geological term.
5. Ferrous differs from non-ferrous.
6. Non-metallic minerals have major industrial uses.
7. Coal, oil, gas and uranium are energy minerals.
8. Renewable flows are energy resources, not mineral stocks.
9. Recycling does not create renewability.
10. Never quote reserves without classification and reference year.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain how the same geological occurrence can move into or out of the reserve category without any change in the rock body. *(10 marks; write no more than 130 words.)*

**Model answer:** A geological occurrence is a physical concentration, whereas a reserve is the economically recoverable part of a known resource under stated conditions. The rock body can remain unchanged while prices rise or fall, extraction and beneficiation technology improves, transport or power reaches the site, legal access changes, or environmental and social costs are re-estimated. Better drilling may also change confidence in grade and continuity. A remote low-grade deposit may enter reserves after a railway and processing innovation; a working deposit may leave reserves if costs, water stress or regulation make recovery uneconomic. Reserve figures are therefore dated economic-geological assessments, not permanent measurements of everything underground.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Formation-process matrix

| Process | Concentration mechanism | Typical association |
|---|---|---|
| magmatic segregation | crystals separate during magma cooling | chromite, magnetite, nickel associations |
| hydrothermal deposition | hot fluids precipitate minerals in veins/replacements | copper, lead-zinc, gold and others |
| sedimentary accumulation | deposition, burial or chemical precipitation | coal, limestone, phosphates, evaporites |
| petroleum-system evolution | organic source, maturation, migration, reservoir, seal and trap | oil and gas |
| metamorphism | heat/pressure recrystallise or remobilise minerals | graphite, some iron/manganese and building stone |
| weathering/lateritisation | leaching removes soluble material and enriches residues | bauxite and some iron/nickel profiles |
| placer concentration | running water, waves or wind sort dense grains | gold, tin and beach-sand heavy minerals |

### Teaching tendencies, not universal rules

Ancient shields and igneous–metamorphic terrains favour many metallic deposits because long tectonic and magmatic histories created and exposed ore bodies. Sedimentary basins host coal and hydrocarbons because organic matter and sediments accumulated, were buried and preserved. Yet “sedimentary basin” alone does not guarantee petroleum; a complete petroleum system is required.

Deposit geometry controls mining:

- vein deposits may require selective underground extraction;
- thick near-surface seams can support open-cast methods;
- dispersed low-grade ore demands large throughput and beneficiation;
- coastal placers require separation but raise shoreline and radioactive-mineral controls.

### Original process diagnostic

Why can bauxite cap a plateau while placer minerals accumulate on a beach?

Lateritisation enriches residual aluminium-bearing material through prolonged tropical weathering and leaching on suitable stable surfaces. Beach waves and currents hydraulically sort resistant dense grains, concentrating heavy minerals in placers. One is residual; the other transported and sorted.

### Guided worked inference

Map evidence shows a stable tropical plateau with lateritic cap, high rainfall history and aluminium enrichment. The appropriate inference is bauxite potential, not certain commercial reserve. Exploration must establish thickness, grade, continuity, access and constraints.

### Revision register — 9 anchors

1. Magmatic differentiation can concentrate metallic minerals.
2. Hydrothermal fluids form veins and replacements.
3. Sedimentary processes create coal, limestone and evaporites.
4. Petroleum needs source, maturation, migration, reservoir, seal and trap.
5. Metamorphism alters mineral assemblages.
6. Lateritisation can enrich bauxite.
7. Placers are mechanically sorted concentrations.
8. Geological setting indicates probability, not guaranteed reserve.
9. Deposit geometry affects extraction method and cost.

### Concept check

**Question:** Compare lateritic and placer mineral concentration and show how each produces a distinctive spatial pattern. *(10 marks; write no more than 140 words.)*

**Model answer:** Lateritic concentration is residual. Under prolonged warm, wet weathering and good drainage, soluble materials are leached while less soluble aluminium- or iron-rich residues accumulate on suitable stable uplands and plateau surfaces. Bauxite therefore often follows lateritised caps rather than river channels. Placer concentration is mechanical and transported. Weathering releases resistant dense grains; rivers, waves or currents sort them by density and size and deposit them in channel bars, floodplains or coastal beach ridges. Gold, tin-bearing minerals and heavy beach-sand minerals may occur in such settings. Both require source material, but laterites record in-situ chemical enrichment while placers record erosion, transport and hydraulic sorting.

**Model word count:** 103 words; ceiling: 140.

### Teaching the tectonic logic

Three world patterns recur:

1. **Shields/cratons:** old exposed crust contains iron formations, gold, nickel, chromium and other metallic ores.
2. **Orogenic belts:** subduction, magmatism, deformation and hydrothermal fluids produce copper and polymetallic provinces.
3. **Sedimentary basins:** subsiding basins preserve coal, petroleum, gas, limestone and evaporites.

Resource geography crosses political borders. The African Copperbelt spans more than one country; Andean mineralisation follows a tectonic arc; West Asian hydrocarbons reflect a basin system, not national boundaries.

### Original map diagnostic

If a map shows a long copper belt parallel to a young mountain chain, which explanation is stronger: uniform soil or tectonic–magmatic history?

Tectonic–magmatic history. Subduction-related magmatism and hydrothermal circulation create elongated mineral provinces.

| West Asia | large sedimentary petroleum systems | export terminals, pipelines and maritime dependence |
| Australia | shield ores plus sedimentary coal/gas | mine–rail–port export corridors |
| Andes | active-margin mineralisation | high-altitude mining and Pacific export |
| southern Africa | old shield and intrusive complexes | mineral corridors with processing and export nodes |

Current output rank may shift while the geological belt persists.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain why major mineral belts often follow tectonic provinces rather than political boundaries. *(15 marks; write no more than 180 words.)*

**Model answer:** Mineral belts record geological processes that predate modern states. Ancient shields extend across borders and contain long-lived iron, gold, nickel or chromium provinces. Young fold mountains and subduction arcs also cross countries; magma and hydrothermal fluids create elongated copper and polymetallic belts along the same tectonic system. Sedimentary basins containing coal, oil, gas or limestone similarly continue beneath several jurisdictions and offshore zones. Political boundaries therefore divide one geological province into different regulatory and transport systems without changing its origin. Production may differ across the border because infrastructure, technology, conflict, investment and ports vary. A strong map answer first traces the shield, orogen or basin and then explains how states organise separate corridors and value chains on top of the shared geological base.

### Teaching India’s shield imprint

The peninsular shield contains old crystalline and metamorphic rocks exposed through long denudation. It therefore hosts many metallic ore belts. The Chota Nagpur and adjoining Odisha–Jharkhand–Chhattisgarh arc combines ore, coal, water and rail access, helping create a heavy-industry region.

The Deccan Trap is a volcanic province, not one mineral. Its resource potential includes basalt building material, black-soil agriculture through weathering, groundwater in fractures/interflow zones, lateritic bauxite on suitable uplands and associated landscape resources. The 2022 PYQ requires “potentials,” so include soil, water and building material rather than inventing a universal metallic-ore claim.

### Guided corridor application

Bailadila ore’s significance depends not only on high-grade occurrence but on mine–rail–port and steel-plant linkages. Similarly, bauxite requires power-intensive aluminium processing that may locate nearer electricity than the mine.

### Revision register — 11 anchors

3. Odisha–Jharkhand belts are major iron/manganese zones.
4. Bailadila is iron ore.
5. Khetri is copper.
6. Zawar/Rampura-Agucha are lead-zinc anchors.
7. Jharia is a major coking-coal field.

10. Deccan Trap potential is multi-resource, not a mine list.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does India’s mineral geography still bear the imprint of the peninsular shield, while its hydrocarbon geography does not? *(15 marks; write no more than 190 words.)*

**Model answer:** The peninsular shield is composed largely of ancient crystalline and metamorphic rocks shaped by prolonged magmatism, deformation, metamorphism and erosion. These processes created and exposed many metallic deposits, producing belts such as Odisha–Jharkhand, Durg–Bastar, Karnataka–Goa, Singhbhum and the Aravalli non-ferrous zone. Their association with coalfields and transport helped form east-central mineral-industrial regions. Hydrocarbons require a different geological system: organic-rich sedimentary source rocks, burial and maturation, porous reservoirs, seals and traps. They therefore occur in onshore and offshore sedimentary basins such as Assam–Arakan, Cambay, Mumbai Offshore, Krishna–Godavari and Cauvery rather than in the crystalline shield core. India’s resource map thus records two geological histories—ancient shield mineralisation for many metals and younger basin evolution for oil and gas.

### Coal-formation and use chain

```text
PLANT MATTER
→ peat accumulation in waterlogged basin
→ burial, compaction and coalification
→ rank progression: lignite → bituminous → anthracite
→ quality/use distinction: thermal coal ≠ metallurgical/coking coal
→ mine → rail/slurry/conveyor → power/steel/industry
```

### Original rank diagnostic

Does higher coal rank automatically mean the best coal for every purpose?

No. Rank, calorific value, ash, moisture, sulphur and coking behaviour matter differently. Steelmaking requires suitable coking coal; thermal plants need fuel compatible with boiler and logistics. “Coal” is not one uniform commodity.

### Teaching world and India patterns

Coal forms in sedimentary basins that preserved abundant vegetation and burial environments. Historical coal–iron proximity helped produce Ruhr, Appalachian, Donets and other heavy-industry regions. Modern bulk transport allows power or steel plants to separate from mines, but freight remains important.

India’s Gondwana coalfields cluster in faulted basins along the Damodar, Mahanadi, Son and Godavari systems. Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro, Talcher, Ib, Korba, Singrauli and Singareni are map anchors. Younger Tertiary coals occur mainly in parts of the north-east. Lignite belongs to still younger, lower-rank deposits, with different basins and uses.

### Guided thermal-location audit

Mine-mouth power reduces coal haulage but needs water, transmission and land. A coastal plant may use imported coal and seawater cooling but faces port dependence and marine/coastal constraints. A pithead location is not automatically optimal if transmission congestion or water stress dominates.

### Worked 2021 Gondwanaland bridge

Gondwanaland heritage explains broad mineral potential, not mining’s GDP share. Extraction depends on ore grade, exploration, access, land and forest constraints, transport, processing, investment and social conflict. Geological abundance and economic contribution are separate propositions.

### Revision register — 10 anchors

1. Coal is a sedimentary organic fuel.
2. Coalification creates different ranks.
3. Rank differs from end-use quality.
4. Thermal and coking coal are not interchangeable.
5. Gondwana basins dominate India’s coal geography.
6. Damodar, Mahanadi, Son and Godavari are key basin systems.
7. Coal influenced steel and thermal-power location.
8. Freight, water and grid alter mine-mouth logic.
9. Geological resource differs from recoverable reserve.
10. Mineral abundance does not equal high economic contribution.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain how coal geology and transport together shaped India’s east-central industrial geography. *(15 marks; write no more than 190 words.)*

**Model answer:** Gondwana sedimentary basins concentrated coal along the Damodar, Mahanadi, Son and adjoining east-central valleys. Jharia and Bokaro supplied important coking-coal resources, while Raniganj, Talcher, Ib, Korba and Singrauli supported thermal and industrial use. Nearby iron-ore belts in Odisha, Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh created a favourable coal–iron combination for steel. Railways, water, power and later slurry or bulk-transport links converted geological proximity into an industrial corridor. Plants did not locate at every mine: coking quality, freight, land, water, markets and agglomeration selected particular nodes. Modern transport allows some separation and coastal imports, but bulky coal still imposes a strong logistics cost. East-central industrial geography therefore reflects basin occurrence filtered by transport and infrastructure rather than coal presence alone.

### Petroleum-system diagram

```text
ORGANIC-RICH SOURCE ROCK
        ↓ burial + thermal maturation
OIL/GAS GENERATION
        ↓ migration
POROUS/PERMEABLE RESERVOIR
        + IMPERMEABLE SEAL
        + STRUCTURAL/STRATIGRAPHIC TRAP
        ↓
ACCUMULATION

Sedimentary basin is necessary context, not sufficient proof.
```

### Teaching why offshore margins matter

Continental shelves and slopes are major sediment sinks. Rivers deliver organic matter and sand; subsidence creates accommodation; rapid burial can preserve organic material; passive margins accumulate thick sediment prisms; deltas place source, reservoir and growth-fault traps together; evaporites can form seals and salt-related traps. Offshore sections may also be better preserved than uplifted and eroded onshore basins.

### Original system diagnostic

A thick sedimentary basin lacks an effective seal. Is commercial petroleum accumulation assured?

No. Generated hydrocarbons may escape. Source, maturation, migration, reservoir, seal and trap must work together and in the correct sequence.

### Offshore–onshore comparison

| Axis | Onshore | Offshore |
|---|---|---|
| setting | intracratonic, rift or foreland basins on land | shelf, slope and deep-water margin basins |
| exploration | land seismic/drilling | marine seismic, rigs and subsea systems |
| cost/field threshold | smaller fields may be viable | high fixed cost favours large accumulations |
| infrastructure | roads, gathering lines and inland terminals | platforms/FPSOs, subsea lines and coastal landfall |
| social footprint | land acquisition and settlement impacts | fisheries/shipping interaction |
| hazard | flood, seismic and land contamination | cyclone, high seas, blowout and marine spill |
| jurisdiction | terrestrial boundaries | EEZs and sometimes contested maritime space |

### Guided India application

### Teaching the conditional “resource curse”

Resource curse is not geological destiny. It describes a possible pattern where concentrated rents, volatility, weak institutions, enclave infrastructure and conflict prevent broad development. Geography adds:

- deposit immobility gives firms and states a fixed extraction site;
- benefits may flow along external corridors;
- burdens concentrate near mine, waste and water systems;

### Guided sustainability boundary

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core definitions

- Occurrence: physical mineral concentration.
- Resource: known concentration with reasonable eventual-use prospects.
- Reserve: recoverable subset under stated technical, economic, legal and date conditions.
- Ore: mineralised material economic to work under stated conditions.
- Ferrous: iron/steel-associated minerals.
- Non-ferrous: metals other than iron.
- Non-metallic: industrial/chemical minerals such as limestone, mica and gypsum.
- Mineral fuel: coal, petroleum, gas or uranium used for energy.

## 2. Formation controls

- Magmatic segregation concentrates minerals during cooling.
- Hydrothermal fluids deposit veins and replacements.
- Sedimentary burial forms coal and supports petroleum systems.
- Lateritisation produces residual enrichment on suitable tropical surfaces.
- Placer action sorts dense resistant grains in rivers and beaches.
- Metamorphism recrystallises and remobilises minerals.
- Deposit process and geometry influence extraction.

## 3. World belts

- Shields: many iron, gold, nickel and chromium provinces.
- Orogenic belts: copper and polymetallic mineralisation.
- Sedimentary basins: coal, oil, gas, limestone and evaporites.
- West Asia/Caspian: major hydrocarbon basin geography.
- Andes: active-margin mineral belt.
- Africa: Copperbelt and southern shield resources.
- Australia: shield ores plus export-linked coal/gas.
- Rankings change; tectonic provinces persist.

## 4. India’s mineral map

- Chota Nagpur and adjoining east-central arc: classic mineral-industrial heartland.
- Odisha–Jharkhand: iron/manganese and associated ores.
- Bastar/Bailadila and Karnataka–Goa: major iron belts.
- Aravallis: copper, lead-zinc and industrial minerals.
- Gondwana basins: Damodar, Mahanadi, Son and Godavari coal systems.
- Assam–Arakan/Cambay: onshore hydrocarbons.
- Mumbai Offshore/KG/Cauvery: offshore/margin hydrocarbons.
- Coastal placers: ilmenite, rutile and monazite.

## 5. Coal

- Coalification progresses from peat through lignite and bituminous toward anthracite.
- Rank, ash, moisture, sulphur and coking behaviour differ.
- Thermal coal differs from metallurgical/coking coal.
- Jharia is a coking-coal anchor.
- Mine-mouth power trades lower freight against water/grid constraints.
- Coastal thermal plants may use imported coal.

## 6. Petroleum and gas

- Petroleum system: source → maturation → migration → reservoir + seal + trap.
- Continental margins are sediment sinks.
- Passive margins accumulate thick preserved sequences.
- Deltas combine source/reservoir sediments and growth faults.
- Offshore needs high-cost marine technology and coastal landfall.
- Onshore faces land acquisition and terrestrial contamination.
- Refineries follow ports, markets and pipelines, not only fields.
- Gas pipelines create fixed dependence; LNG adds maritime flexibility.

## 7. Nuclear and hydro

- Uranium occurs in specific geological provinces; occurrence differs from fuel availability.
- Thorium-bearing monazite occurs in coastal heavy-mineral placers.
- Nuclear plants are weakly pulled by mine location.
- Cooling, safety, grid and regulation shape nuclear siting.
- Hydropower requires head and discharge.
- Theoretical hydro potential differs from realisable capacity.

## 8. Renewable geography and energy measures

- Solar follows insolation, land and grid.
- Wind follows measured corridors, coasts and passes.
- Biomass is limited by dispersed collection and competing use.
- Geothermal follows heat-flow/tectonic settings.
- Potential → feasible potential → installed capacity → generation → delivered power.
- Capacity does not equal generation.
- Storage shifts electricity through time; transmission shifts it through space.

## 9. Critical minerals

- Criticality = importance plus supply risk, not geological rarity alone.
- Mine and processing geography may differ.
- Midstream concentration can be the tightest bottleneck.
- Beneficiation, refining and component manufacture are distinct.
- Recycling creates secondary-resource geography.
- Substitution shifts rather than automatically removes dependence.
- NCMM is a full-chain mission; approval does not prove outcomes.

## 10. Corridors and regional development

- Beneficiation often locates near bulky low-grade ore.
- Smelting/refining follows power, water, chemicals, ports and agglomeration.
- Value density changes transport tolerance.
- Evacuation corridors can bypass local economies.
- Resource curse is conditional, not inevitable.
- Durable development requires local capability, linkages and post-closure assets.

## 11. Currentness and evidence

- IBM/GSI/Coal inventories retain reference date and classification.
- DGH maps distinguish basin and water-depth settings.
- CEA capacity tables are not generation tables.
- MNRE state capacity does not alone prove resource quality.
- Import dependence requires a named denominator.
- Production, reserve and project status must not be interchanged.

## 12. Answer-writing spine

> 🔑 Mnemonic: **G-R-C-V** = Geology → Resource status → Corridor → Value-chain consequence.

- Distribution: belt/basin before country.
- Comparison: matched axes.
- Resource potential: physical base plus access filter.
- Critical minerals: mine to midstream to recycling.
- Energy transition: old map plus new overlay.
- End with a conditional spatial verdict.
