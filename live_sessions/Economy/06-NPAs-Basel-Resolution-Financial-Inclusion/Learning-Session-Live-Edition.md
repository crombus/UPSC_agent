## 🖼️ Loan-stress timeline

```text
Payment due
    │
    ▼
Not paid on due date
  OVERDUE
    │
    ├─ Up to 30 days ─────── SMA-0
    │
    ├─ More than 30–60 ───── SMA-1
    │
    ├─ More than 60–90 ───── SMA-2
    │
    └─ More than 90 days ─── NPA
```

**SMA** provides an early-warning system before a standard term loan becomes an NPA.

---

# 1. Bank loan as an asset

When a bank lends ₹10 lakh:

```text
Borrower receives funds
        │
        ▼
Borrower owes principal + interest
        │
        ▼
Bank records the loan as an ASSET
```

The loan is an asset because it is expected to generate future cash inflows for the bank.

| Borrower’s perspective | Bank’s perspective |
|---|---|
| Loan is a liability | Loan is an asset |
| Interest is an expense | Interest is income |
| Repayment reduces liability | Repayment recovers the asset |

---

# 2. Due, overdue and default

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Due** | Payment must be made on the contractually fixed date |
| **Overdue** | Amount was not paid on its due date |
| **Default** | Failure to fulfil the repayment obligation |
| **NPA** | Regulatory classification after specified performance conditions are breached |

A payment becomes **overdue immediately after non-payment on its due date**, but the loan does not necessarily become an NPA immediately.

```text
Overdue account ≠ automatically NPA
NPA account = sufficiently prolonged non-performance
```

---

# 3. Special Mention Accounts

SMA categories alert the bank before the loan crosses into NPA status.

| Classification | Principal or interest overdue |
|---|---:|
| SMA-0 | Up to 30 days |
| SMA-1 | More than 30 days and up to 60 days |
| SMA-2 | More than 60 days and up to 90 days |
| NPA | More than 90 days |

### Example

A monthly instalment remains unpaid for 75 days:

```text
75 days overdue
      ↓
SMA-2
      ↓
Not yet NPA under the standard term-loan test
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **0–30–60–90: Observe → Warn → Worry → NPA**

SMA classification is an early-warning signal—not a declaration that the borrower is insolvent.

---

# 4. Standard term-loan rule

✅ A term loan normally becomes an NPA when interest or an instalment of principal remains overdue for **more than 90 days**.

The rule focuses on actual repayment performance rather than:

- the borrower’s promises;
- collateral value;
- expected government assistance;
- anticipated future income;
- subjective optimism of bank management.

```text
Record of recovery
        >
Expectation of recovery
```

---

# 5. Other credit facilities

The 90-day concept is adapted to the facility’s design.

## Cash credit and overdraft

A CC/OD account may become **“out of order”** where, for the prescribed period:

- the outstanding continuously exceeds the sanctioned limit or drawing power;
- there are continuously no credits;
- credits are insufficient to cover interest debited.

```text
CC/OD has no ordinary instalment schedule
            ↓
Test account operations and credits
            ↓
“Out of order” classification
```

## Bills purchased or discounted

A bill may become non-performing when it remains overdue beyond the prescribed 90-day period.

## Agricultural advances

Agricultural loans follow crop-season tests:

| Crop | Broad NPA test |
|---|---|
| Short-duration crop | Overdue for two crop seasons |
| Long-duration crop | Overdue for one crop season |

Therefore, the ordinary 90-day term-loan statement should not be mechanically applied to every agricultural loan.

---

# 6. Collateral does not determine performance status

Suppose a ₹1-crore loan is backed by property worth ₹2 crore, but repayment remains overdue beyond the prescribed period.

```text
Valuable collateral
        +
No timely repayment
        =
Loan can still be an NPA
```

Collateral may improve eventual recovery, but it does not convert a non-performing loan into a performing one.

> **Performance classification asks:** Is the borrower paying?
> **Recovery assessment asks:** How much can the bank recover?

---

# 7. Borrower-wise classification

As a general rule, NPA treatment is **borrower-wise within a bank**, not merely facility-wise.

```text
Borrower has:
Term loan A ───── becomes NPA
Cash-credit B ─── apparently regular
        │
        ▼
All credit facilities of that borrower
with the same bank are generally classified NPA
```

This prevents a bank from showing one facility as healthy while another facility of the same borrower remains non-performing.

However:

```text
NPA with Bank X
      ≠
automatic NPA classification by Bank Y
```

Each lender applies prudential rules based on the borrower’s position and payment performance with that lender, subject to applicable coordination and information-sharing requirements.

---

# 8. Income-recognition consequence

For a performing loan, interest may generally be recognised as it accrues.

For an NPA:

```text
Accrued interest
      ✗
Cannot be treated as realised income merely because it became due

Actual cash recovery
      ✓
Can be recognised according to prudential rules
```

Without this rule, a bank could show artificial profits by booking interest that the borrower has not paid.

### Example

```text
Interest due:       ₹10 lakh
Interest received:  ₹0
Loan classified NPA
```

The bank cannot continue treating unpaid interest as normal earned income merely to improve reported profits.

---

# 9. What NPA classification does—and does not mean

| NPA means | NPA does not necessarily mean |
|---|---|
| Loan has ceased performing under prudential rules | Borrower has committed fraud |
| Income recognition becomes conservative | Entire amount is permanently unrecoverable |
| Provisioning becomes necessary | Loan has been waived |
| Recovery or resolution is required | Borrower’s liability disappears |
| Bank profitability and capital may be affected | Collateral has no value |

A viable borrower may sometimes be restructured transparently. A fraudulent or wilful default involves additional legal and behavioural tests.

---

# 10. Recognition versus creation of stress

```text
Weak borrower cash flow
        ↓
Repayment failure
        ↓
NPA recognition
```

The recognition rule normally **reveals** the stress; it does not create the underlying economic distress.

Stricter recognition may initially produce:

- a higher reported NPA ratio;
- lower reported profits;
- greater provisioning;
- capital pressure.

But it also prevents:

- hidden losses;
- evergreening;
- overstated profits;
- continued lending to unviable borrowers.

```text
ECL direction

```

**Learner question / doubt**

> so now many people are lossing job because of AI, and that may effect the repayment. what is done if that person is unable to pay loan what happens then?

Yes—large-scale AI-related job losses could increase household loan defaults, but **job loss does not automatically cancel or insure a loan**.

```text
Job loss
   ↓
EMI missed
   ↓
SMA-0 → SMA-1 → SMA-2
   ↓
More than 90 days overdue
   ↓
NPA classification
   ↓
Restructuring, recovery or settlement
```

## What the borrower should do first

Contact the lender **before missing EMIs** and provide evidence of job loss. Depending on viability and bank policy, the lender may offer:

- longer repayment tenure;
- temporarily reduced EMIs;
- revised repayment schedule;
- short payment holiday;
- restructuring or negotiated settlement.

These are discretionary—not an automatic legal entitlement. A restructuring may also affect the borrower’s credit record.

## If repayment does not resume

| Loan type | Possible consequence |
|---|---|
| Home loan | Secured property may eventually be sold under applicable recovery law |
| Vehicle loan | Vehicle may be repossessed and sold |
| Personal/credit-card loan | Recovery action, settlement, arbitration or civil proceedings |
| Loan with guarantor/co-borrower | Lender may proceed against them |
| Any reported default | Credit score falls, making future borrowing difficult |

For secured loans, sale proceeds are applied against dues. Any surplus must be returned, while an unpaid shortfall may remain recoverable.

> **Mere inability to repay is ordinarily a civil default, not imprisonment.** Fraud, forged documents or certain cheque-related offences are separate matters.

Banks and recovery agents must follow RBI conduct requirements; they cannot threaten, humiliate or harass borrowers.

## Is job loss insured?

Ordinary DICGC insurance protects eligible **bank deposits**, not borrowers or loan repayments. A separate credit-protection policy might cover job loss only when its written terms explicitly include it—many policies cover death or disability but not ordinary unemployment.

## Economy-wide response

If AI displacement becomes systemic, possible policy responses include:

```text
Reskilling + unemployment support
          +
Targeted restructuring
          +
Bank provisioning and capital buffers
          +
Responsible income assessment before lending
```

⚠️ Blanket loan waivers would create fiscal costs and moral hazard. Targeted temporary restructuring for genuinely viable borrowers is generally more sustainable.

## 🖼️ Asset-classification ladder

```text
STANDARD ASSET
Performing normally
      │
      │ crosses NPA recognition test
      ▼
SUBSTANDARD ASSET
NPA for up to 12 months
      │
      │ remains substandard beyond 12 months
      ▼
DOUBTFUL ASSET
Collection becomes increasingly uncertain
      │
      │ loss identified by bank/auditor/RBI
      ▼
LOSS ASSET
Considered uncollectible, but not fully written off
```

This ladder measures deterioration after an asset becomes non-performing.

---

# 1. Standard asset

A **standard asset**:

- does not meet NPA criteria;
- generates normal contractual income;
- carries no presently identified abnormal repayment weakness;
- still requires general prudential provisioning.

```text
Standard asset ≠ risk-free asset
```

Even a currently performing loan may later deteriorate because of unemployment, business failure, interest-rate changes or economic shocks.

---

# 2. Substandard asset

A **substandard asset** is ordinarily an asset that has remained an NPA for **up to 12 months**.

```text
NPA classification begins
          ↓
Substandard stage
          ↓
Higher probability of loss
          ↓
Higher provisioning than standard asset
```

At this stage, repayment is impaired, but recovery or revival may remain possible.

---

# 3. Doubtful asset

An asset becomes **doubtful** after remaining in the substandard category beyond the prescribed 12-month period.

The longer it remains doubtful:

- recovery uncertainty increases;
- collateral may deteriorate;
- legal expenses increase;
- provisioning becomes more stringent.

> “Doubtful” refers to collectability—not uncertainty about whether the loan exists.

---

# 4. Loss asset

A **loss asset** is one where loss has been identified by:

- the bank;
- internal or external auditors;
- RBI inspection;

but the amount has not yet been fully written off.

```text
Loss identified
      +
Amount remains in books
      =
Loss asset
```

A loss asset does not require the bank to prove that literally zero rupees can ever be recovered. It means the asset is regarded as uncollectible enough to demand full recognition of the loss.

---

# 5. Gross NPA

## Amount

**Gross NPAs** represent the total recognised NPA stock before deducting eligible provisions and related adjustments.

## Ratio

```text
                  Gross NPA amount
GNPA ratio = ───────────────────────── × 100
                  Gross advances
```

### Example

```text
Gross advances = ₹1,000 crore
Gross NPAs     = ₹100 crore

GNPA ratio = 100 ÷ 1,000 × 100
           = 10%
```

The GNPA ratio measures the proportion of the gross loan book classified as non-performing.

---

# 6. Net NPA

Net NPA represents the residual NPA exposure after deducting eligible provisions and specified adjustments.

### Simplified conceptual formula

```text
Net NPA amount
  ≈ Gross NPA amount − eligible provisions
```

The formal RBI calculation also incorporates prescribed adjustments such as eligible interest-suspense and related amounts.

### Ratio

```text
                  Net NPA amount
NNPA ratio = ───────────────────────── × 100
              Adjusted net advances
```

The denominator is also adjusted. Therefore:

> NNPA ratio is not always simply GNPA ratio minus the provisioning percentage.

---

# 7. Numerical example

Assume:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Gross advances | ₹1,000 crore |
| Gross NPAs | ₹100 crore |
| Eligible provisions | ₹60 crore |

## GNPA ratio

```text
100 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 10%
```

## Simplified net NPA amount

```text
₹100 crore − ₹60 crore = ₹40 crore
```

## Simplified adjusted net advances

```text
₹1,000 crore − ₹60 crore = ₹940 crore
```

## Simplified NNPA ratio

```text
40 ÷ 940 × 100 ≈ 4.26%
```

| Indicator | Result |
|---|---:|
| GNPA amount | ₹100 crore |
| GNPA ratio | 10% |
| Net NPA amount | ₹40 crore |
| Simplified NNPA ratio | 4.26% |

---

# 8. Economic interpretation

| Indicator | What it broadly reveals |
|---|---|
| GNPA ratio | Overall recognised deterioration in the loan book |
| NNPA ratio | Residual credit risk not covered by eligible provisions |
| Provision Coverage Ratio | Extent to which recognised NPAs are covered by provisions |

```text
High GNPA + low NNPA
        ↓
Large impaired-loan stock
but substantial provisioning

High GNPA + high NNPA
        ↓
Large impaired-loan stock
and substantial uncovered risk
```

A low NNPA ratio does not mean that the original borrowers have repaid all their loans. It may partly reflect higher provisions.

---

# 9. Provision Coverage Ratio

Conceptually:

```text
                         Provisions against NPAs
Provision coverage = ───────────────────────────── × 100
                             Gross NPAs
```

Using the example:

```text
₹60 crore ÷ ₹100 crore × 100 = 60%
```

Higher coverage generally gives the bank a larger cushion against recognised loan losses, though provision quality and collateral assumptions still matter.

---

# 10. How an NPA stock changes

## Additions

New NPAs entering the stock are called **slippages**.

```text
Standard/SMA account
        ↓
Crosses NPA threshold
        ↓
Slippage into GNPA
```

## Reductions

GNPA stock may decline through:

- cash recovery;
- account upgradation after satisfactory performance;
- sale to an ARC or another eligible entity;
- resolution;
- accounting write-off.

```text
Opening GNPA
    + New slippages
    − Recoveries
    − Upgradations
    − Sales/write-offs
    =
Closing GNPA
```

A write-off is not necessarily equivalent to borrower repayment. We will examine this distinction in Subtopic 3.

---

# 11. Why a falling ratio needs interpretation

Assume NPAs remain ₹100 crore.

| Year | Gross advances | GNPA ratio |
|---|---:|---:|
| Year 1 | ₹1,000 crore | 10% |
| Year 2 | ₹2,000 crore | 5% |

The ratio halved even though the absolute NPA stock remained unchanged.

```text
Falling NPA ratio may reflect:
Recovery/upgradation
        OR
Write-offs
        OR
Rapid growth of the denominator
        OR
A combination
```

Therefore, always examine both:

- NPA amount;
- NPA ratio.

- Standard assets remain performing.
- Substandard assets have been NPAs for up to 12 months.
- Doubtful assets have remained substandard beyond 12 months.
- Loss assets contain an identified loss not fully written off.
- GNPA measures recognised impaired loans before provisions.
- NNPA measures residual uncovered exposure after adjustments.
- Provision Coverage Ratio links provisions to gross NPAs.
- Slippages add new accounts to the NPA stock.
- Recoveries, upgrades, sales and write-offs reduce reported GNPA.
- Examine both absolute NPA stock and the ratio.

> tell what id the difference between gross and net.
>

## Gross versus net—simplest explanation

```text
Gross NPA = Total recognised bad loans

Net NPA = Bad loans still uncovered
          after eligible provisions
```

### Example

A bank has ₹100 crore of bad loans and has already set aside ₹60 crore to absorb possible losses:

```text
Gross NPA        ₹100 crore
Less provisions   ₹60 crore
                 ──────────
Net NPA amount    ₹40 crore
```

> **Gross shows the total problem. Net shows how much of that problem remains uncovered.**

The ₹60-crore provision is not repayment by borrowers. It is the bank’s own loss cushion.

## 🖼️ Four different actions

```text
LOAN BECOMES STRESSED
         │
         ├─ Provisioning
         │   Bank creates a loss cushion
         │
         ├─ Write-off
         │   Loan removed from active accounting books
         │
         ├─ Waiver
         │   Borrower's obligation is forgiven
         │
         └─ Recovery
             Borrower actually pays money
```

These terms are not interchangeable.

---

# 1. Provisioning

A **provision** is an amount charged against the bank’s income to absorb an expected loan loss.

### Example

```text
Operating profit before provision = ₹100 crore
Provision for stressed loans      = ₹40 crore
                                   ──────────
Profit after provision            = ₹60 crore
```

Provisioning:

- reduces current reported profit;
- creates a cushion against expected loss;
- generally reduces Net NPA exposure;
- does not remove the loan from the gross loan book;
- does not cancel the borrower’s liability;
- does not represent cash received from the borrower.

```text
Provisioning = bank prepares for loss
Recovery     = bank receives money
```

---

# 2. Effect on Gross and Net NPA

Assume Gross NPAs remain ₹100 crore.

| Provision | Simplified net exposure |
|---:|---:|
| ₹20 crore | ₹80 crore |
| ₹50 crore | ₹50 crore |
| ₹80 crore | ₹20 crore |

```text
More provisioning
      ↓
GNPA may remain unchanged
      ↓
NNPA generally declines
```

This explains how a bank may simultaneously report:

- significant Gross NPAs;
- relatively low Net NPAs.

---

# 3. Write-off

A **write-off** removes the loan from the bank’s active accounting balance sheet after recognising the corresponding loss.

```text
NPA remains unrecovered
        ↓
Adequate provisions created
        ↓
Loan written off for accounting purposes
        ↓
Recovery efforts may continue
```

## Technical write-off

Under RBI’s resolution framework, a technical write-off means that the NPA is written off fully or partly for accounting purposes without:

- waiving the bank’s claim;
- extinguishing borrower liability;
- preventing continued recovery.

### Example

```text
Borrower owes bank: ₹10 lakh
Bank technically writes off: ₹10 lakh

Borrower may still owe: ₹10 lakh
Bank may still pursue recovery
```

The exact recoverable amount can subsequently change through repayment, settlement or legal resolution.

---

# 4. Why banks write off loans

A write-off can:

- present a cleaner active balance sheet;
- remove fully provided assets from headline NPA stock;
- reflect that ordinary recovery is highly uncertain;
- permit specialised recovery monitoring;
- provide applicable accounting or tax treatment.

But:

```text
Lower GNPA after write-off
          ≠
Borrowers repaid the bank
```

Therefore, when evaluating falling NPAs, examine:

- cash recoveries;
- upgradations;
- asset sales;
- write-offs;
- denominator growth.

---

# 5. Loan waiver

A **waiver** forgives the borrower’s obligation to repay the waived amount.

```text
Before waiver:
Borrower legally owes ₹10 lakh

After complete waiver:
Borrower legally owes ₹0
```

A waiver may be funded or absorbed by:

- the lender;
- government fiscal support under a debt-relief scheme;
- another expressly identified party.

| Write-off | Waiver |
|---|---|
| Accounting action | Legal/contractual forgiveness |
| Liability may continue | Waived liability ends |
| Recovery may continue | Recovery of waived amount cannot continue |
| Primarily affects bank books | Directly benefits borrower |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Write-off writes the book; waiver wipes the debt.**

---

# 6. Compromise settlement

A compromise settlement is a negotiated arrangement under which the lender agrees to accept a specified cash amount in full settlement.

### Example

```text
Total bank claim        ₹10 lakh
Settlement payment       ₹7 lakh
Bank sacrifice           ₹3 lakh
```

Once the settlement is completed according to its terms, the lender waives its remaining ₹3-lakh claim.

A settlement differs from a blanket waiver because:

- it is negotiated;
- the borrower pays an agreed amount;
- the lender assesses likely recovery;
- it follows a board-approved policy.

RBI recognises compromise settlements as a valid resolution mechanism, but borrowers cannot demand a particular settlement amount as an automatic right.

---

# 7. Restructuring

Restructuring changes the original loan terms because the borrower faces financial difficulty.

It may modify:

- repayment tenure;
- instalment amount;
- payable amount;
- interest rate;
- moratorium period;
- other contractual conditions.

```text
Temporary but viable difficulty
          ↓
Transparent restructuring
          ↓
Affordable revised cash flow
          ↓
Potential restoration of repayment
```

Restructuring is legitimate where viability is honestly assessed and stress is properly recognised.

---

# 8. Evergreening

**Evergreening** means using fresh finance or artificial arrangements primarily to prevent an existing stressed loan from being recognised as non-performing.

### Simple example

```text
Old loan interest due: ₹5 crore
Borrower cannot pay
        ↓
Bank gives fresh loan: ₹5 crore
        ↓
Fresh loan used to pay old interest
        ↓
Old loan falsely appears regular
```

Economically, the borrower did not generate the cash needed to service the debt. The bank merely moved money in a circle.

## Other forms

- repeated unjustified renewal;
- temporary fund transfers around reporting dates;
- lending through a related entity;
- restructuring without credible viability;
- additional lending used only to service old dues;
- refinancing designed to conceal rather than resolve stress.

---

# 9. Restructuring versus evergreening

| Legitimate restructuring | Evergreening |
|---|---|
| Financial stress recognised | Stress concealed |
| Viability independently assessed | Viability ignored |
| Revised terms match cash flows | Fresh credit merely services old debt |
| Correct classification and provisioning | NPA recognition avoided artificially |
| Intended to restore repayment | Intended to improve reported numbers |

The same instrument—such as additional credit—can be legitimate or abusive depending on its economic purpose.

---

# 10. Haircut

A **haircut** is the reduction in the amount creditors ultimately agree to recover compared with their admitted claim.

```text
Creditor claim     ₹100 crore
Resolution receipt ₹40 crore
                   ─────────
Haircut             ₹60 crore or 60%
```

A haircut may be accepted because ₹40 crore received through timely resolution could be better than ₹15 crore after prolonged liquidation.

Haircut does not automatically mean:

- corruption;
- loan waiver;
- failure of the entire resolution process.

It must be evaluated against liquidation value, time, enterprise viability and alternative recoveries.

6. Settlement involves an agreed payment and lender sacrifice.

## Revision notes

- Gross NPA is the total recognised problem before provisions.
- Net NPA is the remaining uncovered problem after eligible adjustments.
- Provisions are charged against bank income.
- Recovery means actual receipt of money.
- Technical write-offs permit recovery to continue.
- Waivers extinguish the waived obligation.
- Settlements exchange agreed payment for closure of claims.
- Restructuring changes terms to address financial difficulty.
- Evergreening uses artificial finance to hide stress.
- ECL estimates credit losses before they fully crystallise.

## 🖼️ How an ordinary loan becomes an NPA

```text
Weak project or household income
              ↓
Cash flow becomes insufficient
              ↓
Interest/principal payment missed
              ↓
SMA classification
              ↓
More than 90 days overdue
              ↓
NPA recognition
              ↓
Provisioning reduces bank profit
              ↓
Bank capital and new lending weaken
```

NPA recognition is the final regulatory manifestation of an earlier economic problem.

---

# 1. Four broad sources of NPAs

```text
                    NPA CAUSES
                        │
       ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
       │                │                │
 BORROWER/PROJECT    BANK/GOVERNANCE    ECONOMIC SHOCKS
       │                │                │
 Weak cash flow      Weak appraisal     Recession
 Over-leverage       Connected lending  Job losses
 Diversion/fraud     Evergreening       Interest rates
 Cost overruns       Concentration      Commodity prices
```

A fourth category involves **policy, legal and execution delays**, particularly for large infrastructure projects.

---

# 2. Borrower-level causes

## Excessive leverage

Leverage means financing an activity substantially through debt.

```text
Project assets = ₹100 crore
Borrower equity = ₹10 crore
Debt            = ₹90 crore
```

A small decline in project cash flow may make repayment impossible because fixed interest obligations remain.

## Weak business model

- insufficient demand;
- obsolete technology;
- excessive operating costs;
- poor management;
- unrealistic revenue assumptions;
- inability to compete.

## Diversion of funds

Money borrowed for one project may be:

- transferred to related entities;
- used for speculative investments;
- spent on unrelated assets;
- withdrawn by promoters.

Diversion may indicate misconduct, but every business failure is not fund diversion.

---

# 3. Project-related causes

Large infrastructure projects face:

- land-acquisition delays;
- environmental clearances;
- litigation;
- fuel-supply uncertainty;
- regulatory changes;
- construction delays;
- cost escalation;
- absence of supporting infrastructure.

### Example

```text
Power plant expected to start in Year 3
              ↓
Clearances delay completion to Year 6
              ↓
Interest accumulates during construction
              ↓
No electricity revenue yet
              ↓
Borrower cannot service the loan
```

The original project may have appeared viable, but delayed completion changes its financial structure.

---

# 4. Bank-level causes

## Weak appraisal

Before lending, banks should assess:

- expected cash flow;
- promoter contribution;
- leverage;
- project risks;
- market demand;
- collateral;
- sensitivity to adverse scenarios.

Poor appraisal may accept optimistic projections without testing what happens if costs rise or revenues fall.

## Concentration risk

```text
Large exposure to one sector
              +
Sector-wide shock
              =
Many loans deteriorate together
```

Examples include excessive exposure to:

- steel;
- power;
- construction;
- commercial real estate;
- unsecured consumer credit.

## Connected or politically influenced lending

A bank may lend because of:

- promoter connections;
- political influence;
- group relationships;
- pressure to meet growth targets;

rather than economic viability.

## Incentive problem

```text
Loan officer rewarded for loan growth today
                     ↓
Loan may become NPA several years later
                     ↓
Short-term reward, long-term loss
```

This can encourage aggressive lending unless incentives include future asset quality.

---

# 5. Macroeconomic causes

| Shock | Effect on repayment |
|---|---|
| Recession | Sales and profits decline |
| High interest rates | Debt-servicing cost rises |
| Currency depreciation | Foreign-currency debt becomes costlier |
| Commodity-price collapse | Producer revenue declines |
| Inflation | Household disposable income falls |
| Unemployment | Retail borrowers lose repayment capacity |
| Natural disaster/pandemic | Income and production are disrupted |

Your AI-employment example fits here:

```text
AI-driven displacement
        ↓
Household income loss
        ↓
Home/personal/vehicle EMI stress
        ↓
Retail slippages may rise
```

One individual job loss creates idiosyncratic credit risk. Widespread simultaneous displacement creates a **correlated macroeconomic credit shock**.

---

# 6. Genuine default versus wilful default

| Genuine financial distress | Wilful default |
|---|---|
| Borrower lacks repayment capacity | Borrower has capacity but deliberately does not pay |
| May result from economic shock | May involve diversion or siphoning |
| Viable restructuring may help | Stronger recovery and enforcement warranted |
| Not automatically misconduct | Contains a behavioural culpability element |

> Every wilful default involves default, but every default is not wilful.

Job loss, illness or business collapse does not by itself make someone a wilful defaulter.

---

# 7. Interest Coverage Ratio

The **Interest Coverage Ratio** indicates whether operating earnings can cover interest expense.

```text
                         EBIT
Interest Coverage = ─────────────
                    Interest cost
```

### Example

```text
EBIT          = ₹80 crore
Interest cost = ₹100 crore

ICR = 80 ÷ 100 = 0.8
```

An ICR below 1 means operating earnings are insufficient to cover current interest expense.

But:

```text
ICR below 1
     ≠
automatic legal NPA classification
```

NPA classification follows repayment-performance rules. ICR is an early financial-stress indicator.

---

# 8. Twin Balance Sheet problem

The Economic Survey 2016–17 used the term **Twin Balance Sheet problem** for simultaneous distress in:

```text
CORPORATE BALANCE SHEETS
High leverage and weak debt-servicing ability
               ↕
BANK BALANCE SHEETS
High NPAs, weak profits and capital pressure
```

It does not mean:

- two different balance sheets prepared by one bank;
- RBI’s balance sheet plus a commercial bank’s balance sheet;
- the Union and state government balance sheets.

---

# 9. How India’s Twin Balance Sheet problem developed

## Stage 1: Investment boom

During the high-growth period, firms planned major investments in:

- power;
- steel;
- roads;
- ports;
- telecommunications;
- construction.

Banks, particularly PSBs, extended large project loans.

## Stage 2: Projects encountered stress

After lending had occurred:

- economic growth moderated;
- project approvals were delayed;
- global commodity conditions changed;
- costs escalated;
- expected revenues did not materialise.

## Stage 3: Corporate leverage became unsustainable

```text
Large corporate debt
        +
Weak project revenue
        =
Debt-servicing failure
```

## Stage 4: Bank stress was concealed or delayed

Banks sometimes relied on:

- repeated restructuring;
- loan renewal;
- additional finance;
- delayed recognition;
- evergreening.

## Stage 5: Recognition exposed the problem

More rigorous recognition and Asset Quality Review revealed that apparently standard loans were stressed.

---

# 10. The feedback loop

```text
Corporates cannot repay
          ↓
Bank NPAs and provisions rise
          ↓
Bank profits and capital decline
          ↓
Banks become reluctant to lend
          ↓
Corporate investment falls
          ↓
Economic growth weakens
          ↓
Corporate repayment capacity worsens
```

This is why the problem was “twin”: weak corporate and bank balance sheets reinforced each other.

---

# 11. Why recapitalising only banks is insufficient

Suppose the government injects capital into a bank, but:

- the corporate project remains unviable;
- management remains unchanged;
- lending standards remain weak;
- stressed loans remain unresolved.

```text
New bank capital
        ↓
Old corporate stress remains
        ↓
New losses may emerge
```

Similarly, restructuring the corporation without ensuring bank loss recognition can leave hidden stress on the lender’s balance sheet.

A durable response requires both:

```text
Corporate resolution/deleveraging
              +
Bank recognition, capital and governance repair
```

---

# 12. Early-warning indicators

Banks monitor:

- falling sales or operating profit;
- low Interest Coverage Ratio;
- excessive debt-equity ratio;
- repeated cheque returns;
- delayed statutory payments;
- inventory accumulation;
- declining account turnover;
- loan proceeds moving to related entities;
- persistent excess drawings;
- adverse credit-bureau information.

Early warnings are predictive indicators—not substitutes for the legal NPA test.

# 1. Asset Quality Review

The **Asset Quality Review—AQR** was an RBI-led examination initiated in 2015 to identify whether banks were correctly classifying stressed loans.

## Why was it needed?

Some loans that were economically stressed remained reported as standard because of:

- repeated restructuring;
- inadequate recognition;
- evergreening;
- inconsistent classification among banks;
- optimistic assessment of borrower viability.

```text
Reported standard loan
          ↓
RBI examines actual repayment and viability
          ↓
Stress discovered
          ↓
Loan reclassified as NPA
```

## Immediate consequences

AQR led to:

- recognition of additional NPAs;
- increased provisioning;
- lower reported profits;
- pressure on bank capital.

This apparent deterioration did not necessarily mean that AQR created bad loans.

```text
Underlying loss already existed
              ↓
AQR made it visible
```

> 🔑 **Analogy:** A medical test may reveal an illness and worsen the reported diagnosis, but it did not create the illness.

---

# 2. Why recognition is beneficial

Delayed recognition permits:

- overstated profits;
- distribution of unrealised income;
- repeated lending to unviable borrowers;
- delayed resolution;
- erosion of recoverable value.

Prompt recognition permits:

- adequate provisioning;
- credible financial statements;
- earlier recovery or resolution;
- realistic capital assessment;
- accountability for poor lending.

The short-term pain of recognition can improve long-term financial stability.

---

# 3. Prompt Corrective Action

**PCA** is an RBI supervisory framework for timely intervention when a bank breaches specified financial-risk thresholds.

✅ RBI’s current commercial-bank framework monitors three areas:

```text
C — Capital
A — Asset quality
L — Leverage
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: PCA watches the bank’s CAL—Capital, Asset quality and Leverage.**

## Indicators

| Area | Principal indicator |
|---|---|
| Capital | CRAR and CET1 ratio |
| Asset quality | Net NPA ratio |
| Leverage | Tier 1 leverage ratio |

PCA evaluates the financial condition of the **bank**, not whether an individual borrower’s loan is an NPA.

---

# 4. PCA asset-quality thresholds

Under RBI’s July 2026 framework:

| Risk threshold | Net NPA ratio |
|---|---:|
| Threshold 1 | ≥6% but <9% |
| Threshold 2 | ≥9% but <12% |
| Threshold 3 | ≥12% |

Capital and leverage have separate thresholds based on deviations from their regulatory minima.

A breach **may** result in PCA invocation. RBI also considers audited results and its continuing supervisory assessment.

---

# 5. Graded corrective action

```text
Threshold 1
Early weakness
    ↓
Threshold 2
Greater weakness
    ↓
Threshold 3
Severe weakness
```

## Mandatory measures

| Level | Possible mandatory restriction |
|---|---|
| Threshold 1 | Dividend restriction; owners/promoters required to bring capital |
| Threshold 2 | Threshold 1 measures plus restrictions on branch expansion |
| Threshold 3 | Earlier measures plus restrictions on non-technology capital expenditure |

## Discretionary measures

RBI may additionally require:

- capital raising;
- higher provisioning;
- reduction of risky lending;
- lower sectoral concentration;
- stronger recovery efforts;
- management or governance changes;
- restrictions on new business;
- sale of assets;
- special audits or inspections;
- amalgamation or reconstruction.

---

# 6. PCA does not mean bank closure

| PCA is | PCA is not automatically |
|---|---|
| Corrective supervision | Licence cancellation |
| Early regulatory intervention | Liquidation |
| Financial-health restoration | Moratorium |
| Restrictions calibrated to weakness | DICGC payout |
| Protection against further deterioration | Proof that every deposit is lost |

A bank under PCA generally continues operating, subject to the restrictions imposed.

RBI may nevertheless take stronger action separately if circumstances warrant.

---

# 7. Leaving PCA

✅ Under RBI’s July 2026 framework, withdrawal of PCA restrictions is considered when:

- no threshold breach appears in **four continuous quarterly statements**;
- one of these is an audited annual financial statement;
- RBI gains supervisory comfort;
- profitability appears sustainable.

```text
One good quarter
      ≠
automatic exit from PCA
```

---

# 8. AQR versus PCA

| AQR | PCA |
|---|---|
| Reviews loan classification and asset quality | Supervises financially weak banks |
| Reveals hidden stress | Requires corrective measures |
| Primarily diagnostic | Primarily corrective |
| Applied as a system-wide review exercise | Applied to an individual bank |
| Can increase reported NPAs | Can restrict risky expansion |

```text
AQR asks:
“Are these loans correctly classified?”

PCA asks:
“Is this bank financially weak, and what must it do?”
```

# 9. The 4R strategy

## 1. Recognition

Identify stressed loans honestly through:

- prudential classification;
- AQR;
- disclosure;
- ending evergreening.

## 2. Resolution

Address recognised stress through:

- recovery;
- restructuring of viable borrowers;
- SARFAESI or DRT;
- IBC;
- sale to ARCs or NARCL;
- liquidation where necessary.

## 3. Recapitalisation

Restore capital depleted by:

- loan losses;
- provisions;
- write-offs.

Capital may come from:

- government infusion into PSBs;
- retained earnings;
- equity issuance;
- promoters or investors;
- asset rationalisation.

## 4. Reforms

Prevent recurrence through:

- stronger boards;
- improved appraisal;
- accountability;
- better risk management;
- technology and monitoring;
- reduced connected lending;
- revised employee incentives.

# 10. Why all four are necessary

```text
Recognition without resolution
→ problem becomes visible but remains unresolved

Resolution without recognition
→ hidden loans never enter the process

Recognition and resolution without recapitalisation
→ losses may leave banks unable to lend

Capital without reforms
→ fresh money may finance another NPA cycle
```

Therefore:

```text
Recognition
     +
Resolution
     +
Recapitalisation
     +
Reforms
     =
Durable balance-sheet repair
```

# 11. Who bears recapitalisation costs?

When government recapitalises a PSB, public funds are invested in the bank.

This may protect:

- depositors;
- payment continuity;
- credit supply;
- financial stability.

But it raises concerns about:

- fiscal cost;
- taxpayer exposure;
- moral hazard;
- repeated rescue of poor governance.

Recapitalisation should therefore be linked to reform and accountability.

# 2. Basel Committee

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision** develops international banking-supervision standards.

It is associated with the **Bank for International Settlements** in Basel, Switzerland.

Basel standards:

- are international regulatory standards;
- are implemented through national regulators;
- do not directly replace Indian law;
- are adapted by RBI for Indian banks.

```text
Basel Committee develops standards
                 ↓
RBI adapts and implements them in India
```

# 3. Evolution from Basel I to Basel III

| Framework | Central focus |
|---|---|
| Basel I | Minimum capital mainly against credit risk |
| Basel II | Greater risk sensitivity and three supervisory pillars |
| Basel III | Better-quality capital, buffers, leverage and liquidity resilience |

## Basel I

Introduced a basic capital-adequacy framework based largely on credit risk.

Its limitations included:

- broad risk categories;
- limited risk sensitivity;
- inadequate treatment of operational and market complexity.

## Basel II: Three pillars

```text
PILLAR 1
Minimum capital requirements
Credit + market + operational risk

PILLAR 2
Supervisory review
Regulator examines risks and capital planning

PILLAR 3
Market discipline
Disclosure enables external scrutiny
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Requirements → Review → Reporting**

## Basel III

Developed after the Global Financial Crisis to strengthen:

- quality and quantity of capital;
- capital buffers;
- leverage controls;
- liquidity resilience;
- treatment of systemic institutions.

Basel III did not abolish Basel II’s three-pillar structure; it strengthened the framework.

# 4. CRAR

**CRAR** means Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio.

```text
                    Regulatory capital
CRAR = ─────────────────────────────────── × 100
                  Risk-weighted assets
```

CRAR is also called the **Capital Adequacy Ratio**.

It asks:

> How much loss-absorbing capital does the bank hold relative to the risks it has assumed?

---

# 5. Why not divide capital by total assets?

All bank assets are not equally risky.

Consider two ₹100-crore assets:

```text
₹100 crore cash
Risk of credit default: extremely low

₹100 crore unsecured risky loans
Risk of default: considerably higher
```

Treating them identically would encourage banks to hold risky assets without additional capital.

Therefore:

```text
Asset exposure × regulatory risk weight
                 =
Risk-weighted asset
```

---

# 6. Risk-weighted assets

## Simplified example

| Asset | Exposure | Illustrative risk weight | RWA |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cash | ₹100 crore | 0% | ₹0 |
| Government exposure | ₹200 crore | 0% | ₹0 |
| Housing loans | ₹300 crore | 50% | ₹150 crore |
| Corporate loans | ₹400 crore | 100% | ₹400 crore |
| **Total** | **₹1,000 crore** | — | **₹550 crore** |

The risk weights above are only a simplified teaching illustration; actual RBI treatment depends on exposure category, rating, collateral and applicable directions.

```text
Total assets = ₹1,000 crore
RWA          = ₹550 crore
```

Regulatory capital is calculated against ₹550 crore—not mechanically against ₹1,000 crore.

---

# 7. CRAR calculation

Assume:

```text
Eligible capital = ₹66 crore
RWA              = ₹550 crore
```

Then:

```text
CRAR = 66 ÷ 550 × 100
     = 12%
```

If risky assets expand to ₹800 crore while capital remains ₹66 crore:

```text
CRAR = 66 ÷ 800 × 100
     = 8.25%
```

The bank’s capital amount remained unchanged, but its capital adequacy deteriorated because its risk exposure increased.

# 8. Capital structure

```text
TOTAL REGULATORY CAPITAL
            │
      ┌─────┴─────┐
      │           │
   TIER 1       TIER 2
Going-concern  Gone-concern/
capital        supplementary capital
      │
  ┌───┴───┐
 CET1    AT1
```

## Common Equity Tier 1

**CET1** is the highest-quality capital and principally includes:

- ordinary equity;
- share premium meeting conditions;
- retained earnings;
- disclosed eligible reserves;

after prescribed regulatory deductions.

CET1 absorbs losses while the bank remains operational.

## Additional Tier 1

AT1 instruments are designed to absorb losses while the bank remains a going concern, subject to contractual and regulatory conditions.

They may involve:

- loss absorption;
- conversion;
- write-down;
- restrictions on distributions.

AT1 instruments are not ordinary fixed deposits.

## Tier 2

Tier 2 is supplementary capital that generally provides additional loss absorption, particularly closer to failure or liquidation.

---

# 9. Going-concern versus gone-concern capital

| Going-concern capital | Gone-concern capital |
|---|---|
| Absorbs losses while bank continues operating | Absorbs losses when viability has failed or during resolution |
| CET1 is the strongest component | Tier 2 has a supplementary role |
| Protects continuing operations | Protects depositors and senior creditors during failure |

AT1 is formally part of Tier 1 and is intended to absorb losses before ordinary insolvency, although its risks are significantly greater than deposits or ordinary bonds.

## Why does RBI prescribe higher capital than Basel?

Basel standards are **international minimum floors**, not compulsory maximums. Every country may impose stronger requirements according to its banking risks.

RBI requires higher capital because Indian banks face risks such as economic volatility, infrastructure and corporate-loan concentration, historical NPA cycles and unexpected external shocks. The extra capital provides a larger safety cushion, protects depositors and reduces the probability of taxpayer-funded bank rescue.

Thus:

```text
Basel minimum = global common floor
RBI minimum   = floor + additional Indian safety margin
```

## CET1 in simple terms

**CET1 is the bank owners’ strongest and most permanent money.** It mainly consists of ordinary shares and retained profits.

Suppose owners invest ₹100 crore in a bank. If the bank subsequently suffers a ₹30-crore loss, this loss reduces the owners’ capital to ₹70 crore. Depositors are not supposed to bear this first loss. CET1 therefore absorbs losses while the bank continues operating.

```text
Loss occurs
    ↓
CET1 absorbs it first
    ↓
Depositors receive protection
```

## AT1 in simple terms

**AT1 is additional loss-absorbing capital raised through special, high-risk instruments.** Investors provide money to the bank and usually receive interest, but these instruments are not ordinary bonds or fixed deposits.

If the bank becomes seriously weak, AT1 may be written down or converted into equity according to its terms and RBI requirements. Investors can therefore lose principal even before formal liquidation.

```text
Normal bank:
AT1 investor may receive interest

Severely stressed bank:
AT1 may stop paying interest
and may be written down or converted
```

## CET1 versus AT1

| CET1 | AT1 |
|---|---|
| Ordinary shareholder capital | Special loss-absorbing instrument |
| Highest-quality capital | Additional Tier 1 capital |
| Dividends are discretionary | Interest/distributions are discretionary |
| First and permanent loss cushion | Absorbs losses under specified triggers |
| No repayment maturity for ordinary shares | Generally perpetual |
| Ownership stake | Usually not ordinary ownership initially |

```text
TIER 1 CAPITAL
      │
      ├── CET1: core owners’ capital
      └── AT1: supplementary high-risk capital
```

> **Memory aid:** **CET1 = Core Equity Tier 1; AT1 = Additional Tier 1.**

Neither CET1 nor AT1 is a customer deposit. Depositors lend money to the bank; CET1 and AT1 investors provide capital intended to absorb losses.

# 1. Capital Conservation Buffer

The **CCB** is an additional layer of CET1 capital above the ordinary minimum requirement.

```text
Base total CRAR requirement = 9%
CCB                         = 2.5%
                             ─────
Effective total requirement = 11.5%
```

Its purpose is to ensure that banks accumulate capital during normal periods that can absorb losses during stress.

## Is the CCB locked cash?

No. It is not a separate cash account kept with RBI.

```text
CCB = additional loss-absorbing CET1
      not a pile of reserved currency
```

## What happens if a bank enters the buffer?

A bank may continue operating, but restrictions can apply to distributions such as:

- dividends;
- share buybacks;
- discretionary bonuses;
- certain AT1 distributions.

The objective is to conserve earnings and rebuild capital.

---

# 2. Countercyclical Capital Buffer

The **CCyB** is different from the CCB.

| CCB | CCyB |
|---|---|
| Permanent structural buffer | Activated according to excessive system-wide credit growth |
| Protects individual bank resilience | Addresses economy-wide credit-cycle risk |
| Normally 2.5% CET1 | Rate can vary according to regulatory decision |
| Applies in normal capital framework | Built during excessive credit expansion and released during stress |

```text
Credit boom
    ↓
Risk accumulates silently
    ↓
CCyB may be activated
    ↓
Banks build extra capital
```

> **CCB conserves bank capital; CCyB counters the credit cycle.**

---

# 3. Why CRAR alone is insufficient

A bank may apparently maintain a satisfactory CRAR by holding assets carrying low regulatory risk weights while expanding its total balance sheet enormously.

```text
Capital = ₹50 crore
Total exposure = ₹2,000 crore
Risk-weighted assets = ₹400 crore

CRAR = 50 ÷ 400 = 12.5%
```

The bank appears adequately capitalised under CRAR, but its capital is only 2.5% of total exposure.

This is why Basel III introduced a non-risk-weighted **leverage ratio** as a backstop.

# 4. Leverage ratio

```text
                         Tier 1 capital
Leverage ratio = ─────────────────────────── × 100
                    Total exposure measure
```

The exposure measure broadly includes:

- balance-sheet assets;
- derivative exposures;
- securities-financing transactions;
- relevant off-balance-sheet commitments.

Unlike CRAR, it does not rely primarily on risk weights.

## Current RBI minima

| Bank | Minimum leverage ratio |
|---|---:|
| Domestic Systemically Important Bank | 4% |
| Other applicable banks | 3.5% |

### Example

```text
Tier 1 capital        = ₹70 crore
Total exposure measure = ₹2,000 crore

Leverage ratio = 70 ÷ 2,000 × 100
               = 3.5%
```

> **CRAR asks:** How much capital exists against risk-weighted exposure?
> **Leverage ratio asks:** How large is total exposure relative to Tier 1 capital?

# 5. Solvent but illiquid bank

Assume a bank owns sound home loans repayable over 20 years, but depositors demand their money today.

```text
Long-term valuable assets
          +
Immediate cash shortage
          =
Liquidity problem
```

The bank may ultimately have more assets than liabilities and still be unable to make immediate payments.

This is why capital alone cannot ensure liquidity.

---

# 6. Liquidity Coverage Ratio

The **LCR** ensures that a bank possesses enough High-Quality Liquid Assets to survive a severe **30-day liquidity stress period**.

```text
                         Stock of HQLA
LCR = ───────────────────────────────────── × 100
        Total net cash outflows over 30 days
```

Minimum requirement:

```text
LCR ≥ 100%
```

### Example

```text
High-Quality Liquid Assets = ₹120 crore
Expected net cash outflows = ₹100 crore

LCR = 120%
```

The bank has ₹1.20 of liquid assets for each ₹1 of projected net outflow during the stress period.

---

# 7. High-Quality Liquid Assets

HQLA should be assets that can be rapidly converted into cash with little loss of value.

Examples may include:

- cash;
- central-bank reserves meeting conditions;
- eligible government securities;
- specified highly liquid marketable assets subject to limits and haircuts.

A long-term corporate loan is generally not HQLA merely because the borrower is expected to repay eventually.

```text
Valuable asset
      ≠
readily liquid asset
```

---

# 8. Run-off rates

RBI does not assume that every depositor will withdraw simultaneously. Different liabilities receive estimated **run-off rates** under a stress scenario.

### Example

```text
Deposit amount = ₹100 crore
Run-off rate   = 10%

Expected outflow = ₹10 crore
```

A higher run-off rate means the funding source is assumed to be less stable.

---

# 9. April 2026 LCR reform

✅ Effective **1 April 2026**, RBI requires an additional 2.5% run-off factor for retail and small-business deposits enabled for internet and mobile banking.

| Deposit category | Revised run-off rate |
|---|---:|
| Stable retail deposit with internet/mobile banking | 7.5% |
| Less-stable retail deposit with internet/mobile banking | 12.5% |

## Why?

```text
Traditional withdrawal
Branch visit and operational friction

Digital withdrawal
Immediate transfer through phone
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Digital access can make deposits move more rapidly during panic, so liquidity models must recognise faster potential withdrawals.

This does not mean digital banking is unsafe. It means that liquidity regulation must reflect changed depositor behaviour.

---

# 10. Net Stable Funding Ratio

The **NSFR** addresses a longer, one-year funding horizon.

```text
                         Available Stable Funding
NSFR = ───────────────────────────────────────── × 100
                         Required Stable Funding
```

Minimum requirement:

```text
NSFR ≥ 100%
```

## Available Stable Funding

Funding considered more reliable, such as:

- capital;
- long-term borrowing;
- relatively stable deposits.

## Required Stable Funding

The amount of stable funding required according to the nature and maturity of assets.

Long-term and illiquid assets require more stable funding than cash or short-term liquid assets.

---

# 11. LCR versus NSFR

| LCR | NSFR |
|---|---|
| Short-term liquidity resilience | Structural funding resilience |
| 30-day stress horizon | One-year horizon |
| HQLA ÷ net cash outflows | Available ÷ required stable funding |
| Handles sudden withdrawals | Limits long-term maturity mismatch |
| Stock of liquid assets | Stability of funding structure |

```text
LCR:
Can the bank survive the next 30 stressed days?

NSFR:

```


## 🖼️ Recovery architecture

```text
BORROWER DEFAULTS
       │
       ├─ Secured loan
       │      ↓
       │   SARFAESI
       │   Enforce registered security
       │
       ├─ Bank/FI debt claim
       │      ↓
       │     DRT
       │   Adjudication and recovery
       │
       └─ Corporate insolvency
              ↓
             IBC
       Collective resolution
```

These mechanisms overlap, but their purpose and procedure differ.

---

# 1. Secured versus unsecured loan

## Secured loan

A specific asset supports repayment:

- house under a housing loan;
- vehicle under an automobile loan;
- factory or machinery;
- commercial property;
- pledged financial assets.

If default continues, the lender may enforce the security according to law.

## Unsecured loan

No specific asset is charged to the lender:

- many personal loans;
- credit-card dues;
- some consumer loans.

```text
Unsecured default
       ≠
Lender may seize any property it chooses
```

The lender must pursue contractual and legal recovery routes.

---

# 2. SARFAESI Act, 2002

**SARFAESI** stands for:

> Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002.

It enables eligible secured creditors to enforce security interests without first obtaining an ordinary civil-court decree.

```text
Loan becomes NPA
       ↓
Secured creditor issues demand notice
       ↓
Borrower receives 60 days
       ↓
Dues remain unpaid
       ↓
Creditor may take statutory enforcement measures
```

---

# 3. Actions available under SARFAESI

After satisfying legal requirements, the secured creditor may:

- take possession of the secured asset;
- take over management in eligible cases;
- appoint a manager;
- sell, lease or assign the secured asset;
- require a person owing money to the borrower to pay the secured creditor.

For physical possession, assistance may be obtained from the District Magistrate or Chief Metropolitan Magistrate under the statutory process.

---

# 4. What SARFAESI does not permit

A lender cannot:

- seize assets unrelated to the security without legal authority;
- ignore the mandatory notice procedure;
- use harassment or physical intimidation;
- treat every unsecured loan as mortgage-backed;
- automatically appropriate the entire sale proceeds.

### Sale proceeds

```text
Secured asset sold
       ↓
Recovery expenses paid
       ↓
Secured debt satisfied
       ↓
Any lawful surplus returned
```

If the sale proceeds are insufficient, the outstanding balance may remain recoverable through applicable legal processes.

---

# 5. Borrower protections

The borrower may:

- repay within the statutory notice period;
- make objections or representations;
- challenge enforcement measures before the DRT;
- contest unlawful possession, valuation or auction;
- receive any surplus remaining after lawful dues and costs.

Thus:

```text
SARFAESI avoids prior civil-court permission
                    ≠
Creditor action is beyond judicial review
```

The DRT can examine whether the creditor complied with the Act.

---

# 6. Important exclusions and limits

SARFAESI does not operate identically for every asset or debt. Important limitations include:

- it primarily concerns an enforceable security interest;
- agricultural land is excluded;
- certain small-value and legally excluded claims fall outside it;
- the security interest must meet applicable registration requirements;
- statutory procedure must be followed.

A defaulting personal-loan borrower does not automatically face SARFAESI unless an eligible security interest exists.

---

# 7. Debt Recovery Tribunal

DRTs were established under the **Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993** to provide specialised adjudication and recovery for debts due to banks and financial institutions.

✅ The Department of Financial Services currently reports:

- **39 DRTs**;
- **5 Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunals—DRATs**.

## Two important DRT roles

```text
BANK / FINANCIAL INSTITUTION
files Original Application
for debt adjudication and recovery
                 │
                DRT
                 │
BORROWER / GUARANTOR / AFFECTED PARTY
files application challenging
SARFAESI enforcement
```

A DRT is therefore not merely a “bank recovery office.” It is an adjudicatory tribunal that also hears challenges to secured-creditor action.

---

# 8. DRT and DRAT

| Institution | Function |
|---|---|
| DRT | Original adjudication and SARFAESI challenges |
| DRAT | Appeals against DRT orders |

An appeal from DRT ordinarily proceeds to the relevant DRAT, subject to applicable statutory requirements, including prescribed pre-deposit rules for borrower appeals.

---

# 9. SARFAESI versus DRT

| SARFAESI | DRT |
|---|---|
| Substantive enforcement mechanism | Adjudicatory forum |
| Primarily involves secured assets | Determines bank/FI debt claims |
| Creditor begins enforcement without prior civil decree | Tribunal hears parties and issues orders |
| Borrower may challenge measures before DRT | DRT examines legality and debt issues |
| Focuses on security possession and sale | Can issue recovery certificate |

> SARFAESI is a legal power; DRT is a legal forum.

---

# 10. SARFAESI versus IBC

| SARFAESI | IBC |
|---|---|
| Individual secured-creditor enforcement | Collective insolvency process |
| Focus on secured asset | Focus on debtor enterprise |
| Primary objective is recovery through security | Primary objective is value-maximising resolution |
| Management usually remains until enforcement | Corporate control shifts to insolvency framework |
| Does not bind all creditors in the same manner | Committee of Creditors makes collective decisions |

A creditor may have multiple legally available routes, but their interaction is governed by the relevant statutes, moratoriums and judicial rules.

---

# 11. Example: job loss and housing loan

```text
Borrower loses job
       ↓
Contacts bank early
       ↓
Possible bank-approved restructuring
       │
       └─ If viable and approved

If non-payment continues:
SMA → NPA → statutory notice
       ↓
SARFAESI enforcement may eventually begin
       ↓
Borrower may challenge unlawful action before DRT
```

The bank cannot immediately seize the home after one missed EMI. Conversely, genuine unemployment does not permanently prevent enforcement of a valid mortgage.

---

# 12. Recovery versus resolution

## Recovery

Focuses on obtaining the lender’s money through:

- payment;
- sale of collateral;
- attachment;
- recovery certificate;
- settlement.

## Resolution

Examines whether the debtor or business can be reorganised to preserve value.

```text
Recovery asks:
“How can this lender collect its dues?”

Resolution asks:
“How can total economic value be preserved?”
```

SARFAESI and DRT are strongly recovery-oriented. IBC is a collective insolvency-resolution framework, although liquidation may result.

## ✅ Current-affairs anchor

✅ On **16 July 2026**, RBI issued norms for specified non-financial assets acquired by banks in satisfaction of stressed claims, including assets acquired through SARFAESI.

✅ Effective **1 October 2026**:

- such assets may be acquired only against NPA exposures;
- valuation must use the lower of specified net book value or distress-sale value;
- banks must generally dispose of them within a maximum of seven years;
- disposal should ordinarily occur through public auction;
- the asset cannot be sold back to the borrower or related parties.

⚠️ **Inference:** Taking possession is not the end of recovery. Banks must value and dispose of acquired property rather than becoming long-term property-holding businesses.

## Why companies receive different treatment

A company is a separate legal person with limited liability. Under IBC:

## The genuine gap for individuals

✅ The IBC contains provisions for:

- individual insolvency;
- repayment plans;
- bankruptcy and discharge;
- a means-tested fresh-start process.

However, as of September 2026, the general framework for ordinary individuals remains largely **unoperationalised**. The notified IBC regime primarily covers personal guarantors to corporate debtors.

Consequently, an honest salaried borrower suffering unemployment, disability or an uninsured accident lacks a comprehensive, time-bound mechanism comparable to corporate insolvency.

```text
Corporate failure
→ collective resolution
→ possible discharge/reorganisation

Ordinary personal failure
→ separate recoveries
→ settlements depend heavily on lender consent
→ debt may follow the borrower for years
```

That is a legitimate policy asymmetry requiring review.

## Why automatic surrender cannot cancel debt

Allowing every borrower to surrender depreciated collateral as complete payment would create moral hazard:

```text
Borrower receives benefit while asset is valuable
        ↓
Asset is damaged or depreciates
        ↓
Loss transferred entirely to bank
        ↓
Higher interest rates for all borrowers
```

In your car example, the absence of own-damage insurance cannot automatically become the lender’s loss. Otherwise borrowers would have little incentive to insure and preserve collateral.

## Better policy architecture

India could operationalise a personal-insolvency system containing:

1. **Temporary hardship restructuring** for unemployment, illness and disasters.
2. **Single adjudicatory proceeding** instead of multiple recovery actions.
3. **Automatic temporary stay** while repayment capacity is assessed.
4. **Income-based repayment plan** for three to five years.
5. **Discharge of residual unsecured debt** after good-faith compliance.
6. **Fresh start** for genuinely low-income, low-asset debtors.
7. Clear separation between **inability, negligence, fraud and wilful default**.
8. Protection for essential household assets, while respecting valid secured claims.
9. Mandatory disclosure, asset-transfer clawbacks and limits on repeated filings.
10. Credit-record consequences proportionate to the relief obtained.

For financed vehicles specifically, regulation could require lenders to verify continuing own-damage insurance, clearly warn borrowers before expiry and offer transparent optional gap insurance—without compulsory bundling or mis-selling.

⚠️ **Inference:** The law is justified in preserving secured-creditor rights, but it is incomplete because it lacks an effective rehabilitation and discharge mechanism for honest ordinary individuals.

A sound system should neither offer unconditional loan forgiveness nor impose lifelong debt:

```text
Creditor protection
        +
Good-faith borrower rehabilitation
        +
Strong anti-fraud safeguards
        =

```

# 1. What IBC does

The **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016** consolidates the insolvency framework for corporate persons and provides a creditor-driven, time-bound process.

Its objectives include:

- insolvency resolution;
- maximisation of asset value;
- promotion of entrepreneurship;
- improved credit availability;
- balancing stakeholder interests.

```text
IBC is not merely a debt-recovery law
```

Recovery asks how much one creditor can collect. Insolvency resolution addresses the corporate debtor’s total financial distress collectively.

---

# 2. When CIRP can begin

The **Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process—CIRP** may be initiated by:

| Applicant | Broad route |
|---|---|
| Financial creditor | Section 7 |
| Operational creditor | Section 9, after prescribed demand procedure |
| Corporate debtor itself | Section 10 |

The generally applicable minimum corporate-default threshold is **₹1 crore**.

Admission is not automatic merely because an application is filed. NCLT examines the statutory conditions.

---

# 3. NCLT

The **National Company Law Tribunal** is the adjudicating authority for corporate insolvency.

It:

- admits or rejects CIRP applications;
- appoints the interim resolution professional;
- confirms qualifying resolution plans;
- orders liquidation where required;
- decides legal and procedural disputes.

NCLT does not normally prepare the commercial rescue plan itself.

```text
NCLT checks legality
CoC exercises commercial judgement
RP administers the process
```

---

# 4. Moratorium

When CIRP is admitted, a statutory moratorium generally restricts:

- suits and proceedings against the corporate debtor;
- transfer or disposal of corporate assets;
- enforcement of security interests, including SARFAESI action;
- recovery of property held by the corporate debtor under specified arrangements.

## Why?

Without a moratorium:

```text
Creditor A seizes machinery
Creditor B seizes property
Creditor C freezes accounts
             ↓
Business collapses before collective assessment
```

The moratorium creates a temporary calm period for resolution.

It protects the corporate debtor’s resolution process, not necessarily every promoter or personal guarantor.

---

# 5. Insolvency professional

## Interim Resolution Professional

The IRP initially:

- takes control of corporate management;
- receives claims;
- protects assets;
- runs the business as a going concern;
- forms the Committee of Creditors.

## Resolution Professional

The RP subsequently:

- manages CIRP;
- prepares the information memorandum;
- invites resolution applicants;
- examines statutory eligibility;
- presents compliant plans to the CoC.

The suspended board may provide information, but managerial control shifts to the insolvency professional.

---

# 6. Committee of Creditors

The **CoC** principally consists of financial creditors.

Financial creditors supply finance against consideration for the time value of money and generally bear the central financial risk of failure.

The CoC decides matters such as:

- continuation or replacement of the RP;
- interim finance;
- major business actions;
- evaluation of resolution plans;
- approval of a plan;
- liquidation.

A resolution plan requires at least **66% of CoC voting share**.

```text
Voting share
     ≠
one creditor, one vote

Voting share broadly follows financial debt exposure
```

---

# 7. Operational creditors

Operational creditors include suppliers, employees and specified governmental claimants whose debts arise from goods, services, employment or statutory dues.

They generally do not control CoC voting in the same manner as financial creditors, although:

- claims must be submitted;
- representatives may attend in specified circumstances;
- a resolution plan must provide statutory minimum treatment;
- unfair discrimination may be legally scrutinised.

This design is debated because operational creditors can suffer substantial haircuts without exercising equivalent voting control.

---

# 8. Resolution plan

A qualifying resolution applicant proposes how the company’s distress will be addressed.

A plan may involve:

- change of ownership;
- debt reduction;
- conversion of debt into equity;
- sale of non-core assets;
- fresh capital;
- operational restructuring;
- merger or business transfer;
- revised payment schedules.

The plan must be:

- feasible;
- viable;
- compliant with law;
- approved by the required CoC majority;
- confirmed by NCLT.

Once approved, it becomes binding on specified stakeholders.

---

# 9. Why promoters may lose the company

IBC changes the debtor-creditor balance:

```text
Before default
Promoters control the company

After CIRP admission
Control shifts to IRP/RP

After resolution
New investor may own the company
```

Therefore, promoters do not simply retain the business after lenders forgive debt.

Section 29A also disqualifies specified persons from submitting resolution plans, subject to its detailed conditions and exceptions.

---

# 10. Resolution versus liquidation

| Resolution | Liquidation |
|---|---|
| Attempts to preserve the enterprise | Ends and sells the enterprise/assets |
| Business may continue | Operations generally wind down |
| Jobs and networks may survive | Employment and supplier networks may disappear |
| Going-concern value may be retained | Assets may fetch distress-sale value |
| New owner may enter | Proceeds distributed by statutory priority |

> **Resolution is preferred, but liquidation is legally possible when no viable plan succeeds.**

---

# 11. When liquidation may occur

Liquidation may follow where:

- no plan is approved within the permitted process;
- NCLT rejects the approved plan for non-compliance;
- CoC decides to liquidate with the required voting share;
- an approved plan is contravened and statutory consequences follow.

Liquidation is not proof that IBC itself resolved the company successfully. It is the terminal outcome when revival is unavailable or rejected.

---

# 12. Liquidation waterfall

A simplified Section 53 order is:

```text
1. Insolvency-resolution and liquidation costs
2. Secured creditors relinquishing security
   and workmen's dues
3. Employee dues
4. Unsecured financial creditors
5. Government dues and specified secured-creditor shortfalls
6. Remaining debts
7. Preference shareholders
8. Equity shareholders
```

Equity shareholders are last because they receive the upside when the company succeeds and therefore bear first economic loss when it fails.

---

# 13. Secured creditor’s choice

During liquidation, a secured creditor may broadly:

- relinquish security to the liquidation estate and receive according to the waterfall; or
- realise its security separately subject to IBC requirements.

This differs from CIRP, where the moratorium prevents unilateral enforcement against the corporate debtor while collective resolution is underway.

---

# 14. Haircut under IBC

Assume:

```text
Admitted creditor claims = ₹1,000 crore
Resolution-plan payment = ₹400 crore
```

The apparent haircut is:

```text
₹600 crore or 60%
```

But evaluation should compare ₹400 crore with:

- liquidation value;
- fair value;
- likely recovery after delay;
- cost of litigation;
- value preserved by continuing the company.

A large haircut may reflect severe pre-existing destruction of value—not necessarily a concession to promoters.

---

# 15. Statutory timeline

CIRP was designed around:

- an initial 180-day period;
- permissible extension;
- an outer target of 330 days, including legal proceedings.

In practice, complex cases may exceed this period because of:

- litigation;
- valuation disputes;
- delayed admission;
- lack of bidders;
- information deficiencies;
- tribunal capacity.

```text
Delay
   ↓
Machinery deteriorates
Employees leave
Customers disappear
Litigation costs rise
   ↓
Recovery value falls
```

Time is therefore an economic variable, not merely an administrative statistic.

---

# 16. NCLT versus DRT

| NCLT | DRT |
|---|---|
| Corporate insolvency under IBC | Bank/FI recovery adjudication |
| Focus on corporate debtor collectively | Focus on debt recovery and SARFAESI challenges |
| CoC and resolution professional involved | Recovery officer and tribunal process |
| Resolution or liquidation | Recovery certificate or enforcement review |

A corporate loan need not always enter IBC; lenders may use other legally available recovery or restructuring routes.

---

✅ Resolution proceeds have generally exceeded liquidation value in aggregate, supporting the economic case for preserving viable enterprises.

⚠️ Official reviews continue to identify delay, litigation and weak bidder interest as major causes of value erosion.

⚠️ **Inference:** IBC’s success should not be measured only by recovery percentage. Relevant outcomes include business survival, speed, jobs preserved, creditor discipline and pre-admission settlements.

## 🖼️ Stressed-asset transfer

```text
BANK
holds stressed loan
      │
      │ sells financial asset
      ▼
ARC / NARCL
takes over recovery rights
      │
      ├─ Cash payment
      └─ Security Receipts
              ↓
Restructuring · settlement · enforcement · IBC · sale
              ↓
Actual recovery distributed to SR holders
```

The bad loan moves to a specialist institution. The underlying economic loss does not disappear.

---

# 1. Asset Reconstruction Company

An **ARC** is an RBI-registered specialised institution operating under the SARFAESI framework.

It acquires stressed financial assets from lenders and attempts to recover or reconstruct them through:

- restructuring;
- settlement;
- enforcement of security;
- change in management;
- sale of assets;
- IBC proceedings;
- revival or liquidation.

> ARC acquires the lender’s financial claim—not necessarily immediate ownership of every physical asset.

---

# 2. Why banks sell stressed loans

A bank holding an NPA must devote:

- management attention;
- legal resources;
- provisioning;
- recovery staff;
- capital.

Transferring it to an ARC may provide:

- specialist resolution expertise;
- aggregation of loans from several banks;
- faster decision-making;
- cleaner bank management focus;
- transparent recognition of losses.

However, the transfer price determines how much loss the bank must recognise.

---

# 3. Transfer-price example

Assume:

```text
Outstanding loan claim = ₹100 crore
Book value after provisions = ₹70 crore
ARC purchase price = ₹40 crore
```

The bank receives value of ₹40 crore and must recognise the remaining economic/accounting impact according to applicable rules.

```text
₹100-crore claim
       ≠
₹100-crore recoverable value
```

A loan’s face value records what is contractually owed. Its market value reflects likely recovery, timing, collateral and legal risk.

---

# 4. Security Receipts

A **Security Receipt—SR** represents a qualified investor’s interest in the financial assets held by an ARC trust.

```text
ARC trust owns stressed financial assets
             ↓
Investor receives Security Receipt
             ↓
Value depends on actual recovery
```

An SR is not:

- a bank deposit;
- DICGC-insured;
- ordinary equity in the borrower;
- guaranteed to equal the original loan claim;
- automatically liquid or freely traded like a listed share.

Its redemption depends substantially on recovery from the underlying stressed assets.

---

# 5. Cash versus Security Receipt payment

Suppose an ARC buys a stressed asset for ₹40 crore:

```text
Cash component = ₹6 crore
SR component   = ₹34 crore
```

The selling bank receives some immediate cash but continues to bear recovery-related risk through the SR.

Therefore:

```text
Transfer to ARC
       ≠
complete transfer of all economic risk
```

The exact payment structure varies. The famous **15% cash and 85% SR** structure is specifically associated with NARCL transactions, not an automatic rule for every ARC sale.

---

# 6. When SRs are redeemed

Assume SRs have a face value of ₹34 crore.

| Actual distributable recovery | Broad implication |
|---:|---|
| ₹34 crore | SRs may be redeemed fully |
| ₹20 crore | SR holders suffer a shortfall |
| More than ₹34 crore | Additional recovery distributed according to transaction terms |

Recovery may take years and incur legal, management and enforcement expenses.

---

# 7. Asset reconstruction

“Reconstruction” means attempting to improve or realise the value of the stressed claim rather than merely storing it.

```text
Acquire NPA
    ↓
Assess business and collateral
    ↓
Choose strategy
    ├─ Restructure viable business
    ├─ Change ownership
    ├─ Settle claim
    ├─ Enforce security
    ├─ Enter IBC
    └─ Liquidate assets
```

The correct strategy depends on whether the enterprise’s going-concern value exceeds its liquidation value.

---

# 8. What is a bad bank?

A “bad bank” is a popular expression for an institution created to aggregate and resolve stressed assets.

It is not necessarily a conventional bank.

| Conventional bank | “Bad bank”/ARC |
|---|---|
| Accepts deposits | Does not ordinarily accept public deposits |
| Makes regular new loans | Acquires existing stressed loans |
| Provides payment services | Focuses on resolution and recovery |
| Holds diversified banking assets | Specialises in distressed assets |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** A bad bank takes **bad assets**, not ordinary deposits.

---

# 9. NARCL–IDRCL structure

## NARCL

The **National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited**:

- acquires identified large stressed assets;
- issues consideration including cash and SRs;
- holds the acquired financial assets;
- is registered as an ARC.

## IDRCL

The **India Debt Resolution Company Limited** assists with:

- resolution strategy;
- asset management;
- value enhancement;
- operational resolution work.

Simplified:

```text
NARCL = acquisition and ownership vehicle
IDRCL = resolution and management support
```

---

# 10. NARCL’s 15:85 structure

For covered acquisitions:

```text
Purchase consideration
       ├─ 15% cash
       └─ 85% Security Receipts
```

### Example

If NARCL’s acquisition price is ₹1,000 crore:

```text
Cash = ₹150 crore
SRs  = ₹850 crore
```

Remember: this ₹1,000 crore acquisition price may be far below the borrower’s original outstanding debt.

---

# 11. Government guarantee

The Union Government approved a guarantee of up to **₹30,600 crore** for eligible SRs issued by NARCL.

Its purpose is to cover qualifying shortfalls between SR face value and ultimate realisation, subject to the guarantee’s terms and invocation conditions.

The guarantee:

- improves lender confidence;
- facilitates stressed-asset transfer;
- is contingent rather than immediate expenditure;
- does not guarantee that the underlying borrower will repay;
- creates potential fiscal exposure if recoveries are inadequate.

```text
Poor recovery
      ↓
Eligible SR shortfall
      ↓
Government guarantee may be invoked
      ↓
Potential taxpayer cost
```

---

# 12. Why aggregate stressed loans?

A corporate borrower may owe money to ten banks.

Without aggregation:

```text
Ten lenders
Ten strategies
Different incentives
Possible holdouts
Slow resolution
```

With aggregation:

```text
Loans consolidated with one specialist
              ↓
Unified strategy and negotiation
              ↓
Potentially faster resolution
```

This can reduce creditor-coordination problems.

---

# 13. Benefits of the bad-bank model

- cleans management attention at originating banks;
- aggregates fragmented creditor exposures;
- provides specialised expertise;
- facilitates price discovery;
- accelerates restructuring or enforcement;
- allows banks to focus on new lending;
- may preserve viable businesses.

But it succeeds only when assets are actually resolved.

---

# 14. Risks and limitations

## Incorrect transfer price

```text
Price too high
→ originating bank avoids recognising full loss
→ ARC or guarantor bears future loss

Price too low
→ bank recognises excessive immediate loss
→ public value may be transferred cheaply
```

## Warehousing

An ARC may merely hold stressed assets without resolving them.

```text
NPA moved
      ≠
NPA solved
```

## Moral hazard

Banks may expect future bad loans to be transferred, weakening underwriting discipline.

## Risk retained through SRs

If banks receive mostly SRs instead of cash, much of the underlying recovery risk remains connected to the banking system.

## Fiscal exposure

Government-guaranteed SR shortfalls may impose public costs.

# 15. ARC sale versus write-off

| Sale to ARC | Technical write-off |
|---|---|
| Financial claim transferred | Claim remains with lender for recovery |
| Bank receives cash/SRs | Accounting removal from active books |
| ARC takes resolution responsibility | Original lender continues recovery |
| Transfer price reveals market assessment | Does not require an external buyer |

Neither action automatically waives the borrower’s debt.

# 10. Microfinance

Under RBI’s framework, a microfinance loan is broadly a **collateral-free loan** to a household with annual household income up to **₹3 lakh**.

The lender may be:

- a bank;
- Small Finance Bank;
- RRB;
- cooperative institution;
- NBFC-MFI;


# 11. Repayment-capacity safeguard

RBI requires regulated lenders to assess household income and total indebtedness.

Aggregate monthly loan-repayment obligations should not exceed **50% of monthly household income** when a new microfinance loan is granted.

### Example

```text
Monthly household income = ₹20,000
Maximum aggregate monthly loan repayments = ₹10,000
```

This ₹10,000 includes repayment obligations from microfinance and other household loans—not merely the proposed new loan.

# 12. Why household-level assessment matters

Suppose:

```text
Borrower A income       = ₹12,000
Spouse income           = ₹8,000
Household income        = ₹20,000

Existing household EMIs = ₹8,000
Proposed new EMI        = ₹4,000
Total repayment         = ₹12,000
```

```text
₹12,000 ÷ ₹20,000 = 60%
```

The proposed lending would breach the 50% repayment-capacity limit.

Assessing only the individual applicant would miss household-wide over-indebtedness.

# 13. Productive credit versus debt trap

```text

        ↓
Income-generating asset
        ↓
Higher household cash flow
        ↓
Repayment and resilience
```

But:

```text
Multiple high-cost loans
        ↓
Loan used to repay another loan
        ↓
Household debt spiral
        ↓
Coercive recovery and distress
```

Credit access is not automatically welfare-enhancing. Suitability and repayment capacity matter.

# 1. NPA recognition

## Standard term-loan sequence

| Stage | Overdue period |
|---|---:|
| SMA-0 | Up to 30 days |
| SMA-1 | More than 30–60 days |
| SMA-2 | More than 60–90 days |
| NPA | More than 90 days |

Important qualifications:

- CC/OD facilities use an **out-of-order** test.
- Agricultural advances may use crop-season tests.
- Collateral value does not determine whether an account is performing.
- Classification is generally borrower-wise within the same bank.
- NPA does not automatically mean fraud or permanent irrecoverability.

# 2. Asset-classification ladder

```text
STANDARD
Performing
   ↓
SUBSTANDARD
NPA for up to 12 months
   ↓
DOUBTFUL
Remained substandard beyond 12 months
   ↓
LOSS ASSET
Loss identified but not fully written off
```

A loss asset may still produce some later recovery, but prudentially the loss must be recognised.

# 3. Gross and Net NPA

```text
Gross NPA
= total recognised impaired-loan stock

Net NPA
= residual impaired exposure
  after eligible provisions and adjustments
```

### Simplified example

```text
Gross NPA       ₹100 crore
Provision        ₹60 crore
                ──────────
Net exposure     ₹40 crore
```

> **Gross shows the total recognised problem; net shows the remaining uncovered problem.**

A low Net NPA can reflect strong provisioning rather than complete borrower repayment.

# 4. Provision, write-off, waiver and recovery

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Provision | Bank creates a cushion against expected loss |
| Technical write-off | Loan removed from active books; recovery may continue |
| Waiver | Borrower’s specified liability is legally forgiven |
| Recovery | Bank actually receives value |
| Settlement | Bank accepts an agreed amount in full satisfaction |
| Evergreening | Fresh finance conceals an unviable old exposure |

> **Write-off writes the book; waiver wipes the debt.**

# 10. Recovery architecture

## SARFAESI

- Enforces eligible secured interests.
- Ordinarily requires a 60-day demand notice.
- Permits possession and sale under statutory procedure.

## DRT

- Adjudicates bank/FI recovery claims.
- Hears challenges to SARFAESI action.
- Appeals generally proceed to DRAT.

