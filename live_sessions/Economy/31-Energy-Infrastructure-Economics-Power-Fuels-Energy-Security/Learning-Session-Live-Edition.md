━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's first problem

When a headline says that capacity increased, did India actually receive more usable energy? Not necessarily. A power plant may exist but generate little; a household may be connected but receive unreliable supply; a fuel may be available nationally but inaccessible locally.

```text
RESOURCE -> CARRIER -> DELIVERY -> END USE -> HUMAN/ECONOMIC SERVICE
 coal       electricity   grid      motor      irrigation
 crude      diesel        retail    truck      freight
 sun        electricity   mini-grid clinic     refrigeration
```

*Energy policy succeeds at the service end of the chain, not merely at the asset or connection stage.*

### The vocabulary ladder

| Term | Exact meaning | Typical error |
|---|---|---|
| Primary energy | Energy in natural resources before conversion: coal, crude, gas, sunlight, wind, water, biomass, uranium | Treating electricity as a primary resource in every context |
| Secondary energy/carrier | Converted form such as electricity, petrol, diesel or hydrogen | Assuming conversion has no loss |
| Final energy | Energy delivered to the user | Equating it with useful service |
| Useful energy/service | Lighting, heat, mobility, cooling or mechanical work actually obtained | Counting supply without quality or affordability |
| Installed capacity | Rated instantaneous power, normally MW or GW | Reporting it as annual generation |
| Generation | Electrical energy produced over time, MWh/GWh/BU | Adding it directly to MW |
| Peak demand | Highest power requirement during a short interval | Confusing it with annual consumption |
| Capacity factor | Actual generation divided by possible full-rated generation over the period | Calling it technical efficiency |
| PLF | Capacity-utilisation measure commonly used for thermal plants | Treating it as installed capacity |

### A numerical example

A 100 MW solar plant operating at a 25% capacity factor would generate:

```text
100 MW x 8,760 hours x 0.25 = 219,000 MWh = 219 GWh
```

A 100 MW plant at a 75% factor would generate 657 GWh. Equal capacity therefore does not imply equal annual generation. The example establishes unit discipline; it does not by itself compare lifetime cost, emissions or peak contribution.

### Energy access is a ladder

```text
network reaches area
 -> premises connected
 -> account and meter active
 -> supply available
 -> voltage/frequency reliable
 -> bill affordable
 -> appliance or productive asset usable
 -> modern service sustained
```

Connection is necessary but insufficient. Energy poverty can persist through outages, low voltage, unaffordable recurring bills, unaffordable LPG refills or inability to finance appliances.

### UPSC integration

- **2018 GS-III Q1:** energy access for Sustainable Development Goals. Demand: connect affordability, reliability, sustainability and modern energy to development outcomes. Approach: define service access; trace health, education, gender, productivity and clean-cooking channels; qualify connection statistics.
- **Trap:** Non-fossil capacity includes eligible nuclear and hydro; renewable and non-fossil are not synonyms.
- **Answer use:** Begin with the carrier, unit, period and service boundary before presenting a target or achievement.

### Revision notes

1. Energy is a service chain, not merely a resource stock.
2. Electricity is a secondary carrier and only one part of the energy system.
3. MW measures power; MWh measures energy over time.
4. Capacity share cannot establish generation share.
5. Capacity factor measures utilisation, not conversion efficiency.
6. Peak demand and annual energy demand answer different questions.
7. Final energy is not identical to useful energy.
8. Connection does not prove reliable, affordable access.
9. A project announcement is not commissioning; commissioning is not generation.
10. Always state the denominator and time period of an energy statistic.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (10 marks, maximum 150 words):** Why is installed capacity an incomplete measure of energy-infrastructure success?

**Responsive model:** Installed capacity measures rated power, not actual generation, peak availability, network delivery or affordability. Utilisation differs by technology; transmission congestion may curtail available output; a financially weak DISCOM may fail to purchase power or maintain distribution; and connected consumers may still face outages or unaffordable bills. A complete assessment should therefore combine generation, dependable capacity, service quality, access, cost, losses and environmental impact. The correct policy unit is the delivered energy service, not the announced asset.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 2 marks for unit/service distinction + 3 marks for capacity-generation mechanism + 3 marks for delivery constraints + 2 marks for outcome verdict (10 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** Two power plants each have 100 MW installed capacity. Plant X has a 25% capacity factor and Plant Y 75%. Which conclusion follows?

**Model answer:** With equal MW, annual generation varies with utilisation; 75% is three times 25%.

**Misconception to avoid:** Installed capacity, generation and delivered energy service are interchangeable.

### Bridge

Capacity and generation now have to travel through a synchronised network; Lesson 2 turns the service chain into a real-time balance problem.

## Lesson 2 - Why electricity is a coordinated network industry

Progress: 2 / 24 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Why electricity is a coordinated network industry

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The balance board

```text
generation + imports + storage discharge
                    =
demand + exports + storage charging + network losses
```

Electricity cannot be economically stored everywhere by default. System operators must continuously balance injections and withdrawals to maintain frequency and security. This gives value to forecasting, reserves, flexible generation, transmission, storage and demand response.

### One chain, different economic properties

| Layer | Main task | Market property | Governance need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Generation | Convert resources into electricity | Potential competition, but constrained by contracts/fuel/network | Procurement, markets, reliability rules |
| Transmission | Move bulk power | Strong natural monopoly and network externality | Regulated planning, access and returns |
| System operation | Schedule and balance the grid | Coordination monopoly needing neutrality | Independent rules and transparent despatch |
| Distribution wires | Deliver locally | Local natural monopoly | Performance and service regulation |
| Retail/trading | Contract, bill and trade | Potentially contestable with metering/settlement | Universal service and network-cost recovery |

The correct question is not simply "state or market?" Competition is more feasible in generation or trading; duplicating every wire network is wasteful. Regulation is indispensable where monopoly and coordination are structural.

### Four distinctive economic features

1. **Real-time balance:** deviations can threaten the whole interconnected system.
2. **Network externality:** a transmission line can reduce congestion and permit distant generation, creating value beyond its own toll revenue.
3. **External costs:** pollution, carbon, water use, displacement and mine closure may be absent from private prices.
4. **Long asset life:** plants, mines, refineries and networks create lock-in, debt and regional dependence.

### An example and its limit

Suppose a cheap solar project is completed before its evacuation line. Its plant-level cost may be low, yet its usable system value is reduced by curtailment. This establishes why network readiness matters. It does not imply that every transmission project is justified; demand, route, ecology and alternatives still require appraisal.

### Welfare test

```text
social value
= consumer service + investment + reliability + innovation value
- private system cost - fiscal cost - pollution/ecological cost
- distributional and transition cost
```

A low tariff can conceal subsidy, deferred maintenance or pollution. A high tariff can conceal inefficiency or excessive cross-subsidy. Observed price alone cannot settle welfare.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** Central coordination suppresses market innovation.

**Reply:** Neutral system operation and network rules can enable competition by giving generators and consumers predictable access.

**Verdict:** Competition and coordination are complements when applied to the correct layers.

### UPSC integration

- **2018 Prelims Q67:** solar production, silicon wafers and tariff regulation. Economic route: distinguish technology supply chain from tariff-setting institutions.
- **Common trap:** Grid-India system operation is not ownership of all transmission assets.
- **Answer use:** Draw the value chain before assigning institutions or reform instruments.

### Revision notes

1. Electricity balance is continuous, not annual only.
2. Transmission and distribution are network monopolies.
3. System operation must be neutral and reliability-focused.
4. Generation competition still needs network and contract rules.
5. Grid assets create network externalities.
6. Electricity prices may omit environmental costs.
7. Long-lived assets create lock-in and stranded-cost risk.
8. Plant cost and system cost are different.
9. Market design must preserve universal service and network recovery.
10. Judge each layer by its actual economic property.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (15 marks, maximum 250 words):** Explain why electricity reform requires both competition and regulation.

**Responsive model:** Electricity contains contestable and monopoly layers. Generation, trading and some retail functions can benefit from competitive procurement and price discovery. Transmission, distribution wires and real-time operation display scale economies, network externalities and coordination requirements. Regulation must therefore ensure non-discriminatory access, prudent investment, service standards, consumer protection and revenue adequacy. Competition without network neutrality permits exclusion; regulation without contestability can preserve inefficiency. A sound design separates ownership from operation where needed, procures generation competitively, regulates wires transparently and rewards reliability, flexibility and universal service.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 4 marks for real-time balance + 3 marks for layer economics + 5 marks for natural monopoly/externalities + 3 marks for welfare conclusion (15 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** Which function most clearly requires a neutral coordination monopoly rather than competing simultaneous operators?

**Model answer:** Conflicting real-time despatch authorities would endanger reliability and settlement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Every layer of electricity can be governed by either pure competition or pure monopoly.

### Bridge

Once simultaneous balance is visible, ask who sets rules, operates the grid, regulates wires and serves consumers—the institutional map of Lesson 3.

### Institutional map

```text
UNION POLICY: Ministry of Power
TECHNICAL PLANNING/DATA: CEA
INTER-STATE/CENTRAL REGULATION: CERC
INTRA-STATE/RETAIL REGULATION: SERC
APPEAL: APTEL
NATIONAL/REGIONAL OPERATION: Grid-India
STATE OPERATION: SLDC
NETWORK ACCESS/PLANNING: CTU and STU
LAST-MILE SERVICE: DISCOM
EFFICIENCY: BEE and EESL
FINANCE/SUPPORT: PFC and REC Limited
```

### Electricity Act, 2003: durable architecture

The Act consolidated generation, transmission, distribution, trading and use; generally delicensed generation subject to applicable technical and hydro requirements; retained licensing for transmission, distribution and trading; established or strengthened regulatory and appellate institutions; enabled open access and competition; required transparent subsidy treatment; and provided for national policy, grid discipline and consumer protection.

**Status rule:** An amendment Bill is a proposal until enacted and commenced. The official local Economic Survey 2025-26 PDF states **Electricity (Amendment) Bill, 2025**. It must not be confused with the separately notified **Electricity (Amendment) Rules, 2026**: a Bill and notified Rules have different legal status and provenance.

### Federalism is built into the sector

Electricity is in the Concurrent List. Union legislation and inter-state institutions coexist with state tariff decisions, subsidies, ownership, intrastate networks and distribution regulation. Friction can arise over:

- tariff and subsidy choices versus national-market efficiency;
- interstate transmission siting;
- hydro-water and rehabilitation;
- coal revenue and regional transition;
- open-access charges;
- renewable-rich exporting states and consuming states.

Cooperative tools include the Forum of Regulators, common settlement/data standards, coordinated adequacy plans, transparent subsidy accounting and benefit-sharing.

### Three acronym traps

1. **CEA is not CERC:** technical planning/advice/data versus regulation.
2. **Grid-India is not CTU:** system operation versus transmission planning/access.
3. **REC Limited is not an REC certificate:** finance company versus renewable attribute.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Multiple institutions necessarily mean duplication.

**Reply:** Functional separation can reduce conflict of interest, but only if mandates, data and accountability are clear.

**Verdict:** Fragmentation is a risk; concentration of planning, ownership, operation and adjudication in one actor is also a risk.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims route:** institution-function matching is a recurring close-option pattern.
- **2026 status use:** distinguish enacted Electricity Act provisions from the Survey-discussed amendment Bill.
- **Answer use:** Allocate each reform to the correct Union, regulator, system operator, network or state actor.

### Revision notes

1. Electricity is a Concurrent List subject.
2. Ministry makes policy; CEA supplies statutory technical planning/advice.
3. CERC and SERC have different territorial/market perimeters.
4. APTEL hears specified regulatory appeals.
5. Grid-India operates national and regional systems.
6. CTU/STU and load despatch are not identical functions.
7. DISCOM owns the last-mile commercial relationship.
8. BEE implements efficiency architecture.
9. Bill status must never be upgraded into law.
10. Federal coordination is an economic reliability issue, not only a constitutional issue.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (10 marks, maximum 150 words):** Why does institutional separation matter in the electricity sector?

**Responsive model:** Planning, regulation, ownership, operation, supply and adjudication create different incentives. Separating CEA's technical role, CERC/SERC regulation, Grid-India/SLDC operation, CTU/STU network functions, DISCOM service and APTEL appeals reduces conflicts of interest and improves accountability. Yet separation can produce coordination failure. Clear mandates, interoperable data, transparent orders and cooperative federal planning are therefore essential. Institutional design should combine functional independence with system-wide coordination.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 2 marks for institutional map + 2 marks for statutory perimeter + 4 marks for federalism + 2 marks for accountability (10 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** Which pairing is correct?

**Model answer:** Grid-India performs national and regional system-operation functions.

**Misconception to avoid:** CERC, SERCs, Grid-India, CEA, CTU and DISCOMs perform the same function.

### Bridge

Institutions define authority, but electricity still needs contracts and schedules. Lesson 4 follows power from procurement to despatch and adequacy.

CA found: Survey Chapter IX links proposed reform to competition, payment security and regulatory accountability; it does not establish enacted outcomes.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Procurement ladder

```text
LONG-TERM PPA -> revenue certainty and project finance
MEDIUM/SHORT CONTRACT -> demand adjustment
DAY-AHEAD/REAL-TIME MARKET -> near-term balancing and price discovery
ANCILLARY SERVICE -> reliability/frequency support
OPEN ACCESS/CAPTIVE -> eligible consumer procurement choice
```

### What a PPA does

A Power Purchase Agreement allocates capacity or energy, tariff, tenure, performance, payment and change risks. It solves a financing problem by creating predictable revenue, but can lock consumers into:

- capacity no longer needed;
- take-or-pay obligations;
- old high tariffs after technology costs fall;
- failed fuel or transport assumptions;
- transmission-delay costs.

Better contracts require competitive procurement, realistic demand assessment, transparent change-in-law treatment, payment security, performance incentives and limited rebalancing rules.

### Merit order is not a single-variable algorithm

Available plants may be scheduled broadly by short-run marginal cost, but actual despatch also reflects technical minimum, ramping, start-up cost, congestion, reserve, must-run treatment and contracts. Low-variable-cost renewables can displace thermal generation, yet adequate flexible capacity still needs revenue.

### Resource adequacy

Nameplate MW cannot simply be added across technologies. Adequacy asks whether dependable capability can meet demand under:

- peak growth;
- weather-correlated renewable variation;
- generator or line outages;
- hydro and fuel uncertainty;
- extreme heat;
- storage state of charge.

```text
DEPENDABLE SUPPLY AT STRESS HOUR
not
SUM OF NAMEPLATE CAPACITY
```

### Missing money

If low-marginal-cost generation suppresses energy-market prices, rarely used flexible plants may not recover fixed cost even though the system needs them during scarcity. Responses include adequacy obligations, ancillary-service markets, scarcity pricing with protection, firm procurement, storage and demand response.

### Contract sanctity objection

**Objection:** Renegotiating an expensive PPA protects consumers.

**Reply:** Opportunistic renegotiation raises future financing cost and undermines investment.

**Verdict:** Use predefined change and rebalancing clauses; reserve renegotiation for exceptional, transparent circumstances.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims trap:** Open access is network use subject to law, regulation and charges; it is not free electricity.
- **Mains use:** Distinguish cheap energy, firm capacity, flexibility and reliability services.
- **Cross-reference:** General PPP risk allocation belongs to Topic 18; this lesson applies it to power procurement.

### Revision notes

1. PPAs enable finance by stabilising revenue.
2. Long tenure can create stranded or inflexible cost.
3. Power exchanges serve shorter-horizon procurement and balancing.
4. Merit order is constrained by physics, networks and contracts.
5. Low marginal cost is not the same as low total system cost.
6. Adequacy uses dependable contribution, not nameplate addition.
7. Ancillary services pay for reliability capabilities.
8. Missing money concerns fixed-cost recovery of needed capacity.
9. Payment security affects project cost of capital.
10. Contract design should anticipate change rather than rely on ad hoc rescue.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (15 marks, maximum 250 words):** Long-term PPAs solve one market failure but can create another. Analyse.

**Responsive model:** Capital-intensive generation needs predictable offtake because spot-market revenue may be volatile and lenders require payment visibility. A long-term PPA therefore lowers financing risk and enables capacity creation. However, forecasting errors, technological cost decline, fuel disruption or network delay can leave buyers with excess, expensive or inflexible obligations. The solution is not abandonment of contracts but better design: competitive procurement, realistic demand and transmission planning, transparent risk allocation, payment security, performance incentives, change-in-law provisions and limited rebalancing. Contract sanctity should coexist with clearly specified adaptation.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 4 marks for procurement channels + 4 marks for PPA/merit-order mechanism + 4 marks for adequacy risk + 3 marks for balanced design (15 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** A solar-rich system has low midday prices but needs flexible capacity for the evening peak. Which reform addresses the underlying problem most directly?

**Model answer:** The system needs compensation for dependable capacity and flexibility, not only MWh.

**Misconception to avoid:** A low marginal-cost merit order alone guarantees long-run resource adequacy.

### Bridge

Adequate procurement creates costs that must be recovered. The next lesson traces tariffs, subsidies and cross-subsidies to the people who ultimately pay.

## Lesson 5 - Who actually pays the electricity bill

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The tariff is a financing map

```text
fixed/capacity cost
+ fuel/energy cost
+ transmission and wheeling
+ distribution and service
+ prudent losses and return
+ taxes/duties/adjustments
= revenue requirement
```

Utilities have large fixed network and capacity costs plus variable energy costs. A two-part tariff can recover fixed cost through a demand/fixed charge and variable cost through a per-unit charge. If the fixed charge is excessive, access suffers; if fixed cost is recovered mainly through kWh, falling sales can destabilise revenue.

### Four different ways to finance a below-cost tariff

| Mechanism | Who pays now? | Hidden risk |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit state subsidy | State budget/taxpayer | Delay or non-payment to DISCOM |
| Cross-subsidy | Another consumer class | Industrial exit and competitiveness cost |
| Regulatory asset | Future consumers | Carrying cost and deferred tariff shock |
| DISCOM debt/unpaid dues | Lenders and suppliers initially | Liquidity crisis and higher future cost |

These mechanisms are not interchangeable. A political tariff decision without timely subsidy can become utility debt.

### Cross-subsidy death spiral

```text
high-paying consumer charged above cost
 -> consumer shifts to captive/open access
 -> DISCOM loses profitable volume
 -> fixed network cost spread over fewer sales
 -> charges rise
 -> exit incentive strengthens
```

This is a risk, not an automatic outcome. Network charges, access rules, demand growth and universal-service obligations can moderate it.

### Reform sequence

1. Identify lifeline consumers.
2. Meter and account for energy.
3. Disclose cost and subsidy.
4. Pay state subsidy on time.
5. Reduce excessive cross-subsidy gradually.
6. Recover network costs fairly from users who remain connected.
7. Protect small productive users during transition.

### Distributional objection

**Objection:** Cost-reflective tariff means abandoning poor consumers.

**Reply:** Cost transparency can coexist with targeted subsidy or lifeline blocks.

**Verdict:** Concealed under-recovery is neither equitable nor sustainable; explicit protection is superior when administratively feasible.

### UPSC integration

- **2022 GS-III Q12:** renewable target and shift from fossil support. Use the subsidy taxonomy; do not treat consumer relief, producer support and unpriced externalities as identical.
- **Trap:** Government subsidy and cross-subsidy have different payers and fiscal effects.
- **Current caution:** The Survey's discussion of proposed tariff reform is not proof of implementation.

### Revision notes

1. Tariff must recover fixed and variable costs over time.
2. A low tariff does not reveal who ultimately pays.
3. Explicit subsidy is a fiscal transaction.
4. Cross-subsidy transfers cost among consumer classes.
5. Regulatory assets defer recovery; they do not erase cost.
6. Debt can conceal current under-recovery.
7. Rooftop/open-access exit can weaken per-kWh fixed-cost recovery.
8. Lifeline support and cost reflection are compatible.
9. Abrupt withdrawal can harm vulnerable and productive users.
10. Transparent incidence is the first reform principle.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (15 marks, maximum 250 words):** Examine the political economy of electricity cross-subsidy in India.

**Responsive model:** Cross-subsidy permits industrial and commercial consumers to finance below-cost farm or household tariffs. It supports access without an immediate budget outlay, but excessive differentials raise manufacturing cost, encourage captive/open-access exit and weaken DISCOM finances. The remaining consumer base then bears a larger fixed-cost burden. Reform should not abruptly remove protection. It should identify lifeline needs, meter consumption, disclose cost, ensure timely state subsidy, rationalise cross-subsidy gradually and recover common network costs fairly. The objective is transparent social protection, not tariff uniformity.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 3 marks for tariff components + 4 marks for subsidy incidence + 5 marks for cross-subsidy dynamics + 3 marks for reform sequence (15 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** A regulator permits a utility to recover today's approved under-recovery from future tariffs with carrying cost. What has been created?

**Model answer:** The regulator has deferred current recovery into a future claim.

**Misconception to avoid:** A below-cost tariff is costless if the consumer's bill is low.

## Lesson 6 - Diagnosing the DISCOM before prescribing reform

Progress: 6 / 24 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Diagnosing the DISCOM before prescribing reform

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - historical UDAY discussion, canonical RDSS and DISCOM diagnostics
CA search: "Economic Survey 2025-26 power distribution financial discipline smart meters"
CA found: Survey Chapters II and IX treat distribution finance, cost recovery and consumer-facing digitalisation as linked but distinct reforms.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The cash-flow identity

```text
INFLOW
consumer collection + subsidy received + government dues + other income/borrowing

OUTFLOW
power purchase + transmission + staff/O&M + interest/debt + capital expenditure
```

A DISCOM can improve theft control and still remain insolvent if tariffs are inadequate, subsidy is delayed, PPAs are expensive or interest costs are high.

### Four diagnostic lenses

| Indicator | What it asks | What it cannot prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Billing efficiency | Was supplied energy correctly metered/billed? | Whether bills were collected |
| Collection efficiency | Was billed revenue collected? | Whether tariff covers prudent cost |
| AT&C loss | How much input energy fails to become collected revenue? | Full financial viability |
| ACS-ARR gap | Does average realised revenue cover average supply cost? | Physical source of the gap |

In simplified form:

```text
AT&C loss = 1 - billing efficiency x collection efficiency
```

If billing efficiency is 90% and collection efficiency 95%:

```text
1 - (0.90 x 0.95) = 14.5%
```

The calculation illustrates multiplicative recovery. Actual official reporting definitions and periods must be checked before quoting a utility figure.

### Root-cause tree

```text
PHYSICAL: overloaded lines, obsolete equipment, technical loss
COMMERCIAL: theft, bad meters, billing and collection failure
TARIFF/FISCAL: underpricing, delayed tariff order, unpaid subsidy
PROCUREMENT: costly or inflexible PPA, fuel risk
BALANCE SHEET: debt, interest, regulatory assets
GOVERNANCE: weak accounting, political interference, poor service
```

### Reform instruments and their limits

- **UDAY:** historical debt restructuring and operational commitments; debt transfer without operating reform permits recurrence.
- **RDSS:** results-linked support for metering and network modernisation; meter installation is not governance reform.
- **Smart/prepaid meters:** improve data and cash cycle; require correct configuration, privacy, communication and grievance redress.
- **Feeder/transformer metering:** locates loss; data must trigger accountability.
- **DBT/explicit subsidy:** separates welfare from concealed tariff; requires inclusion safeguards and timely payment.
- **Tariff reform:** improves recovery; requires lifeline protection and phasing.

### The vicious cycle

```text
under-recovery -> delayed generator payment/borrowing
 -> weak maintenance -> poor reliability/loss
 -> lower willingness to pay/theft -> deeper under-recovery
```

### UPSC integration

- **Trap:** AT&C loss and ACS-ARR gap are not substitutes.

### Coal value chain

```text
exploration -> mine approval/allocation -> extraction/washing
 -> rail/road/conveyor evacuation -> plant stock
 -> combustion or industrial use -> ash, pollution and closure liability
```

Domestic geological abundance does not remove every import dependence. Thermal coal mainly supports power and heat; coking coal is a specialised steel input. Quality, grade consistency, washing, distance and rail capacity influence delivered cost.

### Resource is not reserve

A **resource** is known or estimated geological occurrence. A **reserve** is the economically recoverable part under current technology, prices, law and conditions. Higher prices or improved technology can change the boundary, but environmental and social constraints remain.

### Coal institutions

- Ministry of Coal: policy and oversight.
- Coal Controller's Organisation: statutory, data, quality and mine-related functions within notified mandate.
- Coal companies/operators: production.
- Railways and ports: evacuation.
- Power and steel users: demand and quality requirements.

The Coal Controller is not Coal India's commercial management and does not personally allocate every block.

### Delivered-coal economics

```text
mine-mouth cost
+ washing/handling
+ rail/port freight
+ inventory and disruption risk
+ pollution, water and closure cost
= social delivered cost
```

A distant domestic mine can be less secure or more expensive for a coastal user than a diversified import, especially when logistics are congested. Energy security is therefore not identical to maximum domestic extraction.

### Water and pollution

Thermal generation needs water for steam-cycle and cooling functions. Location, cooling technology and water source matter. The 2023 Prelims route on seawater and water stress tests statement-level qualification: coastal siting may ease freshwater pressure but does not eliminate intake, discharge, corrosion, ecological or cost issues.

### Transition dilemma

Coal supports dispatchable generation, rail freight, state revenue and district employment. Abrupt closure can concentrate losses; indefinite lock-in raises pollution, carbon and stranded-asset risks. Just transition requires:

- worker mapping and reskilling;
- mine closure and reclamation;
- local economic diversification;
- replacement of local/state revenue;
- reliable alternatives before retirement;
- transparent treatment of debt and contracts.

### UPSC integration

- **2022 Prelims Q72:** Coal Controllers Organisation role and functions.
- **2023 Prelims Q66:** thermal plants, seawater and water stress.
- **Trap:** Domestic resource abundance does not prove quality-specific self-sufficiency.

### Revision notes

1. Coal economics begins at the mine and ends with closure.
2. Thermal and coking coal serve different demands.
3. Resource and reserve are different.
4. Quality and grade assurance affect plant performance.
5. Rail and port capacity affect delivered cost.
6. Inventory and plant stocks provide short-run resilience.
7. Coal Controller is distinct from producers.
8. Water stress depends on site and cooling design.
9. Domestic supply can still face logistics risk.
10. Transition costs are geographically concentrated.
11. Mine closure is an economic liability.
12. Security, affordability and sustainability must be jointly evaluated.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (15 marks, maximum 250 words):** Examine the economic case for a just transition in coal-dependent regions.

**Responsive model:** Coal creates national electricity and industrial value but concentrates employment, public revenue and local supply chains in mining and thermal districts. Rapid retirement without replacement can produce unemployment, fiscal stress, stranded debt and political resistance. Indefinite continuation, however, preserves pollution, water use and carbon risk. A just transition should map workers and informal livelihoods, phase retirement with adequate replacement power, reclaim mines, diversify district economies, reskill labour, replace local revenue and allocate closure liabilities transparently. Justice is not a reason to avoid transition; it is a condition for making transition durable.

### From LCOE to system value

**Levelised Cost of Electricity (LCOE)** estimates discounted lifetime plant cost per unit. It is useful for project comparison but does not fully capture timing, location, transmission, balancing, curtailment, dependable peak contribution or externality.

```text
system value
= energy value
+ capacity value
+ flexibility value
+ network/location value
+ environmental value
```

A higher-LCOE resource can still be valuable if it supplies a scarce evening peak or locational need. A low-LCOE plant can lose value when many identical plants produce simultaneously behind a congested line.

### Cannibalisation and curtailment

```text
many solar plants produce at noon
 -> wholesale price falls at noon
 -> captured price of additional solar falls
```

**Curtailment** means available output is reduced because of congestion, security, oversupply, inflexible conventional generation or scheduling/contract failure. Must-run treatment cannot override physical grid security.

### Procurement architecture

- auctions discover a project tariff;
- PPAs enable finance;
- RPO creates obligated renewable demand;
- REC is a tradable renewable attribute under the regulatory framework;
- rooftop/captive/open-access models diversify procurement;
- transmission and flexibility determine absorption.

### Solar versus conventional energy

Solar offers modular deployment, no fuel import, low operating emissions and distributed access. Constraints include variability, land, materials, transmission, storage, recycling and financing. "No fuel cost" never means "zero system cost."

### 500 GW target discipline

India's COP26 Panchamrit statement set a 500 GW non-fossil installed-capacity objective for 2030. It is not a total-energy-consumption target, a renewable-only generation target or an achieved outcome. The dated Survey capacity share is evidence of progress on a different denominator.

### UPSC integration

- **2020 GS-III Q16:** describe solar benefits versus conventional energy and initiatives. Answer with economic, environmental and access gains plus system constraints.
- **2022 GS-III Q12:** justify renewable target and subsidy shift; distinguish capacity from generation and protect vulnerable users.
- **2025 GS-I:** ecological and economic benefits of solar; separate the two dimensions.

### Revision notes

1. LCOE is a plant-level lifetime metric.
2. System value depends on time, location and capability.
3. Zero fuel cost does not mean zero capital/network cost.
4. Cannibalisation lowers captured price during common output hours.
5. Curtailment can result from congestion or security.
6. Auction tariff is not total delivered-system cost.
7. RPO and REC are distinct compliance mechanisms.
8. Capacity target and generation outcome differ.
9. Non-fossil is broader than renewable.
10. Land, materials and recycling belong in economic appraisal.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (15 marks, maximum 250 words):** "Falling renewable tariffs are necessary but insufficient for an affordable transition." Discuss.

**Responsive model:** Competitive auctions and technology learning reduce project tariffs and fuel-import exposure. However, the electricity system must also finance transmission, balancing, storage, reserves, forecasting, land, recycling and dependable capacity. Simultaneous solar output can depress captured prices; congestion can cause curtailment; weak DISCOM payment can raise financing cost. Policy should therefore compare system value, coordinate generation with networks, procure flexibility and adequacy, improve demand response and protect land and material sustainability. Cheap projects become affordable energy only when reliably integrated.

### Duck curve

```text
MIDDAY: solar output rises -> net grid demand falls
EVENING: solar falls while demand remains -> steep ramp rises
```

The system needs capability that can move energy, ramp generation, shift demand or trade across regions. The problem is not renewable energy itself; it is mismatch between output and demand.

### Flexibility portfolio

| Tool | What it does | Main limit |

| Pumped hydro | Large-scale storage and inertia/flexibility | Site, ecology, water and long gestation |
| Flexible thermal/gas | Dispatch and ramping | Fuel, emissions and minimum-load constraints |
| Demand response | Moves flexible consumption | Metering, incentives and consumer capability |
| Transmission/trade | Shares geographic diversity | Corridor, settlement and coordination |
| Hybrid projects | Smooth complementary output | Contract and design complexity |

### Storage revenue stacking

One asset may earn from energy arbitrage, peak capacity, frequency response, congestion relief, network deferral and backup. Revenue stacking can improve viability, but the same physical capability must not be promised twice at the same moment.

### Levelised storage caution

Storage cost depends on charging energy, round-trip efficiency, cycles, degradation, replacement, duration, financing and residual value. A four-hour battery and a seasonal store solve different problems; comparing one cost number without the service requirement is misleading.

### Resource adequacy under stress

Adequacy modelling should test correlated low-wind/low-solar periods, heat-driven peak demand, outages, hydro uncertainty and storage state of charge. Firm capacity is a probabilistic contribution, not a technology label.

### Missing-money reply

If energy prices alone do not finance rarely used capacity, markets may pay explicitly for capacity or ancillary services. Poorly designed capacity payments can overprocure or reward incumbents; scarcity pricing can be volatile and politically difficult. No instrument is universally superior.

8. Adequacy is probabilistic and stress-tested.

### Financing models

- PSU balance sheet;

### Cost-of-capital example

### Stranded assets

An asset loses economic value when:

- cheaper technology enters;
- demand shifts;
- fuel becomes unavailable;
- environmental rules tighten;
- transmission is delayed;
- contracted tariff becomes uncompetitive;
- climate damage reduces operating life.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Welfare equation

```text
SOCIAL VALUE
= consumer service + investment + reliability + security + innovation
- private system cost - fiscal cost - pollution/ecology - transition cost
```

A low observed tariff can conceal delayed subsidy, deferred maintenance, pollution or debt. A high tariff can conceal weak competition or excessive cross-subsidy. Price alone cannot identify social efficiency.

### Layer-by-layer governance

| Layer | Economic property | Governance implication |
|---|---|---|
| Generation | Potential competition, constrained by fuel, contracts and grids | Auctions and markets plus reliability rules |
| Transmission | Natural monopoly and network externality | Regulated planning, access and return |
| System operation | Coordination monopoly requiring neutrality | Transparent independent despatch |
| Distribution wires | Local natural monopoly | Performance and service regulation |
| Retail/trading | Potential contestability | Choice with metering, settlement and network-cost recovery |

### Two-part tariff dilemma

Fixed networks and capacity create costs even when energy sales fall. Recovering all cost per kWh can destabilise revenue as rooftop or captive supply grows; a high fixed charge can exclude low-use households. A defensible design protects lifeline use, sends a marginal-cost signal and allocates common-network cost transparently.

**Strongest objection:** Layered regulation is too complex. **Reply:** complexity already exists physically; hiding it in one tariff or monopoly only obscures incidence. **Residual:** regulatory capacity and data quality constrain ideal design.

### Revision notes

1. Social value differs from the observed tariff.
2. Reliability and security have economic value.
3. Pollution, fiscal and transition costs belong in welfare analysis.
4. Generation can support competition subject to system rules.
5. Wires retain natural-monopoly features.
6. System operation requires neutrality.
7. Fixed and variable costs should not be confused.
8. Lifeline protection must coexist with network-cost recovery.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (10 marks, maximum 150 words):** Why should electricity reform distinguish natural-monopoly layers from contestable layers?

**Responsive model:** Electricity is one coordinated system but not one uniform market. Transmission and distribution wires have scale economies and network externalities, so duplicating complete networks is inefficient; they require regulated access, prudent investment and service standards. Generation, trading and some retail functions can support competition when metering, settlement and network access are reliable. System operation remains a neutral coordination function because supply and demand must balance continuously. Reform should therefore regulate monopoly infrastructure, procure contestable services competitively and recover common-network costs fairly. The choice is not state versus market in the abstract, but governance matched to each layer's economics.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 3 marks for layer classification, 3 for natural-monopoly logic, 2 for contestability conditions and 2 for the governance verdict (10 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** A consumer buys from a competing supplier but still uses the local wires. Which cost cannot disappear?

**Model answer:** The regulated common-network cost remains because retail choice does not duplicate or eliminate the distribution wires and reliability service.

**Misconception to avoid:** Retail competition makes transmission and distribution networks costless.

### Bridge

The layer test becomes a utility-finance test next: regulatory assets and rigid PPAs show how delayed recovery and contract design transmit into service quality.

### Cash-flow decomposition

```text
INFLOW = collections + subsidy received + government dues + other finance
```

Billing efficiency, collection efficiency, AT&C loss and ACS-ARR gap diagnose different links. A regulator may defer recovery as a regulatory asset, smoothing a tariff shock but shifting cost and carrying charges to future consumers.

### Contract lock-in

Long-term PPAs support project finance, yet can lock in excess capacity, inflexible take-or-pay obligations, old tariffs or failed fuel assumptions. Better contracts combine competitive procurement, realistic demand assessment, change-in-law clauses, availability incentives, payment security and limited rebalancing rules.

### Missing money and adequacy

Low-marginal-cost renewable output can suppress energy-market prices while flexible capacity remains necessary during scarcity. If energy-only revenue cannot finance that capability, options include ancillary services, scarcity pricing with safeguards, capacity or firm-power contracts, storage, demand response and resource-adequacy obligations.

**Inference:** No instrument is universally superior; compare consumer cost, gaming risk, competition and Indian institutional capacity.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (10 marks, maximum 150 words):** Why can repeated regulatory assets and rigid PPAs weaken a DISCOM even when supply appears adequate?

**Responsive model:** A regulatory asset postpones tariff recovery; repeated deferral adds carrying cost, burdens future consumers and obscures present under-recovery. A long-term PPA can support investment, but rigid take-or-pay capacity, old tariffs or failed fuel assumptions may leave the DISCOM paying for power that is costly or no longer needed. Together they compress cash available for generators, maintenance and consumer service. Reform should disclose deferred balances, ensure timely subsidy, review procurement within lawful rebalancing rules and protect contract credibility. Adequate nameplate supply is insufficient when the buyer's cash flow and contract portfolio are unsustainable.

**Misconception to avoid:** One improving DISCOM ratio proves complete financial recovery.

### Price and net-load shapes

```text
simultaneous solar output -> low midday price -> lower captured solar value
midday solar lowers net load -> evening solar decline -> steep ramp
```

Cannibalisation is a market-value effect; the duck curve is a net-demand and ramping effect. Both strengthen the case for transmission, geographic diversity, demand shifting, flexible generation and storage.

### Storage service stack

Storage may earn from arbitrage, peak capacity, ancillary services, congestion relief, network deferral and resilience. The same physical capability must not be paid twice for one event. Duration should fit the need: seconds for frequency, hours for solar shifting, and multi-day stress only when superior to fuel stocks, interconnection or demand flexibility.

### LCOE versus system value

`System value = energy + capacity + flexibility + network/location + externality value.`

LCOE omits timing, location, balancing, transmission, firm capacity and curtailment. Adequacy tests dependable capability under peaks, outages, weather, hydro/fuel uncertainty and storage state of charge—not simple nameplate addition.

### Revision notes

1. Cannibalisation lowers captured price during simultaneous output.
2. The duck curve describes net demand and ramping.
3. Storage can stack distinct services.
4. Double payment for the same capability must be avoided.
5. Duration must match the system need.
6. Charging cost and round-trip loss affect storage economics.
7. LCOE is a plant-cost metric, not complete system value.
8. Adequacy uses dependable, stress-correlated capability.

### Mains application, responsive model and unique rubric

**Question (10 marks, maximum 150 words):** Why can the lowest-LCOE project be less valuable than a costlier flexibility resource?

**Responsive model:** LCOE compares discounted plant-level energy cost but does not fully capture when and where output arrives, its peak contribution, balancing need, transmission, curtailment or externalities. Additional solar may have a low LCOE yet produce during already-saturated midday hours, lowering its captured value and increasing the evening ramp. A costlier storage, hydro, demand-response or flexible resource may supply scarce peak, reserve or congestion relief. Planning should therefore compare total system cost and dependable contribution under stress, not rank technologies by LCOE alone. The appropriate portfolio combines cheap energy with adequate flexibility, networks and resilience.

**Unique scoring rubric:** Award 2 marks for LCOE limits, 3 for cannibalisation/duck-curve mechanism, 3 for system-value services and 2 for portfolio judgement (10 total).

### Concept check

**Question:** A four-hour battery is proposed for a five-day low-wind event. What is the first economic question?

**Model answer:** Ask whether its duration, charging opportunity and cost can cover the multi-day need more efficiently than interconnection, demand flexibility or fuel-backed capacity.

**Misconception to avoid:** All storage durations are interchangeable because every battery stores electricity.

```text
global price/supply shock -> import bill -> rupee pressure
 -> fuel/freight/input inflation -> real-income loss
```

Full pass-through preserves the conservation signal but raises inflation; tax cuts cushion users but cost revenue; general subsidy is fiscally broad; targeted transfers protect vulnerable users; strategic-stock release bridges a temporary disruption but cannot solve chronic dependence.

### Import portfolio

Expected social cost includes purchase price, transport, disruption probability times damage, geopolitical concentration and transition risk. Long-term contracts provide certainty but rigidity; spot purchases provide flexibility but volatility; domestic production has strategic value but geological and ecological limits; inventories buy time at a financing cost.

### Refinery and pipeline coordination

Complex refineries process varied crude but face transition and export-market risks. Pipelines have high fixed and low marginal costs, requiring throughput, open access, anchor demand and coordinated terminals or city-gas expansion.

### Subsidy reform

Classify consumer support, producer support, public-service support and non-pricing of external costs. Replace general suppression gradually with targeted protection and recycle savings toward access, alternatives and resilience.

Long-term contracts add volume certainty; spot purchases add flexibility; domestic output and strategic stocks provide bounded buffers. Diversification can therefore have insurance value even when a marginal source costs more. The portfolio should minimise expected social cost—purchase, infrastructure, disruption, concentration and transition risk—rather than the invoice price or import share alone.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Practice 1 - 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish installed capacity, generation, capacity factor and resource adequacy.

**Model solution:** Installed capacity is the rated instantaneous power of generating assets, measured in MW or GW. Generation is electrical energy actually produced over time, measured in MWh, GWh or billion units. Capacity factor compares actual generation with the output possible at continuous full rating; it indicates utilisation, not efficiency. Resource adequacy asks whether dependable capability can meet demand under peak load, outages, weather variation, fuel uncertainty and storage constraints. Thus equal capacity can produce unequal energy, while high annual generation can still coexist with shortage during a stress hour.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 2 marks for definitions, 3 for distinctions, 3 for adequacy implications and 2 for the conclusion (10 total).

## Practice 2 - 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** Why is the DISCOM described as the commercial hinge of the power sector?

**Model solution:** The DISCOM purchases power, uses transmission and distribution networks, meters and bills consumers, collects revenue and maintains last-mile service. If tariffs are inadequate, subsidy is delayed, billing or collection is weak, PPAs are costly or debt is high, the utility delays generator payments and network investment. Generation capacity can therefore coexist with outages and poor access. Reform must combine energy accounting, technical upgrades, accurate metering, timely tariffs and subsidy, procurement review, professional governance and consumer grievance redress.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 2 marks for DISCOM role, 3 for the cash-flow mechanism, 3 for reform and 2 for the service verdict (10 total).

## Practice 3 - 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Examine the role of natural monopoly in the design of electricity markets.

**Model solution:** Transmission and distribution wires have large fixed costs, scale economies and network externalities; duplicating complete networks is generally inefficient. Real-time system operation also requires a neutral coordinating authority. Generation, trading and some retail functions, however, can support competition when network access, metering and settlement are reliable. Market design should therefore regulate monopoly wires, scrutinise investment and returns, mandate non-discriminatory access and enforce service obligations, while procuring generation competitively and enabling trading. The difficult issue is fixed-cost recovery when high-paying consumers migrate to captive or rooftop supply. Fair network charges, lifeline protection and transparent subsidies are preferable to either unregulated monopoly or artificial duplication.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 3 marks for natural-monopoly logic, 4 for layer-specific design, 5 for market/regulatory safeguards and 3 for the judgement (15 total).

## Practice 4 - 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate strategic petroleum reserves as an instrument of energy security.

**Model solution:** Strategic reserves can bridge a temporary supply disruption, moderate panic and provide time for diplomatic, logistical or demand responses. Their value rises with import concentration, shipping vulnerability and long replacement times. They are nevertheless finite and costly: crude must be purchased, financed, stored, rotated and released under clear rules. Reserves cannot correct chronic import dependence, weak refining/product logistics or sustained high prices. They should therefore complement diversified suppliers and routes, commercial inventories, efficient demand, domestic production where viable and strong ports/pipelines. Success should be judged by days of effective disruption coverage and release capability, not nominal storage volume alone.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 3 marks for reserve purpose, 4 for limitations, 5 for the wider security portfolio and 3 for the verdict (15 total).

## Practice 5 - 20 marks / maximum 250 words

**Question:** Renewable-energy expansion can weaken as well as strengthen electricity-market finances. Discuss.

**Model solution:** Renewable generation lowers fuel exposure, operating emissions and short-run marginal cost. Competitive procurement and modular deployment can reduce project prices. Yet simultaneous solar or wind output can depress captured prices, while the system still needs transmission, reserves, storage and flexible capacity. Thermal or hydro assets used only during scarcity may face a missing-money problem. Rooftop and open-access migration can reduce DISCOM sales from high-paying consumers while common network costs remain. Curtailment and payment delay further raise financing cost. Reform should coordinate generation and transmission, procure flexibility and adequacy, permit compatible storage revenue stacking, use time-of-day tariffs and demand response, and recover network cost fairly. Renewable expansion strengthens finances when it reduces total social cost and payment risk; it weakens them when project tariffs are treated as the whole system cost.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 4 marks for renewable benefits, 6 for market-finance risks, 6 for system reforms and 4 for the reasoned conclusion (20 total).

## Practice 6 - 20 marks / maximum 250 words

**Question:** Design an integrated policy for India's energy security that also protects growth, equity and sustainability.

**Model solution:** Begin with efficient demand: appliance and building standards, industrial efficiency, public transport and demand response. Diversify domestic and imported oil, gas, coal and electricity sources, routes and contract tenures; maintain strategic and commercial stocks; strengthen ports, rail, pipelines, grids and storage. Expand renewables, hydro and nuclear according to system value, while reforming DISCOM tariffs, subsidy payment, procurement and governance. Develop critical-mineral partnerships, domestic processing, recycling, R&D and cyber standards. Use targeted lifeline support rather than universal price suppression. Sequence fossil-asset retirement with dependable alternatives and support workers, coal districts and affected states. Evaluate success through affordability, reliability, import concentration, fiscal exposure, emissions, access and recovery time. Security is diversified resilience, not costly autarky.

**Scoring rubric:** Award 4 marks for demand and supply security, 6 for networks/finance, 6 for equity/sustainability/resilience and 4 for the integrated verdict (20 total).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core proposition

Energy infrastructure succeeds when adequate energy is delivered reliably, affordably and sustainably at the point of use. A resource, connection, project or installed MW is an input, not the final outcome.

## 2. Essential formulae and units

```text
generation + imports + discharge
= demand + exports + charging + losses

capacity factor
= actual generation / possible full-rated generation

simplified AT&C loss
= 1 - billing efficiency x collection efficiency

social energy value
= service + reliability + investment + innovation benefits
- private + fiscal + environmental + distributional costs
```

- MW/GW: instantaneous power/capacity.
- MWh/GWh/BU: energy over time.
- Peak demand: highest short-interval requirement.
- PLF: thermal-plant utilisation measure, not efficiency.

## 3. Electricity chain and institutions

- Generation can support competition, subject to fuel, contract and network constraints.
- Transmission and distribution wires are natural-monopoly networks.
- System operation requires neutral coordination.
- Ministry of Power: Union policy.
- CEA: statutory technical advice, planning, standards, data and monitoring.
- CERC: inter-state/central-sector regulation within mandate.
- SERC: intra-state and retail regulation.
- APTEL: specified regulatory appeals.
- Grid-India/SLDC: national-regional/state system operation.
- CTU/STU: inter-state/intra-state network planning and access.
- DISCOM: last-mile supply, metering, billing, collection and service.
- BEE/EESL: efficiency architecture and aggregation models.
- PFC/REC Limited: finance/support; REC certificate is a different concept.

## 4. Law and status

- Electricity Act, 2003 supplies the durable legal architecture.
- Open access is non-discriminatory network use subject to rules and charges.
- Electricity is a Concurrent List subject.
- Never convert a Bill, proposal, target, tender or sanction into enacted or achieved status.
- Economic Survey 2025-26 discusses the Electricity (Amendment) Bill, 2025 as a proposal.
- The separately notified Electricity (Amendment) Rules, 2026 are not the 2025 Bill.

## 5. Markets, PPAs and adequacy

- Long-term PPA: revenue certainty and project finance, but lock-in risk.
- Short-term markets: adjustment, balancing and price discovery.
- Merit order uses variable cost but faces technical, congestion, reserve and contract constraints.
- Ancillary services compensate reliability capability.
- Missing money arises when energy revenue does not cover needed flexible capacity.
- Adequacy uses dependable stress-hour contribution, not nameplate MW.

## 6. Tariff and DISCOM finance

- Tariff covers fixed/capacity, fuel/energy, network, service, prudent loss, return and adjustments.
- Explicit subsidy is paid by the budget.
- Cross-subsidy is paid by another consumer class.
- Regulatory asset defers current recovery to future consumers.
- Debt conceals under-recovery but does not eliminate cost.
- AT&C loss and ACS-ARR gap diagnose different failures.
- UDAY was historical debt/operational restructuring.
- RDSS supports metering and distribution upgrades; assets alone do not reform governance.

## 7. Access and inclusion

- Access ladder: reach -> connection -> active meter -> availability -> quality -> affordability -> appliance/productive use -> sustained service.
- Clean cooking requires recurring fuel affordability.
- Productive use links electricity to income.
- Lifeline support can coexist with cost-reflective pricing.
- Rooftop, farm power and clean-cooking policies require distributional analysis.

## 8. Coal

- Thermal coal and coking coal are distinct.
- Resource is not reserve.
- Delivered cost includes mine, quality, washing, logistics, inventory and external costs.
- Coal Controller is distinct from commercial producers.
- Water, ash, pollution, safety and closure liabilities belong in appraisal.
- Just transition protects workers, districts, state revenue and reliability while reducing lock-in.

## 9. Oil and gas

- Upstream: exploration/production.
- Midstream: pipelines, terminals, storage, LNG.
- Downstream: refining, marketing and retail.
- PNGRB regulates specified midstream/downstream activities but excludes crude and natural-gas production.
- Crude-import dependence can coexist with refining strength and product exports.
- Pipeline viability requires throughput, access, tariff and anchor demand.
- Retail petroleum price reflects global price, exchange rate, refining/marketing, freight, margin and taxes/support.

## 10. Energy security

- Five dimensions: availability, accessibility, affordability, acceptability, resilience.
- Security is lower vulnerability, not zero trade.
- Diversify suppliers, routes, firms and contract tenures.
- Use strategic and commercial stocks for temporary disruption.
- Efficiency, grids, utility finance and cyber/climate resilience are security assets.
- Oil shock transmits through import bill, currency, inflation, fiscal and growth channels.

## 11. Renewable integration and storage

- LCOE is plant-level; system value includes energy, capacity, flexibility, network and externality value.
- Cannibalisation: common output lowers captured price.
- Curtailment: available output reduced by congestion, security or oversupply.
- RPO creates obligated demand; REC carries the renewable attribute.
- Storage shifts energy and supplies services; it is not primary generation.
- Duration, efficiency, cycles, degradation and charging cost determine storage economics.
- CEA planning estimates cited by the Survey: about 336 GWh by 2029-30 and 411 GWh by 2031-32; projections, not installed outcomes.

## 12. Efficiency and digital systems

- Efficiency reduces energy per service.
- Conservation can reduce service.
- Standards, UJALA, street lighting, PAT, building codes and demand response address different failures.
- Rebound can raise total use after unit efficiency improves.
- Smart meters improve data and control but need privacy, cyber and grievance safeguards.
- India Energy Stack is a Survey-described digital policy architecture; do not overstate deployment.

## 13. Finance and transition

- Bankability requires demand, land, resource, network, regulation, payment security and viable finance.
- Cost of capital can dominate tariffs for capital-intensive assets.
- Stranding arises from demand, technology, fuel, network, climate or regulation.
- Critical-mineral security spans mining, processing, technology, recycling and diversification.
- Carbon pricing changes merit order and incidence; recycle revenue and protect vulnerable users.
- Just transition manages concentrated worker, district and fiscal loss.

## 14. Ethanol

- Brazil: sugarcane route.
- United States: corn/starch route.
- India: cane plus grain routes.
- Benefits: crude substitution, forex, farmer/distillery demand.
- Costs: food/fuel, water, price support, lifecycle emissions and vehicle compatibility.
- Survey figures as of August 2025 must retain that date.

## 15. International and federal resilience

- ISA: India-France, COP21 Paris, 2015.
- GGI-OSOWOG: India-UK, COP26 Glasgow, 2021.
- Wider grids use geographic/time-zone diversity but need finance, standards, settlement and trust.
- Federal energy policy requires benefit-sharing and common data.
- Climate resilience covers heat, drought, flood, cyclone, coastal and correlated-renewable risks.

## 16. Answer-writing checklist

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: ENERGY**

- **E:** mix, demand and access.
- **N:** networks and storage.
- **E:** economics, tariff, subsidy and finance.
- **R:** regulation and reliability.
- **G:** green transition and geopolitics.
- **Y:** yield in affordability, jobs, productivity and emissions.

Always pair an achievement with its qualification:

- capacity with generation and deliverability;
- connection with service;
- cheap tariff with payer and system cost;
- domestic production with quality/logistics/environment;
- renewable addition with network/flexibility;
- blending with food/water/lifecycle effects;
- transition with worker/region/mineral effects.
