## 🖼️ Core mechanism

```text
Millions of savers
(small, liquid deposits)
          │
          ▼
        BANK
 ┌───────────────────────┐
 │ pools savings         │
 │ assesses borrowers    │
 │ diversifies risks     │
 │ transforms maturities │
 │ operates payments     │
 └───────────────────────┘
          │
          ▼
Borrowers and investors
(larger, longer-term loans)
```

Banks connect people who have surplus money with those who need financing.

## 1. Financial intermediation

Without banks:

```text
Saver must personally find borrower
             ↓
Check creditworthiness
             ↓
Negotiate amount and maturity
             ↓
Monitor repayment
             ↓
Bear concentrated default risk
```

A bank performs these functions at scale.

| Bank function | What it solves |
|---|---|
| Savings mobilisation | Collects scattered household savings |
| Borrower screening | Evaluates repayment capacity |
| Credit allocation | Directs funds towards borrowers |
| Monitoring | Tracks loan performance |
| Diversification | Spreads risk across many loans |
| Payments | Transfers money between economic agents |

> **Financial intermediation:** mobilisation of funds from savers and their allocation to borrowers through a financial institution.

---

## 2. Maturity transformation

Depositors generally prefer liquidity, while borrowers often require long-term finance.

```text
Depositor: “I may need my money soon.”
                    │
                    ▼
                   BANK
                    │
                    ▼
Borrower: “I need five years to repay.”
```

The bank transforms:

```text
Shorter-term, withdrawable liabilities
                    ↓
Longer-term, comparatively illiquid assets
```

### Example

A bank receives deposits from 10,000 customers and lends part of the pooled money as:

- housing loans;
- business loans;
- vehicle loans;
- infrastructure finance.

Each depositor can seek withdrawal, but all depositors normally do not withdraw simultaneously. The bank therefore maintains liquid assets while lending the relatively stable portion.

### Important distinction

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Contractual maturity | When money is legally repayable |
| Behavioural maturity | How long the money normally remains with the bank |

A savings deposit may be legally withdrawable immediately but behaviourally remain stable for years.

---

## 3. Size transformation

```text
10,000 deposits of ₹10,000
              ↓ pooled
       ₹10 crore available
              ↓
Several larger loans
```

The bank converts numerous small savings into finance suitable for larger borrowers.

It also performs the reverse: one large borrower’s financing requirement is effectively funded through many small deposits.

---

## 4. Risk transformation

A household lending its entire savings to one firm bears concentrated risk.

A bank instead constructs a diversified portfolio:

```text
Deposits
   ↓
┌──────────┬─────────┬──────────┬──────────┐
Housing    MSMEs     Agriculture Services
loans      loans     loans       loans
└──────────┴─────────┴──────────┴──────────┘
```

Banks reduce—not eliminate—risk through:

- borrower screening;
- collateral;
- diversification;
- provisioning;
- capital buffers;
- continuous monitoring.

> Banks transform and manage risk; they do not make lending risk-free.

---

## 5. The bank balance sheet

| **Assets: bank is owed money** | **Liabilities: bank owes money** |
|---|---|
| Loans | Customer deposits |
| Government securities | Market borrowings |
| Cash and reserves | Other liabilities |
| Other investments | — |

**Bank capital** is the owners’ residual stake:

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Capital
```

### Common language trap

- Your bank deposit is **your asset**.
- The same deposit is **the bank’s liability**.
- Your loan is **your liability**.
- The same loan is **the bank’s asset**.

---

## 6. How a bank earns

```text
Interest earned on loans and investments
                     │
                     ▼
       minus interest paid on funding
                     │
                     ▼
          Net interest income
```

Banks also earn fees, but their basic model depends heavily on the spread between:

- return on assets; and
- cost of funds.

The spread must cover:

- operating costs;
- defaults and provisions;
- regulatory expenses;
- taxes;
- profit.

---

## 7. Why banking is inherently fragile

Banks combine three features:

```text
LEVERAGE
Large assets supported by comparatively small capital

        +

RUNNABLE LIABILITIES
Depositors can demand repayment

        +

MATURITY TRANSFORMATION
Long-term loans funded partly by shorter-term deposits
```

This creates two different dangers.

| Danger | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Liquidity problem | Bank has valuable assets but cannot obtain cash quickly enough |
| Solvency problem | Value of assets is insufficient to repay liabilities |

### Example

Suppose a bank owns a sound twenty-year housing loan. The borrower is repaying normally, but the bank cannot instantly demand the remaining twenty years of payments.

If many depositors demand cash today, the bank can face a **liquidity crisis** despite having valuable assets.

If borrowers permanently default and loan values collapse, the bank may face a **solvency crisis**.

```text
Illiquid ≠ necessarily insolvent
Insolvent → assets are insufficient
```

---

## 8. Why regulation is necessary

A bank failure can affect more than its owners because banks operate:

- household savings;
- payment systems;
- business working capital;
- credit creation;
- connections with other financial institutions.

Therefore, regulation requires:

```text
Capital buffers
      +
Liquidity buffers
      +
Asset-quality supervision
      +
Deposit insurance
      +
Central-bank liquidity support
      +
Resolution mechanisms
```

The objective is not to ensure that no bank ever fails. It is to preserve critical functions and prevent disorderly contagion.

## UPSC traps

1. Banks do not merely transfer the exact deposit of one saver to one borrower.
2. Deposits are liabilities—not assets—of banks.
3. Loans are assets of banks.
4. Maturity transformation creates liquidity risk, not automatic insolvency.
5. Capital absorbs losses; it is not a cash reserve maintained for routine withdrawals.
6. Diversification reduces concentration risk but cannot eliminate systemic risk.
7. Credit growth exceeding deposit growth signals funding pressure, not necessarily bank failure.

## Revision notes

- Banks reduce information and transaction costs.
- They perform financial intermediation.
- They transform maturity, size and risk.
- Withdrawable deposits fund comparatively longer-term assets.
- Behavioural maturity can exceed contractual maturity.
- Deposits are bank liabilities; loans are bank assets.
- Bank capital absorbs losses.
- Liquidity means meeting cash obligations when due.
- Solvency means assets remain sufficient to cover liabilities.
- Leverage, runnable liabilities and maturity mismatch make banks fragile.
- Regulation protects depositors, payments and systemic stability.
- RBI’s June 2026 report found resilience alongside faster credit than deposit growth.

### What limits further lending?

```text
Available deposits and borrowings
            +
Capital relative to risk-weighted assets
            +
CRR, SLR and liquidity requirements
            +
Borrower and sector exposure limits
            +
Creditworthy loan demand
            +
Bank’s internal risk appetite
```

Capital is often the decisive prudential constraint:

```text
Capital adequacy ratio
= Regulatory capital ÷ Risk-weighted assets
```

A ₹100 housing loan may consume less regulatory capital than a ₹100 unsecured risky loan because their **risk weights differ**. Therefore, identical funding can support different quantities of lending.

Also, a bank can expand capacity by:

- attracting new deposits;
- retaining profits as capital;
- issuing shares or eligible capital instruments;
- borrowing from markets;
- receiving loan repayments;
- selling or securitising existing loans.

Thus, the public can **estimate lending headroom** from capital, risk-weighted assets and liquidity disclosures, but only the bank knows its exact real-time capacity and internal limits.

## 1. Three labels answer different questions

| Label | Question answered |
|---|---|
| **Scheduled** | Is it included in the Second Schedule to the RBI Act? |
| **Commercial** | What kind of banking institution and business does it conduct? |
| **Cooperative** | Who owns and governs it? |

These categories are **not mutually exclusive**.

For example, a bank can simultaneously be:

```text
Cooperative
     +
Scheduled
     +
Urban
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** Scheduled = regulatory status; commercial = banking character; cooperative = ownership structure.

---

## 2. Scheduled banks

A **scheduled bank** is included in the **Second Schedule of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934**.

Inclusion is based on statutory eligibility and RBI satisfaction regarding the bank’s affairs. It is not merely a list of government-owned banks.

### Consequences

Scheduled banks:

- maintain prescribed cash reserves with RBI;
- submit regulatory returns;
- may access eligible RBI liquidity and settlement facilities subject to applicable rules;
- operate under stronger and continuous RBI supervision.

### What scheduled status does not mean

```text
Scheduled ≠ government owned
Scheduled ≠ guaranteed never to fail
Scheduled ≠ necessarily a commercial bank
Scheduled ≠ automatically financially superior
```

A private bank, foreign bank or cooperative bank may possess scheduled status.

---

## 3. Commercial banks

A commercial bank performs ordinary banking functions such as:

```text
Accept deposits
      ↓
Provide payment services
      ↓
Extend loans and advances
      ↓
Invest surplus funds
      ↓
Earn interest and fee income
```

Major scheduled commercial-bank groups include:

- public-sector banks;
- private-sector banks;
- foreign banks;
- Regional Rural Banks;
- Small Finance Banks;
- Payments Banks.

These categories differ in ownership or permitted activities, which we will study separately.

### Why “commercial” can mislead

Commercial banking does not mean that the bank lends only to companies.

Commercial banks also finance:

- households;
- agriculture;
- housing;
- education;
- micro and small enterprises;
- governments through securities.

The term describes institutional character, not exclusively corporate customers.

---

## 4. Cooperative banks

Cooperative banks are organised on cooperative principles.

```text
Members
   ↓ own and participate in
Cooperative institution
   ↓ mobilises deposits
   ↓ provides credit
Members/local community
```

Their characteristic principle is generally:

```text
One member, one vote
```

This differs from an ordinary company, where voting power usually follows shareholding.

| Commercial company model | Cooperative model |
|---|---|
| Investor-owned | Member-owned |
| Voting linked largely to shares | Democratic member participation |
| Profit and shareholder value | Member service plus financial viability |
| Wider commercial orientation | Often local or community orientation |

Cooperative status does not eliminate the need for:

- profitability;
- capital;
- professional management;
- credit appraisal;
- regulatory compliance.

---

## 5. Urban and rural cooperative structure

### Urban Cooperative Banks

Urban Cooperative Banks generally serve:

- urban and semi-urban customers;
- small businesses;
- traders;
- salaried households;
- local communities.

An Urban Cooperative Bank can be either:

```text
Scheduled UCB
or
Non-scheduled UCB
```

Therefore, **not every cooperative bank is scheduled**.

### Rural cooperative credit structure

A simplified short-term rural structure is:

```text
State Cooperative Bank
          ↓
District Central Cooperative Bank
          ↓
Primary Agricultural Credit Society
          ↓
Farmers and village borrowers
```

### Crucial distinction

A **Primary Agricultural Credit Society (PACS)** is a grassroots cooperative credit society, but it is not automatically a licensed bank.

Consequently:

- it should not automatically be treated as an RBI-regulated commercial bank;
- deposits with a PACS should not automatically be assumed to receive DICGC bank-deposit insurance.

---

## 6. Why cooperative-bank regulation is complex

Cooperative banks combine two identities:

```text
COOPERATIVE ENTITY
incorporation, membership and cooperative governance
             +
BANK
deposits, lending, liquidity and depositor protection
```

This creates a layered regulatory structure.

| Area | Principal institutional role |
|---|---|
| Banking licence and prudential regulation | RBI |
| Capital, liquidity and banking supervision | RBI |
| Cooperative registration and member-related administration | State or Central cooperative registrar |
| Rural cooperative-bank supervision | RBI/NABARD architecture plus cooperative authorities |

The precise allocation differs across institution types and has evolved through legislation.

> **UPSC trap:** “Cooperative banks are regulated exclusively by state governments” is incorrect because banking activity attracts RBI and banking-law oversight.

---

## 7. Scheduled versus non-scheduled does not mean regulated versus unregulated

A non-scheduled bank is not outside all banking regulation.

```text
Scheduled status
        ↓
Specific RBI Act classification

Banking regulation
        ↓
Broader licensing, prudential and supervisory framework
```

Therefore:

- scheduled banks are regulated;
- non-scheduled banks are also regulated;
- scheduled status affects classification and access, not the existence of all oversight.

## 1. Public-sector banks

A **Public-Sector Bank (PSB)** is a commercial bank in which the government holds controlling ownership.

Current examples include:

- State Bank of India;
- Punjab National Bank;
- Bank of Baroda;
- Canara Bank;
- Union Bank of India.

### Basic model

```text
Government majority ownership
            +
RBI banking regulation
            +
Commercial lending
            +
Developmental and public-policy roles
```

Government ownership does not mean that PSBs are government departments. They remain commercial banking institutions with:

- boards and professional management;
- deposits and loans;
- profitability requirements;
- prudential regulation;
- listed shareholders in many cases.

### Why India uses PSBs

PSBs have historically supported:

- nationwide branch expansion;
- rural banking;
- priority-sector lending;
- government-payment delivery;
- infrastructure and industrial finance;
- crisis-time policy transmission.

### Trade-off

| Strength | Possible weakness |
|---|---|
| Large reach and public confidence | Government influence over credit decisions |
| Ability to support policy programmes | Weaker commercial discipline may arise |
| Crisis-response capacity | Recapitalisation can burden taxpayers |
| Rural and social-banking reach | Governance and accountability can become diffuse |

> **UPSC balance:** Public ownership can support inclusion but does not automatically ensure efficient lending.

---

## 2. Private-sector banks

A **Private-Sector Bank (PVB)** is controlled primarily by private shareholders rather than government.

Examples include:

- HDFC Bank;
- ICICI Bank;
- Axis Bank;
- Kotak Mahindra Bank.

They are still:

- licensed and regulated by RBI;
- subject to capital and liquidity requirements;
- covered by applicable deposit-insurance arrangements;
- required to comply with Priority Sector Lending norms.

```text
Private ownership
       ≠
Absence of public regulation
```

### Typical strengths and risks

| Strength | Associated risk |
|---|---|
| Technology and product innovation | Aggressive expansion |
| Faster commercial decisions | Retail or sectoral concentration |
| Strong cost discipline | Sales-practice and consumer-protection concerns |
| Market accountability | Short-term shareholder pressure |

A private bank is not necessarily safer or riskier than a PSB merely because of ownership. Examine:

- capital;
- asset quality;
- liquidity;
- governance;
- concentration;
- profitability.

---

## 3. Foreign banks in India

A **foreign bank** is incorporated outside India and conducts banking business in India through an approved presence.

Two principal operating forms are:

```text
FOREIGN BANK
     │
 ┌───┴─────────────────┐
 │                     │
Indian branches        Wholly owned Indian subsidiary
of overseas bank       of overseas parent
```

| Branch model | Subsidiary model |
|---|---|
| Part of the foreign legal entity | Separately incorporated Indian entity |
| Parent bank remains directly connected | Indian subsidiary has its own capital and board |
| Cross-border parent exposure is important | Greater local incorporation and ring-fencing |

Foreign banks contribute:

- international trade finance;
- foreign-exchange services;
- multinational corporate banking;
- global capital and expertise;
- competition in specialised financial services.

### Risk

Stress at the overseas parent can potentially affect its Indian operations, particularly through:

- funding;
- confidence;
- cross-border exposures;
- group-level decisions.

RBI therefore regulates their Indian operations even though ownership lies abroad.

---

## 4. Regional Rural Banks

A **Regional Rural Bank (RRB)** is a statutory bank created under the **Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976** to serve rural areas.

### Ownership structure

```text
Central Government       50%
State Government         15%
Sponsor bank             35%
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: 50–15–35**

An RRB is therefore neither:

- wholly owned by the Union government;
- a state cooperative bank;
- merely a rural branch of its sponsor bank.

It is a separate statutory banking institution with joint ownership.

### Sponsor bank’s role

The sponsor bank traditionally assists with:

- management and staff support;
- training;
- technology;
- operational guidance;
- banking expertise.

But the sponsor bank does not simply absorb the RRB into itself.

### Primary purpose

RRBs focus on:

- small and marginal farmers;
- agricultural labourers;
- artisans;
- micro and small enterprises;
- rural households and businesses.

They combine:

```text
Local familiarity of cooperative credit
                    +
Professional structure of commercial banking
```

### Regulation and supervision

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| RBI | Banking regulation |
| NABARD | Rural-credit supervision and developmental support |
| Sponsor bank | Operational and managerial support |
| Governments | Ownership and policy role |

**NABARD:** National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development.

---

## 5. One State, One RRB reform

The fourth phase of RRB consolidation took effect on **1 May 2025**.

✅ Multiple RRBs operating within the same state were amalgamated under the **“One State, One RRB”** principle.

✅ The number of RRBs was reduced from **43 to 28**, covering 26 states and two Union Territories.

### Logic

```text
Several RRBs in one state
           ↓ amalgamation
One larger state-level RRB
           ↓
Common technology and management
           ↓
Lower duplication + greater scale
           ↓
Potentially stronger rural-credit delivery
```

### Potential benefits and risks

| Potential benefit | Possible limitation |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale | Greater organisational distance from local borrowers |
| Unified technology | Integration costs |
| Stronger capital and governance | Concentration into fewer institutions |
| Reduced duplication | Scale does not automatically improve credit appraisal |
| Common branding and processes | Local institutional knowledge may weaken |

## 6. Local Area Banks

A **Local Area Bank (LAB)** is a private-sector bank authorised to operate within a restricted geographical area.

Its purpose is to:

- mobilise local savings;
- provide credit locally;
- serve rural and semi-urban borrowers;
- address regional credit gaps.

```text
Local deposits
      ↓
Local Area Bank
      ↓
Local agriculture, businesses and households
```

### LAB versus RRB

| Feature | Regional Rural Bank | Local Area Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Legal character | Statutory specialised bank | Private-sector bank |
| Ownership | Union, state and sponsor bank | Private promoters/shareholders |
| Main mandate | Rural development and inclusion | Localised commercial banking |
| Area | Notified regional/state territory | Restricted contiguous area |
| Sponsor bank | Yes | No equivalent statutory sponsor |
| NABARD connection | Important | Not structured like an RRB |

```text
                    FINANCIAL INCLUSION
                            │
              ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
```

Both are **differentiated banks**: RBI gives them specialised licences rather than the complete business freedom of a universal commercial bank.

## 1. Why differentiated banks were introduced

Traditional banks did not always serve:

- small and marginal farmers;
- micro and small enterprises;
- migrant workers;
- low-income households;
- geographically remote customers;
- people needing inexpensive remittance services.

Instead of giving every institution identical powers, RBI created specialised models:

```text
Credit inclusion problem
        → Small Finance Banks

Payment and deposit-access problem
        → Payments Banks
```

# Small Finance Banks

## 2. What an SFB can do

A **Small Finance Bank (SFB)** can:

- accept deposits;
- provide payment services;
- extend loans from its balance sheet;
- issue eligible banking products;
- serve individuals and businesses.

Examples include AU Small Finance Bank, Ujjivan Small Finance Bank and Equitas Small Finance Bank.

### Basic balance sheet

```text
Deposits and capital
        ↓
Small Finance Bank
        ↓
Loans to households, farmers,
micro-enterprises and small businesses
```

An SFB is a genuine lending bank—not merely a microfinance institution with a banking label.

---

## 3. Inclusion obligations of SFBs

### Priority Sector Lending

SFBs must direct at least **75% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC)** towards sectors eligible for Priority Sector Lending.

Compare:

| Bank category | Overall PSL target |
|---|---:|
| Most domestic commercial banks | 40% |
| Small Finance Banks | **75%** |

**PSL:** Priority Sector Lending
**ANBC:** Adjusted Net Bank Credit

### Small-loan orientation

At least **50% of an SFB’s loan portfolio** should consist of loans up to **₹25 lakh**.

This prevents the institution from obtaining an inclusion-focused licence and then concentrating primarily on large corporate lending.

> The ₹25 lakh threshold classifies the size of the loan, not the borrower’s income or total wealth.

---

## 4. SFB business model

An SFB earns primarily through:

```text
Interest on loans
      −
Interest paid on deposits and borrowings
      =
Net interest income
```

It may also earn fees by distributing:

- insurance;
- mutual funds;
- pension products;
- other regulated financial services.

### Main risks

| Strength | Risk |
|---|---|
| Access to underserved borrowers | Higher borrower vulnerability |
| Granular small loans | High operating costs per loan |
| Deposit mobilisation | Competition for stable deposits |
| Local knowledge | Geographic or segment concentration |
| Lending income | Credit and default risk |

---

# Payments Banks

## 5. What a Payments Bank can do

A **Payments Bank (PB)** primarily provides:

- small deposits;
- payments;
- remittances;
- mobile and digital banking;
- debit or ATM cards;
- government-benefit transfers;
- distribution of permitted third-party products.

Examples include India Post Payments Bank, Airtel Payments Bank and Fino Payments Bank.

## 6. What it cannot do

A Payments Bank **cannot lend from its own balance sheet**.

It also cannot issue conventional credit cards as a lending product.

```text
Customer deposits
        ✕
Cannot be converted into the bank’s own loan portfolio
```

This is the single most important exam distinction.

---

## 7. Deposit limit

A Payments Bank may hold a maximum end-of-day balance of:

```text
₹2 lakh per individual customer
```

RBI raised the limit from ₹1 lakh to ₹2 lakh on **8 April 2021**.

This is:

- a balance limit, not an annual transaction limit;
- applied per individual customer at the end of the day;
- not the general rule for Small Finance Banks.

---

## 8. What happens to Payments Bank deposits?

Because a Payments Bank cannot lend them like an ordinary bank, RBI requires a highly liquid and conservative deployment structure.

Broadly:

```text
At least 75%
Demand-deposit balances
        ↓
Government securities and Treasury Bills
with maturity up to one year

Up to 25%
        ↓
Current and time/fixed deposits
with other scheduled commercial banks
for operational and liquidity purposes
```

It must also maintain the applicable Cash Reserve Ratio with RBI.

Thus, the model prioritises:

```text
Safety + liquidity + payments
rather than
Loan creation + credit risk
```

---

## 9. How does a Payments Bank earn?

Since it lacks its own lending spread, income may come from:

- transaction and remittance fees;
- commissions;
- interest on permitted investments;
- distribution of insurance or pension products;
- business-correspondent arrangements;
- partnerships with other regulated lenders.

This creates a viability challenge:

```text
Small customer balances
        +
Low transaction charges
        +
No balance-sheet lending
        ↓
Need very large transaction volume
and low operating costs
```

---

## 10. Can a Payments Bank app show a loan offer?

Yes—but the legal lender must be distinguished from the platform displaying the offer.

```text
Customer uses Payments Bank app
             ↓
Loan offer displayed
             ↓
Loan actually provided by
partner bank or NBFC
```

The Payments Bank may act as:

- referral platform;
- business correspondent;
- service facilitator.

It does **not** follow that the Payments Bank itself extended the loan.

> 🔑 Always identify the entity whose balance sheet carries the loan.

---

## 11. Master comparison

| Feature | Small Finance Bank | Payments Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Accept deposits | Yes | Yes, subject to special limit |
| Deposit cap per individual | No special ₹2 lakh category cap | ₹2 lakh end-of-day |
| Provide payments | Yes | Yes |
| Lend from own balance sheet | **Yes** | **No** |
| Main purpose | Deposit and credit inclusion | Payment, remittance and deposit inclusion |
| PSL target | 75% of ANBC | No ordinary lending portfolio |
| Small-loan requirement | 50% of portfolio up to ₹25 lakh | Not applicable |
| Credit risk | Significant | Limited direct lending risk |
| Main earning source | Interest spread plus fees | Fees, commissions and safe investments |
| Credit cards | Permissible subject to rules | Cannot issue as own lending product |
| Debit cards | Yes | Yes |

## ✅ Current-affairs anchor: RBI amendment, June 2026

✅ On **15 June 2026**, RBI issued amendments governing Small Finance Banks’ third-party financial services, effective **1 January 2027**.

The amendment distinguished:

| Arrangement | SFB’s role |
|---|---|
| Agency business | Facilitates sale and servicing for a fee, without assuming product risk |
| Referral service | Directs the customer to a regulated provider but does not sell or service the product |

✅ SFBs may deal only with appropriately regulated financial products under these arrangements.

⚠️ **Inference:** A bank application displaying insurance, mutual funds or another institution’s loan does not establish that the product belongs on that bank’s own balance sheet.

```text
Product displayed by bank
             ≠
Product financially underwritten by bank
```

This is the same analytical rule needed when interpreting loan offers in Payments Bank applications.

## 🖼️ Similar lending, different institutional powers

```text

                            │
              ┌─────────────┴──────────────┐
              │                            │
             BANK                         NBFC
              │                            │

              │                            │
```

An NBFC may provide the same vehicle or housing loan as a bank, but that does not make it a bank.

## 1. What is an NBFC?

A **Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC)** is a company whose principal business consists of financial activities such as:

- loans and advances;
- acquisition of securities;
- leasing;
- hire purchase;
- housing or infrastructure finance;
- other specified financial services.

### RBI’s 50–50 test

Financial activity is ordinarily treated as the company’s principal business when:

```text
Financial assets > 50% of total assets
                 AND
Income from financial assets > 50% of gross income
```

Both conditions must be satisfied.

> A manufacturing company making occasional loans does not become an NBFC merely because it conducts some financial activity.

---

## 2. Why NBFCs exist when banks already lend

NBFCs often specialise in areas where conventional bank processes may be less effective:

- vehicle finance;
- consumer-durable loans;
- housing finance;
- gold loans;
- microfinance;
- infrastructure finance;
- loans to smaller or informally documented businesses;
- last-mile rural and semi-urban credit.

```text
Specialised borrower knowledge
            +
Flexible distribution
            +
Alternative risk assessment
            ↓
Credit for segments underserved by banks
```

Their advantage may come from specialisation—not weaker regulation or risk-free lending.

---

## 3. Deposit powers

### Banks

Banks can accept:

- demand deposits;
- savings deposits;
- current-account deposits;
- fixed and recurring deposits.

A demand deposit can be withdrawn without advance notice according to its terms.

### NBFCs

NBFCs **cannot accept demand deposits**.

Only specifically authorised **deposit-taking NBFCs** may accept permitted public term deposits. Many NBFCs cannot accept public deposits at all.

```text
BANK
Savings/current account ✓

NBFC
Savings/current account ✕

Authorised deposit-taking NBFC
Permitted term deposit only ✓
```

> **UPSC trap:** RBI registration does not automatically authorise an NBFC to accept deposits.

---

## 4. Payment-system access

Banks form part of the payment and settlement system.

They can provide bank-account-based access to:

- cheques;
- NEFT;
- RTGS;
- UPI;
- debit cards;
- ordinary account transfers.

An NBFC does not become a payment-system bank merely because it operates a mobile application.

```text
NBFC application
       ↓
May connect to customer’s bank account
       ↓
Payment ultimately settled through banking/payment infrastructure
```

NBFCs cannot issue cheques **drawn on themselves** as banks do.

### Important nuance

An NBFC may facilitate payments through partner banks or authorised payment entities. The app interface and the institution legally holding the account are not necessarily the same.

---

## 5. Funding structures

### Typical bank funding

```text
Customer deposits
       +
Market borrowings
       +
Shareholder capital
       ↓
Loans and investments
```

Deposits generally provide banks with a comparatively stable and often lower-cost funding base.

### Typical NBFC funding

```text
Equity capital
      +
Loans from banks
      +
Debentures and bonds
      +
Commercial paper
      +
Securitisation
      +
Limited public deposits, where authorised
      ↓
Loans and investments
```

**Commercial paper** is a short-term unsecured market instrument issued by eligible entities.

**Debenture** is a debt instrument through which investors lend to the issuing company.

---

## 6. Why NBFC funding can be fragile

Some NBFCs finance long-term loans through relatively short-term market borrowings.

```text
Short-term commercial paper
             ↓
NBFC
             ↓
Long-term housing/infrastructure loan
```

If investors refuse to renew the commercial paper, the NBFC may face a liquidity problem even when borrowers continue repaying normally.

Banks also perform maturity transformation, but they have:

- demand-deposit franchises;
- central-bank liquidity architecture;
- more extensive liquidity requirements;
- payment-system importance;
- depositor safety nets.

NBFCs therefore possess a different funding-risk profile.

---

## 7. Do banks and NBFCs create money identically?

### Bank lending

When a bank grants and credits a loan:

```text
Bank asset: loan increases
Bank liability: customer deposit increases
```

The bank creates a new deposit, subject to capital, liquidity and other constraints.

### NBFC lending

An NBFC generally lends funds already obtained through:

- capital;
- bank borrowing;
- bonds;
- commercial paper;
- collections from previous loans.

It does not create a demand deposit liability upon itself.

```text
NBFC’s existing bank balance
             ↓ transferred
Borrower’s bank account
```

The deposit moves through the banking system; the NBFC does not independently create bank money.

---

## 8. Deposit insurance

### Bank deposits

Eligible bank deposits are insured by the **Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC)** up to:

```text
₹5 lakh per depositor per bank
including principal and interest
```

### NBFC deposits

Deposits with a deposit-taking NBFC are **not covered by DICGC**.

```text
Bank fixed deposit
      → eligible for DICGC protection

NBFC fixed deposit
      → no DICGC protection
```

RBI registration does not mean that RBI guarantees repayment.

> 🔑 **Regulated does not mean insured or guaranteed.**

---

## 9. Regulatory distinction

| Dimension | Bank | NBFC |
|---|---|---|
| Principal framework | Banking Regulation Act and RBI Act | RBI Act and activity-specific laws/directions |
| Demand deposits | Permitted | Prohibited |
| Payment system | Integral participant | Not a bank-style participant |
| Cheques on itself | Permitted | Not permitted |
| DICGC deposit insurance | Eligible bank deposits covered | Not available |
| Routine bank liquidity architecture | Available subject to rules | No identical routine access |
| CRR and SLR | Applicable as banking requirements | Different liquidity framework |
| Lending | Yes | Yes, according to category |
| Regulation | RBI and applicable authorities | RBI and applicable authorities |

NBFCs are not simply “unregulated shadow banks.” RBI applies a separate framework reflecting their:

- activities;
- size;
- funding;
- customer interface;
- systemic importance.

---

## 10. Interconnection between banks and NBFCs

Banks frequently finance NBFCs:

```text
Household deposits
        ↓
       Bank
        ↓ bank loan
       NBFC
        ↓ specialised loan
Final borrower
```

This expands last-mile credit but creates interconnectedness.

If a large NBFC fails:

```text
NBFC default
     ↓
Losses for lending banks and bond investors
     ↓
Funding-market fear
     ↓
Other NBFCs face refinancing difficulty
     ↓
Credit supply contracts
```

Thus, NBFC stress can become systemic even though NBFCs are not banks.

## 11. Practical depositor test

Before placing money with a financial institution, ask:

```text
1. Is it legally a bank or an NBFC?
2. If NBFC, is it authorised to accept public deposits?
3. Is the product a deposit, bond or market investment?
4. Does DICGC insurance apply?
5. What is the maturity and premature-withdrawal rule?
6. What credit and liquidity risks remain?
```

A higher NBFC deposit interest rate usually compensates for some combination of:

- greater credit risk;
- weaker liquidity;
- absence of deposit insurance;
- longer lock-in.

It is not free additional return.

- NBFCs undertake financial activities as their principal business.
- RBI applies the 50–50 asset-and-income test.
- Banks and NBFCs can both extend credit.
- Only banks may accept demand deposits.
- Only authorised deposit-taking NBFCs may accept permitted term deposits.
- Banks are integral to payment and settlement systems.
- NBFC deposits do not receive DICGC insurance.
- NBFCs depend significantly on banks, bonds and market borrowings.
- Short-term funding of long-term assets creates refinancing risk.
- Banks create deposits when lending; NBFCs do not create deposits upon themselves.
- RBI registration is not a repayment guarantee.
- Bank–NBFC interconnectedness can transmit systemic stress.

## 🖼️ Two-dimensional classification

```text
                         NBFC
                           │
          ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
          │                                 │
 TYPE OF BUSINESS                    REGULATORY RISK
 "What does it do?"                  "How closely regulated?"
          │                                 │
 ICC · HFC · MFI · IFC              Base Layer
 Factor · AA · P2P                  Middle Layer
 CIC · IDF · SPD                    Upper Layer
                                     Top Layer
```

A second classification introduced clearly in 2026 asks:

```text
Does it use public funds?
Does it interact with customers?
              │
       ┌──────┴──────┐
       │             │
    TYPE I         TYPE II
No to both       Any other NBFC
```

These classifications must not be mixed:

> **Activity category**, **Type I/II status** and **regulatory layer** answer different questions.

# Part I: Classification by activity

## 1. Investment and Credit Company

An **Investment and Credit Company (NBFC-ICC)** undertakes activities such as:

- providing loans;
- asset finance;
- acquiring securities;
- financing vehicles, equipment or consumption.

This is the broad residual lending-and-investment category.

Examples of business models may include:

```text
Vehicle finance
Consumer finance
Gold loans
Business loans
Equipment finance
```

---

## 2. Housing Finance Company

A **Housing Finance Company (HFC)** specialises principally in housing finance.

RBI’s current definition broadly requires:

- at least 60% of adjusted total assets in housing finance;
- at least 50% of adjusted total assets in housing finance for individuals.

An HFC is an NBFC, not a bank.

```text
Bank housing loan ─┐
                   ├── Same borrower purpose
HFC housing loan ──┘   Different institution
```

---

## 3. Microfinance Institution

An **NBFC-Microfinance Institution (NBFC-MFI)** specialises in collateral-free microfinance loans to low-income households.

Under current RBI guidance, a microfinance loan is:

- collateral-free;
- given to a household with annual household income up to ₹3 lakh;
- classified by the borrower and loan conditions, not simply by the loan’s stated purpose.

At least 75% of an NBFC-MFI’s assets must generally be deployed in microfinance loans.

### Key trap

Microfinance is not necessarily:

- a women-only loan;
- a group loan;
- an agriculture-only loan.

The current definition focuses primarily on household income and collateral-free lending.

---

## 4. Infrastructure Finance Company

An **Infrastructure Finance Company (NBFC-IFC)** deploys at least 75% of its total assets towards infrastructure lending.

Infrastructure projects often involve:

- large financing needs;
- long repayment periods;
- construction and policy risks;
- asset-liability mismatch.

This explains the specialised prudential treatment.

---

## 5. Infrastructure Debt Fund

An **Infrastructure Debt Fund–NBFC (IDF-NBFC)** primarily refinances qualifying operational infrastructure projects.

```text
Construction-stage project
        ↓ begins operation
Operational infrastructure asset
        ↓ refinanced
IDF-NBFC provides longer-term funding
```

Refinancing operational projects can release the original lender’s funds for new projects.

---

## 6. Core Investment Company

A **Core Investment Company (CIC)** primarily holds shares, debt or loans within its corporate group.

```text
Corporate group
      │
      ▼
Core Investment Company
 ┌────┼───────────┐
 ▼    ▼           ▼
Subsidiary A  Subsidiary B  Subsidiary C
```

Its risk comes from:

- concentration within one group;
- leverage;
- guarantees;
- interconnectedness;
- contagion between group entities.

A CIC is not a mutual fund for ordinary retail investors.

---

## 7. Account Aggregator

An **NBFC-Account Aggregator (NBFC-AA)** facilitates consent-based transfer of financial information.

```text
Customer gives explicit consent
             ↓
Account Aggregator retrieves information
             ↓
Information shared with authorised user
```

The Account Aggregator:

- does not lend;
- does not sell the customer’s data as its own asset;
- should not alter the financial information;
- acts as a consent-based information conduit.

> 🔑 **AA moves data, not money.**

---

## 8. Peer-to-Peer lending platform

An **NBFC-P2P** operates an online platform connecting lenders and borrowers.

```text
Individual lender
       ↓
 P2P platform
       ↓
Individual borrower
```

The platform does not ordinarily lend from its own balance sheet.

It facilitates:

- matching;
- documentation;
- risk information;
- payment arrangements through permitted mechanisms.

> 🔑 **P2P connects lenders and borrowers; it does not become the lender merely by operating the platform.**

---

## 9. Other specialised categories

| Category | Core function |
|---|---|
| NBFC-Factor | Purchases or finances receivables |
| Mortgage Guarantee Company | Guarantees qualifying mortgage repayment |
| Standalone Primary Dealer | Underwrites and trades government securities |
| Non-Operative Financial Holding Company | Holds regulated financial subsidiaries |

UPSC usually tests the functional boundary rather than obscure registration details.

# Part II: Type I versus Type II

## 10. Public funds and customer interface

The 2026 amendment distinguishes NBFCs according to whether they:

1. access **public funds**; or
2. have a **customer interface**.

### Public funds

Public funds are broader than public deposits. They can include funding obtained through:

- bank finance;
- debentures;
- commercial paper;
- inter-corporate deposits;
- other external liabilities;
- indirect funding through group entities.

```text
Public deposits ⊂ Public funds
```

Every public deposit is a public fund, but every public fund is not a public deposit.

### Customer interface

A customer interface exists when the NBFC deals directly with customers in conducting its financial business.

---

## 11. Type I NBFC

A **Type I NBFC**:

- does not access public funds;
- has no customer interface;
- does not intend to acquire either in the future.

These are generally low-risk or internally funded entities without direct retail interaction.

### 2026 exemption

From **1 July 2026**, an entity satisfying these conditions and having assets below **₹1,000 crore** may qualify as an **Unregistered Type I NBFC**, subject to RBI’s prescribed conditions.

It must:

- disclose its status;
- maintain board oversight;
- obtain registration if it later accesses public funds or customers;
- register if the applicable group asset threshold reaches ₹1,000 crore.

The exemption is limited—it does not place the entity entirely beyond RBI’s statutory powers.

---

## 12. Type II NBFC

A **Type II NBFC** is an NBFC other than Type I.

Broadly, it:

```text
Accesses public funds
          OR
Has customer interface
          OR
Intends to have either
```

A consumer-finance, housing-finance or microfinance company dealing with borrowers would ordinarily fall on the Type II side.

> **Type II does not mean “second-rate.”** It indicates greater public or customer exposure.

---

# Part III: Scale-Based Regulation

## 13. Why RBI uses layers

NBFCs range from tiny specialised firms to institutions whose failure could disturb the financial system.

Applying identical rules to all would create two problems:

```text
Same light rules for everyone
      → large NBFCs under-regulated

Same heavy rules for everyone
      → small NBFCs overburdened
```

RBI therefore follows proportional regulation:

```text
Higher size + complexity + leverage
+ interconnectedness + public exposure
                    ↓
Greater systemic risk
                    ↓
Stronger regulation
```

---

## 14. Four regulatory layers

### Base Layer

Generally contains smaller or lower-risk NBFCs and specialised platform entities.

Examples include many:

- smaller non-deposit-taking NBFCs;
- Account Aggregators;
- P2P platforms;
- entities without public funds or customer interface.

Regulation remains real but comparatively lighter.

### Middle Layer

Includes entities requiring stronger prudential oversight, such as:

- all deposit-taking NBFCs;
- larger non-deposit-taking NBFCs;
- various systemically important or specialised categories.

Requirements become stronger regarding:

- capital;
- governance;
- asset classification;
- concentration;
- disclosure;
- risk management.

### Upper Layer

Contains NBFCs specifically identified by RBI as having high systemic significance.

Selection considers factors such as:

- size;
- interconnectedness;
- complexity;
- leverage;
- nature of activity;
- supervisory assessment.

Upper-Layer regulation moves closer to bank-like prudential intensity, but the entity remains an NBFC.

### Top Layer

The Top Layer is intended normally to remain empty.

RBI may place an NBFC there if it believes that the entity’s systemic risk has increased substantially and Upper-Layer rules are insufficient.

```text
BASE → ordinary oversight
  ↓
MIDDLE → enhanced oversight
  ↓
UPPER → stringent systemic oversight
  ↓
TOP → exceptional supervisory concern
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: BMUT — Base, Middle, Upper, Top.**

---

## 15. Type and layer are independent axes

Consider a small lending NBFC dealing directly with customers:

```text
Customer interface
       ↓
Type II

Small asset size and lower systemic risk
       ↓
May remain in Base Layer
```

Therefore:

```text
Type II ≠ automatically Middle or Upper Layer
```

Similarly, activity categories such as HFC or ICC describe the business conducted, while the layer determines regulatory intensity.

---

## ✅ Current-affairs anchor: RBI amendment, April 2026

✅ RBI issued the **NBFC Registration, Exemptions and Scale-Based Regulation Amendment Directions** on **29 April 2026**, effective **1 July 2026**.

✅ It formally clarified:

- Type I NBFC;
- Type II NBFC;
- Unregistered Type I NBFC;
- the ₹1,000 crore threshold and group aggregation rule;
- the obligation to register when public funds or customer interaction begin.

⚠️ **Inference:** RBI is applying regulation according to the risk transmitted to outsiders:

```text
No external funding + no customers
              ↓
Lower immediate public-risk channel

Public funding or customer dealings
              ↓
Greater consumer, liquidity and systemic concerns
```

This is proportional regulation, not deregulation of the entire NBFC sector.

1. Public funds are broader than public deposits.
2. Type I means no public funds and no customer interface.
3. Type II does not mean financially weak.
4. Type I/II and BMUT layers are different classifications.
5. An Account Aggregator transfers information, not funds.
6. A P2P platform is not ordinarily the balance-sheet lender.
7. An HFC is an NBFC, not a specialised bank.
8. The Upper Layer contains systemically important NBFCs selected by RBI.
9. The Top Layer is designed for exceptional risk and normally remains empty.
10. Lighter regulation does not mean absence of all RBI authority.

## 🖼️ Why PSL exists

```text
Commercial credit allocation
             ↓
Funds flow towards safer,
larger and more profitable borrowers
             ↓
Creditworthy but underserved sectors
may receive insufficient finance
             ↓
PRIORITY SECTOR LENDING
             ↓
Mandatory share of bank credit
directed towards specified sectors
```

Priority Sector Lending (**PSL**) adds a developmental mandate to commercial banking.

## 1. PSL is classification, not automatic subsidy

A PSL loan is one that satisfies RBI’s:

- eligible-sector definition;
- borrower or activity criteria;
- applicable loan limits;
- reporting requirements.

```text
Priority-sector loan
       ≠ automatically cheap loan
       ≠ government grant
       ≠ loan without appraisal
       ≠ compulsory approval
```

Banks must still assess repayment capacity and credit risk.

---

## 2. Eight priority sectors

| # | Category |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Agriculture |
| 2 | Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises |
| 3 | Export credit |
| 4 | Education |
| 5 | Housing |
| 6 | Social infrastructure |
| 7 | Renewable energy |
| 8 | Other eligible categories |

The classification contains detailed borrower, purpose and loan-size conditions. For example, every housing loan is not automatically PSL merely because it finances a house.

---

## 3. Target denominator

PSL targets are calculated against the higher of:

```text
Adjusted Net Bank Credit
          OR
Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance-Sheet Exposures
```

**ANBC:** Adjusted Net Bank Credit
**CEOBSE:** Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance-Sheet Exposures

### Example

Suppose:

```text
ANBC   = ₹1,000 crore
CEOBSE = ₹1,100 crore
```

The applicable denominator is ₹1,100 crore.

For a 40% target:

```text
Required PSL = 40% × ₹1,100 crore
             = ₹440 crore
```

> 🔑 **UPSC trap:** The target is not necessarily calculated against total deposits.

---

## 4. Current overall targets

| Bank category | Overall PSL target |
|---|---:|
| Domestic commercial banks, excluding RRBs and SFBs | **40%** |
| Foreign banks with 20 or more branches | **40%** |
| Foreign banks with fewer than 20 branches | **40%**, with a distinct permitted composition |
| Regional Rural Banks | **75%** |
| Small Finance Banks | **60%** |
| Urban Cooperative Banks | **60%** |

### Small Finance Bank correction

From FY 2025–26:

```text
SFB overall PSL target = 60%

40% → allocated according to prescribed sub-targets
20% → any eligible PSL sector where the SFB has an advantage
```

The previous 75% target was reduced by RBI in June 2025.

---

## 5. Important sub-targets

| Sub-target | Domestic commercial banks | RRBs | SFBs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Agriculture | 18% | 18% | 18% |
| Non-corporate farmers within agriculture | 14% | 14% | 14% |
| Small and marginal farmers within agriculture | 10% | 10% | 10% |
| Micro-enterprises | 7.5% | 7.5% | 7.5% |
| Weaker sections | 12% | **15%** | 12% |

These percentages apply to ANBC or CEOBSE, whichever is higher.

### Nested agricultural targets

```text
TOTAL CREDIT BASE
       ↓
18% Agriculture
       ↓ within it
14% Non-corporate farmers
       ↓ within it
10% Small and marginal farmers
```

Do not add 18 + 14 + 10. The latter two are nested sub-targets.

---

## 6. What happens when a bank misses its target?

A PSL shortfall does not normally produce criminal punishment or automatic licence cancellation.

RBI may require the bank to place funds in:

- Rural Infrastructure Development Fund;
- other eligible funds with NABARD;
- funds administered by SIDBI, NHB or MUDRA, as applicable.

**RIDF:** Rural Infrastructure Development Fund
**NABARD:** National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development
**SIDBI:** Small Industries Development Bank of India
**NHB:** National Housing Bank
**MUDRA:** Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency

```text
PSL shortfall
      ↓
Mandatory allocation to specified fund
      ↓
Funds support rural/inclusion-related development
```

The return on such placements may be less attractive than ordinary commercial lending, creating an incentive to meet targets directly.

---

## 7. Priority Sector Lending Certificates

A **Priority Sector Lending Certificate (PSLC)** allows a bank exceeding a target to sell its excess PSL achievement to another bank with a shortfall.

```text
Bank A
Exceeds agriculture target
       ↓ sells PSLC
Bank B
Has agriculture shortfall
```

Crucially:

```text
Loan asset does not move
Borrower does not change
Credit risk does not move
Only PSL achievement is transferred
```

Bank A continues to own and service the loan. Bank B purchases regulatory target achievement for a fee.

### Why PSLCs help

- specialise according to comparative advantage;
- improve target compliance;
- avoid unnecessary transfer of loan assets;
- direct incentives towards underserved sectors.

### Limitation

PSLC trading may enable formal compliance without building genuine local lending relationships.

# Rural-credit coordination

## 8. Why branches alone were insufficient

Opening branches does not guarantee that credit matches local needs.

```text
Bank branches exist
       ↓
But each bank plans independently
       ↓
Duplication in some areas
and neglect in others
       ↓
Need district-level coordination
```

This led to the **Lead Bank Scheme**.

---

## 9. Lead Bank Scheme

Introduced in **1969**, the Lead Bank Scheme assigns a bank responsibility for coordinating banking development in a district.

The Lead Bank does not monopolise lending in that district.

Its role is to:

- assess local credit needs;
- coordinate participating banks;
- connect banks with district administration;
- promote financial inclusion;
- monitor district credit plans;
- identify gaps in banking services.

```text
One bank coordinates
        ≠
Only one bank may lend
```

### Lead District Manager

The **Lead District Manager (LDM)** represents the Lead Bank’s coordinating machinery at district level.

---

## 10. Credit-planning chain

```text
NABARD Potential Linked Credit Plan
                ↓
Assessment of district credit potential
                ↓
Branch and block-level credit plans
                ↓
District Credit Plan / Annual Credit Plan
                ↓
Implementation by participating banks
                ↓
Monitoring and review
```

NABARD’s **Potential Linked Credit Plan (PLP)** estimates the district’s credit potential, particularly for agriculture and rural development.

It does not itself sanction every loan.

---

## 11. Coordination committees

```text
STATE LEVEL
State Level Bankers’ Committee
              │
              ▼
DISTRICT LEVEL
District Consultative Committee
District Level Review Committee
              │
              ▼
BLOCK LEVEL
Block Level Bankers’ Committee
              │
              ▼
BANK BRANCHES AND BORROWERS
```

| Body | Level and role |
|---|---|
| SLBC | State-wide banking coordination |
| DCC | District coordination between banks and government |
| DLRC | Reviews implementation and performance |
| BLBC | Coordinates banking at block level |

These bodies coordinate; they do not replace RBI regulation or individual bank credit appraisal.

---

## 12. Service Area Approach

The **Service Area Approach (SAA)** was introduced to assign rural and semi-urban branches responsibility for defined areas.

Original logic:

```text
Specified branch
       ↓
Defined group of villages
       ↓
Local survey and credit planning
       ↓
Reduced geographical neglect
```

Restrictive branch allocation was subsequently relaxed. The approach remains relevant to planning and implementation of certain government-sponsored and financial-inclusion programmes.

> It should not be understood as a permanent prohibition preventing borrowers from approaching other banks.

---

## 13. Regional-credit weighting

RBI also addresses geographical inequality in PSL flows.

For FY 2024–25 to FY 2026–27:

| District credit condition | Weight on incremental PSL |
|---|---:|
| Comparatively low PSL flow: below ₹9,000 per capita | **125%** |
| Ordinary district | 100% |
| Comparatively high PSL flow: above ₹42,000 per capita | **90%** |

### Example

A bank extends ₹100 of incremental PSL in a low-credit district:

```text
Actual credit = ₹100
Weighted PSL achievement = ₹125
```

This does not mean the borrower receives ₹125. The additional ₹25 exists only for regulatory target computation.

7. PSLC transfers achievement—not the loan or credit risk.
8. The Lead Bank coordinates; it does not monopolise district lending.
9. NABARD’s PLP assesses potential; it does not sanction every loan.
10. A 125% weight changes regulatory recognition, not the amount received by the borrower.

## Revision notes

- PSL directs banking credit towards underserved but important sectors.
- There are eight principal PSL categories.
- Domestic commercial banks generally have a 40% target.
- RRBs have a 75% target.
- SFBs have a 60% target from FY 2025–26.
- Agriculture carries an 18% target.
- Small and marginal farmers carry a 10% sub-target.
- RRB weaker-sections target is 15%; domestic banks and SFBs have 12%.
- Shortfalls may be allocated to RIDF and other eligible funds.
- PSLCs transfer target achievement without transferring loan risk.
- The Lead Bank Scheme coordinates district credit.
- The Service Area Approach supports local credit planning.
- NABARD prepares Potential Linked Credit Plans.
- Low-credit districts receive higher incremental PSL weighting.

# Deposit insurance

## 1. What is DICGC?

The **Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC)** is a wholly owned subsidiary of RBI operating under the DICGC Act, 1961.

Its principal depositor-protection function is:

```text
Insured bank becomes unable
to repay eligible deposits
              ↓
DICGC pays eligible amount
              ↓
Up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank
```

The insured ceiling was increased from ₹1 lakh to **₹5 lakh in 2020**.

---

## 2. What deposits are covered?

Eligible deposits broadly include:

- savings deposits;
- current-account deposits;
- fixed deposits;
- recurring deposits;
- principal and accrued interest.

Coverage applies to eligible deposits with insured banks, including:

- commercial banks;
- Small Finance Banks;
- Payments Banks;
- Regional Rural Banks;
- Local Area Banks;
- covered cooperative banks.

It does not cover:

- NBFC deposits;
- mutual funds;
- shares and bonds;
- insurance policies;
- pension-market investments;
- cryptocurrency;
- losses caused by changes in market value.

---

## 3. The coverage formula

```text
Maximum insurance
= ₹5 lakh
per depositor
per bank
in the same capacity and same right
```

Each part matters.

### Same bank: all branches aggregated

Suppose a person has:

| Deposit in SBI | Balance |
|---|---:|
| Savings account, Delhi branch | ₹2 lakh |
| Fixed deposit, Mumbai branch | ₹4 lakh |
| Total | ₹6 lakh |

Maximum insurance:

```text
₹5 lakh—not ₹5 lakh per branch
```

Different branches do not create separate insurance because SBI remains the same bank.

### Different banks: separate coverage

| Bank | Deposit | Insurance |
|---|---:|---:|
| SBI | ₹4 lakh | ₹4 lakh |
| HDFC Bank | ₹4 lakh | ₹4 lakh |
| Total protection | — | ₹8 lakh |

The “per bank” limit applies separately.

---

## 4. Different capacity and right

Accounts held in legally different capacities may receive separate treatment.

Examples include deposits held as:

- an individual;
- trustee;
- guardian of a minor;
- partner of a firm;
- joint account holder with a distinct combination/order.

But merely opening multiple accounts in the same personal capacity does not multiply insurance.

### Sole proprietorship trap

Deposits held personally and in the name of one’s sole proprietorship are generally aggregated because the proprietor and proprietorship are not separate legal persons.

---

## 5. Who pays the premium?

The insured bank pays the deposit-insurance premium to DICGC.

```text
Depositor
   ✕ does not purchase an individual policy

Insured bank
   ✓ pays premium on assessable deposits
```

DICGC protection arises automatically for eligible deposits in an insured bank.

---

## 6. What deposit insurance does not protect

Deposit insurance protects the depositor within the statutory ceiling. It does not protect:

- bank shareholders;
- managers;
- bond investors;
- every creditor;
- deposits above the ceiling;
- an NBFC merely because RBI regulates it.

```text
Protect depositors
       ≠
Guarantee the whole institution
```

A bank may still be merged, reconstructed or liquidated.

---

# Prompt Corrective Action

## 7. What is PCA?

**Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)** is RBI’s framework for intervening when specified indicators reveal significant financial weakness.

```text
Weakness detected early
          ↓
Corrective restrictions imposed
          ↓
Bank conserves capital and repairs balance sheet
          ↓
Reduced probability of disorderly failure
```

PCA is preventive and rehabilitative—not a declaration that the bank has already failed.

---

## 8. Commercial-bank PCA indicators

For scheduled commercial banks covered by the framework, the principal trigger areas are:

| Area | Indicator |
|---|---|
| Capital | CRAR and Common Equity Tier 1 |
| Asset quality | Net NPA ratio |
| Leverage | Tier 1 leverage ratio |

**CRAR:** Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio
**CET1:** Common Equity Tier 1 capital
**NNPA:** Net Non-Performing Assets

The severity is divided into escalating risk thresholds.

```text
Threshold 1 → initial serious weakness
Threshold 2 → deeper weakness
Threshold 3 → severe weakness
```

---

## 9. Urban Cooperative Bank PCA

RBI’s July 2026 supervisory directions identify three key areas for applicable Urban Cooperative Banks:

| Area | Indicator |
|---|---|
| Capital | CRAR |
| Asset quality | Net NPA ratio |
| Profitability | Consecutive losses |

For Tier 2–4 UCBs, the framework may be invoked when relevant thresholds are breached.

Tier 1 UCBs remain under enhanced monitoring rather than the same PCA chapter.

> **Exam distinction:** Commercial-bank and UCB PCA frameworks are related but not identical.

---

## 10. Possible PCA actions

Depending on severity, RBI may require or impose:

- raising additional capital;
- restrictions on dividends;
- restrictions on branch expansion;
- limits on high-risk lending;
- reduced growth of risk-weighted assets;
- restrictions on capital expenditure;
- governance changes;
- business-model review;
- recovery plans;
- intensified supervision;
- merger or reconstruction exploration.

### What PCA does not automatically mean

```text
PCA ≠ licence cancellation
PCA ≠ depositor payout
PCA ≠ liquidation
PCA ≠ all withdrawals frozen
```

PCA seeks to restore financial health before these more severe outcomes become necessary.

---

## 11. Exiting PCA

A bank is not released merely because one quarterly ratio temporarily improves.

RBI seeks evidence of:

- sustained improvement;
- compliance across relevant indicators;
- viable capital and business plans;
- supervisory confidence;
- corrected governance and risk-management weaknesses.

Thus, exit is based on sustainability—not cosmetic improvement.

---

# Resolution

## 12. What happens if recovery fails?

Possible resolution tools include:

```text
Weak bank
   │
   ├── Reconstruction
   ├── Amalgamation with another bank
   ├── Moratorium or operational restrictions
   ├── Transfer of assets and liabilities
   └── Liquidation as the final route
```

The objective is to preserve:

- insured deposits;
- payment continuity;
- viable assets;
- public confidence;
- broader financial stability.

Resolution should not automatically protect shareholders or failed management.

---

## 13. PCA versus moratorium versus deposit insurance

| Mechanism | Stage | Main effect |
|---|---|---|
| PCA | Bank remains operational but weak | Corrective restrictions |
| Moratorium/all-inclusive restrictions | Acute stress | Limits specified operations or withdrawals |
| Resolution | Bank cannot recover normally | Restructuring, transfer, merger or liquidation |
| DICGC payout | Depositor claims become payable under law | Payment within ₹5 lakh ceiling |

These are sequentially related but not automatically triggered one after another.

---

## 14. Why insurance can create moral hazard

Deposit insurance increases confidence, but it can weaken depositor monitoring.

```text
Depositor expects protection
          ↓
Pays less attention to bank risk
          ↓
Risky banks may attract deposits
without paying full market penalty
```

Possible safeguards include:

- limited insurance ceiling;
- strong supervision;
- capital requirements;
- PCA;
- resolution imposing losses on owners;
- risk-based insurance premiums.

---

## ✅ Current-affairs anchor: risk-based premiums

From **1 April 2026**, DICGC shifted eligible insured banks from a uniform premium towards a **risk-based premium framework**.

```text
Safer risk profile
      ↓
Potentially lower insurance premium

Riskier profile
      ↓
Higher applicable premium
```

✅ The reform changes what banks pay DICGC.

❌ It does not change the depositor’s ₹5 lakh insurance ceiling.

⚠️ **Inference:** Risk-based pricing reduces the unfairness of charging well-managed and risky banks exactly the same insurance rate.

However, premium differentiation cannot replace:

- RBI supervision;
- capital and liquidity rules;
- timely PCA;
- credible resolution.

---

## UPSC traps

1. DICGC covers eligible deposits, not every financial investment.
2. Coverage is ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank—not per account or branch.
3. Principal and interest are combined within the ceiling.
4. Deposits in different banks receive separate coverage.
5. Multiple personal accounts in one bank are aggregated.
6. NBFC deposits are not covered.
7. The insured bank—not the individual depositor—pays DICGC premium.
8. PCA is corrective supervision, not liquidation.
9. PCA does not automatically freeze withdrawals.
10. Deposit insurance protects depositors, not shareholders or management.
11. Risk-based premiums do not increase the ₹5 lakh ceiling.
12. Commercial-bank and UCB PCA indicators are not perfectly identical.

## Revision notes

- DICGC is an RBI-owned deposit insurer.
- Insurance is capped at ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.
- Coverage includes principal and interest.
- Branches and accounts in the same capacity are aggregated.
- Deposits in different banks receive separate limits.
- NBFC deposits are uninsured by DICGC.
- Banks pay the insurance premium.
- PCA enables early supervisory intervention.
- Commercial PCA monitors capital, asset quality and leverage.
- UCB PCA monitors capital, asset quality and profitability.
- PCA actions intensify with the severity of weakness.
- Resolution includes reconstruction, merger and liquidation.
- Insurance can create moral hazard.
- Risk-based premiums began from April 2026.

# Reserve Bank of India

## 1. RBI’s principal regulatory perimeter

RBI regulates or supervises:

- commercial banks;
- banking functions of cooperative banks;
- specified NBFCs;
- Small Finance Banks;
- Payments Banks;
- Regional Rural Banks;
- payment and settlement systems;
- foreign-exchange management under the applicable legal framework.

It also performs broader central-bank functions:

- monetary policy;
- currency issuance;
- liquidity management;
- lender-of-last-resort support;
- financial-stability monitoring;
- management of foreign-exchange reserves;
- government banking and debt-management functions.

### Boundary

RBI does not ordinarily regulate:

- mutual-fund investment portfolios;
- stock exchanges;
- ordinary insurance contracts;
- the National Pension System’s pension intermediaries.

Those belong primarily to other regulators.

# Securities and Exchange Board of India

## 2. SEBI’s principal perimeter

The **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)** regulates the securities market.

Its regulatory sphere includes:

- stock exchanges;
- listed-company disclosures;
- stockbrokers;
- mutual funds and asset-management companies;
- merchant bankers;
- portfolio managers;
- Alternative Investment Funds;
- securities-market intermediaries;
- insider trading and market manipulation;
- public issues of securities.

```text
Shares · bonds · mutual funds
stock exchanges · securities intermediaries
                     ↓
                    SEBI
```

### What SEBI does not do

SEBI does not regulate ordinary:

- savings accounts;
- bank fixed deposits;
- housing loans;
- insurance policies merely because they contain investment features.

# Insurance regulator

## 3. IRDAI’s principal perimeter

The **Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI)** regulates:

- life insurers;
- general insurers;
- health insurers;
- reinsurance;
- insurance intermediaries;
- insurance-product conduct;
- insurer solvency;
- policyholder protection.

**Reinsurance** means insurance purchased by an insurer to transfer part of its own risk.

```text
Policyholder
      ↓ premium
Insurance company
      ↓ transfers part of risk
Reinsurer
```

### Boundary

A bank may sell an insurance policy, but the insurance product does not become a bank deposit.

```text
Bank = distributor
Insurer = risk underwriter
IRDAI = product/insurer regulator
RBI = bank regulator
```

Always identify who legally promises the insurance benefit.

# Pension regulator

## 4. PFRDA’s principal perimeter

The **Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA)** regulates the National Pension System and applicable pension-sector intermediaries.

It regulates participants such as:

- pension funds;
- points of presence;
- central recordkeeping agencies;
- trustees and custodians under the NPS architecture.

**NPS:** National Pension System

The **Atal Pension Yojana** also operates within the PFRDA-administered pension architecture.

### Important boundary

The **Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO)** is not regulated by PFRDA in the same manner as NPS.

```text
NPS/APY → PFRDA

EPF/EPS → EPFO and Ministry of Labour architecture
```

# International Financial Services Centres Authority

## 5. Why IFSCA is different

The **International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA)** is the unified regulator for financial activities conducted within an International Financial Services Centre.

India’s principal IFSC operates at **GIFT City, Gujarat**.

Within the IFSC, IFSCA combines functions that would otherwise fall across:

- RBI;
- SEBI;
- IRDAI;
- PFRDA.

```text
Domestic financial system
RBI + SEBI + IRDAI + PFRDA

GIFT IFSC
Unified regulator → IFSCA
```

IFSCA does not replace these regulators throughout India.

# Why regulatory overlap occurs

## 6. Entity regulation versus product regulation

Suppose an RBI-regulated bank sells three products:

```text
Bank fixed deposit → RBI-regulated banking product

Mutual fund unit → SEBI-regulated investment product

Insurance policy → IRDAI-regulated insurance product
```

The building, application or salesperson may be the same, but the legal product and risk bearer differ.

### Example

HDFC Bank, HDFC Mutual Fund and HDFC Life may share part of a brand identity, but they are separate regulated businesses.

```text
Bank deposit failure risk
        ≠
Mutual-fund market risk
        ≠
Insurance underwriting risk
```

---

## 7. Listed financial companies

A listed NBFC may simultaneously face:

```text
RBI
→ prudential regulation of NBFC activity

SEBI
→ securities-market and listing regulation

Companies Act/MCA
→ corporate-law requirements
```

This is not necessarily duplication. Each regulator addresses a different risk.

# Financial conglomerates

## 8. Why one institution can affect many sectors

A financial group may contain:

```text
Parent financial group
        │
 ┌──────┼─────────┬───────────┐
Bank   NBFC      Insurer      Mutual fund
 │      │           │             │
RBI    RBI        IRDAI          SEBI
```

Risks can move across the group through:

- loans and guarantees;
- shared ownership;
- common technology;
- brand contagion;
- related-party transactions;
- common customers;
- funding dependence.

### Brand contagion

If one group entity fails, customers may withdraw from healthy group entities because they perceive the entire brand as unsafe.

Thus, regulators need both:

```text
Solo supervision
Each legal entity examined separately

        +

Consolidated supervision
Risks across the entire group examined together
```

# Regulatory gaps and arbitrage

## 9. Regulatory arbitrage

**Regulatory arbitrage** occurs when economically similar activities receive materially different regulatory treatment, encouraging business to migrate towards the lighter framework.

```text
Same economic risk
      +
Different regulatory burden
      ↓
Activity shifts to less regulated entity
```

Example:

If bank lending faces stringent capital rules but an economically identical loan can be placed in a lightly regulated group entity, risk may migrate without disappearing.

### Response

- activity-based regulation;
- consolidated supervision;
- information sharing;
- harmonised consumer protection;
- comparable capital and disclosure rules where risks are comparable.

However, complete uniformity is undesirable because banks, insurers, pension funds and mutual funds perform genuinely different functions.

## 10. What is FSDC?

The **Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC)** is an apex coordination forum chaired by the **Union Finance Minister**.

It includes representatives from:

- Ministry of Finance;
- RBI;
- SEBI;
- IRDAI;
- PFRDA;
- IFSCA;
- other relevant authorities.

Its concerns include:

- financial stability;
- inter-regulatory coordination;
- financial-sector development;
- financial inclusion and literacy;
- macroprudential supervision;
- cross-sector risks.

### Critical distinction

```text
FSDC coordinates regulators
       ≠
FSDC directly regulates every institution
```

It does not replace RBI, SEBI, IRDAI or PFRDA.

## 🖼️ Contagion chain

```text
Large NBFC funds long-term loans
with short-term wholesale borrowing
                 │
                 ▼
One repayment/default creates doubt
                 │
                 ▼
Investors refuse to renew funding
                 │
                 ▼
NBFC sells assets or stops lending
                 │
       ┌─────────┼───────────┐
       ▼         ▼           ▼
Banks incur   Debt funds   Other NBFCs face
losses        face losses  funding withdrawal
       └─────────┼───────────┘
                 ▼
        Credit-market contraction
                 ▼
     Real economy investment slows
```

A problem becomes **systemic** when it impairs the functioning of the broader financial system rather than remaining confined to one institution.

---

## 1. Institution-specific versus systemic risk

| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Idiosyncratic risk | Loss concentrated in one institution |
| Systemic risk | Shock disrupts many institutions, markets or financial functions |
| Systematic market risk | Economy-wide risk that diversification cannot eliminate |

A small lender’s failure may remain local. A large, interconnected NBFC can transmit stress through:

- bank loans;
- bonds and commercial paper;
- mutual-fund investments;
- guarantees;
- common borrowers;
- confidence effects.

---

## 2. Asset–liability mismatch

An NBFC balance sheet may contain:

| Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|
| Housing loans lasting 15–20 years | Commercial paper maturing in months |
| Infrastructure loans with delayed cash flows | Short-term bank loans |
| Real-estate finance | Redeemable debentures |
| Vehicle and business loans | Market borrowings |

```text
Cash required soon
        >
Cash naturally received soon
        ↓
Negative liquidity mismatch
```

**Asset-Liability Management (ALM)** examines whether expected cash inflows can meet expected outflows across time buckets.

---

## 3. Rollover risk

Suppose an NBFC issues six-month commercial paper to finance a ten-year loan.

After six months, it normally:

```text
Issues new commercial paper
          ↓
Uses proceeds to repay old paper
```

This is **rolling over** the liability.

If investors refuse to buy the new paper:

```text
Old liability becomes due
        +
Long-term borrower has not yet repaid
        ↓
Liquidity crisis
```

The loan may still be valuable. The immediate problem is that it cannot be converted into cash without delay or loss.

---

## 4. Wholesale versus retail funding

| Retail deposit funding | Wholesale funding |
|---|---|
| Many small depositors | Fewer large institutions |
| Often behaviourally stable | Highly price-sensitive |
| Bank deposits may be insured | Market instruments are uninsured |
| Diversified funding base | Concentrated funding sources |
| Withdrawal risk exists | Rollover can stop abruptly |

Wholesale lenders include:

- banks;
- mutual funds;
- insurance companies;
- pension funds;
- corporations;
- institutional bond investors.

Wholesale funding is not inherently bad, but dependence on short maturities creates fragility.

# IL&FS crisis

## 5. What was IL&FS?

**Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services (IL&FS)** was a large infrastructure-finance group with numerous subsidiaries.

Its assets included exposure to long-gestation projects such as:

- roads;
- power;
- transportation;
- other infrastructure.

Infrastructure projects commonly generate cash only after construction and operation begin.

---

## 6. What happened in 2018?

✅ IL&FS group entities defaulted on financial obligations in **2018**.

The underlying vulnerability combined:

```text
Long-term and delayed infrastructure cash flows
                    +
Shorter-term market borrowing
                    +
High leverage and complex group structure
                    +
Project delays and governance weaknesses
                    ↓
Inability to refinance obligations
```

The government replaced the board under the Companies Act framework because of the broader systemic implications.

### Important qualification

It is incomplete to call IL&FS merely a temporary liquidity problem.

Persistent project weakness, leverage, opaque group exposures and governance failures raised questions about underlying solvency as well.

```text
Liquidity shock
may expose
deeper solvency and governance problems
```

---

## 7. How IL&FS stress spread

### Mutual-fund channel

```text
Debt mutual fund owns IL&FS paper
              ↓
Default or downgrade
              ↓
Security value falls
              ↓
Mutual-fund NAV falls
              ↓
Investors redeem
              ↓
Fund sells other assets
```

**NAV:** Net Asset Value

This can create fire sales even of unrelated securities.

### NBFC confidence channel

Investors could not immediately distinguish strong NBFCs from weak ones.

```text
One large NBFC defaults
        ↓
Investors distrust the category
        ↓
Refuse to refinance other NBFCs
        ↓
Sector-wide funding pressure
```

This is **information contagion**.

### Bank channel

Banks that lend to the troubled NBFC may face:

- delayed repayment;
- provisions;
- capital losses;
- reduced ability or willingness to lend elsewhere.

# DHFL crisis

## 8. What was DHFL?

**Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited (DHFL)** was a major Housing Finance Company.

It raised funding through:

- bank borrowing;
- debentures;
- commercial paper;
- other market instruments.

It used these funds for housing and real-estate-related lending.

---

## 9. What went wrong?

DHFL’s stress became visible in **2019**.

The episode involved a combination of:

- wholesale-funding dependence;
- maturity mismatch;
- real-estate and concentrated exposures;
- weakening market confidence;
- serious governance and alleged fund-diversion issues.

```text
Funding confidence falls
         ↓
New borrowing becomes difficult
         ↓
Existing obligations continue maturing
         ↓
Defaults emerge
         ↓
Solvency and governance problems intensify
```

DHFL subsequently entered a formal insolvency-resolution process and its resolution involved acquisition through the approved process.

### Key lesson

A regulated NBFC can still fail.

```text
RBI regulation
      ≠
Guarantee against failure
```

Regulation aims to reduce risk and contain damage—not eliminate commercial loss.

# Systemic amplification mechanisms

## 10. Direct contagion

Direct contractual exposure creates losses:

```text
NBFC defaults
      ↓
Bank that lent to it suffers loss
      ↓
Bank capital declines
```

## 11. Indirect contagion

Institutions with no direct exposure may still suffer because of:

- common asset holdings;
- falling market prices;
- investor panic;
- funding withdrawal;
- margin calls;
- rating downgrades;
- common borrowers losing credit.

### Fire-sale spiral

```text
Institution needs cash
       ↓
Sells assets quickly
       ↓
Prices fall
       ↓
Other holders record losses
       ↓
They also sell
       ↓
Prices fall further
```

---

## 12. Liquidity–solvency feedback loop

```text
Initial funding shortage
          ↓
Assets sold below normal value
          ↓
Accounting and economic losses
          ↓
Capital declines
          ↓
Lenders become more fearful
          ↓
Funding shortage worsens
```

Thus:

> A liquidity problem can become a solvency problem if assets must be sold at distressed prices.

The reverse also operates: suspected insolvency causes lenders to withdraw liquidity.

# Regulatory lessons

## 13. Stronger ALM requirements

NBFCs must monitor:

- cumulative maturity gaps;
- expected inflows and outflows;
- funding concentration;
- unencumbered liquid assets;
- stress scenarios;
- contingency funding plans.

## 14. Liquidity buffers

Applicable large and deposit-taking NBFCs maintain prescribed liquidity buffers, including requirements based on **High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA)**.

These assets should be:

- readily saleable;
- relatively safe;
- available during market stress;
- not already pledged elsewhere.

## 15. Scale-based regulation

The largest and most interconnected NBFCs enter the Upper Layer and face stronger:

- capital;
- governance;
- disclosure;
- concentration;
- risk-management requirements.

This directly reflects the lesson that:

```text
Same legal label
      ≠
Same systemic importance
```

## 16. Governance and consolidated supervision

Regulators examine:

- related-party transactions;
- group guarantees;
- subsidiary exposures;
- board risk oversight;
- audit quality;
- movement of funds within groups.

A healthy-looking parent can conceal weakness in subsidiaries, while subsidiary defaults can damage the parent.

## 17. Funding diversification

A safer funding structure combines:

```text
Long-term bonds
+ bank finance
+ securitisation
+ equity capital
+ matched-maturity borrowing
```

Diversification does not eliminate risk, but it reduces dependence on one market remaining open.

## 18. Qualified evaluation

NBFC interconnectedness has two sides:

| Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|
| Banks fund specialised lenders | Stress transmits back to banks |
| Mutual funds deepen bond financing | Redemptions can create funding freezes |
| NBFCs reach underserved borrowers | Rapid withdrawal contracts last-mile credit |
| Securitisation releases capital | Risk may become difficult to trace |
| Financial groups create economies of scope | Intra-group contagion becomes possible |

The solution is not to eliminate NBFCs or all interconnection. It is to ensure that interconnection remains:

- transparent;
- adequately capitalised;
- maturity-matched;
- diversified;
- stress-tested;
- resolvable.

## 1. Must every NBFC register with RBI?

**Generally yes, but there are exemptions.**

```text
Company satisfies the 50–50 principal-business test
                  ↓
Normally requires RBI registration
and prescribed Net Owned Funds
```

Exceptions include:

- certain entities already regulated under another statutory framework;
- **Unregistered Type I NBFCs** from 1 July 2026, if they have:
  - no public funds;
  - no customer interface;
  - assets below ₹1,000 crore, including the applicable group test.

If such a Type I entity later obtains public funding, serves customers or crosses the threshold, it must seek RBI registration.

Also, a manufacturing company providing an occasional employee or group loan does not automatically become an NBFC; financial activity must be its principal business.

## 2. Are NBFC deposits insured?

**No. NBFC deposits are not insured by DICGC.**

```text
Eligible bank deposit
→ DICGC protection up to ₹5 lakh

Deposit-taking NBFC deposit
→ No DICGC protection
```

This remains true even when:

- RBI has registered the NBFC;
- RBI has authorised it to accept term deposits;
- the NBFC possesses a high credit rating.

> **RBI-regulated does not mean RBI-guaranteed or DICGC-insured.**

# Bank governance

## 1. Board versus management

| Board of directors | Senior management |
|---|---|
| Approves strategy | Executes strategy |
| Sets risk appetite | Operates within risk limits |
| Oversees major exposures | Conducts lending and operations |
| Appoints and supervises leadership | Manages employees and systems |
| Demands accurate information | Supplies information and reports |
| Holds management accountable | Remains accountable to board |

### Risk appetite

A bank’s **risk appetite** specifies how much and what kind of risk it is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

```text
Business objective
       +
Permissible risk
       +
Capital and liquidity capacity
       =
Board-approved risk appetite
```

It should contain limits on:

- borrower concentration;
- sectoral exposure;
- unsecured lending;
- market risk;
- liquidity mismatch;
- related-party exposure;
- operational and cyber risk.

---

## 2. Three lines of defence

```text
FIRST LINE
Business units
Own and manage risks
       │
       ▼
SECOND LINE
Risk management and compliance
Challenge, monitor and set controls
       │
       ▼
THIRD LINE
Internal audit
Independent assurance to the board
```

### First line

Loan officers and business units must:

- assess borrowers;
- follow underwriting standards;
- monitor repayments;
- report emerging stress.

### Second line

Risk and compliance functions must:

- set limits;
- independently challenge business decisions;
- monitor regulatory compliance;
- aggregate institution-wide risks.

### Third line

Internal audit examines whether:

- controls actually operate;
- reports are reliable;
- management follows approved policies;
- exceptions are concealed or repeatedly overridden.

> Internal audit must be independent of the activities it audits.

---

## 3. Credit-governance chain

```text
Loan application
       ↓
Independent appraisal
       ↓
Sanction by authorised authority
       ↓
Documentation and disbursement
       ↓
Continuous monitoring
       ↓
Early-warning identification
       ↓
NPA recognition and provisioning
       ↓
Recovery, restructuring or resolution
```

Weakness at any stage can create future losses.

### Common governance failures

- lending based on influence rather than viability;
- excessive exposure to one borrower or sector;
- related-party lending;
- evergreening;
- delayed recognition of stress;
- inflated collateral valuation;
- weak audit;
- incentive structures rewarding loan growth without loan quality.

**Evergreening** means granting fresh finance primarily to prevent an existing stressed loan from appearing overdue rather than because the new loan is independently viable.

---

## 4. Ownership-specific governance pressures

### Public-sector banks

Possible pressures include:

- multiple public-policy objectives;
- government appointment influence;
- weaker individual accountability;
- fear of commercially legitimate decisions later being questioned;
- periodic dependence on taxpayer recapitalisation.

But public ownership can also support:

- nationwide inclusion;
- crisis lending;
- infrastructure finance;
- public-payment delivery.

### Private-sector banks

Possible pressures include:

- aggressive growth targets;
- executive dominance;
- sales incentives;
- related-party concerns;
- prioritisation of short-term shareholder returns.

Therefore:

```text
Public ownership has governance risks
Private ownership has governance risks
Foreign ownership has governance risks
```

No ownership structure substitutes for effective supervision and accountability.

# Recapitalisation

## 5. What recapitalisation does

Suppose:

```text
Bank capital = ₹90
Risk-weighted assets = ₹1,000

CRAR = 90 ÷ 1,000 = 9%
```

Government or investors inject ₹30:

```text
New capital = ₹120

CRAR = 120 ÷ 1,000 = 12%
```

Recapitalisation can:

- absorb recognised losses;
- improve capital adequacy;
- restore lending capacity;
- increase confidence;
- facilitate balance-sheet repair.

### What it cannot do alone

```text
New capital
      +
Same weak appraisal
      +
Same incentives and governance
      =
Future bad loans
```

Recapitalisation repairs the stock of capital. Governance reform changes the future flow of decisions.

# Bank consolidation

## 6. Consolidation is not recapitalisation

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recapitalisation | Fresh capital enters the bank |
| Merger/amalgamation | Two or more banks combine |
| Privatisation | Government transfers controlling ownership |
| Resolution merger | Weak bank transferred to a stronger institution |
| Organic expansion | Bank grows without combining with another bank |

A merger may occur with or without fresh capital.

## 8. Why merge banks?

Potential advantages include:

```text
Larger capital base
       +
Greater geographic diversification
       +
Common technology
       +
Reduced duplication
       +
Capacity to finance larger projects
       ↓
Potentially stronger institution
```

### Economies of scale

Fixed costs such as:

- cybersecurity;
- core banking software;
- compliance;
- analytics;
- treasury operations;

can be distributed across a larger business.

### Diversification

A geographically or sectorally broader bank may be less dependent on one region or borrower group.

---

## 9. Why mergers can fail to deliver

### Integration risk

Combining banks requires integration of:

- core banking systems;
- customer databases;
- branches;
- employees;
- promotion and salary structures;
- risk models;
- lending cultures;
- internal controls.

```text
Legal merger completed
        ≠
Operational integration completed
```

### Hidden asset-quality problems

A larger balance sheet may simply combine:

```text
Good assets + bad assets
```

Size does not remove non-performing loans.

### Too-big-to-fail problem

A larger bank may become so important that authorities cannot permit disorderly failure.

This can create:

- moral hazard;
- greater fiscal exposure;
- concentration;
- reduced market discipline.

### Competition

Fewer large banks may:

- reduce customer choice;
- weaken competitive pressure;
- increase concentration;
- create uniform lending behaviour.

### Local knowledge

Centralisation can weaken relationships with:

- small enterprises;
- rural borrowers;
- local industries;
- regional communities.

| Question | Test |
|---|---|
| Financial | Does the combined bank have adequate capital and asset quality? |
| Operational | Can technology and processes be integrated? |
| Governance | Who is accountable for risks and decisions? |

A merger should be evaluated against outcomes—not the number of banks eliminated.

# Regulatory trade-offs

## 11. Safety versus credit growth

```text
Higher capital and liquidity requirements
               ↓
Greater resilience
               but
Potentially higher lending cost
and slower balance-sheet growth
```

Weak requirements encourage excessive leverage; excessively rigid requirements may restrict productive credit.

---

## 12. Inclusion versus loan quality

Directed lending and inclusion can expand access, but poorly designed targets may cause:

- mechanical lending;
- weak appraisal;
- political interference;
- repeated restructuring;
- concentration in vulnerable sectors.

The correct objective is:

```text
More access
      +
Suitable products
      +
Sound underwriting
      +
Responsible recovery
```

---

## 13. Competition versus stability

| More competition | Greater concentration |
|---|---|
| Better prices and innovation | Greater scale and efficiency |
| More consumer choice | Easier supervision of fewer institutions |
| Risk of aggressive lending | Too-big-to-fail risk |
| Fragile new entrants possible | Reduced competitive pressure |

Neither maximum fragmentation nor maximum concentration is automatically optimal.

---

## 14. Innovation versus consumer protection

Digital lending can reduce:

- transaction costs;
- physical distance;
- processing time;
- documentation friction.

But it can increase:

- mis-selling;
- data misuse;
- opaque pricing;
- algorithmic discrimination;
- cyber risk;
- aggressive recovery.

Governance must treat technology risk as a board issue, not merely an information-technology issue.

---

## 15. Uniformity versus proportionality

```text
Identical rules for all institutions
→ ignores differences in size and activity

Very different rules
→ encourages regulatory arbitrage
```

Scale-based regulation attempts to impose:

> Similar regulation for similar risks, with stronger rules as systemic importance increases.

```text
Capital prevents losses from destroying a bank
Governance prevents avoidable losses from being created
Supervision detects weaknesses

```

Consolidation can provide scale and diversification, but sustainable banking strength requires:

- independent boards;
- professional management;
- accurate recognition of stress;
- sound underwriting;
- cyber resilience;
- effective competition;
- credible accountability.

# 4. Depositor protection and supervisory correction

## DICGC

```text
Coverage = maximum ₹5 lakh
           per depositor
           per bank
           in the same capacity and right
```

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Several branches of one bank | Aggregated |
| Savings and FD in one bank | Aggregated |
| Deposits in two different banks | Separately covered |
| Joint and individual capacity | May be treated separately |
| Deposit-taking NBFC | Not covered |
| Insurance premium | Paid by bank |

Risk-based DICGC premiums introduced from 1 April 2026 changed bank pricing—not the ₹5-lakh depositor ceiling.

## PCA

PCA is:

- intensified corrective supervision;
- triggered by specified financial weaknesses;
- intended to restore institutional health.

PCA is **not automatically**:

- liquidation;
- moratorium;
- licence cancellation;
- DICGC payout.

Banks and NBFCs both intermediate credit, but differ in liabilities, payment privileges and safety-net access.

| Dimension | Point |

- prevents regulatory arbitrage;
- protects uninformed depositors;
- supports financial stability;
- permits specialised credit without granting bank-like privileges.

**Conclusion:**
Regulation should increasingly follow economic function and systemic risk while preserving the statutory bank–NBFC distinction.

# 11. Twenty-mark answer framework

### Question

**Evaluate the roles of commercial banks, RRBs, cooperative banks, differentiated banks and NBFCs in balancing growth, inclusion and stability.**

## Answer matrix

| Institution | Contribution | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial banks | Deposits, payments and large-scale credit | Concentration and systemic risk |
| RRBs | Rural and agricultural outreach | Regional concentration and viability |
| Cooperative banks | Local knowledge and community access | Governance and dual-control legacy |
| SFBs | Inclusion plus small-ticket lending | Concentration and transition risk |
| Payments Banks | Low-cost payments and savings | Cannot lend from own balance sheet |
| NBFCs | Specialised and underserved credit | Funding fragility and no deposit safety net |

### Analytical body

1. **Growth:** mobilise savings and allocate capital.
2. **Inclusion:** reach rural, informal and thin-file borrowers.
3. **Stability:** capital, liquidity, supervision and deposit insurance.
4. **Tensions:** inclusion versus underwriting; innovation versus conduct; competition versus concentration.
5. **Reforms:** proportional regulation, stronger governance, interoperable infrastructure and coordinated supervision.

### Conclusion

India requires institutional diversity within a common stability architecture. The objective is not to make every intermediary identical, but to ensure that similar risks receive similar regulation.
