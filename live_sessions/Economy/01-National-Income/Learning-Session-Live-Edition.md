## 🖼️ Visual: One economic activity, three viewpoints

```text
         Firm produces bread worth ₹100
                      │
        ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
        ↓             ↓             ↓
     OUTPUT          INCOME      EXPENDITURE
 Bread worth ₹100   Wages, rent,  Buyer spends
                    interest and     ₹100
                       profit
        └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                      ↓
          Production = Income = Expenditure
```

Every act of production simultaneously:

1. creates output;
2. generates income for someone;
3. results in expenditure by a buyer.

This identity is the foundation of national-income accounting.

# 1. What is national-income accounting?

✅ **Fact:** National-income accounting systematically measures economic production, income and expenditure during a specified period.

It answers questions such as:

- How much did the economy produce?
- Which sectors produced it?
- How much income accrued to residents?
- Who consumed or invested the output?
- Was growth due to higher production or merely higher prices?

## Flow versus stock

| Flow | Stock |
|---|---|
| Measured over a period | Measured at one point in time |
| GDP during 2025–26 | Wealth on 31 March 2026 |
| Income earned during a month | Bank balance on a particular date |
| Investment during a year | Existing capital stock |

> 🔑 **UPSC trap:** GDP is a **flow**, not a stock.

---

# 2. Circular flow of income

## Simple two-sector economy

```text
                   GOODS AND SERVICES
           Firms ─────────────────────→ Households
             ↑                              │
             │                              │
             │                              ↓
      Factors of production             Consumption
      labour, land, capital             expenditure
             │                              │
             ↓                              ↑
         Households ←──────────────────── Firms
                  WAGES, RENT,
              INTEREST AND PROFIT
```

Households provide factors of production to firms. Firms compensate them through wages, rent, interest and profits. Households then spend income on goods and services.

## Real flow and money flow

| Real flow | Money flow |
|---|---|
| Labour and other factors move to firms | Factor payments move to households |
| Goods and services move to households | Consumption spending moves to firms |

✅ These flows move in opposite directions.

---

# 3. Three-sector and four-sector economy

## Government added

```text
Households and firms
       │
       ├── taxes → Government
       └── government expenditure and transfers ← Government
```

## Foreign sector added

```text
Exports → income enters domestic economy
Imports → expenditure leaks abroad
```

Thus, the expenditure identity becomes:

```text
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
```

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **C** | Private final consumption |
| **I** | Investment or capital formation |
| **G** | Government final expenditure |
| **X** | Exports |
| **M** | Imports |

### Why subtract imports?

Imports may already appear inside household consumption, investment or government expenditure. They are subtracted because GDP must count only **domestically produced** output.

> 🔑 Imports are subtracted to remove foreign production—not because imports are inherently harmful.

---

# 4. Domestic territory versus normal residents

This distinction separates **domestic product** from **national product**.

## Domestic principle

✅ Domestic measures follow the **location of production**.

> Was production undertaken within India’s economic territory?

## National principle

✅ National measures follow the income of **normal residents**.

> Did the factor income accrue to an Indian normal resident?

```text
GDP → WHERE was production undertaken?
GNP/GNI → WHO earned the factor income?
```

## Example matrix

| Activity | Indian GDP | Indian GNP/GNI |
|---|---:|---:|
| Indian-owned factory operating in Maharashtra | Included | Included |
| Foreign-owned factory operating in Gujarat | Included | Profit accruing abroad requires adjustment |
| Indian resident’s enterprise operating abroad | Excluded | Resident factor income included |
| Foreign tourist purchasing a service in India | Included | Included through domestic production |
| Personal gift sent by an overseas relative | Not production | Not factor income |

⚠️ Ownership does not determine GDP. **Location of production** does.

---

# 5. Normal residence

✅ A normal resident is an institutional unit whose **centre of predominant economic interest** lies within the economy.

This is an economic concept—not simply citizenship or nationality.

| Person | Likely treatment |
|---|---|
| Indian citizen settled and economically active abroad long-term | Not an Indian normal resident for that activity |
| Foreign citizen residing and working in India on a continuing basis | May be an Indian normal resident |
| Tourist visiting India briefly | Not a normal resident |
| Indian government diplomatic establishment abroad | Part of India’s economic territory under national-accounting conventions |

> 🔑 **Nationality ≠ normal residence.**

---

# 6. Factor income versus transfer

## Factor or primary income

Payment received for contributing to production:

- compensation of employees;
- rent;
- interest under the relevant accounting treatment;
- profits and operating surplus.

## Transfer

A payment without a corresponding current productive service:

- gifts;
- pensions and social benefits;
- many personal remittances;
- scholarships;
- certain subsidies.

```text
Work or productive asset supplied → factor income
No current productive service     → transfer
```

⚠️ **Major trap:** Every remittance received from abroad is not net factor income from abroad.

- Salary earned abroad by a qualifying resident may be factor income.
- A personal transfer sent to family is not factor income merely because it crosses a border.

---

# 7. Gross domestic product in plain language

✅ Gross domestic product (**GDP**) is the market value of final goods and services produced within domestic territory during an accounting period.

Every word matters:

| Word | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Gross | Depreciation has not been deducted |
| Domestic | Based on location of production |
| Product | Measures current production |
| Market value | Different outputs are expressed in money |
| Final | Avoids double counting intermediate goods |
| During a period | GDP is a flow |

## Example

Suppose an Indian bakery produces bread worth ₹1 lakh this year:

- it contributes to Indian GDP;
- it does not matter whether the bakery is owned by an Indian or foreign company;
- only current value added or final output is counted;
- the resale of last year’s bread-making machine is not current production.

# 9. Prelims traps

1. **GDP follows ownership.** ❌
   GDP follows the location of production.

2. **GNP follows citizenship.** ❌
   It follows normal residence and factor-income accrual.

3. **Every foreign remittance is factor income.** ❌
   Personal transfers differ from factor income.

4. **Imports are subtracted because they reduce welfare.** ❌
   They are subtracted to exclude foreign production.

5. **GDP measures wealth at a point in time.** ❌
   GDP measures production over a period.

6. **Transfers constitute current production.** ❌
   They redistribute purchasing power without corresponding current output.

# Revision notes

1. National accounts measure production, income and expenditure.
2. Production, income and expenditure are three views of the same activity.
3. GDP is a flow measured over a period.
4. Domestic measures follow production location.
5. National measures follow normal residence.
6. Normal residence is not the same as citizenship.
7. Factor income arises from productive contribution.
8. Transfers do not represent current productive services.
9. Imports are subtracted to remove foreign output.
10. GDP counts current final production within domestic territory.
11. Foreign ownership does not exclude production from Indian GDP.
12. Base-year revisions update the statistical representation of the economy.

# 1. Gross value added (GVA)

✅ **Definition:** Gross value added is the value of output produced minus the value of intermediate inputs consumed in producing it.

```text
GVA = Value of output − Intermediate consumption
```

## Example

A baker:

- sells bread for ₹100;
- uses flour, electricity and other intermediate inputs worth ₹60.

```text
GVA = ₹100 − ₹60 = ₹40
```

The baker contributes ₹40 of new value—not ₹100—because ₹60 was already generated at earlier production stages.

## Why GVA matters

GVA shows the contribution of:

- agriculture;
- manufacturing;
- construction;
- trade;
- financial services;
- public administration;
- other economic sectors.

> 🔑 **GVA answers:** “How much value did producers and sectors add?”

---

# 2. Gross domestic product (GDP)

✅ GDP measures the value of final goods and services produced within domestic territory during an accounting period.

Officially:

```text
GDP at market prices
=
GVA at basic prices
+ product taxes
− product subsidies
```

## Why make this adjustment?

GVA measures value from the producer side. The final buyer pays a price affected by taxes and subsidies on products.

### Example

Suppose:

- total GVA at basic prices = ₹1,000;
- product taxes = ₹120;
- product subsidies = ₹20.

```text
GDP at market prices
= 1,000 + 120 − 20
= ₹1,100
```

| Aggregate | Perspective |
|---|---|
| GVA at basic prices | Amount accruing to producers before product-tax adjustment |
| GDP at market prices | Value of final domestic output at purchasers’ prices |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Taxes take GDP above GVA; subsidies pull it below.**

---

# 3. Product taxes and subsidies

## Product tax

A tax payable per unit or as a proportion of the value of a good or service:

- Goods and Services Tax;
- excise-type product levies;
- customs duties under the relevant accounting treatment.

## Product subsidy

Government support linked to a particular product or its quantity/value.

⚠️ Product taxes differ from general production taxes. The GDP–GVA bridge specifically uses **taxes less subsidies on products**.

## Why GDP and GVA growth may differ

```text
Underlying production growth
             +
Change in net product taxes
             ↓
GDP growth may differ from GVA growth
```

If tax collections rise sharply relative to subsidies, GDP growth may exceed GVA growth even when producer-side growth is unchanged.

---

# 4. Gross and net

“Gross” means depreciation has **not** been deducted.

“Net” means depreciation has been deducted.

```text
Net aggregate = Gross aggregate − Depreciation
```

The official term for depreciation is **consumption of fixed capital (CFC)**.

## What is depreciation?

Capital assets wear out or become obsolete:

- machines deteriorate;
- buildings require replacement;
- software becomes obsolete;
- vehicles lose productive capacity.

Part of gross output merely compensates for this loss.

## Domestic conversion

```text
NDP = GDP − depreciation
```

## National conversion

```text
NNP/NNI = GNP/GNI − depreciation
```

### Example

If:

- GDP = ₹1,100;
- depreciation = ₹100;

then:

```text
NDP = ₹1,000
```

> 🔑 **Gross shows total production; net better reflects production remaining after maintaining capital.**

---

# 5. Domestic and national

## Domestic product

Follows the **location of production**:

```text
GDP/NDP → production inside domestic territory
```

## National product or income

Follows the income of **normal residents**:

```text
GNP/GNI and NNP/NNI → income accruing to normal residents
```

The bridge is net primary or factor income from abroad.

```text
Net factor income from abroad
=
Factor income received from abroad
− factor income paid abroad
```

## Formula

```text
GNP/GNI = GDP + NFIA
```

Modern official terminology generally prefers **gross national income (GNI)**; older textbooks frequently use **gross national product (GNP)**.

For standard UPSC conceptual purposes:

```text
GNP ≈ GNI
```

They express the gross income accruing to normal residents after cross-border primary-income adjustment.

---

# 6. Numerical example

Suppose India has:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| GDP at market prices | ₹1,100 |
| Factor income received from abroad | ₹80 |
| Factor income paid abroad | ₹100 |
| Depreciation | ₹90 |

## Step 1: Calculate NFIA

```text
NFIA = 80 − 100 = −₹20
```

## Step 2: Calculate GNP/GNI

```text
GNP/GNI = 1,100 + (−20)
        = ₹1,080
```

## Step 3: Calculate NNP/NNI

```text
NNP/NNI = 1,080 − 90
        = ₹990
```

Because NFIA is negative, national income is lower than domestic output.

---

# 7. Positive and negative NFIA

| Situation | Result |
|---|---|
| Residents earn more factor income abroad than non-residents earn domestically | NFIA positive; GNI exceeds GDP |
| Non-residents earn more domestically than residents earn abroad | NFIA negative; GNI is below GDP |
| Both flows equal | NFIA zero; GDP equals GNI |

⚠️ GDP may be high while GNI is comparatively lower if a significant part of income generated domestically accrues to non-residents.

---

# 8. National income

## Traditional textbook formulation

National income is often expressed as:

> **Net national product at factor cost.**

```text
National income
≈ NNP at factor cost
```

## Modern statistical presentation

Modern national accounts increasingly use:

- GNI;
- NNI;
- basic prices;
- market prices;
- primary-income terminology.

⚠️ **Exam discipline:** Do not combine an aggregate stated at market prices with a factor-cost formula without making the required tax–subsidy adjustment.

## Legacy conversion

In traditional simplified examination language:

```text
NNP at factor cost
=
NNP at market prices
− indirect taxes
+ subsidies
```

But when using current official data, follow the price basis stated in that release rather than mechanically applying old terminology.

---

# 9. GDP versus GNI

Consider a foreign-owned company producing in India:

```text
Production in India → included in Indian GDP
Profit sent abroad → factor income paid abroad
                   → reduces Indian GNI relative to GDP
```

Consider an Indian resident enterprise producing abroad:

```text
Production abroad → excluded from Indian GDP
Resident income received → factor income from abroad
                         → raises Indian GNI
```

| Question | Use |
|---|---|
| How much was produced inside India? | GDP |
| How much gross income accrued to Indian residents? | GNI/GNP |
| What remains after capital consumption? | NDP or NNI |
| Which sectors added value? | GVA |

---

# 10. Per-capita income

✅ Per-capita income represents average income per person.

```text
Per-capita income
=
National income ÷ population
```

⚠️ It is an average and does not reveal income distribution.

Two countries may have equal per-capita income but very different:

- inequality;
- poverty;
- public services;
- regional disparities;
- environmental quality.

---

# 11. Current linkage

✅ India’s revised **2022–23-base national-accounts series** retains the crucial distinction between:

- GVA at basic prices;
- GDP at market prices;
- gross and net aggregates;
- domestic and national income.

⚠️ When reading current GDP reports, first check:

```text
Current or constant prices?
GDP or GVA?
Gross or net?
Domestic or national?
Old series or revised series?
```

Comparing numbers without reading these labels can produce false conclusions.

---

# 12. Prelims traps

1. **GDP = GVA.** ❌
   GDP adds net product taxes to GVA.

2. **Gross means inflation-adjusted.** ❌
   Gross means before depreciation.

3. **National means production by citizens.** ❌
   It follows normal residents, not citizenship.

4. **All remittances are NFIA.** ❌
   Personal transfers differ from factor income.

5. **NDP exceeds GDP.** ❌
   NDP equals GDP minus depreciation.

6. **GDP must always exceed GNI.** ❌
   Their relation depends on the sign of NFIA.

7. **Per-capita income measures equality.** ❌
   It is only an average.

# Revision notes

1. GVA equals output minus intermediate consumption.
2. GVA measures producer and sectoral contribution.
3. GDP equals GVA plus product taxes minus product subsidies.
4. Gross aggregates include depreciation.
5. Net aggregates exclude depreciation.
6. GDP and NDP are domestic measures.
7. GNI/GNP and NNI/NNP are national measures.
8. GNI equals GDP plus net primary income from abroad.
9. NFIA may be positive, negative or zero.
10. National income is traditionally described as NNP at factor cost.
11. Modern official accounts generally use GNI/NNI and explicit price bases.
12. Per-capita income is an average, not a distribution measure.

# 1. The price switch

## Basic price

✅ Basic price is the amount receivable by the producer, excluding taxes on products and including subsidies on products.

## Market price

✅ Market price reflects the price paid by the purchaser after the product-tax and subsidy adjustment.

```text
GDP at market prices
=
GVA at basic prices
+ taxes on products
− subsidies on products
```

## Example

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| GVA at basic prices | ₹800 |
| Product taxes | ₹100 |
| Product subsidies | ₹30 |

```text
GDP at market prices
= 800 + 100 − 30
= ₹870
```

The ₹70 difference is **net taxes on products**.

---

# 2. Product taxes versus other production taxes

This distinction is important in modern national accounts.

| Tax | Trigger | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Tax on products | Quantity or value of a particular good/service | GST or excise-type levy |
| Other tax on production | Carrying out production, irrespective of output quantity | Business licence or tax on production premises |

## Where do they enter?

```text
GVA at factor cost
 + other taxes on production
 − other subsidies on production
 = GVA at basic prices

GVA at basic prices
 + taxes on products
 − subsidies on products
 = GDP at market prices
```

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** The direct bridge from GVA at basic prices to GDP at market prices uses **product taxes and product subsidies**, not every government tax or subsidy.

---

# 3. Factor cost: older but examinable terminology

Factor cost reflects payments accruing to the factors of production:

- wages;
- rent;
- interest;
- profits;
- mixed income.

Modern Indian official releases emphasise **basic prices** and **market prices**, but older textbooks and questions may use factor cost.

## Conversion

```text
GVA at basic prices
=
GVA at factor cost
+ other production taxes
− other production subsidies
```

Therefore:

```text
GVA at factor cost
=
GVA at basic prices
− other production taxes
+ other production subsidies
```

> 🔑 Product-level adjustment leads to market prices; general production-level adjustment leads toward factor cost.

---

# 4. The capital-consumption switch

Fixed capital loses productive value through:

- normal wear and tear;
- expected obsolescence;
- ordinary accidental damage covered by accounting conventions.

The official term is **consumption of fixed capital (CFC)**.

```text
Net = Gross − CFC
Gross = Net + CFC
```

## Complete domestic ladder

```text
GDP
 │
 │ − CFC
 ↓
NDP
```

## Complete national ladder

```text
GNI/GNP
    │
    │ − CFC
    ↓
NNI/NNP
```

### Example

| Aggregate | Amount |
|---|---:|
| GDP | ₹2,000 |
| CFC | ₹180 |

```text
NDP = 2,000 − 180 = ₹1,820
```

⚠️ Depreciation is not a tax, transfer or payment abroad. It measures capital used up during production.

---

# 5. The residence switch

✅ Net primary income from abroad converts a domestic aggregate into a national aggregate.

```text
NPIA/NFIA
=
Primary income received from abroad
− Primary income paid abroad
```

Then:

```text
GNI = GDP + NPIA
NNI = NDP + NPIA
```

These two routes must produce the same answer:

```text
Route A:
GDP + NPIA − CFC = NNI

Route B:
GDP − CFC + NPIA = NNI
```

## Example

Suppose:

- GDP = ₹3,000;
- primary income received from abroad = ₹140;
- primary income paid abroad = ₹200;
- CFC = ₹240.

```text
NPIA = 140 − 200 = −60

GNI = 3,000 − 60 = 2,940
NNI = 2,940 − 240 = ₹2,700
```

Alternative route:

```text
NDP = 3,000 − 240 = 2,760
NNI = 2,760 − 60 = ₹2,700
```

# 6. Complete conversion grid

| Starting aggregate | Adjustment | Result |
|---|---|---|
| GVA at basic prices | + product taxes − product subsidies | GDP at market prices |
| GDP | − CFC | NDP |
| GDP | + net primary income from abroad | GNI |
| GNI | − CFC | NNI |
| NDP | + net primary income from abroad | NNI |
| GVA at factor cost | + other production taxes − other production subsidies | GVA at basic prices |

## One integrated formula

```text
NNI at market prices
=
GVA at basic prices
+ net product taxes
+ net primary income from abroad
− CFC
```

---

# 7. Directional logic

## Product-tax increase

If GVA remains unchanged but net product taxes rise:

```text
GVA unchanged
Net product taxes increase
           ↓
GDP rises relative to GVA
```

## Product-subsidy increase

If GVA remains unchanged but product subsidies rise:

```text
Net product taxes fall
           ↓
GDP narrows relative to GVA
```

## Negative NPIA

```text
Income paid abroad > income received
                   ↓
GNI < GDP
```

## High depreciation

```text
Gross production may be high
             ↓
Large capital replacement requirement
             ↓
Net production substantially lower
```

---

# 8. Why gross and net tell different stories

Suppose two economies each report GDP of ₹10 lakh crore:

| Economy | CFC | NDP |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | ₹1 lakh crore | ₹9 lakh crore |
| B | ₹3 lakh crore | ₹7 lakh crore |

Economy B must devote more output to replacing consumed capital.

⚠️ **Inference:** GDP alone may overstate sustainable additions to productive capacity when depreciation is very high.

# 9. Common formula traps

1. **GDP = GVA − net product taxes.** ❌
   GDP equals GVA **plus** net product taxes.

2. **Gross → net by subtracting inflation.** ❌
   Subtract depreciation.

3. **Domestic → national by adding remittances.** ❌
   Add net primary income from abroad.

4. **Basic price excludes every tax.** ❌
   It specifically excludes taxes on products; other production taxes enter the basic-price framework.

5. **Market-price GDP directly measures factor earnings.** ❌
   Market prices include net product taxes.

6. **Depreciation converts nominal GDP into real GDP.** ❌
   It converts gross into net.

7. **NPIA and net exports are the same.** ❌
   Net exports concern goods and services; NPIA concerns cross-border primary income.

# Revision notes

1. Three switches are price basis, residence and capital consumption.
2. GDP equals GVA plus product taxes minus product subsidies.
3. Product taxes depend on particular goods or services.
4. Other production taxes arise from conducting production.
5. Factor cost is mainly legacy terminology in current Indian accounts.
6. Net equals gross minus consumption of fixed capital.
7. GNI equals GDP plus net primary income from abroad.
8. NNI can be calculated from either GNI or NDP.
9. Negative NPIA makes GNI lower than GDP.
10. High depreciation creates a large gap between gross and net output.
11. Net exports are not net primary income from abroad.
12. Always preserve the stated price basis while converting aggregates.

## 🖼️ Visual: Three windows onto one economy

```text
                    ECONOMIC PRODUCTION
                            │
         ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
         ↓                  ↓                  ↓
   PRODUCTION METHOD    INCOME METHOD    EXPENDITURE METHOD
  What value was added? Who received it? Who purchased output?
         │                  │                  │
```

If bread worth ₹100 is produced and sold:

- ₹100 of final output exists;
- ₹100 of income is distributed;
- ₹100 is spent on that output.

# 1. Production or value-added method

✅ This method sums the **value added** at each stage of production.

```text
Value added
=
Value of output
− Intermediate consumption
```

## Wheat-to-bread example

| Stage | Sale value | Intermediate input | Value added |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Farmer sells wheat | ₹20 | ₹0 | ₹20 |
| Miller sells flour | ₹35 | ₹20 | ₹15 |
| Baker sells bread | ₹50 | ₹35 | ₹15 |
| **Total** | **₹105** |  | **₹50** |

Correct GDP contribution:

```text
₹20 + ₹15 + ₹15 = ₹50
```

It is also equal to the final bread value of ₹50.

## Double counting

If all sales are added:

```text
20 + 35 + 50 = ₹105
```

This incorrectly counts:

- wheat inside flour;
- flour inside bread;
- the same embedded value repeatedly.

> 🔑 Count either the **final output** or the **value added at every stage**, never both.

---

# 2. Final versus intermediate goods

A good is not inherently final or intermediate. Its classification depends on **use**.

| Item | Use | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Milk bought by a household for drinking | Final consumption | Final good |
| Milk bought by a sweet manufacturer | Input into production | Intermediate good |
| Machine purchased by a factory | Capital formation | Final investment good |
| Steel used to manufacture that machine | Input | Intermediate good |

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** A machine purchased by a producer is a final good because it is capital formation, not an intermediate good consumed within the current production cycle.

---

# 3. Income method

✅ Production generates income for those contributing labour, capital and enterprise.

The income method adds:

- compensation of employees;
- rent and similar property income under the relevant framework;
- operating surplus or profit;
- mixed income of self-employed persons;
- relevant taxes less subsidies on production;
- depreciation when constructing a gross aggregate.

## Why “mixed income”?

For a self-employed shopkeeper or farmer, it may be difficult to separate:

- wages for personal labour;
- return on owned capital;
- entrepreneurial profit.

These are recorded together as **mixed income**.

## Income flow

```text
Value created by production
          ↓
Wages + operating surplus + mixed income
          ↓
Income-side estimate of value added
```

⚠️ Transfer payments are excluded because they do not compensate current productive activity.

Examples:

| Receipt | Income-method treatment |
|---|---|
| Salary for current work | Included |
| Profit from current production | Included |
| Mixed income of self-employed producer | Included |
| Government pension transfer | Excluded from current production |
| Personal gift | Excluded |
| Lottery prize | Excluded |

---

# 4. Expenditure method

✅ The expenditure method sums spending on final domestically produced output.

## Simplified identity

```text
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
```

| Component | Modern Indian-account term | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Private consumption (**C**) | PFCE | Household and private final consumption |
| Investment (**I**) | Capital formation | Fixed assets, inventories and related investment |
| Government consumption (**G**) | GFCE | Current government services |
| Exports (**X**) | Exports | Domestic output purchased abroad |
| Imports (**M**) | Imports | Foreign output embedded in expenditure |

## Fuller investment component

Investment includes:

```text
Gross fixed capital formation
       +
Change in inventories/stocks
       +
Valuables under accounting rules
```

---

# 5. Government expenditure trap

Not every government payment is government final consumption.

| Government payment | GDP treatment |
|---|---|
| Salaries of government teachers | Government final consumption |
| Purchase of administrative services | Government final consumption |
| Construction of a highway | Capital formation |
| Pension payment | Transfer; not current production |
| Unemployment benefit | Transfer |
| Subsidy | Affects price/income accounting; not itself purchase of final output |

> 🔑 **GFCE is not the same as total government expenditure.**

Government capital expenditure that creates assets enters capital formation rather than government final consumption.

---

# 6. Inventories

Goods produced but not sold during the year still count in that year’s GDP.

Why?

```text
Produced this year
      ↓
Adds to inventory
      ↓
Treated as investment by producer
```

When the inventory is sold later:

- the current production was already counted earlier;
- only relevant trade margins or new services are added later.

⚠️ Rising inventories can mean:

- planned stock-building due to expected demand; or
- unwanted accumulation because sales are weak.

Therefore, inventory growth is not automatically positive.

---

# 7. Exports and imports

## Exports

Exports are added because they are:

- produced domestically;
- purchased by foreign residents;
- not included in domestic private or government consumption.

## Imports

Imports are subtracted because they may already appear in:

- consumption;
- investment;
- government expenditure.

```text
Imported machine:
included initially in investment
                ↓
subtract imports
                ↓
foreign production removed from domestic GDP
```

> 🔑 Imports are a subtraction for accounting location, not a judgment that they are economically undesirable.

---

# 8. Why all three methods should agree

Suppose a firm produces output worth ₹1 lakh:

```text
Production method → ₹1 lakh of final value added/output
Income method     → ₹1 lakh distributed as income
Expenditure method→ ₹1 lakh spent on the output
```

Therefore:

```text
Output = Income = Expenditure
```

This is an accounting identity, not a claim that every person’s income equals their expenditure.

---

# 9. Why official estimates differ in practice

Actual estimates use different datasets:

| Method | Typical data challenge |
|---|---|
| Production | Measuring output and intermediate inputs |
| Income | Capturing informal wages, profits and mixed income |
| Expenditure | Measuring household consumption and investment accurately |
| Government | Consolidating Union, state and local accounts |
| External sector | Timing and valuation of trade and service flows |

Consequently, official tables may contain a **statistical discrepancy**.

```text
Theoretical equality
        +
Different sources, timing and revisions
        ↓
Statistical discrepancy in estimates
```

⚠️ A statistical discrepancy does not overturn the accounting identity; it reflects measurement limitations.

# 11. Choosing the method

| Question | Best starting method |
|---|---|
| Which sector contributed most? | Production/GVA |
| How was value distributed between labour and enterprise? | Income |
| Was growth driven by consumption or investment? | Expenditure |
| Are imports weakening the demand contribution? | Expenditure |
| Is intermediate-input cost squeezing value added? | Production |

A strong Mains answer often combines all three rather than treating GDP as one undifferentiated number.

1. Adding every sale at every stage gives GDP. ❌
   It causes double counting.

2. Every producer purchase is intermediate. ❌
   Capital goods are final investment goods.

3. Government transfers enter GFCE. ❌
   Transfers do not purchase current output.

1. Production, income and expenditure are three views of one activity.
2. The production method sums value added.
3. Final output value equals the sum of value added.
4. Double counting occurs when embedded intermediate values are repeatedly added.
5. Final versus intermediate status depends upon use.
6. Capital goods are final investment goods.
7. The income method includes compensation, surplus and mixed income.
8. Transfers do not represent current factor income.
9. Expenditure GDP equals consumption, investment, government consumption and net exports.
10. Inventories are included in investment.

# 1. Nominal GDP

✅ Nominal GDP values current production using **current-year prices**.

```text
Nominal GDP
=
Current-year quantities × current-year prices
```

It may increase because:

- more goods and services were produced;
- prices increased;
- both occurred.

## Example

| Year | Quantity produced | Price | Nominal GDP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Year 1 | 100 units | ₹10 | ₹1,000 |
| Year 2 | 100 units | ₹12 | ₹1,200 |

Nominal GDP increased by 20%, although physical production did not change.

> 🔑 Nominal growth is not necessarily real economic growth.

---

# 2. Real GDP

✅ Real GDP values current production using the prices of a fixed **base year**.

```text
Real GDP
=
Current-year quantities × base-year prices
```

Using Year 1 as the base:

| Year | Quantity | Base-year price | Real GDP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Year 1 | 100 | ₹10 | ₹1,000 |
| Year 2 | 100 | ₹10 | ₹1,000 |

Real GDP is unchanged because output volume is unchanged.

## Output-growth example

| Year | Quantity | Current price | Nominal GDP | Real GDP at ₹10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base year | 100 | ₹10 | ₹1,000 | ₹1,000 |
| Current year | 110 | ₹12 | ₹1,320 | ₹1,100 |

```text
Nominal growth = 32%
Real growth    = 10%
```

The difference largely reflects price change.

---

# 3. Current prices and constant prices

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GDP at current prices | Nominal GDP |
| GDP at constant prices | Real GDP |
| GVA at current prices | Nominal GVA |
| GVA at constant prices | Real GVA |

This is independent of basic versus market prices.

Therefore, India can report:

- GVA at current basic prices;
- GVA at constant basic prices;
- GDP at current market prices;
- GDP at constant market prices.

> 🔑 **Current/constant = price year. Basic/market = valuation perspective.**

---

# 4. Base year

✅ The base year supplies the reference prices and weights used to calculate constant-price output.

A good base year should broadly be:

- reasonably normal;
- supported by detailed datasets;
- not dominated by exceptional shocks;
- recent enough to represent the economy’s structure.

## Why revise it?

Economic structure changes:

```text
New industries emerge
Digital services expand
Consumption patterns change
Relative prices change
Old products decline
New data sources become available
           ↓
Old base becomes less representative
           ↓
Base year is revised
```

✅ India’s revised national-accounts series uses **2022–23** as its base year, replacing **2011–12**.

---

# 5. What changes during rebasing?

A base-year revision may update:

- price reference year;
- sectoral weights;
- production classifications;
- enterprise coverage;
- informal-sector estimates;
- administrative databases;
- price indices and deflators;
- estimation methodology.

⚠️ It is not merely replacing “2011–12” with “2022–23” in a formula.

## Consequence

The new series may produce different:

- GDP levels;
- sectoral shares;
- historical growth rates;
- nominal–real gaps.

This can occur even though the underlying historical factories and farms have not changed.

> 🔑 A statistical revision changes measurement of the past, not the past itself.

# 6. GDP deflator

✅ The GDP deflator measures the price change in domestically produced final output.

```text
GDP deflator
=
(Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100
```

In the base year:

```text
Nominal GDP = Real GDP
GDP deflator = 100
```

## Example

If:

- nominal GDP = ₹1,320;
- real GDP = ₹1,100;

then:

```text
GDP deflator
= (1,320 ÷ 1,100) × 100
= 120
```

This indicates that the overall price level of the measured domestic output is 20% above the base-year level.

⚠️ A deflator value of 120 is an **index level**, not automatically a 120% inflation rate.

---

# 7. Calculating deflator inflation

Suppose:

| Year | Deflator |
|---|---:|
| Year 1 | 120 |
| Year 2 | 126 |

```text
Deflator inflation
=
[(126 − 120) ÷ 120] × 100
= 5%
```

Do not simply subtract and call the result the inflation percentage unless the base is 100.

---

# 8. GDP deflator versus CPI and WPI

| Feature | GDP deflator | CPI | WPI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main coverage | Domestically produced final output | Household consumption basket | Mainly wholesale goods |
| Imports | Excluded as foreign production | Imported consumer goods may enter | Relevant traded goods may enter |
| Weights | Change with current output composition | Fixed and periodically revised basket | Fixed and periodically revised basket |
| Main use | Convert nominal domestic output into real output | Cost of living and retail inflation | Wholesale goods-price movement |
| Services | Broad domestic services included | Selected household services included | Limited service coverage |

## Key distinctions

- CPI can rise due to an imported consumer good.
- GDP deflator excludes imported production.
- GDP deflator covers investment and government output, not merely household consumption.
- CPI is more directly relevant to household cost of living.

> 🔑 The GDP deflator is broader for domestic production but is not a consumer cost-of-living index.

---

# 9. Single and double deflation

To calculate real GVA:

```text
GVA = Output − Intermediate consumption
```

## Single deflation

One price index, or a related volume indicator, is used to estimate real value added.

## Double deflation

Output and intermediate consumption are separately converted into constant prices:

```text
Real GVA
=
Real output
− Real intermediate consumption
```

## Why does this matter?

Suppose:

- output prices rise slowly;
- imported input prices rise rapidly.

Using one common deflator may distort real value added. Separate deflation better captures the changing relationship between input and output prices.

```text
Nominal output ÷ output-price index
             ↓
         Real output

Nominal inputs ÷ input-price index
             ↓
         Real inputs

Real output − real inputs = real GVA
```

✅ India’s revised series expands such sector-sensitive methods, particularly where suitable price data are available.

---

# 10. Negative implicit GVA deflator

Under double deflation, an implicit deflator for a sector’s value added may sometimes turn negative.

This does not necessarily mean every output price in the sector fell.

It can result when:

- output and input prices move differently;
- real value added grows faster than nominal value added;
- intermediate-input price dynamics materially affect the calculation.

⚠️ Interpret a sectoral GVA deflator together with its output and input-price movements rather than treating it as a simple retail-price index.

| What is the economy’s current money size? | Nominal GDP |

# 13. Prelims traps

1. Nominal GDP measures only price change. ❌
   It reflects both quantity and price.

2. Real GDP is GDP after depreciation. ❌
   Depreciation produces net GDP, not real GDP.

3. GDP deflator includes imports. ❌
   It covers domestic output.

4. CPI and GDP deflator have identical baskets. ❌
   Their coverage and weights differ.

5. Deflator 125 means inflation is 125%. ❌
   It means the measured price level is 25% above the base-year level.

6. Rebasing changes only the reference year. ❌
   It may change datasets, weights and methodology.

7. Every negative sectoral deflator means all sector prices fell. ❌
   Double-deflation input–output dynamics may also produce it.

8. Nominal GDP is useless. ❌
   It is essential for fiscal ratios and economy-size comparisons.

# Revision notes

1. Nominal GDP uses current quantities and current prices.
2. Real GDP uses current quantities and base-year prices.
3. Current prices mean nominal; constant prices mean real.
4. Nominal growth reflects output growth plus price change.
5. Real growth isolates output-volume change.
6. The base year supplies reference prices and weights.
7. India moved from the 2011–12 to the 2022–23 base.
8. Rebasing may update data, classifications, weights and methodology.
9. GDP deflator equals nominal GDP divided by real GDP times 100.
10. The GDP deflator covers domestically produced final output.
11. CPI is a household cost-of-living measure.
12. Double deflation separately deflates output and intermediate consumption.
13. Sectoral implicit deflators require careful interpretation.
14. Do not splice different GDP series without a comparable back series.

**Learner question / doubt**

> so tell me more about deb-to-gdp ratio, what you meant by denominator.

The **denominator** is the bottom part of a ratio:

```text
                    Government debt
Debt-to-GDP ratio = ───────────────── × 100
                     Nominal GDP
```

## Example

Suppose:

- government debt = ₹60 lakh crore;
- nominal GDP = ₹100 lakh crore.

```text
Debt-to-GDP = (60 ÷ 100) × 100 = 60%
```

Debt is the **numerator**; GDP is the **denominator**.

## Why use nominal GDP?

Government debt is recorded in current rupees. Therefore, it must be compared with GDP measured in current rupees:

```text
Nominal debt ÷ nominal GDP ✅
Nominal debt ÷ real GDP    ❌ incompatible price bases
```

Real GDP removes price changes and is intended for measuring output-volume growth. Using it against a nominal debt stock produces a distorted ratio.

## Why GDP is used

GDP approximates the economy’s income and tax-generating capacity.

> A ₹10 lakh crore debt is much more burdensome for a ₹20 lakh crore economy than for a ₹100 lakh crore economy.

## Ratio can fall even when debt rises

| Year | Debt | Nominal GDP | Debt-to-GDP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | ₹60 lakh crore | ₹100 lakh crore | 60% |
| 2 | ₹66 lakh crore | ₹120 lakh crore | 55% |

Debt increased by ₹6 lakh crore, but nominal GDP grew faster, so the ratio fell.

```text
Debt growth < nominal GDP growth
               ↓
Debt-to-GDP ratio falls
```

## Inflation effect

Inflation raises nominal GDP and may mechanically reduce the ratio, but this does not guarantee better fiscal health because:

- interest rates may rise;
- borrowing costs may increase;
- expenditure may also become more expensive;
- debt maturity and currency composition still matter.

⚠️ Always check what “debt” means:

- Union government debt;
- state government debt;
- combined general-government debt;
- gross or net debt.

Different definitions produce different ratios.

## 🖼️ Visual: The production-boundary test

```text
Did current production occur?
           │
      ┌────┴────┐
      │         │
     No        Yes
      │         │
 Excluded   Is it inside the
            production boundary?
                  │
             ┌────┴────┐
             │         │
            No        Yes
             │         │
        Excluded   Include current
                   value added/output
```

GDP does not measure every valuable human activity or every monetary payment. It measures production falling within a defined accounting boundary.

# 1. Four inclusion tests

Ask these questions:

```text
1. Was something produced during the current period?
2. Is it a good or service within the production boundary?
3. Is its value not already embedded elsewhere?
4. Did production occur within domestic territory?
```

A “yes” to all generally means inclusion in GDP.

---

# 2. Final goods and services

✅ Final goods and services are included because they are purchased for:

- consumption;
- investment;
- government use;
- export;
- inventory accumulation.

Examples:

| Transaction | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Household buys newly produced refrigerator | Included as consumption |
| Factory buys a new machine | Included as investment |
| Government builds a bridge | Included as capital formation |
| Firm exports software services | Included as exports |
| Producer retains unsold current output | Included as inventory investment |

---

# 3. Intermediate goods

Intermediate goods are excluded **separately** when their value is already contained in final output.

```text
Flour used in bread
       ↓
Flour value embedded in bread price
       ↓
Count bread or stage-wise value added
       ↓
Do not separately add flour again
```

⚠️ Exclusion does not mean intermediate production is economically unimportant. Its value is captured through value added or the final product.

---

# 4. Current production versus old assets

## Second-hand goods

The full resale value of an old asset is excluded because it was counted when originally produced.

| Transaction | GDP treatment |
|---|---|
| Sale of a five-year-old car | Car’s resale value excluded |
| Dealer’s current commission | Included |
| Repair before resale | Included |
| New accessories installed | Included |

> 🔑 Old asset excluded; current services surrounding its sale included.

## Existing financial assets

Purchasing shares or bonds transfers ownership of financial claims; it does not itself represent current production.

| Transaction | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Purchase of existing shares | Excluded |
| Stockbroker’s current service fee | Included |
| New factory built using raised capital | Included as investment |

⚠️ Financial investment and national-accounting investment are different concepts.

---

# 5. Transfer payments

Transfers redistribute income without purchasing current goods or services.

Examples:

- pensions;
- scholarships;
- unemployment benefits;
- gifts;
- donations;
- many personal remittances.

```text
Government pension payment
             ↓
Recipient receives purchasing power
             ↓
No current service supplied in exchange
             ↓
Transfer excluded from GDP
```

If the pensioner later buys food, that consumption expenditure enters GDP through the food currently produced and sold.

> 🔑 Transfer itself is excluded; subsequent expenditure on current output is included.

---

# 6. Paid and unpaid household work

| Activity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Household employs a paid domestic worker | Included |
| Restaurant prepares a meal | Included |
| Homemaker prepares the same meal for family | Conventionally excluded |
| Commercial childcare service | Included |
| Parent provides unpaid childcare | Conventionally excluded |

## Why exclude unpaid household services?

Practical reasons include:

- no observed market transaction;
- difficulty assigning consistent prices;
- difficulty defining output and hours;
- risk of very large measurement revisions based on assumptions.

## Why is this criticised?

Unpaid household and care work:

- requires time and skill;
- supports the paid economy;
- is disproportionately undertaken by women;
- would often require paid substitutes if not performed within households.

⚠️ **Inference:** GDP’s production boundary can understate socially necessary work and reinforce gender blindness.

> 🔑 Excluded from GDP does not mean economically or socially worthless.

---

# 7. Own-account production

Not every non-market activity is excluded.

Some goods produced for own use may be estimated and included:

- crops retained for household consumption;
- construction of one’s own dwelling;
- qualifying own-account software or capital creation.

Why?

These activities produce goods or assets that could meaningfully be exchanged or valued.

The boundary is stricter for unpaid household **services** than for goods produced for own consumption.

---

# 8. Imputed values

An imputed value is assigned even when no explicit payment occurs.

## Owner-occupied housing

A homeowner does not pay rent to themselves, but the dwelling supplies a housing service.

National accounts may estimate:

```text
Imputed rent
=
Estimated rental value of owner-occupied housing
```

This improves comparability between:

- households that rent;
- households that own similar homes.

Without imputation:

```text
Identical housing service
Renting household → counted
Owner household   → not counted
```

GDP would change merely because ownership arrangements changed.

---

# 9. Government services

Many government services have no market sale price:

- policing;
- defence;
- public administration;
- government schooling;
- public-health administration.

They are commonly valued using production costs such as:

- employee compensation;
- consumption of fixed capital;
- intermediate inputs.

⚠️ This measures input cost rather than the quality or social outcome of the service.

```text
Higher government cost
         ↓
May increase measured output
         ↓
Does not automatically prove better service quality
```

---

# 10. Informal activity

⚠️ Informal does **not** mean automatically excluded from GDP.

National accounts attempt to estimate production by:

- small unregistered firms;
- self-employed workers;
- household enterprises;
- informal traders;
- unincorporated service providers.

Because complete accounts are unavailable, statisticians use:

- enterprise surveys;
- labour surveys;
- administrative indicators;
- benchmark–indicator methods;
- sector-specific ratios and extrapolation.

## Measurement problem

```text
Benchmark survey conducted
          ↓
Ratios used to estimate later years
          ↓
Sudden shock changes productivity or firm survival
          ↓
Old ratio may become inaccurate
```

India’s revised series seeks better informal-sector coverage through updated surveys such as the Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises.

---

# 11. Illegal and underground production

Conceptually, mutually agreed production may fall within the national-accounting production boundary even if it is illegal or concealed.

However, actual measurement is extremely difficult because participants deliberately avoid records.

⚠️ Do not confuse:

- activity conceptually inside the production boundary;
- activity successfully measured in official GDP.

---

# 12. Digital economy

## Included

- paid software;
- digital subscriptions;
- e-commerce commissions;
- advertising services;
- platform fees;
- qualifying software and database investment;
- paid cloud services.

## Difficult cases

Users may receive a “free” digital service while platforms earn through advertising or data-supported business models.

The platform’s measurable advertising and commercial revenue enters GDP, but the full consumer benefit from free search, messaging or information is not directly measured.

> 🔑 GDP records market production, not the entire consumer surplus created by digital services.

---

# 13. Environmental depletion

Production that damages the environment may still increase GDP.

```text
Forest extraction → timber output rises → GDP rises
Environmental depletion → not fully deducted from GDP
```

Similarly:

- pollution-control expenditure can add to GDP;
- disaster reconstruction can add to GDP;
- the original welfare and asset loss may not be fully reflected.

This is why environmental and wealth accounts are needed alongside GDP.

# 16. Prelims traps

1. Informal production is excluded from GDP. ❌
   It is conceptually included and statistically estimated.

2. Every non-market activity is excluded. ❌
   Imputed rent and some own-account production are included.

3. Every monetary payment enters GDP. ❌
   Transfers and financial-asset purchases do not represent current production.

4. Second-hand sales increase GDP by their full value. ❌
   Only current associated services enter.

5. Unsold goods are excluded. ❌
   They enter inventory investment.

6. Unpaid household work is worthless because it is excluded. ❌
   Accounting exclusion is not a welfare judgment.

7. Government services are valued by market profit. ❌
   Non-market services are largely valued through production costs.

# Revision notes

1. GDP counts current production within a defined boundary.
2. Final goods, investment, government services, exports and inventories are included.
3. Intermediate inputs are not separately added.
4. Old assets are excluded, but current brokerage and repair services count.
5. Transfers redistribute income without creating current output.
6. Paid household services count; unpaid household services generally do not.
7. Some own-account goods and assets are included.
8. Owner-occupied housing is valued through imputed rent.
9. Government non-market services are largely valued by cost.
10. Informal activity is conceptually included and estimated.
11. Underground activity may be conceptually included but difficult to measure.
12. Free digital consumer benefits are not fully captured.
13. Environmental depletion is not adequately deducted from GDP.
14. GDP’s boundary is statistical, not a complete measure of social value.

```text
GDP GROWTH
    │
    ├── tells us ──────────────→ More measured output was produced
    │                           Economy's tax/debt capacity may expand

                                Recognition of unpaid work
```

> 🔑 GDP is a production measure, not a complete welfare score.

# 1. What GDP is useful for

GDP remains indispensable for measuring:

- size of economic activity;
- real output growth;
- sectoral composition;
- consumption and investment demand;
- fiscal capacity;
- debt and deficit ratios;
- productivity and business-cycle analysis;
- international market size.

## Example

A government needs nominal GDP to interpret:

```text
Fiscal deficit ÷ nominal GDP
Government debt ÷ nominal GDP
Tax revenue ÷ nominal GDP
```

Real GDP is used to evaluate actual output growth.

---

# 2. GDP versus welfare

Two economies can have identical GDP per capita yet differ greatly in welfare.

| Dimension | Economy A | Economy B |
|---|---|---|
| Per-capita GDP | Same | Same |
| Income distribution | Relatively equal | Highly unequal |
| Public healthcare | Strong | Weak |
| Air quality | Good | Hazardous |
| Working hours | Moderate | Excessive |
| Personal safety | High | Low |

GDP alone cannot tell us which population enjoys greater well-being.

---

# 3. Distribution and inequality

Per-capita GDP is:

```text
GDP or national income ÷ population
```

It is an average.

## Example

Two five-person economies each have total income of ₹500:

```text
Per-capita income = ₹100
```

But distribution may be:

| Economy | Individual incomes |
|---|---|
| A | 100, 100, 100, 100, 100 |
| B | 10, 10, 20, 60, 400 |

The average is identical, but welfare and opportunity differ sharply.

Complementary indicators include:

- poverty rates;
- consumption distribution;
- Gini coefficient;
- income and wealth shares;
- Inequality-adjusted Human Development Index.

---

# 4. Employment quality

GDP may grow without proportionate growth in secure employment.

```text
GDP growth
     │
     ├── automation-led productivity
     ├── capital-intensive production
     ├── high-profit digital sectors
     └── informal low-quality employment
```

Therefore, examine alongside GDP:

- labour-force participation;
- unemployment;
- worker-population ratio;
- real wages;
- formal versus informal work;
- underemployment;
- employment elasticity of growth.

> 🔑 “Jobless growth” does not necessarily mean literally zero new jobs; it means employment growth is weak relative to output growth.

---

# 5. Unpaid care and household work

GDP excludes much unpaid household service:

- cooking;
- cleaning;
- childcare;
- eldercare;
- household management.

This can create a paradox:

```text
Unpaid household childcare → excluded
Same service purchased commercially → included
```

The underlying service may be similar, but the accounting treatment changes when it enters the market.

⚠️ GDP can therefore rise when activity shifts from unpaid to paid production even if the total service received by society changes little.

Time-use surveys complement GDP by showing how paid and unpaid work is distributed.

---

# 6. Environmental damage

GDP records current production but does not fully deduct:

- resource depletion;
- pollution;
- biodiversity loss;
- carbon emissions;
- long-term ecological damage.

## Example

```text
Forest cleared
     ↓
Timber sold → GDP increases
     ↓
Lost ecosystem services → not fully deducted
```

Similarly, pollution-related illness may generate additional healthcare expenditure, raising GDP even though welfare deteriorated.

Complementary approaches include:

- environmental-economic accounts;
- natural-capital accounts;
- adjusted net savings;
- emissions and pollution indicators;
- green GDP concepts.

---

# 7. Defensive and reconstruction expenditure

A cyclone destroys homes and infrastructure:

```text
Initial destruction
      ↓
Loss of wealth and welfare
      ↓
Reconstruction expenditure
      ↓
Current GDP increases
```

GDP records reconstruction as new production but does not fully subtract the destroyed wealth from the current production measure.

> 🔑 GDP is a flow of production; disasters also destroy stocks of wealth.

---

# 8. Quality of public services

Government non-market services are often valued through their input costs.

```text
More spending on education
            ↓
Measured government output may rise
```

But cost does not automatically indicate:

- learning outcomes;
- teacher quality;
- hospital effectiveness;
- administrative efficiency.

For welfare analysis, combine expenditure data with outcome indicators.

# 10. Potential GDP

✅ Potential GDP is the sustainable level of output an economy can produce without generating destabilising inflationary pressure.

It depends on:

- labour supply;
- capital stock;
- productivity;
- technology;
- institutions;
- infrastructure.

## Output gap

```text
Output gap
=
Actual GDP − Potential GDP
```

### Negative output gap

```text
Actual output < potential output
```

Possible implications:

- idle capacity;
- weak demand;
- unemployment;
- scope for cyclical support.

### Positive output gap

```text
Actual output > sustainable potential
```

Possible implications:

- overheating;
- inflationary pressure;
- capacity constraints.

⚠️ Potential GDP is estimated, not directly observed. Different models produce different results.

---

# 11. Incremental capital-output ratio (ICOR)

ICOR indicates how much additional capital is associated with an additional unit of output.

```text
ICOR
≈ Investment rate ÷ GDP growth rate
```

## Interpretation

| ICOR | Broad implication |
|---|---|
| Lower | Capital is producing more additional output |
| Higher | More investment is required for each unit of added output |

Suppose:

- investment rate = 30% of GDP;
- growth rate = 6%.

```text
ICOR ≈ 30 ÷ 6 = 5
```

If the same investment rate produces 10% growth:

```text
ICOR ≈ 30 ÷ 10 = 3
```

⚠️ A high investment rate alone does not guarantee growth. Project execution, capacity utilisation, technology and institutional quality matter.

ICOR is an aggregate diagnostic, not a precise explanation of where inefficiency lies.

# 12. PPP versus market-exchange-rate GDP

## Market-exchange-rate GDP

Domestic GDP is converted using the prevailing currency exchange rate.

Best suited for:

- external purchasing power;
- international finance;
- trade capacity;
- market size for foreign investors;
- foreign-currency debt comparison.

## Purchasing-power-parity GDP

PPP uses a common basket to adjust for differences in domestic price levels.

Best suited for:

- comparing real output volumes;
- comparing living standards;
- assessing domestic purchasing power.

```text
Same income converted at market exchange rate
                ≠
Same quantity of goods purchasable domestically
```

⚠️ A country’s PPP GDP ranking may be much higher than its market-exchange-rate ranking.

> 🔑 PPP asks what money can buy domestically; market exchange rates ask what the currency converts into internationally.

# 13. Estimate vintages

GDP data arrive in stages because complete information is unavailable immediately.

Typical sequence includes:

```text
Early/advance estimate
        ↓
Provisional estimate
        ↓
Revised estimate
        ↓
Later finalised estimate
```

Later estimates incorporate fuller:

- tax data;
- corporate accounts;
- agricultural output;
- government accounts;
- household and enterprise surveys.

# Final revision sheet

1. GDP is territorial; GNI is resident-income based.
2. GVA equals output minus intermediate consumption.
3. GDP equals GVA plus net product taxes.
4. Gross includes depreciation; net excludes it.
5. Production, income and expenditure estimates should theoretically agree.
6. Inventories are investment.
7. Transfers and old-asset values are excluded.
8. Informal output is conceptually included and estimated.
9. Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP uses constant prices.
10. The GDP deflator covers domestically produced final output.
11. Base revisions update weights, data and methodology.
12. GDP measures output, not complete welfare.
13. Per-capita GDP does not measure distribution.
14. Employment and human-development indicators must complement GDP.
15. Environmental damage is not adequately deducted.
16. PPP and market-exchange-rate GDP answer different questions.
17. Potential GDP enables output-gap analysis.
18. ICOR links investment effort with additional output.
19. Estimate vintage must accompany current GDP figures.
20. Never splice incompatible GDP series casually.
