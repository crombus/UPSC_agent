## 🖼️ Visual: Price level versus inflation rate

```text
PRICE LEVEL
₹100 ──→ ₹110 ──→ ₹115 ──→ ₹117
          +10%      +4.5%     +1.7%
             INFLATION RATE

Prices are still rising throughout,
but they are rising more slowly.
                    ↓
              DISINFLATION
```

> 🔑 **Core distinction:** The price level tells us how expensive things are; inflation tells us how quickly that level is rising.

## 1. What is inflation?

✅ **Inflation** is a sustained and sufficiently broad rise in the general price level.

Three conditions matter:

```text
Sustained + broad-based + general price increase
```

### Not every price increase is inflation

| Situation | Inflation? |
|---|---|
| Onion prices rise temporarily | Not necessarily |
| Mobile-phone prices rise because of a new model | Not necessarily |
| Food, housing, transport and services rise persistently | Yes, general inflation |
| Prices rise only during a one-day festival shortage | Usually a temporary relative-price movement |

Inflation concerns the overall basket, not every individual item.

Some prices may fall even while the general price level rises.

## 2. Inflation rate

Suppose a representative basket costs:

```text
Last year: ₹1,000
This year: ₹1,050
```

The price level increased by ₹50, giving inflation of 5%.

```text
Inflation rate
= (Current price index − Previous price index)
  ─────────────────────────────────────────── × 100
              Previous price index
```

Detailed calculations and base effects will come in Subtopic 2.

## 3. Disinflation

✅ **Disinflation** means the inflation rate falls while the general price level may continue rising.

```text
Year 1: prices rise by 8%
Year 2: prices rise by 5%
Year 3: prices rise by 3%
```

Prices have not returned to their original level. They merely rise more slowly.

### Household example

```text
Basket price:
₹100 → ₹108 → ₹113.40 → ₹116.80
        8%      5%          3%
```

The basket becomes more expensive every year despite disinflation.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Disinflation = deceleration, not decline.**

## 4. Deflation

✅ **Deflation** means a sustained fall in the general price level.

```text
₹100 → ₹97 → ₹94
        −3%    −3.1%
```

Deflation may sound beneficial because goods become cheaper, but persistent deflation can be damaging:

```text
Consumers postpone purchases
          ↓
Firm revenue and profits decline
          ↓
Production, wages and employment weaken
          ↓
Demand falls further
```

Debt also becomes harder to repay because its nominal amount remains fixed while incomes and prices may decline.

## 5. Reflation

✅ **Reflation** is deliberate policy action intended to raise demand, prices and output after deflation or severe economic weakness.

Possible tools include:

- lower interest rates;
- greater public expenditure;
- tax relief;
- additional liquidity.

❌ Reflation does not necessarily mean uncontrolled inflation. Its initial purpose is to restore demand toward normal levels.

## 6. Headline inflation

✅ **Headline inflation** measures inflation in the complete consumer basket, including volatile components such as food and fuel.

```text
HEADLINE CPI
Food + fuel + housing + clothing + services + other items
```

It reflects the price change actually faced across the full representative household basket.

✅ India’s inflation-targeting framework uses **headline CPI**, not core inflation or WPI, as its formal nominal anchor.

## 7. Core inflation

✅ **Core inflation** conventionally excludes food and fuel because they are often highly volatile.

```text
Headline inflation
− food
− fuel
≈ core inflation
```

Core inflation helps assess whether price pressure has become:

- broad;
- persistent;
- demand-sensitive;
- embedded in wages, services and expectations.

### Example

```text
Vegetable crop failure
        ↓
Food prices rise sharply
        ↓
Headline inflation rises
        ↓
Core inflation may remain stable
```

This suggests a concentrated food shock rather than economy-wide demand overheating.

## 8. Headline versus core

| Headline inflation | Core inflation |
|---|---|
| Includes food and fuel | Conventionally excludes food and fuel |
| Better reflects complete household basket | Helps assess underlying persistence |
| Highly affected by weather and energy shocks | Often more responsive to demand and expectations |
| India’s formal inflation target | Analytical indicator, not the formal target |

### Important trap

❌ Core inflation is not always lower than headline inflation.

If food prices fall while services remain expensive:

```text
Headline inflation < core inflation
```

## 9. Current India anchor

✅ MoSPI’s latest available official CPI release at the start of this session is for **July 2026**, using the revised **2024=100** series.

| Indicator | Provisional year-on-year rate |
|---|---:|
| Headline CPI inflation | **4.45%** |
| Consumer food inflation | **5.52%** |

⚠️ Interpretation:

- food inflation was higher than overall headline inflation;
- this does not prove every food item rose by 5.52%;

## 10. Why these distinctions matter

Suppose inflation changes from 7% to 4%:

```text
Wrong conclusion:
“Prices have fallen.”

Correct conclusion:
“Prices are still rising, but more slowly.”
```

Suppose headline rises while core remains stable:

```text
Likely diagnosis:
volatile food/fuel pressure

Not automatically:
excess economy-wide demand
```

Diagnosis determines policy:

- broad demand inflation may justify monetary tightening;
- a temporary vegetable shortage needs supply and logistics measures;
- persistent deflation may require reflationary policy.

## UPSC traps

- ❌ Inflation means every commodity becomes more expensive.
  ✅ It concerns a sustained rise in the general price level.

- ❌ Disinflation means falling prices.
  ✅ It means a falling positive inflation rate.

- ❌ Deflation and disinflation are identical.
  ✅ Deflation means the price level falls.

- ❌ Core inflation is India’s formal policy target.
  ✅ Headline CPI is the formal anchor.

- ❌ Core inflation is necessarily below headline inflation.
  ✅ Falling food or fuel prices can make headline lower.

## Revision notes

1. ✅ Inflation is a sustained general price rise.
2. ✅ A single commodity-price spike is not automatically inflation.
3. ✅ Price level and inflation rate are different.
4. ✅ Disinflation means prices rise more slowly.
5. ✅ Deflation means the general price level falls.
6. ✅ Persistent deflation can weaken demand, output and employment.
7. ✅ Reflation seeks to revive prices and activity after weakness.
8. ✅ Headline inflation includes food and fuel.
9. ✅ Core inflation conventionally excludes food and fuel.
10. ✅ India targets headline CPI inflation.

## Mastery MCQ 1

An economy’s representative price index rises as follows:

```text
Year 1: 100
Year 2: 108
Year 3: 112
```

Which statement is correct?

A. The economy experienced deflation between Years 2 and 3.
B. Prices fell because inflation declined from 8% to about 3.7%.
C. The economy experienced disinflation because prices rose at a slower rate.
D. The general price level returned toward its Year 1 level.

---

✅ **Correct — C.**

The price level rose from **108 to 112**, so prices did not fall. Inflation slowed from **8% to about 3.7%**: this is **disinflation**.

### Mastery MCQ 2

Food prices decline, but housing and service prices continue rising persistently. Consequently, headline inflation falls below core inflation.

Which explanation is correct?

A. Core inflation includes food but excludes services.
B. Falling food prices reduce headline inflation, while core inflation reflects persistent non-food and non-fuel pressures.
C. Headline inflation excludes volatile commodities.
D. The economy must be experiencing deflation.

---

✅ **Correct — B. Subtopic 1 mastered: 2/2 consecutive correct.**

Falling food prices reduce **headline inflation**, while persistent housing and service inflation keeps **core inflation** elevated.

## 🖼️ How a price index is constructed

```text
Select representative basket
             ↓
Record prices across markets
             ↓
Assign expenditure weights
             ↓
Calculate weighted basket cost
             ↓
Express relative to base year = 100
             ↓
Compare index across time
             ↓
Calculate inflation rate
```

## 1. Why do we need a price index?

An economy contains millions of prices:

```text
Rice ↑   Smartphones ↓   Rent ↑   Petrol ↓   School fees ↑
```

We cannot describe inflation using one commodity. A **price index** combines representative price movements into one weighted number.

✅ **Price index:** A statistical measure showing the average change in prices of a selected basket relative to a base period.

## 2. Basket and weights

A representative basket contains goods and services purchased by households.

Items are not given equal importance. Their **weights** generally reflect their share in household expenditure.

### Illustrative household basket

| Item | Expenditure share/weight | Price rise |
|---|---:|---:|
| Food | 50% | 10% |
| Housing | 30% | 4% |
| Transport | 20% | 5% |

Approximate weighted inflation:

```text
(0.50 × 10%) + (0.30 × 4%) + (0.20 × 5%)
= 5% + 1.2% + 1%
= 7.2%
```

The 10% food-price rise has the greatest impact because food has the largest weight.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Impact = price change × weight**

### UPSC implication

A commodity may experience a huge price rise but contribute little to overall inflation if its basket weight is very small.

Conversely, a modest rise in a heavily weighted component can significantly influence inflation.

## 3. Base year

The base year is the reference period whose index is usually fixed at **100**.

```text
Base year price index = 100
Current price index   = 110
```

This means the weighted basket is approximately 10% more expensive than in the base period.

❌ It does not mean that every item became 10% costlier.

❌ It does not mean the basket costs ₹110. An index is a statistical number, not a rupee price.

## 4. Inflation from index values

Suppose:

```text
July 2025 CPI = 103
July 2026 CPI = 107.5
```

Then year-on-year inflation is:

```text
107.5 − 103
─────────── × 100 ≈ 4.37%
    103
```

### Three numbers that must not be confused

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Index level: 107.5 | Price level relative to the base |
| Index-point increase: 4.5 | Numerical difference between index values |
| Inflation: 4.37% | Percentage change relative to the earlier index |

A rise of four index points does not automatically mean 4% inflation.

## 5. Year-on-year versus month-on-month

### Year-on-year inflation

```text
CPI in July 2026
compared with
CPI in July 2025
```

This controls for recurring seasonal patterns better and is the commonly reported headline measure.

### Month-on-month inflation

```text
CPI in July 2026
compared with
CPI in June 2026
```

It captures recent price momentum but can be heavily affected by seasonal changes.

| Measure | Best use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Year-on-year | Annual inflation trend | Strong base effects |
| Month-on-month | Recent momentum | Seasonal volatility |

## 6. Base effect

The inflation rate depends on both:

```text
Current price level
        AND
Previous comparison base
```

### Visual example

| Year | Price index | Annual inflation |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2024 | 100 | — |
| 2025 | 120 | 20% |
| 2026 | 126 | 5% |

Inflation falls from 20% to 5%, even though the index rises from 120 to 126.

Why? The 2026 increase is measured against an already high 2025 base.

```text
High previous-year base
          ↓
Smaller measured inflation rate
          ↓
Favourable base effect
```

Now consider:

| Year | Price index |
|---|---:|
| 2024 | 100 |
| 2025 | 102 |
| 2026 | 108 |

Because the previous base was relatively low:

```text
Low previous-year base
          ↓
Larger measured inflation rate
          ↓
Adverse base effect
```

> 🔑 **Base effect changes the measured rate—not necessarily current price momentum.**

## 7. Base effect versus genuine price moderation

Suppose vegetable prices remain unchanged this month, but they had surged in the corresponding month last year.

Year-on-year food inflation may decline sharply because the current prices are compared against last year’s elevated level.

```text
Falling year-on-year inflation
                ≠
Current prices necessarily falling
```

To diagnose inflation correctly, examine:

- year-on-year inflation;
- month-on-month movement;
- seasonally adjusted momentum, where available;
- component-level price changes;
- the previous year’s comparison base.

## 8. Why revise a base year?

Consumption patterns change over time:

```text
Old basket:
more spending on cereals and traditional goods

New basket:
greater spending on housing, transport,
communications, healthcare and digital services
```

An outdated basket can misrepresent present household expenditure.

A base revision updates:

- commodities and services included;
- expenditure weights;
- classifications;
- price-collection markets;
- data sources and methodology.

## 9. India’s CPI 2024 series

✅ **Official facts:**

| Feature | Revised CPI series |
|---|---|
| Base | **2024=100** |
| Introduced | February 2026 |
| Weight source | **HCES 2023–24** |
| Classification | COICOP 2018 |
| Major divisions | 12 |
| Basket size | 358 items |
| First index period | January 2026 |

The revision reflects newer consumption patterns and expands the representation of goods and services.

### Weight changes matter

If food’s expenditure share declines while housing and services gain weight:

```text
Same component-level price changes
                 +
Different weights
                 ↓
Different measured headline inflation
```

Thus, changes between two CPI series can arise from:

1. actual price movements;
2. changed weights;
3. changed basket composition;
4. changed coverage or methodology.

## 10. Series-break trap

An index under `2012=100` and one under `2024=100` have different reference structures.

❌ Their index levels should not be directly joined or compared as though they form one unchanged series.

✅ Use an officially provided:

- back series;
- linking factor;
- splicing methodology.

Inflation rates calculated consistently within each official series may be compared more meaningfully than raw index levels, but methodological changes must still be acknowledged.

## UPSC traps

- ❌ Every basket item has equal importance.
  ✅ Items carry expenditure-based weights.

- ❌ Base year 100 means the basket costs ₹100.
  ✅ It is a reference index value.

- ❌ A five-point index rise always means 5% inflation.
  ✅ The percentage change depends on the starting index.

- ❌ Falling inflation proves prices are falling.
  ✅ The price level may still be rising.

- ❌ Base effects reflect only present supply and demand.
  ✅ They arise from the previous comparison value.

- ❌ Index levels from different base series can be directly spliced.
  ✅ An official linking method is required.

## Revision notes

1. A price index summarises changes across a weighted basket.
2. Weights represent relative expenditure importance.
3. Overall impact depends on both weight and price movement.
4. The base-year index is conventionally 100.
5. An index is unitless, not a rupee price.
6. Inflation is the percentage change in the index.
7. Year-on-year data compares the corresponding month one year earlier.
8. Month-on-month data better captures recent momentum.
9. A high previous base can mechanically lower current inflation.
10. Base revision updates baskets, weights and methodology.
11. India’s revised CPI uses `2024=100`.
12. Different index series require official linking methods.

## 🖼️ Visual comparison

```text
HOUSEHOLD PURCHASES        WHOLESALE GOODS          DOMESTIC PRODUCTION
        ↓                         ↓                         ↓
       CPI                       WPI                  GDP DEFLATOR

 Goods + services             Goods only          Final goods + services
 Retail/consumer stage      Wholesale stage       Produced within India
```

## 1. Consumer Price Index

✅ **CPI measures changes in retail prices paid by households for a representative basket of goods and services.**

Its basket includes categories such as:

- food;
- housing;
- clothing;
- transport;
- healthcare;
- education;
- communication and other services.

### Main uses

```text
Household cost of living
          +
Monetary-policy inflation target
          +
Wage, pension and allowance indexation
```

✅ India’s inflation-targeting framework uses **headline CPI**.

✅ CPI is compiled by the **National Statistical Office under MoSPI**.

✅ Current base: **2024=100**.

## 2. Wholesale Price Index

✅ **WPI measures average price changes for goods at the wholesale or first major transaction stage.**

It covers three broad groups:

| WPI group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary articles | Food articles, minerals and crude petroleum |
| Fuel and power | Coal, electricity and petroleum products |
| Manufactured products | Metals, chemicals, textiles and machinery |

### Critical limitation

```text
WPI = GOODS ONLY
Services = excluded
```

Thus, WPI cannot fully represent the inflation experienced by a service-consuming household.

✅ WPI is released by the **Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT**, Ministry of Commerce and Industry.

✅ Current base: **2022–23**.

### Main uses

- tracking wholesale goods-price pressure;
- studying producer and input-cost conditions;
- analysing manufacturing and commodity-price trends;
- deflating some nominal economic series.

## 3. GDP deflator

✅ The **GDP deflator** measures the price change of all final goods and services produced within the domestic economy.

```text
GDP deflator
= Nominal GDP
  ─────────── × 100
    Real GDP
```

### Example

```text
Nominal GDP = ₹220 lakh crore
Real GDP    = ₹200 lakh crore

GDP deflator = 220 ÷ 200 × 100 = 110
```

This indicates that the price level of domestic final output is approximately 10% above the base-year level.

### Coverage

```text
Consumption
+ Investment
+ Government output
+ Exports
− Imports
```

More precisely, imports are excluded because they are not produced domestically.

## 4. The decisive comparison

| Feature | CPI | WPI | GDP deflator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measures | Consumer prices | Wholesale goods prices | Prices of domestic final output |
| Goods | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Services | Yes | **No** | Yes |
| Imports | Can affect consumer basket | Imported inputs may affect transactions | **Excluded directly** |
| Exports | Generally not household consumption | Export-related goods may enter relevant transactions | **Included** |
| Basket | Representative household basket | Selected wholesale goods | All current domestic final output |
| Weights | Relatively fixed until revision | Relatively fixed until revision | Change with production composition |
| Frequency | Monthly | Monthly | Derived from national accounts |
| Main policy role | Inflation target and cost of living | Goods/input-price analysis | Economy-wide output-price measure |
| Current base | 2024 | 2022–23 | 2022–23 |

## 5. Fixed basket versus changing basket

CPI and WPI use specified baskets and weights between base revisions.

The GDP deflator is broader and its effective composition changes with what the economy currently produces.

```text
CPI/WPI:
Specified basket → fixed weights between revisions

GDP deflator:
Current domestic output → changing composition
```

Therefore, the GDP deflator captures new domestically produced goods and services more automatically.

## 6. Imported goods trap

Suppose the price of imported smartphones rises sharply.

### CPI

Households pay higher retail prices:

```text
Imported smartphone price ↑
             ↓
CPI may rise
```

### GDP deflator

Imported smartphones are not produced in India:

```text
Import price ↑
      ↓
Not directly included in GDP deflator
```

However, imported inputs can indirectly raise the prices of final goods produced domestically.

## 7. Why CPI and WPI diverge

CPI and WPI can move differently because of:

| Reason | Effect |
|---|---|
| Services | Included in CPI, excluded from WPI |
| Different weights | Same price shock has different impact |
| Retail margins | Affect CPI more directly |
| Transport and distribution costs | Create wholesale-retail gaps |
| Taxes and subsidies | May affect stages differently |
| Housing costs | Significant in CPI, absent from WPI |
| Time lag | Wholesale changes may reach retail prices later |

### Transmission example

```text
Crude oil price ↑
       ↓
WPI fuel component ↑
       ↓
Transport and production costs ↑
       ↓
Retail goods and services prices ↑
       ↓
CPI may rise with a lag
```

Transmission is not necessarily complete. Firms may absorb some input-cost increase by accepting lower profit margins.

## 8. Which index should be used?

| Question | Appropriate measure |
|---|---|
| What inflation do households broadly face? | CPI |
| What are wholesale goods prices doing? | WPI |
| How are prices changing across domestic final output? | GDP deflator |
| What should RBI formally target? | Headline CPI |
| How do we convert nominal GDP into real GDP? | GDP deflator |
| Are services showing persistent retail inflation? | CPI/core CPI, not WPI |

No index is universally “best.” The correct measure depends on the analytical question.

## 9. Nominal versus real GDP

```text
Nominal GDP growth
= change in quantities + change in prices

Real GDP growth
= change in quantities after removing price effects
```

Suppose nominal GDP grows by 10%, but the GDP deflator rises by approximately 6%.

Real output growth is roughly:

```text
10% − 6% ≈ 4%
```

This subtraction is an approximation. The exact relationship is multiplicative.

## 10. India’s 2026 statistical revisions

✅ Current official framework:

| Indicator | Revised base |
|---|---|
| CPI | 2024=100 |
| WPI | 2022–23 |
| National accounts/GDP | 2022–23 |

⚠️ These revisions modernise baskets, weights and datasets, but they also create **series breaks**. Older and newer index levels should not be mechanically joined without official linkage.

## UPSC traps

- ❌ CPI covers only goods.
  ✅ CPI covers goods and services.

- ❌ WPI measures the complete cost of living.
  ✅ It excludes services and does not directly measure household retail experience.

- ❌ GDP deflator includes imported final goods.
  ✅ It covers domestically produced final output.

- ❌ GDP deflator excludes exports.
  ✅ Exports are domestically produced and therefore included.

- ❌ RBI formally targets WPI inflation.
  ✅ It targets headline CPI inflation.

- ❌ One index is always superior.
  ✅ Suitability depends on the question being examined.

## Revision notes

1. CPI measures household retail prices.
2. WPI measures wholesale goods prices.
3. WPI excludes services.
4. The GDP deflator covers domestic final goods and services.
5. Imports are excluded directly from the GDP deflator.
6. Exports are included in the GDP deflator.
7. CPI and WPI use specified weighted baskets.
8. GDP-deflator composition changes with current output.
9. CPI–WPI divergence reflects weights, services, margins and transmission lags.
10. RBI formally targets headline CPI.
11. CPI is compiled by MoSPI; WPI by DPIIT.
12. The relevant index depends on the purpose of measurement.

## 🖼️ Inflation diagnosis map

```text
                         INFLATION
                             │
        ┌────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
        ↓                    ↓                     ↓
 Excess demand         Rising costs          Supply constraints
 DEMAND-PULL           COST-PUSH              STRUCTURAL
        │                    │                     │
Income/credit ↑        Wages, fuel,            Storage, logistics,
Govt spending ↑        inputs, taxes ↑         productivity gaps
                             │
                             ↓
                     IMPORTED INFLATION
                  Global prices ↑ / rupee ↓
```

An inflation episode can involve several causes simultaneously.

## 1. Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand grows faster than the economy’s productive capacity.

```text
Consumption + investment + government spending + net exports
                              ↑
                Aggregate demand rises
                              ↓
      Economy approaches capacity constraints
                              ↓
            Too much demand chasing
            insufficient current output
                              ↓
                     Prices rise
```

### Possible triggers

- rapid credit expansion;
- rising household income;
- tax reductions;
- large government expenditure;
- low interest rates;
- strong export demand;
- optimistic expectations.

### Output-gap test

```text
Actual output > sustainable potential output
                     ↓
           Positive output gap
                     ↓
      Labour and machinery become scarce
                     ↓
         Wages and prices accelerate
```

> 🔑 **Demand growth becomes inflationary mainly when supply cannot expand correspondingly.**

Strong consumption alone does not prove demand-pull inflation. Capacity utilisation, labour markets, core inflation and the output gap must also be examined.

## 2. Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation occurs when production costs rise and firms pass them to consumers.

```text
Input costs rise
       ↓
Cost of production rises
       ↓
Aggregate supply contracts
       ↓
Prices rise + output may fall
```

### Sources

| Cost shock | Indian example |
|---|---|
| Energy | Crude-oil or electricity costs increase |
| Raw materials | Steel, fertiliser or edible-oil prices rise |
| Wages | Wage growth exceeds productivity growth |
| Taxes | Higher indirect taxes increase production costs |
| Logistics | Freight and transport costs rise |
| Currency | Imported inputs become costlier after depreciation |

Cost-push inflation is especially difficult because it can produce:

```text
Higher inflation + lower output + unemployment
```

This combination may develop into **stagflation**.

## 3. Demand-pull versus cost-push

| Indicator | Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|---|
| Initial disturbance | Aggregate demand rises | Aggregate supply falls |
| Output | Usually rises initially | Usually falls |
| Employment | Usually improves initially | May weaken |
| Prices | Rise | Rise |
| Core inflation | Often broad and persistent | Depends on pass-through |
| Typical response | Demand restraint | Supply repair plus calibrated monetary response |

```text
Prices ↑ + output ↑  → likely demand pressure
Prices ↑ + output ↓  → likely adverse supply shock
```

This is a diagnostic rule, not an infallible test.

## 4. Food inflation

Food inflation frequently begins as a supply shock:

```text
Poor rainfall / heatwave / crop disease
                    ↓
             Agricultural output ↓
                    ↓
              Food prices ↑
```

Other causes include:

- storage losses;
- transport disruption;
- seasonal shortages;
- export-import restrictions;
- changes in minimum support prices;
- global edible-oil or fertiliser prices.

### Second-round effects

A temporary food shock can become broader:

```text
Food prices ↑
      ↓
Workers demand higher wages
      ↓
Service and production costs ↑
      ↓
Core inflation and expectations ↑
```

Thus, food inflation may begin as a relative-price shock but eventually generate general inflation.

## 5. Imported inflation

Imported inflation arises when foreign goods or inputs become costlier in domestic currency.

Two principal channels:

### Global-price channel

```text
World crude price ↑
        ↓
India's import bill ↑
        ↓
Fuel, freight and production costs ↑
        ↓
Domestic inflation ↑
```

### Exchange-rate channel

```text
₹80 per US$ → ₹88 per US$
           ↓
More rupees required per dollar
           ↓
Dollar-priced imports become costlier
           ↓
Imported inflation
```

Illustration:

| Dollar price of oil | Exchange rate | Rupee cost |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $100 | ₹80/$ | ₹8,000 |
| $100 | ₹88/$ | ₹8,800 |

The global dollar price remained unchanged, but rupee depreciation raised the domestic cost by 10%.

### Pass-through is not automatic

The final CPI effect depends on:

- taxes and subsidies;
- administered prices;
- firm profit margins;
- contracts and inventories;
- competitive conditions;
- strength and duration of the shock.

## 6. Structural inflation

Structural inflation reflects persistent supply rigidities that prevent production and distribution from responding efficiently to demand.

### Indian examples

```text
Agricultural demand changes
          ↓
Demand for milk, protein and vegetables ↑
          ↓
Supply responds slowly
          ↓
Persistent food inflation
```

Common bottlenecks include:

- fragmented agricultural markets;
- inadequate cold storage;
- weak transport and logistics;
- power shortages;
- low agricultural productivity;
- skill mismatches;
- urban housing constraints;
- slow supply response in health and education.

Unlike a temporary crop shock, structural inflation requires long-term reforms.

## 7. Built-in or wage-price inflation

Inflation can become self-sustaining through expectations:

```text
Expected inflation ↑
        ↓
Workers demand higher wages
        ↓
Firms' costs rise
        ↓
Firms increase prices
        ↓
Expected inflation rises further
```

This is the **wage-price spiral**.

However:

```text
Nominal wage growth > productivity growth
```

is more inflationary than wage growth supported by equal productivity gains.

## 8. Fiscal inflation

Government expenditure can contribute through two different routes.

### Demand route

```text
Government spending ↑
        ↓
Aggregate demand ↑
        ↓
Demand-pull pressure near full capacity
```

### Cost/supply route

Productive expenditure on roads, power, irrigation and logistics can expand capacity and reduce inflation over time.

Therefore:

❌ Every fiscal deficit is automatically inflationary.

✅ Its effect depends on:

- economic slack;
- method of financing;
- composition of spending;
- supply response;
- credibility and expectations.

## 9. Monetary inflation

Persistent excessive money and credit growth can support general inflation when it raises nominal demand beyond real productive capacity.

```text
Money and credit ↑
       ↓
Spending demand ↑
       ↓
If output cannot respond
       ↓
General inflation ↑
```

But money growth does not mechanically cause equal inflation if:

- money demand rises;
- banks do not lend;
- households save the funds;
- the economy has unused capacity;
- output expands.

## 10. Administered-price inflation

Government-regulated prices may be revised for:

- electricity;
- public transport;
- cooking fuel;
- fertiliser;
- procurement-linked commodities.

Such revisions can directly raise prices and indirectly increase other production costs.

This is not necessarily demand-pull inflation.

## 11. Current India anchor

✅ July 2026 CPI data showed food-price pressure exceeding overall headline inflation.

⚠️ Diagnosis:

```text
Food inflation > headline inflation
             ↓
Possible concentrated food-supply pressure
             ↓
Not sufficient alone to prove economy-wide
excess demand
```

A proper diagnosis would examine:

- which food groups drove the rise;
- crop and weather conditions;
- month-on-month momentum;
- core inflation;
- wages and capacity utilisation;
- global commodity and exchange-rate movements.

## 12. Matching policy to cause

| Dominant cause | More appropriate response |
|---|---|
| Excess aggregate demand | Monetary and fiscal restraint |
| Temporary vegetable shortage | Imports, buffer stocks and logistics |
| Oil-price shock | Tax calibration, diversification and efficiency |
| Rupee depreciation | Macroeconomic stability and calibrated intervention |
| Storage bottleneck | Warehousing and supply-chain reform |
| Unanchored expectations | Credible monetary policy and communication |

> 🔑 **UPSC answer principle:** Diagnose the source before prescribing the cure.

Interest-rate increases can restrain second-round demand and expectations, but they cannot produce onions, crude oil or rainfall.

## UPSC traps

- ❌ Every inflation episode is caused by excess money.
  ✅ Supply, structural and imported shocks can initiate inflation.

- ❌ Strong demand always causes inflation.
  ✅ Inflation intensifies when demand exceeds available capacity.

- ❌ Food inflation cannot affect core inflation.
  ✅ Wage and expectation effects can create second-round transmission.

- ❌ Currency depreciation is inflationary only when global prices rise.
  ✅ It can raise rupee import costs even with unchanged dollar prices.

- ❌ Every fiscal deficit is equally inflationary.
  ✅ Its effect depends on slack, financing and expenditure composition.

- ❌ Monetary policy can directly eliminate supply shortages.
  ✅ It mainly manages demand, expectations and second-round effects.

## Revision notes

1. Demand-pull inflation arises when demand exceeds productive capacity.
2. Cost-push inflation follows rising production costs or contracting supply.
3. Demand-pull tends to raise prices and output initially.
4. Cost-push can raise prices while reducing output.
5. Food shocks can spread through wages and expectations.
6. Global prices and currency depreciation generate imported inflation.
7. Pass-through depends on taxes, subsidies and firm margins.
8. Structural inflation reflects persistent supply bottlenecks.
9. Wage growth above productivity can sustain inflation.
10. Fiscal effects depend on economic slack and spending composition.
11. Monetary expansion is inflationary when nominal demand outruns output.
12. Policy must be matched to the inflation source.

## 🖼️ From temporary shock to persistent inflation

```text
Oil/food shock
      ↓
Direct price increase ─────────────── FIRST-ROUND EFFECT
      ↓
Transport and input costs
      ↓
Firms pass costs to consumers
      ↓
Workers expect continuing inflation
      ↓
Higher wage demands
      ↓
Firms raise prices again ──────────── SECOND-ROUND EFFECT
      ↓
Inflation becomes broad and persistent
```

## 1. Inflation transmission

**Transmission** is the process through which an initial shock spreads across sectors, prices and time.

Example:

```text
Crude oil price ↑
       ↓
Petrol/diesel and freight costs ↑
       ↓
Food distribution and factory costs ↑
       ↓
Retail goods prices ↑
       ↓
Wage demands and service prices ↑
```

A crude-price shock may therefore spread beyond fuel.

## 2. First-round effects

✅ **First-round effects** are the direct and immediate consequences of a shock.

| Initial shock | Direct effect |
|---|---|
| Poor harvest | Vegetable prices rise |
| Global crude-price rise | Fuel prices rise |
| Rupee depreciation | Imported goods become costlier |
| Electricity-tariff revision | Household electricity cost rises |

These effects may be temporary if the original shock reverses.

## 3. Second-round effects

✅ **Second-round effects** occur when the initial shock influences wages, expectations and prices in unrelated sectors.

```text
Food inflation
      ↓
Expected living cost ↑
      ↓
Wage demands ↑
      ↓
Restaurant, transport and service costs ↑
      ↓
Core inflation ↑
```

This converts a narrow relative-price shock into broader inflation.

> 🔑 **First round changes the affected price; second round changes price-setting behaviour.**

## 4. Cost pass-through

A producer facing higher input costs has three choices:

```text
Higher input cost
       │
       ├── Raise selling price → consumer bears cost
       ├── Reduce profit margin → firm bears cost
       └── Improve efficiency  → cost partly absorbed
```

### Degree of pass-through

| Condition | Likely pass-through |
|---|---|
| Strong consumer demand | Higher |
| Limited competition | Higher |
| Persistent cost shock | Higher |
| Weak demand | Lower |
| Strong competition | Lower |
| Temporary shock | Lower |
| Government tax/subsidy adjustment | May reduce or delay it |

Pass-through is rarely instantaneous or complete.

## 5. Inflation expectations

People make decisions partly according to what they expect future inflation to be.

```text
Expected inflation ↑
        ↓
Workers seek higher wages
Firms pre-emptively raise prices
Consumers advance purchases
Lenders demand higher interest
        ↓
Actual inflation may rise
```

Expectations can become partly **self-fulfilling**.

## 6. Anchored versus unanchored expectations

### Anchored expectations

People believe inflation will eventually return near the central bank’s target.

```text
Temporary food shock
        ↓
No major change in long-term wages/prices
        ↓
Shock fades
```

### Unanchored expectations

People believe high inflation will continue.

```text
Temporary shock
       ↓
Wages and contracts reset upward
       ↓
Broad price increases
       ↓
Persistent inflation
```

Credible policy helps keep expectations anchored.

## 7. How expectations are formed

| Type | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Adaptive | People extrapolate past inflation |
| Forward-looking | People consider policy, supply conditions and future risks |
| Rational | People use available information without systematic error |
| Salience-based | Frequently purchased items disproportionately influence perceptions |

In India, food and fuel prices can heavily influence household perceptions because they are visible and purchased frequently.

## 8. Persistence

✅ **Inflation persistence** means inflation remains elevated after the original shock should have faded.

Sources include:

- staggered wage contracts;
- rent and fee revisions;
- backward-looking indexation;
- repeated supply shocks;
- sticky service prices;
- unanchored expectations;
- wage-price feedback;
- delayed cost pass-through.

### Goods versus services

| Goods inflation | Services inflation |
|---|---|
| Often responds quickly to commodity prices | Often adjusts gradually |
| Supply can sometimes recover quickly | Wages form a large share of costs |
| Frequently more volatile | Often relatively sticky |
| May reverse sharply | Can signal persistence |

## 9. Inflation inertia

Inflation may continue simply because earlier inflation is built into present contracts.

```text
Last year’s inflation
        ↓
Current wage revision
        ↓
Current production cost
        ↓
Current price increase
        ↓
Next year’s wage revision
```

This is **inflation inertia** or built-in inflation.

## 10. Current RBI anchor

✅ RBI’s July 2026 Inflation Expectations Survey covered **5,905 households across 19 cities**.

| Survey measure | Median |
|---|---:|
| Current inflation perception | 7.8% |
| Three-month-ahead expectation | 9.2% |
| One-year-ahead expectation | 9.4% |

⚠️ These figures are:

- household perceptions and expectations;
- not official CPI inflation;
- not RBI’s inflation forecasts;
- based on surveyed urban households, not the complete Indian population.

The difference between perceived inflation and measured CPI may reflect the greater visibility of frequently purchased food, fuel and service items.

## 11. Why RBI monitors expectations

The RBI cannot reverse rainfall failure or produce crude oil. It can nevertheless prevent a temporary shock from spreading by:

- maintaining policy credibility;
- moderating excessive demand;
- communicating its inflation commitment;
- discouraging pre-emptive price and wage increases;
- keeping medium-term expectations anchored.

Monetary policy mainly targets the **generalisation and persistence** of supply shocks, not their physical source.

## 12. Mains answer chain

For a question on food inflation:

```text
Weather shock
→ food supply falls
→ headline inflation rises
→ household expectations rise
→ wage demands increase
→ service prices increase
→ core inflation becomes persistent
→ calibrated monetary + supply response required
```

## UPSC traps

- ❌ Every input-cost rise passes fully into CPI.
  ✅ Firms may absorb it through margins or efficiency.

- ❌ First-round and second-round effects are identical.
  ✅ Second-round effects spread through behaviour, wages and expectations.

- ❌ Household inflation expectations equal official CPI.
  ✅ They are subjective survey perceptions.

- ❌ A temporary supply shock requires no monetary attention.
  ✅ Policy may be needed if expectations and second-round effects emerge.

- ❌ High expectations necessarily produce inflation.
  ✅ They increase the risk; credibility and weak demand may interrupt transmission.

## Revision notes

1. Transmission explains how inflation spreads through the economy.
2. First-round effects are direct consequences of the original shock.
3. Second-round effects operate through wages, expectations and wider pricing.
4. Pass-through can be partial, delayed or absorbed in margins.
5. Strong demand and low competition increase pass-through.
6. Expected inflation influences present wage and pricing decisions.
7. Anchored expectations limit persistence.
8. Unanchored expectations can make inflation self-reinforcing.
9. Services inflation is often stickier than goods inflation.
10. Indexation and staggered contracts generate inflation inertia.
11. Household perceptions are not the same as measured CPI.
12. Monetary credibility prevents temporary shocks from becoming general inflation.

## 🖼️ Distributional effects

```text
Unexpected inflation
        │
        ├── Cash and fixed income lose purchasing power
        ├── Savers/lenders lose if interest is fixed
        ├── Borrowers gain as real debt burden falls
        ├── Flexible-income groups adjust more easily
        └── Poor households suffer more from food inflation
```

Inflation is not merely an average price increase. It redistributes income and wealth.

## 1. Purchasing power

```text
Real purchasing power
= Nominal income adjusted for inflation
```

Suppose income rises by 5% while prices rise by 8%:

```text
Nominal income ↑ 5%
Price level    ↑ 8%
Real income    ↓ approximately 3%
```

The exact real-income change is:

```text
1.05
──── − 1 ≈ −2.78%
1.08
```

> 🔑 **Nominal income growth does not guarantee greater purchasing power.**

## 2. Who suffers most?

| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| Fixed pensioners | Income adjusts slowly while prices rise |
| Daily-wage workers | Depends on bargaining power and wage adjustment |
| Poor households | Essentials occupy a large expenditure share |
| Cash holders | Real value of money balances declines |
| Fixed-rate lenders | Repayment buys fewer goods |
| Informal workers | Often lack automatic indexation |

Food inflation is particularly regressive because poorer households spend a larger proportion of income on food.

## 3. Borrowers versus lenders

Suppose a borrower takes ₹1 lakh at a fixed 6% interest rate.

If unexpected inflation rises to 10%:

```text
Real interest rate ≈ 6% − 10% = −4%
```

The money repaid has lower purchasing power.

```text
Unexpected inflation
        ↓
Borrower gains
Lender loses
```

If inflation is fully anticipated, lenders may demand a higher nominal interest rate in advance.

## 4. Fisher relationship

```text
Nominal interest rate
≈ Real interest rate + Expected inflation
```

If lenders require a 3% real return and expect 5% inflation:

```text
Required nominal rate ≈ 3% + 5% = 8%
```

The exact formula is:

```text
(1 + nominal rate)
= (1 + real rate)(1 + expected inflation)
```

The subtraction rule is a useful approximation at moderate rates.

## 5. Savings

High or unpredictable inflation can discourage conventional financial saving because future purchasing power becomes uncertain.

Households may shift from:

```text
Cash and fixed deposits
          ↓
Gold, land, housing or inflation-protected assets
```

Possible consequences include:

- reduced supply of long-term financial savings;
- greater speculative asset demand;
- inefficient capital allocation;
- pressure on external balances when imported gold demand rises.

However, positive real deposit rates can preserve incentives to save financially.

## 6. Investment and business decisions

### Moderate, stable inflation

A low and predictable positive inflation rate can coexist with growth because firms can plan prices, wages and investment.

### High or volatile inflation

```text
Inflation uncertainty ↑
          ↓
Future costs and returns harder to estimate
          ↓
Risk premium and borrowing costs ↑
          ↓
Long-term investment may decline
```

Firms may favour short-term or speculative activity over productive long-term investment.

## 7. Relative-price distortion

Inflation does not raise all prices simultaneously or equally.

```text
One price rises first
        ↓
Firms and households cannot tell:
real scarcity signal OR general inflation?
        ↓
Production and consumption decisions become distorted
```

The price system becomes less informative.

## 8. Menu and shoe-leather costs

### Menu costs

Costs incurred when firms repeatedly revise:

- catalogues;
- labels;
- contracts;
- software prices;
- accounting systems.

### Shoe-leather costs

When cash loses value rapidly, people make greater efforts to minimise cash balances:

- more frequent bank transactions;
- accelerated spending;
- greater liquidity management.

The term is metaphorical; physical shoes need not literally wear out.

## 9. Inflation tax

Inflation reduces the real value of currency held by the public.

```text
Government creates money
          ↓
General prices rise
          ↓
Real value of existing money balances falls
```

This purchasing-power loss is called an **inflation tax**, although it is not a formally legislated tax.

The associated revenue from money creation is called **seigniorage**.

## 10. Taxation effects

### Bracket creep or fiscal drag

If tax thresholds are not indexed:

```text
Nominal wages rise with inflation
          ↓
Taxpayer enters higher tax bracket
          ↓
Tax burden rises despite little real-income gain
```

### Capital-gains distortion

Part of a nominal asset-price increase may merely compensate for inflation, yet taxation may treat it as a gain unless indexation is provided.

## 11. Government finances

Inflation can initially:

- increase nominal tax revenue;
- reduce the real value of fixed-rate public debt;
- raise seigniorage.

But it may also:

- increase subsidy expenditure;
- raise wages and pensions;
- increase borrowing costs;
- raise project and procurement costs;
- weaken fiscal credibility.

Thus, inflation is not an effortless fiscal solution.

## 12. External competitiveness

If domestic prices rise faster than trading partners’ prices, without compensating productivity gains or exchange-rate adjustment:

```text
Indian goods become relatively expensive
          ↓
Exports may weaken
Imports become relatively attractive
          ↓
Trade balance may deteriorate
```

Currency depreciation can restore price competitiveness but may simultaneously worsen imported inflation.

## 13. Inflation and growth

The relationship is nonlinear:

```text
Very low/deflationary inflation
→ demand postponement and stagnation risk

Low and stable positive inflation
→ planning and price adjustment possible

High and volatile inflation
→ uncertainty, distortion and weaker investment

Hyperinflation
→ monetary and contractual breakdown
```

Therefore, price stability does not mean permanently zero inflation. It means inflation sufficiently low and stable that it does not materially distort decisions.

## 14. Anticipated versus unanticipated inflation

| Anticipated inflation | Unanticipated inflation |
|---|---|
| Contracts can adjust | Arbitrary redistribution |
| Nominal rates incorporate expectations | Fixed-rate lenders lose |
| Wages may be indexed | Fixed-income groups suffer |
| Still creates transaction costs | Causes greater uncertainty |

Unexpected and volatile inflation is generally more damaging than equally sized predictable inflation.

## 15. Current India anchor

✅ RBI’s July 2026 survey reported household median current inflation perception of **7.8%**, above measured headline CPI inflation.

⚠️ This does not establish that official CPI is incorrect. Household perception can differ because:

- frequently purchased essentials are more salient;
- different households consume different baskets;
- local prices differ;
- households may remember price increases more than decreases.

This matters because perceived inflation can influence wage demands, saving choices and consumption even when measured inflation is lower.

## UPSC traps

- ❌ Inflation harms everyone equally.
  ✅ Effects depend on income adjustment, assets, debt and consumption basket.

- ❌ Borrowers always lose from inflation.
  ✅ Unexpected inflation can benefit fixed-rate borrowers.

- ❌ Nominal wage growth means real income growth.
  ✅ Wage growth must exceed inflation.

- ❌ Inflation automatically increases saving.
  ✅ It may shift savings toward physical assets.

- ❌ All inflation damages growth equally.
  ✅ high, volatile and unexpected inflation is especially harmful.

- ❌ Inflation tax is an ordinary statutory tax.
  ✅ It is the erosion of real money balances.

## Revision notes

1. Inflation reduces purchasing power when income adjustment lags.
2. Poor households are especially exposed to essential-goods inflation.
3. Unexpected inflation redistributes from fixed-rate lenders to borrowers.
4. Real interest is approximately nominal interest minus inflation.
5. Inflation can divert savings toward gold, land and housing.
6. Volatility weakens long-term investment and planning.
7. Uneven price changes distort scarcity signals.
8. Menu costs arise from repeated price revision.
9. Shoe-leather costs arise from avoiding depreciating cash.
10. Inflation tax erodes the real value of money balances.
11. Unindexed tax thresholds produce bracket creep.
12. High domestic inflation can weaken external competitiveness.
13. Stable moderate inflation is less harmful than volatile inflation.
14. Perceived inflation can affect behaviour even when it differs from CPI.

## 🖼️ Business-cycle phases

```text
Economic
activity
   ▲                         PEAK
   │                       /\
   │          EXPANSION   /  \   CONTRACTION
Trend ───────────────────/────\────────────────
   │                   /        \
   │             TROUGH          \ RECOVERY
   │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────► Time
```

A business cycle is the recurring fluctuation of economic activity around its long-term trend.

## 1. Trend growth versus cyclical growth

```text
Long-term trend
= growth in productive capacity

Business cycle
= temporary movement around that trend
```

Trend growth depends on:

- labour-force growth;
- capital formation;
- productivity;
- technology;
- institutions.

Cyclical movement depends on changes in:

- aggregate demand;
- credit;
- inventories;
- investment;
- expectations;
- external shocks.

An economy can grow while experiencing a cyclical slowdown if growth falls below its trend rate.

## 2. Expansion

During expansion:

```text
Demand ↑ → Production ↑ → Employment ↑
             ↓
        Income and profits ↑
             ↓
        Further demand ↑
```

Typical features:

- rising output;
- falling unemployment;
- stronger investment;
- improving profits;
- expanding credit;
- increasing capacity utilisation.

Inflation may remain stable initially if unused capacity exists.

## 3. Peak or boom

The peak is the upper turning point.

```text
Economy approaches capacity
            ↓
Labour and input shortages
            ↓
Wages and costs accelerate
            ↓
Inflation and interest rates rise
```

Possible symptoms include:

- positive output gap;
- labour shortages;
- high capacity utilisation;
- asset-price exuberance;
- excessive leverage;
- broad demand-pull inflation.

A high GDP growth rate alone does not prove an economy is overheating.

## 4. Contraction or recession

During contraction:

```text
Demand ↓
   ↓
Sales and profits ↓
   ↓
Production and investment ↓
   ↓
Employment and income ↓
   ↓
Demand falls further
```

Typical features:

- weakening output;
- rising unemployment;
- falling capacity utilisation;
- declining credit growth;
- inventory accumulation;
- lower inflationary pressure.

A contraction becomes severe and prolonged when adverse feedback loops dominate.

## 5. Trough and recovery

The trough is the lower turning point.

Recovery may begin through:

- inventory rebuilding;
- lower interest rates;
- fiscal support;
- improving exports;
- replacement investment;
- restored confidence.

```text
Demand stabilises
       ↓
Inventories clear
       ↓
Production restarts
       ↓
Employment and income recover
```

## 6. Potential and actual output

✅ **Potential output** is the sustainable level of production consistent with normal use of resources and stable inflation.

It is not the absolute physical maximum.

### Output gap

```text
Output gap
= Actual output − Potential output
  ─────────────────────────────── × 100
          Potential output
```

### Negative output gap

```text
Actual output < Potential output
```

Consequences:

- unemployed resources;
- weak wage pressure;
- subdued demand;
- generally lower inflation.

### Positive output gap

```text
Actual output > Sustainable potential output
```

Consequences:

- capacity strain;
- labour shortages;
- wage pressure;
- demand-pull inflation risk.

Potential output cannot be directly observed; it must be estimated.

## 7. Business cycle and unemployment

During contraction:

```text
Output growth ↓ → Labour demand ↓ → Unemployment ↑
```

During expansion, the reverse commonly occurs.

This inverse relationship is associated with **Okun’s law**:

> Faster output growth relative to potential generally reduces cyclical unemployment.

It is an empirical tendency, not a fixed law or universal numerical ratio.

## 8. Types of unemployment across the cycle

| Type | Meaning | Cyclical? |
|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Time spent changing or finding jobs | No |
| Structural | Skills or locations do not match vacancies | No |
| Seasonal | Work varies by season | No |
| Cyclical | Insufficient aggregate demand | Yes |

Demand stimulus is most directly effective against cyclical unemployment. Structural unemployment needs reskilling and labour-market reforms.

## 9. Inflation–unemployment relationship

### Short-run Phillips curve

During demand-led expansion:

```text
Demand ↑
  ↓
Output and employment ↑
  ↓
Unemployment ↓
  ↓
Wages and inflation may ↑
```

This suggests a short-run inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation.

### Why it is not permanent

Once workers and firms adjust their inflation expectations:

```text
Expected inflation ↑
        ↓
Higher wages demanded at every employment level
        ↓
Short-run Phillips curve shifts upward
```

There is no stable permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment.

## 10. Stagflation exception

An adverse supply shock can produce:

```text
Costs ↑
  ↓
Prices ↑ + Output ↓
  ↓
Inflation ↑ + Unemployment ↑
```

This is **stagflation**.

It disproves the claim that inflation and unemployment must always move in opposite directions.

## 11. Indicators

| Type | Examples | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | New orders, credit conditions, stock prices, expectations | Move before economy |
| Coincident | Output, sales, employment, income | Move with economy |
| Lagging | Unemployment duration, bad loans, some wage measures | Respond later |

No single indicator establishes the cycle. Analysts use a dashboard.

## 12. Inventories and acceleration

Suppose firms expect high demand and produce aggressively, but sales disappoint:

```text
Unsold inventories ↑
        ↓
New production orders ↓
        ↓
Output and employment weaken
```

When inventories become too low during recovery, restocking can amplify expansion.

Investment also depends on changes in demand:

```text
Demand growth accelerates
          ↓
Firms require additional capacity
          ↓
Investment rises disproportionately
```

This is the **accelerator principle**.

## 13. Procyclical and countercyclical policy

| Policy | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Countercyclical | Leans against the cycle | Fiscal support during recession |
| Procyclical | Reinforces the cycle | Spending cuts during deep recession |
| Acyclical | Unrelated to cycle | Neutral structural measure |

Automatic stabilisers—such as progressive taxes and unemployment support—can moderate the cycle without a new policy decision.

## 14. Current India anchor

✅ MoSPI reported **7.8% year-on-year real GDP growth** for April–June 2026.

⚠️ This supports an inference of strong economic momentum, but it does not independently prove:

- the economy is at its peak;
- actual output exceeds potential;
- demand-pull inflation exists;
- unemployment is universally low.

Diagnosis also requires:

- capacity utilisation;
- investment and consumption composition;
- labour-market data;
- core inflation;
- credit growth;
- estimates of potential output.

> 🔑 **High growth describes speed; the output gap describes pressure relative to capacity.**

## UPSC traps

- ❌ A slowdown means output necessarily contracts.
  ✅ Output may continue growing at a lower rate.

- ❌ Potential output is the economy’s absolute maximum.
  ✅ It is sustainable output without destabilising inflation.

- ❌ High GDP growth automatically proves overheating.
  ✅ Growth must be assessed relative to potential and capacity.

- ❌ All unemployment is cyclical.
  ✅ Structural and frictional unemployment may persist during expansion.

- ❌ Inflation and unemployment always move inversely.
  ✅ Stagflation can raise both.

- ❌ Two negative quarters are India’s legally binding recession definition.
  ✅ It is a popular technical rule, not a complete official diagnosis.

## Revision notes

1. Business cycles are fluctuations around long-term trend output.
2. The major phases are expansion, peak, contraction and trough.
3. A slowdown can occur despite positive growth.
4. Potential output is sustainable, not absolute maximum output.
5. A negative output gap indicates economic slack.
6. A positive output gap creates overheating risk.
7. Output gaps are estimated rather than directly observed.
8. Cyclical unemployment rises during contraction.
9. Okun’s law links output weakness with unemployment.
10. The Phillips relationship is mainly short-run and expectation-dependent.
11. Stagflation combines inflation with weak output and employment.
12. Inventories and investment can amplify cycles.
13. Countercyclical policy leans against fluctuations.
14. Multiple indicators are needed to identify the cycle.

## 🖼️ Two-arm inflation strategy

```text
                     INFLATION
                         │
        ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
        ↓                                 ↓
Broad demand, expectations         Food/fuel/supply shock
        ↓                                 ↓
       RBI                         Union/State governments
        │                                 │
Interest rates                    Buffers and imports
Liquidity                         Taxes and subsidies
Communication                     Logistics and storage
Credit conditions                 Production reforms
        └────────────────┬────────────────┘
                         ↓
               Coordinated response
```

## 1. Flexible inflation targeting

India follows **flexible inflation targeting**:

```text
Primary objective: price stability
              +
Growth considered while pursuing stability
```

✅ Official framework for **1 April 2026–31 March 2031**:

| Element | Provision |
|---|---|
| Measure | All-India headline CPI inflation |
| Point target | **4%** |
| Upper tolerance limit | **6%** |
| Lower tolerance limit | **2%** |

> 🔑 The objective is **4%**, not simply any inflation rate between 2% and 6%. The band permits temporary shocks and measurement uncertainty.

## 2. Why not zero inflation?

Zero inflation can create risks:

- reduced room for real-wage adjustment;
- greater chance of deflation;
- weaker monetary-policy space;
- measurement problems;
- postponement of consumption and investment.

A low positive target provides a buffer against deflation while preserving purchasing power.

## 3. Monetary Policy Committee

The six-member MPC determines the policy repo rate.

```text
3 RBI members + 3 Central Government appointees
```

Key features:

- each member has one vote;
- decisions are by majority;
- the RBI Governor has a casting vote in a tie;
- resolutions and voting records are published;
- the committee must meet at least four times yearly.

This structure combines institutional accountability with expert deliberation.

## 4. Failure condition

The RBI is deemed to have failed to meet the inflation target if average CPI inflation is:

```text
Above 6%
    OR
Below 2%
for three consecutive quarters
```

It must then submit a report to the Central Government explaining:

1. reasons for failure;
2. proposed remedial action;
3. estimated time required to restore inflation to target.

❌ A single monthly CPI reading above 6% does not automatically constitute formal failure.

## 5. Repo-rate channel

The repo rate influences the cost at which banks obtain short-term funds from the RBI.

### Rate increase

```text
Repo rate ↑
     ↓
Money-market and bank lending rates ↑
     ↓
Credit, consumption and investment moderate
     ↓
Aggregate demand and pricing power weaken
     ↓
Inflation pressure falls with a lag
```

### Rate reduction

```text
Repo rate ↓
     ↓
Borrowing conditions ease
     ↓
Demand and investment may strengthen
```

Transmission is neither immediate nor complete.

## 6. Other monetary-policy channels

| Channel | How it affects inflation |
|---|---|
| Interest-rate channel | Changes borrowing and saving incentives |
| Credit channel | Changes availability and cost of bank finance |
| Expectations channel | Signals commitment to price stability |
| Exchange-rate channel | Rate changes influence capital flows and import costs |
| Asset-price channel | Alters wealth, collateral and spending |
| Liquidity channel | Influences short-term funding conditions |

### Major operating instruments

- repo rate;
- Standing Deposit Facility;
- Marginal Standing Facility;
- open-market operations;
- variable-rate repo/reverse-repo operations;
- Cash Reserve Ratio;
- communication and forward guidance.

## 7. Monetary-policy limitations

Monetary tightening cannot directly:

- produce food;
- improve rainfall;
- unload blocked ports;
- create crude oil;
- build cold storage immediately.

Its role during supply inflation is to:

- contain second-round demand;
- anchor expectations;
- prevent broad generalisation;
- limit exchange-rate and wage-price feedback.

Excessive tightening against a temporary supply shock may unnecessarily reduce output and employment.

## 8. Government supply-side measures

| Inflation source | Possible response |
|---|---|
| Food shortage | Release buffer stocks |
| Domestic supply deficit | Temporarily facilitate imports |
| Excess exports during shortage | Calibrated export restrictions |
| Hoarding | Stock-limit enforcement and monitoring |
| Logistics disruption | Improve transport and market access |
| High fuel costs | Adjust taxes or subsidies selectively |
| Agricultural volatility | Irrigation, storage and crop diversification |
| Market fragmentation | Agricultural marketing reforms |

These measures address physical availability more directly than interest-rate changes.

## 9. Buffer stocks

```text
Government procurement during surplus
                  ↓
             Buffer stock
                  ↓
Release during shortage or price spike
                  ↓
Market supply ↑ → price pressure moderates
```

### Risks

- fiscal and storage costs;
- spoilage;
- distorted production incentives;
- crowding out private trade;
- poorly timed releases.

Buffers are stabilisation instruments, not substitutes for productivity reform.

## 10. Trade-policy tools

### Lower import duty

```text
Imported supply becomes cheaper
              ↓
Domestic availability rises
              ↓
Price pressure may fall
```

### Export restriction

```text
More domestic availability
              ↓
Short-run domestic prices may moderate
```

But frequent restrictions can:

- hurt farmers;
- undermine policy credibility;
- discourage investment;
- disrupt global buyers;
- reduce future supply response.

These measures should therefore be temporary and predictable.

## 11. Fiscal policy

### Demand management

During overheating:

- reduce unproductive expenditure;
- improve fiscal consolidation;
- withdraw broad demand stimulus.

### Supply expansion

Productive public expenditure on:

- logistics;
- power;
- irrigation;
- transport;
- warehousing;

can reduce structural inflation while supporting potential growth.

### Subsidy dilemma

Subsidies may protect consumers temporarily but can:

- burden public finances;
- conceal price signals;
- encourage overconsumption;
- delay adjustment.

Targeted support is generally less distortionary than universal suppression.

## 12. Policy mix by inflation type

| Inflation diagnosis | Main response |
|---|---|
| Demand-pull | Monetary tightening and fiscal restraint |
| Temporary vegetable shock | Supply release, imports and logistics |
| Persistent food inflation | Agricultural productivity and market reform |
| Imported fuel inflation | Tax calibration, diversification and efficiency |
| Unanchored expectations | Credible monetary action and communication |
| Stagflation | Calibrated mix; avoid crushing output |
| Deflation/recession | Monetary and fiscal easing |

## 13. Policy trade-offs

### Growth–inflation trade-off

```text
Rate increase
   ├── Inflation pressure ↓
   └── Credit, output and employment may weaken
```

### Exchange-rate trade-off

Higher rates may support the currency and reduce imported inflation, but can raise borrowing costs.

### Food-policy trade-off

Export restrictions can lower consumer prices but reduce farmers’ returns.

### Fuel-policy trade-off

Tax cuts reduce inflation but lower government revenue.

A strong UPSC answer must identify both benefit and cost.

| “Repo rate solves crop shortage” | Monetary policy cannot create supply |

| “All deficit financing is equally inflationary” | Direct monetisation expands reserve money most directly |

| “Strong demand always causes inflation” | Productive capacity may expand |

## 3. Deficit-financing trap

Possible deficit financing:

```text
Borrow from public
Borrow from banks
External borrowing
Asset sales
Direct central-bank monetisation
```

Direct monetisation is generally the most inflationary because:

```text
Central bank creates reserve money
              ↓
Government spends it
              ↓
Monetary base and nominal demand rise directly
```

⚠️ Final inflation still depends on economic slack and supply response. It is not an automatic one-for-one relationship.

## 4. Demand-pull identification

Likely demand-pull factors:

- expansionary fiscal spending near capacity;
- rapid credit growth;
- lower taxes raising disposable income;
- strong investment and consumption;
- rising export demand.

Not necessarily demand-pull:

- crop failure;
- global crude-price rise;
- rupee depreciation;
- logistics disruption;
- indirect-tax increase.

These are principally supply or cost shocks.

## 6. 2019 growth with low inflation framework

Low inflation alongside strong growth may reflect:

- productivity gains;
- improved supply;
- lower commodity prices;
- unused capacity;
- credible monetary policy.

But the combination must be tested for:

- rural wage and demand weakness;
- output-gap conditions;
- sectoral divergence;
- measurement and base effects;
- employment quality.

> **Balanced verdict:** Low inflation is beneficial when supply-led; it may indicate weak demand when accompanied by unemployment and unused capacity.

## 7. 2022 inflation–unemployment framework

Welfare transfers protect consumption but cannot independently solve inflation or unemployment.

```text
Immediate protection
Transfers + food support + indexation
                ↓
Inflation control
Supply reform + credible monetary policy
                ↓
Employment creation
Investment + MSMEs + skills + labour-intensive sectors
```

### Critical trade-off

Aggressive demand compression may lower inflation but worsen:

- MSME credit stress;
- employment;
- investment;
- incomes.

Policy should distinguish cyclical unemployment from structural unemployment and supply inflation from demand inflation.
