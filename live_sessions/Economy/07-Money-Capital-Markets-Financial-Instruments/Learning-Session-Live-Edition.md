## 🖼️ Financial-system flow

```text
SURPLUS UNITS                              DEFICIT UNITS
Households and institutions               Government and businesses
          │                                         ▲
          │ savings                                 │ finance
          ▼                                         │
   FINANCIAL SYSTEM ─────────────────────────────────┘
          │
          ├─ Banks and intermediaries
          └─ Financial markets and instruments
```

A financial system channels savings from those possessing surplus funds to those needing funds for consumption, investment or public expenditure.

## 1. What is a financial market?

A **financial market** is an arrangement through which financial claims are issued or traded.

It need not be a physical location. Transactions may occur electronically through exchanges, auction platforms or negotiated markets.

### Core functions

| Function | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Mobilisation of savings** | Converts idle savings into investible funds |
| **Capital allocation** | Directs funds towards competing users |
| **Price discovery** | Determines interest rates, yields and security prices |
| **Liquidity** | Permits investors to exit before final maturity |
| **Risk allocation** | Transfers risk to participants willing to bear it |
| **Monetary transmission** | Carries RBI policy and liquidity conditions into market rates |

## 2. Direct versus indirect finance

```text
DIRECT FINANCE

Saver ──buys bond/share──► Issuer
Issuer receives funds directly through a market claim

INDIRECT FINANCE

Saver ──deposit──► Bank ──loan──► Borrower
                  intermediary
```

| Direct finance | Indirect finance |
|---|---|
| Issuer sells a security | Intermediary accepts funds and lends them |
| Bond or share is created | Deposit and loan are separate contracts |
| Investor bears market/issuer risk | Bank evaluates and transforms risk |
| Example: corporate bond | Example: bank loan |

A mutual fund is also an intermediary, but unlike a bank deposit it pools investor money into market securities and does not ordinarily guarantee the investment’s value.

## 3. What is a financial instrument?

A **financial instrument** is a contract that creates:

- a financial asset for one party; and
- a corresponding liability or equity claim for another.

```text
Company issues bond
        │
        ├─ Investor: financial asset
        └─ Company: financial liability
```

For an equity share:

```text
Investor receives ownership claim
Company records equity—not a repayable debt liability
```

## 4. Financial versus physical assets

| Financial asset | Physical/real asset |
|---|---|
| Contractual claim | Tangible or productive resource |
| Bond, deposit, share, Treasury Bill | Land, machinery, gold, vehicle |
| Has a counterparty claim | Can exist without another party owing payment |
| May represent debt or ownership | Represents direct control of a real asset |

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** A financed car does not become a financial instrument. The **loan contract** is financial; the car is a physical asset and may serve as collateral.

Gold is normally a real asset. A gold ETF unit or Sovereign Gold Bond is a financial instrument linked to gold.

## 5. Six-question instrument framework

For every unfamiliar instrument, ask:

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **Who issues it?** | Government, bank, company or fund |
| **What is its maturity?** | Money-market or capital-market tendency |
| **What claim does it give?** | Debt, ownership, pooled claim or derivative |
| **Is it collateralised?** | Nature of credit and counterparty risk |
| **Can it be traded?** | Liquidity and price-discovery potential |
| **Who regulates it?** | RBI, SEBI, IFSCA or another authority |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Issuer–Maturity–Claim–Collateral–Trade–Regulator.**

## 6. Apply the framework

| Instrument | Issuer | Maturity/character | Claim | Regulator/architecture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Treasury Bill | Central Government | Short-term | Government debt | RBI-managed market |
| Commercial Paper | Eligible company | Short-term | Unsecured debt | RBI framework |
| Certificate of Deposit | Eligible bank/institution | Short-term | Debt claim | RBI framework |
| Equity share | Company | No maturity | Ownership | SEBI securities market |
| Corporate bond | Company | Usually medium/long-term | Debt | SEBI market framework |
| Mutual-fund unit | Mutual fund | Pooled investment | Proportionate portfolio claim | SEBI |

## 7. Maturity is important—but not sufficient

A common initial classification is:

```text
Up to one year       → Money market
Beyond one year      → Capital market
No maturity/equity   → Capital market
```

But classification must also consider:

- instrument purpose;
- claim structure;
- issuer;
- market convention;
- regulatory framework.

For example, equity has no repayment maturity but belongs to the capital market because it supplies long-term risk capital.

## 8. Primary and secondary functions

```text
PRIMARY MARKET
New security issued
        ↓
Fresh funds reach issuer

SECONDARY MARKET
Existing security traded
        ↓
Funds normally move between investors
```

A secondary market does not ordinarily provide fresh money to the original issuer. It nevertheless helps issuers indirectly by providing:

- investor exit;
- continuous valuation;
- liquidity;
- lower liquidity premium;
- greater willingness to buy new issues.

## 9. Risk is multidimensional

| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Credit risk** | Issuer may not repay |
| **Market risk** | Price may change adversely |
| **Interest-rate risk** | Bond price may fall when rates rise |
| **Liquidity risk** | Instrument may be difficult to sell |
| **Rollover risk** | Short-term funding may not be renewed |
| **Operational/settlement risk** | Trading or payment systems may fail |

⚠️ A liquid instrument is not automatically safe.
⚠️ A short-maturity instrument is not automatically risk-free.
⚠️ A government security can have negligible domestic credit risk but still carry price risk.

## 11. Why UPSC cares

UPSC often presents unfamiliar assets and asks whether they are:

- financial instruments;
- money-market instruments;
- government debt;
- collateral;
- primary or secondary-market transactions.

Instead of memorising names, apply the six-question framework.

## Revision notes

1. Financial markets transfer funds and allocate risk.
2. Direct finance creates a direct market claim on the issuer.
3. Indirect finance operates through an intermediary.
4. A financial instrument creates a financial claim.
5. Physical collateral does not itself become a financial instrument.
6. Debt promises payment; equity provides ownership.
7. Primary issuance gives fresh funds to the issuer.
8. Secondary trading generally transfers ownership between investors.
9. Secondary-market liquidity can lower primary financing costs.
10. Analyse every instrument through issuer, maturity, claim, collateral, tradability and regulator.
11. Liquidity, safety and return are separate dimensions.
12. Government securities may still carry interest-rate and market-price risk.

**Learner question / doubt**

> explain commercial paper and certificate of bond

There is no standard instrument called a **“certificate of bond.”** You likely mean **Certificate of Deposit (CD)**. Here is the distinction:

| Feature | Commercial Paper (CP) | Certificate of Deposit (CD) | Bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Eligible companies and financial institutions | Banks and eligible financial institutions | Government or companies |
| Purpose | Short-term corporate funding | Short-term institutional funding | Medium/long-term borrowing |
| Market | Money market | Money market | Capital market |
| Maturity | Short-term, up to one year | Short-term, up to one year | Usually beyond one year |
| Security | Normally unsecured | Unsecured deposit instrument | May be secured or unsecured |
| Return | Issued at discount, redeemed at face value | Usually issued at discount | Interest/coupon or discount |
| Main risk | Corporate default and rollover risk | Issuing institution’s credit risk | Credit and interest-rate risk |

```text
Company needs short-term money → Commercial Paper
Bank needs short-term money    → Certificate of Deposit
Issuer needs longer funding    → Bond
```

### Example

A company issues a ₹1 lakh CP for ₹97,000 and repays ₹1 lakh at maturity. The ₹3,000 difference is the investor’s return.

A CD operates similarly, but its issuer is a **bank or eligible financial institution**, not an ordinary company.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **CP = Company Paper; CD = Deposit issued by banks; Bond = Bigger tenure.**

---

**Learner question / doubt**

> what here means unsecured?

**Unsecured means no specific asset is pledged as collateral.**

```text
Secured borrowing
Loan/bond → backed by identified property or securities
Default → lender may enforce that collateral

Unsecured borrowing
CP/CD → based mainly on issuer’s creditworthiness
Default → investor has no specific pledged asset to seize
```

For example, a company issuing **Commercial Paper** does not mortgage a factory specifically against that CP. Investors rely on the company’s financial strength and repayment capacity.

Unsecured does **not** mean there is no legal repayment obligation. The investor remains a creditor and can pursue legal recovery, but lacks priority over a designated collateral asset. Hence CP is generally issued by financially sound, highly rated entities.

## 🖼️ Two independent classifications

```text
CLASSIFICATION 1: By maturity/purpose
Money market  ↔  Capital market

CLASSIFICATION 2: By transaction stage
Primary market ↔ Secondary market
```

These classifications answer different questions:

- **Money/capital:** What is the instrument’s maturity and financing purpose?
- **Primary/secondary:** Is it newly issued or already circulating?

## 1. Money market

The **money market** deals primarily with short-term funds and instruments having original maturity of **up to one year**.

```text
Temporary liquidity need
          ↓
Short-term instrument
          ↓
Money market
```

### Principal purposes

- working-capital finance;
- temporary government borrowing;
- bank liquidity management;
- monetary-policy transmission;
- parking short-term surplus funds.

### Important instruments

| Instrument | Issuer/participants | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Call money | Banks and eligible institutions | Overnight unsecured funds |
| Notice money | Eligible institutions | 2–14 days |
| Term money | Eligible institutions | Beyond 14 days and up to one year |
| Treasury Bill | Central Government | Short-term sovereign debt |
| Commercial Paper | Eligible companies | Unsecured short-term debt |
| Certificate of Deposit | Banks/eligible institutions | Negotiable short-term debt |
| Repo/TREPS | Market participants | Collateralised short-term borrowing |

## 2. Capital market

The **capital market** provides medium- and long-term finance and includes equity, which ordinarily has no maturity.

```text
Long-term investment need
          ↓
Equity or longer-term debt
          ↓
Capital market
```

### Principal purposes

- financing factories and infrastructure;
- corporate expansion;
- long-term government borrowing;
- ownership capital;
- long-horizon savings and investment.

### Important instruments

- equity shares;
- preference shares;
- corporate bonds and debentures;
- dated government securities;
- State Development Loans;
- mutual-fund and ETF units.

## 3. Direct comparison

| Dimension | Money market | Capital market |
|---|---|---|
| Typical maturity | Up to one year | Beyond one year or no maturity |
| Main purpose | Liquidity and short-term funding | Investment and long-term financing |
| Expected price volatility | Usually lower | Often higher |
| Rollover risk | Important | Generally lower for long-term funding |
| Examples | T-Bills, CP, CD, call money | Bonds, G-Secs and equity |
| Main Indian regulator | Principally RBI | Mainly SEBI; RBI has major G-Sec responsibilities |

⚠️ “Money market” does not mean a market where currency notes are bought and sold. It means a market for **short-term funds**.

## 4. Primary market

A transaction belongs to the **primary market** when a new security is issued and the issuer receives fresh funds.

```text
Company issues new shares
Investor pays company
Company receives fresh capital
```

Examples:

- IPO;
- follow-on public offer;
- rights issue;
- fresh corporate-bond issue;
- private placement;
- RBI auction of newly issued T-Bills or G-Secs for the government.

The primary market can issue both money-market and capital-market instruments.

## 5. Secondary market

A transaction belongs to the **secondary market** when an existing security is transferred between investors.

```text
Investor A sells existing share to Investor B
                    ↓
Payment normally goes to Investor A
                    ↓
Company receives no fresh money
```

Examples:

- trading listed shares on NSE or BSE;
- trading an existing corporate bond;
- trading an outstanding G-Sec through NDS-OM;
- buying or selling ETF units on an exchange.

## 6. The two-by-two matrix

| | **Primary—new issue** | **Secondary—existing instrument** |
|---|---|---|
| **Money market** | Government issues a new 91-day T-Bill | Investor sells that T-Bill before maturity |
| **Capital market** | Company issues a new long-term bond or IPO | Existing bond or share is traded |

> 🔑 **Maturity determines money versus capital; issuance stage determines primary versus secondary.**

## 7. Apply this to CP, CD and bonds

### Commercial Paper

```text
Company issues new CP → Primary money market
Investor resells CP   → Secondary money market
```

### Certificate of Deposit

```text
Bank issues new CD → Primary money market
Existing CD traded → Secondary money market
```

### Corporate bond

```text
Company issues new 5-year bond → Primary capital market
Investor resells existing bond → Secondary capital market
```

Thus, “primary market” does not mean only IPOs, and “secondary market” does not mean only shares.

## 8. Why secondary markets matter

Although the issuer receives no fresh money from ordinary secondary trading, liquid secondary markets help primary issuance.

```text
Easy investor exit
        ↓
Lower liquidity risk
        ↓
Greater demand for new issues
        ↓
Potentially lower borrowing cost
```

Secondary markets also provide:

- price discovery;
- continuous valuation;
- portfolio adjustment;
- collateral valuation;
- information about expected interest rates and credit risk.

## 9. Does a rising share price finance the company?

Not directly.

```text
Existing share price rises
          ↓
Company receives no automatic cash
```

But the company may benefit indirectly because:

- future share issuance may obtain a better valuation;
- borrowing conditions may improve;
- acquisitions using shares may become easier;
- market confidence may strengthen.

⚠️ A high market price does not guarantee sound fundamentals.

## 10. Current-affairs anchor

✅ **Fact:** RBI continued auctions of Treasury Bills and dated government securities during 2026.

- A new T-Bill auction is a **primary money-market** transaction.
- Later trading of that T-Bill is a **secondary money-market** transaction.
- A new dated G-Sec auction is a **primary capital-market** transaction.
- Later G-Sec trading is a **secondary capital-market** transaction.

## 11. UPSC traps

- ❌ All government securities belong to the money market.
  ✅ T-Bills are short-term; dated G-Secs belong to the capital market.

- ❌ Every exchange transaction is secondary.
  ✅ New issues may use electronic exchange infrastructure but remain primary issuance.

- ❌ Primary market means equity issuance only.
  ✅ New debt instruments are also issued in primary markets.

- ❌ Secondary markets are economically unproductive because issuers receive no money.
  ✅ They provide liquidity and price discovery that support primary financing.

## Revision notes

1. Money market primarily covers original maturity up to one year.
2. Capital market covers longer-term debt and equity.
3. Equity belongs to the capital market despite having no maturity.
4. Primary market means issuance of a new security.
5. Secondary market means transfer of an existing security.

7. A new T-Bill is primary money market.
8. A traded existing T-Bill is secondary money market.

## 🖼️ Why banks borrow overnight

```text
BANK A                                  BANK B
Temporary cash deficit                  Temporary cash surplus
        │                                       │
        └──── overnight unsecured loan ─────────┘
                         │
                         ▼
                  CALL MONEY MARKET
```

Banks experience daily mismatches between receipts and payments. The call money market allows eligible institutions to redistribute very short-term liquidity.

## 1. Classification by borrowing period

| Segment | Maturity |
|---|---|
| **Call money** | Overnight |
| **Notice money** | More than one day and up to 14 days |
| **Term money** | More than 14 days and up to one year |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Call tonight, Notice fortnight, Term thereafter.**

All three belong to the **money market** because their maturity does not exceed one year.

## 2. What does “unsecured” mean here?

Call, notice and term money are traditionally **unsecured borrowing**.

```text
Bank lends funds
      ↓
No specific government security is pledged
      ↓
Repayment depends upon borrower’s creditworthiness
```

This differs from repo or TREPS:

| Call money | Repo/TREPS |
|---|---|
| Unsecured | Collateralised |
| No identified security pledged | Eligible securities support borrowing |
| Greater direct counterparty exposure | Collateral reduces counterparty exposure |
| Overnight inter-institutional funding | Short-term secured funding |

⚠️ Unsecured does not mean legally optional. The borrower remains obligated to repay.

## 3. Why would one bank borrow overnight?

Consider a simplified situation:

```text
Morning:
Bank receives deposits and repayments

During the day:
Customers withdraw cash
Businesses make large payments
Clearing and settlement obligations arise

End of day:
Temporary liquidity deficit
         ↓
Borrow overnight in call market
```

The deficit need not mean insolvency. It may merely reflect the timing of inflows and outflows.

## 4. Call rate

The **call rate** is the interest rate charged on an overnight unsecured loan in the call money market.

Rates vary across transactions because:

- borrower creditworthiness differs;
- transaction timing differs;
- market liquidity changes;
- lenders’ surplus positions vary.

Therefore, many individual call rates can exist during one day.

## 5. What is WACR?

**WACR** means **Weighted Average Call Rate**.

It combines overnight call transactions by giving greater importance to larger transactions.

### Example

| Transaction | Amount | Rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Loan 1 | ₹100 crore | 5% |
| Loan 2 | ₹300 crore | 6% |

Simple average:

\[
(5+6)/2=5.5\%
\]

But the second transaction is three times larger.

\[
WACR=\frac{(100\times5)+(300\times6)}{400}=5.75\%
\]

Thus, WACR better represents the effective overnight unsecured funding rate.

## 6. RBI’s operating target

The RBI announces the **policy repo rate**, but operational monetary policy focuses on keeping overnight market rates—especially WACR—aligned around it.

```text
Policy decision
      ↓
RBI manages banking-system liquidity
      ↓
WACR moves near policy repo rate
      ↓
Other short-term rates adjust
      ↓
Deposit, lending and bond rates respond gradually
```

The repo rate is the **policy signal**. WACR shows how that signal is reflected in the overnight unsecured market.

## 7. Policy corridor

As of 17 September 2026:

```text
MSF: 5.50%       ← ceiling
      │
Repo: 5.25%      ← policy anchor
      │
SDF: 5.00%       ← floor
```

| Facility | Function |
|---|---|
| **SDF** | RBI absorbs overnight surplus liquidity without collateral |
| **Repo operations** | RBI injects liquidity against collateral |
| **MSF** | Eligible banks obtain emergency overnight liquidity against securities |

### Why a corridor?

If an eligible bank can place surplus funds with RBI near the SDF rate, it normally has little reason to lend substantially below that rate.

If it can obtain emergency funds through MSF, it normally has little reason to borrow substantially above the MSF rate—subject to access and market conditions.

Therefore, SDF and MSF help bound overnight rates.

## 8. Liquidity conditions and WACR

### Excess liquidity

```text
Many banks have surplus funds
          ↓
Competition to lend
          ↓
WACR tends to fall
          ↓
May move towards SDF floor
```

### Liquidity deficit

```text
Many banks need overnight funds
          ↓
Competition to borrow
          ↓
WACR tends to rise
          ↓
May move towards repo/MSF side
```

RBI responds using:

- repo or variable-rate repo auctions to inject liquidity;
- SDF or variable-rate reverse repo operations to absorb liquidity;
- durable tools such as OMOs where appropriate.

The Economic Survey 2025–26 reported that WACR averaged eight basis points below repo up to 8 January 2026, illustrating generally close policy transmission.

## 10. Call market versus RBI facilities

A call-money transaction occurs between eligible market participants.

An SDF, repo or MSF transaction involves the **RBI as counterparty**.

```text
Call money: Bank A ↔ Bank B
RBI repo:   RBI    ↔ Bank
SDF:        Bank   → RBI
MSF:        RBI    → Bank
```

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** The call rate is market-determined; the policy repo rate is set by the MPC.

## 11. Why not borrow long-term for every daily shortage?

Long-term borrowing for a one-day mismatch would:

- increase unnecessary interest costs;
- create maturity mismatch;
- lock the bank into funding it does not continuously need.

Short-duration markets permit efficient daily balance-sheet adjustment.

## 🖼️ Basic repo transaction

```text
TODAY

Borrower/seller ──securities──► Cash lender/buyer
Borrower/seller ◄────cash────── Cash lender/buyer

ON AGREED FUTURE DATE

Original borrower ◄─securities─ Original lender
Original borrower ─cash + return► Original lender
```

A **repo—repurchase agreement** is legally structured as a sale of securities followed by their agreed repurchase. Economically, it functions like **collateralised borrowing**.

## 1. Who is borrowing?

Suppose Bank A needs ₹100 crore overnight.

```text
Bank A transfers G-Secs to Bank B
Bank B transfers ₹100 crore to Bank A
Next day:
Bank A pays ₹100 crore + repo interest
Bank B returns the G-Secs
```

- **Bank A:** cash borrower and security seller.
- **Bank B:** cash lender and security buyer.
- **G-Secs:** collateral/economic security for the transaction.

> 🔑 **Repo: sell now, repurchase later.**

## 2. Repo versus reverse repo

The same transaction has two perspectives:

| Perspective | Description |
|---|---|
| **Repo** | Borrowing cash by selling securities with an agreement to repurchase |
| **Reverse repo** | Lending cash by buying securities with an agreement to resell |

```text
Bank A sees REPO
Bank B sees REVERSE REPO
```

⚠️ Repo and reverse repo are not necessarily two different contracts; they may be the two parties’ views of the same contract.

## 3. How is the repo return calculated?

Assume:

- cash borrowed: ₹100 crore;
- overnight repo rate: 5%;
- duration: one day.

Approximate interest:

\[
100\text{ crore}\times5\%\times\frac{1}{365}
\]

The borrower repurchases the securities at a slightly higher price, and the difference represents the lender’s return.

## 4. What is collateral?

Collateral is an asset supporting repayment.

If the borrower fails to repurchase:

- the lender possesses the transferred securities;
- those securities can protect the lender against loss;
- legal and settlement rules govern enforcement.

Collateral reduces credit exposure but does not eliminate every risk.

### Remaining risks

- collateral price may decline;
- settlement may fail;
- collateral may be illiquid;
- legal documentation may be defective;
- operational systems may fail.

## 5. What is a haircut?

A **haircut** is the difference between the collateral’s market value and the cash lent against it.

Example:

```text
Market value of securities = ₹105 crore
Cash lent                  = ₹100 crore
Haircut                     = ₹5 crore
```

Percentage haircut:

\[
\frac{105-100}{105}\times100 \approx 4.76\%
\]

### Why require a haircut?

If collateral prices fall before the transaction is closed, the lender still has a buffer.

```text
Collateral value falls slightly
           ↓
Haircut absorbs part of the decline
           ↓
Lender remains better protected
```

Riskier or more volatile collateral ordinarily requires a larger haircut.

## 6. Call money versus repo

| Dimension | Call money | Repo |
|---|---|---|
| Security | Unsecured | Collateralised |
| Structure | Direct cash loan | Sale and agreed repurchase |
| Credit exposure | Greater direct counterparty exposure | Reduced by collateral |
| Typical use | Overnight liquidity | Short-term secured liquidity |
| Rate | Call rate/WACR | Repo-market rate |

A financially weaker borrower may find collateralised borrowing easier than unsecured borrowing—but it must possess eligible collateral.

## 7. RBI repo versus market repo

### RBI repo operation

```text
RBI provides cash
       ↕
Eligible bank provides collateral
```

Purpose:

- inject system liquidity;
- implement monetary policy;
- influence overnight rates.

The RBI is the counterparty.

### Market repo

```text
Market participant A ↔ Market participant B
```

Purpose:

- redistribute liquidity among participants;
- finance security inventories;
- obtain secured short-term funding.

The RBI is not necessarily a party.

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Not every repo transaction occurs at the MPC’s policy repo rate. Market-repo rates are market-determined.

## 8. What is TREPS?

**TREPS** means **Tri-Party Repo Dealing and Settlement**.

A tri-party repo includes a third-party agent between borrower and lender.

```text
Cash borrower ───────┐
                     │
               TRI-PARTY AGENT
               • selects/allocates collateral
               • values collateral
               • applies margins/haircuts
               • supports settlement
               • manages substitution
                     │
Cash lender ─────────┘
```

The third-party agent does not usually become the ultimate borrower or lender. It manages the transaction infrastructure.

## 9. Why is a tri-party agent useful?

Without such an agent, every borrower and lender must repeatedly:

- verify collateral eligibility;
- calculate its value;
- apply haircuts;
- transfer securities;
- monitor price changes;
- process settlement and return.

TREPS standardises and centralises these functions.

### Benefits

- lower operational burden;
- efficient collateral management;
- reduced settlement risk;
- greater market participation;
- improved liquidity;
- anonymous or platform-based dealing architecture.

## 10. CBLO versus TREPS

**CBLO—Collateralised Borrowing and Lending Obligation** was an earlier Indian collateralised money-market mechanism.

It was replaced by the tri-party repo framework:

```text
Earlier mechanism: CBLO
          ↓
Transition in 2018
          ↓
Current mechanism: TREPS
```

⚠️ In a historical UPSC question, CBLO remains a **money-market instrument**, even though the current framework is TREPS.

> 🔑 **CBLO is the old name UPSC may test; TREPS is the current architecture.**

## 11. Does collateral become the lender’s permanent property?

Normally, no.

The repo agreement requires the lender to return equivalent securities when the borrower repurchases them.

```text
Normal completion:
cash repaid + repo return
           ↓
securities returned
```

Only default or contractual enforcement changes the normal outcome.

## 12. Why can TREPS rates differ from call rates?

| TREPS | Call money |
|---|---|
| Collateralised | Unsecured |
| Broader secured liquidity pool | Direct counterparty exposure |
| Lower credit-risk premium likely | Greater credit-risk premium possible |
| Depends on collateral supply | Depends on unsecured limits and confidence |

TREPS rates may therefore be below call rates, although liquidity and market conditions can change the relationship.

However, collateralisation can shift rather than eliminate risk:

```text
Counterparty risk falls
        but
Collateral, liquidity and infrastructure risk remain
```


## 1. Treasury Bills

A **Treasury Bill—or T-Bill—is a short-term debt instrument issued by the Central Government** through RBI auctions.

| Feature | T-Bill |
|---|---|
| Issuer | Government of India |
| Maturities | 91, 182 and 364 days |
| Market | Money market |
| Coupon | None |
| Issue method | Issued at discount |
| Redemption | Face value |
| Credit risk | Sovereign exposure |
| Tradability | Tradable in secondary market |

### Discount mechanism

```text
Investor pays today:       ₹97
Government repays later:  ₹100
Investor’s return:          ₹3
```

T-Bills are therefore **zero-coupon instruments**. Their return comes from the difference between purchase price and face value.

> 🔑 **T-Bill: discount today, face value at maturity.**

## 2. Dated Government Securities

A **dated G-Sec** is a longer-term debt security issued by the Central Government with a specified maturity date.

```text
Investor lends principal
          ↓
Government pays periodic coupon
          ↓
Government repays principal at maturity
```

Example:

- Face value: ₹100
- Coupon: 7% annually
- Maturity: 10 years

The investor generally receives ₹7 annually and ₹100 principal at maturity.

| T-Bill | Dated G-Sec |
|---|---|
| Up to one year | More than one year |
| Money market | Capital market |
| Normally no coupon | Usually periodic coupon |
| Issued at discount | May be issued near, below or above face value |
| Short-term financing | Medium- or long-term financing |

⚠️ **Coupon rate is not necessarily the investor’s current yield.** Market price changes after issuance.

## 3. State Development Loans

**SDLs are dated securities issued by State Governments.**

| Dimension | Central G-Sec | SDL |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Union Government | State Government |
| Auction conducted through | RBI | RBI |
| Borrowing beneficiary | Union | Issuing state |
| Typical maturity | Medium/long term | Medium/long term |
| Market classification | Capital market | Capital market |
| Yield | Benchmark sovereign yield | Usually carries a spread over comparable G-Sec |

### Why may SDL yields be higher?

⚠️ **Inference:**

```text
Lower liquidity + state-specific fiscal perception
                         ↓
Investors demand additional return
                         ↓
SDL yield generally exceeds comparable G-Sec yield
```

This difference is called a **spread**.

> An SDL is not a security issued by RBI. RBI conducts the auction and settlement on behalf of the state.

## 4. RBI’s role

RBI commonly acts as the government’s:

- debt manager;
- auction manager;
- settlement-system operator;
- government-securities market regulator;
- liquidity manager through separate monetary-policy operations.

```text
Government = borrower and issuer
RBI        = auction/debt-management intermediary
Investor   = lender
```

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** RBI’s conduct of the auction does not make RBI the borrower.

## 5. Primary and secondary markets

### Primary market

```text
Government issues new G-Sec
          ↓
Investors provide fresh funds
          ↓
Government receives borrowing proceeds
```

### Secondary market

```text
Existing investor sells G-Sec to another investor
          ↓
Ownership changes
          ↓
Government ordinarily receives no fresh money
```

Thus, the same government security begins in the **primary market** and may subsequently trade in the **secondary market**.

## 6. Auction intuition

Investors submit bids indicating the price or yield at which they are willing to lend.

```text
Higher price paid for security
              ↕
Lower yield earned

Lower price paid for security
              ↕
Higher yield earned
```

### Competitive bidding

Large or informed participants specify their desired price or yield.

### Non-competitive bidding

Eligible smaller investors seek an allocation without quoting a specific competitive yield; allotment occurs at the auction-derived price.

This mechanism facilitates retail participation without requiring sophisticated yield forecasting.

## 7. Government borrowing and liquidity

New government borrowing withdraws investible cash from buyers:

```text
Investors pay for new securities
             ↓
Money moves to government account
             ↓
Banking-system liquidity may decline
```

Government spending has the opposite directional effect:

```text
Government spends
       ↓
Funds enter private bank accounts
       ↓
Banking-system liquidity increases
```

This explains why cancelling a scheduled T-Bill auction can ease liquidity: investors do not have to transfer funds for the new securities.

## 8. Borrowing versus money creation

Government borrowing does not automatically mean new money is created.

```text
Government sells security to existing investor
                    ↓
Existing funds are transferred
                    ↓
Debt increases, but no automatic money creation
```

Monetary effects depend on:

- who purchases the security;
- how the transaction is financed;
- RBI’s liquidity operations;
- subsequent government expenditure.

## 9. Major risks

Even a sovereign debt instrument has risks beyond outright default.

| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interest-rate risk | Existing bond price falls when market rates rise |
| Inflation risk | Real purchasing power of repayments declines |
| Reinvestment risk | Coupon receipts may be reinvested at lower rates |
| Liquidity risk | Security may not sell quickly at a fair price |
| Rollover risk | Maturing short-term debt must be refinanced |
| Fiscal risk | Persistent borrowing raises interest burden |

### T-Bill rollover risk

```text
Short maturity
      ↓
Frequent repayment/refinancing
      ↓
Repeated exposure to prevailing interest rates
```

Short maturity reduces price volatility but increases refinancing frequency.

## 1. Commercial Paper

**Commercial Paper—CP—is a short-term, unsecured money-market debt instrument issued by eligible companies and other permitted entities.**

```text
Investors provide short-term funds
                ↓
Company issues Commercial Paper
                ↓
Company repays at maturity
```

Typical purposes include:

- working capital;
- inventory financing;
- receivables financing;
- bridging temporary cash-flow gaps;
- diversifying away from bank loans.

| Feature | Commercial Paper |
|---|---|
| Issuer | Eligible companies and other permitted entities |
| Market | Money market |
| Maturity | Up to one year |
| Security | Unsecured |
| Nature | Debt/promissory obligation |
| Form | Dematerialised |
| Regulator | RBI framework |
| Return | Commonly issued at discount |

### CP discount example

```text
Investor pays:       ₹98 lakh
Issuer repays:      ₹100 lakh
Investor earns:       ₹2 lakh
```

CP does not give the investor ownership in the company. It creates a **creditor–debtor relationship**.

## 2. What does “unsecured” mean?

No specific company asset is pledged to the CP investor.

```text
SECURED BORROWING                 UNSECURED CP

Loan backed by machinery          No identified collateral
or government securities          pledged to investor
          │                                  │
Lender can enforce                 Investor relies mainly on
collateral rights                  issuer's repayment capacity
```

Unsecured does **not** mean:

- repayment is optional;
- the instrument is illegal;
- the investor has no claim;
- the issuer can default without consequences.

The investor still has a legal debt claim, but lacks a claim over specifically pledged collateral.

## 3. Why would investors buy unsecured CP?

Investors evaluate:

- issuer’s credit rating;
- balance-sheet strength;
- cash flows;
- maturity;
- prevailing interest rates;
- group reputation;
- refinancing capacity.

A financially strong company may borrow through CP more cheaply than through a conventional bank loan.

⚠️ **Inference:** Since CP lacks collateral, weaker issuers generally face higher yields or may lose market access altogether.

## 4. CP rollover risk

Companies frequently repay maturing CP using operating cash flows or fresh borrowing.

```text
Old CP matures
      ↓
Company issues new CP
      ↓
Proceeds repay old CP
```

If confidence suddenly collapses:

```text
Investors refuse new CP
          ↓
Company cannot refinance
          ↓
Short-term liquidity stress
```

This is **rollover risk**. A solvent company can still encounter a liquidity crisis if short-term markets close.

## 5. Certificate of Deposit

A **Certificate of Deposit—CD—is a negotiable, unsecured money-market instrument issued by eligible banks and specified financial institutions against deposited funds.**

```text
Investor places funds
          ↓
Bank issues a marketable CD
          ↓
CD is repaid at maturity
```

| Feature | Certificate of Deposit |
|---|---|
| Issuer | Eligible banks/specified financial institutions |
| Market | Money market |
| Nature | Negotiable deposit instrument |
| Security | Unsecured |
| Holder’s position | Creditor/depositor, not shareholder |
| Tradability | Transferable in the market |
| Purpose | Short-term wholesale funding |

## 6. CD versus ordinary fixed deposit

| Dimension | Certificate of Deposit | Ordinary fixed deposit |
|---|---|---|
| Market orientation | Wholesale money-market instrument | Retail or conventional bank deposit |
| Negotiability | Tradable/transferable | Normally not freely market-traded |
| Liquidity route | Sale to another investor | Premature withdrawal under bank terms |
| Typical investor | Institutional/large investor | Retail and institutional depositors |
| Regulatory character | Money-market security | Deposit account contract |

> 🔑 A CD is **not a certificate proving ownership of a bond**. It is a negotiable bank deposit instrument.

## 7. CP versus CD

| Dimension | Commercial Paper | Certificate of Deposit |
|---|---|---|
| Main issuer | Company/permitted non-bank entity | Eligible bank/institution |
| Economic purpose | Corporate short-term borrowing | Bank wholesale funding |
| Instrument character | Promissory debt instrument | Negotiable deposit instrument |
| Security | Unsecured | Unsecured |
| Market | Money market | Money market |
| Investor relationship | Company creditor | Bank creditor/depositor |
| Main risk source | Corporate credit quality | Issuing institution’s credit quality |

### Most important distinction

```text
Need to identify CP or CD?
          ↓
Ask: “Who issued it?”
          ↓
Company → CP
Bank    → CD
```

## 8. CP and CD versus bond

| CP | CD | Bond |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate/permitted issuer | Bank/institution issuer | Government or corporate issuer |
| Short-term | Generally short-term | Usually medium/long-term |
| Money market | Money market | Usually capital market |
| Unsecured | Unsecured | May be secured or unsecured |
| Working-capital funding | Bank funding | Long-term financing |

The word **bond** does not automatically mean secured. Corporate bonds and debentures may also be secured or unsecured, depending on their terms.

## 9. CP versus bank loan

```text
BANK LOAN                           COMMERCIAL PAPER

Bank lends from its balance sheet   Investors lend through market
            │                                  │
Negotiated loan contract            Tradable money-market instrument
            │                                  │
May require collateral              CP is unsecured
```

CP involves **disintermediation**: the company accesses market investors instead of relying exclusively on a bank loan.

However, institutions such as mutual funds and banks may themselves invest in CP.

## 10. Credit rating does not guarantee repayment

A rating is an expert assessment of relative credit risk.

```text
Credit rating
     ≠
Government guarantee
     ≠
Collateral
     ≠
Certainty of repayment
```

Ratings can change when:

- profitability deteriorates;
- debt rises;
- cash flow weakens;
- refinancing becomes difficult;
- sectoral conditions worsen.

Investors must conduct their own risk assessment.

```text
          ↓

          ↓

```

When liquidity is abundant, CP and CD rates may soften—subject to issuer-specific credit risk.

## 🖼️ Anatomy of a bond

```text
Investor gives principal to issuer
              ↓
Issuer promises:
├─ periodic coupon payments
└─ principal repayment at maturity
              ↓
Bond may trade before maturity
              ↓
Market price changes → yield changes
```

A bond creates a **creditor claim**, not ownership.

## 1. Face value, coupon and maturity

Consider a ten-year bond:

| Feature | Example |
|---|---:|
| Face value | ₹1,000 |
| Coupon rate | 8% |
| Annual coupon | ₹80 |
| Maturity | 10 years |
| Principal repaid at maturity | ₹1,000 |

The annual coupon is:

\[
₹1,000 \times 8\% = ₹80
\]

Once issued as a fixed-rate bond, the contractual coupon normally remains ₹80 even when market interest rates change.

## 2. Coupon is not yield

- **Coupon rate:** contractual interest divided by face value.
- **Current yield:** annual coupon divided by current market price.
- **Yield to maturity—or YTM:** overall annualised return if purchased at the current price and held to maturity, assuming promised payments occur.

### Example

| Market price | Coupon received | Current yield |
|---:|---:|---:|
| ₹1,000 | ₹80 | 8.0% |
| ₹800 | ₹80 | 10.0% |
| ₹1,200 | ₹80 | 6.67% |

The coupon remains ₹80, but the yield changes because the purchase price changes.

> 🔑 **Coupon belongs to the contract; yield belongs to the price paid.**

## 3. Why price and yield move inversely

Suppose new bonds begin offering 10%, while an old ₹1,000 bond still pays only ₹80.

Nobody would pay ₹1,000 for the old 8% bond when a comparable new bond offers 10%. Its price must fall.

```text
Market interest rate rises
           ↓
Existing fixed coupon becomes less attractive
           ↓
Bond price falls
           ↓
Its effective yield rises
```

Conversely:

```text
Market interest rate falls
           ↓
Existing high coupon becomes attractive
           ↓
Bond price rises
           ↓
Its effective yield falls
```

### Core rule

\[
\text{Bond price} \uparrow \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Yield} \downarrow
\]

\[
\text{Bond price} \downarrow \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Yield} \uparrow
\]

## 4. Premium, par and discount

| Market price | Description | YTM relative to coupon |
|---:|---|---|
| Above face value | Premium bond | YTM below coupon rate |
| Equal to face value | Par bond | YTM approximately equals coupon |
| Below face value | Discount bond | YTM above coupon rate |

Why does a discount bond have a higher YTM?

The investor receives:

1. coupon payments; and
2. a capital gain when the below-par bond is redeemed at face value.

## 5. What is duration?

**Duration measures a bond’s sensitivity to changes in interest rates**, while also reflecting the timing of its cash flows.

```text
LONGER DURATION
      ↓
Cash flows recovered later
      ↓
Greater sensitivity to rate changes
      ↓
Larger price movement
```

Approximate relationship:

\[
\%\Delta P \approx -D \times \Delta y
\]

If duration is 7 and yield rises by one percentage point:

\[
\%\Delta P \approx -7\%
\]

This is an approximation, not an exact rule for large yield changes.

## 6. What increases duration?

| Factor | Effect on duration |
|---|---|
| Longer maturity | Usually increases |
| Lower coupon | Usually increases |
| Lower yield | Usually increases |
| Earlier/larger coupon payments | Usually decreases |
| Zero-coupon structure | Duration equals maturity |

### Comparison

```text
10-year zero-coupon bond
Principal received only at the end
              ↓
Higher duration

10-year high-coupon bond
Substantial cash received earlier
              ↓
Lower duration
```

> 🔑 **Long maturity does not always mean identical duration. Coupon timing matters.**

## 7. Duration risk example

Consider two bonds:

| Bond | Maturity | Coupon | Relative rate sensitivity |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| A | 2 years | 8% | Lower |
| B | 20 years | 8% | Higher |

If market yields rise equally, Bond B ordinarily suffers the larger percentage price fall because more of its value depends on distant cash flows.

This is **duration risk** or **interest-rate risk**.

## 8. Holding until maturity

A common misconception is:

> “Price changes do not matter if I hold the bond until maturity.”

This is only partly true.

If the issuer pays as promised, the investor may still receive contractual coupons and principal. But the investor continues to face:

- opportunity cost from holding a low-coupon bond;
- inflation risk;
- credit/default risk;
- reinvestment risk;
- liquidity needs that may force an early sale.

A mark-to-market loss becomes especially important if the bond must be sold before maturity.

## 9. Reinvestment risk

A high-coupon bond pays substantial cash before maturity. Those coupons must be reinvested.

```text
Market rates fall
       ↓
Coupon payments reinvested at lower rates
       ↓
Realised return may be below initial expectation
```

Thus:

- low-coupon/zero-coupon bonds have greater duration risk;
- high-coupon bonds may have greater reinvestment exposure.

## 10. Credit spread

A corporate bond normally offers a higher yield than a comparable government security.

\[
\text{Credit spread}
=
\text{Corporate bond yield}
-
\text{Comparable G-Sec yield}
\]

Example:

| Instrument | Yield |
|---|---:|
| Ten-year G-Sec | 7% |
| Ten-year corporate bond | 9% |
| Credit spread | 2 percentage points |

The additional yield compensates for factors such as:

- default risk;
- lower liquidity;
- uncertainty;
- recovery risk;
- complexity;
- risk aversion.

## 11. Why spreads widen

```text
Corporate outlook weakens
          ↓
Expected default/recovery risk worsens
          ↓
Investors demand higher yield
          ↓
Corporate bond price falls
          ↓
Credit spread widens
```

Spreads can also widen during general market panic even if a particular company’s fundamentals have not changed significantly.

### Spread interpretation

| Movement | Possible signal |
|---|---|
| Spread narrows | Improving confidence or stronger risk appetite |
| Spread widens | Rising credit/liquidity concern or risk aversion |

⚠️ A high yield is not automatically a bargain; it may be compensation for high risk.

## 12. Yield curve

The **yield curve** plots yields of similar-credit-quality bonds across maturities.

```text
Yield
  │                         /
  │                    /
  │               /
  │          /
  │_____ /________________ Maturity
      Short             Long
```

### Shapes

| Shape | Pattern | Possible interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Long yields above short yields | Term premium, growth/inflation uncertainty |
| Flat | Similar yields | Transition or uncertainty |
| Inverted | Short yields above long yields | Tight policy and expectations of future slowdown/rate cuts |

⚠️ An inverted curve is a signal, not a guaranteed recession forecast.

## 13. What moves government bond yields?

```text
Inflation expectations ───────┐
Government borrowing supply ──┤
RBI policy expectations ──────┤
Banking liquidity ────────────┼─► G-Sec yield
Global interest rates ────────┤
Currency and oil pressures ───┤
Investor demand ──────────────┘
```

### Government borrowing effect

⚠️ **Inference:**

```text
Larger bond supply
       ↓
Investors may demand lower prices
       ↓
Yields rise
```

This is not mechanical. Strong demand from banks, insurers, pension funds or foreign investors can absorb additional supply.

## 14. Current-affairs anchor

✅ **Fact:** RBI publishes government-security yields and conducts auctions and liquidity operations affecting the debt-market environment.

✅ **Fact:** Government bond yields reflect multiple factors, including inflation expectations, policy expectations, borrowing supply and liquidity.

⚠️ **Inference:** When yields rise, long-duration portfolios face greater mark-to-market losses than short-duration portfolios.

⚠️ **Inference:** Corporate spreads may widen independently of G-Sec yields if corporate credit concerns increase.

## 🖼️ How companies raise share capital

```text
                       COMPANY NEEDS CAPITAL
                                │
              ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
              │                                   │
         PUBLIC ROUTE                      SELECTED INVESTORS
              │                                   │
       ┌──────┴──────┐                   ┌────────┴─────────┐
      IPO            FPO             Private placement  Preferential issue
 First public   Further public
    offer           offer

Existing shareholders → Rights issue
Reserves converted into capital → Bonus issue
```

## 1. Equity shares

An equity share represents **ownership in a company**.

Equity shareholders generally have:

- voting rights;
- a residual claim on profits;
- possible dividends;
- possible capital appreciation;
- limited liability;
- the last claim during liquidation.

```text
Company income
      ↓
Operating expenses
      ↓
Interest and debt repayment
      ↓
Preference claims
      ↓
Residual belongs to equity shareholders
```

> 🔑 **Debt receives a contractual claim; equity receives the residual claim.**

## 2. Equity versus bonds

| Dimension | Equity share | Bond |
|---|---|---|
| Legal position | Owner | Creditor |
| Return | Dividend and capital gain | Interest and principal |
| Return guaranteed? | No | Contractually promised, subject to default |
| Maturity | Normally none | Specified maturity |
| Voting rights | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Liquidation priority | Last | Ahead of shareholders |
| Upside | Potentially unlimited | Normally contractually limited |

A profitable company is not legally required to distribute all profits as dividends.

## 3. Preference shares

Preference shares occupy an intermediate position between ordinary equity and debt.

Their holders receive preference regarding:

1. dividend payment; and
2. repayment of capital during liquidation.

```text
Payment priority

Secured creditors
       ↓
Other creditors
       ↓
Preference shareholders
       ↓
Equity shareholders
```

Preference shareholders ordinarily have more limited voting rights than equity shareholders.

### Major types

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cumulative | Unpaid dividends accumulate |
| Non-cumulative | Missed dividend normally does not accumulate |
| Convertible | Can convert into equity under specified terms |
| Non-convertible | Cannot convert into equity |
| Redeemable | Company repays capital under specified terms |
| Participating | May share in additional surplus under the terms |

⚠️ A preference dividend may be stated at a fixed rate, but preference shares remain **share capital**, not automatically debt.

## 4. IPO

An **Initial Public Offering—IPO—is the first public issue of shares by an unlisted company**, typically followed by stock-exchange listing.

```text
Unlisted company
       ↓ IPO
Shares offered to public
       ↓
Listing on stock exchange
       ↓
Shares become publicly tradable
```

An IPO may contain:

- a **fresh issue**;
- an **offer for sale**;
- or both.

## 5. Fresh issue versus Offer for Sale

### Fresh issue

```text
New shares created
       ↓
Investors pay company
       ↓
Company's share capital increases
       ↓
Existing ownership may be diluted
```

The company receives fresh funds.

### Offer for Sale—or OFS

```text
Existing shareholder sells shares
             ↓
Investor pays selling shareholder
             ↓
Company ordinarily receives no fresh capital
```

| Question | Fresh issue | OFS |
|---|---|---|
| Who sells? | Company | Existing shareholder |
| New shares created? | Yes | No |
| Company receives funds? | Yes | Ordinarily no |
| Dilution? | Usually yes | No increase in total shares merely from sale |

⚠️ An IPO does not necessarily mean all proceeds go to the company.

## 6. Dilution

Suppose a founder owns 80 of 100 shares:

\[
\text{Ownership}=80\%
\]

The company issues 100 new shares, but the founder buys none:

\[
\text{New ownership}=\frac{80}{200}=40\%
\]

The founder still owns 80 shares, but the ownership percentage falls. This is **dilution**.

Dilution may affect:

- voting control;
- earnings per share;
- dividend share;
- promoter influence.

## 7. FPO

A **Further Public Offer—FPO—is a subsequent public issue by an already-listed company.**

| IPO | FPO |
|---|---|
| First public offer | Later public offer |
| Company was unlisted | Company is already listed |
| Establishes public listing | Raises further public capital |

## 8. Rights issue

A rights issue offers new shares primarily to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings.

```text
Existing shareholder owns 10% of company
                 ↓
Receives right to buy roughly 10% of new issue
                 ↓
Can preserve proportionate ownership by subscribing
```

Rights may be **renounceable**, meaning the shareholder may transfer the entitlement, subject to issue terms and regulations.

### Why use a rights issue?

- existing owners receive priority;
- promoters can maintain control;
- public marketing costs may be lower;
- shareholders can avoid dilution by subscribing.

If a shareholder does not subscribe or transfer the entitlement, their percentage ownership may fall.

## 9. Bonus issue

A bonus issue distributes additional shares to existing shareholders without collecting fresh money from them.

```text
Company reserves
       ↓
Capitalised into share capital
       ↓
Bonus shares issued proportionately
```

Example: A 1:1 bonus gives one additional share for every share held.

Immediately after a proportionate bonus issue:

- number of shares rises;
- market price generally adjusts downward;
- proportional ownership remains unchanged;
- company receives no fresh funds;
- total shareholder wealth does not mechanically double.

> 🔑 **Rights issue raises money; bonus issue does not.**

## 10. Private placement

A private placement offers securities to a **selected group of identified investors**, rather than making a general public offer.

Possible advantages:

- faster fund-raising;
- negotiated terms;
- lower distribution cost;
- access to sophisticated investors.

Possible concerns:

- weaker broad price discovery;
- preferential treatment;
- minority-shareholder dilution;
- related-party or control concerns.

## 11. Preferential issue

A preferential issue is an issue of specified securities by a listed company to selected persons on a preferential basis under regulatory pricing, disclosure and approval rules.

It may be used for:

- strategic investment;
- promoter infusion;
- debt restructuring;
- acquisition financing;
- bringing in institutional investors.

It differs from a rights issue because shares are not offered proportionately to all existing shareholders.

## 12. Qualified Institutional Placement

A **QIP** enables a listed company to issue eligible securities to **Qualified Institutional Buyers**, subject to SEBI rules.

```text
Listed company
       ↓
Selected institutional investors
       ↓
Faster capital raising without a broad public offer
```

QIP investors may include eligible institutional entities such as mutual funds, insurers and specified financial institutions.

## 13. Book building in an IPO

In book building, investors bid within a price band.

```text
Price band announced
       ↓
Investors submit bids
       ↓
Demand at different prices recorded
       ↓
Issue price discovered
       ↓
Shares allotted
```

Book building assists price discovery but does not guarantee that the post-listing market price will rise.

## 14. Primary or secondary market?

| Transaction | Classification |
|---|---|
| Company issues new IPO shares | Primary market |
| Company makes rights issue | Primary market |
| Company makes fresh preferential issue | Primary market |
| Investor sells listed share on NSE/BSE | Secondary market |
| Existing shareholder sells shares through IPO OFS | Public issue process, but funds go to selling shareholder |

The decisive question is:

> **Are new securities being issued, or are existing securities changing owners?**

**Learner question / doubt**

> why company issues bonus share it has no value

A bonus share **has value**, but it does not create additional wealth at the moment of issue because the company is divided into more shares.

### Example: 1:1 bonus

```text
BEFORE                         AFTER

10 shares × ₹200              20 shares × ₹100
= ₹2,000                      = ₹2,000
```

The investor receives additional shares, while the market price adjusts proportionately. It resembles cutting one pizza into more slices—the number of slices increases, not the pizza.

## Why companies issue bonus shares

1. **Improve affordability and liquidity:** A lower per-share price may make trading easier for smaller investors.
2. **Capitalise accumulated reserves:** Accounting reserves are converted into share capital.

```text
Reserves decrease
Share capital increases
Total net worth remains unchanged
```

3. **Signal confidence:** Management may indicate confidence in future earnings, although this is not a guarantee.
4. **Reward shareholders without cash outflow:** Shareholders receive additional shares while the company retains cash for expansion or debt repayment.
5. **Increase shares in circulation:** A larger free float may improve trading activity.

### Bonus share versus dividend

| Bonus issue | Cash dividend |
|---|---|
| No cash paid | Cash paid |
| Company retains liquidity | Company’s cash decreases |
| More shares issued | Number of shares unchanged |
| No immediate wealth creation | Provides actual cash income |

> 🔑 A bonus issue changes the **number and accounting composition** of shares—not the company’s underlying economic value immediately.

---

**Reserves** are accumulated profits or other accounting surpluses retained within the company instead of being distributed as dividends.

```text
Company earns profit
        ↓
Pays tax and dividends
        ↓
Retained amount accumulates
        ↓
Shown under “reserves and surplus”
```

They form part of shareholders’ equity but are **not necessarily idle cash**. The corresponding money may already be invested in machinery, inventory or other assets.

### Bonus issue accounting

```text
Before bonus:
Share capital      ₹10 crore
Reserves           ₹90 crore
Total equity      ₹100 crore

After bonus:
Share capital      ₹20 crore
Reserves           ₹80 crore
Total equity      ₹100 crore
```

The company transfers an amount from reserves to share capital. No new asset or cash enters the company.

## 🖼️ Complete securities transaction

```text
BUYER places order                    SELLER places order
        │                                     │
        └────────► TRADING PLATFORM ◄─────────┘
                          │
                     Trade matched
                          ↓
                       CLEARING
              Who owes money/securities?
                          ↓
                     SETTLEMENT
           Money delivered ↔ security delivered
                          ↓
                      DEPOSITORY
             Ownership record is updated
```

> 🔑 **Trading agrees the deal; clearing calculates obligations; settlement completes it; depository records ownership.**

## 1. Stock exchange

A stock exchange provides an organised market where buy and sell orders meet.

Indian examples:

- NSE;
- BSE.

Functions include:

- order matching;
- price discovery;
- market surveillance;
- publication of prices and volumes;
- access to listed securities;
- investor-protection mechanisms.

The exchange ordinarily does not issue the company’s shares or become their final owner.

## 2. Primary versus secondary exchange activity

```text
PRIMARY MARKET

Company → new securities → investors
Company receives fresh funds

SECONDARY MARKET

Investor A → existing security → Investor B
Investor A receives sale proceeds
```

Stock exchanges primarily facilitate secondary-market trading, though exchange infrastructure also supports public-issue applications and listing.

## 3. NDS-OM

**NDS-OM means Negotiated Dealing System–Order Matching.**

It is an electronic order-driven platform associated with trading in government securities.

```text
Buyer enters G-Sec bid
          ↓
Seller enters G-Sec offer
          ↓
Compatible orders matched
          ↓
Trade sent for clearing and settlement
```

| Platform | Main association |
|---|---|
| NSE/BSE | Listed shares, corporate securities and derivatives |
| NDS-OM | Government securities |
| TREPS | Tri-party repo transactions |
| TReDS | MSME trade-receivable financing |

⚠️ These names are not interchangeable.

## 4. Clearing

After a trade is matched, the system determines:

- what each party must deliver;
- how much money is payable;
- when settlement is due;
- applicable margins;
- net obligations.

Example:

```text
A buys ₹10 lakh securities from B
A sells ₹4 lakh securities to C

Gross obligations:
A pays ₹10 lakh and receives ₹4 lakh

Possible net cash obligation:
A pays ₹6 lakh
```

Netting reduces the number and value of separate transfers.

## 5. Clearing corporation

A clearing corporation stands between market participants to manage settlement obligations.

Through **novation**, it may become:

```text
Buyer to every seller
        and
Seller to every buyer
```

Original arrangement:

```text
Buyer ↔ Seller
```

After novation:

```text
Buyer ↔ Clearing corporation ↔ Seller
```

This reduces direct bilateral counterparty exposure, although risk becomes concentrated in critical market infrastructure.

## 6. CCIL

**CCIL means Clearing Corporation of India Limited.**

It plays a central role in clearing and settlement across important segments, including:

- government securities;
- money-market transactions;
- foreign-exchange transactions;
- related collateralised markets.

CCIL is not:

- RBI;
- a commercial bank;
- a depository;
- the issuer of G-Secs.

## 7. How clearing corporations control risk

| Tool | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Initial margin | Covers potential future exposure |
| Mark-to-market margin | Covers current price losses |
| Netting | Reduces gross obligations |
| Collateral | Supports participant obligations |
| Default fund | Absorbs specified default losses |
| Position limits | Restricts excessive exposure |
| Default waterfall | Determines loss-absorption order |

```text
Defaulting member’s resources
             ↓
Margins/collateral
             ↓
Default fund and other prescribed resources
             ↓
Further loss-allocation mechanisms
```

A clearing corporation reduces risk; it cannot make risk disappear.

## 8. Settlement

Settlement is the final exchange of:

- funds from buyer to seller; and
- securities from seller to buyer.

### Delivery versus Payment

**DvP—Delivery versus Payment—links securities delivery with payment.**

```text
Security delivered
       only if
Payment is completed
```

It reduces **principal risk**, where one side performs but the other does not.

## 9. Settlement cycle

A notation such as **T+1** means:

```text
T   = trade date
T+1 = settlement on the next working settlement day
```

A shorter cycle can reduce:

- outstanding counterparty exposure;
- margin duration;
- time before investors receive assets or funds.

But it also demands:

- faster fund availability;
- efficient brokers and banks;
- reliable technology;
- timely securities delivery.

## 10. Depositories

A depository holds securities electronically and maintains ownership records.

India’s two major securities depositories are:

- **NSDL—National Securities Depository Limited**;
- **CDSL—Central Depository Services (India) Limited**.

```text
Physical certificate system          Demat system

Paper certificate                    Electronic entry
Transfer paperwork                   Digital ownership update
Loss/forgery risk                    Reduced paper-related risk
Slow transfer                        Faster settlement
```

A depository resembles a bank for securities:

| Bank | Depository |
|---|---|
| Holds money balances | Holds securities electronically |
| Uses bank accounts | Uses demat accounts |
| Transfers money | Transfers securities |

But a depository is not a bank and does not ordinarily lend the investor’s securities merely because it records them.

## 11. Depository Participant

Investors usually access a depository through a **Depository Participant—or DP**.

Possible DPs include eligible:

- banks;
- brokers;
- financial institutions.

```text
Investor
   ↓
Depository Participant
   ↓
NSDL or CDSL
```

⚠️ The DP is the investor-facing intermediary; NSDL/CDSL maintain the central depository infrastructure.

## 12. Demat account versus trading account

| Account | Function |
|---|---|
| Trading account | Places buy and sell orders |
| Demat account | Holds securities electronically |
| Bank account | Pays and receives money |

### Buying shares

```text
Order placed through trading account
                   ↓
Money debited through banking channel
                   ↓
Shares credited to demat account
```

### Selling shares

```text
Shares debited from demat account
                   ↓
Sale executed through trading account
                   ↓
Money credited through banking channel
```

## 13. Custodian versus depository

A **custodian** provides safekeeping, settlement and administrative services—often for institutional investors.

A **depository** maintains the central electronic ownership and transfer infrastructure.

They may interact closely but are not identical.

## 14. Entire institutional chain

```text
              EQUITY TRADE

Investor
   ↓ order
Broker
   ↓
NSE/BSE
   ↓ matched trade
Clearing corporation
   ↓ obligations and settlement
Depository — NSDL/CDSL
   ↓
Investor’s demat account updated
```

```text
              G-SEC TRADE

Market participant
   ↓ order
NDS-OM
   ↓ matched trade
CCIL
   ↓ clearing and settlement
SGL/demat ownership records updated
```

## 15. Why market infrastructure matters

A financial instrument is useful only if participants trust that:

- ownership is authentic;
- trades are matched fairly;
- obligations are calculated correctly;
- money and securities will be delivered;
- defaults will be managed;
- records cannot be casually altered.

```text
Strong infrastructure
        ↓
Lower settlement risk
        ↓
Greater investor confidence
        ↓
More market participation and liquidity
        ↓
Potentially lower financing cost
```

## How an interbank ATM withdrawal works

```text
Customer uses Bank A card at Bank B ATM
                 ↓
Bank B ATM sends request to NFS
                 ↓
NFS routes request to Bank A
                 ↓
Bank A verifies PIN, balance and limits
                 ↓
Approval returns through NFS
                 ↓
Bank B ATM dispenses cash
                 ↓
Interbank obligations are cleared and settled
```

NFS does not itself decide whether your account has sufficient funds—the **card-issuing bank** authorises the transaction.

```text
                                    │
```

## 1. Mutual fund

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests according to a stated mandate.

```text
Many investors
      ↓
Mutual fund scheme
      ↓
AMC manages portfolio
      ↓
Shares, bonds, money-market instruments, etc.
      ↓
Returns/losses belong proportionately to investors
```

A mutual-fund unit represents a proportionate interest in the scheme’s portfolio—not a bank deposit or guaranteed return.

## 2. Mutual-fund institutional structure

| Institution | Function |
|---|---|
| Sponsor | Establishes the mutual fund |
| Trustees | Oversee whether investor interests and regulations are respected |
| AMC | Makes investment and portfolio-management decisions |
| Custodian | Safekeeps the scheme’s securities |
| Registrar/transfer agent | Maintains investor and transaction records |
| SEBI | Regulates mutual funds |

These functions are separated to reduce conflicts and improve accountability.

## 3. Net Asset Value

**NAV is the per-unit value of the mutual-fund scheme.**

\[
NAV=
\frac{\text{Market value of assets}-\text{liabilities}}
{\text{units outstanding}}
\]

Example:

| Component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Securities and cash | ₹105 crore |
| Liabilities | ₹5 crore |
| Net assets | ₹100 crore |
| Units outstanding | 10 crore |
| NAV | ₹10 |

If asset prices fall, NAV ordinarily falls.

> 🔑 **NAV is valuation—not a guaranteed redemption amount or promised return.**

## 4. Open-ended and closed-ended funds

| Open-ended fund | Closed-ended fund |
|---|---|
| Units generally purchased/redeemed through the fund | Fixed initial corpus/tenure |
| Transactions generally occur at applicable NAV-based price | Exit may depend on exchange trading or maturity |
| Units vary with subscriptions/redemptions | Units normally remain relatively fixed |
| Greater redemption-liquidity obligation | Market price may diverge from NAV |

## 5. Active and passive funds

### Active fund

The manager selects securities to outperform a benchmark.

- higher research and trading involvement;
- usually higher costs;
- possibility of outperformance or underperformance.

### Passive fund

The scheme attempts to replicate an index.

- rule-based portfolio;
- usually lower cost;
- return broadly follows the benchmark minus expenses and tracking difference.

## 6. SIP

A **Systematic Investment Plan—or SIP—is a method of investing periodically**, not a distinct asset class.

```text
₹5,000 invested every month
          ↓
More units when NAV is low
Fewer units when NAV is high
          ↓
Purchase cost is averaged over time
```

SIP can encourage discipline but cannot guarantee profit or prevent loss.

## 7. ETF

An **Exchange-Traded Fund** is a pooled fund whose units trade on a stock exchange.

```text
Fund holds portfolio/index basket
                 ↓
ETF units listed on exchange
                 ↓
Investors buy/sell units intraday
```

| Mutual fund transaction | ETF transaction |
|---|---|
| Usually with the fund/AMC | Usually with another investor on exchange |
| Applicable NAV-based pricing | Live market price |
| Demat not always necessary | Demat and trading account generally needed |
| End-of-day NAV central | Intraday price fluctuates |

ETFs may track:

- equity indices;
- bonds;
- gold or silver;
- sectors;
- international indices.

## 8. ETF price versus NAV

An ETF’s exchange price can temporarily differ from its underlying NAV.

\[
\text{Premium/discount}
=
\text{ETF market price}-\text{NAV}
\]

Authorised participants and market makers can create or redeem large blocks of ETF units, helping align market price with underlying portfolio value.

However, alignment may weaken during:

- market stress;
- low liquidity;
- foreign-market closure;
- disruption in underlying assets;
- wide bid–ask spreads.

## 9. Mutual fund versus ETF versus share

| Dimension | Mutual-fund unit | ETF unit | Company share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Represents | Pooled portfolio | Exchange-traded pooled portfolio | Ownership in one company |
| Diversification | Usually yes | Usually yes | No automatic diversification |
| Intraday trading | Usually no | Yes | Yes |
| Pricing | Applicable NAV | Market price around NAV | Market price |
| Management | Active or passive | Commonly passive, but not necessarily | Company management operates business |

## 10. Alternative Investment Fund

An **AIF is a privately pooled investment vehicle** collecting money from sophisticated investors under a defined investment policy.

AIFs may invest in assets or strategies not commonly available through ordinary retail mutual funds.

### Categories

| Category | Broad orientation | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Category I** | Sectors considered socially/economically desirable | Venture capital, SME, infrastructure, social-impact funds |
| **Category II** | Private equity/debt strategies without routine leverage | Private-equity and debt funds |
| **Category III** | Complex strategies; leverage may be employed subject to rules | Hedge-fund-style and trading strategies |

AIFs generally involve:

- larger investment commitments;
- lower liquidity;
- longer holding periods;
- sophisticated strategies;
- higher risk and fees;
- restricted investor access.

⚠️ An AIF is not automatically safer because participation is restricted.

## 11. Derivative

A derivative derives its value from an underlying variable such as:

- share;
- index;
- commodity;
- currency;
- interest rate;
- bond.

```text
Underlying value changes
          ↓
Derivative contract value changes
```

Derivatives may be used for:

1. hedging;
2. speculation;
3. arbitrage.

The contract itself is not inherently beneficial or harmful; purpose, leverage and risk management determine the outcome.

## 12. Futures

A futures contract creates a binding obligation for both parties.

```text
Buyer must buy
Seller must sell
At agreed future terms
```

| Feature | Futures |
|---|---|
| Rights/obligations | Both parties obligated |
| Upfront full payment | Usually no |
| Margin | Required |
| Daily adjustment | Mark-to-market |
| Payoff | Symmetrical |

### Hedging example

An importer expecting to pay dollars after three months fears rupee depreciation. A currency-futures position can offset part of the increased rupee cost.

## 13. Leverage and margin

Suppose exposure worth ₹10 lakh requires margin of ₹1 lakh.

\[
\text{Leverage}=10\times
\]

A 5% movement in the underlying equals ₹50,000—half the posted margin.

```text
Small underlying movement
          ↓
Large percentage gain or loss on margin
```

Margin is a risk-control deposit, not the maximum possible loss in every derivative position.

## 14. Options

An option gives the **buyer a right, but not an obligation**, to transact at a specified strike price.

| Option | Buyer’s right |
|---|---|
| Call option | Buy the underlying |
| Put option | Sell the underlying |

The buyer pays a **premium**.

```text
Option buyer:
Maximum immediate contractual loss generally = premium paid

Option seller/writer:
Receives premium but assumes the corresponding obligation
```

### Example

A farmer or producer fearing a price fall can use a put option to establish downside protection while retaining some upside.

## 15. Futures versus options

| Futures | Options |
|---|---|
| Both parties obligated | Buyer has a right |
| Symmetrical payoff | Asymmetrical payoff |
| Margin required | Buyer pays premium; writer usually posts margin |
| Gain for one mirrors loss for another | Buyer can allow option to expire |
| Strong exposure to price movements | Downside for buyer generally limited to premium |

## 16. Swaps

A swap is an agreement to exchange streams of cash flows.

### Interest-rate swap

```text
Party A pays fixed rate
          ↕
Party B pays floating rate
```

It can transform exposure from fixed to floating or vice versa without replacing the original loan.

### Currency swap

Parties exchange payment obligations in different currencies.

```text
Indian company needs dollars
Foreign company needs rupees
             ↓
Exchange currency principal/cash flows
             ↓
Reverse exchange under agreed terms later
```

Uses include:

- hedging exchange-rate risk;
- accessing foreign-currency funding;
- reducing borrowing cost;
- managing asset–liability currency mismatch.

## 17. Central-bank currency swap

A bilateral central-bank currency-swap arrangement differs from an ordinary corporate swap.

```text
Central Bank A ↔ Central Bank B
Domestic currency ↔ Foreign currency
```

Its purpose may include:

- emergency foreign-currency liquidity;
- trade-settlement support;
- financial-stability protection.

It does not mean currencies are permanently exchanged or that exchange-rate risk disappears.

## 18. Hedging versus speculation

### Hedge

```text
Existing real-world risk
          +
Offsetting derivative position
          ↓
Reduced net exposure
```

### Speculation

```text
No corresponding underlying exposure
          +
Derivative position
          ↓
Deliberate market-risk exposure
```

The same contract can serve either purpose.

## 19. Major risks

| Product | Important risks |
|---|---|
| Mutual fund | Market, credit, liquidity and manager risk |
| ETF | Market risk, tracking error, liquidity and price–NAV divergence |
| AIF | Illiquidity, valuation opacity, leverage and concentration |
| Futures | Leverage, margin calls and basis risk |
| Options | Premium loss for buyer; potentially large writer losses |
| Swaps | Counterparty, currency, interest-rate and basis risk |

## 🖼️ Different financing routes

```text
                      BUSINESS NEEDS FUNDS
                              │
       ┌──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┐
       │                      │                      │
   Bank loan            Corporate bond            Equity
       │                      │                      │
Bank is creditor      Investors are creditors   Investors become owners
```

## 1. Corporate bond

A corporate bond is a debt security through which a company borrows from investors.

```text
Investors provide funds
          ↓
Company issues bonds
          ↓
Company promises interest and principal
```

Bondholders:

- are creditors, not owners;
- generally receive contractual interest;
- rank ahead of shareholders during liquidation;
- do not ordinarily receive voting rights;
- face default and market risk.

## 2. Corporate bond versus bank loan

| Bank loan | Corporate bond |
|---|---|
| Bank directly lends | Market investors lend |
| Negotiated contract | Tradable security |
| Usually held on bank balance sheet | May trade in secondary market |
| Relationship-based monitoring | Disclosure, trustees and ratings important |
| Terms may remain private | Listed bonds require market disclosures |

Corporate bonds diversify financing beyond banks and can provide longer-term funds.

## 3. Secured and unsecured bonds

### Secured bond

Specific assets or security interests support repayment.

```text
Company defaults
       ↓
Bond trustee/security holder may enforce collateral
       ↓
Recovery proceeds distributed under legal priority
```

### Unsecured bond

No specific asset is pledged. Investors rely on:

- issuer cash flows;
- credit quality;
- legal promise;
- repayment priority;
- covenants.

A debenture or bond is **not automatically secured** merely because it is a formal security.

## 4. Convertible bonds

A convertible bond begins as debt but can convert into equity under specified terms.

```text
Initially: creditor claim
             ↓ conversion
Later: ownership claim
```

| Convertible bond | Ordinary bond |
|---|---|
| May become equity | Remains debt until repayment |
| Offers potential equity upside | Return primarily interest and principal |
| May carry lower coupon | May require higher coupon |
| Can dilute existing shareholders | No equity dilution if repaid normally |

## 5. Public issue versus private placement

Corporate bonds can be issued through:

- public issue to a broad investor base;
- private placement to selected investors.

India’s corporate-bond market has historically relied heavily on private placements.

### Trade-off

```text
Private placement
     ↓
Faster negotiated fund-raising
     but
Narrower participation and potentially weaker secondary liquidity
```

## 6. Credit rating

A credit rating is an opinion about the relative likelihood of timely debt repayment.

Typical long-term rating hierarchy:

```text
Lower expected credit risk
AAA
 AA
  A
 BBB
 BB
  B
  C
  D
Higher/default risk
```

The precise meaning and modifiers depend on the rating agency’s scale.

### What ratings assess

- business risk;
- financial leverage;
- cash flows;
- debt-servicing capacity;
- industry conditions;
- management and governance;
- security structure;
- repayment history.

## 7. What a credit rating does not mean

A rating is not:

- a repayment guarantee;
- deposit insurance;
- a recommendation to buy;
- protection against interest-rate losses;
- permanent;
- proof that fraud is impossible.

```text
AAA bond
   can still face
Price risk + liquidity risk + downgrade risk
```

## 8. Rating downgrade

Suppose a bond is downgraded:

```text
Perceived credit risk rises
             ↓
Investors demand higher yield
             ↓
Existing bond price falls
             ↓
Credit spread widens
```

Some regulated institutional investors may also be required to reduce holdings if a bond falls below their permitted credit-quality threshold.

## 9. Rating-agency conflict

The issuer commonly pays for the rating.

```text
Issuer pays agency
        ↓
Possible incentive conflict
        ↓
Need for regulation, disclosure and surveillance
```

SEBI regulates credit-rating agencies in India. Regulatory supervision reduces but cannot eliminate judgment errors or conflicts.

## 10. Debenture trustee

A debenture trustee represents bondholders’ collective interests.

Functions may include:

- monitoring covenants;
- checking security creation;
- communicating defaults;
- enforcing security;
- acting for dispersed bondholders.

The trustee does not guarantee repayment from its own funds merely by accepting the role.

## 11. Who invests in bonds and G-Secs?

Major investor classes include:

| Investor | Typical reason |
|---|---|
| Banks | Liquidity management, SLR and investment |
| Insurance companies | Match long-term liabilities |
| Pension funds | Stable long-duration assets |
| Mutual funds | Portfolio investment across debt schemes |
| Foreign portfolio investors | Yield and diversification, subject to rules |
| Retail investors | Income, diversification and capital preservation objectives |
| Provident funds | Long-term liability matching |

⚠️ Retail investors, pension funds and insurance companies are not conceptually prohibited from government and corporate debt markets; participation remains subject to applicable regulations and instrument eligibility.

## 12. What is TReDS?

**TReDS means Trade Receivables Discounting System.**

It is an RBI-regulated electronic platform that helps MSMEs obtain early payment against accepted invoices.

## 🖼️ TReDS transaction

```text
1. MSME supplies goods to large buyer
                 ↓
2. MSME raises invoice
                 ↓
3. Buyer accepts invoice on TReDS
                 ↓
4. Multiple financiers bid to discount it
                 ↓
5. MSME selects suitable bid
                 ↓
6. Financier pays MSME early, after discount
                 ↓
7. Buyer pays financier on invoice due date
```

## 13. Discounting example

An MSME has an accepted invoice of ₹10 lakh due after 60 days.

A financier offers ₹9.8 lakh immediately.

```text
MSME receives now:       ₹9.8 lakh
Buyer pays at maturity: ₹10.0 lakh
Financier’s gross return: ₹0.2 lakh
```

The discount compensates the financier for time, funding cost and risk.

## 14. Why TReDS matters

MSMEs frequently face:

```text
Goods delivered
       ↓
Invoice accepted late or paid after months
       ↓
Working capital remains blocked
       ↓
Production and wages suffer
```

TReDS converts an accepted receivable into immediate liquidity through competitive financier bidding.

Benefits include:

- faster MSME cash flow;
- reduced dependence on informal credit;
- transparent price discovery;
- competition among financiers;
- digital transaction records;
- financing generally without recourse to the MSME under the framework.

## 15. TReDS limitations

TReDS cannot help effectively when:

- buyers do not join the platform;
- invoices are not accepted promptly;
- disputes exist over delivery;
- financiers show little interest;
- buyer credit quality is weak;
- MSMEs lack digital awareness.

> 🔑 **TReDS finances accepted invoices; it does not force a buyer to accept a disputed invoice.**

## 16. TREPS versus TReDS

| TREPS | TReDS |
|---|---|
| Tri-party repo | Trade receivables discounting |
| Collateralised money-market borrowing | MSME invoice financing |
| Securities used as collateral | Accepted invoices financed |
| Short-term financial-market liquidity | Working-capital support |
| Repo-market infrastructure | Receivables-financing platform |

The similar names are a common UPSC trap.

## 17. GIFT City IFSC

**GIFT City’s International Financial Services Centre is located at Gandhinagar, Gujarat.**

It is designed to conduct internationally oriented financial business that might otherwise occur in centres such as Singapore, Dubai or London.

Activities include:

- international banking;
- foreign-currency financing;
- international bond listings;
- fund management;
- insurance and reinsurance;
- aircraft and ship leasing;
- exchange trading.

## 18. IFSCA

The **International Financial Services Centres Authority—IFSCA—is the unified regulator within the IFSC.**

```text
Rest of India:
RBI + SEBI + IRDAI + PFRDA
perform different regulatory roles

GIFT IFSC:
IFSCA acts as unified regulator
for IFSC financial activities
```

IFSCA does not replace RBI, SEBI, IRDAI or PFRDA throughout India. Its unified jurisdiction concerns the IFSC.

## 19. Why GIFT IFSC?

```text
Indian/global entity needs international finance
                    ↓
Uses foreign-currency and global-facing services
                    ↓
Activity conducted through Indian IFSC
                    ↓
Potentially retains financial activity and expertise in India
```

Potential benefits:

- access to international investors;
- foreign-currency fund-raising;
- financial-sector employment;
- development of specialised services;
- reduced migration of financial activity offshore.

Potential risks:

- regulatory arbitrage;
- tax-base concerns;
- money-laundering and compliance risk;
- concentration in niche activities;
- excessive claims about real-economy benefits.

2. A secondary-market purchase of an existing corporate bond ordinarily provides funds to the selling investor rather than fresh funds to the issuer.
3. NDS-OM is a depository that holds equity shares electronically.
4. TReDS is a tri-party platform for collateralised repo transactions.

Which statements are correct?

**A. 1 and 2 only**
**B. 1, 2 and 3 only**
**C. 3 and 4 only**
**D. 1, 2, 3 and 4**

---

✅ **Correct — A. 1 and 2 only**

- CP is short-term unsecured corporate borrowing.
- Secondary trading pays the selling investor, not the original issuer.
- NDS-OM is a G-Sec trading platform, not a depository.
- TReDS finances MSME receivables; **TREPS** is the tri-party repo platform.
