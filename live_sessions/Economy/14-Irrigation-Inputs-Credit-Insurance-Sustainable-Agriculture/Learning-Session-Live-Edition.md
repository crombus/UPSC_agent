### Visual: one crop season as a linked system

```text
Rain / stored water / groundwater
              |
              v
 Soil condition + seed choice + nutrients + plant protection
              |
              v
  Timely labour, machinery, extension and working capital
              |
              v
       Yield and harvest quality
              |
       weather or biological shock?
          /                 \
        no                   yes
        |                    |
    farm income       savings / insurance /
                         relief / adaptation
```

The farm does not produce through one input. Its output is limited by the weakest complementary input and by the risk surrounding the whole season.

### The learner's problem

Why can a farmer receive subsidised fertiliser and still obtain a poor yield? Because fertiliser cannot compensate for late irrigation, unsuitable seed, a pest outbreak, missing credit or bad advice. Agricultural productivity is a **system outcome**.

**Fact:** The canonical owner places soil, water, seed and knowledge before credit-financed cultivation, weather risk, insurance and diversification.

**Inference:** A policy that reduces one private cost may simply move the bottleneck. Cheap power may make pumping easier but deepen aquifer stress; cheap nitrogen may raise current use but worsen nutrient imbalance.

### Five constraints that must be separated

| Constraint | Question | Primary policy family |
|---|---|---|
| Natural-resource constraint | Is usable water and healthy soil available? | Irrigation, watershed, soil governance |
| Technology/input constraint | Is suitable seed, nutrient, machinery or protection available? | Research, quality control, input delivery |
| Knowledge constraint | Does the cultivator know what, when and how much to apply? | Extension, KVKs, advisories |
| Liquidity constraint | Can the farmer pay before harvest revenue arrives? | KCC, banks, cooperatives, NABARD |
| Risk constraint | Who bears a shock that destroys expected output? | Insurance, diversification, relief |

### A simple production illustration

Suppose attainable yield is represented as:

```text
Yield = f(water, seed, nutrients, soil, knowledge, timing)
```

This is not an additive shopping list. If water arrives after the critical crop stage, extra fertiliser cannot restore the lost biological window. Likewise, irrigation raises the expected return to improved seed, making timely credit more valuable.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "The system approach is too broad; policy must choose a single measurable intervention."

**Reply:** Individual schemes still need measurable outputs, but evaluation must trace the causal chain from asset or subsidy to access, use, yield, income and externality.

**Residual:** Convergence can become administrative vagueness. Clear ownership, denominators and outcome indicators remain necessary.

### UPSC integration

- **Syllabus:** different types of irrigation and irrigation systems; supporting link to direct and indirect farm subsidies and sustainable development.
- **Verified PYQ linkage:** No single direct PYQ owns this systems introduction. It is the answer frame required to connect the verified irrigation, groundwater, IFS and sustainable-practice demands.
- **Trap:** "More inputs" does not necessarily mean "higher sustainable productivity."
- **Answer use:** Open a broad farm-support answer with the chain `resource -> input -> finance -> risk -> resilience`.

### Revision notes

1. Farm productivity is a complementary-input outcome.
2. Water, soil and seed set attainable biological yield.
3. Credit finances the interval before harvest revenue.
4. Extension converts knowledge into farm decisions.
5. Insurance transfers specified risk; it does not create productivity.
6. Diversification reduces dependence on one crop or income stream.
7. Subsidies affect behaviour as well as current cost.
8. Technology adoption depends on farm size, tenancy, finance, maintenance and markets.
9. Convergence requires a clear causal chain and clear accountability.

### Embedded practice

**MCQ 1.** A farmer has improved seed and subsidised fertiliser but receives irrigation after the critical growth stage. Which principle best explains the low yield?

A. Agricultural inputs are complementary and timing-sensitive.  
B. Crop insurance determines biological yield.  
C. Formal credit always substitutes for irrigation.  
D. Fertiliser quantity alone determines attainable output.

**Answer: A**

- **A - Correct:** A missing or late complementary input can bind the production system.
- **B - Incorrect:** Insurance transfers covered loss after a trigger; it does not grow the crop.
- **C - Incorrect:** Credit can finance irrigation but cannot physically substitute for water.
- **D - Incorrect:** Nutrients work within soil, water, crop and timing constraints.

**MCQ 2.** Which intervention principally addresses a seasonal liquidity constraint?

A. Watershed ridge treatment  
B. Kisan Credit Card working-capital access  
C. Crop Cutting Experiments  
D. Organic certification

**Answer: B**

- **A - Incorrect:** Watershed treatment addresses soil-water management.
- **B - Correct:** KCC is designed around crop and eligible allied working-capital needs.
- **C - Incorrect:** CCEs support yield assessment for insurance.
- **D - Incorrect:** Certification verifies a production standard and market claim.

**Original Mains micro-model - 10 marks**

**Question:** Why should agricultural productivity policy be designed as a water-input-credit-insurance nexus?

**Model:** Define complementarity; show water and soil as physical constraints; explain that credit finances seed, nutrients and operations before harvest; show insurance as risk transfer rather than productivity creation; add the externality problem of cheap power or distorted fertiliser prices; conclude that convergence must preserve instrument-specific accountability.

**Transition:** Once agriculture is treated as a system, the first task is to measure water correctly. "Irrigated" can mean created capacity, land served, repeated crop use, useful delivery or output per unit water.

### Visual: the irrigation accounting ladder

```text
ULTIMATE IRRIGATION POTENTIAL
             |
             v
     POTENTIAL CREATED
             |
             v
     POTENTIAL UTILISED
             |
             v
  RELIABLE FARM-GATE DELIVERY
             |
             v
 USEFUL CROP WATER / OUTPUT
```

Each step answers a different question. A canal may exist without reliable delivery; delivery may occur without efficient application; efficient application may not reduce basin extraction.

### Definitions and calculations

| Measure | Meaning | Common trap |
|---|---|---|
| Net irrigated area | Physical area irrigated at least once in a year | It does not count repeated seasons |
| Gross irrigated area | Sum of all irrigated crop instances | It can exceed net irrigated area |
| Irrigation intensity | Gross irrigated area / net irrigated area x 100 | It is not water-use efficiency |
| Potential-utilisation ratio | Potential utilised / potential created x 100 | It says nothing by itself about crop output |
| Application efficiency | Water stored in the crop root zone / water delivered to field x 100 | Scale is field-level |
| Water productivity | Crop output or value / water consumed or applied | Denominator must be stated |

### Worked example 1: irrigation intensity

A village has 100 hectares irrigated in Kharif. Of these, 60 hectares are irrigated again in Rabi.

```text
Net irrigated area   = 100 ha
Gross irrigated area = 100 + 60 = 160 ha
Irrigation intensity = 160 / 100 x 100 = 160%
```

A 160% intensity means repeated irrigated cropping. It does not prove that each litre was used efficiently.

### Worked example 2: potential utilisation

```text
Potential created = 50,000 ha
Potential utilised = 35,000 ha
Utilisation ratio = 35,000 / 50,000 x 100 = 70%
```

The 30% gap may reflect incomplete field channels, unreliable releases, land development, maintenance, farmer demand or measurement. It cannot be diagnosed from the ratio alone.

### Types of irrigation: classify by source and delivery

| Type | Economic strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Canal | Large command and gravity distribution | Head-tail inequity, siltation, drainage burden |
| Tank | Local storage and supplementary irrigation | Encroachment, siltation, catchment degradation |
| Well/tubewell | Flexible on-demand control | Aquifer depletion and energy use |
| Lift irrigation | Reaches higher terrain or distant fields | Recurring power and maintenance cost |
| Drip | Controlled root-zone application | Capital, clogging and rebound |
| Sprinkler | Flexible pressurised application | Wind loss, energy and suitability limits |

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Efficiency means water not delivered to the root zone is wasted."

**Reply:** Some field "loss" is non-beneficial evaporation, but some seepage becomes recharge or downstream return flow. Basin accounting must distinguish recoverable return flow from irrecoverable consumption.

**Residual:** Water productivity measured in kilograms may favour low-value crops differently from productivity measured in rupees. State the numerator too.

### UPSC integration

- **Verified link:** **2024 GS-III Q13**, 15 marks/250 words - challenges of the Indian irrigation system and government measures.
- **Directive/demand:** The ledger records "State measures"; a complete response must first separate source, conveyance, distribution, application and sustainability failures.
- **Approach:** `accounting distinction -> system-specific problems -> named interventions -> implementation limits`.
- **Trap:** High irrigation intensity can coexist with groundwater depletion.

### Revision notes

1. Potential created is an infrastructure measure.
2. Potential utilised is a service-use measure.
3. Net area counts land; gross area counts crop instances.
4. Irrigation intensity is gross divided by net, multiplied by 100.
5. Efficiency needs a scale and denominator.
6. Water productivity may be physical or economic.
7. Return flow is not always a social loss.
8. Reliable and equitable delivery is distinct from project completion.
9. Compare like measures and like periods.

### Embedded practice

**Answer: C**

- **A - Incorrect:** That reverses the ratio.
- **B - Incorrect:** It would apply if gross and net areas were equal.

- **D - Incorrect:** Gross area would need to be 160 hectares.

**Answer: D**

- **A - Incorrect:** Created capacity is several causal steps away from output.
- **B - Incorrect:** Repeated irrigated seasons make gross area exceed net area.
- **C - Incorrect:** Some seepage may recharge an aquifer or support downstream use.
- **D - Correct:** Field application and basin consumption use different boundaries.

A. Irrigation intensity  
B. Potential created  
C. Aquifer recharge rate

**Answer: A**

- **A - Correct:** Its numerator counts all irrigated crop instances.
- **B - Incorrect:** This is designed irrigation capacity.
- **C - Incorrect:** This is a hydrological flow.
- **D - Incorrect:** This belongs to insurance pricing.

### Visual: where a canal system can fail

```text
Reservoir / barrage
      |
 main canal ---- seepage / silt / breach
      |
 distributary -- rotation and measurement
      |
 minor / outlet - head captures more?

 HEAD -------- MIDDLE -------- TAIL
 surplus       uncertain       scarcity

 drainage failure -> waterlogging -> salinity
```

### Canal economics

A canal is a network service, not merely a storage structure. Its high fixed cost can spread water across a large command. Yet its outcome depends on maintenance, release timing, measurement, field channels and drainage.

**Head-tail problem:** Upstream users can receive earlier or larger flows, leaving tail-end cultivators with unreliable supply. This is both an efficiency and equity failure.

**Waterlogging and salinity:** Excess application plus inadequate drainage raises the water table. Evaporation can leave salts in the root zone and reduce land productivity.

**Seepage qualification:** Lining can improve conveyance where leakage is harmful, but seepage may recharge groundwater in some hydrogeological settings. The correct test is net system effect, not a slogan.

### Tank and lift systems

```text
TANK:
catchment -> runoff -> local storage -> supplementary irrigation

LIFT:
river/canal/lower reservoir -> pump + energy -> higher command
```

Tanks are decentralised but depend on catchment treatment, desiltation and protection from encroachment. Lift irrigation overcomes gravity constraints but creates recurring electricity, pump and maintenance costs.

### Command area and CADWM

**Command area:** land designed to receive water from an irrigation project.

**Command Area Development and Water Management:** the last-mile family of field channels, drains, land shaping, measurement, scheduling and farmer coordination that converts potential into use.

**Current anchor:** Ministry of Jal Shakti/PIB M-CADWM material, retrieved 23 September 2026, describes pilots using pressurised piped networks, SCADA and IoT. These technologies can improve measurement and control, but a pilot or sanctioned cluster is not proof of nationwide outcome.

### Water Users Associations and pricing

| Governance tool | Intended function | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Rotational delivery | Predictable allocation | Schedule not enforced |
| Water Users Association | Local distribution and maintenance | Elite capture or weak authority |
| Volumetric charge | Scarcity and O&M signal | Measurement cost and unreliable service |
| Area/crop charge | Administratively simple | Weak link to actual volume |
| Drainage investment | Protect soil and command productivity | Neglected because benefit is less visible |

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Privatisation or full-cost pricing will solve canal inefficiency."

**Reply:** Price can influence demand and fund maintenance only if users receive measurable, reliable service and can adjust crop or technology.

**Residual:** A uniform price can harm small or tail-end farmers. Service standards, lifeline protection and transparent allocation must accompany reform.

### UPSC integration

- **Verified link:** **2024 GS-III Q13** - irrigation-system challenges and government measures.
- **Verified link:** **2020 GS-III Q14**, 15 marks/250 words - measures to improve water storage and irrigation under depletion.
- **Approach:** Separate `storage -> conveyance -> command delivery -> drainage -> user governance`.
- **Trap:** A completed dam is not the same as utilised potential.

### Revision notes

1. Canal irrigation is a network service.
2. Command area differs from watershed.
3. Head-tail inequality is a distribution failure.
4. Waterlogging and salinity require drainage.
5. Tank performance depends on its catchment.
6. Lift irrigation trades gravity constraints for energy cost.
7. CADWM links the outlet to the farm.
8. WUAs need authority, inclusion, finance and data.
9. Pricing works only with reliable and measurable service.
10. M-CADWM technology is an instrument, not an outcome.

### Visual: the common-pool pumping game

```text
                    SHARED AQUIFER
               /         |          \
          Farmer A    Farmer B    Farmer C
             |           |           |
        private crop  private crop private crop
          benefit       benefit      benefit
               \         |          /
                cumulative pumping
                        |
                falling water table
                        |
          deeper wells + higher energy + exclusion
```

Groundwater is **subtractable**: water pumped by one user is unavailable to others. Excluding pumpers is difficult where rights, metering and aquifer knowledge are weak. That makes it a common-pool resource.

### Why private incentives diverge from social cost

An individual farmer compares expected crop return with private pumping cost. The farmer does not bear the full future cost imposed on neighbours when the water table falls.

```text
Private marginal cost < social marginal cost
                      ->
             excessive extraction risk
```

### The water-energy-crop nexus

| Link | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Cheap or unmetered power | Lowers perceived marginal pumping cost |
| Procurement or stable crop return | Encourages persistence of water-intensive crops |
| Assured groundwater access | Supports repeated cropping |
| Falling water table | Raises horsepower, energy and capital requirements |
| Deepening race | Excludes small farmers who cannot finance new wells |

This is why an electricity subsidy is both income support and a resource-use signal.

### A groundwater case

Two farmers each pump 100 units from an aquifer recharged by 150 units.

```text
Total extraction = 200
Recharge = 150
Net depletion = 50
```

Each farmer may rationally continue because reducing alone sacrifices output without securing the aquifer. Collective rules are needed.

### Governance toolbox

1. Aquifer mapping and water budgets.
2. Community disclosure of recharge and extraction.
3. Feeder separation, measurement or incentive-compatible power support.
4. Crop diversification and procurement alignment.
5. Recharge where hydrogeologically suitable.
6. Caps or collective rules in stressed units.
7. Protection for small and drinking-water users.

**Atal Bhujal Yojana:** a named community-groundwater-management anchor in water-stressed areas. Its logic is demand-side behaviour and local water budgeting, not merely construction.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Recharge structures can restore groundwater without changing farmer incentives."

**Reply:** Recharge helps where technically suitable, but unlimited extraction can absorb the gain. Supply augmentation without demand governance can intensify the pumping race.

**Residual:** Metering and crop shifts have political and distributional costs. Reform needs transition finance and credible alternatives, not abrupt withdrawal.

### UPSC integration

- **Verified link:** **2025 GS-III Q13**, 15 marks/250 words - factors responsible for groundwater depletion and government steps.
- **Directive:** Examine: establish causes, evaluate responses and expose residual gaps.
- **Approach:** `common-pool structure -> power/crop incentives -> unequal consequences -> Atal Jal/recharge/measurement/crop reform -> qualified verdict`.
- **Trap:** A functioning borewell does not prove sustainable groundwater.

### Revision notes

1. Groundwater is flexible privately but shared hydrologically.
2. Common-pool resources are difficult to exclude users from and are subtractable.
3. Private pumping cost omits part of the social depletion cost.
4. Cheap power changes extraction behaviour.
5. Crop incentives and water incentives interact.
6. Deepening wells can exclude poorer farmers.
7. Recharge is not a substitute for extraction control.
8. Atal Jal emphasises local plans and behavioural change.
9. Reform must combine equity, energy and crop transitions.
10. Aquifer is the correct hydrological management scale.

### Drip and sprinkler

| Feature | Drip | Sprinkler |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | Emitters near root zone | Pressurised spray over crop area |
| Strength | Precise, frequent application; fertigation | Flexible coverage; suitable for many field situations |
| Constraint | Clogging, filtration, layout cost | Wind drift, evaporation, energy |
| Best answer | Method plus crop/soil/water-quality context | Method plus wind, energy and application context |

### Irrigation scheduling and tensiometers

A tensiometer measures soil-water tension. It helps answer **when** to irrigate; it is not a pump, rainfall gauge or source of water.

Example:

```text
Calendar rule: irrigate every 5 days
Measurement rule: irrigate when soil-water tension crosses crop threshold
```

Measurement improves decisions only where the farmer controls timing and quantity.

### Fertigation

Fertigation applies fertiliser through irrigation water. It can split nutrients near the root zone and reduce some leaching, but it demands soluble inputs, correct concentration, maintenance and water-quality management.

### Efficiency calculation

Before drip:

```text
Water applied = 1,000 units/ha
Useful crop water = 500 units/ha
Application efficiency = 50%
Area = 10 ha
Total withdrawal = 10,000 units
```

After drip:

```text
Water applied = 700 units/ha
Area expands to 16 ha
Total withdrawal = 11,200 units
```

Per-hectare use fell 30%, but total withdrawal rose 12%. This is the rebound problem.

### Current anchor and caution

**Fact:** The June 2026 PMKSY backgrounder reported 110.92 lakh hectares under micro-irrigation.

**Inference:** Coverage measures adoption, not maintained functionality, consumptive saving or aquifer recovery. Evaluation needs crop, source, area expansion and groundwater trend.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "The rebound argument means micro-irrigation is useless."

**Reply:** It remains valuable for precision, labour, fertigation, crop quality and drought management. The argument is that subsidy must be paired with basin or aquifer governance.

**Residual:** Caps may be hard to enforce. Community budgets, energy incentives and crop-market reform must reinforce one another.

- **Approach:** Explain mechanism and benefits, then capital/maintenance constraints and basin-level rebound.
- **Trap:** "Per drop more crop" is not equivalent to "less total groundwater extraction."

### Three insurance designs

| Design | Trigger | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual indemnity | Assessed individual loss | Closest to actual farm loss | Costly verification and moral hazard |
| Area-yield index | Notified-area yield relative to threshold | Pools covariate crop risk | Basis risk within area |
| Weather index | Measured rainfall/temperature parameter | Fast, objective trigger | Weather station may not match crop loss |

### PMFBY essentials

PMFBY, launched in Kharif 2016, is an area-yield-based public-private crop-insurance architecture with additional specified localised/post-harvest provisions under guidelines.

Farmer premium shares commonly cited in official scheme material:

| Crop/season | Farmer share of actuarial premium |
|---|---:|
| Kharif food and oilseed crops | 2% |
| Rabi food and oilseed crops | 1.5% |
| Annual commercial/horticultural crops | 5% |

Government supports the balance subject to applicable sharing and scheme rules. These are farmer premium shares, not guaranteed claim percentages.

### Insurance unit and threshold yield

In an area-yield design:

```text
Threshold yield = historical average yield x indemnity level

Indicative claim ratio =
(threshold yield - actual area yield) / threshold yield
```

If the area yield remains above threshold, a badly affected individual plot may receive no area-yield claim. That is basis risk, explored next.

### Covered stages

Scheme guidelines can include:

- prevented sowing/planting risk;
- standing-crop losses from notified perils;
- localised calamities;
- specified post-harvest losses;
- mid-season adversity provisions.

Exact notice periods, crops, districts and operational conditions must be checked for the season.

### PMFBY scale and technology reported 29 August 2026

**Fact:** The August 2026 PIB backgrounder reported PMFBY cumulative applications and paid-claim figures for scheme years 2016-17 to 2024-25 and described YES-TECH and WINDS.

- **YES-TECH:** technology-assisted yield estimation.
- **WINDS:** weather information network and data system.

**Inference:** Technology can reduce measurement delay and dispute, but model error, missing data and governance remain.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "A low farmer premium proves the insurance is cheap and effective."

**Reply:** Affordability is only one dimension. Timely enrolment, notified crop, data, claim speed, basis risk and farmer comprehension determine value.

**Residual:** Large premium support creates fiscal and contracting incentives that require transparent tendering, data audit and grievance redress.

- No directly routed Mains PYQ through 2026 asks PMFBY alone.
- Insurance remains a canonical Topic 14 owner because it connects farm credit, covariate risk and resilience.
- **Trap:** Premium rate is not claim rate; crop loss is not automatically a covered trigger.
- **Answer use:** Compare indemnity and index insurance before evaluating PMFBY.

1. Insurance pools and transfers specified risk.
2. It does not guarantee profit or full income.
3. Indemnity insurance assesses individual loss.
4. Area-yield insurance uses a notified unit.
5. Weather index uses measured parameters.
6. PMFBY farmer premium shares differ by crop category.
7. Government supports the actuarial premium balance under rules.
8. Threshold and actual yield determine area-yield claims.
9. Notified crop, season, unit and notice conditions matter.

### Visual: four farms, one area index

```text
NOTIFIED INSURANCE UNIT

Farm A: normal yield        100

Farm D: good irrigation     110
                         -------
Area average                85
Threshold yield             80

Index says: no area-yield claim
Farm C says: severe loss
Difference = BASIS RISK
```

Basis risk is the mismatch between an index-based payout and an individual's actual loss.

### Both directions of basis risk

| Situation | Result |
|---|---|
| Individual loss but index does not trigger | Farmer receives no/low payout despite loss |
| Index triggers but individual loss is small | Farmer may receive payout above individual loss |

The first undermines trust; the second raises fiscal/insurance cost but preserves objective rules.

### Why basis risk arises

- insurance unit too large;
- sparse or poorly located weather stations;
- CCE sampling error;
- crop or sowing data error;
- localised pest, hail or drainage event;
- timing mismatch;
- model error in remote sensing.

### Technology can narrow, not abolish, the gap

| Tool | Contribution | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| CCE app/geotagging | Better audit trail | Sampling and field execution |
| Remote sensing | Spatial coverage | Calibration and cloud/model error |
| Weather stations | Objective parameter data | Location and maintenance |
| Smartphones/CROPIC | Crop observation and imagery | Access, validation and privacy |
| Direct transfer | Faster payment channel | Bad upstream data still produces wrong payment |

### Risk layering

```text
Frequent, small loss       -> farm savings / agronomy
Moderate insurable loss    -> insurance
Severe covariate disaster  -> public relief / catastrophe layer
Long-run climate shift     -> adaptation and livelihood transformation
```

Insurance is inefficient for every small predictable loss and inadequate for every systemic catastrophe.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Use individual loss assessment everywhere to eliminate basis risk."

**Reply:** Individual assessment narrows index mismatch but raises verification cost, delay, fraud and moral hazard across millions of small plots.

**Residual:** Hybrid design is still data-intensive. Smaller units, localised-cover protocols and auditable technology require administrative capacity.

### UPSC integration

- No direct verified PYQ through 2026 names basis risk.
- It is the advanced concept that prevents a superficial PMFBY answer.
- **Trap:** Faster payment does not prove accurate loss measurement.
- **Answer use:** Always add one numerical basis-risk scenario and a risk-layering conclusion.

### Revision notes

1. Basis risk is index-loss mismatch.
2. It can operate in both payout directions.
3. Larger units can hide local loss.
4. CCE error affects area-yield claims.
5. Weather stations may not represent every farm.
6. Remote sensing requires calibration.
7. Digital payment fixes the last mile, not upstream data.
8. Insurance belongs to a risk layer.
9. Adaptation addresses changing expected loss, not only one shock.
10. Hybrid design trades precision against cost and delay.

### Integrated Farming System

IFS deliberately links crop and allied enterprises so that:

- waste from one becomes input to another;
- income arrives at different times;
- household food and nutrition diversify;
- climate and market risk is spread;
- labour is used across seasons.

It is not mere coexistence. Integration requires material, financial or labour linkages.

**Verified Mains links:** **2019 GS-III Q3** and **2022 GS-III Q14** ask how IFS helps agricultural production and small/marginal farmers.

### Agroforestry

Trees on farms can supply fodder, fuel, fruit, timber, shade, soil protection and carbon storage. Constraints include long gestation, tree-tenure/felling/transit rules, market access, water competition and unsuitable species choice.

### Millets and diversification

Millets can fit dryland resilience and nutrition strategies, but "low water" is crop- and location-relative. Demand, processing, procurement and consumer preference determine farm return.

### Climate-Smart Agriculture

CSA is commonly framed around three objectives:

1. sustainably increase productivity and income;
2. adapt and build resilience;
3. reduce or remove greenhouse-gas emissions where possible.

Trade-offs exist. An irrigation pump may stabilise yield yet increase energy emissions or groundwater stress.

**Verified objective link:** 2021 Prelims Q59 tests CSA, CCAFS and ICRISAT relationships. No answer key is claimed locally.

### NMSA architecture

The official NMSA strategy emphasises location-specific rainfed planning, convergence and consortium/single-window knowledge involving SAUs, KVKs and ICAR.

**NMSA evidence caution, anchored to 25 July 2026:** Programme counts and "climate resilient village/variety" outputs are not sufficient without adoption, yield stability, income and resource indicators.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Diversification always lowers risk."

**Reply:** It lowers risk when income streams are imperfectly correlated and each enterprise is viable. Poorly chosen enterprises can multiply complexity and disease/market exposure.

**Residual:** Small farms face labour, skill, working-capital and market constraints. Extension and collective services determine whether integration is manageable.

### The five policy contradictions

| Instrument | Intended benefit | Possible contradiction |
|---|---|---|
| Free/cheap power | Affordable irrigation | Groundwater over-extraction |
| Cheap urea | Nutrient affordability | N-P-K imbalance and pollution |
| Credit expansion | Input and capital access | Over-borrowing or asset mismatch |
| Insurance subsidy | Affordable risk transfer | Weak claim experience or fiscal opacity |
| Irrigation subsidy | Efficiency/adoption | Rebound and unequal capture |

The correct response is not to abolish support but to redesign its incidence and behavioural signal.

### Reform sequence

1. **Measure the resource:** aquifer, soil, command and rainfall data.
2. **Protect basic access:** drinking water, smallholder and tail-end safeguards.
3. **Price or cap harmful margins:** not necessarily the first essential unit.
4. **Create alternatives:** crop markets, power-transition incentives, custom services.
5. **Finance adoption:** KCC, investment credit, JLG/FPO channels.
6. **Layer risk:** agronomy, savings, insurance, relief and adaptation.
7. **Audit outcomes:** resource, income, equity and fiscal metrics.

### Subsidy incidence

The legal recipient of a subsidy may not capture its full economic benefit. A fertiliser subsidy paid to a manufacturer is intended to lower farmer price, but pass-through depends on supply, availability, quality and market structure. An electricity subsidy may benefit the pump owner more than a landless tenant.

### Technology boundary conditions

```text
Technology benefit
 = technical potential
 x reliable access
 x correct use
 x complementary infrastructure
 x viable incentive
```

If any multiplier is close to zero, a high-tech device can become a low-impact asset.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks / 150 words

### Question 1

**Differentiate irrigation efficiency from water productivity. Why does the distinction matter?**

**Model answer:** Irrigation efficiency relates useful water at a defined stage to water supplied at that stage; examples include conveyance and application efficiency. Water productivity relates crop output or value to water applied or consumed. The first focuses on a physical flow ratio; the second on output per unit water. A lining or drip project may improve local efficiency, but basin saving may be smaller if seepage was recoverable or cropped area expands. Conversely, a crop shift can raise economic water productivity without changing field hardware. Policy should therefore report the scale, denominator, return flow, crop area and whether output is physical or monetary. The distinction prevents a plot-level efficiency gain from being advertised automatically as aquifer recovery.

### Question 2

**Explain why extension is a productive agricultural input.**

**Model answer:** Extension converts research and data into location-specific decisions on variety, sowing time, nutrient dose, pest threshold, irrigation schedule and risk. Its mechanism is not one-way messaging: ICAR/SAU knowledge is demonstrated through KVKs and adapted through farmer feedback. It raises the return to seed, fertiliser, water and machinery by improving timing and combination. It also reduces information asymmetry in new technologies and insurance. Its limits are generic advisories, weak local staffing, unequal digital access and conflict of interest in input-linked advice. Extension should be measured by changed practice and farm outcome, not messages sent.

## 15 marks / 250 words

### Question 3

**"The crisis of Indian irrigation is increasingly a governance crisis rather than only an infrastructure shortage." Analyse.**

**Model answer:** India still needs context-specific storage and last-mile assets, but several failures arise after infrastructure is created. Canal commands face incomplete field channels, poor maintenance, head-tail inequity, unmeasured releases, waterlogging and weak drainage. Groundwater offers flexible private control but is a common pool: cheap power and crop incentives reduce private pumping cost while depletion is shared. Tank systems lose capacity through catchment degradation and encroachment. Micro-irrigation improves application yet can produce rebound if area or water-intensive crops expand.

Government responses include AIBP, CADWM/M-CADWM, WDC-PMKSY, PDMC under RKVY, Jal Shakti/Catch the Rain and Atal Bhujal. Their effectiveness depends on aquifer and basin data, reliable delivery, WUAs, tail-end representation, operation-and-maintenance finance, crop alternatives and accountable measurement. Thus infrastructure remains necessary, but governance determines who receives water, at what reliability, with what resource consequence.

### Question 4

**Assess the strengths and limitations of KCC and crop insurance as instruments of farm resilience.**

**Model answer:** KCC addresses the liquidity gap between input purchase and harvest through a revolving limit for eligible production and allied needs. Interest support can lower formal borrowing cost and reduce dependence on informal lenders. Crop insurance transfers specified covariate risk through subsidised premium and area-yield or related triggers. Together, credit enables risk-taking and insurance protects part of the downside.

Their limits differ. KCC may exclude tenants because operational cultivation is not recorded; late sanction, small limits and rollover can weaken value. Cheap credit can finance unsustainable extraction or unsuitable assets. Insurance faces basis risk, data error, notification conditions, claim delay and low comprehension. Account/application counts also overstate unique reach if denominators are ignored.

Reform requires cultivator-sensitive records, JLGs, cash-flow lending, appropriate loan maturity, transparent premium/claim data, smaller or hybrid insurance units, auditable technology and grievance redress. Neither instrument substitutes for price policy, extension or climate adaptation.

## 20 marks / 250-300 words

### Question 5

**Design an integrated strategy for sustainable intensification of Indian agriculture.**

**Model answer:** Sustainable intensification means raising stable output and income from existing resources without degrading the stocks on which future production depends.

First, manage water at the correct scale: watershed treatment in rainfed areas, command-area completion and drainage in canal systems, and aquifer budgets with power/crop-incentive reform for groundwater. Couple micro-irrigation with extraction monitoring to prevent rebound.

Second, raise input productivity: plural seed systems with quality assurance; soil-test-based N-P-K-secondary/micronutrient management; gradual correction of urea distortion; INM, IPM and reliable extension. Provide machinery as a service through transparent custom hiring.

Third, finance inclusion: match loan maturity to cash flow, use KCC and FY 2025-26 MISS support, strengthen cooperatives/NABARD channels, and recognise tenants through credible leases/JLGs.

Fourth, layer risk: agronomy and savings for frequent small loss, transparent crop insurance for moderate covariate loss, public catastrophe relief and long-run adaptation.

Fifth, diversify through IFS, agroforestry, millets and locally suitable conservation, organic or natural practices. Evaluate crop-specific transition economics rather than ideology.

Finally, measure reliable access, water productivity, net income, tenant/tail-end inclusion, soil/aquifer trend and fiscal cost. This shifts policy from input maximisation to resilience maximisation.

### Question 6

**Agricultural subsidies are simultaneously instruments of welfare, production and environmental change. Discuss with reference to irrigation, power, fertiliser, credit and insurance.**

**Model answer:** Farm subsidies protect income, make inputs affordable and support food security. Irrigation capital support expands access; cheap power enables pumping; fertiliser support lowers nutrient cost; interest subvention improves formal credit affordability; premium subsidy makes insurance purchasable.

Yet each changes marginal incentives. Cheap or unmetered power can intensify common-pool groundwater extraction. Urea's relative price can bias nutrient use. Irrigation equipment subsidy may be captured by larger farmers or cause rebound. Cheap credit may encourage rollover or unsuitable assets. Insurance subsidy can expand enrolment while data, basis risk and claim experience remain weak. Economic incidence may differ from the legal payment route: a manufacturer, landlord, pump owner or input dealer may capture part of the benefit.

Reform should not equate rationalisation with abrupt withdrawal. Protect basic smallholder access, but meter or price harmful margins; use direct/transparent benefit routes where appropriate; build crop and energy alternatives; target tenants and tail users; strengthen soil, aquifer and claims data; and publish outcome-based fiscal metrics. A subsidy is justified when its distributional and social benefits exceed fiscal and ecological cost.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core thesis

Agricultural resilience is produced by a chain:

```text
resource security
 -> reliable irrigation
 -> suitable seed and balanced inputs
 -> knowledge and timely operations
 -> affordable formal finance
 -> layered risk management
 -> diversified, sustainable production
```

The chain fails at its weakest complementary input. Public support must therefore be convergent without erasing instrument-specific accountability.

## B. Water and irrigation

- Net irrigated area counts land irrigated at least once.
- Gross irrigated area counts every irrigated crop instance.
- Irrigation intensity = gross/net x 100.
- Potential created is designed capacity; potential utilised is actual use.
- Application efficiency is root-zone storage divided by field delivery.
- Water productivity must specify output/value and water denominator.
- Canal challenges: incomplete last mile, head-tail inequity, silt, breach, unreliable rotation, waterlogging and salinity.
- Tanks need catchment protection, desiltation and collective maintenance.
- Lift irrigation overcomes gravity but needs recurring energy and repair.
- Groundwater is a common-pool resource: difficult exclusion plus subtractability.
- Water-energy-crop incentives determine extraction.
- Recharge without demand management can be absorbed by extra pumping.
- Watershed = catchment draining to a common outlet; treat ridge to valley.
- PMKSY combines project completion, access, watershed and efficiency families; date component status.
- PDMC is implemented under RKVY according to the official PMKSY portal rechecked 23 September 2026.
- Jal Shakti/Catch the Rain is campaign and convergence mode.
- Atal Jal emphasises community groundwater plans and behaviour.
- Drip targets root zones; sprinkler distributes pressurised spray.
- Tensiometer measures soil-water tension for scheduling.
- Fertigation applies soluble nutrients through irrigation.
- Plot saving is not automatically basin saving.

## C. Seeds, soil, nutrients and protection

- Seed sequence: nucleus -> breeder -> foundation -> certified.
- Quality assurance is not universal agro-climatic suitability.
- Formal and informal seed systems both matter.
- Tissue culture and bud-chip settlings are propagation techniques.
- Seeds Act 1966 remains the verified legal anchor; draft Bill status must be dated.
- Extension links ICAR/SAU research, KVK demonstration and farmer feedback.
- Soil health has physical, chemical and biological dimensions.
- N, P and K perform different functions.
- Urea supplies nitrogen.
- Urea price/subsidy architecture differs from NBS for eligible P&K fertilisers.
- Fertiliser DBT reimburses eligible firms after authenticated sale.
- INM combines mineral, organic and biological sources.
- Nano-fertiliser claims require crop-soil-dose evidence.
- Biochar is context-dependent carbon-rich material from limited-oxygen heating.
- IPM = prevention + monitoring + threshold + integrated response.
- IPM does not mean no pesticide.
- Mechanisation improves timeliness and can reduce drudgery.
- CHCs convert expensive ownership into a shared service.

## D. Agricultural credit

- Farm credit bridges the interval between expenditure and harvest income.
- Match repayment maturity with the income-generating asset.
- Channels: commercial banks, RRBs and cooperatives.
- Cooperative short-term structure: StCB -> DCCB -> PACS.
- NABARD supports refinance, development, specified supervision and RIDF.
- RBI Directions updated 7 August 2026 prescribe the cited domestic-bank PSL position: 40% overall; 18% agriculture; 14% NCF; 10% SMF, nested as applicable.
- KCC is revolving credit for eligible needs.
- FY 2025-26 MISS provides the cited interest-support framework; prompt repayment conditions matter.
- The 11 March 2026 official anchor reported more than 7.72 crore operational KCCs and about Rs 10.20 lakh crore outstanding.
- Account count is not unique farmer count.
- Tenant-title mismatch creates exclusion.
- JLG/SHG social collateral can reduce information and collateral barriers.
- Cheap credit cannot help a farmer denied access.
- Farmer distress is multi-causal; avoid monocausal suicide claims.

## E. Crop insurance

- Insurance transfers specified covered risk; it does not guarantee income.
- Individual indemnity is closest to farm loss but costly to assess.
- Area-yield index pools covariate crop risk but creates basis risk.
- Weather index is objective and fast but may mismatch actual loss.
- Standard PMFBY farmer shares: 2% Kharif food/oilseed; 1.5% Rabi food/oilseed; 5% annual commercial/horticultural.
- Premium share is not claim share.
- Threshold yield and actual area yield drive the index.
- Covered stages and notice conditions are season-specific.
- YES-TECH assists yield estimation; WINDS strengthens weather data.
- Digital payment does not correct wrong upstream data.
- Basis risk can mean loss without payout or payout without matching loss.
- Reduce basis risk through unit design, data density, calibrated technology, localised protocols and grievance redress.
- Risk layering: savings/agronomy -> insurance -> public catastrophe relief -> adaptation.

## F. Sustainable and climate-resilient agriculture

- Conservation agriculture: minimum disturbance, soil cover and diversification.
- Zero tillage is one practice, not the full system.
- Organic farming is standards/certification linked.
- Natural farming emphasises on-farm ecological processes and reduced purchased inputs.
- Permaculture is integrated ecological design.
- IFS creates material, labour or cash-flow linkages across enterprises.
- Agroforestry combines trees with crop/livestock systems.
- Millets can support dryland resilience, subject to value-chain viability.
- CSA combines productivity, adaptation and mitigation where possible.
- Objectives can conflict; water-saving hardware may raise energy use or total withdrawal.
- NMSA emphasises location-specific rainfed planning and knowledge convergence.
- Transition evaluation needs yield, cost, labour, soil, risk and net-income data.

## G. High-yield traps

1. Created potential != utilised potential.
2. Irrigation intensity != efficiency.
3. Application efficiency != basin saving.
4. Watershed != command area.
5. Recharge increase != aquifer recovery if extraction rises more.
6. Urea != balanced fertiliser.
7. Fertiliser DBT != unconditional farmer transfer.
8. Draft Seeds Bill != enacted Act.
9. IPM != chemical prohibition.
10. PSL sub-targets are not additive.
11. KCC != grant.
12. Premium != indemnity.
13. Application/account != unique beneficiary.
14. Technology adoption != technology distribution.
15. Organic != natural != conservation != permaculture.
16. Diversification != guaranteed risk reduction.
17. Provisional answer key != verified final key.
