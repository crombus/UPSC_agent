# Industrial Policy, 1991 Reforms, PSUs and Disinvestment — Live Learning Edition

**GS-III syllabus anchor (verbatim):** “Effects of liberalization on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth.”

Industrial policy is the framework shaping industrial entry, competition, technology, location, finance and strategic capability. It is wider than licensing, subsidies or public ownership. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Start with the practical problem

A steel plant needs power, railways, finance and skilled labour. Each supplier may wait for the others to invest. Prices alone may not coordinate these large complementary investments, especially in a capital-scarce economy. Industrial policy tries to solve that problem.

```text
Public purpose
   ↓
Identify failure: coordination / monopoly / learning / security / regional imbalance
   ↓
Choose instrument: ownership / licensing / finance / infrastructure / standards / procurement
   ↓
State obligation + accountability + review rule
   ↓
Test entry, productivity, jobs, technology, exports and resilience
```

The visual separates **objective** from **instrument**. Public ownership, a tariff or a licence is defensible only if it solves a stated problem better than available alternatives.

**Fact / canonical concept:** Industrial policy may operate through law, infrastructure, credit, trade rules, standards, skills, procurement or ownership. **Inference:** The real choice is not “state versus market” in the abstract. It is which institution addresses a specific failure at lower social cost.

### The 1948 mixed-economy settlement

**Fact:** The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948 accepted private enterprise while assigning the state a major strategic and developmental role. It established a mixed-economy direction; it did **not** yet create the 1956 Schedule A/B/C structure.

The settlement responded to a narrow industrial base, weak infrastructure, scarce patient capital and the goal of economic autonomy. Private capital could contribute, but the state was expected to coordinate and undertake large, long-gestation investments.

**Criticism:** Coordination can turn into discretionary control, rent seeking and suppression of entrepreneurship.

**Reply:** Uncoordinated private expectations can also produce chronic underinvestment. The defensible response is a clear mandate, transparent instrument, measurable outcome and periodic review—not blind faith in either ownership form.

### UPSC use and traps

- Use 1948 as the mixed-economy baseline; do not project the 1956 schedules backward.
- Industrial policy is not synonymous with subsidies.
- A pro-market entry reform can coexist with strategic public investment.
- **Stable concept:** no separate current claim is needed for the 1948 settlement. The dated Survey anchor only shows that coordination and state capacity remain live analytical issues.

### Revision notes

1. Industrial policy shapes entry, competition, technology, location and capability.
2. Coordination failure arises when complementary investments depend on one another.
3. Ownership is one instrument, not the definition of policy.
4. Infrastructure, finance, standards and procurement are also industrial-policy tools.
5. The 1948 Resolution established a mixed-economy settlement.
6. Private enterprise remained legitimate.
7. The state assumed strategic, developmental and coordinating roles.
8. The 1948 settlement differs from the 1956 schedules.
9. Market failure must be weighed against government failure.
10. Good policy specifies objective, instrument, accountability and review.


### Concept check

**Question:** A government wants steel capacity but power, rail and finance investors each wait for the others. What failure exists and what is the policy test?

**Model answer:** It is a coordination failure. Specify the missing complementarity, choose the least-cost instrument, assign accountability and review whether capacity, productivity and public value actually improve.

**Misconception to avoid:** State ownership is not the definition of industrial policy.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Why is industrial policy better understood as a coordination framework than as a synonym for state ownership?

#### Evidence-rich response

Industrial policy addresses failures that a single firm cannot solve. A steel plant, for example, needs power, rail, finance and skills to arrive together; each investor may wait for the others. The 1948 Industrial Policy Resolution therefore adopted a mixed economy: private enterprise remained legitimate, while the state accepted strategic and coordinating roles. The mechanism is broader than ownership—public infrastructure, development finance, standards, procurement and regulation can align complementary investment. Yet intervention can create discretion and rent seeking. Hence the test is not “state versus market” but: identify the failure, choose the least-cost instrument, publish accountability and review entry, productivity, jobs and resilience. Ownership is justified only when alternatives cannot secure the public purpose.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: coordination failure; at least three instruments; accurate 1948 mixed-economy evidence; government-failure qualification; review criterion.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Schedule | Intended leadership | Core logic |
|---|---|---|
| A | State responsibility for listed industries | Strategic/basic commanding heights |
| B | Increasing state role, with private scope | Progressive public leadership |
| C | Remaining industries generally open to private enterprise under policy controls | Private production within planning |

The system ranked sectors by the intended public role; it did not erase private industry.

### Why commanding heights?

Heavy industry, power, transport and basic materials have broad linkages and require large, patient investment. In the 1950s, shallow capital markets and the autonomy objective supported public-sector leadership alongside the Second Plan’s heavy-industry strategy.

```text
Capital scarcity + infrastructure gaps + autonomy goal
                         ↓
         public investment in basic industry
                         ↓
      steel / machinery / power / transport
                         ↓
          wider industrial possibilities
```

**Fact:** The 1956 Resolution deepened the public role through Schedules A, B and C. **Inference:** Its strength was foundational capacity; its weakness was the danger that protected producers would face weak cost, quality and innovation discipline.

A public enterprise may be justified at birth because capital is unavailable. That does not settle whether it should retain the same mandate decades later. Historical rationale and present performance are different tests.

**Criticism:** The architecture concentrated decisions and weakened competition.

**Reply:** The proper counterfactual is whether adequate basic capacity would have emerged under the capital and infrastructure constraints of the time. A balanced verdict credits capacity creation while demanding later adaptation.

### UPSC use and traps

- “Public-sector leadership” is more accurate than “all industry became public.”
- Schedules A/B/C are policy categories, not Plans.
- Link commanding heights to long gestation, capital scarcity and economy-wide spillovers.
- Retest mandates as technology, private capability and regulatory capacity change.

### Revision notes

1. The 1956 Resolution formalised Schedules A, B and C.
2. Schedule A placed listed industries under state responsibility.
3. Schedule B envisaged an increasing state role.
4. Schedule C retained a broad private domain.
5. Commanding heights meant basic and strategic sectors with wide linkages.
6. The model complemented the Second Plan.
7. Capital scarcity and long gestation supported public leadership.
8. Capacity creation and enterprise efficiency are separate questions.
9. Protection may weaken innovation discipline.
10. Historical mandates require periodic retesting.


### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the 1956 model be historically rational yet unsuitable as a permanent ownership rule?

**Model answer:** The architecture addressed capital scarcity and basic-industry coordination, but retention today needs a fresh enterprise-level mandate, competition and alternatives test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Historical contribution does not create an indefinite retention right.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (15 marks · 250 words):** Evaluate the developmental logic and later limitations of the 1956 industrial architecture.

#### Evidence-rich response

The Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 translated the Second Plan’s heavy-industry strategy into Schedules A, B and C. Schedule A placed listed commanding heights under state responsibility; Schedule B envisaged growing public participation; Schedule C preserved a private domain under planning controls. This was developmentally rational because steel, machinery, power and transport required large, long-gestation investment when domestic capital markets and private capability were shallow. Public capacity could create economy-wide linkages and strategic autonomy.

Its limitation was institutional. Protection, administrative allocation and weak competitive pressure could reduce cost and innovation discipline; multiple objectives and soft budgets obscured accountability. The correct evaluation is therefore dynamic: credit the architecture for foundational capacity, but do not convert that history into permanent ownership. Each enterprise must be retested against its present strategic mandate, private alternatives, market structure, regulatory capacity and measurable public-service obligation. The 1956 model solved a historical coordination problem; it did not settle every later retain-or-exit decision.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: schedules; capital-scarcity rationale; named basic-industry evidence; protection/soft-budget limitation; dynamic mandate test.

## Lesson 3 — Licence raj and the 1980s transition: why controls emerged and why they loosened

Progress: 3 / 18 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Licence raj and the 1980s transition: why controls emerged and why they loosened

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — IDR Act licensing and the pre-1991 industrial-policy chronology
CA search: "India industrial delicensing history IDR Act 1951 Sixth Seventh Plan"
CA found: no separate current claim required; historical sequence is the teaching anchor
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Imagine a 1975 machine-tool entrepreneur. Demand is not enough: permissions may affect entry, capacity, location, expansion, imports and technology. The regime tries to allocate scarce capital and foreign exchange, but every permission also creates delay and bargaining power.

```text
Scarcity + planning priorities
          ↓
IDR Act 1951 licensing: entry / capacity / location / expansion
          ↓
intended allocation ────── administrative delay and rent risk
          ↓                              ↓
planned capacity                 weak rivalry and innovation
```

**Fact:** The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 underpinned industrial licensing. Controls sought planned allocation, regional dispersal, reduced concentration and foreign-exchange conservation. The analytical error is to confuse original purpose with actual effect.

### The 1980s were a transition

Productivity, modernisation, exports and capacity utilisation became pressing. The Sixth Plan emphasised modernisation and productivity. The Seventh Plan pushed technology upgradation, capacity use and export growth, with selective delicensing and a larger practical private role.

This was neither unchanged licence raj nor completed liberalisation. Piecemeal relaxation could even favour incumbents holding existing licences and networks. Transparent, general rules were needed to avoid selective privilege.

### MRTP, concentration and the control architecture

The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 addressed concentration of economic power and restrictive trade practices, but the pre-1991 architecture also required large “MRTP companies” above an asset threshold to obtain prior approval for expansion, new undertakings and takeovers. This mixed two different tasks: controlling harmful conduct and controlling firm size before conduct occurred.

```text
Large asset size
   ↓ pre-entry scrutiny under the old MRTP threshold
permission for expansion / new undertaking / takeover
   ↓
intended check on concentration
   └─ risk: size treated as wrongdoing even without abusive conduct

Post-reform direction:
firm size is not itself the offence → examine combinations, cartels and abuse of dominance
```

The threshold rose during the 1980s as policy became more permissive. The 1991 policy removed the asset-threshold approval requirement. The Competition Act, 2002 later supplied a conduct-and-effects-oriented framework. The safe distinction is: **MRTP-era prior size control is not the same as modern competition law**.

### Licence is not regulation

A licence is prior permission to enter or expand. Regulation can set general competition, safety, environmental and consumer rules after entry. Abolishing discretionary entry control does not abolish regulation.

### UPSC use and traps

- Do not assign systemic post-1991 financial deregulation to the Sixth or Seventh Plan.
- Do not describe the 1980s as a clean break.
- Distinguish IDR Act licensing from environmental or safety standards.
- Use purpose–effect–reform as the analytical sequence.

### Revision notes

1. IDR Act 1951 underpinned industrial licensing.
2. Licences influenced entry, capacity, location and expansion.
3. Controls aimed at planned allocation and forex conservation.
4. Administrative discretion created delay and rent risks.
5. Protected incumbents could face weak rivalry.
6. Sixth Plan stressed modernisation and productivity.
7. Seventh Plan stressed technology, capacity utilisation and exports.
8. The 1980s saw selective, not systemic, loosening.
9. Delicensing is not abolition of all regulation.
10. General rules are preferable to case-by-case permission where feasible.


### Concept check

**Question:** Why is replacing prior size approval with competition law not the same as abandoning control of monopoly?

**Model answer:** Modern competition law targets harmful combinations and conduct rather than treating size alone as guilt; the state changes the object and method of control.

**Misconception to avoid:** Delicensing does not mean monopoly conduct is lawful.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** How did licensing and MRTP-era controls pursue legitimate goals yet become barriers to industrial dynamism?

#### Evidence-rich response

Licensing under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 sought planned allocation, regional dispersal and foreign-exchange conservation. The MRTP Act, 1969 similarly sought to curb concentration, but its asset threshold required large firms to obtain prior approval for expansion and takeovers. These aims were legitimate; the mechanism became restrictive because case-by-case permission generated delay, bargaining power and incumbent advantage, while size could be treated as wrongdoing without proven abuse. The Sixth and Seventh Plans raised thresholds and selectively loosened controls; the 1991 policy removed most licensing and MRTP threshold approval. The Competition Act, 2002 later shifted scrutiny toward cartels, harmful combinations and abuse. Thus reform replaced discretionary entry control with conduct- and effects-based rules, not with absence of regulation.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: IDR Act; MRTP threshold logic; 1980s transition; 1991 replacement; licence-versus-regulation distinction.

## Lesson 4 — The 1991 crisis and reform package: stabilisation, structural reform and causal sequence

Progress: 4 / 18 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: The 1991 crisis and reform package: stabilisation, structural reform and causal sequence

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — balance-of-payments crisis, LPG reforms and New Industrial Policy 1991
CA search: "1991 India industrial reform stabilisation structural reform sequence"
CA found: no separate current claim required; the lesson concerns a settled historical reform sequence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

A crisis has two treatment horizons. Stabilisation stops the immediate deterioration; structural reform changes the arrangements that generated vulnerability or blocked adjustment.

```text
External-payments stress + macro imbalance
                 ↓
STABILISATION: financing / demand restraint / exchange adjustment
                 ↓
STRUCTURAL CHANGE: delicensing + trade + exchange + finance reforms
                 ↓
new prices, entry conditions and competitive pressures
```

**Fact:** The New Industrial Policy of 1991 substantially dismantled industrial licensing and narrowed public-sector reservation. It formed part of a wider trade, exchange-rate and financial reform package. Liberalisation was not merely the sale of government shares.

### What the New Industrial Policy changed

| Reform axis | Pre-reform constraint | 1991 direction | Continuing boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industrial licensing | prior approval across a wide field | licensing abolished for most industries | a narrow list and other legal clearances remained |
| Public-sector reservation | broad commanding-height reservation | reserved field sharply narrowed | strategic and sovereign functions retained special treatment |
| MRTP companies | asset-size threshold triggered prior expansion/takeover approval | threshold-based prior approval removed | harmful combinations and conduct still require competition oversight |
| Foreign investment | restrictive approvals and foreign-exchange control | automatic approval opened in specified high-priority industries and policy progressively widened | sectoral caps, security screening and government route remained where prescribed |
| Foreign technology | case-by-case control | easier approval for specified agreements | technology depth and domestic learning were not automatic |

The reform was therefore a replacement diagram: **administrative allocation by prior permission gave way to entry, price and investment decisions disciplined increasingly by markets and general law**.

### Why package thinking matters

A firm freed to expand gains little if it cannot import technology, obtain finance or move goods reliably. Trade and exchange reform change input costs and export incentives; financial reform changes capital allocation; delicensing changes entry and expansion. Their complementarity produces the larger behavioural effect.

- **Liberalisation:** reduction of controls to expand choice and competition.
- **Privatisation:** increase in private ownership or control.
- **Globalisation:** deeper cross-border integration in trade, investment, finance and technology.

They overlap but are not synonyms. Delicensing can occur without PSU sale; minority disinvestment can occur without control transfer.

**Criticism:** Crisis-driven reform was simply externally imposed.

**Reply:** External constraint shaped timing and choices, but domestic reform debates and selective 1980s changes predated the crisis. The balanced account separates compulsion, domestic design and phased implementation.

### UPSC use and traps

- Separate short-run stabilisation from long-run incentive reform.
- Write the connected package, not an LPG slogan.
- Do not claim every reform occurred instantly.
- Liberalisation changed the state’s role; it did not abolish it.

### Revision notes

1. The trigger included a severe balance-of-payments crisis.
2. Stabilisation addresses immediate macro/external imbalance.
3. Structural reform changes institutions and incentives.
4. NIP 1991 substantially reduced licensing.
5. Public-sector reservation narrowed.
6. Trade and exchange reform changed input and export incentives.
7. Financial reform changed allocation and prudential rules over time.
8. LPG components are related but distinct.
9. Reform effects depend on institutional complementarities.
10. The 1980s transition prevents an overnight-break story.


### Concept check

**Question:** Classify delicensing and trade reform as stabilisation or structural reform, and explain why.

**Model answer:** They are structural reforms because they change rules and incentives; stabilisation addresses the immediate external and macro imbalance.

**Misconception to avoid:** A crisis trigger does not make every reform a short-run stabilisation measure.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Why must the 1991 reforms be analysed as a connected stabilisation and structural package?

#### Evidence-rich response

The 1991 response had two horizons. Stabilisation addressed the immediate balance-of-payments and macroeconomic stress through financing, demand restraint and exchange adjustment. Structural reform changed incentives: the New Industrial Policy substantially delicensed industry, narrowed public-sector reservation and removed MRTP asset-threshold approval; trade, exchange and financial reforms altered input access, export prices and capital allocation. These measures reinforced one another—a firm legally free to expand still needs imported technology, finance and market access. Domestic reform debates and 1980s selective loosening also predated the crisis, so “external imposition” is incomplete. Implementation was phased and imposed worker, firm and regional adjustment costs. Hence 1991 was neither one devaluation nor simple privatisation; it replaced administrative allocation with competition and market signals while preserving regulatory and developmental responsibilities.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: stabilisation/structural distinction; four reform axes; firm-level complementarity; gradualism; continuing-state qualification.

## Lesson 5 — From licence to competition: how liberalisation transmits to firms, productivity and industrial growth

Progress: 5 / 18 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: From licence to competition: how liberalisation transmits to firms, productivity and industrial growth

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — liberalisation, competition and industrial-growth channels
CA search: "Economic Survey 2025-26 industry transformation technology manufacturing 29 January 2026"
CA found: Economic Survey 2025-26, Chapter 8, tabled 29 January 2026; retrieved 24 September 2026
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Lower entry/capacity barriers
          ↓
entry + threat of entry + efficient expansion
          ↓
price/quality rivalry + technology adoption + reallocation
          ↓
productivity and scale — if finance, skills, power and logistics respond
          ↓
industrial output, exports and jobs, distributed unevenly
```

Liberalisation changes incentives; it does not magically make every firm productive.

### A factory-entry case

An incumbent makes 100 units at ₹120 each and sells at ₹140. A new firm can produce at ₹105. Entry may force upgrading, lower margins or exit; consumers gain and resources may shift to the efficient producer. But if unreliable logistics add ₹30 per unit, legal entry freedom alone may not yield the expected gain.

The mechanism has four channels:

1. **Entry and contestability** discipline incumbents.
2. **Reallocation** moves capital and market share toward productive firms.
3. **Technology access** and rivalry encourage upgrading.
4. **Scale and exports** can lower average cost and reward quality.

**Inference:** Aggregate industrial growth hides heterogeneous outcomes. Large firms may adapt faster; import-competing workers can be displaced; infrastructure-rich regions attract more investment; capital-intensive output can rise without proportionate employment.

**Fact:** The Economic Survey 2025-26 was tabled on 29 January 2026. Chapter 8 discusses industrial transformation and global integration and advances a capability/strategic-indispensability frame; retrieved 24 September 2026. A short-period indicator cannot by itself prove completed structural transformation.

### Investment and FDI: route, control and capability

Foreign direct investment is a lasting investment relationship carrying a degree of influence or control; portfolio investment is primarily a financial claim in traded securities. India uses an **automatic route**, where prior government approval is not required, and a **government route**, where approval is required. Sectoral caps, entry conditions, beneficial-ownership rules and security restrictions remain policy variables.

```text
FDI inflow
  ├─ capital
  ├─ technology and managerial practice
  ├─ supplier and export-network access
  └─ competition
        ↓
possible productivity and scale gains
        ↓ conditional on
absorptive capacity + domestic suppliers + skills + infrastructure + competition
```

**Current official anchor:** DPIIT’s June 2026 factsheet reports provisional FDI equity inflow of ₹1,86,661 crore (US$19.817 billion) in April–June 2026 and cumulative equity inflow of US$807.545 billion from April 2000 to June 2026. These are inflow measures, not proof of domestic value addition, technology transfer or job quality.

**Criticism:** Exposure can destroy domestic capacity before firms learn.

**Reply:** Temporary support can create learning space, but it needs measurable spillovers, performance tests and sunset review. Otherwise learning protection becomes incumbent protection.

### UPSC use and traps

- Never jump from delicensing directly to growth; show intermediate channels.
- Separate productivity from employment intensity.
- Name finance, power, logistics, skills, standards and contract enforcement.
- Examine sector, firm-size and regional distribution.

### Revision notes

1. Entry freedom raises actual and potential competition.
2. Threat of entry can discipline incumbents.
3. Reallocation rewards relatively productive firms.
4. Technology access can support upgrading.
5. Scale can strengthen exports.
6. Infrastructure and finance are complementary.
7. Productivity growth need not equal job growth.
8. Adjustment costs fall unevenly.
9. Aggregate growth masks sectoral variation.
10. Protection needs learning targets and review.
11. FDI differs from portfolio investment through a lasting influence/control relationship.
12. The automatic and government routes govern entry approval.
13. Sectoral conditions and security screening may remain.
14. FDI inflow is an input, not proof of technology diffusion.
15. Absorptive capacity determines domestic spillovers.


### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a large FDI inflow coexist with weak domestic technology diffusion?

**Model answer:** Capital can enter without supplier absorption, R&D, skills or competitive diffusion. Inflow is an input; domestic capability is an outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** FDI inflow is not a synonym for technology transfer or domestic value addition.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Trace the conditional channel from delicensing and FDI to productivity and industrial growth.

#### Evidence-rich response

Delicensing lowers entry and expansion barriers. Actual or threatened entry then pressures incumbents to cut cost, improve quality and adopt technology; market share and capital can move toward more productive firms. FDI can reinforce this channel through capital, managerial practice, technology and supplier/export networks. DPIIT’s June 2026 factsheet reports provisional FDI equity inflow of ₹1,86,661 crore (US$19.817 billion) in April–June 2026, but an inflow is only an input. Domestic productivity depends on power, logistics, finance, skills, competition and firms’ absorptive capacity. Without supplier learning or R&D, investment may remain an assembly enclave. Effects also vary across workers, firm sizes and regions. Therefore judge reform by additional value, diffusion, durable jobs and exports—not by delicensing or FDI volume alone.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: complete transmission chain; automatic/government-route awareness; named DPIIT evidence; absorptive-capacity condition; distributional qualification.

## Lesson 6 — Liberalisation without laissez-faire: competition, sector regulation, standards and state capacity

Progress: 6 / 18 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Liberalisation without laissez-faire: competition, sector regulation, standards and state capacity

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — economic reforms and the changing regulatory role of the state
CA search: "Economic Survey 2025-26 deregulation state capacity industry chapter 8"
CA found: Chapter 8, Economic Survey 2025-26, tabled 29 January 2026; retrieved 24 September 2026
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Removing a gatekeeper does not remove the need for traffic rules. Liberalisation replaces many case-by-case permissions with general, predictable rules.

| Problem after entry is freed | Appropriate institution/tool |
|---|---|
| Cartel or abuse of dominance | Competition law and CCI |
| Network monopoly | Independent sector regulation and access rules |
| Unsafe product | Technical and consumer standards |
| Pollution | Environmental standards, monitoring and liability |
| Information asymmetry | Disclosure, audit and certification |
| Coordination gap | Infrastructure, skills and facilitation |

### The regulatory-state shift

The older state often asked, “May this firm enter?” The reformed state should increasingly ask, “Are the rules fair, is rivalry protected, are harms priced and are public obligations enforced?” This is not a smaller state in every sense; it requires more specialised capacity.

**Fact:** Competition policy and sector regulators remain necessary after delicensing. **Inference:** Weak administration can turn nominal liberalisation into incumbent dominance, unsafe production or private monopoly.

Standards perform two roles. They protect users and can help firms enter demanding export markets. But opaque standards can become non-tariff barriers or compliance burdens, especially for smaller firms.

**Criticism:** Regulation recreates the licence raj.

**Reply:** Rule-based, general, appealable regulation differs from discretionary prior permission. The answer is proportionality and transparency, not absence of rules.

**Current anchor:** The Economic Survey 2025-26, tabled 29 January 2026, links industrial progress to state capacity, facilitation and problem-solving; retrieved 24 September 2026. This supports an institutional argument, not a claim that implementation is complete.

### UPSC use and traps

- Liberalisation is not laissez-faire.
- Competition policy protects the process of rivalry, not each competitor.
- Sector regulation is especially important where network features limit entry.
- State capacity includes expertise, data, enforcement, coordination and appeal.

### Revision notes

1. Delicensing removes prior permission, not all rules.
2. Competition law addresses cartelisation and dominance abuse.
3. Sector regulators address industry-specific network and access issues.
4. Standards protect safety and can support exports.
5. Environmental rules internalise social costs.
6. Disclosure reduces information asymmetry.
7. Regulation should be rule-based and appealable.
8. Weak enforcement can produce private monopoly.
9. Over-compliance can burden smaller firms.
10. Liberalisation changes the form of state capacity.


### Concept check

**Question:** What replaces industrial licensing in a liberalised but well-regulated economy?

**Model answer:** Predictable competition, sector, environmental, safety and consumer rules replace discretionary prior entry selection, alongside public goods and enforcement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Rule-based regulation is not a disguised return to universal licensing.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Why does liberalisation require a stronger rule-based state rather than no state?

#### Evidence-rich response

Liberalisation removes discretionary prior permission; it does not remove market failure. Competition law must check cartels, harmful combinations and abuse of dominance. An **agency problem** arises when decision-makers’ incentives diverge from the principals’ goals and monitoring is weak. A **soft budget constraint** exists when an enterprise expects losses to be covered, reducing the credibility of failure.

### Governance and performance architecture

DPE frames public-enterprise policy and performance systems; administrative ministries carry sector responsibility; boards manage enterprises; DIPAM handles Union investment and public-asset-management transactions. Memoranda of Understanding and performance scorecards can clarify targets, but a score is useful only when commercial outcomes are separated from explicit policy obligations.

```text
Board → strategy, risk, appointments and oversight
Management → operations and investment
Independent audit/disclosure → information
Competition/sector regulator → market discipline
Parliament/public → accountability
```

Ratna autonomy attempts to shorten approval chains and let qualified boards take investment decisions. Its success still depends on professional directors, timely appointments, credible consequences and freedom from informal interference.

### Diagnose before prescribing

| Symptom | Possible cause | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|
| Loss despite explicit social service | under-compensation | transparent budget support |
| High controllable cost | weak management/incentives | board and operational reform |
| Persistent excess staffing | political hiring/transition failure | negotiated labour plan |
| Low investment | fiscal constraint or policy uncertainty | recapitalise, partner or change ownership |
| Monopoly rent | protected market power | competition/regulation |
| Obsolete mandate | changed technology/market | merge, sell or close |

**Criticism:** Autonomy may weaken public accountability.

**Reply:** Autonomy should be bounded by clear objectives, professional boards, disclosure, audit and consequence management. Micromanagement is not the same as accountability.

**Inference:** The most useful counterfactual is not “public firm versus ideal private firm.” It is the actual feasible alternatives after accounting for regulation, liabilities, labour and transaction costs.

### UPSC use and traps

- Loss is a symptom, not a complete diagnosis.
- A bailout expectation alters behaviour before the bailout occurs.
- Separate legacy liabilities from current operating performance.
- Governance reform, competition and ownership change are distinct levers.

### Revision notes

1. Agency problems arise from dispersed ownership and weak monitoring.
2. Multiple principals can issue conflicting goals.
3. Soft budgets weaken exit and creditor discipline.
4. Political interference can distort hiring and investment.
5. Public-service costs should be separated from inefficiency.
6. Legacy debt can obscure current operating performance.
7. Board autonomy needs disclosure and consequence management.
8. Competition can discipline both public and private firms.
9. Ownership change is not a cure for every failure.
10. Diagnosis must compare feasible counterfactuals.


### Concept check

**Question:** How does an expected bailout alter management and creditor behaviour even before money is provided?

**Model answer:** Rescue expectation weakens the credibility of failure, reducing creditor discipline and management effort: the budget constraint becomes soft.

**Misconception to avoid:** A bailout matters through expectations, not only after cash is transferred.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Diagnose the main governance channels of PSU underperformance and identify matched remedies.

#### Evidence-rich response

PSU underperformance often begins with an agency chain: citizens are diffuse owners, ministries combine policy and shareholder roles, boards receive conflicting objectives, and managers face weak consequences. Expected recapitalisation creates a soft budget constraint, reducing creditor and managerial discipline; legacy debt or unfunded service obligations can simultaneously hide viable operations. Remedies must match causes. DPE performance systems and Ratna autonomy can clarify targets and shorten approvals, but only professional boards, timely appointments, disclosure and consequence management make autonomy effective. Public-service costs should be budgeted separately; commercial segments should face competition; obsolete mandates may require merger, sale or closure. Ownership change is one lever, not a cure for weak regulation, hidden liabilities or ministry interference. Diagnosis must precede prescription.

**Question (15 marks · 250 words):** Distinguish disinvestment, strategic sale, privatisation, closure and asset monetisation.

## Lesson 10 — Valuing and financing a sale: enterprise value, equity value, control premium and fiscal arithmetic

Progress: 10 / 18 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Valuing and financing a sale: enterprise value, equity value, control premium and fiscal arithmetic

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — disinvestment proceeds, strategic sale and fiscal-use debate
CA search: "DIPAM strategic disinvestment valuation liabilities control premium India"
CA found: official DIPAM strategic-disinvestment and policy pages retrieved 24 September 2026; no unverified pipeline value used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Value the business before dividing the claim

```text
Present value of future operating cash flow
          + surplus/non-operating assets
          = enterprise value
          − debt and debt-like liabilities
          ± cash and transaction adjustments
          = equity value
          + control premium, where justified
          = possible consideration range
```

**Enterprise value** values the operating business available to all capital providers. **Equity value** is the residual attributable to shareholders after debt-like claims and adjustments. A control premium may arise because the buyer gains power over strategy and cash flows; it is not an arbitrary surcharge.

### Worked transaction

Assume expected free cash flow to the firm is ₹600 crore next year, grows at 4%, and the risk-adjusted discount rate is 10%. A simplified perpetuity gives:

`Enterprise value = 600 / (0.10 − 0.04) = ₹10,000 crore.`

If debt-like liabilities are ₹3,200 crore and surplus cash is ₹400 crore:

`Equity value = 10,000 − 3,200 + 400 = ₹7,200 crore.`

A 15% control premium would imply `₹8,280 crore`, before due diligence and transaction adjustments. Change growth to 3% and enterprise value falls to about ₹8,571 crore. Valuation is therefore an assumption-sensitive range, not a single self-evident truth.

Land needs careful treatment: operating land supports cash flow and should not automatically be added again; surplus land may be separately valued, subject to title, use restrictions and liabilities. Pension, environmental, tax and litigation liabilities matter. Market timing affects comparable multiples and buyer appetite.

### Fiscal arithmetic

**Fact / canonical concept:** Disinvestment proceeds are non-debt capital receipts arising from sale of an asset. They are non-recurring. Using them to finance recurring expenditure can improve the current-year financing picture without permanently improving the revenue base.

```text
Sell public equity → cash receipt now + lower future dividend claim
                  → possible efficiency gain/loss
                  → possible retained liabilities
```

No numerical receipt series is used here: the official Budget at a Glance 2026-27 document was rechecked on 24 September 2026, but its displayed “Other Receipts” aggregate should not be silently converted into a disinvestment-only historical series.

**Criticism:** Strategic sales necessarily undersell public assets.

**Reply:** Underpricing is a genuine risk, but “book value” is not market value. Transparent bidding, independent valuation, liability disclosure, competitive buyer participation and reasoned reserve prices are stronger safeguards than assuming either sale or retention is always optimal.

### UPSC use and traps

- Do not confuse enterprise value with equity value.
- Do not add operating land twice.
- A high headline price can coexist with government retention of liabilities.
- Receipts are capital, non-recurring and distinct from efficiency gains.

### Revision notes

1. DCF values future cash flows, not historical cost.
2. Enterprise value belongs to all capital providers.
3. Equity value adjusts enterprise value for debt, cash and liabilities.
4. Control can command a premium.
5. Valuation changes with growth, risk and timing assumptions.
6. Land requires title and operating/surplus classification.
7. Pension, tax, environmental and litigation liabilities matter.
8. Competitive bidding aids price discovery.
9. Disinvestment proceeds are capital receipts.
10. Asset-sale receipts are non-recurring.
11. Fiscal receipt and efficiency effect are separate.


### Concept check

**Question:** Why can adding the full value of operating land to a cash-flow valuation double count value?

**Model answer:** The forecast cash flow already reflects use of operating land; adding the same land again is valid only if it is genuinely surplus and separately realisable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Book value, market value and transaction value are not interchangeable.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks · 150 words):** Explain why valuation of a strategic sale must go beyond the headline bid.

#### Evidence-rich response

A strategic-sale bid is meaningful only after defining what is being sold. Discounted operating cash flow yields enterprise value; debt-like, pension and contingent liabilities reduce the residual equity claim, while surplus cash and genuinely non-operating assets may be added. A buyer may pay a control premium because decision rights change. Operating land already supports forecast cash flow and cannot be counted again unless it is separable surplus property. Fiscal analysis must then compare the cheque with transaction costs, liabilities retained, foregone dividends, future support avoided and the value of restructuring or waiting. Since proceeds are one-off capital receipts, they do not constitute recurring fiscal correction. Transparent assumptions and sensitivity ranges are therefore superior to a single headline valuation.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: enterprise/equity distinction; debt and surplus treatment; control premium; double-count warning; fiscal counterfactual.

**Question (15 marks · 250 words):** Why must privatisation be sequenced with competition and a just transition?

**Question:** What design feature most clearly separates capability policy from permanent protection?

**Model answer:** Time-bound, contestable support tied to additionality and an enforceable sunset or redesign rule distinguishes learning policy from entitlement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Output growth alone does not prove policy additionality.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (20 marks · 250 words):** Design an evaluation framework that prevents strategic industrial policy from becoming permanent rent protection.

#### Evidence-rich response

Strategic industrial policy should begin with a named failure—coordination, learning spillover, security or resilience—and a counterfactual showing why markets alone will underprovide capability. Support must be contestable, time-bound and conditional on measurable additional investment, productivity, domestic supplier development, R&D, exports or recovery capacity. Evaluation should separate gross output from additionality and examine distribution across firms, workers and regions.

Political economy is central: beneficiaries are concentrated and can lobby for renewal, while costs are dispersed. Disclosure, independent evaluation, competition among recipients and a credible sunset reduce capture. Results should trigger graduation where learning is achieved, redesign where constraints were misdiagnosed, and exit where additionality fails. Evaluation should also ask whether benefits diffuse to MSME suppliers, whether employment is durable and whether regional concentration worsens. Economic Survey 2025-26’s strategic-indispensability idea supplies the objective: build reliable capabilities that global production networks value, not permanent insulation. Its 7.00% H1 FY26 real-industry-GVA growth and 46.3% medium/high-tech manufacturing share are useful baselines, not causal proof of policy success. Even well-designed metrics can be gamed, so qualitative technology depth and domestic linkages must accompany numerical targets. The conclusion is conditional activism—state support earns continuation only through verified public value and must remain reversible.

#### Unique scoring rubric

Award for: named failure/counterfactual; conditional metrics; additionality; redesign/exit rule; capture and qualified verdict.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish disinvestment from privatisation and explain why the distinction matters for governance. (150 words)

### Model answer

Disinvestment is the sale of part or all of government equity in a public enterprise. Privatisation is the transfer of ownership/control to a private strategic buyer; it is therefore a narrower outcome, often achieved through strategic disinvestment. A minority market sale can generate receipts, deepen public float and expose a firm to market disclosure while government retains management control. Strategic disinvestment changes the controlling principal and may alter incentives more deeply.

The distinction matters because expected outcomes differ. Minority sale cannot be assumed to solve ministry interference or soft-budget expectations. Control transfer does not by itself create competition, remove legacy liabilities or fund public-service obligations. Closure and asset monetisation are also separate: closure ends activity, while monetisation may transfer time-bound operating rights without selling enterprise equity.

Hence every proposal should specify stake, buyer, control, liabilities, market structure and public obligations. Governance analysis must follow the actual transaction, not the generic label “disinvestment.”

### Scoring ceiling

A response that defines the terms but omits control, monetisation and governance consequences should not cross the middle band. Full marks require a named control-transfer example and a qualification.

## 15-mark question

**Question:** Liberalisation changed the form of state intervention rather than ending it. Analyse with reference to industrial growth. (250 words)

### Model answer

Liberalisation reduced discretionary licensing and expanded entry, competition and market-based allocation. The 1991 package connected industrial delicensing with trade, exchange and financial reforms. Its intended transmission was: lower barriers → entry and threat of entry → reallocation and technology adoption → productivity, scale and industrial growth.

Yet this chain requires state action in a different form:

- competition policy to check cartels and dominance;
- sector regulation for networks and essential services;
- environmental, safety and product standards;
- infrastructure, logistics, power, finance and skills;
- contract enforcement and predictable taxation;
- adjustment support for workers and lagging regions;
- strategic capability support where learning, security or resilience creates spillovers.

The results are heterogeneous. Productive firms may expand and consumers gain, but import-competing firms can exit; capital-intensive output may not create enough jobs; investment can cluster in stronger regions. Privatisation may improve incentives but create private monopoly unless entry/access rules are credible.

Thus laissez-faire is not the logical endpoint of liberalisation. The state moves from selecting capacity through case-by-case permission toward setting general rules, providing public goods, correcting failures and evaluating strategic support. Industrial growth depends on both competitive pressure and capable institutions.

### Scoring ceiling

A chronology-only answer cannot reach the top band. Full marks require the transmission mechanism, at least four post-liberalisation state functions, heterogeneous effects and a qualified conclusion.

## 20-mark question

**Question:** “The future of public-sector reform lies in separating mandate, ownership, market structure and fiscal arithmetic.” Discuss. (250 words)

### Model answer

Public-sector debate becomes ideological when four questions are collapsed.

**First, mandate:** A PSU may serve security, natural-monopoly, developmental, universal-service or commercial objectives. Each needs a separate metric. Strategic redundancy or below-cost universal service cannot be assessed by profit alone, but such obligations must be explicit and costed.

**Second, ownership:** Minority disinvestment leaves control with government; strategic disinvestment transfers control; privatisation transfers it to a private buyer; closure and asset monetisation are different instruments. Ownership should change only after comparing regulation, contracting and governance reform.

**Third, market structure:** A private monopoly may exploit consumers more aggressively than a public monopoly. Restructuring, access rules, tariff oversight, competition law and service standards must accompany ownership change. Labour and regional transition also affect feasibility and welfare.

**Fourth, fiscal arithmetic:** Sale proceeds are non-recurring capital receipts. A credible valuation uses future cash flow, debt-like liabilities, surplus assets, land status, control premium and market timing. Headline consideration must be read with retained liabilities, foregone dividends and future support avoided.

The New PSE Policy notified on 4 February 2021 supplies a strategic/non-strategic framework and a bare-minimum public-presence principle, but enterprise appraisal remains necessary. Air India’s completed transaction demonstrates control transfer and liability design, not a universal rule.

Therefore reform should publish mandates, separate owner/regulator roles, harden budget constraints, value transparently, protect contestability and review outcomes. Public value—not ownership ideology or annual receipt—should decide.

### Scoring ceiling

A sale-versus-retain opinion without the four-part framework is capped below the top band. Full marks require mandate, ownership, market structure and fiscal arithmetic, with one completed case and one implementation qualification.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Syllabus thesis

“Effects of liberalization on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth” requires a three-part answer: policy chronology, causal effects on firms/industry, and institutional/distributional qualification.

## 2. Chronology

- **1948:** mixed-economy settlement; public and private roles coexist.
- **1951:** IDR Act provides licensing architecture.
- **1956:** Schedules A/B/C; commanding heights and public-sector leadership.
- **Sixth Plan:** modernisation and productivity.
- **Seventh Plan:** technology, capacity utilisation, exports and early delicensing.
- **1991:** crisis-linked stabilisation plus industrial, trade, exchange and financial reforms.
- **Post-1991:** competition/regulatory state, PSU reform and capability strategy.
- **4 February 2021:** New PSE Policy notified.

## 3. Core definitions

- **Industrial policy:** state framework shaping entry, competition, technology, location and strategic capability.
- **Liberalisation:** reduction of controls to expand economic choice and competition.
- **Disinvestment:** sale of part or all of government equity.
- **Strategic disinvestment:** entire/substantial share sale with management-control transfer.
- **Privatisation:** strategic disinvestment where equity/control pass to private buyer.
- **Asset monetisation:** unlocking value from an asset, often through time-bound operating/revenue rights while ownership is retained.
- **Soft budget constraint:** expectation that losses will be rescued, weakening discipline.
- **Natural monopoly:** cost structure where one network supplier may be efficient; ownership does not define it.

## 4. 1991 mechanism

```text
delicensing + trade/exchange reform + financial reform
  → entry and expansion
  → rivalry and technology
  → reallocation and scale
  → productivity/exports
```

Necessary complements: power, logistics, skills, finance, standards, contract enforcement and regulatory capacity.

## 5. Effects and qualifications

- Consumers may gain through price, variety and quality.
- Efficient firms may expand; weak firms may exit.
- Productivity may rise without equal job growth.
- Capital-intensive sectors and strong regions may gain disproportionately.
- Import exposure can discipline firms but impose worker/supplier adjustment.
- Protection may aid learning only with measurable performance and sunset review.

## 6. Liberalisation is not laissez-faire

- Competition law checks anti-competitive conduct.
- Sector regulators manage network access, tariffs and service standards.
- Product/environmental standards address safety and externalities.
- State capacity shifts toward general rules, enforcement, public goods and coordination.

## 7. PSU mandate and diagnosis

Test strategic, natural-monopoly, developmental, public-service and commercial roles separately. Publish and fund non-commercial obligations. Separate owner, regulator, board and purchaser roles. Diagnose agency problems, multiple principals, political interference, soft budgets, legacy liabilities and obsolete mandates.

## 8. Transaction distinctions

Minority sale: shares sold, control retained. Strategic sale: substantial/all stake plus control. Private buyer makes it privatisation; another CPSE keeps it public. Closure ends activity. Monetisation generally transfers rights rather than enterprise equity.

## 9. Valuation and fiscal boundary

`Enterprise value − debt-like liabilities + surplus cash = equity value`, with control premium and transaction adjustments where justified. Use future cash flow, liability, land-title, environmental, pension, tax and market-timing evidence. Disinvestment proceeds are non-recurring capital receipts, not proof of productivity or permanent fiscal correction.

## 10. Air India case

PIB PRID 1792950 dated 27 January 2022 records completed strategic disinvestment to Talace. It demonstrates control transfer and liability design. It does not prove universal suitability of privatisation or long-run welfare success.

## 11. New PSE Policy

Four strategic-sector groups: atomic/space/defence; transport/telecom; power/petroleum/coal/minerals; banking/insurance/financial services. Retain bare-minimum public commercial presence; assess others for privatisation, merger, subsidiarisation or closure. Non-strategic enterprises face privatisation where feasible, otherwise closure. Respect policy exclusions and enterprise-level tests.
