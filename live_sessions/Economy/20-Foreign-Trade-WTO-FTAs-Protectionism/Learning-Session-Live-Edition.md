*The productivity table shows why relative sacrifice, not sheer output, determines specialisation.*

### The puzzle: can the less productive country still gain?

**Absolute advantage** asks who can produce more with the same resources. **Comparative
advantage** asks who gives up less of another good. Trade is governed by the second
question. Even if one country is absolutely better at producing both goods, specialisation
can still raise joint output when relative opportunity costs differ.

In the visual, India gives up two kilograms of rice to produce one metre of cloth. The
partner gives up only one kilogram. Cloth is therefore relatively cheaper for the partner,
while rice is relatively cheaper for India. If India specialises more in rice and the
partner more in cloth, they can exchange at a rate between one and two kilograms of rice
per metre of cloth and both can gain.

### What the model establishes - and what it does not

The model establishes a **possibility of mutual gain**, not an automatic guarantee that
every worker, firm or region gains. Distribution depends on ownership, mobility, market
power, adjustment costs and policy. A displaced worker cannot instantly become an export
engineer merely because national income rises.

Comparative advantage can also evolve. Education, logistics, technology, scale and
industrial learning can create new capabilities. Hence India need not choose between
static efficiency and capability-building; it must distinguish temporary, performance-
linked support from permanent shelter.

### India lens

India's relative strength in digitally delivered services does not imply that it should
abandon manufacturing. Services can supply design, finance, software and logistics to
manufacturing, while manufacturing creates scale, learning and broad employment. The
correct policy question is: *where can India lower opportunity cost over time without
making consumers and downstream producers finance indefinite inefficiency?*

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Comparative advantage assumes frictionless adjustment and ignores strategic
dependence. **Reply:** The criticism qualifies the policy conclusion, not the arithmetic.
Use the model as a baseline, then add learning externalities, security risks, unemployment
and unequal bargaining power.

```text

Partner will export cloth if it receives > 1 rice.

Mutual-gain interval:  1 rice < price of 1 cloth < 2 rice
                       ^ terms of trade must settle here
```

*The interval marks the exchange rates at which neither country would prefer self-production.*

### From production gain to exchange gain

Specialisation raises the potential joint output. The **terms of trade (ToT)** decide how
that gain is divided. In a two-good example, ToT is the export price relative to the import
price. A country gains when the international exchange rate is better than its own
opportunity cost, after allowing for transport, insurance and transaction costs.

Suppose India's domestic cost of one cloth is two rice, while the partner's is one rice.
At 1.5 rice per cloth, India imports cloth more cheaply than producing it; the partner
receives more rice than its own cloth cost. At exactly one or two, one side captures the
entire textbook gain. Outside the interval, one party will reject trade.

### Why observed trade need not match the simple model

Real products differ in quality. Firms face scale economies, patents, networks, finance
and standards. Exchange rates and commodity-price shocks move ToT. Large buyers or sellers
may influence price. Therefore, a country can export more physical volume yet receive less
import capacity if export prices fall sharply.

For India, crude-oil prices can worsen the merchandise terms of trade, while high-value
services can cushion foreign-exchange earnings. Do not confuse ToT with the trade balance:
ToT is a **price ratio**; the trade balance is a **value difference** between exports and
imports.

### Dynamic specialisation

Learning-by-doing can lower future opportunity cost. That supports selective industrial
policy only when learning is measurable, support is time-bound, and firms face export or
productivity discipline. Otherwise "dynamic comparative advantage" becomes an unfalsifiable
excuse for rent protection.

*The welfare sketch separates transfers from the two efficiency losses created by the price wedge.*

### A tariff is a wedge, not a magic factory

An **ad valorem tariff** is a percentage of import value; a **specific tariff** is a fixed
amount per physical unit. In the small-country case, a tariff raises the domestic price
from the world price to the world price plus tariff. Domestic producers expand output,
consumers reduce demand, imports fall and government receives revenue.

Example: a component costs ₹1,000 at the border. A 20% ad valorem tariff raises its
pre-tax landed price to ₹1,200. If an Indian downstream firm needs ten units, its input bill
rises by ₹2,000 before GST, logistics or financing. Protection of the component producer
can therefore act as an anti-export tax on the downstream firm.

### Welfare map

Consumers lose more than producers and government gain in the competitive small-country
model. The residual consists of:

1. **production distortion** - costly domestic units replace cheaper imports; and
2. **consumption distortion** - mutually beneficial consumption is suppressed.

A large country may improve its ToT if foreign exporters cut prices, but retaliation and
supply substitution can erase the theoretical gain. Revenue is a transfer, not a net
resource gain; the deadweight triangles are efficiency losses.

### Incidence and distribution

The legal payer at customs is not necessarily the economic bearer. Importers may absorb
some margin, foreign suppliers may cut price, and consumers or downstream firms may pay
the rest. Elasticities, market concentration, invoicing currency and exchange-rate
movement determine incidence.

### Policy test

A tariff can be defensible for learning, emergency adjustment or security, but the answer
must identify the externality, beneficiary, cost bearer, review date and alternative
instrument. A broad tariff often protects both efficient and inefficient firms and invites
lobbying for extension.

### Visual

```text
NOMINAL PROTECTION EXAMPLE

Free-trade output price = 100
Free-trade imported input = 60
Free-trade value added = 40

After policy: output price = 120; input price = 66
Domestic value added = 54

Effective rate of protection = (54 - 40) / 40 x 100 = 35%
Nominal output tariff = 20%, but effective protection = 35%.
```

*The value-added calculation reveals why a tariff structure can protect far more than its headline rate suggests.*

### Quantity can be restricted even when the tariff looks modest

An **import quota** fixes quantity. A **tariff-rate quota (TRQ)** applies a lower tariff
within a quota and a higher tariff beyond it. Unlike ordinary tariff revenue, **quota
rent** may accrue to licence holders or foreign exporters, depending on allocation.
Auctioning licences can transfer some rent to government.

Under a binding quota, domestic price rises until demand minus domestic supply equals the
permitted import quantity. If demand surges, the price can rise further because quantity
cannot respond. A tariff is therefore more transparent in price terms; a quota is more
predictable in quantity terms.

### Effective protection

The **effective rate of protection (ERP)** measures protection to domestic value added,
not just the tariff on final output:

```text
ERP = (value added after tariff structure - free-trade value added)
      / free-trade value added x 100
```

In the visual, a 20% final-good tariff and 10% input tariff raise value added from 40 to
54, producing 35% effective protection. Reverse the structure - low output duty and high
input duty - and ERP may be small or negative. This is why tariff escalation and inverted
duty structures matter for Make in India and export competitiveness.

### Policy comparison

Quotas create licence-allocation politics, conceal the tariff equivalent and can entrench
incumbents. Tariffs preserve some import response and collect public revenue. Yet both can
raise consumer prices and invite evasion. A TRQ can balance access and sensitivity, but
its administration must be transparent.

### The WTO is not a command for zero tariffs

The WTO is a rules-based framework for negotiated market access, non-discrimination,
transparency and dispute settlement. **Most-favoured-nation (MFN)** treatment generally requires an advantage given to one
member to be extended to other members. **National treatment** compares imported and
domestic products, services or IP after entry into the market. A customs tariff at the
border is not by itself a national-treatment violation.

**Bound tariff** is the negotiated ceiling in a member's schedule. **Applied tariff** is
the rate actually charged. The difference is tariff water or policy space. Raising an
applied rate within the binding is not the same legal act as breaching the binding, though
it may still damage predictability.

### Exceptions are structured, not loopholes without conditions

FTAs may discriminate in favour of members under relevant conditions. Developing-country
preferences, trade remedies, balance-of-payments provisions, and general exceptions for
specified public objectives also exist. Their presence shows that the system is better
described as **managed, rule-bound openness** than pure laissez-faire.

### Developing-country perspective

Equal formal rules can have unequal practical effects when compliance capacity differs.
Special and differential treatment, longer implementation periods and technical
assistance seek to address this, but developing countries argue that policy space,
agriculture, technology access and voice remain uneven.

### UPSC trap

MFN does not mean the “best rate for a favourite ally.” It means non-discrimination among
members, subject to legal exceptions. National treatment does not require imported goods
to avoid customs duty; it governs internal treatment after importation.

```text
Market price support
= (administered price - fixed external reference price)
  x eligible production
```

production 10 million units, measured market-price support is ₹14,000 million before
relevant adjustments. The political dispute arises because the fixed external reference
price uses an old base and because procurement, inflation and eligible-production
interpretation matter.

Developing countries argue that historically larger support in advanced economies and
old reference prices constrain current food-security programmes asymmetrically. The
counterargument is that administered-price procurement can affect production and trade.

updated methods, transparency and anti-export safeguards.

### A standard can protect life or protect a lobby

The correct question is not whether a standard affects trade - most standards do. Ask
whether its objective, evidence, design and administration are legitimate and
non-discriminatory.

**SPS measures** protect humans, animals or plants from specified food-safety, pest and
disease risks. The agreement emphasises scientific principles, sufficient evidence and
risk assessment, while encouraging harmonisation with relevant international standards.
**TBT measures** cover other technical regulations, standards and conformity-assessment
procedures, such as many labelling, quality or electrical specifications.

### Three India cases

1. An importing country requires mangoes to be treated against a scientifically identified
   pest. This is principally SPS.
2. It requires a non-safety consumer label in a specified format. This is principally TBT.
3. It accepts laboratory certificates only from an expensive domestic body even when
   equivalent accredited Indian testing exists. The conformity-assessment design may
   impose an unnecessary barrier.

### Equivalence, harmonisation and capacity

Harmonisation uses international standards as a reference. **Equivalence** recognises that
a different measure can achieve the importing member's appropriate level of protection.
For Indian exporters, the real constraint may be traceability, accredited laboratories,
testing cost and small-firm capability rather than the legal tariff.

### Precaution without arbitrariness

Governments need not wait for harm to occur, but uncertainty does not justify disguised
discrimination. The measure should connect to risk, use available evidence, be reviewed
as evidence changes and avoid a more trade-restrictive design than necessary for the
chosen objective.

### Visual


*The decision tree begins with the legal cause of injury instead of the political claim that imports are harmful.*

### “Imports hurt us” is not yet a legal case

Trade remedies require a defined predicate, evidence of injury and causal linkage.
**Dumping** compares export price with normal value; low price alone is not enough.
Anti-dumping action additionally requires material injury and causation. **Countervailing
duty** addresses injurious subsidised imports. **Safeguards** address serious injury or
threat from an import surge without requiring dumping or foreign subsidy.

This yields different standards:

- anti-dumping: dumping margin + material injury + causal link;
- countervailing: actionable subsidy + injury + causal link;
- safeguard: increased imports + serious injury/threat + causal link, generally with
  temporary adjustment logic.

### India institutional chain

DGTR is the national investigating authority for specified trade-remedy matters. It
examines evidence and recommends action. A DGTR initiation or recommendation is not the
same as a final notified duty. This distinction is a frequent prelims and policy-reporting
trap.

### Case drill

Imported steel becomes cheaper because global demand collapses. That is not automatically
dumping. If exporters price below the relevant normal value and cause injury, the
anti-dumping route may apply. If a foreign government grants an actionable subsidy, the
countervailing route may apply. If imports simply surge fairly and cause serious injury,
the safeguard route is the relevant test.

### Political-economy caution

Remedies can restore fair conditions and adjustment time, but repeated use can become
ordinary protection, raise input costs and trigger retaliation. User industries and
consumers must therefore be part of the injury-incidence assessment.

### The label does not tell you the operative concession

An FTA reduces barriers among members under schedules, exclusions, phase-downs and rules
of origin. It does not mean every tariff becomes zero on day one. A customs union goes
further by adopting a common external tariff. Modern agreements also cover services,
investment, digital trade, standards, procurement, environment or mobility, but coverage
varies.

Legal status must be exact. The Department of Commerce 2025-26 highlights, retrieved on
24 September 2026, distinguish announcements, signatures and implementation. They record
the India-EU FTA as announced on 27 January 2026 and the India-UK CETA as signed on

does not establish operative concessions. The same publication warns that its snapshot
creates no legal rights and that treaty text controls obligations.

The official India-EU release posted 27 January 2026 states that negotiations were
concluded and describes market-access commitments, while entry into force remains a
separate legal event. India-EFTA TEPA entered into force on 1 October 2025 according to
official Commerce material located in the audit. India-UAE CEPA and India-Australia ECTA
are operational earlier agreements. Every answer should pair the agreement name with its
status date.

### Depth versus breadth

A shallow agreement reduces tariffs on selected goods. A deeper agreement may discipline
services, standards and investment, improving predictability but constraining some
regulatory discretion. Sensitive lists and phase-downs manage adjustment; too many
different schedules and origin rules create a “noodle bowl” of compliance.

### Utilisation test

Signing is not using. Firms need preference margins, origin compliance, certificates,
awareness, logistics and buyer relationships. FTA evaluation therefore asks not only
“what was offered?” but “what share of eligible trade claimed the preference, and why?”

**Progress: 12/21 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Rules of origin and value-addition calculations**

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual

```text
FTA VALUE-ADDITION EXAMPLE

FOB export value                         = ₹1,000
Non-originating imported inputs          = ₹  350

Regional value content (build-down)
= (FOB - non-originating inputs) / FOB x 100
= (1,000 - 350) / 1,000 x 100
= 65%

If the product rule requires 40%, the numerical threshold is met.
Other product-specific rules may still apply.
```

*The RVC arithmetic tests economic origin; it does not let the shipping port determine nationality.*

### Preference follows origin, not the shipping route

Rules of origin determine a product's economic nationality. They prevent simple
trans-shipment, where a non-member good merely passes through an FTA partner to obtain a
lower tariff. **Preferential origin** decides FTA or preference eligibility.
**Non-preferential origin** can matter for MFN treatment, remedies, marking, quotas or
procurement.

Common tests include:

- **wholly obtained** for goods such as locally harvested crops;
- **change in tariff classification** after sufficient processing;
- **regional value content (RVC)**;
- specified manufacturing processes; and
- combinations of these tests.

### Calculation and cumulation

The visual's build-down formula produces 65% RVC. Meeting 40% does not automatically
qualify the good if the agreement also requires a tariff-heading change or excludes a
minimal operation. **Cumulation** lets qualifying inputs from specified partner countries
count as originating, supporting regional supply chains.

### Operational documents

The exporter needs correct product classification, supplier declarations, cost records,
certificate or origin declaration, and retention of evidence. The importer may face
verification. Fraud control is necessary, but excessive paperwork can erase a modest
preference margin.

### India case

For an Indian garment using imported fabric, origin can turn on tariff shift, regional
value and specific processing. For an electronics assembly, a high gross export value may
coexist with low domestic value added. Policy should reward real capability without using
unworkably strict rules that prevent firms from joining supply chains.

1. Origin is economic nationality, not port of shipment.
2. Preferential and non-preferential origin serve different purposes.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual

```text
Before FTA:
Domestic cost = 120
Partner cost + tariff = 110
Non-member cost + tariff = 100  -> import from non-member

After partner gets zero tariff:
Partner cost = 90
Non-member cost + tariff = 100  -> switch to partner

If partner resource cost 90 < non-member resource cost 80,
the switch is TRADE DIVERSION despite a lower consumer price.
```

*The source-switch example shows how a lower private price can coexist with higher world resource cost.*

### More intra-FTA trade is not automatically more welfare

**Trade creation** occurs when lower-cost partner imports replace higher-cost domestic
production. Resources move toward more efficient production. **Trade diversion** occurs
when a preferred partner displaces a lower-cost non-member because the tariff preference
changes the ranking after tax.

The consumer may still see a lower price under diversion, while government loses tariff
revenue and the world uses a higher-cost producer. Welfare depends on consumer gain,
producer loss, revenue loss and resource-cost change. Gross bilateral trade growth cannot
identify the result.

### A practical evaluation grid

| Question | Creation signal | Diversion risk |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-FTA source | costly domestic supply | efficient non-member |
| Partner productivity | globally competitive | preference-dependent |
| External tariff | moderate | high |
| Origin rules | enable efficient sourcing | force costly sourcing |
| Dynamic effect | scale and learning | locked-in dependence |

Dynamic gains can include competition, scale, investment and standards upgrading.
Dynamic losses can include hub-and-spoke dependence, bargaining asymmetry or tariff
revenue erosion without productivity gains.

### India application

An FTA that lowers the cost of high-quality machinery may create productivity gains beyond
the static price effect. An agreement that diverts an intermediate from the world's
cheapest supplier can hurt downstream exports. Evaluate at product and value-chain level,
not through the bilateral deficit alone.

- **Mains use:** Define creation and diversion with a numerical source-switch example.

1. Trade creation replaces costly domestic production.
2. Trade diversion replaces a cheaper non-member source.
3. Consumer price and world resource cost can move differently.
4. Tariff revenue loss belongs in welfare calculation.
5. Gross bilateral trade cannot prove creation.
6. External tariff height affects diversion risk.

### Services trade is not only a laptop crossing no border

GATS combines general obligations with sector- and mode-specific scheduled commitments on
market access and national treatment. “The country opened services” is therefore too
vague. Ask: which sector, which mode, what limitation, and what horizontal condition?

### India stakes

India has strengths in Mode 1 digitally delivered services and interests in Mode 4
temporary professional mobility. Tourism, education and medical travel involve Mode 2.
Indian firms investing abroad use Mode 3. Data governance, licensing, recognition of
qualifications, visas, social-security contributions and local-presence conditions shape
actual access.

February 2026.

Mode 4 is not permanent migration and does not cover persons seeking access to the
employment market as such. Mode 3 is not a cross-border digital transaction.

### Imports can be an input into exports

A global value chain (GVC) splits design, components, assembly, logistics, marketing and
after-sales service across countries. Participation has a **backward linkage** when exports
use foreign value added and a **forward linkage** when a country's value added enters
another country's exports.

The policy objective is not the lowest possible import content. It is rising domestic
capability and value capture while firms remain connected to efficient networks.
Prematurely forcing every component to be domestic can raise cost, reduce quality and
exclude firms from the chain.

### Effective protection returns

Suppose an exported machine sells for ₹10 lakh and uses imported precision parts worth
₹6 lakh. A 20% parts tariff adds ₹1.2 lakh. If the exporter cannot raise the world price,
the burden comes from margin, wages, local sourcing or lost orders. A tariff intended to
support upstream production can therefore contract downstream demand.

The Economic Survey 2025-26 argues that logistics, infrastructure, trade facilitation,
technology and sustained GVC participation can strengthen manufacturing productivity.
Its external-sector chapter also frames supply chains increasingly through resilience and
strategic autonomy, not cost alone.

### Upgrading ladder

```text
assembly -> reliable process -> component capability -> design/IP
         -> branding/distribution -> standards leadership
```

India should measure domestic value added, firm learning, supplier deepening and export
survival - not celebrate gross value alone. Imported capital goods and intermediate inputs
may be essential during upgrading.

### Risk qualification

GVC concentration creates exposure to geopolitical controls, chokepoints, shipping
disruption and single-source technology. The answer is multi-sourcing, inventories for
critical items, interoperability and domestic capability, not blanket autarky.

### Market access on paper is not delivery at the buyer's door

An exporter needs information, finance, quality systems, accredited testing, origin proof,
customs clearance, logistics and grievance resolution. A ten-percentage-point preference
can be worthless if certification costs twelve points or delivery is unreliable.

**Trade facilitation** lowers time, uncertainty and administrative cost without requiring
lower product standards. Risk-based customs can inspect high-risk consignments while
allowing compliant traders faster clearance. Digital documentation can reduce repetition,
but poor interoperability merely converts paper queues into portal queues.

### Standards as capability

Standards infrastructure includes laboratories, metrology, accreditation, certification,
traceability and mutual-recognition arrangements. It creates a public-good problem:
individual MSMEs may not finance the ecosystem even though an industry benefits.

### India case: preference to purchase order

The Department of Commerce's 2025-26 FTA material highlights product-specific origin,
SPS/TBT cooperation and services commitments. The Economic Survey 2025-26 notes the Trade
Connect ePlatform's tariff explorer for eligible exports. These are enabling tools, not
proof that utilisation or exports automatically rise.

### Reliability premium

A buyer values low defect rates, on-time delivery and rapid correction. Export policy must
therefore join infrastructure, customs, standards, credit and firm capability. Frequent
export bans or minimum export prices may offer short-run domestic relief but can push
buyers toward reliable alternatives; the Survey's external-sector chapter warns of this
reputational cost in agricultural trade.

### The infant must eventually grow

The **infant-industry argument** says a new sector may have high initial cost but lower
future cost through learning, scale or supplier development. Private firms may underinvest
when learning spills over to rivals or when complementary infrastructure is missing.

The argument can justify temporary support, but not any tariff. A credible policy specifies
the learning externality, duration, milestones, competition, export discipline and exit.
If costs never converge and lobbying blocks withdrawal, consumers finance inefficiency.

### Other protection arguments

- adjustment time for workers and firms after a sudden shock;
- anti-dumping or countervailing action against defined unfair trade;
- security of critical supply;
- environmental or labour objectives;
- strategic trade under scale economies and oligopoly.

Each has a different instrument. Worker adjustment may be better served by income support
and retraining than by permanent product tariffs. A security risk may justify diversified
procurement and stockpiles rather than full domestic production.

### Political economy

Benefits are concentrated among protected firms; costs are dispersed among consumers and
downstream users. This asymmetry encourages lobbying and makes temporary barriers sticky.
Protection can also invite retaliation, currency pressure, smuggling and anti-export bias.

### India design rule

Prefer direct, transparent and reviewable support for capability gaps. Tie assistance to
productivity, quality, R&D, supplier development or exports. Preserve competition and
allow failure. The Economic Survey 2025-26 frames the global environment as more
protectionist and bilateral, but its policy logic emphasises capability and diversification
rather than indiscriminate closure.

### Resilience is the capacity to absorb a shock, not the absence of trade

The Economic Survey 2025-26 distinguishes ordinary economic policy from **economic
statecraft**, where economic tools pursue foreign-policy or security objectives. Supply
chains are increasingly judged by resilience, technology control and strategic autonomy
as well as efficiency.

Three concepts must stay separate:

- **self-reliance:** sufficient domestic capability and policy autonomy;
- **resilience:** ability to continue or recover through diversification, inventories,
  substitution and coordination;
- **autarky:** extreme withdrawal from international exchange.

### Security externality

A firm chooses the cheapest supplier, but society may value continuity during war,
sanctions or disaster. That gap can justify policy. Yet “critical” must be bounded.
Treating every product as strategic spreads resources thin and entrenches protection.

### Portfolio solution

For a critical mineral or semiconductor input:

```text
domestic exploration/production
+ recycling and efficiency
+ trusted-partner agreements
+ diversified commercial sources
+ strategic stocks
+ substitution R&D
= resilience portfolio
```

No single element is enough. Domestic production may remain costly; foreign concentration
may remain risky; stockpiles expire or become obsolete.

### Climate and security trade measures

Carbon border measures, local-content rules, export controls and sanctions blur trade,
industry and security policy. Their legitimacy depends on non-discrimination,
proportionality, transparency and genuine connection to the stated objective. India should
contest discriminatory design while improving emissions measurement, standards and
domestic capability.

The European Commission's official CBAM page, retrieved on 24 September 2026, states that
the definitive regime applies from **1 January 2026** to selected imports in cement, iron
and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen. Importers face authorisation,
reporting, verification and certificate obligations linked to embedded emissions; the
certificate price follows EU ETS allowance auction prices rather than a single fixed
ad valorem tariff. For India, the analytical response has two tracks: contest any
discriminatory or inequitable design through trade and climate diplomacy, while building
credible measurement, verification and lower-carbon production for exposed exporters.

### Verdict

Efficiency is not the only value, but security is not a blank cheque. Use expected-loss
analysis: probability of disruption × social cost, compared with the continuing cost of
the resilience instrument.

### Why enforcement changes bargaining

WTO dispute settlement ordinarily moves from consultations to a panel and, where invoked,
appellate review, followed by adoption and implementation procedures. The Appellate Body
was designed as a standing seven-person body capable of upholding, modifying or reversing
panel legal findings.

The official WTO page retrieved on 24 September 2026 states that the Appellate Body is
currently unable to review appeals because of ongoing vacancies and that the last sitting
member's term expired on 30 November 2020. Panels, consultations and notified appeals
continue. Therefore, “WTO dispute settlement has ended” is false; “binding appellate review
is structurally impaired” is accurate.

### Appeal into the void

When a party appeals a panel report into a non-functioning appellate stage, final adoption
can be blocked under the ordinary route. This weakens enforceability and can favour members
with greater bargaining power. Some members use interim arbitration arrangements, but
participation is not universal; do not attribute membership without a dated list.

### Reform tensions

Members debate:

- precedent and the degree of judicial interpretation;
- timeliness and procedural discipline;
- accessibility for developing countries;
- remand authority and standard of review;
- appointment security and political control; and
- how plurilateral interim solutions relate to multilateral legitimacy.

### Developing-country view

A rules-based system can protect smaller economies from pure power bargaining, but
litigation is expensive and remedies are prospective. Reform should restore credible
review while improving speed, legal clarity, assistance and compliance.

### Balanced verdict

The WTO remains valuable for common rules, notifications, committees, negotiations and
panels. Bilateral FTAs cannot fully replace a multilateral baseline because overlapping
clubs fragment rules and leave outsiders exposed.

capability and calibrated protection. The important word is **selective**. India withdrew
from RCEP negotiations in November 2019 amid market-access and domestic-sensitivity
concerns, but subsequently operationalised agreements with the UAE and Australia and
India-EFTA TEPA on 1 October 2025.

The Department of Commerce FTA highlights for 2025-26, retrieved on 24 September 2026,
record the India-UK CETA signature on 24 July 2025 and the India-EU FTA announcement on
27 January 2026. The official India-EU release of 27 January says negotiations were
concluded and describes goods, services, origin, SPS/TBT, trade-remedy and CBAM provisions.
The official India-UK status page was searched and rechecked on 24 September 2026, but no
official India or UK ratification or entry-into-force notification was verified. The
defensible status boundary is therefore signed on 24 July 2025; do not infer operative
concessions from signature alone or convert conclusion or signature into entry into force
without a dated legal notification.

Foreign Trade Policy 2023 was launched on 31 March 2023 and took effect on 1 April 2023
under the official Commerce/DGFT record searched on 24 September 2026. Its process
re-engineering, digitalisation and remission orientation illustrates how domestic export
policy must fit trade disciplines, but policy announcement alone cannot solve logistics,
credit or standards constraints.

### Five tests for every agreement

1. **Market access:** preference margin after exclusions and phase-downs.
2. **Usability:** origin, customs, standards and awareness.
3. **Capability:** investment, technology, supplier and services linkages.
4. **Distribution:** consumers, workers, farmers, MSMEs and sensitive sectors.
5. **Strategy:** diversification, trusted supply and compatibility with multilateral rules.

### Bilateralism's benefit and cost

FTAs can move faster than consensus, tailor commitments and secure market access. But they
discriminate, multiply rules, divert trade and weaken the incentive to repair the
multilateral system. A small exporter may face a different origin calculation for every
market.

### Final policy framework

India should pursue competitive input access, standards capacity, logistics, trade
finance, services mobility and product-market diversification. Protection should be
targeted at demonstrated learning or security externalities, linked to performance and
reviewed. Adjustment support should follow workers and firms rather than freeze the old
production structure.

The final objective is not the largest possible trade surplus. It is higher productivity,
domestic value addition, stable access to essential imports, quality employment and policy
space consistent with credible rules.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - Tariff incidence

**Question:** *A tariff intended to support domestic manufacturing may reduce exports.
Explain in 150 words.*

**Model answer:** A tariff raises the landed price of the covered import. When the import is
a final consumer good, domestic producers may gain price space while consumers lose. When
it is an intermediate input, downstream producers face higher cost. Exporters generally
cannot pass that cost into world prices, so margins, wages, local procurement or orders
fall. The effective rate of protection can therefore be negative even when the final-good
tariff is positive. Tariff revenue is a transfer, while production and consumption
distortions are efficiency losses. India should assess the whole value chain, retain
competitive access to inputs, use remission for genuine exported content and support
capability directly through technology, logistics and standards. Protection should have a
defined externality, beneficiary, performance benchmark and sunset.

## 10 marks - WTO non-discrimination

**Question:** *Differentiate MFN treatment and national treatment with suitable examples.*

**Model answer:** MFN compares one foreign trading partner with another. Ordinarily, a
tariff advantage granted to one WTO member must be extended to others, subject to lawful
exceptions such as an FTA. National treatment compares imported and domestic goods,
services or IP after the foreign item has entered the market. Charging customs duty at the
border is not by itself a national-treatment violation; imposing a discriminatory internal
tax afterward may be. Thus, MFN addresses external partner discrimination, while national
treatment addresses internal discrimination. In GATS and TRIPS the principles operate with
agreement-specific qualifications. UPSC answers should not call MFN zero tariff or treat an
FTA preference as automatically unlawful.

## 15 marks - AoA and food security

**Question:** *Examine the conflict between WTO agricultural-support disciplines and
developing-country public stockholding.*

**Model answer:** The AoA disciplines market access, domestic support and export
competition. Amber support enters AMS unless exempt, while Green, Blue, Article 6.2 and de
minimis rules create policy space. Public procurement at an administered price may be
measured as the administered-price/fixed-reference-price gap multiplied by eligible
production. India argues that the old reference base and inflation exaggerate measured
support and constrain food security and smallholder support. The Bali Peace Clause provides
conditional interim protection for covered programmes but is not an unlimited permanent
exemption. Critics fear production and export spillovers. A durable solution should update
measurement, preserve genuine food-security stocks, assist notification, and prohibit
commercial displacement through subsidised exports.

## 15 marks - FTAs

**Question:** *An FTA should be judged by utilisation and upgrading, not by signed tariff
coverage alone. Discuss.*

**Model answer:** Headline coverage omits exclusions, phase-downs, origin rules and legal
status. A firm uses a preference only when the tariff margin exceeds classification,
certification, record and logistics costs. Trade creation can improve efficiency, while
trade diversion can replace a cheaper non-member. Modern FTAs also influence services,
standards, investment, mobility and digital rules. India should track preference
utilisation, domestic value addition, exporter survival, supplier upgrading, consumer
benefit and adjustment loss. MSMEs require origin guidance, accredited testing, trade
finance and digital customs. Agreement success is therefore a firm-level capability and
productivity outcome, not the number of tariff lines announced.

## 20 marks - Protectionism and bilateralism

**Question:** *“Bilateral trade agreements are a necessary hedge against protectionism but
an incomplete substitute for multilateralism.” Analyse.*

**Model answer:** Bilateral agreements can secure faster, tailored access when multilateral
negotiations stall. India has pursued selective agreements across the Gulf, Indo-Pacific
and Europe while retaining WTO engagement. They can deepen services, investment, standards
and supply-chain cooperation. Yet preferences discriminate against outsiders, multiply
origin regimes, risk trade diversion and offer weaker protection to countries outside the
club. A large partner may also exercise bargaining power. WTO rules provide a common MFN
baseline, transparency, committees and dispute procedures, although appellate review is
impaired. India should use FTAs to diversify markets and build capability, while pressing
for restored WTO dispute settlement, development-sensitive agriculture rules and policy
space. Domestic logistics, standards, competitive inputs and worker adjustment determine
whether access becomes real upgrading.

## 20 marks - Economic security

**Question:** *Design an Indian trade strategy that combines efficiency, employment,
industrial capability and national security.*

**Model answer:** Start from comparative advantage and competitive inputs, then identify
specific market failures or security externalities. For ordinary goods, predictable low
cost and competition should dominate. For critical technologies and inputs, use a
portfolio: domestic capacity, trusted partners, multi-sourcing, recycling, strategic
stocks and substitution R&D. Industrial support should be transparent, time-bound,
competitive and tied to productivity, quality and exports. Tariff structures must be
tested for effective protection and downstream anti-export bias. FTAs should be evaluated
through origin usability, services, standards and value addition. Workers and MSMEs need
adjustment finance, skills and compliance infrastructure. WTO-compatible rules and a
restored dispute system protect India from raw power bargaining. The goal is resilient
integration, not autarky or a trade-surplus fetish.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core trade theory

1. Absolute advantage compares productivity; comparative advantage compares opportunity cost.
2. Mutual-gain terms of trade lie between partner opportunity costs.
3. Trade creates potential aggregate gain; distribution and adjustment remain policy questions.
4. Terms of trade is an export-price/import-price ratio, not the trade balance.
5. Comparative advantage can evolve through learning, logistics, skills and technology.

## B. Border instruments and calculations

1. Tariff = customs tax; ad valorem is percentage, specific is per unit.
2. Small-country tariff: consumer loss = producer gain + revenue + two deadweight losses.
3. Quota fixes quantity; quota rent depends on licence allocation.
4. TRQ applies lower in-quota and higher out-of-quota tariffs.
5. ERP = change in domestic value added / free-trade value added × 100.
6. High input duties can create anti-export bias even with final-good protection.

## C. WTO principles

1. MFN compares foreign partners; national treatment compares import and domestic treatment after entry.
2. Bound tariff is a ceiling; applied tariff is charged; the gap is tariff water.
3. WTO is qualified, rule-bound openness, not compulsory zero tariffs.
4. Exceptions include qualified FTAs, development preferences, remedies and public-policy provisions.
5. Transparency and predictability are independent benefits of rules.

## D. Agreement map

1. GATT - goods; GATS - services; TRIPS - IP minimum standards.
2. TRIMS disciplines covered goods-related local-content/trade-balancing measures.
3. SPS - food, animal and plant health risks; TBT - other technical regulation.
4. SCM, Anti-Dumping and Safeguards have separate predicates.
5. AoA pillars: market access, domestic support and export competition.

## E. Agriculture boxes and stockholding

1. Green Box - criteria-based minimal distortion; Blue Box - production-limiting payments.
2. Amber support feeds AMS unless exempt.
3. Developing-country de minimis: 10% product-specific and 10% non-product-specific.
4. Article 6.2 covers specified development support.
5. Market price support uses administered price minus fixed reference price times eligible production.
6. Peace Clause is conditional interim protection, not a permanent unlimited exemption.
7. Permanent solution status must be verified from a dated official outcome.

## F. SPS, TBT and remedies

1. Legitimate standards can still be discriminatory in design or administration.
2. SPS emphasises science, evidence and risk assessment.
3. Equivalence accepts different measures achieving the required protection.
4. Anti-dumping = dumping + material injury + causation.
5. Countervailing = actionable subsidy + injury + causation.
6. Safeguard = import surge + serious injury/threat + causation.
7. DGTR recommendation is not the final notified duty.

## G. FTAs and origin

1. Status ladder: negotiating -> concluded -> signed -> ratified -> in force.
2. FTA is not zero duty on all goods on day one.
3. Evaluate exclusions, phase-downs, services, standards and adjustment.
4. Origin tests: wholly obtained, tariff shift, RVC and specified process.
5. RVC build-down = (FOB - non-originating inputs) / FOB × 100.
6. Cumulation counts qualifying partner inputs.
7. Trade creation replaces costly domestic supply; diversion replaces a cheaper outsider.
8. Utilisation measures actual preference claiming, not announcement.

## H. Services and GVCs

1. GATS Modes: 1 service crosses; 2 consumer crosses; 3 firm presence; 4 person temporarily crosses.
2. GATS Mode 3 is broader than BoP cross-border services.
3. FY2025-26 Commerce release dated 15 April 2026: merchandise exports USD 441.78 billion; estimated services exports USD 418.31 billion; estimated total exports USD 860.09 billion.
4. March 2026 services values in that release were estimates based on latest February RBI data.
5. Gross exports differ from domestic value added.
6. Competitive imported inputs can enable downstream exports.
7. Upgrading ladder: assembly -> process -> components -> design/IP -> brand/distribution.

## I. Protection, resilience and reform

1. Infant-industry support needs learning evidence, milestones, competition and sunset.
2. Worker adjustment may need income support and skills rather than permanent tariffs.
3. Resilience = diversification + stocks + substitution + domestic option.
4. Self-reliance is not autarky.
5. Security externality must be bounded by expected-loss analysis.
6. WTO Appellate Body could not review appeals on the official page retrieved 24 September 2026.
7. Panels and consultations continue; dispute settlement is impaired, not absent.
8. Bilateral FTAs complement but do not replace a multilateral baseline.

## J. India policy formula

> **Competitive inputs + standards and logistics + usable FTAs + services mobility +
> GVC upgrading + worker/MSME adjustment + narrow strategic capability + WTO reform =
> open and resilient integration.**
