**Learning route:** `transaction → omitted damage → instrument match`

### See the missing-price wedge

```text
FIRM / HOUSEHOLD DECISION
private benefit - private cost
              |
              | emissions, pollution, resource depletion
              v
THIRD-PARTY DAMAGE
health + crop loss + heat stress + flood loss + ecosystem damage
              |
              v
SOCIAL COST = PRIVATE COST + EXTERNAL COST
```

*The price paid by the buyer can omit costs borne by people who were not part of the transaction.*

### Start with the learner's problem

Suppose a factory earns Rs 10 lakh from production and pays Rs 7 lakh for labour, fuel and materials. Its private surplus is Rs 3 lakh. If nearby households bear Rs 2 lakh of health and cleaning costs from pollution, society's net surplus is only Rs 1 lakh. The factory is not necessarily malicious; the market signal is incomplete.

**Stable concept; no separate current claim:** an **externality** is a cost or benefit imposed on others without full market compensation. Climate change is dominated by a negative externality because emitters receive the benefit of energy or production while part of the damage is dispersed across places, people and time.

### Four linked market failures

| Failure | Economic meaning | Climate example | Why private choice underperforms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative externality | A third party bears an unpriced cost | CO2 emissions | Emitter does not face full social cost |
| Public good | Non-rival and difficult to exclude | Stable climate, climate information | Free-riding weakens voluntary provision |
| Common-pool resource | Rival in use, difficult to exclude | Atmospheric absorptive capacity | Each user has an incentive to overuse |
| Information failure | Buyers or lenders lack reliable data | Hidden carbon intensity or climate exposure | Capital and consumption may be misallocated |

These failures overlap. A stable climate has public-good properties, while the atmosphere's limited ability to absorb greenhouse gases resembles a common resource. Carbon disclosure addresses information failure but does not by itself internalise the externality.

### Private, external and social cost

Let:

```text
MPC = marginal private cost
MEC = marginal external cost
MSC = marginal social cost = MPC + MEC
MPB = marginal private benefit
MSB = marginal social benefit
```

A competitive market chooses output where `MPB = MPC`. Social efficiency requires `MSB = MSC`. When `MEC > 0`, unregulated output and emissions are generally above the social optimum.

### India-centred calculation

A brick unit can produce one additional batch at a private cost of Rs 80,000. Buyers value it at Rs 95,000. Local air pollution and incremental climate damage are valued for appraisal at Rs 25,000.

```text
Private net benefit = 95,000 - 80,000 = +15,000
Social net benefit  = 95,000 - (80,000 + 25,000) = -10,000
```

The private decision is "produce"; the social decision is "do not produce in this form" unless cleaner technology reduces the external cost. The calculation establishes the wedge. It does not establish that every estimate of damage is exact; valuation uncertainty returns in Lesson 2.

### Why bargaining alone is insufficient

The Coasean insight is that clear rights and low transaction costs can permit bargaining. Climate change has billions of affected parties, long time horizons, scientific uncertainty and strategic free-riding. Transaction costs are therefore enormous. Bargaining remains useful for local pollution disputes but cannot substitute for collective climate policy.

### Policy families

1. **Price instruments:** tax emissions or subsidise verified positive spillovers.
2. **Quantity instruments:** cap emissions, set performance standards or mandate obligations.
3. **Information instruments:** disclosure, labels, taxonomies and verification.
4. **Public provision:** resilient infrastructure, research, early-warning systems and ecosystem restoration.
5. **Institutional correction:** property or use rights, liability, producer responsibility and international cooperation.

No single tool closes every failure. A carbon price addresses the emissions wedge; adaptation infrastructure addresses residual damage; disclosure reduces information asymmetry; social protection addresses distributional incidence.

### Can nature be priced?

**Criticism:** putting a monetary value on environmental damage commodifies life and nature.
**Reply:** appraisal already makes implicit choices when it approves a road, power plant or regulation. Explicit valuation exposes assumptions and can be supplemented by legal thresholds and safe minimum standards.
**Balanced residual:** use monetary estimates for comparison, but retain rights, ecological limits, irreversibility and distribution as constraints rather than pretending every value is perfectly substitutable.

### Ten anchors to retain

- Externality = uncompensated third-party effect.
- Social cost = private cost + external cost.
- The market output can be excessive when marginal external cost is omitted.
- Climate stability has public-good properties.
- Atmospheric absorptive capacity has common-resource features.
- Information failure can misprice both emissions and climate risk.
- Bargaining works best with clear rights and low transaction costs.
- Climate change has large transaction costs and intergenerational effects.
- Price, quantity, information and public-investment tools solve different failures.
- Monetary appraisal should be combined with rights, thresholds and distribution.

**Learning route:** `damage stream → assumptions → sensitivity`

### Damage diary → present value

```text
1 EXTRA TONNE OF CO2e TODAY
     |
     +-> heat / health loss in year 1
     +-> crop / water damage in year 5
     +-> coastal / infrastructure loss in year 20
     +-> ecosystem and tail-risk damage later
                         |
                         v
discount each future marginal damage to today
                         |
                         v
SOCIAL COST OF CARBON (SCC)
```

*SCC is a present-value estimate of the incremental damage caused by one additional tonne, not the observed price of a traded credit.*

### Definition and boundary

**Stable concept; no separate current claim:** the **Social Cost of Carbon** is the discounted present value of future marginal damages caused by one additional tonne of CO2. In broader policy usage, estimates may be expressed per tonne of CO2 or CO2-equivalent, so the gas and unit must be stated.

It is not automatically:

- a statutory carbon-tax rate;
- the market price of an allowance or credit;
- the average damage from all historical emissions;
- a compensation payment actually received by victims.

### The core present-value equation

```text
SCC = sum from t=0 to T of [Marginal Damage_t / (1 + r)^t]

r = social discount rate
t = number of years from the present
```

Three analytical modules sit behind the result:

1. an emissions trajectory and climate response;
2. a damage function translating climate effects into welfare loss;
3. a discounting rule translating future loss into present value.

### Calculation 1: simple present value

Suppose one extra tonne causes estimated marginal damages of:

| Year | Future damage |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | Rs 105 |
| 2 | Rs 110.25 |
| 3 | Rs 115.76 |

At a 5% discount rate:

```text
PV1 = 105 / 1.05 = 100
PV2 = 110.25 / 1.05^2 = 100
PV3 = 115.76 / 1.05^3 ≈ 100
SCC ≈ Rs 300 per tonne
```

At 10%, the same future damages have a lower present value:

```text
95.45 + 91.12 + 86.97 ≈ Rs 273.54
```

The exercise establishes the discount-rate channel. It does not prove that 5% or 10% is ethically correct.

### Why discounting is contested

| Lower discount rate | Higher discount rate |
|---|---|
| Gives greater weight to future generations | Gives greater weight to present consumption and opportunity cost |
| Raises SCC and supports stronger early action | Lowers SCC and may support gradual action |
| Better reflects long-lived or irreversible damage | May better reflect scarce capital in a fast-growing economy |
| Risks overcommitting present resources if poorly calibrated | Risks underweighting catastrophic or irreversible future loss |

Two ideas must be separated:

- **pure time preference:** how much less weight is given merely because people live later;
- **growth/consumption effect:** future people may be richer, so an extra rupee may yield less marginal utility.

Poor and climate-vulnerable future populations complicate the assumption that future generations will always be richer.

### Calculation 2: expected damage under uncertainty

Assume an additional tonne has:

- 90% probability of ordinary discounted damage of Rs 400;
- 10% probability of severe discounted damage of Rs 2,400.

```text
Expected SCC = 0.90(400) + 0.10(2,400)
             = 360 + 240
             = Rs 600
```

Ignoring the low-probability severe state would understate expected damage by Rs 200. Yet expected value can still understate societal aversion to catastrophic, irreversible loss. Sensitivity analysis should therefore show discount rates, damage functions and tail risks rather than offer false precision.

### Distribution weights

One rupee of damage to a poor household can reduce welfare more than one rupee to a rich household. A distribution-weighted SCC gives greater weight to damage falling on lower-income populations. This improves equity sensitivity but introduces an explicit normative choice. State the weighting rule rather than hiding it.

### SCC, shadow price and project appraisal

A government considering a project can calculate:

```text
climate cost = project emissions x chosen SCC
```

If a road alternative emits 100,000 additional tonnes and the appraisal SCC is Rs 2,000 per tonne, the climate-cost entry is Rs 20 crore. This is a **shadow cost** in appraisal. It is not proof that the project paid Rs 20 crore or that a market traded at Rs 2,000.

### Model-risk hearing

**Criticism:** integrated assessment models combine uncertain climate, growth and damage assumptions, creating a number that looks more precise than the evidence.
**Reply:** transparent ranges and sensitivity analysis are better than assigning an implicit value of zero.
**Residual:** use SCC as one decision input alongside physical thresholds, distribution, adaptation capacity and precaution.

### SCC sensitivity card

- SCC values the marginal damage of one additional tonne.
- It is a present-value estimate, not an observed market price.
- State CO2 versus CO2e and the currency/base year.
- Lower discount rates generally raise SCC.
- Damage functions and climate sensitivity create uncertainty.
- Expected value includes probability-weighted severe outcomes.
- Tail risk and irreversibility may justify precaution beyond expected value.
- Distribution weights make equity assumptions explicit.
- Project appraisal can use SCC as a shadow price.
- Always present ranges and sensitivity rather than false precision.

### Instrument control panel

```text
CARBON TAX
government fixes PRICE -> emissions quantity adjusts

CAP-AND-TRADE
government fixes total QUANTITY -> allowance price adjusts

BASELINE-AND-CREDIT
government fixes performance BASELINE / INTENSITY TARGET
better performer earns credits; worse performer surrenders credits
```

*All three create incentives, but they allocate uncertainty and define the unit differently.*

### Begin with the decision problem

Two cement plants can abate at different costs. Plant A can cut one tonne for Rs 500; Plant B needs Rs 1,500. A uniform carbon price of Rs 1,000 makes A abate and B pay. Society obtains the cheaper reduction first. This is the cost-effectiveness logic of market instruments.

### Carbon tax

A carbon tax imposes a statutory charge per tonne. If tax `T` exceeds a firm's marginal abatement cost, it will usually abate; if abatement costs more than `T`, it pays the tax.

**Strengths:** price predictability, administratively familiar revenue collection, possible revenue recycling.
**Limits:** emissions quantity is uncertain; tax coverage and measurement can be difficult; political resistance rises with visible energy prices.

### Cap-and-trade

The regulator fixes a total cap and issues allowances. Trading produces a market price. Firms with low abatement cost sell; high-cost firms buy.

**Strengths:** quantity control under the cap, cost-effective trading, auction revenue.
**Limits:** volatile prices, allowance over-allocation, market power, monitoring complexity and competitiveness concerns.

### Baseline-and-credit

Each entity receives a benchmark, often emissions per unit of output.

```text
Credits = (baseline intensity - actual intensity) x eligible output
```

If a steel plant's target is 2.0 tCO2e per tonne of steel, actual intensity is 1.8 and eligible output is 1,000 tonnes:

```text
credits = (2.0 - 1.8) x 1,000 = 200 tCO2e
```

If output doubles while intensity falls modestly, absolute emissions can still rise:

```text
Year 1: 2.0 x 1,000 = 2,000 tCO2e
Year 2: 1.8 x 2,000 = 3,600 tCO2e
```

Intensity improved by 10%, yet total emissions rose by 80%. This is the central boundary for India's PAT/CCTS discussion.

### Tax versus trading under uncertainty

The classic Weitzman insight is not "tax always wins" or "cap always wins." If marginal damages rise sharply near a dangerous threshold, quantity certainty can be valuable. If abatement costs are highly uncertain and damages change gradually, price certainty may avoid extreme compliance costs. Hybrid designs use price floors, ceilings or reserves.

### Free allocation, auctioning and incidence

Free allocation can protect trade-exposed firms during transition, but excessive allocation creates windfall gains and weakens scarcity. Auctioning raises public revenue that can fund household rebates, green infrastructure or worker transition. The legal taxpayer or obligated firm may pass some cost to consumers, workers or suppliers; incidence depends on market structure and demand elasticity.

### Revenue-recycling calculation

A tax covers 10 million tonnes at Rs 1,000 per tonne:

```text
gross revenue = Rs 1,000 crore
```

If 40% funds equal household transfers, 30% funds grid and public transport, 20% funds worker-region adjustment and 10% administration/MRV, the tax can change both emissions incentives and distribution. Revenue use is not an afterthought; it shapes political durability.

### Adversarial instrument test

**Criticism:** carbon pricing lets rich polluters "pay to pollute."
**Reply:** a stringent price or cap makes pollution costly and can finance public transition, while standards can constrain activities with unacceptable local harms.
**Residual:** combine carbon pricing with local-pollution limits, technology standards, competition policy and justice safeguards.

- Baseline-and-credit fixes a benchmark, often intensity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Output is expected to grow rapidly and abatement costs are uncertain. What does a baseline-and-credit system control, and what does it leave uncertain?

**Model answer:** It controls performance relative to an output or intensity baseline and creates credits or obligations around that benchmark. Absolute emissions remain uncertain because output growth can outweigh efficiency gains; baseline stringency and MRV therefore matter.

Pricing can reduce future emissions, but it cannot eliminate climate impacts already locked in or compensate every irreversible loss. Policy must separate mitigation, adaptation and loss and damage.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

**Learning route:** `objective → appraisal → residual loss`

### Three climate ledgers

```text
CAUSE OF FUTURE WARMING --------> MITIGATION
emissions / weak sinks            reduce emissions or enhance sinks

EXPOSURE + VULNERABILITY -------> ADAPTATION
heat, flood, drought              reduce expected harm

UNAVOIDED / UNAVOIDABLE HARM ---> LOSS AND DAMAGE RESPONSE
residual destruction              relief, recovery, rehabilitation, finance
```

*Mitigation changes the hazard trajectory; adaptation changes exposure or vulnerability; loss-and-damage arrangements address residual harm.*

### The practical distinction

A cyclone shelter does not reduce global emissions; it reduces mortality and asset loss. Closing a coal unit may reduce emissions but does not automatically protect workers. Rebuilding homes after irreversible coastal loss is not ordinary mitigation or anticipatory adaptation.

### Benefit profiles

| Dimension | Mitigation | Adaptation | Loss and damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main benefit | Avoided global warming | Avoided local/regional loss | Response to residual harm |
| Geography | Benefits diffuse globally | Benefits often local | Benefits affected communities |
| Revenue | Often possible from energy savings or sales | Frequently weak/direct revenue absent | Usually fiscal, grant or insurance need |
| Time | Long-lived global benefit | Often near-term resilience benefit | After or alongside realised/locked-in harm |
| Finance problem | Technology and transition capital | Public-good and avoided-loss monetisation | Equity, responsibility and fiscal capacity |

### Adaptation appraisal calculation

A city can spend Rs 100 crore on drainage. Without the project, expected annual flood loss is Rs 25 crore. With it, expected loss falls to Rs 10 crore for 10 years. Ignoring discounting:

```text
annual avoided loss = 25 - 10 = Rs 15 crore
10-year avoided loss = Rs 150 crore
net benefit = 150 - 100 = Rs 50 crore
benefit-cost ratio = 150 / 100 = 1.5
```

Now add distribution: if most avoided loss accrues to low-income settlements with limited insurance, the welfare benefit may exceed the simple rupee total. But maladaptation remains possible if drainage merely shifts water to another settlement.

### Why adaptation is underfinanced

1. avoided losses are harder to bill than electricity sales;
2. benefits are dispersed and public;
3. local governments may have weak revenue and project-preparation capacity;
4. uncertain climate scenarios complicate cash-flow forecasting;
5. vulnerable populations have low ability to pay;
6. investors may not capture social and ecosystem co-benefits.

This is a case for public finance, concessional capital, guarantees, resilient-infrastructure standards and local capacity - not proof that all adaptation must be grant-funded.

### Insurance: useful but incomplete

Risk pooling can cover probabilistic loss, price risk and speed recovery. It becomes unaffordable when losses are frequent, correlated and near-certain. Insurance also cannot replace physical adaptation or compensate fully for non-economic loss such as culture, identity and biodiversity.

### Loss and damage

**Stable concept; no separate current claim:** economic loss includes income, property and infrastructure; non-economic loss includes life, health, culture, territory and ecosystem services. Attribution, responsibility, access and grant-versus-loan design are central political-economy questions.

**Current anchor:** UNFCCC COP30 decisions recorded progress in operationalising the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage and a 2025-2026 first set of interventions; official UNFCCC decision/outcome pages retrieved 3 October 2026. The lesson does not convert announced finance into disbursement or India-specific receipt.

### Mitigation-adaptation interaction

- efficient cooling reduces heat vulnerability but can increase electricity demand unless efficient and clean;
- mangroves adapt coasts and store carbon, but permanence and local rights matter;
- climate-resilient agriculture can protect yields and sometimes reduce emissions;
- poorly designed seawalls can create ecological loss and transfer erosion.

### What finance cannot repair

**Criticism:** "development is adaptation" can become an excuse to postpone mitigation.
**Reply:** health, income, infrastructure and state capacity genuinely reduce vulnerability.
**Residual:** development must be climate-informed; otherwise it can lock in exposed assets and high emissions. Adaptation and mitigation are complements, not substitutes.

### Objective-sorting checkpoint

### Concept check

**Misconception to avoid:** Insurance payout, adaptation investment and loss-and-damage support are not interchangeable labels.

### Three-column recall sheet

- Mitigation reduces emissions or enhances sinks.
- Adaptation reduces exposure or vulnerability.
- Loss and damage addresses residual realised or unavoidable harm.
- Mitigation benefits are globally dispersed; adaptation benefits are often local.
- Adaptation often lacks a direct revenue stream.
- Avoided-loss appraisal needs baselines and discounting.
- Insurance pools risk but fails for near-certain or systemic loss.
- Non-economic loss cannot be reduced to property values.
- Maladaptation shifts or increases risk.
- Development helps adaptation only when climate-informed.
- Finance claims must separate pledge, approval, disbursement and outcome.

### Net-zero arithmetic

Suppose a jurisdiction emits 100 units in 2050 and removes 20:

```text
net emissions = gross emissions - verified removals
              = 100 - 20
              = 80
```

It is not net zero. If gross emissions fall to 25 and durable removals are 25, net emissions are zero. The example shows why aggressive gross reduction remains necessary: removals are limited, costly and exposed to permanence risk.

### Carbon budget and timing

Two pathways can reach the same annual emission level in 2050 but use very different cumulative budgets.

```text
Path A: early reductions -> lower cumulative emissions
Path B: late steep reductions -> higher cumulative emissions before 2050
```

Climate outcomes respond strongly to cumulative CO2. A distant net-zero year does not erase excess emissions before that year.

### Mitigation-adaptation allocation

Mitigation assets such as renewable electricity may have saleable output. Adaptation benefits frequently appear as avoided loss, service continuity and welfare protection. Private return can therefore be below social return even when the project is highly desirable.

```text

### Mobilisation calculation

A development bank provides:

- Rs 100 crore concessional loan;
- Rs 50 crore first-loss guarantee.

total project finance = 100 + 300 + 100 = Rs 500 crore
public/concessional instruments = Rs 150 crore
private finance = Rs 400 crore if sponsor equity is private
```

But "Rs 400 crore mobilised by Rs 150 crore" may overstate causality if part of the private finance would have arrived anyway. A conservative mobilisation claim requires an attribution method and counterfactual.

### Currency, tenor and project risk

Climate infrastructure often needs long-tenor local-currency finance. Foreign-currency debt can look cheap until depreciation raises repayment. Construction risk, offtaker risk, policy risk, technology risk and demand risk should be allocated to the party best able to manage them, not merely shifted off the public balance sheet.

- project-level identifiers;
- climate-relevance coefficients;
- separation of face value from grant equivalent;
- no double counting across donor, intermediary and recipient;
- reporting of disbursement and outcome, not pledge alone;
- additionality and safeguards.

### International equity

Developing economies argue that historical responsibility, differentiated capacity and higher cost of capital justify concessional international finance and technology access. Creditor countries emphasise mobilisation, private capital and measurable results. A balanced answer distinguishes legal commitments, negotiated goals, voluntary flows and actual disbursement.

### Taxonomy: classification, not certification of impact

A green taxonomy classifies economic activities against environmental objectives and safeguards. It can:

- reduce information asymmetry;
- improve comparability;
- guide lending, bonds and disclosure;
- identify exclusions or transition categories.

It cannot by itself prove that a financed project occurred, performed as promised or caused an additional outcome.

### Binary versus traffic-light design

| Design | Advantage | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Binary green/not green | simple and clear | abrupt cliff effects and exclusion of credible transition |
| Traffic light: green/amber/red | recognises transition pathways | ambiguity and lobbying around thresholds |
| Activity plus performance threshold | links label to measurable standard | data and verification burden |

For a developing economy, taxonomy design must distinguish activities already low-carbon, enabling activities and time-bound transition investments without making "transition" a permanent shelter for high emissions.

### Additionality tests

Additionality asks whether the claimed environmental benefit exceeds a credible counterfactual.

1. **Regulatory additionality:** was the action already legally required?
2. **Financial additionality:** would it occur without the finance or carbon revenue?
3. **Technological/common-practice additionality:** is it beyond ordinary practice?
4. **Environmental additionality:** are measured reductions beyond baseline?

No single test is sufficient. A mandatory pollution-control device may lack regulatory additionality for a carbon credit but remain eligible for a conventional loan.

### Baseline calculation

A plant historically emits 100 units. A new process emits 75. If credible business-as-usual without the project would have fallen to 90:

```text
observed reduction from historical level = 100 - 75 = 25
additional reduction against baseline = 90 - 75 = 15
```

Crediting 25 would overstate the additional outcome by 10. Baseline inflation creates non-additional credits.

### MRV

- **Measurement:** calculate activity and emissions using defined methods.
- **Reporting:** disclose data, assumptions, boundary and uncertainty.
- **Verification:** independent check against rules and evidence.

Verification does not make a weak methodology strong. It can verify that a flawed rule was followed.

### Double counting

| Form | Example | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Double issuance | two credits for one tonne | registry controls |
| Double use | one credit used twice | retirement/cancellation |
| Double claiming | buyer and host both count same mitigation toward targets | authorisation/corresponding adjustment where required |
| Double financing claim | multiple funders each claim the whole project | proportional attribution and project identifiers |

- vague claims without a metric;

- boundary shifting to exclude Scope 3 or outsourced activity;
- use-of-proceeds label without allocation proof;
- intensity improvement presented as absolute reduction;
- purchased offsets presented as operational decarbonisation;

### Greenium calculation

Suppose:

- conventional 10-year government bond yield = 7.10%;
- comparable sovereign green bond yield = 7.06%.

```text
greenium = conventional yield - green yield
          = 0.04 percentage point
          = 4 basis points
```

For Rs 10,000 crore, a simple first-year interest comparison is:

```text
0.04% x Rs 10,000 crore = Rs 4 crore
```

This approximation ignores issuance timing, price, duration and market movement. A greenium can reduce borrowing cost, but illiquidity can also push green yields higher.

### Definition

**Stable concept; no separate current claim:** blended finance strategically combines concessional or public resources with commercial capital to improve risk-return conditions for development or climate outcomes.

It can address:

- first-of-a-kind technology risk;
- project-preparation gaps;
- long construction periods;
- weak borrower credit;
- revenue uncertainty;
- currency risk;
- low-income customer affordability.

### Instrument mechanics

| Instrument | What it changes | Public risk |
|---|---|---|
| Grant | lowers capital/operating cost | full grant amount |
| First-loss tranche | absorbs initial portfolio loss | capped subordinated amount |
| Guarantee | pays on specified default/risk event | contingent liability |
| Viability-gap funding | closes project viability gap | upfront/linked subsidy |
| Subordinated debt | protects senior lenders | higher loss exposure |
| Technical assistance | improves pipeline and capacity | programme cost |
| Currency hedge facility | reduces exchange-rate volatility | pricing and tail exposure |

### Weighted-cost illustration

A Rs 500 crore project is financed by:

- Rs 100 crore concessional debt at 3%;
- Rs 300 crore commercial debt at 10%;
- Rs 100 crore equity with expected return of 15%.

Simple weighted financing cost:

```text
(100/500 x 3) + (300/500 x 10) + (100/500 x 15)
= 0.6 + 6 + 3
= 9.6%
```

Without concessional debt, if the Rs 100 crore also cost 10%, the simple weighted cost would be 11%. The blend lowers cost, but the appraisal must ask whether 3% capital was necessary and whether climate/social benefits justify the subsidy.

### Guarantee expected-loss calculation

A Rs 200 crore guarantee covers 50% of lender loss. Estimated default probability is 8%, and loss given default is 60%.

```text
expected underlying loss = 200 x 0.08 x 0.60 = Rs 9.6 crore
expected guarantee payout at 50% cover = Rs 4.8 crore
```

The face value is Rs 200 crore; expected fiscal cost is not automatically Rs 200 crore. Tail risk can still exceed expected loss.

### Additionality and crowding out

Blending is additional when it enables a project, technology, geography or beneficiary that commercial finance would not serve on reasonable terms. It is weak when it subsidises an already bankable asset or rewards poor structuring.

Use competitive allocation, minimum-concessionality tests, sunset clauses, transparent subsidy equivalents and upside-sharing.

### Risk allocation principle

Allocate risk to the party best able to manage it:

- construction risk -> contractor/project sponsor;
- policy-change risk -> government only where it controls the rule;
- demand risk -> shared according to market design;
- technology performance -> supplier warranties/insurance;
- currency risk -> local-currency finance or explicit hedge;
- climate physical risk -> resilient design, insurance and residual sharing.

### Blended-finance safeguards

- Blended finance mixes concessional/public and commercial capital.
- It should target a defined market failure.
- First loss, guarantees and grants have different fiscal exposures.
- Face value, expected loss and maximum loss differ.
- Weighted financing cost can fall through concessional capital.
- Mobilisation ratio is not impact.
- Subsidy should be minimum necessary.
- Competitive allocation and sunset clauses reduce rent seeking.
- Risk belongs with the party best able to manage it.
- Contingent liabilities require disclosure and stress testing.

**Trap:** leverage ratio does not measure development impact or fiscal cost.
**Answer use:** barrier-instrument-risk bearer-additionality-exit.

*Climate risk becomes financial risk through ordinary credit, market, operational, liquidity and insurance channels.*

### Risk taxonomy

- **Acute physical risk:** cyclone, flood, wildfire, heatwave.
- **Chronic physical risk:** sea-level rise, average heat, water stress, changing rainfall.
- **Transition risk:** policy, technology, demand, price or legal change during decarbonisation.
- **Liability risk:** claims for damage, failure to disclose or misleading green claims.

These categories interact. A delayed transition can reduce near-term policy shock but increase later physical risk and the chance of abrupt repricing.

### Firm-level transmission

A cement producer can face:

- plant damage or water shortage;
- carbon-cost increase;
- demand shift to low-carbon materials;
- capital expenditure for process change;
- CBAM-related reporting and cost;
- litigation or disclosure liability.

Its probability of default can rise while collateral value and cash flow fall simultaneously.

### Bank calculation

A bank has a Rs 1,000 crore loan portfolio in a flood-prone region.

Before stress:

```text
probability of default (PD) = 2%
loss given default (LGD) = 40%
expected credit loss = 1,000 x 0.02 x 0.40 = Rs 8 crore
```

After severe flood scenario:

```text
PD = 6%
LGD = 55%
expected credit loss = 1,000 x 0.06 x 0.55 = Rs 33 crore
incremental expected loss = Rs 25 crore
```

This scenario does not predict the exact future loss. It reveals sensitivity to assumptions.

### Household and distribution channel

Low-income households often have:

- climate-sensitive livelihoods;
- weak insurance;
- lower savings and credit access;
- housing in exposed locations;
- high food and energy expenditure shares.

A food-price shock and lost workdays can create loan delinquency, distress sale and health loss. Climate-risk management that focuses only on large firms misses this transmission.

### Sovereign and fiscal channel

Disasters reduce tax revenue while increasing relief, reconstruction and social-protection expenditure. Public utilities can face asset damage and revenue loss. State governments and urban local bodies differ in fiscal capacity, creating a federal resilience problem.

### Transition-risk time paradox

```text
orderly early transition -> moderate, planned adjustment
delayed transition -> lower immediate cost but larger abrupt future adjustment
no transition -> escalating physical damage and eventual disorder
```

The optimal path is not costless; it minimises combined transition and physical loss subject to development and energy-security constraints.

### Liability and greenwashing

Misstated climate exposure, false green claims or failure to meet fiduciary/disclosure duties can generate legal and reputational loss. Liability risk connects Lessons 7 and 8 to bank and investor balance sheets.

### Transmission-channel recall

- Physical risk may be acute or chronic.
- Transition risk arises from policy, technology, markets and preferences.
- Liability risk includes damage and misleading-claim exposure.
- Climate risk transmits through standard financial-risk channels.
- PD and LGD can rise together after a hazard.
- Collateral concentration creates geographic vulnerability.
- Households transmit climate shocks to banks and welfare systems.
- Disasters affect both revenue and public expenditure.
- Delayed transition can produce abrupt repricing.
- Scenarios explore sensitivity; they do not predict one future.
- Distribution and federal capacity belong in financial-risk analysis.

```text

      v

      v

      v

      v

      v

      v

```

*Recycling is important, but it usually preserves less embedded labour, energy and component value than reuse, repair or remanufacture.*

### Linear versus circular metabolism

```text
LINEAR: extract -> make -> use -> discard
CIRCULAR: design -> use -> maintain -> reuse -> remanufacture -> recycle residual
```

A circular economy aims to reduce virgin extraction, waste and pollution while retaining products, components and materials at high value. It is not literally a closed loop: collection losses, quality degradation, energy use and thermodynamic limits remain.

### Material-flow accounting

For a production system:

```text
Material input = product output + recovered by-product + waste + stock accumulation
```

Example:

- virgin input: 1,000 tonnes;
- recycled input: 200 tonnes;
- saleable product: 900 tonnes;
- recoverable by-product: 150 tonnes;
- process waste: 100 tonnes;
- stock increase: 50 tonnes.

Check:

```text
input = 1,200
output/accounted = 900 + 150 + 100 + 50 = 1,200
recycled-content rate = 200 / 1,200 = 16.67%
process-waste rate = 100 / 1,200 = 8.33%
```

Do not call the system "100% circular" merely because all waste is recorded.

### Product-life calculation

If a phone is normally replaced every 3 years, extending average life to 5 years reduces annual replacement demand per continuing user:

```text
old annual replacement rate = 1/3 = 0.333
new rate = 1/5 = 0.20
reduction = (0.333 - 0.20) / 0.333 ≈ 40%
```

Actual material savings depend on market growth, repair inputs and rebound.

### Circular strategies and limits

| Strategy | Value preserved | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Sharing/intensification | service from fewer products | transport, coordination, wear |
| Repair | whole product | spare parts, design locks, skill |
| Refurbishment | product with restored condition | quality assurance |
| Remanufacture | components and embodied value | reverse logistics and standards |
| Recycling | material | contamination, downcycling, energy |
| Energy recovery | some energy value | destroys material and may pollute |

### Emissions channel

Circularity can reduce emissions by lowering:

- virgin extraction;
- primary material processing;
- manufacturing of replacement products;
- waste treatment and landfill methane;
- logistics, where local loops are efficient.

But repeated transport, energy-intensive recycling or lower product efficiency can offset gains. Life-cycle boundaries matter.

## Lesson 18 - Carbon-credit integrity: baselines, additionality, permanence, leakage, verification and retirement

Progress: 18 / 19 | Stage: Optional Advanced | Subtopic: Carbon-credit integrity: baselines, additionality, permanence, leakage, verification and retirement

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - canonical Topic 25, BEE compliance/offset architecture and official Article 6 material

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

**Learning route:** `baseline → custody → retirement`

### Custody chain of one credited tonne

```text
ELIGIBLE PROJECT
   -> conservative baseline
   -> additional action
   -> monitored reduction/removal
   -> leakage adjustment
   -> permanence / reversal safeguard
   -> independent verification
   -> registry issuance
   -> transfer
   -> retirement
```

*A certificate is credible only when the whole chain is sound.*

### Reduction, avoidance and removal

- **Reduction:** emissions fall relative to a baseline.
- **Avoidance:** an expected emissions source does not occur.
- **Removal:** greenhouse gas is taken from the atmosphere and stored.

These have different measurement and permanence profiles. Avoided deforestation is not identical to geological storage; renewable generation is not an atmospheric removal.

### Baseline integrity

An inflated baseline manufactures credits. Use conservative assumptions, standardised benchmarks where suitable, transparent data and periodic updating. Dynamic baselines can reflect technological change but create uncertainty for investment.

### Additionality

The project must exceed the credible no-credit counterfactual. Tests include legal requirement, investment barrier, common practice and financial dependence on credit revenue. Mechanical pass/fail tests can be gamed; reasoned documentation and ex-post review remain important.

### Leakage

A forest project prevents clearing inside its boundary, but agriculture shifts outside. If on-site avoided emissions are 100,000 tCO2e and estimated leakage is 20%:

```text
net before other deductions = 100,000 x (1 - 0.20)
                            = 80,000 tCO2e
```

Ignoring leakage overstates the climate benefit.

### Permanence and buffer pool

Biological carbon can reverse through fire, disease or land-use change. If verified removals are 50,000 tonnes and a 15% buffer is withheld:

```text
tradable credits = 50,000 x 0.85 = 42,500
buffer = 7,500
```

Buffer pools spread reversal risk but may fail under correlated systemic fires. Long monitoring, replacement obligations and conservative crediting are needed.

### Verification, registry and retirement

Verification checks the project report. The registry assigns serial numbers and tracks ownership. Retirement permanently removes the unit from circulation for a claim. A transfer without retirement does not establish final use.

### Offset hierarchy

```text
measure -> reduce own emissions -> substitute/innovate
          -> neutralise limited residual emissions with high-integrity credits
```

Using credits before serious operational reduction can delay structural change. Yet high-integrity finance can support mitigation outside the buyer's boundary. The issue is sequencing, quality and claim wording.

### Crediting-period and vintage

Credit vintage is the period in which the mitigation occurred, not necessarily the issue or purchase date. Old vintages may represent real reductions but can have weaker additionality to a current claim and different methodology.

### Integrity failure chain

**Criticism:** counterfactual credits can never prove what would have happened.
**Reply:** policy routinely uses counterfactual appraisal; conservative methods and random/benchmark evidence can improve credibility.
**Residual:** do not claim certainty; discount uncertain units and restrict offset use where direct abatement is feasible.

### Tonne-integrity checkpoint

### Concept check

**Question:** A credit is verified and issued, but the project was non-additional and the same serial number remains active after use. Which integrity failures invalidate the claim?

**Model answer:** Non-additionality means the claimed mitigation was not caused by the crediting incentive. Failure to retire the serial number permits reuse or double claiming. Verification of activity cannot cure a false baseline or broken custody-and-retirement chain.

**Misconception to avoid:** Issuance is not integrity; additionality, custody, retirement and claims all remain necessary.

### Credit-quality application

**Probable framing:** "A carbon credit is an accounting claim before it becomes a climate outcome."
**Trap:** issuance, ownership and retirement are different registry events.
**Answer use:** unit-baseline-additionality-leakage-permanence-verification-registry-retirement-claim.

**Mains prompt:** Construct a tonne-level integrity test for a carbon credit from baseline selection to final retirement and claim wording.

**Ceiling:** 10 marks; maximum 150 words.

**Model (baseline–custody–claim):** A credit is credible only if the causal tonne and its ownership survive every link from baseline to retirement. Define the unit and distinguish reduction, avoidance and removal. Build the integrity chain from conservative baseline and additionality to leakage, permanence, MRV, serialised registry and retirement. Add vintage, safeguards and claim wording. Use leakage/buffer calculations. Conclude that markets reduce cost only when the credited tonne is real, additional, durable and uniquely claimed.

- Reduction, avoidance and removal are distinct.
- Baselines define the counterfactual.
- Additionality asks whether credit revenue/action changes the outcome.

### Circular firm as an asset loop

```text
CONVENTIONAL SALE
producer sells product -> buyer bears maintenance/end-of-life -> replacement demand

PRODUCT-AS-A-SERVICE
producer retains ownership -> earns from service -> durability/repair has value

INDUSTRIAL SYMBIOSIS
Firm A residue/heat/water -> verified input for Firm B -> lower virgin input and waste
```

*Business-model incentives determine whether durability is profitable or whether firms benefit from rapid replacement.*

### Circular business models

1. **Product as a service:** payment for lighting, mobility, cooling or machine uptime.
2. **Leasing and take-back:** producer retains residual-value interest.
3. **Repair and refurbishment:** extend useful life.
4. **Remanufacture:** rebuild to defined performance using recovered components.
5. **Sharing platforms:** raise utilisation of underused assets.
6. **Industrial symbiosis:** one firm's by-product becomes another's input.
7. **Secondary-material marketplaces:** improve quality discovery and contracting.

### Incentive logic

Under a one-time sale, a producer can profit from replacement. Under a service contract, failure creates producer cost, so durability, modularity and energy efficiency can become profitable. This result depends on contract design, consumer protection and competition; service models can also lock users into proprietary ecosystems.

### Total-cost-of-ownership calculation

Machine A:

- purchase Rs 10 lakh;
- maintenance Rs 2 lakh/year for 5 years;
- residual value Rs 1 lakh.

Machine B (remanufactured service model):

- upfront/service present cost Rs 14 lakh;
- maintenance included;
- residual obligation with provider.

Ignoring discounting:

```text
A total cost = 10 + (2 x 5) - 1 = Rs 19 lakh
B total cost = Rs 14 lakh
```

A lower purchase price can hide a higher lifecycle cost. Public procurement should compare service, uptime, energy and end-of-life, not sticker price alone.

### Industrial-symbiosis calculation

Firm A produces 10,000 tonnes of slag. Firm B can substitute it for 8,000 tonnes of virgin input after quality treatment costing Rs 500/tonne. Virgin input costs Rs 1,200/tonne; transport is equal.

```text
gross virgin-cost avoided = 8,000 x 1,200 = Rs 96 lakh
treatment cost = 8,000 x 500 = Rs 40 lakh
gross operating saving = Rs 56 lakh
```

The environmental benefit also depends on processing emissions, safety and avoided disposal.

### Rebound effect

Efficiency lowers the effective price of a service and can increase use.

```text
engineering energy saving = 30%
service use rises = 10%
new energy use = 0.70 x 1.10 = 0.77 of original
net saving = 23%, not 30%
```

If use rises enough, savings can be erased. Rebound does not mean efficiency is useless; it means policy should measure actual system outcomes.

### Competition and market power

Repair restrictions, proprietary parts, software locks and information asymmetry can block circularity. Standards for interoperability, right-to-repair, warranties and quality certification can expand markets. Poorly designed mandates can, however, expose consumers to unsafe parts or reduce innovation incentives.

### Trade and competitiveness

Resource efficiency can reduce import dependence for critical minerals and materials. Quality standards, traceability and stable demand are needed for secondary materials. Export markets may increasingly require lifecycle and recycled-content evidence, but every current trade rule must be cited by jurisdiction and date.

**Question:** Distinguish the Social Cost of Carbon, a carbon tax and a traded carbon price in one causal chain.

**Question:** Why does a complete climate-finance architecture require three different funding logics?

**Model answer:** Mitigation can often generate saleable energy or efficiency cash flow; adaptation frequently produces dispersed avoided losses and public-good benefits; loss and damage addresses residual harm that cannot be prevented. ### Check 4 - CCTS boundary

**Question:** Why can falling GEI coexist with rising absolute emissions?

**Model answer:** Absolute emissions equal emissions intensity multiplied by output. If output grows faster than intensity falls, total emissions rise. Therefore CCTS performance must be read with absolute-emission disclosure and sector transition plans.

**Question:** Name the separate gates between an eligible activity and a valid internationally used mitigation outcome.

**Model answer:** Methodology eligibility, project validation/registration, monitoring, verification, issuance, host authorisation, transfer, registry tracking, corresponding adjustment where applicable and final retirement/claim are distinct gates.

### Check 6 - circular hierarchy

**Question:** Why is remanufacture normally more circular than material recycling?

**Model answer:** Remanufacture preserves product components, embedded energy and labour, whereas recycling usually destroys product-level value to recover material. Lifecycle evidence can qualify the hierarchy where transport, contamination or energy use changes the result.

## 10 marks - Social Cost of Carbon

**Question:** Distinguish the Social Cost of Carbon from a carbon tax and a traded carbon price. Why can the three values differ? (150 words)

**Model answer:** The Social Cost of Carbon is a model-based present value of marginal future damage from one additional tonne of CO2 or CO2e. A carbon tax is a statutory levy fixed by government. A traded price emerges from allowance or credit scarcity, compliance demand and market rules. SCC varies with climate response, damage functions, discounting, distribution weights and tail risk. A tax also reflects political feasibility, administrative capacity, competitiveness and revenue policy. A market price reflects cap or baseline stringency, allocation, banking, liquidity and permitted offsets. Thus SCC can guide ambition, but tax and market prices need not equal it. A credible answer states unit, currency/base year and boundary, then uses sensitivity rather than a timeless universal number.

### Scoring rubric

| Credit | What the examiner should see |
|---:|---|
| 20% | Exact three-way definition: damage estimate, statutory levy and scheme-generated price. |
| 40% | Named determinants: discounting/damage assumptions; political-administrative tax choice; scarcity, banking and liquidity. |
| 20% | Qualification on unit, currency/base year, uncertainty and absence of one universal SCC. |
| 20% | A concise judgement explaining how SCC may inform—but does not mechanically determine—the other prices. |

## 10 marks - Circular hierarchy

**Question:** Why is recycling only one component of a circular economy? (150 words)

**Model answer:** Recycling recovers material after product value has largely been destroyed. Circularity first avoids unnecessary throughput, redesigns products, extends life and preserves products/components through reuse, repair, refurbishment and remanufacture. These options generally retain more embedded labour, energy and material value. Recycling remains essential for residual material but faces collection loss, contamination, downcycling and energy use. A circular policy therefore combines design standards, repair access, product-service models, secondary-material quality, EPR and green procurement. Material-flow and lifecycle metrics should track virgin input, product life, actual recovery and absolute footprint. The objective is not maximum recycling alone but minimum virgin-resource and waste intensity for a required service.

### Scoring rubric

| Credit | What the examiner should see |
|---:|---|
| 20% | Defines circular economy as value and material retention rather than waste disposal alone. |
| 40% | Orders prevention, reuse, repair, refurbishment/remanufacture and recycling with embedded-value reasoning. |
| 20% | Qualifies the hierarchy through contamination, collection loss, downcycling, energy use and lifecycle evidence. |
| 20% | Ends with measurable India-relevant tools: design standards, EPR, repair access and secondary-material demand. |

## 15 marks - Carbon-pricing choice

**Question:** Compare carbon tax, cap-and-trade and baseline-and-credit. Which design considerations matter for India? (250 words)

**Model answer:** A carbon tax fixes price and leaves emission quantity responsive; cap-and-trade fixes an absolute allowance quantity and lets price adjust; baseline-and-credit rewards performance relative to an output/intensity benchmark. All can equalise marginal abatement cost, but they allocate uncertainty differently. Tax offers price and revenue predictability but uncertain quantity. A cap offers quantity certainty but can generate volatility and allocation rents. Baseline-credit fits expanding industry but may allow absolute emissions to rise when output outpaces efficiency.

For India, design must consider industrial heterogeneity, MRV capacity, energy security, trade exposure, market liquidity and distribution. PAT created experience in intensity targets and certificate trading; CCTS extends this toward GHG intensity. Strong design requires conservative baselines, tightening trajectories, verified data, transparent banking, limits on weak offsets and absolute-emission disclosure. Auction or tax revenue can fund grids, technology, household relief and worker-region transition. Carbon pricing should complement local pollution standards, industrial finance and green procurement. The correct choice is therefore not a slogan but a coherent architecture suited to sector and institutional capacity.

| Credit | What the examiner should see |
|---:|---|
| 20% | Correctly allocates price and quantity uncertainty across the three instruments. |
| 40% | Uses PAT/CCTS, MRV, industrial heterogeneity, banking, allocation and revenue recycling as named evidence. |
| 20% | Identifies baseline inflation, volatility, leakage and intensity-versus-absolute-emissions limitations. |
| 20% | Recommends an institutionally feasible Indian architecture rather than declaring one universal winner. |

## 15 marks - Climate finance and greenwashing

**Question:** "India's climate-finance challenge is as much about integrity and risk allocation as about volume." Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer:** Volume is constrained by high cost of capital, short tenor, currency risk, weak project pipelines and limited revenue from adaptation. Yet larger flows without integrity can relabel ordinary finance, double count mobilisation or fund non-additional projects. A robust architecture separates grants, debt, equity and guarantees; commitment, disbursement and outcome; mitigation, adaptation and transition; and public from private sources.

Taxonomy can guide eligibility, but baseline, MRV, additionality and safeguards determine credibility. Green and sovereign bonds need allocation and impact reports; blended finance needs minimum concessionality and contingent-liability disclosure; adaptation needs public and concessional finance because avoided-loss benefits are not fully monetisable. RBI's dated bank directions and SEBI's BRSR changes illustrate movement toward governance, assurance and impact data, while the final taxonomy status must be stated carefully.

India should deepen local-currency bond markets, project preparation and DFIs; use guarantees selectively; publish mobilisation methods; integrate green budgeting; and support MSME data capacity. Finance becomes transformational only when it changes investment and produces verified, equitable outcomes.

| Credit | What the examiner should see |
|---:|---|
| 20% | Separates finance volume from integrity, terms, additionality and risk allocation. |
| 40% | Uses taxonomy, SGrB allocation/impact reporting, blended finance, BRSR/RBI evidence and adaptation revenue weakness. |
| 20% | Tests greenwashing, double counting, contingent liability, MSME capacity and draft/final status. |

## 20 marks - CCTS and Article 6

**Question:** Analyse India's emerging carbon-market architecture from PAT and CCTS to Article 6 participation. Identify the principal integrity and development safeguards. (Maximum 250 words)

**Model answer:** PAT established energy-intensity targets, verification and tradable Energy Saving Certificates. The CCTS, notified on 28 June 2023 and amended on 19 December 2023, creates compliance and offset mechanisms. Its compliance arm assigns defined GHG-intensity targets, issues Carbon Credit Certificates for verified over-performance and requires acquisition/surrender for shortfall through a regulated institutional chain. This can lower compliance cost and encourage industrial learning, but intensity improvement does not guarantee absolute decline. Baseline inflation, thin liquidity, generous crediting, verification weakness and unequal transition cost are major risks.

Article 6 adds international cooperation. Article 6.2 governs transferred mitigation outcomes; Article 6.4 is a UN-supervised mechanism; authorisation and corresponding adjustments prevent double national claiming where applicable. India's approved activity lists indicate policy eligibility, not automatic project registration, issuance or exportability. CCTS units must not be presumed fungible internationally.

Safeguards should include conservative baselines, additionality, leakage/permanence rules, serialised registries, retirement, absolute-emission disclosure, strategic host authorisation, protection of NDC space, benefit sharing, community rights and clear claim language. Industrial finance, technology, green procurement and worker-region support must complement the market. India should use carbon markets as one instrument of structural decarbonisation, not as a substitute for it.

| 20% | Proposes registry, retirement, authorisation, community and just-transition safeguards without assuming fungibility. |

## 20 marks - Integrated climate-economy strategy

**Question:** Design a climate-economy strategy for India that reconciles growth, adaptation, finance, circularity, federalism and competitiveness. (Maximum 250 words)

**Model answer:** India requires affordable energy and industrial growth while facing physical climate risk, capital constraints and trade pressure. Strategy should begin with common measurement: absolute/intensity emissions, hazard exposure, material flows, finance stages and distribution. Carbon pricing/CCTS and performance standards should change incentives; grids, storage, R&D and blended finance should make low-carbon substitution feasible. Public and concessional finance should prioritise adaptation, local resilience and vulnerable communities where revenue is weak.

Green taxonomy, bond allocation/impact reporting, BRSR/RBI risk governance and stress testing should direct capital without greenwashing. Circular policy should place reduction, durability, repair and remanufacture before recycling, backed by EPR, quality standards, informal-worker integration and secondary-material demand. Federal transition grants should support coal regions, local bodies and unequal state capacity. Carbon revenue and efficiency gains should fund rebates, public transport, skills and social protection.

For competitiveness, firms need product-level MRV, efficient energy/material use and technical engagement with CBAM, while India preserves the distinction between EU border rules and domestic CCTS. Milestones, sunset clauses, public dashboards and grievance mechanisms should prevent rent seeking. The objective is a sequenced portfolio that raises productivity and resilience while reducing emissions and material dependence fairly.

## 1. Foundational climate economics

- Climate change combines a **negative externality**, **global public-good** mitigation, **common-pool** sinks and intergenerational distribution.
- The private emitter does not bear full marginal social damage; uncorrected markets overproduce emissions.
- Policy choices include pricing, quantity regulation, standards, information, public investment, innovation support and rights/governance.
- Select instruments by failure: externality, public good, information asymmetry, capital constraint, coordination failure or distributional barrier.

## 2. SCC, discounting and carbon value

- **SCC**: present value of marginal damage caused by one additional tonne of CO2/CO2e.
- `SCC = sum of marginal damage_t / (1 + r)^t`.
- Lower discount rate, higher climate sensitivity, convex damage or tail-risk weight generally raises SCC.
- **SCC != carbon tax != traded carbon price**.
- Always state currency/base year, gas/unit, geographic boundary, discounting and model uncertainty.
- Use scenario/sensitivity ranges rather than false precision.

## 3. Carbon-pricing instruments

- Tax fixes price; cap fixes aggregate allowance quantity; baseline-credit fixes a performance benchmark.
- Cost effectiveness arises when heterogeneous sources face a common marginal incentive and can respond flexibly.
- Tax revenue or allowance auction revenue can support household relief, public investment and transition.
- Banking stabilises intertemporal compliance but requires rules; excessive borrowing can postpone action.
- Free allocation may address leakage temporarily but can create windfall rents.
- Intensity improvement can coexist with rising absolute emissions: `emissions = intensity x output`.

## 4. Mitigation, adaptation and loss and damage

- Mitigation reduces future hazard; adaptation reduces exposure/vulnerability; loss-and-damage response addresses residual harm.
- Adaptation finance is constrained by public-good benefits, weak cash revenue, dispersed avoided losses and local capacity.
- Appraise adaptation using expected avoided loss, distribution, robustness, flexibility and maladaptation risk.
- Efficient portfolios integrate all three rather than using adaptation as a substitute for mitigation.

## 5. NDC, net zero and just transition

- NDCs are nationally determined plans under the Paris architecture; compare metric, base, scope, target year, conditions and implementation.
- Net zero balances residual anthropogenic emissions with removals at a specified boundary/time.
- A finite carbon budget makes delay costly: later action requires steeper cuts or more removals.
- Just transition addresses workers, regions, consumers, MSMEs and fossil-dependent public finance.
- Sequence alternatives, finance and capability before or alongside closures.

## 6. Climate-finance architecture

- Separate **source** (public/private; domestic/international), **instrument** (grant/debt/equity/guarantee), **use** and **finance stage**.
- Commitment, approval, allocation, disbursement, output, outcome and impact are different.
- Concessional finance is strongest where revenue capture is weak, first-mover risk is high or public benefits dominate.
- Mobilisation claims require a transparent counterfactual and attribution method.
- Climate finance must not be equated with every broad development expenditure.

## 7. Taxonomy, MRV and greenwashing

- Taxonomy classifies activities using objectives, thresholds, safeguards and transition rules; it does not prove impact by itself.
- **MRV**: measurement/monitoring, reporting and verification.
- **Additionality** asks whether the outcome exceeds a credible baseline because of the intervention.
- Double counting includes double issuance, double use, double claiming and double financing attribution.
- Greenwashing tests: vague claim, selective boundary, absent baseline, weak evidence, hidden trade-off and misleading net wording.
- As checked to 3 October 2026, India's official climate-finance taxonomy located for this session remained a draft framework released in May 2025; do not call it final.

## 8. Green, sustainability and sovereign green bonds

- Green/social/sustainability bonds are use-of-proceeds instruments; SLBs link general-purpose financing terms to issuer KPIs/SPTs.
- Bond integrity chain: framework -> project selection -> ring-fencing/tracking -> allocation report -> impact report -> external review.
- A sovereign green bond finances eligible public expenditure but remains sovereign debt.
- Avoid counting eligible requirement, issuance, allocation, expenditure and environmental impact as identical.
- Indian SGrB allocation/issuance figures must carry the relevant Budget or borrowing-calendar date.

## 9. Blended finance

- Blended finance uses public/philanthropic concessional capital to alter risk-return and mobilise suitable private capital.
- Instruments: first-loss, guarantee, subordinated debt, concessional loan, viability-gap support, FX/liquidity facility and technical assistance.
- Tests: additionality, minimum concessionality, fair risk sharing, mobilisation method, contingent liability, safeguards and exit.
- De-risk the specific non-commercial barrier; do not socialise ordinary commercial loss.

## 10. Climate risk and finance

- Physical risk: acute events and chronic trends.
- Transition risk: policy, technology, market, demand and legal change during decarbonisation.
- Transmission: hazard/shock -> exposure -> cash flow/collateral/cost -> financial risk -> system feedback.
- Scenario analysis explores conditional futures; stress testing quantifies resilience under severe assumptions.
- Key outputs: loss, PD/LGD, valuation, liquidity, capital and concentration; always state assumptions.
- RBI directions for commercial banks were issued on 28 November 2025 and phase requirements by bank category; retain exact scope/date.
- SEBI's 28 March 2025 BRSR circular changed value-chain assessment/disclosure features; BRSR is disclosure, not impact certification.

## 11. PAT, CCTS and credit integrity

- PAT built industrial intensity targets, Designated Consumers, verification and tradable ESCerts.
- CCTS was notified on 28 June 2023 and amended on 19 December 2023.
- Compliance mechanism: target -> verified performance -> issue CCC for over-performance / surrender for shortfall -> registry and trading.
- GEI target rules were notified in 2025 for specified sectors including aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, and pulp and paper; retain sector/date status.
- Credit integrity: conservative baseline, additionality, quantification, verification, leakage control, permanence/buffer, serialisation and retirement.
- A registry prevents some forms of duplication but cannot repair a weak methodology.
- Do not treat draft sector material as final notification.

## 12. Article 6

- Article 6.2: cooperative approaches using authorised ITMOs and accounting.
- Article 6.4: UN-supervised crediting mechanism; Article 6.8: non-market approaches.
- Corresponding adjustment prevents the same mitigation outcome from being counted toward both transferring and acquiring national targets where applicable.
- Host-country eligibility lists are policy gates, not project registration, issuance or transfer guarantees.
- Export decisions should consider NDC headroom, strategic sectors, price and domestic development benefit.
- CCTS, Article 6 units and voluntary credits are not automatically interchangeable.

## 13. Circular economy and material flow

- Objective: deliver service with lower virgin input, waste and lifecycle impact while retaining product/component/material value.
- Priority: avoid/reduce -> reuse/share -> repair -> refurbish/remanufacture -> recycle -> recover -> dispose.
- Core identity: `inputs = useful stock addition + outputs/waste`, with system boundaries stated.
- Track product life, reuse/repair, recycled content/input, actual collection/recovery, virgin displacement, absolute material footprint and lifecycle effects.
- Rebound occurs when lower cost increases use enough to offset efficiency savings.
- Circularity is not established by a high recycling percentage alone.

## 14. EPR and informal integration

- EPR transfers post-consumer responsibility toward producers and can create design, collection and recycling incentives.
- Effective chain: producer obligation -> collection/traceability -> accredited processing -> verified certificate -> safe residual disposal.
- Risks: paper compliance, fraudulent certificates, cherry-picking valuable waste and unsafe informal processing.
- Integrate waste pickers through registration, contracts, fair payment, PPE, social protection, training and material-recovery infrastructure.
- Evaluate actual recycling/reuse, not collection or certificate purchase alone.

## 15. Distribution, federalism and competitiveness

- Climate policy incidence varies across income, geography, occupation, sector and time.
- Carbon-pricing regressivity depends on consumption baskets, pass-through, revenue recycling and alternatives.
- States and ULBs differ in exposure, fiscal dependence, administrative capacity and investment need.
- Tools: formula grants, transition funds, project-preparation support, shared data and outcome-linked transfers.
- EU CBAM entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026; treat it as an EU import instrument, not an Indian tax.
- Competitiveness response: efficient production, clean power/materials, product-level MRV, finance/technology support and careful trade engagement.

## 16. Integrated transition test

For every proposal ask:

1. What market/institutional failure is targeted?
2. What metric and boundary are used?
3. Through what causal mechanism will behaviour change?
4. Who pays, benefits and bears risk?
5. Which enabling finance, infrastructure and capability are missing?
6. How will MRV, additionality and double counting be handled?
7. What distributional/federal/trade effects arise?
8. What milestone, review and correction rule applies?
