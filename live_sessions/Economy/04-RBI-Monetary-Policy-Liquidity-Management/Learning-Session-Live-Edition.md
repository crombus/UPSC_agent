## 🖼️ RBI’s functional architecture

```text
                         RESERVE BANK OF INDIA
                                   │
             ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
             │                     │                     │
       Monetary system       Banking system        Public authority
             │                     │                     │
     ┌───────┴───────┐      ┌──────┴──────┐       ┌──────┴──────┐
 Monetary policy   Liquidity  Regulation  Bankers'  Government  Currency
 implementation   management supervision   bank      banker      management
             │                     │                     │
      Price stability       Financial stability     Debt management
       with growth          and depositor trust      and forex reserves
```

*RBI is not merely the institution that changes the repo rate; it is India’s central monetary and financial authority.*

## 1. Legal and institutional identity

| Feature | Position |
|---|---|
| Legal foundation | **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** |
| Commenced operations | **1 April 1935** |
| Ownership | Nationalised in **1949** |
| Institutional status | **Statutory body**, not constitutional body |
| Headquarters | Mumbai |
| Leadership | Governor and Central Board |
| Broad objective | Monetary stability and operation of currency and credit |
| Current policy objective | Price stability while keeping growth in mind |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: “34–35–49”**
> Act in **1934** → operations in **1935** → nationalisation in **1949**

✅ **Fact:** The Constitution does not create the RBI. Parliament established it through legislation.

⚠️ **Inference:** RBI possesses operational expertise and autonomy, but it is not sovereign or institutionally detached from the Union government.

---

## 2. The seven central-banking roles

| RBI role | What it does | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Monetary authority | Implements monetary policy and manages liquidity | MPC decides the policy rate |
| Currency authority | Issues and manages banknotes | ₹1 note and coins are issued by Government of India |
| Banker to government | Maintains government accounts and facilitates payments | This differs from commercial banking |
| Public-debt manager | Manages Union and state government borrowing | Debt management is not the MPC’s function |
| Bankers’ bank | Holds bank reserves and provides settlement facilities | Banks maintain specified reserves with RBI |
| Lender of last resort | Provides emergency liquidity to solvent but illiquid banks | Liquidity support cannot cure insolvency |
| Regulator and supervisor | Regulates banks and specified financial institutions | RBI does not regulate every financial entity |

### Additional responsibilities

```text
Foreign-exchange management ──→ FEMA administration + reserve management
Payment-system oversight    ──→ safety and efficiency of payment systems
Financial stability         ──→ supervision + liquidity + crisis response
Financial inclusion         ──→ access, payment infrastructure and credit policy
```

## 3. RBI versus MPC

```text
Union Government
      │
      ├── Notifies the inflation target
      │
      ▼

      │
      ├── Decides policy repo rate
      │

```

| Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|
| “RBI Governor alone fixes the repo rate” | Incorrect under the MPC framework |
| “MPC manages daily banking liquidity” | Incorrect; RBI conducts operations |
| “MPC regulates commercial banks” | Incorrect; this is an RBI function |
| “RBI and MPC are identical institutions” | Incorrect; MPC is a statutory decision-making committee within the framework |

## 4. Currency-issue trap

Under Section 22 of the RBI Act, RBI has the sole right to issue **banknotes**, subject to the special treatment of the ₹1 note.

| Currency instrument | Issuer | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| ₹1 note | Government of India | Finance Secretary |
| Other banknotes | RBI | RBI Governor |
| Coins | Government of India | No signature |

RBI nevertheless manages the circulation and distribution of coins on behalf of the government.

> **Prelims trap:** “All currency in India is issued by RBI” is false.

---

## 5. Lender of last resort

```text
Temporary cash shortage
          │
          ▼
 Bank owns sound assets
but lacks immediate liquidity
          │
          ▼
 RBI may provide emergency liquidity
          │
          ▼
 Payment failure and contagion avoided
```

### Liquidity versus solvency

| Condition | Meaning | Central-bank response |
|---|---|---|
| Illiquidity | Assets exceed liabilities, but cash is temporarily unavailable | Emergency liquidity can help |
| Insolvency | Liabilities exceed viable asset value | Lending alone does not repair the institution |

⚠️ Providing unlimited funds to an insolvent bank can create **moral hazard** and transfer losses to the financial system.

## 🖼️ Monetary-policy decision chain

```text
Union Government + RBI consultation
                 │
                 ▼
        Inflation target notified
         4% CPI, band of 2–6%
                 │
                 ▼
       MPC assesses the economy
 Inflation │ Growth │ Risks │ Forecasts
                 │

        Policy repo rate decision
                 │
                 ▼
       RBI implements the decision
   Liquidity operations + communication
                 │
                 ▼
 Interest rates → demand → output → inflation

```

*The government determines the target framework; the MPC chooses the policy rate; RBI implements the decision.*

## 1. Flexible inflation targeting

India formally adopted the statutory framework through the **2016 amendment to the RBI Act, 1934**.

| Component | Current position |
|---|---|
| Target measure | Headline **CPI Combined** inflation |
| Point target | **4%** |
| Lower tolerance limit | **2%** |
| Upper tolerance limit | **6%** |
| Current notification | 1 April 2026–31 March 2031 |
| Target notifier | Union government, in consultation with RBI |
| Policy-rate decision-maker | Monetary Policy Committee |
| Wider objective | Price stability while keeping growth in mind |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: “4 inside 2–6”**

### Why is it called “flexible”?

```text
Strict inflation targeting
Inflation deviation → immediate mechanical tightening

Flexible inflation targeting
Inflation deviation
       ↓
Check source, persistence, expectations and growth
       ↓
Return inflation towards target over a suitable horizon
```

It is flexible because the MPC considers:

- whether inflation is temporary or persistent;
- whether it comes from supply or demand;
- whether it is becoming broad-based;
- the condition of growth and employment;
- the lag with which monetary policy operates.

It does **not** mean that the 4% target is optional.

---

## 2. Target, tolerance band and forecast

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **4% target** | The medium-term inflation anchor |
| **2–6% band** | Space for temporary shocks and forecasting uncertainty |
| **Inflation forecast** | MPC’s expected future path, not a new target |
| **Observed inflation** | Actual CPI reading, which may temporarily differ from 4% |

### Critical distinction

```text
Inflation at 5%
      │
      ├── Above the 4% target
      ├── Still within the 2–6% tolerance band
      └── Does not automatically require a rate increase
```

The MPC must examine whether inflation is expected to return towards 4%, rather than responding mechanically to every monthly deviation.

---

## 3. Monetary Policy Committee

### Composition: six members

```text
                       MPC
                        │
       ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
       │                                 │
  Three RBI members               Three external members
       │                                 │
  • RBI Governor                  • Appointed by Union government
  • Deputy Governor               • Selected through statutory process
  • One RBI officer               • Four-year terms
```

| Rule | Position |
|---|---|
| Chairperson | RBI Governor |
| Total membership | Six |
| RBI members | Three |
| External members | Three |
| Voting | One vote per member |
| Decision | Majority vote |
| Tie | Governor has casting vote |
| Statutory minimum | At least four meetings each year |
| Present practice | Normally six bi-monthly meetings |
| Quorum | Four members |

> The Governor does not possess a veto. The casting vote operates only when votes are equally divided.

---

## 4. What does the MPC decide?

### MPC decides

- the policy repo rate;
- the monetary-policy stance;
- the resolution and policy rationale.

### RBI separately performs

- liquidity operations;
- banking regulation and supervision;
- currency management;
- forex-reserve management;
- payment-system oversight;
- government banking and debt management.

> **Prelims trap:** MPC is not the entire RBI and does not perform all central-banking functions.

---

## 5. Inflation-target failure

RBI is deemed to have failed to meet the target when average CPI inflation is:

```text
Above 6% for three consecutive quarters
                    OR
Below 2% for three consecutive quarters
```

It is **not** triggered by:

- one monthly CPI reading above 6%;
- three months of inflation above 6%;
- WPI inflation leaving the band;
- core inflation exceeding 4%;
- inflation merely differing from RBI’s forecast.

Following failure, RBI must send the Union government a report explaining:

1. the reasons for failure;
2. proposed remedial actions;
3. the estimated period for returning inflation to target.

---

## 6. Policy stance versus policy action

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate action | Repo increased, reduced or retained |
| Policy stance | Broad orientation of future policy |
| Neutral stance | Flexibility to move in either direction |
| Liquidity condition | Surplus or deficit funds in the banking system |

Therefore:

```text
Repo unchanged ≠ neutral stance necessarily
Neutral stance ≠ neutral banking liquidity
Rate cut       ≠ immediate rise in bank credit
```

## 1. Can RBI replace CPI with WPI?

**No—not unilaterally.**

Under **Section 45ZA of the RBI Act**, the inflation target is expressed through **CPI Combined** and notified by the Union government in consultation with RBI.

```text
RBI can analyse WPI
        ≠
RBI can make WPI the legal inflation target
```

Replacing CPI with WPI would require changing the statutory framework through Parliament, followed by an appropriate government notification.

CPI is preferred because monetary policy ultimately targets the cost of living faced by households; WPI largely measures wholesale goods prices and excludes services.

---

## 2. How can six members produce a tie?

The Governor is one of the six members and first exercises an **ordinary vote**.

```text
Three vote for a rate cut
Three vote against a rate cut
             ↓
          Tie: 3–3
             ↓
Governor exercises a second, casting vote
             ↓
Governor’s preferred side prevails
```

Thus, the Governor effectively votes twice **only when the first vote is tied**.

With five members present and voting, an ordinary tie cannot occur. With four or six voting members, a tie is possible.

---

## 3. What if a member is absent?

| Situation | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Six members present | Normal meeting |
| Five members present | Meeting can proceed |
| Four members present | Meeting can proceed |
| Fewer than four | No quorum; meeting cannot proceed |
| Governor absent | MPC Deputy Governor must be present and presides |
| Member on leave | No vote for that meeting |
| Proxy requested | **Not permitted** |

Section **45ZI** requires a quorum of four, including:

- the Governor; or
- when the Governor is absent, the MPC’s Deputy Governor.

Only members **present and voting** participate. Another member cannot cast an absentee’s vote.

The Act expressly grants the casting vote to the **Governor**, not to the Deputy Governor merely because the latter is presiding.

---

## 4. Can an MPC member be removed?

Yes. The Union government can remove a government-appointed external member under **Section 45ZE** for specified reasons, including:

- insolvency;
- physical or mental incapacity;
- conviction involving moral turpitude;
- undisclosed material conflict of interest;
- acquisition of a prejudicial financial or other interest;
- absence from three consecutive meetings without leave;
- abuse of position detrimental to public interest.

A reasonable opportunity to be heard applies to specified removal grounds.

The three RBI members are **ex officio members**. Their MPC membership depends upon holding the relevant RBI office or nomination rather than the four-year external-member tenure.

> **6 members → quorum 4 → no proxy → Governor’s second vote only on a tie.**

## 🖼️ The liquidity corridor

```text
                Liquidity shortage
                       ▲
       MSF 5.50% ──────┤ Ceiling: RBI lends overnight
                       │
      Repo 5.25% ──────┤ Policy signal / corridor centre
                       │
       SDF 5.00% ──────┤ Floor: RBI absorbs overnight funds
                       ▼
                 Liquidity surplus
```

*RBI manages liquidity so the overnight market rate remains near the repo rate and within the corridor.*

## 1. Four different rates

| Rate | Direction of funds | Main purpose | Collateral |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Repo** | RBI → banking system | Inject liquidity at policy rate | Government securities |
| **SDF** | Banking system → RBI | Absorb surplus liquidity | Not required |
| **MSF** | RBI → eligible banks | Emergency overnight borrowing | Government securities |
| **WACR** | Bank ↔ bank market | Actual overnight market rate | Unsecured |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **SDF sinks; MSF supplies.**

---

## 2. Repo rate: the policy signal

A repo is a collateralised repurchase transaction:

```text
Today:
Bank gives securities ──→ RBI
RBI gives cash       ──→ Bank

Later:
Bank repays cash + interest
RBI returns securities
```

The repo rate signals the intended monetary-policy position, but it is not the rate charged on every loan in the economy.

```text
MPC changes repo
       ↓
RBI manages overnight liquidity
       ↓
Money-market rates respond
       ↓
Deposit and lending rates may respond
       ↓
Consumption, investment and inflation change
```

---

## 3. Standing Deposit Facility: corridor floor

The **SDF** allows eligible entities to park surplus overnight funds with RBI.

Its distinctive feature is that RBI absorbs money **without providing collateral**.

```text
Banks have excess cash
         ↓
Very low interbank rates possible
         ↓
Banks may deposit with RBI at SDF
         ↓
Downward movement of rates is restrained
```

SDF became the corridor floor in **April 2022**, replacing the fixed-rate reverse repo in that role.

### SDF versus reverse repo

| SDF | Reverse repo |
|---|---|
| No collateral is required | RBI supplies securities as collateral |
| Standing overnight facility | Can be fixed or auction-based operation |
| Current corridor floor | No longer the operative corridor floor |
| Absorbs liquidity | Also absorbs liquidity |

> **Prelims trap:** SDF and reverse repo both absorb liquidity, but they are not identical.

---

## 4. Marginal Standing Facility: corridor ceiling

Under MSF, eligible banks can obtain overnight liquidity from RBI at a rate above repo.

```text
Bank faces urgent overnight shortage
              ↓
Ordinary market funding is costly or unavailable
              ↓
Bank approaches RBI under MSF
              ↓
MSF rate limits extreme upward pressure on WACR
```

Banks may access MSF by using eligible government securities, including permitted use of securities maintained for SLR purposes.

### MSF versus lender of last resort

| MSF | Lender-of-last-resort intervention |
|---|---|
| Routine standing facility | Exceptional crisis support |
| Pre-announced operational mechanism | May involve special arrangements |
| Addresses overnight liquidity | Addresses severe systemic stress |
| Available under prescribed conditions | Based on crisis-specific assessment |

---

## 5. WACR: the operating target

**WACR** means the weighted average call rate—the average interest rate on unsecured overnight loans in the call-money market, weighted by transaction value.

```text
Final objective       → Inflation near target
Policy rate           → Repo rate
Operating target      → WACR
Operational mechanism → Liquidity adjustment
```

RBI does not administratively fix each WACR transaction. It influences WACR by adjusting the availability and price of liquidity.

### Liquidity conditions and WACR

| Banking-system condition | Likely pressure on WACR |
|---|---|
| Large liquidity surplus | Towards the SDF floor |
| Balanced liquidity | Near the repo rate |
| Significant liquidity deficit | Towards the MSF ceiling |
| Severe market stress | Potentially near the ceiling |

---

## 6. Why create a corridor?

Without standing facilities, temporary payment flows could make overnight rates highly volatile.

```text
SDF provides a deposit option
             ↓
       Effective floor
             ↓
 Repo provides policy anchor
             ↓
      MSF provides funding
             ↓
       Effective ceiling
```

The corridor therefore:

- limits excessive overnight-rate volatility;
- communicates RBI’s policy-rate signal;
- supports orderly payment settlement;
- anchors short-term money-market rates.

---

## 7. Bank Rate is not MSF

The **Bank Rate** and MSF rate are currently aligned numerically, but they remain distinct concepts.

| MSF rate | Bank Rate |
|---|---|
| Rate on overnight MSF borrowing | Statutory reference rate under RBI Act |
| Part of liquidity corridor | Used for specified penal and regulatory purposes |
| Involves an actual liquidity facility | Need not represent a current transaction |

> **Exam rule:** Equal numerical values do not make two instruments legally identical.

## 1. What are overnight funds?

They are funds borrowed today and repaid on the **next working day**.

Usually, this does not involve trucks carrying physical cash. It means transferring electronic reserve balances maintained at RBI.

```text
Customers of Bank A transfer ₹1,000 crore to Bank B
                         ↓
Bank A’s RBI balance falls by ₹1,000 crore
Bank B’s RBI balance rises by ₹1,000 crore
                         ↓
Bank A may face a temporary settlement shortage
                         ↓
It borrows overnight from another bank or RBI
```

A bank may need overnight money because of:

- unexpectedly large customer transfers or withdrawals;
- tax payments moving money into government accounts;
- loan disbursements;
- temporary CRR or settlement-balance shortages;
- uneven distribution of liquidity across banks.

## 2. Why deposit surplus money with RBI?

Banks maintain funds with RBI for different reasons:

| Facility or balance | Compulsory? | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| CRR balance | Yes | Regulatory reserve requirement |
| Settlement balance | Operationally necessary | Completing interbank payments |
| SDF deposit | Voluntary | Earning interest on overnight surplus |
| Excess reserve | Voluntary | Payment safety or precaution |

A surplus bank may use SDF because leaving funds idle earns nothing, while lending them to another bank might offer a rate lower than SDF or involve counterparty risk.

```text
Bank with surplus                     Bank with shortage
       │                                      │
Lend in call market                    Borrow in call market
       OR                                     OR
Deposit under SDF                      Borrow through repo/MSF
```

Therefore, the system can have an aggregate surplus while particular banks still face shortages.

## 3. What is the Bank Rate?

The Bank Rate is the statutory rate at which RBI is prepared to buy or rediscount eligible bills and commercial paper.

Today, it mainly functions as:

- a statutory reference rate;
- a rate linked to certain penalties;
- a broader central-bank signalling rate.

| Repo rate | Bank Rate |
|---|---|
| Based on a repurchase transaction | Traditionally based on lending or rediscounting |
| Government securities are temporarily exchanged | No repurchase agreement is essential to its definition |
| Central policy and liquidity rate | Mainly a statutory and penal reference today |
| Currently 5.25% | Currently 5.50% |

The Bank Rate is currently aligned numerically with MSF, but:

```text
Bank Rate = MSF rate numerically
Bank Rate ≠ MSF institutionally
```

MSF is an actual overnight borrowing facility; Bank Rate has a separate statutory identity.

## 4. Fractional-reserve banking

The correct term is **fractional-reserve banking**, not fractional lending.

```text
Bank receives deposits
        │
        ├── Maintains required reserves and liquid assets
        │
        └── Uses its balance-sheet capacity for loans and investments
```

### Indian reserve requirements

| Requirement | What is maintained? | Where? |
|---|---|---|
| CRR | Cash balance | With RBI |
| SLR | Cash, gold or approved securities | Maintained by the bank |
| Capital requirement | Shareholder capital against risk | Bank’s balance sheet |

Your basic intuition is correct: banks do not retain every deposited rupee as cash. But the modern process is more nuanced:

```text
Bank approves a ₹10 lakh loan
           ↓
Creates a ₹10 lakh deposit for borrower
           ↓
Loan becomes bank’s asset
Deposit becomes bank’s liability
           ↓
Bank needs reserves when that deposit is transferred
```

Banks are constrained not merely by cash reserves, but also by:

- capital adequacy;
- liquidity requirements;
- availability of creditworthy borrowers;
- asset quality and provisioning;
- funding costs;
- RBI policy and regulation.

> 🔑 **Correction:** Banks do not simply lend the exact currency deposited by customers. Lending creates deposits, while reserves are needed principally for regulation and settlement.

## Capital is calculated differently

Suppose a bank has ₹100 of **risk-weighted assets**:

```text
Minimum CET1 capital             ₹5.50
Minimum total Tier 1 capital     ₹7.00
Minimum total capital            ₹9.00
Capital Conservation Buffer      ₹2.50
Effective total requirement     ₹11.50
```

These are **nested requirements**, not amounts that should all be added together.

The additional 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer must be composed of CET1 capital. Consequently:

- CET1 including buffer: **8%**
- total capital including buffer: **11.5%**

Additional requirements may apply to systemically important banks or under specialised supervisory rules.

## How are they decided?

- **CRR:** RBI changes it according to liquidity, monetary and financial-stability conditions.
- **SLR:** RBI prescribes it under the Banking Regulation Act to ensure a stock of liquid assets.
- **Capital ratios:** RBI adapts international Basel standards to Indian banking conditions.
- **MPC:** It determines the policy repo rate—not these three ratios.

> 🔑 **Core distinction:**
> CRR and SLR help a bank meet **liquidity obligations**; capital protects creditors when the bank suffers **losses**.

## 1. Reserve money or M0

Reserve money is also called:

| Component | Example |
|---|---|
| Currency in circulation | Notes and coins held by public and banks |
| Bankers’ deposits | CRR and other reserve balances with RBI |
| Other RBI deposits | Deposits of specified institutions with RBI |

## 2. Broad money or M3

```text
M1 =
Currency with public
+ Demand deposits with banks
+ Other deposits with RBI

M3 =
M1
+ Time deposits with banks
```

M3 is much larger than reserve money because commercial-bank lending creates deposits.

## 3. How a bank creates deposit money

Suppose a bank approves a ₹100 loan:

| Bank assets | Bank liabilities |
|---|---|
| Loan: +₹100 | Borrower’s deposit: +₹100 |

```text
Bank approves loan
        ↓
Credits borrower’s account
        ↓
New deposit is created
        ↓
Borrower transfers the deposit
        ↓
Reserves move between banks
```

The bank does not necessarily take ₹100 from another customer’s deposit and physically hand it to the borrower.

However, unlimited money creation is impossible because banks face:

- CRR and liquidity requirements;
- capital requirements;
- settlement needs;
- funding costs;
- credit risk;
- loan demand;
- RBI regulation.

## 4. Money multiplier

The observed money multiplier is:

\[
\text{Money multiplier}=\frac{M3}{\text{Reserve money}}
\]

If:

- M3 = ₹240 lakh crore;
- reserve money = ₹60 lakh crore;

then:

\[
\text{Multiplier}=\frac{240}{60}=4
\]

This means ₹1 of monetary base supports approximately ₹4 of broad money. It does **not** mean every ₹1 was mechanically lent four times.

---

## 5. Textbook versus real multiplier

### Simplified textbook model

\[
m=\frac{1}{r}
\]

If the required reserve ratio is 10%:

\[
m=\frac{1}{0.10}=10
\]

This result assumes:

- the public holds no additional currency;
- banks maintain no excess reserves;
- every available amount is lent;
- every loan returns to the banking system;
- borrowers are willing and creditworthy.

These assumptions rarely hold completely.

### More realistic intuition

```text
Money multiplier rises when:
• required reserve ratio falls
• public holds less currency
• banks retain fewer excess reserves
• banks are willing and able to lend

Money multiplier falls when:
• required reserve ratio rises
• public withdraws more currency
• banks accumulate precautionary reserves
• weak demand or credit risk restrains lending
```

A simplified expanded expression is:

\[
m=\frac{1+c}{c+r+e}
\]

where:

- \(c\) = currency-to-deposit ratio;
- \(r\) = required-reserve ratio;
- \(e\) = excess-reserve ratio.

```text
CRR increased
     ↓
More bank funds required with RBI
     ↓
Smaller immediate lending/liquidity capacity
     ↓
```

```text
CRR reduced
     ↓
Required RBI balances decline
     ↓
Banking liquidity is released
     ↓

```

A CRR reduction does **not guarantee** more lending. Banks may retain liquidity if:

## 🖼️ Transmission chain

```text
MPC changes repo rate
          ↓
RBI aligns WACR with repo
          ↓
Money-market and bond yields change
          ↓
Bank funding and deposit costs change
          ↓
Loan rates and credit availability change
          ↓
Consumption, investment and exchange rate respond
          ↓
Aggregate demand changes
          ↓
Output and inflation respond with a lag
```

*Changing repo is the beginning of monetary transmission—not its final outcome.*

## 1. Expansionary transmission

```text
Repo rate ↓
     ↓
Short-term market rates ↓
     ↓
Deposit and lending rates may decline
     ↓
Borrowing becomes cheaper
     ↓
Consumption and investment may rise
     ↓
Aggregate demand and output rise
     ↓
Inflationary pressure may increase later
```

Every arrow is conditional. A repo cut does not force banks to lend or borrowers to borrow.

## 2. Contractionary transmission

```text
Repo rate ↑
     ↓
Market and bank rates rise
     ↓
Credit demand and interest-sensitive spending weaken
     ↓
Aggregate demand moderates
     ↓
Inflationary pressure declines with a lag
```

Higher rates can control demand-driven inflation more effectively than a temporary shortage of food or fuel.

---

## 3. Five transmission channels

| Channel | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Interest-rate channel | Policy rate changes borrowing and saving incentives |
| Bank-credit channel | Funding and balance-sheet conditions affect loan supply |
| Asset-price channel | Bond, equity and property values affect wealth and financing |
| Exchange-rate channel | Interest differentials affect capital flows and rupee conditions |
| Expectations channel | Credible policy shapes future inflation and wage-price behaviour |

### Exchange-rate channel

```text
Domestic rates ↓
       ↓
Rupee assets may become relatively less attractive
       ↓
Possible capital outflow / rupee depreciation
       ↓
Exports become more competitive
but imported inflation may rise
```

This is a likely direction, not an automatic result.

---

## 4. First leg versus final leg

| Transmission stage | Typical speed |
|---|---|
| Repo → WACR | Relatively rapid |
| WACR → money-market rates | Usually rapid |
| Market rates → new bank loans | Moderate |
| New rates → outstanding loans | Slower |
| Credit → spending and investment | Slower |
| Spending → inflation and output | Several quarters |

RBI can closely influence the first leg by managing liquidity. It cannot directly control every later behavioural response.

## 5. EBLR and MCLR

### External Benchmark-based Lending Rate

An EBLR-linked loan is priced as:

\[
\text{Loan rate}=\text{external benchmark}+\text{bank spread}
\]

Permitted external benchmarks include:

- RBI policy repo rate;
- specified Treasury-bill yields;
- other approved market benchmarks.

```text
Repo changes
      ↓
External benchmark changes
      ↓
Loan rate resets relatively quickly
```

EBLR has applied mandatorily to specified new floating-rate retail and MSE loans since October 2019.

### MCLR

MCLR is an internal bank benchmark based on:

- marginal funding cost;
- negative carry from CRR;
- operating costs;
- tenor premium.

```text
Repo changes
      ↓
Bank deposit and funding costs adjust
      ↓
MCLR changes
      ↓
Loan rate changes on reset date
```

| EBLR | MCLR |
|---|---|
| External benchmark | Internal bank benchmark |
| Faster policy-rate linkage | Depends on bank funding cost |
| Generally quicker resetting | Often slower transmission |
| Spread remains relevant | Funding composition remains relevant |

## 7. Supply shocks and monetary policy

Suppose food inflation rises because rainfall damages crops:

```text
Crop loss → food supply ↓ → food prices ↑
```

A repo-rate increase cannot produce additional crops.

But RBI may still react if:

```text
Food prices ↑
     ↓
Households expect persistent inflation
     ↓
Wages and prices across sectors rise
     ↓
Inflation becomes generalised
```

Therefore, monetary policy focuses on preventing **second-round effects**, even when it cannot remove the original supply shock.

## 4. What is sterilisation?

Sterilisation means offsetting the domestic-liquidity effect of a forex intervention.

### RBI buys dollars

```text
RBI buys dollars
       ↓
Rupee liquidity increases
       ↓
Possible unwanted monetary expansion
       ↓
RBI sells government securities / conducts VRRR
       ↓
Excess rupee liquidity is absorbed
```

### RBI sells dollars

```text
RBI sells dollars
       ↓
Rupee liquidity decreases
       ↓
Possible unwanted monetary contraction
       ↓
RBI purchases securities / conducts VRR
       ↓
Rupee liquidity is restored
```

| Forex intervention | Initial liquidity effect | Sterilising operation |
|---|---|---|
| RBI buys dollars | Injection | OMO sale or absorption operation |
| RBI sells dollars | Absorption | OMO purchase or injection operation |

Sterilisation separates two objectives:

```text
Forex intervention → influence exchange-market disorder
Liquidity operation → preserve domestic monetary conditions
```

| Domestic liquidity effect remains | Liquidity effect is offset |
| Monetary base changes | Monetary base broadly restored |

| Simpler operation | Requires additional instruments |

Sterilisation need not offset the initial liquidity effect perfectly or immediately.

```text
Special Drawing Rights
        +
Reserve position in IMF
```

### Important traps

- Foreign Currency Assets are not held entirely in US dollars.
- A change in reserves does not necessarily equal RBI intervention.

- Reserves are assets of RBI, not government revenue available for ordinary spending.

## 7. Why hold reserves?

| Purpose | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Volatility management | Supply foreign currency during disorderly conditions |
| Import cover | Finance essential imports during external stress |
| External-debt confidence | Support repayment capacity and investor confidence |
| Crisis buffer | Address sudden capital-flow reversals |
| International payments | Meet sovereign foreign-currency requirements |

Large reserves improve resilience but also involve:

- valuation risk;
- opportunity cost;
- sterilisation cost;
- returns generally lower than some alternative investments.

## 8. Forward-market intervention

RBI may also intervene through forwards:

```text
RBI agrees today
to buy or sell dollars
on a future date
```

Unlike a spot transaction, the full rupee-liquidity effect may arise later when the contract settles.

Therefore:

- spot intervention affects current liquidity directly;
- forward intervention creates future commitments;
- forward positions must be considered alongside headline reserves.

## 9. Monetary-policy trilemma

A country cannot simultaneously maintain all three without limits:

```text
             Fixed exchange rate
                    /\
                   /  \
                  /    \
                 /      \
Free capital mobility──Independent monetary policy
```

India preserves room for monetary-policy independence through:

- a managed rather than fixed exchange rate;
- incomplete capital-account convertibility;
- discretionary intervention and sterilisation.

### MCQ 1

RBI purchases US dollars and wants to neutralise the resulting increase in domestic rupee liquidity. Which operation is appropriate?

**A.** Purchase government securities outright and credit additional reserves to commercial banks.
**B.** Reduce CRR so that banks can deploy a larger proportion of their deposit liabilities.

## 🖼️ RBI’s simplified balance sheet

```text
             RBI ASSETS                        RBI LIABILITIES
┌────────────────────────────┐     ┌────────────────────────────┐
│ Foreign securities        │     │ Currency notes issued      │
│ Gold and foreign deposits │     │ Bank reserve deposits      │
│ Government securities     │  =  │ Government deposits        │
│ Loans to financial system │     │ Revaluation/risk accounts  │
│ Other assets              │     │ Capital and reserves       │
└────────────────────────────┘     └────────────────────────────┘
```

*RBI creates monetary liabilities—currency and bank reserves—to acquire financial assets.*

## 1. Why are currency notes an RBI liability?

When RBI issues currency, it places a monetary claim into circulation.

```text
RBI acquires an asset
        ↓
Issues currency or creates bank reserves
        ↓
RBI assets and liabilities both expand
```

Modern currency is not ordinarily redeemable for gold, but notes issued remain an accounting liability of RBI.

### Important exception

The ₹1 note is issued by the Government of India and is therefore a government liability rather than an RBI note liability.

## 2. RBI’s major assets

| Asset | Why RBI holds it |
|---|---|
| Foreign securities and deposits | Reserve management and external stability |
| Gold | Reserve diversification and confidence |
| Government securities | Monetary operations and income |
| Repo loans and advances | Liquidity support to financial system |
| Other domestic assets | Operational and statutory functions |

When RBI purchases a government security:

```text
RBI government-security asset ↑
Bank reserve liability ↑
Reserve money ↑
```

## 3. RBI’s principal income

| Income source | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Foreign-asset income | Interest on foreign securities and deposits |
| Domestic-security income | Interest on government securities |
| Repo and lending income | Interest on liquidity supplied |
| Forex transaction gains | Realised gains from currency transactions |
| Fees and commissions | Government banking and other services |
| Seigniorage | Benefit from issuing non-interest-bearing currency liabilities |

### Seigniorage

The simplified idea is:

```text
RBI issues low-cost, non-interest-bearing currency
                       ↓
Acquires interest-earning assets
                       ↓
Earns income on those assets
```

Seigniorage should not be treated merely as the entire face value of currency minus printing cost.

## 4. RBI’s expenditure

RBI expenses include:

- currency printing and distribution;
- employee and administrative costs;
- interest paid on SDF and absorption operations;
- agency commissions paid to banks;
- technology and currency-management expenditure;
- provisions against financial risks.

## 5. Realised income versus revaluation gains

Suppose the rupee depreciates and the rupee value of RBI’s foreign assets rises.

```text
Foreign asset’s rupee value ↑
              ↓
Accounting revaluation gain
              ↓
Not necessarily cash income available for transfer
```

| Realised income | Revaluation balance |
|---|---|
| Interest actually earned | Change caused by market prices or exchange rates |
| Transaction gain realised | Gain may reverse later |
| Can contribute to surplus | Normally retained in revaluation accounts |
| More readily distributable | Protects RBI against valuation losses |

> **Prelims trap:** Every increase in RBI’s balance sheet does not create distributable profit.

## 6. Surplus transfer to government

Under **Section 47 of the RBI Act**:

```text
RBI income
   − operating expenditure
   − statutory allocations
   − risk provisions
   = transferable surplus
```

The surplus is transferred to the **Central Government**.

It is not determined simply by government expenditure requirements.

### Economic Capital Framework

The framework determines:

- the financial risks RBI must provide for;
- the size of risk buffers;
- the amount of surplus that may safely be transferred.

The **Bimal Jalan Committee** is associated with RBI’s Economic Capital Framework.

### Current position

For 2025–26:

| Indicator | Official figure |
|---|---:|
| Surplus transferred | **₹2,86,588.46 crore** |
| Contingent Risk Buffer | **6.5% of balance sheet** |
| RBI balance-sheet size | Approximately **₹91.97 lakh crore** |

✅ The transfer occurred after strengthening the risk provision; the entire gross income was not transferred.

## 7. RBI and payment systems

```text
 ├── Oversees safety and settlement
```

| RBI | NPCI |
|---|---|
| Statutory regulator | Payment-system operator |

## 8. CBDC versus UPI

| CBDC or e₹ | UPI |
|---|---|

```text
Bank deposit moves from one account to another

CBDC payment:
RBI-issued digital currency changes holder
```

This does not mean RBI owns all payment data or operates every payment application.

## 3. Legal-tender trap

| Instrument | Money? | Legal tender? |
|---|---:|---:|

## 4. Expansionary-policy trap

### Expansionary actions

- repo-rate reduction;
- CRR reduction;
- OMO purchase;
- VRR liquidity injection;
- reduction in restrictive reserve conditions.

### Contractionary actions

- repo-rate increase;
- CRR increase;
- OMO sale;
- VRRR absorption;
- tighter liquidity conditions.

But:

```text
Expansionary instrument
          ≠
Guaranteed expansion in loans or output
```

Transmission may fail because of weak demand, stressed banks, high risk or poor expectations.

## 5. Counterparty trap

Not every financial institution can directly access every RBI facility.

| Entity | Broad position |
|---|---|
| Scheduled commercial banks | Access subject to facility rules |
| Primary dealers | Access to specified market facilities |
| NBFCs | No universal direct LAF access |
| Households | No direct repo or MSF access |
| State governments | Not ordinary monetary-policy counterparties |

Therefore, “RBI lends to banks at repo” is a simplification; actual eligibility is facility-specific.
