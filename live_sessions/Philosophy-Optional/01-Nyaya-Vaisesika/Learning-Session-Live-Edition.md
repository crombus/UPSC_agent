# MUST-NEEDED / CORE LEARNING SESSION

## Lesson 1 — Two allied schools and one liberation-directed architecture

### Visual first: the system as a chain

```text
VAIŚEṢIKA: WHAT EXISTS                    NYĀYA: HOW IT IS KNOWN
seven ontological categories             sixteen inquiry/debate topics
              \                           /
               \                         /
                REALIST WORLD + VALID COGNITION
                              │
                       error is removed
                              │
                  defects → action → rebirth end
                              │
                         liberation
```

**Simple start.** Nyāya is chiefly the discipline of valid cognition, proof and debate associated
with Gautama. Vaiśeṣika is chiefly the ontology and atomism associated with Kaṇāda. They are allied
systems, not historically identical schools. Their later convergence permits a joint
Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika account, while answers must still identify the original division of labour.

| Nyāya’s sixteen topics of inquiry | Function |
|---|---|
| valid means, objects, doubt, purpose, example, doctrine | begin and orient inquiry |
| demonstration-members, hypothetical reasoning, ascertainment | establish a conclusion |
| truth-debate, wrangling, cavil | distinguish inquiry from victory-seeking |
| fallacious reason, quibble, futile rejoinder, defeat-ground | diagnose dialectical failure |

The twelve objects of valid knowledge are self, body, senses, objects, cognition, mind, activity,
defects, rebirth, fruit, suffering and release. This is a soteriological route, not Vaiśeṣika’s
complete ontology. Do not confuse debate-fault **jāti** with the ontological universal also called
**jāti/sāmānya**.

**Historical qualification.** Classical Nyāya accepts perception, inference, comparison and
testimony. Classical Vaiśeṣika accepts perception and inference and reduces the other two; the
later synthesis commonly uses Nyāya’s four. Developed Nyāya and later Vaiśeṣika are theistic, but
Kaṇāda’s foundational stratum does not unambiguously foreground a creator God.

**Argument.** If false cognition sustains defect and action, then disciplined knowledge can remove
the causal conditions of suffering. Logic is therefore practical and soteriological, not a
detached formal game.

**Presupposition.** Reality has stable, knowable structure and cognition can successfully reach it.

**Objection.** Combining two schools conceals historical differences.

**Reply.** Treat the synthesis as late and systematic, while assigning epistemology primarily to
Nyāya and categories/atomism primarily to Vaiśeṣika.

**Residual.** Later synthesis does not erase disagreements among historical authors.

**Exam use.** In any broad answer, open with “historically allied, later fused; Nyāya supplies
pramāṇa and dialectic, Vaiśeṣika supplies padārtha and atomism.”

## Lesson 2 — The positive categories: substances, qualities, motions, universals and particulars

### Visual first: the seven ontological categories

```text
PADĀRTHA: whatever is nameable and knowable
├─ independent base: substance (dravya)
├─ dependent features: quality (guṇa), motion (karma)
├─ repeatable identity: universal (sāmānya)
├─ irreducible numerical difference: particularity (viśeṣa)
├─ inseparable tie: inherence (samavāya)
└─ structured non-being: absence (abhāva)
```

### Substance, quality and motion

The nine substances are earth, water, fire, air, ether, time, direction/space, self and atomic
mind. A substance is a locus of qualities and motions. The twenty-four traditional qualities
include colour, taste, smell, touch, number, magnitude, distinctness, conjunction, disjunction,
priority, posteriority, cognition, pleasure, pain, desire, aversion, effort, heaviness, fluidity,
viscidity, disposition, merit, demerit and sound. Consciousness is therefore a quality of self,
not its essence.

Motion here is physical **karma**, not moral karma: upward movement, downward movement,
contraction, expansion and locomotion. Motion produces conjunction and disjunction.

### Universal (sāmānya)

```text
many cows ── determinate recognition ──> “cow”
     \________ one real cow-ness ________/
```

A universal is one, eternal and present in many particulars through inherence. Higher and lower
universals ground classification and predication. Nyāya argues that resemblance or exclusion
cannot explain stable common predication unless some objective common structure exists.

Six checks prevent indiscriminate universals:

| Obstructor of universalhood | Why the proposed universal fails |
|---|---|
| one instance | a universal needs plural loci; ether-ness is blocked if ether is one |
| identical extension | two co-extensive universals are redundant |
| cross-over | partially overlapping classes disrupt nested/disjoint ordering |
| regress | universalhood cannot require a further universal without end |
| loss of function | a universal in ultimate particularities would erase individuation |
| lack of relation | a universal of inherence would need another inherence relation |

Universals are held to be perceived in determinate cognition—“this is a cow”—and, more
controversially, through universal-mediated extraordinary perception. The Buddhist exclusion
theory (**apoha**) objects that universals are causally inert abstractions. Nyāya replies that
exclusion presupposes stable exclusion-classes and that the universal is not spatially divided.
The residual issue is whether realism explains predication or reifies it.

### Ultimate particularity (viśeṣa)

Ultimate particularity differentiates eternal atoms, selves, minds, ether, time and direction
when shared universals and qualities cannot. It is self-differentiating (**svato-vyāvartaka**).

**Objection:** what differentiates two particularities?
**Reply:** each differentiates by its own nature, ending regress.
**Residual:** “self-differentiating” may name an explanatory stopping point rather than prove it.

**Example.** Two eternal earth-atoms could share every class-quality yet remain numerically two;
viśeṣa supplies the “thisness.”

**Exam use.** A critical answer must separate the logical function—preserving numerical inference—
from the metaphysical status—an imperceptible primitive inferred to perform that function.

## Lesson 3 — Inherence and absence: relation and negation made real

### Visual first: two relations that must never be confused

```text
BROWN COLOUR ──inheres in──> TABLE       BOOK ──rests on──> TABLE
 quality cannot stand alone              two substances remain separable
        SAMAVĀYA                                 SAṂYOGA
 eternal inseparable tie                 produced/destructible conjunction
```

### Inherence (samavāya)

Inherence is one, eternal and inferred: the inseparable relation between whole and parts,
substance and quality, substance and motion, universal and instance, and eternal substance and
its ultimate particularity. **Ayutasiddhatva**—ontological inseparability—is necessary but not
sufficient. A glued book and table may be physically difficult to separate while remaining two
independent substances. The relata’s category-types and asymmetric dependence must be shown.

**Relation-regress objection.** If inherence links A and B, another relation seems needed to link
inherence to them.
**Reply.** Inherence is self-linking (**svarūpa-sambandha**), not linked by another inherence.
**Residual.** The system stops regress by a primitive exactly where critics demand explanation.

**One-or-many objection.** One inherence operating in every pair seems indiscriminate.
**Reply.** Its operation is fixed by the obtaining relata, not by a free-floating glue.

### Absence (abhāva)

| Kind | Temporal/locus structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| prior absence (prāgabhāva) | beginningless, ends with production | pot before the pot is made |
| posterior absence (pradhvaṃsābhāva) | begins at destruction, has no end | pot after destruction |
| enduring locus-relative absence (atyantābhāva) | counterpositive absent in this locus at all times | intrinsic heat in air |
| mutual absence (anyonyābhāva) | reciprocal non-identity | air is not fire |

Later Vaiśeṣika admits absence as a seventh category because true negative cognition has a
structured object. Every absence has a locus (**anuyogin**) and a counterpositive (**pratiyogin**).
“Absolute negation is impossible” rejects unrestricted, subjectless blank negation; it does not
reject locus-relative atyantābhāva.

The counterpositive is the **conceptual target of denial**, not necessarily an existing positive
thing. “There is no hare’s horn on the table” identifies *hare’s horn* as what is denied and the
table as the relevant locus; understanding that target does not commit Vaiśeṣika to an instantiated
hare’s horn. Thus a determinate absence needs specifiable content and a locus, but it does not
require that the counterpositive presently—or ever—exist. This blocks the mistaken inference from
“every absence has a counterpositive” to “every counterpositive is a real positive object.”

### How “there is no jar on the table” is known

```text
visible table + competent observer + no obstruction
                 │
          jar would appear if present
          /                         \
NYĀYA: real jar-absence             BUDDHIST RECONSTRUCTION:
qualifies perceived table           warranted non-apprehension/
                                    exclusion; no negative entity
```

Nyāya uses qualifier–qualified perception or, where appropriate, inference; it does not need
Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā’s independent non-cognition pramāṇa. Both accounts require visibility conditions.
Nyāya presses the object-of-true-negation problem; the Buddhist denies that truth requires an
extra negative entity.

**Application trap.** “Air lacks heat” concerns absence of heat as air’s intrinsic quality;
heated air remains possible. “Air is not fire” is mutual difference.

## Lesson 4 — Perception: contact, determinacy and extraordinary reach

### Visual first: Gautama’s definition unpacked

```text
sense–object relation (sannikarṣa)
          ↓ produces (utpanna)
knowledge not caused by words (avyapadeśya)
          ↓
non-errant (avyabhicārin)
          ↓
determinate / non-doubtful (vyavasāyātmaka)
          = valid perception
```

The non-verbal clause excludes cognition caused by testimony; it does not make later verbal
expression impossible. The determinacy clause excludes doubt, although later Nyāya also admits a
prior pre-predicative, indeterminate phase (**nirvikalpaka**) before determinate
(**savikalpaka**) awareness.

### Six ordinary sense relations

| Relation | What becomes perceptible |
|---|---|
| conjunction | the contacted substance |
| inherence in the conjoined | its quality, such as colour |
| inherence in what inheres in the conjoined | a universal of that quality |
| inherence | sound through the auditory organ |
| inherence in what inheres | sound-universal |
| qualifier–qualified relation | an absence qualifying its perceived locus |

### Three extraordinary modes

1. **Universal-mediated perception (sāmānyalakṣaṇa):** cognition through a universal, used in
   classification and the defence of induction.
2. **Cognition-mediated perception (jñānalakṣaṇa):** prior cognition mediates a present experience,
   as in “seeing” fragrant sandalwood or the shell-silver illusion.
3. **Yogic perception (yogaja):** subtle, distant, past or future objects become accessible.

**Objection.** Universal- and cognition-mediated perception stretch “contact” until inference or
memory is relabelled perception.

**Reply.** Nyāya treats the universal or prior cognition as a distinctive relation within its
realist ontology, not as verbal or inferential derivation.

**Residual.** The defence is strongest only if real universals and extraordinary contact are
independently credible.

**Exam use.** On “ordinary and extraordinary perception,” state the six ordinary relations, all
three extraordinary forms, then evaluate whether universal perception is justified.

## Lesson 5 — Inference: sign, pervasion, demonstration and fallacy

### Visual first: from smoke to fire

```text
SUBJECT (pakṣa): hill
SIGN (hetu): smoke is present on hill
PERVASION (vyāpti): wherever smoke, fire
SUBSUMPTIVE REFLECTION (parāmarśa):
“this hill has smoke pervaded by fire”
                         ↓
CONCLUSION (sādhya): hill has fire
```

For another person, Nyāya states five members: thesis (**pratijñā**), reason (**hetu**),
universal-with-example (**udāharaṇa**), application (**upanaya**) and conclusion
(**nigamana**). Inference for oneself need not recite them.

### Classification by direction or basis of uniformity

| Type | Definition and example | Comparator and residual rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Pūrvavat** | From a perceived cause to an unperceived effect: dark clouds are taken as a sign of future rain. | Use it when the sign is understood as causally prior and the conclusion as its expected effect; do not relabel an observed consequence as prediction. |
| **Śeṣavat** | From a perceived effect to an unperceived cause: a swollen muddy river supports an inference to earlier rain. | Use it when the sign is a consequence and the target is its explaining cause. Some textbooks also call elimination-to-the-remainder (**pariśeṣa**) śeṣavat: after rival explanations are excluded, the residue is retained. State which sense is intended. |
| **Sāmānyatodṛṣṭa** | From a uniform relation not presented as the usual observed cause–effect pair: changed celestial position supports unperceived motion. | Use it only when regularity warrants the conclusion but neither “known cause to effect” nor “known effect to cause” accurately describes the route. |

The three are not merely past, future and present tenses. Their decisive question is the
**direction and kind of uniformity** linking sign and target. The residual reading of śeṣavat is a
second classificatory usage, not permission to merge causal diagnosis with elimination without
notice.

### Classification by form of concomitance

| Type | How pervasion is established | Teaching example and comparator condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Kevalānvayi** (positive-only) | Agreement in presence alone: the hetu occurs only in cases belonging to the sādhya-domain. | Schematic example: “Whatever is an object of valid cognition is knowable; this pot is such an object; therefore it is knowable.” No genuine non-knowable counter-instance lies within the stated domain, so a negative comparator (**vipakṣa**) is unavailable; demand positive application, not an invented negative case. |
| **Kevalavyatireki** (negative-only) | Agreement in absence: exclusion from cases lacking the sādhya does the work. | Earth’s possession of smell distinguishes it from the other elements: where the relevant earth-difference is absent, smell is absent. There is no independent positive comparator (**sapakṣa**) of the required kind apart from the subject-domain, so the negative test carries the inference. |
| **Anvayavyatireki** (positive-and-negative) | Both co-presence and co-absence establish pervasion. | Smoke–fire: smoky kitchens supply positive comparators, while fireless lakes supply negative comparators; smoke is found with fire and excluded where fire is absent. Both **sapakṣa** and **vipakṣa** are usable. |

**Residual qualification.** The five valid-sign marks express a diagnostic ideal, but comparator
requirements apply according to inference-type. Do not fault a kevalānvayi inference merely
because no genuine vipakṣa exists, or a kevalavyatireki inference merely because no independent
sapakṣa exists. The mixed type alone straightforwardly displays both comparator classes.

### Five marks of a valid sign

| Mark | Diagnostic demand |
|---|---|
| presence in subject (pakṣadharmatā) | the sign is actually in the case at issue |
| presence in positive comparators (sapakṣa-sattva) | where applicable, it occurs with the target |
| absence in negative comparators (vipakṣa-asattva) | it does not occur where the target is absent |
| undefeated (abādhita) | stronger knowledge does not refute the conclusion |
| no equal counter-sign (asatpratipakṣa) | an equally strong opposite reason is absent |

These are not the five syllogism-members. Comparator conditions must be qualified for
positive-only or negative-only inference.

### How pervasion is grasped

```text
repeated co-presence and co-absence
        + search for counterexample
        + removal of hidden condition (upādhi)
        + reductive doubt-removal (tarka)
        + universal-mediated perception
        = warranted vyāpti
```

Pervasion is asymmetric: smoke implies fire, but fire does not always imply smoke. Wet fuel is a
hidden condition in the bad direction “fire therefore smoke.” Hypothetical reasoning is an aid,
not an independent pramāṇa. Finite observation alone is insufficient.

### Fallacy families

| Fallacy | Failure |
|---|---|
| irregular (savyabhicāra) | common (**sādhāraṇa**), subject-only (**asādhāraṇa**) or overwide without testable contrast (**anupasaṃhārin**) |
| contradictory (viruddha) | sign establishes the opposite target |
| counterbalanced (satpratipakṣa) | an equal opposing sign blocks settlement |
| unproved (asiddha) | subject (**āśrayāsiddha**), sign-in-subject (**svarūpāsiddha**) or pervasion (**vyāpyatvāsiddha**) is unestablished |
| defeated (bādhita) | stronger perception or knowledge refutes the target |

The mapping is subtype-sensitive, not a rigid one-to-one pairing. A common irregular sign violates
negative exclusion; a subject-only sign lacks positive comparators; an overwide sign leaves no
negative comparator. Subject-locus absence, sign absence and unproved pervasion are distinct
unproved subtypes.

**Cārvāka objection.** No finite survey removes every hidden condition; tarka uses inference and
becomes circular.
**Nyāya reply.** It claims warranted cognition, not exhaustive omniscience; tarka removes a rival
supposition, universals support unrestricted scope, and wholesale denial of inference risks
self-contradiction.
**Residual.** Deny real universals and the induction solution remains vulnerable.

## Lesson 6 — Comparison, testimony, sentence-meaning and cognition’s result

### Visual first: what is newly known?

```text
NYĀYA UPAMĀNA
instruction “gavaya resembles cow”
 → forest perception + remembered instruction
 → NEW: “this is what ‘gavaya’ denotes”

MĪMĀṂSĀ UPAMĀNA
known cow + present gavaya
 → NEW: “the absent cow resembles this gavaya”
```

Nyāya comparison yields a word–referent relation. Perception presents the animal, testimony gives
the earlier instruction and memory retains it, but none alone yields the new naming-cognition.
Mīmāṃsā changes the fruit: similarity qualifying the absent remembered cow; prior testimony is
not required. Kumārila treats similarity through shared qualities, while Prabhākara makes it a
distinct category. Both use novelty to defend independence; they disagree over whether comparison
teaches a **name** or a **resemblance**.

### Why Nyāya refuses four reductions

| Reduction | Nyāya’s irreducibility response |
|---|---|
| **Perception** | The eye presents this animal and its features, but a word’s denotative relation is not a visible quality of the animal. |
| **Testimony** | The earlier instruction supplies general resemblance; it does not identify this encountered individual as what “gavaya” denotes. |
| **Inference** | The naming-cognition uses no independently established vyāpti and no perceived feature as a probans proving a conclusion. |
| **Memory** | Memory reproduces the instruction; it cannot by itself generate the novel judgement that this present animal is the word’s referent. |

Testimony, perception and memory are therefore auxiliary conditions. Nyāya identifies cognition
of similarity as the operative instrument and the new word–object relation as its fruit.

### Reciprocal reduction debate

**Mīmāṃsā against Nyāya.** The episode appears decomposable into heard testimony, present
perception and recollection, so positing a fifth operation looks redundant.

**Nyāya reply.** Neither component separately nor their mere collection delivers denotation.
Only similarity encountered in the remembered-instruction context yields “this is what ‘gavaya’
means”; naming that distinctive productive role **upamāna** is not simple addition.

**Nyāya against Mīmāṃsā.** Its result concerns an already known cow; the absent cow seems supplied
by memory, and symmetrical resemblance appears inferentially reconstructible from shared
features.

**Mīmāṃsā reply.** Memory cannot reproduce the specific cow-to-gavaya relation because that
relation was never earlier cognised. Present perception gives the gavaya-side, not a qualification
of the absent cow; an inferential reduction also presupposes the similarity-cognition it claims to
derive. The residual issue for both schools is whether a distinctive result establishes a distinct
pramāṇa or only a complex cooperation of admitted sources.

### Testimony (śabda)

A reliable speaker (**āpta**) knows correctly, intends truth and can express it clearly. Status
alone is not authority. Verbal cognition requires expectancy, semantic fitness, proximity and
speaker-intention. Failure examples are a disconnected noun-list, “wet it with fire,” words
uttered days apart and an ambiguous “bring saindhava” without context. Testimony can be secular or
Vedic, perceptible-subject or imperceptible-subject. Later Nyāya links Vedic authority to an
omniscient speaker; Mīmāṃsā rejects the authorship premise.

### Sentence-meaning (śābdabodha)

```text
NYĀYA: words present separate meanings
        → memory retains them
        → four sentence-conditions connect them
        → one qualifier–qualified cognition

PRĀBHĀKARA: words denote meanings only as already connected
            → learned from sentence-guided behaviour
            → connection is built into denotation
```

Prābhākara’s strongest argument is language-learning by observing action and variation in whole
sentences. Nyāya replies that stable isolated meanings explain dictionaries and novel sentences;
the causal combining steps need not be introspectively slow. Prābhākara replies that isolated
utterances are elliptical sentences and that connected functions can recombine. Do not equate
Nyāya with Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā or Prābhākara with Bhartṛhari’s indivisible sentence-theory.

### Validity and result

Nyāya holds both validity (**parataḥ-prāmāṇya**) and invalidity
(**parataḥ-aprāmāṇya**) to be externally ascertained. Memory is not a pramāṇa because it
reproduces rather than newly discloses. In the Nyāya cognition episode, knower (**pramātṛ**),
instrument (**pramāṇa**), object (**prameya**) and resultant true cognition
(**pramā/pramiti/pramāṇaphala**) are analytically distinct. For Dignāga-style Buddhism, the
object-aspect (**grāhyākāra**) and subject-aspect (**grāhakākāra**) are functional aspects of one
momentary cognition.

**Mutual objection.** Nyāya asks how one event produces itself; Buddhism says functional aspects
need not be two substances. Buddhism asks why an enduring knower is needed; Nyāya invokes
object-contact and causal differentiation to separate truth from error. The unresolved dispute is
whether functional non-difference can explain epistemic success without independent realism.

## Lesson 7 — Appearance: how real things can still be misperceived

### Visual first: shell–silver error

```text
present shell perception
        + activated silver impression
        + cognition-mediated extraordinary perception
        + failure to discriminate location
        = “this is silver”

both terms real; their synthesis/location is wrong
```

Nyāya’s **anyathākhyāti** is error as mislocation or wrong synthesis of realities: the “this” is
present, silver exists elsewhere, and prior silver-cognition mediates its presentative appearance.
It is more than memory because silver is experienced as here.

| Rival | Error-account |
|---|---|
| Prābhākara | failure to discriminate perception and memory |
| Bhāṭṭa/Kumārila | positive false identification: the present “this” is falsely judged to be silver |
| Yogācāra | internal cognition projected outward |
| standard Mādhyamika doxography | non-existent object |
| Advaita | object neither real nor unreal |

The Bhāṭṭa/Kumārila alternative is commonly called **viparītakhyāti**, but the label is not wholly
exclusive or uniform. Defective conditions disclose shell and silver’s shared lustre without the
shell’s differentiating marks; silver-memory is activated, and the cognition positively but falsely
identifies **“this as silver.”** Both terms may be real, yet their predicative synthesis here is
false. This is not Prābhākara’s mere failure to distinguish two valid cognitions. It also differs
from Nyāya: Bhāṭṭa theory does not require cognition-mediated extraordinary perception of a
particular silver elsewhere. Silver is remembered, not perceptually imported into the present
locus. Strictly, what is false is the resulting cognition or judgement, since a genuine
sense-object-generated perception is not itself false for Kumārila.

> **Terminology guardrail:** write “Bhāṭṭa positive misapprehension, commonly called
> viparītakhyāti.” Do not claim that the label is exclusive to Kumārila, reduce the account to
> Prābhākara non-discrimination, or import Nyāya’s jñānalakṣaṇa mechanism.

**Objection.** Cognition-mediated perception is invented to preserve realism.
**Reply.** It explains the phenomenological presence of an absent real without creating an
indefinable object.
**Residual.** The theory shifts pressure to the credibility of extraordinary perception and the
mechanism joining perception with memory.

**Exam use.** The role of cognition-mediated perception is the bridge, not the whole theory:
present base → memory trace → presentative silver → non-discrimination → erroneous judgement.

## Lesson 8 — Self, atomic mind and liberation

### Visual first: the cognition chain

```text
object ↔ sense ↔ atomic mind ↔ enduring self
                              │
                 desire, aversion, effort,
                 pleasure, pain, cognition
                 inhere as episodic qualities
```

The self is an enduring substance distinct from body, senses and mind. Gautama’s six canonical
signs are not six names for one generic premise; each contributes a distinct route to a bearer:

| Canonical sign | Distinct inferential force | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Desire (icchā)** | Intentional wanting is directed toward an object as wanted and therefore requires a subject to whom the wanting belongs. | Desire is episodic; the desired object, bodily organ or sense is not thereby the owner of wanting. |
| **Aversion (dveṣa)** | Rejection and avoidance have negative intentional direction and require a subject that dislikes or seeks to avoid. | A bodily withdrawal is an instrumentally expressed event; it does not make the organ itself the experiencer of aversion. |
| **Effort (prayatna)** | Striving initiates or restrains action; inert body, senses and atomic mind function as instruments and therefore point to an agent using them. | The inference is to an agent of volition, not to every bodily motion as conscious effort. |
| **Pleasure (sukha)** | A positively felt episode requires an experiencer in whom it inheres and for whom it is pleasant. | Pleasure is an adventitious quality, not the self’s essence, and Nyāya does not retain ordinary pleasure in apavarga. |
| **Pain (duḥkha)** | A negatively felt episode likewise requires an experiencer; its ownership also explains why cessation of suffering can motivate release. | Pain’s negative valence differs from pleasure, though both use quality–substance reasoning; pain does not persist in liberation. |
| **Cognition (jñāna/buddhi)** | Cognition is an episodic quality and so requires a substance-locus; body, senses and mind are causal instruments rather than its ultimate owner. | Consciousness is not the self’s permanent essence; manifest cognition requires self–mind–sense–object conditions. |

Their first-person appropriation as “I want,” “I reject,” “I strive,” “I enjoy,” “I suffer” and
“I know” gives cumulative support to one persisting self. Memory and recognition strengthen
diachronic persistence but are supplementary, not substitutes when the six canonical reasons are
asked.

Atomic mind explains limited attention: one mind connects self with one sense-cognition at a time.
Against Buddhism, Nyāya says a mere stream struggles with recognition, ownership and moral
continuity. Against Advaita, it denies that consciousness is self’s essence. The pressure returns:
if a self can exist without cognition, is it an empty bearer? Nyāya answers that cognition needs
mind–sense–object conditions, as deep sleep already suggests.

### Liberation (apavarga)

```text
true knowledge
 → false cognition ends
 → attachment/aversion/delusion end
 → action ceases
 → rebirth ceases
 → suffering ceases
 → apavarga
```

Liberation is absolute cessation of suffering, not standard positive bliss. The self remains
without pleasure, pain, desire, aversion or ordinary cognition because their causal conditions no
longer obtain.

**Stone-like objection.** Why seek an unconscious condition?
**Reply.** Freedom from every possibility of suffering is intrinsically rational; bliss may be
relational and attachment-producing.
**Residual.** The account secures invulnerability at the cost of positive experiential value.

## Lesson 9 — God and the cumulative natural theology of Udayana

### Visual first: God’s bounded role

```text
ETERNAL ATOMS + ETERNAL SELVES + BEGINNINGLESS KARMA
                         │
        require ordering / first motion / fruit allocation
                         │
              ĪŚVARA: intelligent efficient cause
                         │
              not the material cause; no ex nihilo atoms
```

Developed Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika presents God as eternal, omniscient, bodiless, efficient cause, atomic
arranger, karmic governor and trustworthy source of the Veda. Theism must be historically
qualified: it is explicit in later synthesis, not mechanically assigned to every early stratum.

| Proof | Inferential kernel | Main pressure |
|---|---|---|
| from effect (kāryāt) | composite ordered world requires intelligent maker | potter analogy leaps to bodiless cosmic maker |
| from arrangement (āyojanāt) | unconscious atoms/adṛṣṭa cannot initiate ordered first motion | why cannot natural order suffice? |
| from sustenance (dhṛtyādeḥ) | maintained and dissolved order implies governor | order need not be personal |
| from scripture (śruteḥ) | authoritative Veda implies omniscient source | Mīmāṃsā denies authorship |

Additional commonly enumerated strands are from words (**padāt**), cognition
(**pratyayataḥ**), meaningful sentences (**vākyāt**) and determinate numerical atomic combination
(**saṅkhyāviśeṣāt**). They cannot replace the four named proofs when those four are asked.
Udayana’s force is cumulative: physical effect, first motion, continuing order, moral distribution
and testimony converge on one explanatory hypothesis.

**Problem of evil.** Karma explains differential suffering; God distributes rather than
arbitrarily creates deserts. The residual cost is that God appears constrained by beginningless
karma.

### Nyāya and Yoga

Nyāya infers a creator/orderer and moral governor. Classical Yoga’s God is a special conscious
witness untouched by affliction, action, fruit and latent deposit, a beginningless teacher and aid
to meditative absorption. Both accept God, but not by the same demonstration or for the same
primary function.

**Exam verdict.** The case is strongest as cumulative explanation inside Nyāya realism and weaker
as a demonstrative proof binding an opponent who rejects inference, real effects or Vedic
authorship.

## Lesson 10 — Causation: relevance, novelty and prior absence

### Visual first: producing cloth

```text
threads (inherent/material cause)
 + conjunction/qualities in threads (non-inherent cause)
 + weaver/tools (efficient cause)
 + absence of blockers
 = NEW whole: cloth
```

Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika defends non-pre-existence of the effect (**asatkāryavāda**) or new production
(**ārambhavāda**). The effect does not pre-exist **as that effect** in the cause; this is not
creation from nothing. Production would be pointless if the finished effect already existed.

A cause is an invariable, unconditional and relevant antecedent. **Ananyathāsiddha** marks
indispensable relevance. Five standard **anyathāsiddha** exclusions show what mere priority cannot
make causal: the staff’s colour, the potter’s father, sound produced by the staff, omnipresent
space and the potter’s donkey. These represent incidental quality, remote antecedent, co-effect,
ever-present background and accidental companion.

The complete causal complex includes inherent, non-inherent and efficient causes and absence of
obstruction. Thus “the seed does not contain the tree” means no latent numerically identical tree,
not that any seed can produce anything.

### Prior absence against Sāṃkhya

Before production, the new whole has prior absence; production terminates it. Sāṃkhya replies that
only manifestation is absent while the effect exists latently. Nyāya rejoins that new identity,
structure and powers need genuine production. Sāṃkhya asks why a non-existent effect arises only
from a specific cause. Nyāya answers through the complete specific causal complex, not ex nihilo
creation. The dispute turns on cause–effect identity, not the label “prior absence” alone.

## Lesson 11 — Atomistic creation and the reality of wholes

### Visual first: cyclic construction

```text
dissolution: compounds separate; eternal atoms remain
                         ↓
God + souls’ unseen moral force initiate first motion
                         ↓
2 atoms → imperceptible dyad
3 dyads → triad / minimum perceptible magnitude
                         ↓
larger compounds → bodies and world
                         ↓
new cycle of dissolution
```

Earth, water, fire and air have eternal, partless, indivisible, imperceptible atoms. Ether, time,
direction, selves and minds are eternal substances but not four elemental atom-types. Infinite
physical division would leave no ultimate basis for finite compounds, so atomism stops regress.

Two atoms form a dyad; three **dyads**, not three atoms, form a triad. Creation recombines eternal
materials; God does not create atoms ex nihilo. First motion is explained in later synthesis by
God’s volition working with the unseen moral force of souls, subject to the earlier theism caution.

Nyāya also accepts a real whole (**avayavin**) over and above parts, inhering in them and possessing
new causal powers. Buddhist reduction asks whether the whole is identical with its parts or a
mysterious extra. Nyāya answers that practical cognition and emergent properties require a new
whole. The residual pressure is dependence on the already contested inherence relation.

# PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

## Exact owned PYQs, each solved once

### 2018 Q5(a) · 10 marks

**Question:** How do the Vaiśeṣika philosophers explain the difference of the relationships in the
two cases—(i) the brown colour of the table and (ii) the book on the table? Discuss.

**Model solution:** Brown is a quality and the table a substance; the quality cannot exist as a
free-standing brown and therefore inheres in the table through the eternal inseparable relation
of samavāya. The book and table are two independently existing substances related by produced and
destructible conjunction, saṃyoga. Spatial closeness is therefore insufficient to identify
inherence. Inherence requires category-specific ontological dependence, while conjunction permits
separation. The distinction lets Vaiśeṣika explain dependent predication without confusing it
with accidental contact. A critic may question the imperceptible inherence relation, but merely
calling both relations “contact” leaves the quality–substance dependence unexplained.

### 2018 Q5(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** How do the Buddhists and the Nyāya philosophers explain our knowledge of 'the absence
of the jar on the table'? Answer in detail.

**Model solution:** Both accounts require a visible table, competent senses and no obstruction.
Nyāya treats jar-absence as a real qualifier of the perceived table: the table is the locus and jar
the counterpositive; qualifier–qualified perception yields “no jar here.” A Buddhist
reconstruction accepts perception of the positive table and warranted non-apprehension or
exclusion, but rejects an additional negative entity. Nyāya asks how a true negative judgement
can lack a real object; Buddhism replies that warranted non-apprehension need not reify absence.
Neither view should be confused with the Bhāṭṭa doctrine of an independent non-cognition pramāṇa.

### 2018 Q6(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** How do the Naiyāyikas prove the existence of God? Do the Yoga philosophers prove God
in the same way? If yes, how? And if no, why? Give reasons for your answer.

**Model solution:**

**Thesis.** Naiyāyikas do not merely affirm God through scripture. Developed Nyāya infers an
eternal, omniscient and bodiless **efficient cause** by combining several explanatory routes. God
orders eternal atoms, initiates cosmic combination, governs the world and allocates karmic fruits;
he is not the material creator of atoms or selves. Classical Yoga accepts Īśvara, but does **not**
establish him through the same cumulative creator-inference.

**Nyāya’s cumulative proof.**

1. **From effect (kāryāt):** the world is a composite, ordered and dependent effect. As a pot
   requires an intelligent potter, the cosmic effect requires an intelligent maker. The conclusion
   is an efficient cause, not a material source.
2. **From arrangement (āyojanāt):** eternal, unconscious atoms cannot initiate their own purposive
   first conjunction at the beginning of a creative cycle. Unconscious **adṛṣṭa** also cannot
   intelligently arrange them. An intelligent impeller is therefore inferred.
3. **From sustenance and governance (dhṛtyādeḥ):** the continued regulation, support and dissolution
   of the cosmos require governing intelligence, not merely an initial maker.
4. **From karmic allocation:** unseen merit and demerit are themselves non-conscious. Their
   proportionate distribution as pleasure and pain calls for an omniscient administrator.
5. **From scripture (śruteḥ, with padāt/vākyāt):** authoritative, meaningful Vedic sentences whose
   scope exceeds ordinary cognition are traced to an omniscient trustworthy source.

These strands identify one explanatory hypothesis: a bodiless omniscient agent whose volition
works with atoms and the beginningless karma of selves.

**Does Yoga prove the same God in the same way?** No. Pātañjala Yoga characterises Īśvara as a
**puruṣa-viśeṣa**, a special conscious witness untouched by afflictions, action, karmic fruits and
latent deposits. He is a beginningless teacher, while **īśvara-praṇidhāna** assists concentration
and samādhi. Yoga therefore places God primarily within soteriological discipline. Its classical
account does not reconstruct Udayana’s inference from composite effect, atomic first motion,
cosmic governance and Vedic authorship. Both systems accept an exceptional omniscient being and
associate him with liberation, but agreement in name does not establish identity of proof,
metaphysical role or practical function.

**Objections and replies.** A Buddhist may reject the pot–potter analogy because observed makers
are embodied and the world is not known to be an artefact. Nyāya replies that composite ordered
dependence, not bodily manufacture, is the relevant sign, though the analogy remains strained.
Sāṃkhya invokes unconscious prakṛti; Nyāya answers that unconscious nature cannot purposively
arrange atoms or allocate karma. Mīmāṃsā denies that the Veda has an author, weakening the
scriptural route; Nyāya answers from meaningful authoritative discourse. The problem of evil is
met through karma, but this makes God appear constrained by an order he administers.

**Conclusion.** Nyāya offers a premise-sensitive cumulative natural theology; Yoga offers a
special puruṣa and meditative support. They converge in theism but not in demonstration. The
Nyāya case is strongest for one who already accepts inference, effects, karmic governance and
authoritative testimony; it does not bind an opponent who rejects those premises.

### 2019 Q5(a) · 10 marks

**Question:** Justify the status of Abhāva as a category in Vaiśeṣika philosophy.

**Model solution:** Negative cognitions are determinate and truth-apt: “no jar on the table” has
the table as locus and jar as counterpositive. Reducing it to a positive table-cognition omits what
the judgement asserts. Later Vaiśeṣika therefore admits absence as a seventh category with prior,
posterior, locus-relative enduring and mutual forms. The posit explains pre-production,
destruction, enduring non-possession and difference. The objection is ontological inflation:
language or non-apprehension may suffice. Vaiśeṣika replies that a true cognition requires an
appropriate object. Its justification is strongest within realism, though the Buddhist challenge
shows that negative truth need not obviously entail a negative entity.

### 2019 Q6(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** What is the role of Jñānalakṣaṇa-pratyakṣa in explaining Anyathākhyāti?

**Model solution:** In shell–silver error, ordinary perception first presents a shell as “this,”
while a previous silver-experience has left a memory-impression. **Jñānalakṣaṇa-pratyakṣa** is the
cognition-mediated extraordinary relation through which that prior silver-cognition makes the
elsewhere-real silver presentative in the current episode. It therefore performs a precise bridging
role: memory supplies the activated content, but cognition-mediated perception explains why silver
is experienced not merely as remembered but as perceptually present **here**. Failure to
discriminate the present locus from the mediated silver then yields the erroneous unitary judgement
“this is silver.”

The mechanism preserves Nyāya realism. The shell is real, silver is real elsewhere, and error lies
in location or synthesis rather than in an unreal or indefinable object. Prābhākara instead treats
error as failure to discriminate perception and memory. Bhāṭṭa positive misapprehension, commonly
called **viparītakhyāti**, instead treats “this is silver” as a positively false identification:
defective apprehension of shared lustre activates silver-memory, but no Nyāya-style extraordinary
perception of silver elsewhere is required. Yogācāra treats the apparently external object as
cognition’s own projected form; Advaita calls the error-object neither real nor unreal. Nyāya
replies that these alternatives either under-explain silver’s felt presence or multiply
ontological obscurity. The main objection to Nyāya is that extraordinary perception appears
introduced to save realism. Its reply is that bare recollection cannot explain the presentative
“this silver here,” though the residual question remains whether this mediated episode should
still count as perception.

### 2019 Q7(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** How does Udayana prove the existence of God through Kāryāt, Āyojanāt, Dhṛtyādeḥ and
Śruteḥ? Discuss.

**Model solution:**

**Introduction and thesis.** Udayana’s project is not one undifferentiated design argument. The
four named proofs move from different explananda—effect, first conjunction, continuing governance
and authoritative revelation—to an eternal, omniscient and bodiless Īśvara. Their conclusion is an
**efficient**, not material, cause because atoms and selves remain eternal. Their intended force is
cumulative: each answers a pressure left by the others.

| Proof | Structured reconstruction | Explanatory result |
|---|---|---|
| **Kāryāt — from effect** | The world is composite, ordered and dependent, hence an effect; effects such as pots require intelligent makers; therefore the world requires an intelligent maker. | God explains production of the cosmic whole without becoming its material substance. |
| **Āyojanāt — from arrangement** | Primordial atoms are unconscious and initially uncombined; unconscious atoms and adṛṣṭa cannot purposively begin ordered first motion; therefore an intelligent impeller must initiate conjunction. | God explains why eternal materials form a determinate cosmos rather than remaining inert or combining arbitrarily. |
| **Dhṛtyādeḥ — from support, regulation and dissolution** | The cosmos is not only produced but sustained, ordered and dissolved through regular processes; continuing governance is better explained by intelligence than by unguided material factors. | God becomes cosmic governor, not a maker who withdraws after origination. |
| **Śruteḥ — from scripture** | The Veda is treated as authoritative discourse about matters beyond ordinary perception; meaningful and reliable instruction requires a competent source; therefore its adequate source must be omniscient. | God is the supreme **āpta**, grounding later Nyāya’s account of Vedic testimony. |

The proofs converge further with karmic administration: non-conscious merit and demerit cannot
allot their own results with moral precision, whereas an omniscient governor can connect deeds and
fruits. The four arguments therefore identify one hypothesis with physical, moral and epistemic
roles.

**Objections and Nyāya replies.** Buddhist critics deny that the world is an effect in the relevant
artefactual sense. The potter analogy also seems disanalogous: potters are embodied, finite and work
on external material. Nyāya replies that the probans is composite ordered dependence and that
agency need not essentially require embodiment. The reply protects the inference, but the
disanalogy survives. Sāṃkhya claims that prakṛti’s own transformation explains order and asks why a
perfect being would create. Nyāya responds that unconscious nature cannot allocate karma or
purposively initiate atomic motion; divine action follows the adṛṣṭa of selves rather than unmet
desire. Mīmāṃsā denies the premise of śruteḥ by holding the Veda authorless and uses apūrva rather
than God to link ritual and fruit. Nyāya answers that meaningful authoritative sentences and moral
allocation are better explained by an omniscient speaker, but the proof loses force if
authorlessness is granted. Finally, karma answers the problem of unequal suffering, yet leaves God
apparently administering a beginningless order he did not create.

**Critical evaluation.** Kāryāt and āyojanāt depend on moving from local artefacts or agents to the
world as a whole; dhṛtyādeḥ risks treating regularity as personal governance; śruteḥ depends on a
contested theory of authorship. No single proof is coercive. Udayana’s stronger strategy is
abductive and cumulative: effecthood, atomic arrangement, sustained order, karmic distribution and
testimony are claimed to receive a unified explanation in Īśvara.

**Conclusion.** Udayana establishes a coherent theistic terminus within Nyāya realism, but the
proofs remain premise-sensitive. Their philosophical merit lies less in an isolated syllogism than
in a converging case whose force rises or falls with Nyāya’s theories of causation, inference,
agency and testimony.

### 2020 Q6(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** Explain the concepts of 'Anyathāsiddha' and 'Ananyathāsiddha' in the context of
Asatkāryavāda.

**Model solution:** Asatkāryavāda requires a disciplined cause-definition because the new effect
cannot arise from every earlier condition. An ananyathāsiddha antecedent is invariable,
unconditional and causally indispensable. Anyathāsiddha antecedents are otherwise accounted for
or irrelevant: the staff’s colour, potter’s father, staff-sound, omnipresent space and donkey
illustrate incidental, remote, co-effect, ubiquitous and accidental cases. The distinction
prevents temporal priority from becoming causal sufficiency. The complete causal complex includes
material, non-inherent and efficient causes plus absence of blockers. Thus new production remains
specific rather than ex nihilo or arbitrary.

### 2020 Q8(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** Critically evaluate the logical and metaphysical status of Viśeṣa in the context of
Vaiśeṣika Philosophy.

**Model solution:** Logically, viśeṣa preserves inference to numerically many eternal atoms,
selves and minds when universals and qualities are shared. Metaphysically, it is an irreducible
ultimate particularity residing only in eternal substances. A regress threatens: if it
differentiates atoms, what differentiates particularities? Vaiśeṣika calls each
self-differentiating. The reply blocks regress but appears stipulative, and the entity is inferred
solely for individuation. Hence viśeṣa is systemically necessary for pluralist realism but
epistemically costly: it solves identity collapse by positing a primitive whose own difference is
asserted rather than independently explained.

### 2021 Q5(a) · 10 marks

**Question:** Does the seed contain the tree? Discuss with reference to Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika Philosophy.

**Model solution:** The seed does not contain the tree as a pre-existent effect. Before production,
the numerically new tree has prior absence; a suitable seed, auxiliary conditions and absence of
blockers constitute its causal basis. Production terminates prior absence and begins a new whole
with new organization and powers. This is not creation from nothing or denial of specificity:
only an appropriate causal complex produces that tree. Sāṃkhya calls the tree latent, but Nyāya
argues that mere “manifestation” under-explains novelty. The debate turns on whether cause and
effect are numerically identical, not on whether the seed is causally necessary.

### 2021 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain with reference to Nyāya Philosophy, the nature of śabda as the advice of āpta
(a reliable person).

**Model solution:** Śabda is fresh true cognition generated by reliable instruction. An āpta must
know the matter, intend truthful communication and possess expressive competence; rank or
confidence is insufficient. Sentence-cognition further requires expectancy, semantic fitness,
proximity and intention. Testimony is independent because perception or inference cannot give
every knower all remote, historical or imperceptible facts. It covers secular and Vedic speech.
Later Nyāya explains Vedic authority through omniscient divine authorship, while Mīmāṃsā contests
that premise. Reliability therefore belongs to both speaker and intelligible utterance.

### 2021 Q5(c) · 10 marks

**Question:** Is ‘inseparability’ (ayuta-siddhatva) a necessary condition or a sufficient condition
for defining characteristics (lakṣaṇa) of samavāya (inherence)? Explain with reference to
Vaiśeṣika Philosophy.

**Model solution:** Inseparability is necessary because a quality cannot be established apart from
its substance, a whole depends on parts and a universal occurs in instances. It is not sufficient:
a book glued to a table may resist separation while remaining an independent substance joined by
conjunction. One must specify the relata’s ontological types and asymmetric dependence. Nor should
an additional inherence be posited between inherence and its relata, since that creates regress.
Vaiśeṣika treats samavāya as a self-linking primitive inferred from dependent predication. Thus
ayutasiddhatva is indispensable but becomes a full definition only with category-specific
dependence.

### 2022 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Question:** What is the ontological status of Sāmānya, according to Vaiśeṣika Philosophy?
Critically examine.

**Model solution:** Sāmānya is a real, eternal, repeatable universal, one in many and related to
instances by inherence. It grounds classification, common predication and determinate perception.
Six obstructors prevent ontological excess. Buddhist apoha theory objects that universals are
causally inert and that exclusion can explain concepts. Vaiśeṣika replies that exclusion and
resemblance presuppose stable classes and that universal presence is non-spatial. The theory
strongly integrates ontology, semantics and inference, but its force depends on controversial
inherence and on treating “this is a cow” as perception of cow-ness rather than conceptual
construction.

### 2022 Q7(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** How do Naiyāyikas respond to Cārvāka’s objections against inference (anumāna) and
establish inference as an independent means of knowledge? Critically discuss.

**Model solution:** Cārvāka argues that finite observations cannot establish universal pervasion,
hidden conditions may remain and inference about inference is circular. Nyāya answers through
repeated agreement and difference, search for counterinstances, removal of hidden conditions,
hypothetical doubt-removal and universal-mediated perception. Subsumptive reflection then applies
the warranted pervasion to the present sign. Inference is independent because its new conclusion
is not reducible to perception. Nyāya also charges global rejection with practical and
self-referential difficulty. Yet its solution remains conditional on real universals and
extraordinary perception, so it establishes disciplined warrant more convincingly than absolute
certainty.

### 2023 Q5(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** Present an account of Vaiśeṣika’s view of negation in the light of their statement —
“Negation always has a counterpositive and absolute negation is an impossibility.”

**Model solution:** An absence is always absence **of** a counterpositive in a locus; it is not
featureless nothing. Vaiśeṣika admits prior, posterior, enduring locus-relative and mutual absence.
The quoted denial of “absolute negation” rejects an unrestricted, subjectless negation without
counterpositive or locus. It does not abolish atyantābhāva, which means that a specified
counterpositive is absent in a specified locus across time. The counterpositive is a conceptual
target and need not exist: “a hare’s horn is absent on this table” specifies what is denied without
asserting a real hare’s horn. Structured negation explains truth-conditions, though a nominalist
may still deny that negative truth requires a separate category.

### 2023 Q6(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** Elucidate Naiyāyikas view of ordinary and extraordinary perception. Are they
justified in accepting that universals are perceived? Discuss.

**Model solution:** Ordinary perception uses six relations to reach substances, qualities,
quality-universals, sound, sound-universals and absences. Extraordinary perception is
universal-mediated, cognition-mediated or yogic. Nyāya says determinate awareness “this is a cow”
presents the individual as qualified by real cow-ness; universal-mediated perception also helps
extend cognition beyond surveyed instances. Critics call this conceptual classification or an ad
hoc solution to induction. Nyāya replies that stable predication and exclusion require objective
commonness. Its acceptance is coherent within realist ontology, but not independently compelling
to a Buddhist nominalist who rejects both universals and inherence.

### 2023 Q7(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Elucidate Naiyāyikas account of fallacies of the middle term in relation to five
characteristics of valid middle term.

**Model solution:**

**Introduction.** A middle term (**hetu**) is valid not because it merely appears in a
five-membered syllogism, but because it reliably connects the subject (**pakṣa**) with the property
to be proved (**sādhya**). Nyāya’s five characteristics state the positive discipline of a valid
sign; the fallacies (**hetvābhāsa**) show the different ways that discipline can fail. The relation
is diagnostic and subtype-sensitive, not a mechanical one-family-to-one-mark equation.

**Five characteristics of a valid middle term.**

1. **Pakṣadharmatā:** the hetu must occur in the subject—smoke must actually be on this hill.
2. **Sapakṣa-sattva:** where positive comparators are applicable, the hetu occurs in cases known to
   possess the sādhya—smoke occurs in fiery kitchens.
3. **Vipakṣa-asattva:** the hetu is absent from negative cases lacking the sādhya—smoke is absent
   from a fireless lake.
4. **Abādhita:** no stronger pramāṇa defeats the intended conclusion; perception defeats an
   inference that fire is cold.
5. **Asatpratipakṣa:** no equally strong counter-reason establishes the opposite conclusion.

These marks jointly secure subject-relevance, positive and negative pervasion, compatibility with
established knowledge and freedom from dialectical counterbalance.

**Fallacies in relation to the marks.**

| Fallacy | Exact failure and relation to valid-sign tests |
|---|---|
| **Savyabhicāra / anaikāntika** (irregular) | A **sādhāraṇa** sign occurs in both positive and negative cases and violates vipakṣa-asattva. An **asādhāraṇa** sign is confined to the subject and supplies no usable positive comparator, so sapakṣa support cannot be established. An **anupasaṃhārin** sign is so all-embracing that no negative comparator remains, making restriction untestable. |
| **Viruddha** (contradictory) | The hetu is pervaded by the opposite of the intended sādhya. It does not merely give weak support; it proves the contrary and reverses the required concomitance. |
| **Satpratipakṣa** (counterbalanced) | An equally forceful rival hetu supports the opposed conclusion. The original sign may exist, but asatpratipakṣa fails and the debate remains unsettled. |
| **Asiddha** (unproved) | **Āśrayāsiddha** lacks an established subject-locus; **svarūpāsiddha** lacks the hetu in the subject and directly defeats pakṣadharmatā; **vyāpyatvāsiddha** lacks established pervasion, often because an upādhi remains. The three must not be collapsed into “the sign is absent.” |
| **Bādhita** (defeated) | A stronger pramāṇa contradicts the sādhya, so abādhita fails—for example, direct perception defeats “fire is cold.” |

**Comparator qualification.** The standard marks must respect the form of inference.
Kevalānvayi inference has no genuine vipakṣa; kevalavyatireki inference has no independent
sapakṣa of the ordinary sort; anvayavyatireki uses both. Thus absence of a comparator that the
inference-type itself excludes is not automatically a fallacy. What matters is whether the
available positive or negative route establishes unconditional pervasion.

**Objection and reply.** A critic may argue that the five marks simply redescribe successful
inference and cannot guarantee a universal from finite cases. Nyāya replies that the marks work
with repeated agreement and difference, counterexample-search, removal of upādhi, tarka and
universal-mediated perception. Nevertheless, the answer depends on Nyāya’s realism about
universals and its expanded account of perception.

**Conclusion.** The five characteristics are a positive ideal for a probans; the fallacies are a
plural set of diagnostic breakdowns. Their relation is therefore systematic but not bijective.
A high-quality account maps each subtype to the precise subject, comparator, pervasion,
defeasibility or counter-reason failure rather than matching five names against five names.

### 2023 Q8(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** Write a note on Nyāya notion of Prāgabhāva (prior non-existence). How does this notion
help Naiyāyikas in defending their position on causation against the Sāṃkhya view of causation?
Critically discuss.

**Model solution:** Prior absence is beginningless but ends when an effect is produced. Nyāya uses
it to argue that the numerically new effect was not already present as that effect, supporting
asatkāryavāda. Sāṃkhya replies that only manifest form was absent; the effect existed latently in
its specific cause. Nyāya cites new identity, structure and capacities, while Sāṃkhya presses
causal specificity: why this effect from this cause if absent? Nyāya answers with material,
non-inherent and efficient causes plus absence of blockers. Prior absence sharpens the dispute but
does not decide it without a defended criterion of cause–effect identity.

### 2024 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the six reasons offered by the Naiyāyikas to prove the existence of the self.

**Model solution:** Gautama identifies desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition as
six signs of self. **Desire** is object-directed wanting and therefore requires someone to whom the
want belongs. **Aversion** is rejection or avoidance and likewise needs a disliking subject rather
than a bodily organ. **Effort** is active striving or restraint; body, senses and atomic mind are
instruments, so volitional initiation points to an agent using them. **Pleasure** is a positively
felt episodic quality and therefore requires an experiencer; it is not the self’s essence and does
not persist as ordinary pleasure in release. **Pain** is the corresponding negative episode, whose
ownership explains both suffering and the motive for apavarga; it too ceases in liberation.
**Cognition** is an adventitious quality requiring a substance-locus, while sense and mind explain
its causal mediation rather than ultimate ownership. Their appropriation as “I want, reject,
strive, enjoy, suffer and know” cumulatively supports one enduring bearer. Memory and recognition
strengthen persistence but are supplementary, not members of the canonical six. Buddhist stream
theory challenges the substantial bearer; Nyāya replies that unified ownership, agency and moral
continuity are better explained by a persisting self.

### 2024 Q5(c) · 10 marks

**Question:** Do these two sentences “Air does not have heat” and “Air is not fire” refer to the
same type of absence or abhāva, according to the Vaiśeṣikas? Discuss.

**Model solution:** They express different absences. “Air does not have heat” concerns enduring
locus-relative absence of heat as air’s intrinsic quality; contact with fire may heat air without
making heat constitutive of air. “Air is not fire” expresses mutual absence or reciprocal
non-identity between two substances. The first negates a quality–substance inherence, the second
negates identity. Treating both as generic negation loses the structured locus, counterpositive
and relation that Vaiśeṣika ontology was designed to preserve.

### 2025 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Question:** Present an exposition of the debate between Naiyāyikas and Buddhists with reference
to the notion of Pramāṇa and Pramāṇaphala.

**Model solution:** Nyāya distinguishes the operative means—such as sense-object relation or
subsumptive reflection—from the resultant fresh true cognition, within a structure containing
knower, object, means and result. Dignāga-style Buddhism treats presenting and resultant awareness
as functional aspects of one momentary cognition. Nyāya objects that producer and product cannot
be identical and asks how independent-object success is secured. Buddhism replies that functional
distinction is not self-production and questions the need for a substantial knower. Nyāya invokes
causal contact to distinguish truth from error. The dispute reflects realist pluralism versus
cognition-centred functional analysis.

### 2025 Q5(e) · 10 marks

**Question:** Present an exposition of Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika’s theory of causation.

**Model solution:** Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika defends new production: the effect does not pre-exist as effect
and its prior absence ends at production. A cause is an invariable, unconditional and relevant
antecedent; otherwise-established antecedents are excluded. Causes are inherent/material,
non-inherent and efficient, and the complete complex includes absence of blockers. Threads,
their conjunction and the weaver jointly produce a new cloth-whole. Sāṃkhya objects that an absent
effect cannot arise; Nyāya denies ex nihilo creation and cites causal specificity. The theory’s
strength is explaining novelty; its difficulty is preserving continuity without latent identity.

### 2025 Q6(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Present a detailed account of Gautama’s definition of Perception.

**Model solution:**

**Introduction.** Gautama defines perception in substance as
**indriyārtha-sannikarṣa-utpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri
vyavasāyātmakam pratyakṣam**: perception is cognition produced by sense–object relation, not
word-caused, non-errant and determinate. A detailed account must reconstruct every clause and then
show how later Nyāya expands the apparently simple notion of contact.

**Clause-by-clause reconstruction.**

1. **Indriyārtha-sannikarṣa — sense–object relation.** Perceptual cognition depends causally on an
   appropriate relation between a competent sense and its object. It is not merely a vivid thought.
   “Contact” is later differentiated so that qualities, universals and absences can be perceived
   through their relations to a contacted locus.
2. **Utpannam — produced.** The cognition must arise from that relation. This excludes a later
   memory of an earlier contact and any knowledge whose operative cause is testimony.
3. **Avyapadeśyam — not caused by words.** The clause distinguishes perception from **śabda**. It
   cannot mean permanently ineffable, because determinate perception can later be expressed as
   “this is a blue pot.” The proper contrast is causal: words do not produce the perception.
4. **Avyabhicāri — non-errant.** Doubtful or illusory presentation does not count as valid
   perception in the final epistemic sense. Shell-silver may phenomenally appear perceptual, but
   its erroneous judgement is excluded.
5. **Vyavasāyātmakam — determinate.** Valid perception is definite rather than doubtful. It presents
   a qualified object, not an unresolved oscillation between alternatives.

**Indeterminate and determinate phases.** Later Naiyāyikas distinguish **nirvikalpaka**,
pre-predicative awareness, from **savikalpaka**, qualified and nameable awareness such as “this is a
blue pot.” This creates an interpretive tension: Gautama’s determinacy clause appears to define
perception as determinate, while the later theory admits a prior indeterminate phase. The safest
resolution is that the clause excludes doubt in completed valid perception; whether the
pre-predicative phase itself has full pramā-status remains contested.

**Six ordinary relations.** Nyāya explains the range of ordinary perception through: (i)
**saṃyoga**, which presents a conjoined substance; (ii) **saṃyukta-samavāya**, which presents a
quality inhering in that substance; (iii) **saṃyukta-samaveta-samavāya**, which presents the
universal inhering in that quality; (iv) **samavāya**, used for sound; (v)
**samaveta-samavāya**, used for sound-universal; and (vi)
**viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣya-bhāva**, through which jar-absence qualifies the perceived table. The last case
shows that perception can disclose structured absence rather than a free-floating negative object.

**Extraordinary perception.** **Sāmānyalakṣaṇa** perceives through a real universal and supports
classification and Nyāya’s response to induction. **Jñānalakṣaṇa** allows prior cognition to mediate
a present experience, as in “seeing” fragrance in sandalwood or the absent silver in illusion.
**Yogaja** gives yogic access to subtle, distant, past or future objects.

**Critical evaluation.** Critics argue that universal-mediated and cognition-mediated perception
stretch contact until conceptual classification, inference or memory is renamed perception.
Nyāya replies that real universals and prior cognition establish distinctive perceptual relations,
not verbal or inferential derivations. Yet this reply is compelling only within its realist
ontology.

**Conclusion.** Gautama’s definition sharply separates perception from memory, testimony, error
and doubt. Later contact-relations make it systematically comprehensive, but the inclusion of
indeterminate and extraordinary modes leaves genuine pressure on the meanings of “determinate”
and “sense–object relation.”

### 2026 Q5(a) · 10 marks

**Question:** According to Nyaya view of inference (Anumana), what are the characteristics that
ascertain the validity of inferential sign (Hetu)? Discuss.

**Model solution:** A valid sign must occur in the subject, occur in positive instances where
applicable, be absent from negative instances, remain undefeated by stronger knowledge and lack an
equally strong counter-sign. These characteristics establish subject-relevance, positive and
negative pervasion, compatibility with the wider knowledge-system and dialectical superiority.
They are not the five members of a demonstration. Their application must respect inference-type:
a negative-only inference may lack an observed positive comparator. Fallacies reveal failures of
these conditions, but subtype-sensitive analysis prevents a rigid one-to-one mapping.

### 2026 Q5(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** Critically explain the nature of Samavaya (inherence) as propounded in Vaisesika
philosophy.

**Model solution:** Samavāya is one, eternal and inferred, relating inseparable category-pairs:
whole–parts, substance–quality, substance–motion, universal–instance and eternal substance–
particularity. It differs from produced conjunction between separable substances. Inseparability
is necessary, not sufficient; ontological dependence and relata-types matter. Critics raise
relation-regress and ask how one inherence selectively relates many pairs. Vaiśeṣika calls it
self-linking and says relata determine its operation. The doctrine explains dependent
predication, but its primitive status means it ends regress by stipulation as much as by further
analysis.

### 2026 Q6(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** Bring out the main points of contestation between Naiyayikas and Prabhakara
Mimamsakas with reference to knowledge through words (Sabdabodha).

**Model solution:** Both explain one sentence-cognition from successive words and accept
expectancy, fitness, proximity and intention. Nyāya says words first present stable individual
meanings; memory retains them and syntactic conditions produce connected qualifier–qualified
meaning. Prābhākara says words denote meanings only as already connected, learned from
sentence-guided behaviour and variation. Nyāya cites lexical stability, isolated words and novel
sentences; Prābhākara calls isolated uses elliptical and says connected functions recombine.
Prābhākara objects that Nyāya invents an unexperienced combining stage; Nyāya replies that causal
steps need not be introspectable. The dispute is structural and pedagogical, not about speed.

### 2026 Q8(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Present an account of Upamana as an instrument of knowledge (Pramana). How is Nyaya
view of Upamana different from Mimamsa view? Discuss.

**Model solution:**

**Introduction.** Upamāna is an independent pramāṇa only if it produces a cognition not reducible
to perception, inference, testimony or memory. Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā agree on this novelty test but
disagree about what comparison newly reveals. Nyāya makes it semantic—the relation between a word
and its referent—whereas Mīmāṃsā makes it relational—similarity qualifying an absent remembered
object.

**Nyāya account.** A reliable speaker tells a hearer that a **gavaya** resembles a cow. In a forest
the hearer perceives a cow-like animal, recalls the instruction and cognises: “This is what the
word ‘gavaya’ denotes.” The resulting **upamiti** is knowledge of the
**saṃjñā–saṃjñi-sambandha**, the name–named relation. Similarity is the operative instrument;
testimony, perception and memory are necessary auxiliary conditions.

**Why Nyāya rejects reduction.**

1. It is not **perception**: the animal and its features are perceived, but denotation is not a
   visible property on its body.
2. It is not **testimony**: the sentence gave a general resemblance and did not identify this
   encountered individual as the referent.
3. It is not **inference**: no independently known vyāpti or probans is applied to prove the
   naming-cognition.
4. It is not **memory**: recollection reproduces the old instruction, whereas “this is a gavaya”
   is a novel judgement concerning the present animal.

**Mīmāṃsā account.** One has previously known a cow and now perceives a gavaya as cow-like. The cow
is absent but remembered, and comparison newly yields: “That cow resembles this gavaya.” Earlier
naming-instruction is not required. The fruit is similarity residing in the absent familiar
object, not knowledge that a word denotes the present object. It is not perception because the cow
is absent; not memory because that specific cow-to-gavaya relation was never previously cognised;
and not inference because no independently established universal concomitance produces it.

| Axis | Nyāya | Mīmāṃsā |
|---|---|---|
| New object known | word–referent relation | similarity qualifying the absent cow |
| Direction | familiar cow to unfamiliar gavaya and its name | present gavaya back to absent familiar cow |
| Prior testimony | essential | unnecessary |
| Function | semantic acquisition | disclosure of an unperceived similarity-relation |
| Similarity ontology | real relation among particulars sharing universals | Kumārila explains it through shared qualities; Prabhākara treats it as a distinct category |

**Reciprocal objections and replies.** Mīmāṃsā argues that Nyāya’s episode is only testimony plus
perception plus memory. Nyāya replies that neither source, separately or merely aggregated, yields
the denotative relation; similarity in the remembered-instruction context has a distinctive
productive role. Nyāya objects that Mīmāṃsā oddly makes comparison teach something about an
already-known cow; the absent cow seems supplied by memory, and symmetrical resemblance appears
inferentially recoverable. Mīmāṃsā replies that memory cannot reproduce a relation never before
known, present perception gives only the gavaya-side, and an inferential reconstruction already
presupposes the similarity it claims to derive.

**Critical assessment.** Nyāya explains ordinary word-learning more naturally, but its opponent
can still ask whether a new result requires a new pramāṇa rather than coordinated old ones.
Mīmāṃsā better isolates cognition of an absent term, yet its reversed direction can appear to
inform us about what was already familiar. Both accounts depend on a realist treatment of
similarity and on a fine distinction between operative instrument and supporting conditions.

**Conclusion.** The decisive difference is not whether comparison occurs, but **what is newly
known**. Nyāya’s upamāna is name-learning; Mīmāṃsā’s is absent-object similarity. Their reciprocal
reduction arguments show why both claim independence, while the surviving issue is whether
novelty of cognitive fruit is sufficient to multiply pramāṇas.

## Original solved Mains practice

### Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish universal (sāmānya) from ultimate particularity (viśeṣa) in Vaiśeṣika.

**Model solution:** Universal explains repeatable identity; ultimate particularity explains
irreducible numerical difference. Cow-ness is one eternal feature in many cows through inherence,
grounding classification and predication. Viśeṣa resides only in eternal substances and prevents
otherwise identical atoms or selves from collapsing into one. Universal therefore answers “how
are many alike?”, while particularity answers “how are eternal similars still many?” Buddhist
apoha challenges the first as reification; regress challenges the second. Vaiśeṣika replies through
perceived commonness and self-differentiating particularity. The pair reveals the system’s realist
ambition and its reliance on primitive inherence and individuation.

### Original 15-marker

**Question:** Critically assess whether Nyāya’s account of inference defeats Cārvāka scepticism.

**Model solution:** Nyāya correctly observes that inference needs unconditional pervasion, not
mere frequency. It combines repeated agreement/difference, counterexample-search,
hidden-condition removal, tarka and universal-mediated perception. This is superior to naive
enumeration and explains actual disciplined inquiry. Cārvāka nevertheless argues that no finite
procedure guarantees the absence of an unknown condition and that extraordinary perception is
introduced to save universality. Nyāya answers that knowledge requires warranted success, not
omniscient enumeration, and that total rejection of inference undermines ordinary action and the
rejection itself. On balance, Nyāya defeats practical global scepticism, but its theoretical
solution remains conditional on realist universals.

### Original 20-marker

**Question:** “Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika’s strength is the integration of ontology and epistemology; its
weakness is the burden placed on primitive relations.” Analyse.

**Model solution:** The claim is substantially justified. Vaiśeṣika categories explain substances,
qualities, universals, particulars, relations and absences; Nyāya pramāṇas explain access to them.
Universal realism supports predication and induction; inherence connects universal–instance,
quality–substance and whole–parts; cognition-mediated perception preserves realism in error;
enduring self secures ownership; causation and atomism explain production; God orders the cosmic
and moral scheme. This integration is unusually systematic. Yet samavāya is declared self-linking,
viśeṣa self-differentiating and universal-mediated perception a special access to unsurveyed
instances. Each stops a regress or closes an explanatory gap by a primitive. The system replies
that every ontology must end somewhere and that these posits explain otherwise ungrounded facts.
A graded verdict is appropriate: the primitives are not arbitrary within the system, but their
necessity is compelling mainly after its pluralist realism is granted.

## A1 — Navya-Nyāya precision as a diagnostic tool

Later Navya-Nyāya, especially Gaṅgeśa, develops a finer language for cognition, relation,
qualification and absence. Its value is not ornamental terminology; it shows that the tradition
kept refining ambiguity in claims such as “absence qualifies a locus” or “a sign is pervaded by a
target.”

## A2 — Inherence as a stress test for every realist relation

The regress against inherence resembles a general problem: if a relation needs another relation
to connect it to relata, explanation never begins. Vaiśeṣika’s self-linking primitive is therefore
not mere evasion; it is one possible stopping strategy. Its cost is asymmetric dialectic when the
school rejects an opponent’s primitive for being unexplained.

**Deploy when:** critically evaluating samavāya, whole–part realism or the economy of categories.
**Stop after:** one objection, the self-linking reply and the residual charge of stipulation.
**Do not:** replace the five relata-pairs or conjunction distinction with abstract regress-talk.

## A3 — Ultimate particularity and primitive thisness

Viśeṣa may be read as a disciplined “thisness” posit: it preserves plurality where qualitative
description is exhausted. This makes it relevant to the logical possibility of indiscernible
eternals, but the school’s own universalhood-blockers reveal awareness of ontological inflation.

**Deploy when:** evaluating logical versus metaphysical status or comparing individuation models.
**Stop after:** showing why plurality requires it and why self-differentiation looks stipulated.
**Do not:** introduce it into ordinary accounts of perceptible individual difference.

## A4 — Udayana as cumulative rational theist

Reading Udayana cumulatively is stronger than listing proofs. Effect, atomic initiation,
sustenance, moral allocation and testimony mutually reinforce one explanatory hypothesis. Yet
accumulation cannot transform several premise-dependent arguments into demonstration; it instead
raises comparative explanatory power.

**Deploy when:** a 20-marker asks proof, evaluation or the relation among proofs.
**Stop after:** showing convergence and one residual—embodied-artisan analogy, Vedic authorship or
karma-constrained divine freedom.
**Do not:** substitute cumulative language for the exact four named inferential kernels.

# BOUNDED EXPERT REFERENCE

## E1 — Three architectural commitments

1. **Object realism:** cognition succeeds by reaching independent objects.
2. **Qualificative structure:** ordinary cognition presents a qualificand as qualified.
3. **Causal individuation of knowledge-sources:** pramāṇas differ by operative cause and novel
   result.

These commitments jointly explain perception, sentence-meaning, error and the instrument/result
debate. **Deploy** only when a broad question asks systematic unity. **Stop** after using the triad
to explain one doctrine; do not convert a focused answer into system-summary.

## E2 — The cognitive-result diagnostic

Many inter-school disputes become clear by asking: “What exactly is newly known?” In comparison,
Nyāya answers “word–referent relation,” Mīmāṃsā “similarity in an absent object.” In testimony,
the issue is how separate or connected meanings yield one sentence-cognition. In
pramāṇa–phala, the issue is whether means and result are distinct entities or functions.

**Deploy:** in comparative epistemology.
**Stop:** once object, instrument and result are identified for both sides.
**Avoid:** reducing metaphysical disagreement to psychology alone.

## E3 — Controlled cross-tradition discriminators

| Comparison | Limited value | Stop rule |
|---|---|---|
| pervasion and induction scepticism | both question the leap from observed cases to universality | return immediately to hidden-condition removal and real universals |
| inherence and relation-regress | both expose regress in connecting relations | do not import an Absolute or treat the theories as identical |
| five-member demonstration and syllogism | both structure proof | stress empirical example and proof-for-another |
| Udayana and design/cosmological arguments | both infer intelligence from effect/order | retain eternal atoms, karma and efficient-cause-only doctrine |

Expert comparison earns marks only after the Indian doctrine is complete.

# CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## System identity and answer opening

- Historically two allied schools: Gautama’s Nyāya and Kaṇāda’s Vaiśeṣika; later synthesis.
- Nyāya: valid cognition, inference, debate, error and natural theology.
- Vaiśeṣika: categories, relations, atomism and production.
- Classical pramāṇas: Nyāya four; Vaiśeṣika two, with later synthesis using four.
- Theism belongs explicitly to developed Nyāya/later synthesis; qualify early Vaiśeṣika.

## Two category maps

- Sixteen Nyāya inquiry-topics are not seven Vaiśeṣika ontological kinds.
- Twelve knowables form the release-route: self to suffering and release.
- Seven ontological categories: substance, quality, motion, universal, ultimate particularity,
  inherence and absence.
- Nine substances; twenty-four qualities; five physical motions.
- Cognition is a quality of self, not self’s essence.

## Universal, particularity and inherence

- Universal: eternal one-in-many, perceived in determinate cognition, related by inherence.
- Six universalhood-blockers: single instance, co-extension, cross-over, regress, function-loss,
  missing relation.
- Ultimate particularity: self-differentiating individuator of eternal similars.
- Inherence: one eternal inferred inseparable relation in five standard pairs.
- Inseparability necessary, not sufficient; state category-specific asymmetric dependence.
- Conjunction is produced, destructible and between separable substances.
- Critical residual: inherence and particularity stop regress through primitives.

## Absence and negation

- Prior absence: beginningless, ends at production.
- Posterior absence: begins at destruction, endless thereafter.
- Enduring locus-relative absence: specified counterpositive absent in a locus through time.
- Mutual absence: reciprocal non-identity.
- Every absence has locus and counterpositive; unrestricted blank negation is not a determinate
  absence.
- A counterpositive is the conceptual target of denial and need not exist; “hare’s horn absent on
  this table” specifies content without positing a real horn.
- “Air lacks intrinsic heat” differs from “air is not fire.”
- No-jar: Nyāya perceives real absence qualifying table; Buddhist account uses warranted
  non-apprehension without negative entity.

## Perception

- Definition chain: sense–object relation → produced cognition → not word-caused → non-errant →
  determinate.
- Indeterminate phase is pre-predicative; determinate phase is qualified and nameable.
- Six relations reach substance, quality, quality-universal, sound, sound-universal and absence.
- Extraordinary: universal-mediated, cognition-mediated, yogic.
- Trap: non-verbal does not mean forever inexpressible.
- Critical residual: special perception depends on realist universals and expanded contact.

## Inference

- Subject, target, sign; pervasion; subsumptive reflection.
- Five demonstration-members differ from five valid-sign marks.
- Direction/uniformity types: cause→effect (pūrvavat), effect→cause (śeṣavat), and non-causal
  uniformity (sāmānyatodṛṣṭa); declare separately if śeṣavat means elimination-to-residue.
- Concomitance types: positive-only, negative-only and mixed; do not invent a vipakṣa for the first
  or an independent sapakṣa for the second.
- Five marks: subject-presence, positive presence, negative absence, undefeatedness, no equal rival.
- Pervasion is asymmetric and unconditional; remove hidden condition in the correct direction.
- Tarka aids knowledge-sources; it is not an independent pramāṇa.
- Fallacies: irregular, contradictory, counterbalanced, unproved, defeated.
- Map subtypes, not crude families, to failed conditions.
- Cārvāka residual: finite warrant becomes strongest only with universal realism.

## Comparison, testimony and language

- Nyāya comparison gives word–referent knowledge after instruction, perception and memory.
- Mīmāṃsā comparison gives similarity in the absent remembered object, without prior instruction.
- Nyāya rejects reductions to perception, testimony, inference and memory; each supplies at most an
  auxiliary condition and none yields denotation.
- Reciprocal debate: Mīmāṃsā alleges decomposition; Nyāya identifies similarity’s distinctive
  productive role. Nyāya alleges memory/inference; Mīmāṃsā says the absent-object relation is new.
- Reliable testimony needs knowledge, truth-intent, expressive competence and sentence fitness.
- Four sentence conditions: expectancy, fitness, proximity, intention.
- Nyāya sentence meaning: isolated meanings first, connection afterward.
- Prābhākara: meanings denoted only as connected; language learned from sentence-guided action.
- Memory is reproductive, hence not a pramāṇa.
- Nyāya: validity and invalidity both externally ascertained.
- Instrument/result: Nyāya causal distinction; Buddhism functional aspects of one cognition.

## Error

- Anyathākhyāti: two real terms, wrong location/synthesis.
- Shell present; silver impression activated; cognition-mediated presentation; non-discrimination.
- Bhāṭṭa: positive false identification, commonly viparītakhyāti; silver-memory contributes to
  “this is silver,” but no Nyāya-style extraordinary perception of elsewhere-silver is required.
- Contrast Prābhākara failure-to-discriminate, Yogācāra internal projection and Advaita’s
  indefinable error-object.
- Guardrail: do not treat the Bhāṭṭa label as uncontested or collapse it into Prābhākara akhyāti.
- Residual: special perception preserves realism but needs independent defence.

## Self and liberation

- Enduring self differs from body, senses and atomic mind.
- Exact six signs: desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain, cognition.
- Distinct force: wanting and rejection need an intentional subject; effort needs an agent rather
  than inert instruments; pleasure and pain need an experiencer; cognition needs a substance-locus.
- Pleasure, pain and cognition are episodic qualities, not the self’s essence, and do not persist
  as ordinary episodes in apavarga.
- Memory/recognition are supplementary.
- Atomic mind explains serial attention.
- Release chain: true knowledge → no error → no defects → no action → no birth → no suffering.
- Liberated self remains without ordinary cognition or standard positive bliss.
- Stone objection remains the main evaluative pressure.

## God and proofs

- God: eternal omniscient efficient cause, arranger and karmic governor; atoms remain material.
- Four named proofs: effect, arrangement, sustenance, scripture.
- Treat Udayana cumulatively but reconstruct each inference.
- Extra proofs cannot substitute for named proofs.
- Mīmāṃsā attacks Vedic authorship; Sāṃkhya invokes unconscious prakṛti; Buddhism attacks analogy.
- Karma replies to evil but constrains divine independence.
- Yoga God is special puruṣa and meditative support, not proved by the same full natural theology.

## Causation and atomism

- New production: effect does not pre-exist as that effect; not creation from nothing.
- Cause: invariable, unconditional, relevant antecedent.
- Five excluded antecedents show incidental, remote, co-effect, ubiquitous and accidental cases.
- Three causes: inherent/material, non-inherent, efficient; include absence of blockers.
- Prior absence supports novelty; Sāṃkhya replies with latent manifestation.
- Four elemental atom-types; atoms eternal, partless and imperceptible.
- Two atoms form dyad; three dyads form triad.
- First motion in later synthesis: God plus souls’ unseen moral force.
- Cyclic creation recombines; dissolution separates; atoms persist.
- Whole is a new entity over parts; reductionist pressure returns to inherence.

## Last-page examiner traps and answer spine

- Do not call Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika originally identical.
- Do not interchange sixteen inquiry-topics and seven ontological categories.
- Do not make consciousness the essence of Nyāya self.
- Do not call apavarga positive bliss without qualification.
- Do not confuse conjunction with inherence, or physical karma with moral karma.
- Do not say three atoms form a triad; three dyads do.
- Do not deny atyantābhāva when explaining “absolute negation impossible.”
- Do not attribute independent non-cognition to Nyāya.
- Do not confuse five inference-members with five sign-characteristics.
- Do not use a rigid one-to-one map between fallacy families and valid-sign marks.
- Do not make Nyāya comparison the Mīmāṃsā definition.
- Do not equate Nyāya semantics with Bhāṭṭa theory without qualification.
- Do not make God the material creator of eternal atoms.
- Write every answer as: **claim → mechanism/argument → example → objection → reply → residual →
  directive-shaped verdict**.
- Final graded line: **Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika gives Indian philosophy a powerful realist integration of
  ontology, epistemology and causal explanation; the same completeness depends on contested
  primitives—universal, inherence, particularity and special perception—that remain its deepest
  philosophical pressure points.**
