## 🖼️ Visual: Division of philosophical labour

```text
                     REALITY CAN BE KNOWN
                              │
             ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
             │                                 │
         NYĀYA                            VAIŚEṢIKA
   “How do we know?”                  “What really exists?”
             │                                 │

  inference · error · proof         qualities · relations · absence
             │                                 │

                            │
       Correct knowledge → removal of error → liberation
```

*Nyāya supplies the method of knowing; Vaiśeṣika supplies the inventory of reality.*

## 1. The common foundation: realism

✅ **Fact:** Both schools broadly defend **realism**—the external world exists independently of our cognition and can be validly known.

| Question | Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika answer |
|---|---|
| Does the external world exist? | ✅ Yes, independently of the knower |
| Are objects merely mental constructions? | ✅ No |
| Can knowledge correspond to reality? | ✅ Yes, through valid means of knowledge |
| Why does error occur? | ✅ Because cognition sometimes misidentifies an independently existing object |
| Why pursue knowledge? | ✅ Correct knowledge removes error and contributes to liberation |

⚠️ **Inference:** Their realism connects epistemology and metaphysics: a means of knowledge can be valid because there is an objective reality against which cognition may be tested.

---

## 2. Nyāya: the science of rational inquiry

✅ **Fact:** Nyāya is classically associated with **Gautama’s Nyāya-sūtra**.

✅ Its principal field is the theory of valid knowledge (**pramāṇa-śāstra**), including:

- valid cognition;
- perception and inference;
- testimony and comparison;
- doubt and reasoning;
- debate and demonstration;
- fallacious arguments;
- the theory of perceptual error.

Nyāya is therefore more than formal logic:

```text
Suffering
   ↓
False knowledge and confusion
   ↓
Disciplined inquiry using valid knowledge
   ↓
Knowledge of reality
   ↓
Removal of error
   ↓
Liberation (apavarga)
```

✅ **Fact:** Logic is spiritually significant because valid knowledge helps eliminate the ignorance responsible for suffering.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Nyāya is not logic pursued merely for argumentative victory; it is disciplined rational inquiry directed towards liberation.

---

## 3. Vaiśeṣika: the classification of reality

✅ **Fact:** Vaiśeṣika is classically associated with **Kaṇāda’s Vaiśeṣika-sūtra**.

✅ Its principal field is ontology: the systematic study of what exists.

It examines:

| Ontological problem | Vaiśeṣika response |
|---|---|
| What constitutes reality? | Knowable categories (**padārthas**) |
| What supports qualities and actions? | Substance (**dravya**) |
| How are individual objects classified? | Universals and particularity |
| How are inseparable entities connected? | Inherence (**samavāya**) |
| Is non-existence knowable? | Yes, as absence (**abhāva**) |
| How is the physical world formed? | Through combinations of eternal atoms |

✅ The name derives from the importance given to **ultimate particularity (viśeṣa)**, which distinguishes eternal entities that might otherwise appear qualitatively identical.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Vaiśeṣika constructs a pluralistic realist ontology by reducing the complexity of experience to fundamental categories of being.

---

## 4. Why are they studied together?

✅ **Fact:** Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika began as historically distinct but allied systems.

✅ They gradually converged because their doctrines were complementary:

```text
VAIŚEṢIKA: What exists?
                 +
NYĀYA: How is it known and proved?
                 ↓
A shared realist account of:
world · self · causation · atoms · liberation · later theism
```

| Primary ownership | Nyāya | Vaiśeṣika |
|---|---:|---:|
| Valid knowledge and inference | ✓ | |
| Debate and fallacies | ✓ | |
| Theory of error | ✓ | |
| Ontological categories | | ✓ |
| Atomism | | ✓ |
| Inherence and particularity | | ✓ |
| Self and liberation | ✓ | ✓ |
| Causation and later theism | ✓ | ✓ |

✅ Later **New Nyāya (Navya-Nyāya)**, especially associated with **Gaṅgeśa**, developed an exceptionally precise analytical vocabulary.

⚠️ **Historical qualification:** Do not project the complete later synthesis backward onto every early text.

---

## 5. Two category systems—do not confuse them

| Nyāya | Vaiśeṣika |
|---|---|
| Sixteen topics of rational inquiry | Seven categories of being |
| Organises investigation and debate | Classifies objective reality |
| Includes doubt, example and fallacy | Includes substance, quality and absence |
| Primarily methodological | Primarily ontological |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Nyāya investigates; Vaiśeṣika inventories.**

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Both traditions use the word *padārtha*, but their lists perform different functions. Nyāya’s sixteen are topics of inquiry; Vaiśeṣika’s seven are ontological categories.

---

## 6. Important theism qualification

✅ **Fact:** The developed Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika system accepts God (**Īśvara**) as the intelligent or efficient cause who orders eternal atoms.

⚠️ **Qualification:** Kaṇāda’s foundational text does not unambiguously foreground a creator God. Explicit natural theology belongs especially to later thinkers such as **Udayana** and the developed synthesis.

> **Safe answer:** “Later Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika is explicitly theistic,” rather than “every stage of early Vaiśeṣika taught the same creator doctrine.”

## 🖼️ Visual: Vaiśeṣika’s inventory of reality

```text
                    KNOWABLE REALITY
                  Category (padārtha)
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       │                  │                  │
   EXISTENTS          RELATIONAL         NON-EXISTENCE
       │              STRUCTURE               │
 ┌─────┼─────┐       ┌─────┼─────┐        Absence
 │     │     │       │     │     │        (abhāva)
Substance Quality Action Universal Particularity Inherence
(dravya) (guṇa) (karma) (sāmānya) (viśeṣa) (samavāya)
```

*The seven categories explain objects, their properties, movement, classification, individuality, inseparable relations and absence.*

## 1. Meaning of category (padārtha)

✅ **Fact:** A category (**padārtha**) is literally that which can be:

```text
Named (abhidheya)
      +
Known (jñeya)
      +
Objectively existent (astitva)
```

✅ Vaiśeṣika eventually accepts **seven categories**:

| Category | Meaning | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Substance (**dravya**) | independent substratum | Supports qualities and actions |
| Quality (**guṇa**) | dependent property | Characterises a substance |
| Action (**karma**) | physical movement | Produces conjunction and separation |
| Universal (**sāmānya**) | one common nature in many | Grounds classification |
| Particularity (**viśeṣa**) | ultimate differentiator | Individuates eternal entities |
| Inherence (**samavāya**) | inseparable relation | Connects entities that cannot exist separately |
| Absence (**abhāva**) | objective non-existence | Grounds negative judgments |

⚠️ **Historical qualification:** The earlier system is commonly described as accepting six positive categories. Absence (**abhāva**) was explicitly established as the seventh category later.

---

# 2. Substance (dravya)

✅ **Definition:** Substance is the substratum in which qualities and actions inhere.

```text
                 POT — SUBSTANCE
                       │
          ┌────────────┼────────────┐
          │            │            │
       brown        circular      moving
      QUALITY        QUALITY       ACTION
```

A colour cannot float independently. It must be the colour **of something**. Similarly, movement must be the movement **of a substance**.

## Nine substances

| Type | Substance | Distinctive point |
|---|---|---|
| Material element | Earth (**pṛthivī**) | Associated characteristically with smell |
| Material element | Water (**ap**) | Associated characteristically with taste |
| Material element | Fire (**tejas**) | Associated characteristically with colour |
| Material element | Air (**vāyu**) | Associated characteristically with touch |
| Non-atomic element | Ether (**ākāśa**) | Substratum of sound |
| All-pervasive condition | Time (**kāla**) | Grounds temporal relations |
| All-pervasive condition | Space/direction (**dik**) | Grounds spatial relations |
| Conscious subject | Self (**ātman**) | Substratum of cognition and other mental qualities |
| Internal organ | Mind (**manas**) | Connects the self selectively with the senses |

### Important classification

```text
Nine substances
      │
      ├── Physical elements: earth, water, fire, air, ether
      ├── Ordering conditions: time and space
      ├── Knower: self
      └── Internal instrument: mind
```

✅ Earth, water, fire and air possess eternal atoms, while their composite objects are non-eternal.

✅ Ether, time, space and self are all-pervasive rather than atomic.

✅ Mind is eternal and atomic, but not a physical element like earth or water.

⚠️ **UPSC traps**

- Ether, time and space are **substances**, not merely abstract concepts.
- Mind and self are **different substances**.
- Consciousness is not the eternal essence of the Nyāya self; it arises as a **quality of the self** under appropriate conditions.
- A composite pot is not eternal merely because its constituent atoms are eternal.

---

# 3. Quality (guṇa)

✅ **Definition:** A quality is a dependent feature that inheres in a substance but cannot independently possess action.

```text
SUBSTANCE
   │
   ├── possesses QUALITY
   │       └── dependent and inactive
   │
   └── performs ACTION
           └── produces conjunction or separation
```

✅ The developed system recognises **twenty-four qualities**.

| Group | Important qualities |
|---|---|
| Sensory | Colour, taste, smell, touch and sound |
| Quantitative | Number and magnitude |
| Relational | Distinctness, conjunction, separation, priority and posteriority |
| Psychological | Cognition, pleasure, pain, desire, aversion and effort |
| Physical | Heaviness, fluidity, viscidity and disposition/momentum |
| Moral | Merit (**dharma**) and demerit (**adharma**) |

## Philosophical significance

✅ Cognition, pleasure, pain and desire are classified as qualities of the self.

Therefore:

```text
Self ≠ consciousness itself
Self = enduring substance
Consciousness = episodic quality arising in the self
```

This distinguishes Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika from Advaita Vedānta, where consciousness is treated as the self’s essential nature.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika converts psychological states into ontological qualities located in an enduring self.

---

# 4. Action (karma)

⚠️ Here, **action (karma)** means **physical motion**, not the moral law of karma.

✅ Action belongs only to substances and produces conjunction or separation.

## Five kinds of action

| Action | Example |
|---|---|
| Upward movement (**utkṣepaṇa**) | Throwing a ball upward |
| Downward movement (**avakṣepaṇa**) | A fruit falling |
| Contraction (**ākuñcana**) | Bending the arm |
| Expansion (**prasāraṇa**) | Straightening the arm |
| General locomotion (**gamana**) | Walking or displacement |

## Why action matters

```text
Action
  ↓
Conjunction or separation
  ↓
New arrangement of substances
  ↓
Production or destruction of composites
```

✅ Action is therefore essential to the Vaiśeṣika explanation of physical change and atomistic creation.

---

# 5. Substance, quality and action compared

| Test | Substance | Quality | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can exist as a substratum? | Yes | No | No |
| Can support qualities? | Yes | No | No |
| Can possess motion? | Yes | No | It is motion |
| Produces conjunction/separation? | Indirectly | No | Yes |
| Example | Moving red ball | Redness | Movement |

### The moving red ball

```text
Ball       → substance (dravya)
Redness    → quality (guṇa)
Movement   → action (karma)
```

The categories are irreducible: the ball, its redness and its movement perform different ontological roles.

---

## 6. Why seven categories?

⚠️ **Inference:** The system attempts to explain every ordinary judgment:

| Judgment | Category required |
|---|---|
| “This is a pot.” | Substance |
| “The pot is brown.” | Quality |
| “The pot is moving.” | Action |
| “This and that are both pots.” | Universal |
| “This eternal atom differs from another.” | Particularity |
| “Brownness is inseparable from this brown object.” | Inherence |
| “There is no pot here.” | Absence |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **S-Q-A-U-P-I-A**
> **Substance–Quality–Action–Universal–Particularity–Inherence–Absence**

## 🖼️ Visual: Four explanatory problems

```text
Many cows share one nature ──────────→ UNIVERSAL (sāmānya)
Identical eternal atoms remain many ─→ PARTICULARITY (viśeṣa)
Colour cannot separate from its object → INHERENCE (samavāya)
“No jar is on the table” is meaningful → ABSENCE (abhāva)
```

# 1. Universal (sāmānya)

✅ **Definition:** A universal is an eternal, objective common nature that exists in many individual objects.

```text
 Cow A ─┐
 Cow B ─┼── cow-ness (gotva)
 Cow C ─┘
```

✅ Individual cows differ in colour, size and age, yet all are correctly recognised as cows. Vaiśeṣika explains this common classification through the real universal **cow-ness**.

| Feature | Universal |
|---|---|
| Number | One |
| Duration | Eternal |
| Location | Present in many individuals |
| Function | Grounds common classification and predication |
| Relation to instances | Inherence (**samavāya**) |

> 🔑 **Answer line:** A universal is “one in many”—numerically one but instantiated in multiple particulars.

### Why not mere resemblance?

```text
Claim: Cows are grouped because they resemble one another.
Problem: Resemblance itself requires a common basis.
Reply: Cow-ness objectively grounds the resemblance.
```

### Buddhist objection

✅ Buddhist exclusion theory (**apoha**) rejects independently real universals. “Cow” means the exclusion of non-cows rather than participation in a metaphysical cow-ness.

✅ **Nyāya reply:** Excluding non-cows appears to presuppose a stable basis for identifying cows. Universal realism better explains positive recognition and repeated classification.

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Not every general expression denotes a genuine universal. Vaiśeṣika uses restrictions on universalhood to prevent unlimited multiplication of entities.

---

# 2. Ultimate particularity (viśeṣa)

✅ **Definition:** Ultimate particularity is the self-differentiating principle that distinguishes numerically different eternal substances.

Imagine two earth-atoms:

```text
Atom A: eternal + indivisible + earth-atom
Atom B: eternal + indivisible + earth-atom
                    │
         Same class and properties
                    ↓
Why are they two rather than one?
                    ↓
Ultimate particularity (viśeṣa)
```

✅ It resides only in eternal substances:

- atoms of earth, water, fire and air;
- ether;
- time;
- space;
- selves;
- minds.

✅ It does not individuate ordinary composite objects because their distinguishable parts, qualities and positions already explain their difference.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Universal unites; particularity divides.**

| Universal | Ultimate particularity |
|---|---|
| Explains similarity | Explains numerical difference |
| One in many | Unique to each eternal entity |
| Grounds classification | Grounds individuation |
| Present in ordinary individuals | Restricted to eternal substances |

### Regress objection

If particularity differentiates atoms, what differentiates one particularity from another?

✅ **Reply:** Particularities are **self-differentiating (svato-vyāvartaka)**. No additional differentiator is needed.

⚠️ **Criticism:** Calling them self-differentiating may merely stop the regress by definition rather than solve it independently.

---

# 3. Inherence (samavāya)

✅ **Definition:** Inherence is the eternal and inseparable relation connecting entities that cannot exist separately in the relevant ontological form.

## 🖼️ Inherence versus conjunction

```text
BROWN COLOUR IN A TABLE             BOOK ON A TABLE
          │                               │
  cannot be detached               can be removed intact
          │                               │
 INHERENCE (samavāya)            CONJUNCTION (saṃyoga)
```

| Feature | Inherence (**samavāya**) | Conjunction (**saṃyoga**) |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | Inseparable | Separable contact |
| Status | Independent category | Quality |
| Duration | Eternal as a relation | Produced and destructible |
| Example | Brownness in a table | Book on a table |
| Separation | Destruction of the dependent term | Ordinary removal |

## Five principal loci of inherence

1. **Part and whole** — threads in cloth.
2. **Substance and quality** — brownness in a table.
3. **Substance and action** — movement in a ball.
4. **Universal and individual** — cow-ness in a cow.
5. **Eternal substance and particularity** — a unique particularity in an atom.

### Why is inherence needed?

Suppose cloth were merely externally connected to its threads:

```text
Threads placed together ≠ automatically a new whole
                         ↓
We nevertheless perceive one cloth
                         ↓
Inherence explains the whole’s inseparable presence in its parts
```

### Relation-regress objection

If inherence connects colour and substance, must another relation connect inherence to them?

✅ **Reply:** Inherence is a **self-linking relation (svarūpa-sambandha)** and requires no further relation.

⚠️ **Critical evaluation:** This stops an infinite regress, but critics argue that the stopping point is stipulated.

> 🔑 **Exam distinction:** Inseparability is necessary for inherence, but mere closeness or difficulty of separation is insufficient. The relation must express genuine ontological dependence.

---

# 4. Absence (abhāva)

✅ **Definition:** Absence is an objective negative fact—the non-existence of something in a specified locus.

In the judgment:

> “There is no jar on the table.”

```text
Table → locus (anuyogin)
Jar   → counterpositive, the absent object (pratiyogin)
```

Absence is therefore not empty nothingness. It possesses a definite structure: **the absence of X in Y**.

## Four kinds of absence

### Timeline visual

```text
Before production       Object exists       After destruction
───────────────────┬────────────────────┬──────────────────────
 Prior absence     │                    │ Posterior absence
 (prāgabhāva)      │                    │ (pradhvaṃsābhāva)
 Beginningless     │                    │ Begins at destruction
 Ends at production│                    │ Endless
```

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prior absence (**prāgabhāva**) | Effect does not exist before production | Pot before the potter produces it |
| Posterior absence (**pradhvaṃsābhāva**) | Thing does not exist after destruction | Pot after it is smashed |
| Absolute absence (**atyantābhāva**) | Something is always absent from a locus | Colour in air, under the relevant doctrine |
| Mutual absence (**anyonyābhāva**) | One entity is not another | A pot is not a cloth |

## Key temporal distinction

| Absence | Beginning | End |
|---|---|---|
| Prior absence | None | Production |
| Posterior absence | Destruction | None |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Prior ends; posterior begins.**

## Absolute versus mutual absence

- **“Air does not possess heat”** expresses the absence of a property in a locus: absolute absence.
- **“Air is not fire”** expresses the difference between two entities: mutual absence.

⚠️ They are not the same negative judgment.

## How is absence known?

✅ Nyāya generally explains perceptible absence through perception rather than accepting non-cognition (**anupalabdhi**) as an independent means of knowledge.

```text
I inspect the visible table
          +
A jar would be perceptible if present
          +
No obstruction prevents observation
          ↓
Direct cognition: “The table is jar-less”
```

Necessary conditions include:

- the locus is perceptible;
- the observer’s senses are functioning;
- the absent object would have been perceptible;
- no obstruction conceals it.

⚠️ **School distinction:** Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā accepts non-cognition as an independent means of valid knowledge; Nyāya usually reduces absence-knowledge to perception or inference.

# 1. What is valid knowledge?

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|

| Object (**prameya**) | What is known |

> 🔑 **Simple analogy:** Eyes are the instrument; seeing the flower correctly is the result.

# 2. Perception (pratyakṣa)

✅ Perception is cognition produced through the appropriate relation between a sense faculty and its object.

```text
Flower → eye → mind → self
                    ↓
            “This is a flower”
```

Examples:

- seeing a blue pot;
- hearing a bell;
- touching a warm surface;
- perceiving the absence of a jar on a visible table under suitable conditions.

✅ It gives comparatively direct knowledge rather than knowledge mediated by a sign or verbal statement.

⚠️ **Qualification:** Genuine perception must be non-errant and definite. Illusion does not count as valid perception merely because it feels perceptual.

We will study its definition and classifications in **Subtopic 5**.

---

# 3. Inference (anumāna)

✅ Inference is knowledge of an unperceived fact through a sign known to be invariably connected with it.

```text
Smoke perceived on hill
          +
Smoke is invariably connected with fire
          ↓
Fire inferred on hill
```

| Element | Hill example |
|---|---|
| Subject (**pakṣa**) | Hill |
| Probandum (**sādhya**) | Fire to be proved |
| Reason (**hetu**) | Smoke |
| Universal relation (**vyāpti**) | Wherever there is smoke, there is fire |
| Conclusion | The hill has fire |

⚠️ Merely observing smoke is insufficient. The knower must already understand its unconditional relation with fire.

We will study inference, universal concomitance and fallacies in **Subtopic 6**.

---

# 4. Comparison (upamāna)

✅ Comparison produces knowledge of the relation between a word and its referent through perceived similarity.

## Classical wild-ox example

```text
Forester says:
“A wild ox (gavaya) resembles a cow.”
                 ↓
Traveller later sees a cow-like wild animal
                 ↓
Recognises:
“This is what the word ‘gavaya’ denotes.”
```

The result is not merely:

> “This animal resembles a cow.”

It is:

> “This animal is the referent of the previously explained word ‘gavaya’.”

✅ Comparison therefore performs a distinctive role in **semantic acquisition**.

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Upamāna is not ordinary analogy used in an argument. It specifically establishes a word–object relation through resemblance.

---

# 5. Testimony (śabda)

✅ Testimony is valid verbal knowledge arising from the instruction of a reliable speaker (**āpta**).

## Reliable speaker test

```text
Correct knowledge of subject
              +
Intention to communicate truthfully
              +
Ability to communicate clearly
              ↓
Reliable speaker (āpta)
```

⚠️ Authority, prestige or confidence alone does not make someone epistemically reliable.

## Why testimony is independent

Much of our knowledge cannot be individually perceived or inferred:

- distant places;
- historical events;
- another person’s direct experience;
- specialised technical knowledge;
- matters communicated through reliable records.

Rejecting all testimony would make ordinary and scholarly life impossible.

## Conditions for understanding a sentence

| Condition | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Expectancy (**ākāṅkṣā**) | Words require one another to complete the sense | “Bring…” expects an object |
| Semantic fitness (**yogyatā**) | Meanings must be mutually compatible | “Sprinkle with water,” not “sprinkle with fire” |
| Proximity (**sannidhi/āsatti**) | Words must occur sufficiently together | Not disconnected across unrelated contexts |
| Speaker’s intention (**tātparya**) | Intended meaning resolves ambiguity | “Bank” as riverbank or financial institution |

✅ Testimony can be:

- secular (**laukika**) or Vedic (**vaidika**);
- about perceptible objects or imperceptible matters.

---

# 6. Why memory is not a pramāṇa

✅ Memory (**smṛti**) reproduces an earlier cognition; it does not independently disclose its object anew.

```text
Yesterday: perceive the monument
                ↓
Today: remember the monument
                ↓
Memory depends upon yesterday’s cognition
```

Therefore, memory may be true and useful without being an independent means of valid knowledge.

> 🔑 **Distinction:** Every pramāṇa produces knowledge, but every mental episode that preserves knowledge is not a pramāṇa.

---

# 7. Why only four?

Other schools accept additional means:

| Proposed means | Meaning | Nyāya treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Postulation (**arthāpatti**) | Inferring an explanatory fact | Reducible to inference |
| Non-cognition (**anupalabdhi**) | Knowing absence through non-apprehension | Reducible to perception or inference |
| Presumption/inclusion (**sambhava**) | Knowing the included from the containing quantity | Reducible to inference |
| Tradition (**aitihya**) | Anonymous report | Reliable testimony or epistemically defective report |

✅ Classical Vaiśeṣika initially accepts only **perception and inference**, reducing comparison and testimony to inference.

✅ The later combined Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika system generally employs Nyāya’s fourfold scheme.

---

# 8. How is validity known?

Nyāya accepts **externally established validity (parataḥ-prāmāṇya)**.

```text
Initial cognition: “There is water ahead.”
                      ↓
Successful action: approach and obtain water
                      ↓
Later confirmation establishes validity
```

Similarly:

```text
Initial cognition: “There is water ahead.”
                      ↓
Approach reveals mirage
                      ↓
Later contradiction establishes invalidity
```

✅ For Nyāya, both validity and invalidity are known through subsequent confirmation or disconfirmation.

| School | Validity | Invalidity |
|---|---|---|
| Nyāya | Externally known | Externally known |
| Mīmāṃsā, broadly | Intrinsic | Externally discovered |

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Nyāya does not accept externality only for error. It treats **both validity and invalidity** as externally ascertained.

| Means | Immediate basis | Knowledge produced |

| Perception | Appropriate sense–object relation | “This flower is red.” |
| Inference | Sign and universal concomitance | “The hill has fire.” |
| Comparison | Similarity plus prior description | “This is a gavaya.” |
| Testimony | Reliable meaningful statement | “The city lies beyond this mountain.” |

# 1. Definition of perception

✅ Gautama defines perception (**pratyakṣa**) substantially as:

> Knowledge produced by sense–object contact, non-verbal, non-errant and determinate.

The Sanskrit formulation is:

> **indriyārtha-sannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam**

## Clause-by-clause explanation

| Clause | Meaning | What it excludes |
|---|---|---|
| Sense–object contact (**indriyārtha-sannikarṣa**) | Appropriate relation between a sense and its object | Inference and testimony |
| Produced (**utpannam**) | Cognition must arise from that relation | Memory |
| Non-verbal (**avyapadeśyam**) | Not produced merely through words | Verbal testimony |
| Non-errant (**avyabhicāri**) | Corresponds correctly to its object | Illusion |
| Determinate (**vyavasāyātmakam**) | Definite rather than doubtful | Uncertainty |

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Gautama defines perception by its cause, independence from words and truth-character.

---

# 2. Why “contact” does not mean physical touching

“Sense–object contact” is a technical expression. It includes several relations through which a sense apprehends:

- substances;
- their qualities;
- their universals;
- sound;
- absence.

For example, the eye may be connected with a flower, but its colour exists **in** the flower. Nyāya therefore needs indirect relational chains to explain how colour is seen.

---

# 3. Indeterminate and determinate perception

## 🖼️ Two-stage model

```text
STAGE 1: Indeterminate perception (nirvikalpaka)
                   “Something”
                       │
       No explicit name, class or predication
                       ↓
STAGE 2: Determinate perception (savikalpaka)
              “This is a blue pot”
                       │
     Subject + property + class + relation
```

## Indeterminate perception (nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa)

✅ It is pre-predicative awareness before an object is explicitly classified or described.

```text
Object is presented
but not yet judged as:
“this object possessing blueness and pot-ness”
```

It supplies the material later organised into determinate cognition.

## Determinate perception (savikalpaka-pratyakṣa)

✅ It apprehends a qualified object in a structured and nameable form:

> “This is a blue pot.”

| Component | Cognised content |
|---|---|
| Qualified subject (**viśeṣya**) | This pot |
| Qualifier (**viśeṣaṇa**) | Blueness |
| Qualification relation | Blueness belonging to this pot |
| Universal | Pot-ness |

⚠️ **Conceptual tension:** Gautama calls perception both “non-verbal” and “determinate.” Later Naiyāyikas explain that perception need not be **caused by words**, even when its determinate content can subsequently be expressed through words.

> 🔑 **Distinction:** Determinate perception is verbally expressible, but it is not necessarily verbally produced.

---

# 4. Ordinary perception

Ordinary perception (**laukika-pratyakṣa**) operates through standard sense–object relations.

```text
                 ORDINARY PERCEPTION
                         │
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             │                       │
          External                Internal
     Five external senses      Mind apprehending
                               pleasure, pain,
                               desire and cognition
```

✅ External perception employs sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch.

✅ Internal perception involves the mind apprehending psychological qualities such as pleasure, pain, desire and cognition in the self.

---

# 5. Six sense–object relations

## Relational ladder

```text
1. Sense ─ contact ─ object
2. Sense ─ contact ─ object ─ inherence ─ quality
3. Sense ─ contact ─ object ─ inherence ─ quality ─ inherence ─ universal
4. Sense ─ inherence ─ sound
5. Sense ─ inherence ─ sound ─ inherence ─ sound-universal
6. Visible locus ─ qualified by ─ absence
```

| Relation | What is apprehended | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Conjunction (**saṃyoga**) | Substance | Eye sees a pot |
| Inherence in the conjoined (**saṃyukta-samavāya**) | Quality or action | Eye sees the pot’s colour |
| Inherence in what inheres in the conjoined (**saṃyukta-samaveta-samavāya**) | Universal of a quality | Eye apprehends colour-ness through colour |
| Inherence (**samavāya**) | Sound | Auditory faculty apprehends sound |
| Inherence in what inheres (**samaveta-samavāya**) | Universal of sound | Auditory faculty apprehends sound-ness |
| Qualifier–qualified relation (**viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣya-bhāva**) | Absence | Eye perceives the table as jar-less |

## Example: seeing a red rose

```text
Eye
 │
 ├── conjunction with rose
 │        → rose perceived
 │
 ├── connected-inherence with redness
 │        → redness perceived
 │
 └── connected-inherited-inherence with colour-ness
          → universal colour-ness perceived
```

⚠️ The terminology is difficult, but the governing principle is simple:

> The sense contacts the substance directly and reaches dependent properties through their inherence in that substance.

# 6. Perception of absence

When you see an empty table:

```text
Eye perceives table
        ↓
Table is presented as qualified by jar-absence
        ↓
“There is no jar on the table”
```

✅ The eye does not contact “nothing.” It contacts the visible locus—the table—and apprehends it as qualified by an absence.

Necessary conditions include:

- the table is visible;
- the senses are functioning;
- a jar would be visible if present;
- no curtain or obstruction conceals it.

---

# 7. Extraordinary perception

Extraordinary perception (**alaukika-pratyakṣa**) is not produced through the ordinary six relations alone.

| Type | Meaning | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Universal-mediated perception (**sāmānyalakṣaṇa**) | Apprehension mediated by a universal | Through perceived smoke-ness, cognition extends to smoke as a class |
| Cognition-mediated perception (**jñānalakṣaṇa**) | Previous cognition mediates present experience | “Seeing” fragrant sandalwood as fragrant |
| Yogic perception (**yogaja**) | Extraordinary yogic apprehension | Cognition of subtle, distant, past or future objects |

## Universal-mediated perception

✅ Nyāya treats universals as perceptible rather than merely inferred.

```text
See an individual cow
         ↓
Apprehend cow-ness
         ↓
Cognition reaches the class of cows through the universal
```

⚠️ Critics question how one perceived instance could perceptually connect us to all instances.

## Cognition-mediated perception

Suppose you have previously smelled sandalwood:

```text
Present sight of sandalwood
           +
Past cognition of its fragrance
           ↓
Experience: “fragrant sandalwood”
```

The fragrance is not presently contacted by the eye. Previous cognition mediates the present quasi-perceptual experience.

✅ This becomes essential to Nyāya’s theory of illusion (**anyathākhyāti**).

## Yogic perception

✅ Nyāya accepts that advanced yogins can perceive objects ordinarily inaccessible because they are subtle, distant, past or future.

⚠️ For critical evaluation, this category is difficult to verify independently and depends upon broader yogic assumptions.

---

# 8. Perception and error

A misleading experience is not valid perception merely because it arises vividly.

```text
Shell appears as silver
        ↓
Cognition does not correspond to present object
        ↓
Excluded by “non-errant” clause
        ↓
Explained separately through theory of error
```

Nyāya therefore distinguishes:

| Experience | Status |
|---|---|
| “This is a shell,” when a shell is present | Valid perception |
| “This is silver,” when a shell is present | Erroneous cognition |
| “Is this silver or shell?” | Doubt |
| Remembering a previously seen shell | Memory |

## 🖼️ Visual: Complete inferential mechanism

```text
See smoke on hill
       ↓
```

> 🔑 Inference requires **a sign in the subject + its universal relation with what must be proved**.

# 1. Structure of inference

✅ Inference (**anumāna**) is knowledge of an unperceived fact through a sign known to be invariably related to it.

| Element | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject (**pakṣa**) | Where something is proved | Hill |
| Probandum (**sādhya**) | Property to be proved | Fire |
| Reason (**hetu**) | Perceived sign | Smoke |
| Universal concomitance (**vyāpti**) | Unconditional relation | Wherever smoke exists, fire exists |
| Positive example (**sapakṣa**) | Similar case containing both | Kitchen |
| Negative example (**vipakṣa**) | Case lacking the probandum and reason | Lake |
| Conclusion | Inferential cognition | Hill has fire |

---

# 2. Five-member demonstration

The five-member demonstration (**pañcāvayava**) is mainly an **inference for another person (parārthānumāna)**.

```text
1. Thesis         The hill has fire.
2. Reason         Because it has smoke.
3. Example        Wherever there is smoke, there is fire,
                  as in a kitchen.
4. Application    This hill possesses smoke of that kind.
5. Conclusion     Therefore, the hill has fire.
```

| Member | Sanskrit term | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | **pratijñā** | States what must be proved |
| Reason | **hetu** | Supplies the inferential sign |
| Example | **udāharaṇa** | States concomitance with an accepted instance |
| Application | **upanaya** | Applies the general relation to the subject |
| Conclusion | **nigamana** | Restates the established thesis |

## Why five members?

```text
Thesis alone       → unsupported assertion
Reason alone       → relation not demonstrated
Example            → establishes general relation
Application        → connects relation with present case
Conclusion         → completes communicated proof
```

⚠️ This is not merely an expanded Aristotelian syllogism. It explicitly includes an example and application because it is designed for public demonstration and debate.

---

# 3. Inference for oneself and another

| Type | Function |
|---|---|
| Inference for oneself (**svārthānumāna**) | Internal reasoning producing one’s own conclusion |
| Inference for another (**parārthānumāna**) | Communicated proof using five members |

✅ One does not normally recite five propositions internally whenever one sees smoke. The expanded form makes the reasoning inspectable to another person.

---

# 4. Three causal forms

| Form | Movement of reasoning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| From cause to effect (**pūrvavat**) | Known cause → unperceived effect | Dark clouds → future rain |
| From effect to cause (**śeṣavat**) | Known effect → unperceived cause | Swollen muddy river → previous rain |
| Based on general uniformity (**sāmānyatodṛṣṭa**) | Non-causal regularity | Changed celestial position → motion |

⚠️ Some texts also discuss **residual inference (pariśeṣa)** under *śeṣavat*. State the classification you are using rather than combining different textbook schemes.

---

# 5. Universal concomitance (vyāpti)

✅ Universal concomitance is the invariable and unconditional relation between the reason and what it proves.

```text
All smoke is accompanied by fire.
But
All fire is not accompanied by smoke.
```

Therefore:

```text
Smoke → Fire       valid direction
Fire  ⇏ Smoke      invalid without qualification
```

This is **unequal pervasion**: smoke is narrower than fire.

> 🔑 **UPSC trap:** Concomitance has a direction. Reversing it commits an illicit conversion.

## Not mere repeated association

Seeing smoke with fire many times does not by itself prove universal concomitance.

Nyāya requires:

```text
Repeated positive observation
          +
Repeated negative observation
          +
No counterexample
          +
Elimination of hidden conditions
          +
Reasoning that removes residual doubt
          ↓
Warranted cognition of universal concomitance
```

---

# 6. Positive and negative agreement

## Agreement in presence (anvaya)

```text
Kitchen: smoke + fire
Hearth:  smoke + fire
Furnace: smoke + fire
```

## Agreement in absence (vyatireka)

```text
Lake: no fire → no smoke
Open sky: no fire → no smoke
```

⚠️ Finite observations remain vulnerable to an undiscovered exception. Nyāya therefore adds the elimination of conditioning factors.

---

# 7. Conditioning adjunct (upādhi)

A conditioning adjunct is a hidden condition that makes an apparent universal relation conditional rather than genuine.

Consider the reverse inference:

> “This place has smoke because it has fire.”

This fails because smokeless fire is possible.

```text
Fire + wet combustible material → smoke
Fire without that condition     → may be smokeless
```

The relevant fuel condition explains why fire does not universally entail smoke.

| Proposed relation | Result |
|---|---|
| Smoke → fire | Treated as unconditional |
| Fire → smoke | Conditional and invalid |
| Fire with suitable smoky fuel → smoke | Qualified relation |

> 🔑 **Rule:** A genuine universal concomitance must be **unconditioned (anaupādhika)**.

---

# 8. Hypothetical reasoning (tarka)

✅ Hypothetical reasoning removes doubt by demonstrating the unacceptable consequence of a contrary assumption.

```text
Suppose smoke were not invariably related to fire
                       ↓
Smoke could occur entirely without fire
                       ↓
Contradicts systematically tested experience
                       ↓
Contrary supposition rejected
```

⚠️ Hypothetical reasoning is an **aid to valid knowledge**, not a fifth independent means.

It helps expose:

- self-dependence;
- circularity;
- mutual dependence;
- infinite regress;
- contradiction with perception or established inference.

---

# 9. Subsumptive reflection (parāmarśa)

The immediate trigger of inferential cognition is not smoke alone but a structured reflection:

> “This hill possesses smoke that is invariably pervaded by fire.”

```text
Smoke on hill             → knowledge of reason in subject
Smoke pervaded by fire    → recalled universal relation
Combined reflection       → subsumptive reflection
                            ↓
                       Fire inferred
```

✅ This combines:

1. the reason’s presence in the subject; and
2. the reason’s universal relation with the probandum.

---

# 10. Five conditions of a valid reason

| Condition | Requirement | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Presence in subject (**pakṣadharmatā**) | Smoke exists on the hill | Unproved reason |
| Presence in positive instances (**sapakṣa-sattva**) | Smoke occurs in fiery kitchens | Irregularity |
| Absence from negative instances (**vipakṣa-asattva**) | Smoke is absent where fire is absent | Irregularity |
| Not contradicted (**abādhita**) | Fire is not defeated by stronger knowledge | Stultification |
| No equal counter-reason (**asatpratipakṣa**) | No equally strong reason proves no fire | Counterbalance |

> 🔑 A valid reason must be **present, positively supported, negatively controlled, undefeated and unopposed**.

---

# 11. Five major fallacies of the reason

## Fallacy map

```text
Reason fails
   │
   ├── Relation irregular ─────────→ Irregular reason
   ├── Proves the opposite ────────→ Contradictory reason
   ├── Equally opposed ────────────→ Counterbalanced reason
   ├── Not established ────────────→ Unproved reason
   └── Defeated by stronger knowledge → Stultified reason
```

| Fallacy | Meaning | Example structure |
|---|---|---|
| Irregular (**savyabhicāra**) | Reason occurs without what it supposedly proves | “This animal is a cow because it has horns” |
| Contradictory (**viruddha**) | Reason proves the opposite conclusion | A property supporting impermanence used to prove permanence |
| Counterbalanced (**satpratipakṣa**) | Equally strong reason proves the contrary | One inference proves X; another equally strong one proves not-X |
| Unproved (**asiddha**) | Reason is absent or itself unestablished | “Sky-lotus is fragrant because it is a lotus” |
| Stultified (**bādhita**) | Stronger knowledge contradicts the conclusion | “Fire is cold because it is a substance” |

## Link with valid-reason conditions

| Fallacy | Condition violated |
|---|---|
| Unproved | Presence in subject |
| Irregular | Positive or negative distribution |
| Counterbalanced | Absence of counter-reason |
| Stultified | Non-contradiction |
| Contradictory | Correct direction of concomitance |

```text
             ↓

             ↓
Finite observation cannot guarantee universality
             ↓

             ↓

```

2. Counterexamples are actively sought.
3. Hidden conditions are systematically eliminated.

5. Real universals make general cognition possible.
6. A blanket assertion that “all inference is invalid” itself appears to rely upon inference or universalisation.

⚠️ **Critical assessment:** Nyāya offers warranted empirical knowledge, not mathematical certainty. Its strongest reply depends upon accepting its realism about universals.

## 🖼️ Visual: Shell–silver illusion

```text
        │                                  │
        ↓                                  ↓
```

# 1. What exactly is an error?

✅ Nyāya explains illusion through **misapprehension elsewhere (anyathākhyāti)**:

- **anyathā** — elsewhere or otherwise;
- **khyāti** — cognition or apprehension.

✅ In the shell–silver illusion:

| Component | Ontological status |
|---|---|
| Present shell | Real |
| Silver previously encountered elsewhere | Real |
| Present awareness of “this” | Real cognition of the shell-locus |
| Memory trace of silver | Based on a real previous object |
| Judgment “this is silver” | False synthesis |

> 🔑 **Core doctrine:** The components of error are real; the error lies in combining them under the wrong spatial attribution.

Nyāya does **not** claim that nonexistent silver is created on the beach.

---

# 2. Complete mechanism

## Stage 1: Defective perception of the shell

Due to distance, poor light or sensory defect, the shell’s distinctive features are not clearly apprehended.

```text
Shell perceived only vaguely as “this”
```

## Stage 2: Activation of a memory trace

The shell’s brightness resembles previously perceived silver.

```text
Brightness → awakens memory of silver
```

## Stage 3: Cognition-mediated perception

Previous cognition mediates a presentative awareness of silver.

✅ Through **cognition-mediated perception (jñānalakṣaṇa-pratyakṣa)**, silver is experienced with perceptual vividness even though it is not physically present there.

## Stage 4: Failure of discrimination

The mind fails to distinguish:

```text
“This” belongs to the presently perceived shell
Silver belongs to an object encountered elsewhere
```

## Stage 5: False qualified judgment

The subject and predicate are wrongly united:

> “This is silver.”

The error is corrected by closer perception:

> “This is shell, not silver.”

---

# 3. Why memory alone is insufficient

Ordinary memory presents its object as past:

> “I previously saw silver.”

Illusion presents silver as existing **here and now**:

> “This is silver.”

Therefore, Nyāya introduces cognition-mediated perception to explain how remembered silver acquires presentative force.

| Ordinary memory | Illusion |
|---|---|
| “That silver was previously perceived.” | “This object is silver.” |
| Object presented as past or absent | Object presented as currently located here |
| No immediate action toward present locus | Subject may reach for the apparent silver |

> 🔑 Cognition-mediated perception bridges the gap between recollection and vivid misperception.

---

# 4. Error preserves realism

```text
Real present locus: shell
           +
Real remembered object: silver elsewhere
           +
Real causal defect
           ↓
False relation between real items
```

✅ Nyāya explains error without declaring the external world unreal.

⚠️ **Inference:** Illusion proves that cognition can misrepresent reality, not that reality is itself indeterminate or mind-dependent.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Nyāya relocates error from the object’s existence to the cognition’s relational structure.

---

# 5. Rival theories of error

| Theory | School | What appears in illusion? |
|---|---|---|
| Misapprehension elsewhere (**anyathākhyāti**) | Nyāya | Real silver existing elsewhere is mislocated here |
| Non-apprehension (**akhyāti**) | Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā | Failure to distinguish present perception from memory |
| Self-apprehension (**ātmakhyāti**) | Yogācāra | Internal cognition appears as an external object |
| Apprehension of non-being (**asatkhyāti**) | Standard Mādhyamika doxography | A nonexistent object is apprehended |
| Indefinable appearance (**anirvacanīya-khyāti**) | Advaita Vedānta | Error-object is neither real nor unreal |

## Nyāya versus Prābhākara

Both recognise:

- perception of the shell-locus;
- memory of silver;
- failure of discrimination.

However:

| Nyāya | Prābhākara |
|---|---|
| Accepts a positively false relational cognition | Explains error mainly as non-discrimination |
| “This is silver” is erroneous | Perception and memory are individually valid but not distinguished |
| Uses cognition-mediated perception | Does not require the same presentative mechanism |

## Nyāya versus Advaita

Advaita treats the apparent silver as **indefinable—neither real nor unreal**.

Nyāya objects:

```text
If silver is real → it should remain after correction
If wholly unreal → it could not appear or motivate action
Advaita: neither real nor unreal
Nyāya objection: obscure third ontological status
```

✅ Nyāya claims that real entities plus cognitive misrelation are sufficient; no indefinable entity is needed.

⚠️ **Advaita reply:** The error-object cannot be fully real because it is sublated, but it cannot be sheer non-being because it appears. Its empirical appearance justifies the indefinable status.

---

# 6. Important distinction: source versus location

The silver’s **source** is previous genuine experience. Its falsely assigned **location** is the present shell.

```text
Silver:
existence → real
source → previous perception
actual location → elsewhere
apparent location → here
```

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Real there, wrongly placed here.**

---

# 7. Critical assessment

## Strengths

✅ Preserves external realism.
✅ Explains why illusion contains recognisable content.
✅ Connects error with memory, similarity and sensory defects.
✅ Explains action toward the illusory object.
✅ Allows correction through stronger perception.

## Difficulties

⚠️ Cognition-mediated perception may appear introduced specifically to solve the illusion problem.

⚠️ If silver truly exists elsewhere, critics ask how cognition can establish an extraordinary perceptual relation with a distant object.

⚠️ The theory may describe false attribution without completely explaining how two distinguishable cognitions become fused.

⚠️ Its plausibility depends upon accepting Nyāya’s broader theory of extraordinary perception.

# 1. The self (ātman)

✅ Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika accepts the self as:

- real;
- eternal;
- all-pervasive;
- numerically distinct in different living beings;
- different from body, senses and mind;
- the substance in which psychological qualities inhere.

## Self is not body

The body continuously changes, but personal appropriation persists:

```text
Childhood body → adult body → ageing body
                       │
              “I experienced all three”
                       ↓
            Enduring subject required
```

## Self is not a sense faculty

Each sense reveals only its specific object:

- eye sees colour;
- ear hears sound;
- nose smells;
- tongue tastes;
- skin feels touch.

Yet one subject coordinates them:

> “I see the mango and also taste it.”

✅ Cross-sensory unity indicates a common self that owns different sensory cognitions.

## Self is not mind

Mind is an instrument connecting the self with a sense faculty. An instrument cannot be identical with its user.

```text
Self → uses mind → connects with sense → apprehends object
```

# 2. Six canonical signs of the self

Gautama identifies six signs:

```text
Desire → aversion → effort → pleasure → pain → cognition
```

| Sign | Argument |
|---|---|
| Desire (**icchā**) | Requires a subject who desires |
| Aversion (**dveṣa**) | Requires a subject who rejects |
| Effort (**prayatna**) | Requires an agent who strives |
| Pleasure (**sukha**) | Requires an experiencer |
| Pain (**duḥkha**) | Requires an experiencer |
| Cognition (**jñāna**) | As a quality, requires a substance in which it inheres |

✅ These changing qualities require an enduring locus.

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Memory, recognition and bodily coordination support the self, but they are not substitutes for Gautama’s canonical six when the question explicitly asks for six reasons.

# 3. Is consciousness the essence of self?

**No.** Nyāya treats cognition or consciousness as an episodic **quality of the self**, not its permanent essence.

```text
Self + mind + sense + object
               ↓
          Cognition arises

Missing required connection
               ↓
    No manifest cognition
```

| Nyāya | Advaita Vedānta |
|---|---|
| Self is a substance possessing cognition | Self is consciousness itself |
| Cognition arises conditionally | Consciousness is intrinsic |
| Self can exist without manifest cognition | Self can never cease to be consciousness |

✅ Deep sleep and liberation are used to show that the self may persist without ordinary cognition.

⚠️ **Objection:** A self devoid of consciousness appears to be an empty metaphysical substratum.

✅ **Nyāya reply:** A substance need not permanently manifest every quality it can possess.

# 4. Atomic mind (manas)

✅ Mind is:

- an internal sense or instrument;
- eternal;
- atomic rather than all-pervasive;
- distinct for each self;
- the link between self and external senses.

## Why must mind be atomic?

If the self were simultaneously connected through mind with every sense, all cognitions would arise at once.

But experience is selective:

```text
Reading intensely
      ↓
Clock continues sounding
      ↓
Sound is not consciously attended to
      ↓
Mind was connected with sight, not hearing
```

✅ Atomic mind explains sequential attention.

> 🔑 **Rule:** The self is all-pervasive; mind is atomic; cognition arises when mind connects the self with a relevant sense.

# 5. Bondage and liberation

## Bondage chain

```text
False knowledge (mithyā-jñāna)
           ↓
Defects: attachment, aversion and delusion
           ↓
Voluntary activity (pravṛtti)
           ↓
Merit and demerit
           ↓
Rebirth
           ↓
Pleasure mixed with inevitable suffering
```

## Liberation chain

```text
Knowledge of reality (tattva-jñāna)
             ↓
Removal of false knowledge
             ↓
Cessation of defects
             ↓
Cessation of bondage-producing action
             ↓
End of rebirth
             ↓
Absolute cessation of suffering
             ↓
Liberation (apavarga)
```

✅ Liberation means the complete cessation of suffering (**duḥkha-atyanta-nivṛtti**).

⚠️ Standard Nyāya does not primarily describe it as positive eternal bliss.

## Nature of the liberated self

The liberated self remains, but ordinary qualities cease:

- pain;
- pleasure;
- desire;
- aversion;
- effort;
- ordinary cognition.

### Stone-like liberation objection

Critics ask:

> Why seek liberation if it resembles an unconscious stone?

✅ **Nyāya reply:** The goal is not to acquire a pleasurable experience but to eliminate suffering absolutely. Pleasure is unstable and may generate renewed attachment.

⚠️ **Critical evaluation:** Nyāya secures permanent freedom from suffering but risks making liberation existentially unattractive.

# 6. Theory of causation

✅ Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika defends **non-pre-existence of the effect (asatkāryavāda)**, also called the doctrine of new production (**ārambhavāda**).

> The effect does not already exist in its material cause; production generates a genuinely new entity.

## 🖼️ Nyāya versus Sāṃkhya

```text
SĀṂKHYA
Effect already exists latently in cause
Seed ── manifestation ──→ Tree

NYĀYA–VAIŚEṢIKA
Effect is absent before production
Seed + conditions ── new production ──→ Tree
```

## Why is the effect new?

1. Before production, the effect has **prior absence (prāgabhāva)**.
2. If the effect already existed, production would be redundant.
3. The effect possesses a new structure and capacities.
4. Therefore, production is a genuine beginning.

### Does the seed contain the tree?

✅ The seed contains causal capacities and material conditions, but not a pre-existing tree in latent metaphysical form.

```text
Seed ≠ concealed miniature tree
Seed + water + soil + heat + time → newly produced tree
```

> 🔑 **Answer line:** The cause contains the conditions for the effect, not the already existent effect itself.

# 7. What counts as a cause?

✅ A cause is an unconditional and invariable antecedent indispensable to producing the effect.

Not everything preceding an effect is its cause.

| Antecedent | Causal status |
|---|---|
| Clay used for the pot | Indispensable |
| Potter’s operation | Indispensable |
| Potter’s donkey standing nearby | Accidental |
| Colour of the potter’s stick | Irrelevant |
| Potter’s father | Remote antecedent |
| Space surrounding the process | Ever-present background |

## Indispensable versus accidental antecedent

- **Indispensably relevant (ananyathāsiddha):** cannot be otherwise explained or omitted from the causal account.
- **Otherwise established/accidental (anyathāsiddha):** merely accompanies or remotely precedes production.

✅ This distinction prevents the fallacy that everything occurring before an effect is its cause.

# 8. Three kinds of cause

Using cloth production:

| Cause | Meaning | Cloth example |
|---|---|---|
| Inherent/material cause (**samavāyi-kāraṇa**) | Material in which the effect inheres | Threads |
| Non-inherent cause (**asamavāyi-kāraṇa**) | Quality or arrangement inhering in the material cause | Conjunction and colour of threads |
| Efficient cause (**nimitta-kāraṇa**) | Agent or instrument initiating production | Weaver and loom |

## Pot example

```text
Clay particles       → inherent/material cause
Their conjunction    → non-inherent cause
Potter and wheel     → efficient cause
Pot                  → newly produced effect
```

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Non-pre-existence of the effect does not deny causal continuity. It denies only that the completed effect already exists before production.

# 9. Atomism

✅ Physical reality is ultimately composed of eternal atoms (**paramāṇu**).

An atom is:

- eternal;
- partless;
- indivisible;
- imperceptible;
- the ultimate material cause of composite objects.

## Why indivisible?

```text
Suppose every physical unit has smaller parts
                    ↓
Division continues infinitely
                    ↓
No ultimate material basis remains
                    ↓
Finite composite objects lack a foundation
                    ↓
Partless atoms must terminate division
```

✅ Earth, water, fire and air possess distinct kinds of atoms.

⚠️ Ether, time and space are substances but are not composed of physical atoms.

---

# 10. From atoms to visible objects

## Combination sequence

```text
Atom + Atom
     ↓
Imperceptible dyad (dvyaṇuka)

Three dyads
     ↓
Triad (tryaṇuka/trasareṇu)
     ↓
Minimum perceptible magnitude in standard exposition
     ↓
Larger combinations
     ↓
Gross physical objects
```

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** A triad is formed from **three dyads**, not merely from three individual atoms in the standard account.

## Creation and dissolution

```text
DISSOLUTION
Gross objects → compounds separate → eternal atoms remain

CREATION
Atoms acquire motion → dyads → triads → gross world
```

✅ Creation does not produce atoms; it reorganises eternal atoms into new composites.

✅ During cosmic dissolution, compounds perish while atoms persist.

✅ In the later synthesis, unseen moral force (**adṛṣṭa**) and God’s direction initiate the first atomic motion. God’s detailed role belongs to the next subtopic.

# 11. Connecting causation and atomism

```text
Eternal atoms
     │ material causes
     ↓
New combinations
     │
     ↓
Composite effect newly begins
     │
     ↓
Prior absence ends at production
```

A pot did not exist before production, although its atomic and material causes existed.

Therefore:

```text
Material continuity + structural novelty
                  =
Non-pre-existence of effect without creation from nothing
```

> 🔑 **Answer-grabbing formulation:** Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika combines continuity of material causes with novelty of composite effects.

# 1. Nature of God

✅ Developed Nyāya and later Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika accept God as:

- eternal;
- omniscient;
- supremely powerful;
- bodiless;
- untouched by karmic bondage;
- an intelligent cosmic governor;
- a reliable source of Vedic testimony.

## Efficient—not material—cause

```text
Material cause of world → eternal atoms
Efficient cause          → God
```

God orders pre-existing atoms; God does not transform himself into the universe.

| Potter analogy | Cosmic account |
|---|---|
| Clay supplies material | Atoms supply material |
| Potter intelligently arranges clay | God intelligently arranges atoms |
| Pot is produced | Ordered world is produced |

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Nyāya’s God is not the material cause of the world.

# 4. Argument from effect (kāryāt)

```text
The world is composite and organised
                 ↓
Composite organised entities are effects
                 ↓
Effects require an intelligent maker
                 ↓
The world requires an intelligent maker
                 ↓
God
```

| Inferential element | Content |
|---|---|
| Subject (**pakṣa**) | World |
| Reason (**hetu**) | Being composite and produced |
| Probandum (**sādhya**) | Possessing an intelligent maker |
| Example | Pot requiring a potter |

### Objection

The pot analogy may fail:

- pots are finite objects;
- potters possess bodies and tools;
- no world-maker is observed;
- the universe may not be an effect in the same sense.

### Nyāya reply

✅ The inference concerns the need for intelligence, not bodily similarity. Embodiment is not essential to agency.

⚠️ **Critical assessment:** The inference is persuasive only if the world’s order legitimately establishes effecthood rather than merely assuming it.

# 5. Argument from atomic combination (āyojanāt)

At cosmic dissolution, atoms persist but compounds disappear.

Creation requires first motion:

```text
Motionless eternal atoms
            ↓
How does ordered combination begin?
            ↓
Unconscious atoms cannot form purposeful arrangements unaided
            ↓
Intelligent initiator required
            ↓
God
```

✅ God initiates ordered atomic conjunction in accordance with the unseen moral potential (**adṛṣṭa**) of souls.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Atoms explain what the world is made from; God explains why they enter an ordered creative process.

### Objection

Why cannot the atoms possess an intrinsic tendency to combine?

### Nyāya reply

An intrinsic tendency would operate continuously. It would not explain alternating cycles of creation and dissolution or morally differentiated embodiments.

⚠️ Critics may still argue that invoking divine volition merely relocates the explanatory problem.

---

# 6. Argument from sustenance (dhṛtyādeḥ)

```text
World originates
      +
World remains systematically ordered
      +
World undergoes regulated dissolution
      ↓
Continuing governing intelligence
```

✅ God is required not only to initiate creation but also to sustain order and govern dissolution.

### Objection

Natural laws may explain continuing order without a conscious governor.

### Nyāya reply

Laws describe regularity but do not independently explain:

- why such laws obtain;
- why atoms conform to them;
- how moral consequences are allocated.

⚠️ Modern criticism may interpret order as emergent rather than purposively designed.

---

# 7. Argument from scripture (śruteḥ)

```text
Veda contains authoritative knowledge
                 ↓
Reliable testimony requires a competent knower
                 ↓
Its extraordinary scope requires omniscience
                 ↓
God is its authoritative source
```

✅ Later Nyāya treats God as the supreme reliable speaker (**āpta**).

### Mīmāṃsā objection

The Veda is eternal and authorless. Its validity does not require God.

### Nyāya reply

Meaningful, ordered sentences are better explained through competent authorship than through an authorless collection of words.

⚠️ **Critical assessment:** This argument depends upon first accepting Vedic authority. Otherwise, it risks circularity:

```text
Veda proves God
God guarantees Veda
```

A strong answer must acknowledge this difficulty.

---

# 8. Argument from karmic administration

```text
Actions generate unseen moral potential
                  ↓
Unconscious potential cannot knowingly allocate exact results
                  ↓
Different souls receive appropriate fruits
                  ↓
Intelligent moral administrator required
                  ↓
God
```

✅ God does not arbitrarily create suffering; he distributes consequences according to beginningless karma.

## Problem of evil

**Objection:** If God is omnipotent and benevolent, why does suffering exist?

**Nyāya reply:** Suffering follows the souls’ own karma rather than divine cruelty.

⚠️ **Residual difficulty:** If God must follow an independent karmic law, divine omnipotence appears limited. If God established that law, responsibility for suffering returns partly to God.

# 10. Nyāya and Yoga compared

✅ Both accept God (**Īśvara**), but not in exactly the same way.

| Issue | Nyāya | Classical Yoga |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Omniscient efficient cause and governor | Special conscious self (**puruṣa-viśeṣa**) |
| World role | Arranges atoms and governs karma | Not established through Udayana’s atomic creator argument |
| Main proof/function | Natural theology and cosmic explanation | Soteriological and meditative support |
| Scripture | Supreme reliable source | Beginningless teacher |
| Practice | Knowledge of God supports explanation and liberation | Devotion to God aids concentration and samādhi |

> 🔑 **Conclusion:** Same name does not imply identical metaphysical role or proof.

⚠️ Classical Yoga does not present Udayana’s complete cumulative creator inference. Its God is principally a special conscious self untouched by afflictions and karma.
