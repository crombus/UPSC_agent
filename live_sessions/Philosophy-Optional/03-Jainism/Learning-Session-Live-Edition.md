## 1.1 Simple intuition — what problem is Jainism solving?

Ordinary language forces you to choose sides: is a mango ripe or unripe, is a person good or bad, does a
thing exist or not? Jainism's starting intuition is that **most disputes in philosophy are disputes
between people who have each grasped one real, true, but partial fact about a complex object, and then
insisted their partial fact is the whole truth.** The tradition builds an entire philosophical system —
metaphysics, logic, ethics and a theory of liberation — around correcting that one habit of mind:
one-sidedness (**ekānta**). Before any of the technical vocabulary is introduced, hold on to this plain
question, because every later lesson answers a piece of it: *if reality really is complicated, how should
a finite, honest mind talk about it without either lying by oversimplifying or going silent out of
excessive caution?*

## 1.2 Who is a Tīrthaṅkara, and what is Jainism named after? ✅

**Plain-language definition.** ✅ "Jaina" derives from **jina**, "conqueror": one who has conquered
passions and karmic bondage. A **Tīrthaṅkara** ("ford-maker") is a liberated teacher who re-establishes the
path across the ocean of transmigration (**saṃsāra**) for others — not a creator God, and not a founder who
invents the teaching from nothing.

**Technical doctrine.** ✅
1. Jaina tradition recognises **twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras** in the present cosmic cycle.
2. **Ṛṣabha** is named as the first, **Pārśva** as the twenty-third, and **Vardhamāna Mahāvīra** as the
   twenty-fourth and most recent.
3. Mahāvīra **systematised and renewed** an already existing tradition; he should never be described in a
   UPSC answer as having invented Jainism out of nothing.
4. **Śvetāmbara** and **Digambara** traditions differ on canon, monastic practice (most visibly, clothing)
   and some doctrinal details, while sharing the central grammar of soul, karma, many-sided reality, vows
   and liberation.

**Historical caution.** ❓ Mahāvīra and Pārśva are historical anchors, but precise dates and claims about
the earliest Tīrthaṅkaras require source-specific qualification. A UPSC philosophy answer needs the
doctrinal framework above, not legendary chronology — do not invent regnal years or biographical detail
that is not independently verified.

**Philosophical consequence.** ✅ Jainism therefore combines an **uncreated cosmos** with **religious
authority grounded in perfected teachers**. It is classified as heterodox (**nāstika**) because it rejects
Vedic authority — **not** because it denies soul, ethics, rebirth or spiritual perfection. This is a
standard UPSC trap: heterodoxy in Indian philosophy tracks acceptance of the Veda as a valid means of
knowledge, not acceptance or denial of a soul.

## 1.3 The *Tattvārthasūtra*: the shared doctrinal grammar of both sects ✅

**Simple framing.** Every philosophical school needs one text that both major internal factions can point
to as authoritative, so that sectarian disputes remain disputes *within* a shared grammar rather than
disputes about whether there is a grammar at all. For Jainism that text is the *Tattvārthasūtra*.

**Technical doctrine.** ✅ The *Tattvārthasūtra*, or *Tattvārthādhigama-sūtra*, of **Umāsvāti** (in
Śvetāmbara usage) / **Umāsvāmī** (in Digambara usage) is the first systematic Sanskrit exposition of Jaina
philosophy and is authoritative for **both** major sects — an unusual cross-sectarian achievement given
that Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions otherwise differ in scriptural canon and practice.

**Argument for why this text matters (step by step).** ✅
1. It supplies a compact doctrinal grammar for Jaina metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, bondage and
   liberation in one place.
2. Its cross-sectarian authority is unusual precisely because the two major traditions differ elsewhere.
3. It states the path through the **ratna-traya** ("three jewels"): **samyag-darśana** (right vision),
   **samyag-jñāna** (right knowledge) and **samyak-cāritra** (right conduct) — the spine that Lesson 7
   builds on in full.
4. It systematises the **seven tattvas**, the **five astikāyas plus kāla**, and the classification of
   **jñāna**, without reducing Jainism to only ethics or only logic — a caution worth keeping through every
   later lesson, since UPSC answers that treat Jainism as "just ahiṃsā" or "just relativism" under-score.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ A UPSC answer improves noticeably when Jainism is presented as a *systematic
darśana* (philosophical school) rather than a loose collection of ascetic practices.

**Distinction to hold onto immediately.** ✅ The Tīrthaṅkaras are liberated teachers and ford-makers, **not**
creator Gods; Jainism remains **anīśvaravāda** (non-theistic about creation) — the full argument for this
is given in §1.6 below.

**Example.** ✅ The formulation that right vision, right knowledge and right conduct together constitute
the path to *mokṣa* may be safely used in an answer without inventing a specific sūtra number.

**Objection (sectarian-history objection).** ❓ A critic may ask whether a text accepted by both
Śvetāmbaras and Digambaras erases real sectarian differences.

**Reply.** ⚠️ It does not erase them; it supplies a shared doctrinal framework within which disagreements
about canon, practice and interpretation can still remain live.

**Names worth dropping carefully in an answer (not to be inflated with invented biographical detail).** ✅
- **Kundakunda** — Digambara thinker associated with the *Samayasāra* and the **niścaya-naya /
  vyavahāra-naya** distinction (the "real" versus "practical" standpoint distinction inside Jaina ethics).
- **Siddhasena Divākara** and **Samantabhadra** — important early names in the logic of *anekānta* and
  *syādvāda*, useful to cite when an answer asks for the historical development of Jaina logic.
- **Akalaṅka**, **Māṇikyanandi**, **Hemacandra** and **Yaśovijaya** — major later Jaina logicians for the
  mature *pramāṇa* (means-of-knowledge) tradition, cited again in Lesson 3 Part B.

## 1.4 Pluralism, previewed ✅

**Simple framing.** If the cosmos has no single creator organising everything into one plan, what does
hold it together? Jainism's answer is: **many independent, real kinds of existence**, each doing a
distinct explanatory job, none reducible to the others.

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism is a **pluralistic** system: it accepts many real, individual souls (*jīvas*), and
several irreducible non-conscious substances, rather than one underlying substance or one universal self.

**Preview table (fully taught in Lesson 3).**

| Question pluralism answers | Jaina resource |
|---|---|
| What experiences, knows, suffers, is liberated? | Many individual **jīva**s |
| What is corporeal/material? | **Pudgala** |
| What makes motion possible at all? | **Dharma-dravya** |
| What makes rest possible at all? | **Adharma-dravya** |
| What accommodates all of the above? | **Ākāśa** (space) |
| What explains succession/change-order? | **Kāla** (time) |

**Distinction.** ✅ This is not merger pluralism (many finite manifestations of one Absolute, as in some
readings of Advaita) — it is **irreducible plurality**: each *jīva* is really distinct from every other
*jīva*, and liberation is the liberation of that individual, not an absorption into anything larger. Lesson
8 returns to this point in full when it asks, using the exact 2025 PYQ wording, "Is Jaina philosophy
pluralistic and realistic?"

## 1.5 Visual — anīśvaravāda at a glance (why no creator God?)

```text
QUESTION: Who made the world, and who assigns each soul its condition?

JAINISM'S ANSWER — an argument chain, not a bare denial:

  (1) The six substances (Lesson 3) are eternal in their SUBSTANTIAL continuity.
              │
              ▼
  (2) Their changing MODES (paryāya) explain ordinary production and destruction —
      nothing needs to be created "from nothing."
              │
              ▼
  (3) Karma (materially conceived — Lesson 6) explains embodied inequality and
      consequence WITHOUT any divine allocation of rewards and punishments.
              │
              ▼
  (4) A creator therefore adds NO necessary explanatory work, and raises further
      questions: what was the creator's MOTIVE? what MATERIAL did the creator use?
      why is the creation IMPERFECT? who is RESPONSIBLE for suffering?
              │
              ▼
  (5) Liberation depends on SELF-PURIFICATION (Lesson 7), not divine grace.

CONCLUSION: Jainism is anīśvaravāda (non-theistic about creation) — but this is
compatible with revering a perfected teacher (Jina / Tīrthaṅkara / siddha) as
worthy of honour BECAUSE of achieved perfection, not because that being created
or governs the universe.
```

**Caption.** This chain matters because "non-theistic" is frequently misread by candidates as "anti-
religious" or "materialist." Jainism is neither: it is a **religion of self-effort within an uncreated
moral cosmos**.

## 1.6 Technical doctrine and argument: no creator, but perfected religious authority ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism rejects a creator God because the cosmos and its substances are **beginningless
and uncreated**; production and destruction concern *modes*, not the creation of substance from nothing.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The six substances (taught fully in Lesson 3) are eternal in their substantial continuity.
2. Their changing modes explain ordinary production and destruction.
3. Karma explains embodied inequality and consequence without divine allocation.
4. A creator therefore adds no necessary explanatory work, and raises further questions about motive,
   material, imperfection and responsibility for suffering.
5. Liberation depends on self-purification, not divine grace.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jainism denies a **creator** but not divinity in the honorific sense. A liberated
conqueror (**Jina**), perfected soul (**siddha**), or ford-maker (**Tīrthaṅkara**) is worthy of reverence
because of *achieved* perfection, not because that being created or governs the universe.

**Exam formula (safe to reuse verbatim in an answer).** ⚠️ "Jainism is best described as a religion of
self-effort within an uncreated moral cosmos: non-theistic about creation, but neither materialist nor
spiritually negative."

## 1.7 Comparative treatment: where does Jainism sit among Indian schools on this question? ✅/⚠️

| Axis | Jainism | Cārvāka | Advaita Vedānta | Nyāya (later/theistic phase) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Creator God | ✅ Denied (*anīśvaravāda*) | ✅ Denied | ⚠️ Īśvara is empirical-soteriological, not an ultimate second reality | ✅ Affirmed in later Nyāya |
| Soul | ✅ Many real, permanent *jīvas* | ✅ Denied / reduced to body | ✅ Ultimately one *ātman-Brahman* | ✅ Many eternal selves |
| Liberation | ✅ Central, self-effort driven | ✅ Denied or emptied of meaning | ✅ Realisation of non-duality | ✅ Self freed from suffering |
| Ethics without God | ✅ Karma-pudgala and vows carry the full ethical weight | ⚠️ This-worldly prudence, no metaphysical ethics | ⚠️ Karma operates but is finally sublated in realisation | ✅ Ethics tied to a theistic order in later Nyāya |

**Why they disagree (not just a label exercise).** ⚠️ Jainism and Cārvāka agree in rejecting a creator, but
for opposite reasons: Cārvāka rejects the soul, karma and liberation along with God, while Jainism keeps
soul, karma and liberation and removes only the creator. Jainism and Advaita disagree because Advaita's
non-dualism ultimately treats plurality (including the plurality of *jīvas*) as provisional, whereas
Jainism insists that plurality is final and real. Jainism and theistic Nyāya disagree because Nyāya finds a
creator-God explanatorily necessary to account for the moral order of karma and cosmic arrangement, while
Jainism finds this cosmic bookkeeping already handled by the intrinsic, self-executing logic of material
karma (fully taught in Lesson 6) — no external administrator is required.

## 1.8 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (comparative-religion objection).** ✅ A critic may object that a religion without a creator God
and without divine grace cannot supply a compelling reason to be moral, or a satisfying answer to why
suffering exists.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Suffering and inequality are fully explained by the self-executing causal mechanism of
material karma (Lesson 6): the moral order is *built into* the metaphysics of the cosmos rather than
externally administered. Motivation to be moral does not require a rewarding/punishing God; it follows
directly from the doctrine that every act, especially every passion-driven act, has real, traceable,
person-specific karmic consequence.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ This reply is internally coherent and gives Jaina ethics unusual causal
seriousness. Its cost, addressed in full in Lesson 8, is that it must independently defend the coherence of
"material karma acting on an immaterial soul" without appeal to any theistic guarantor.

## 1.9 UPSC integration

- **Syllabus relevance.** This lesson is foundational context for all three syllabus limbs (theory of
  reality, *saptabhaṅgīnaya*, bondage and liberation) — examiners rarely set a question on §1.2–§1.6 alone,
  but every "Discuss Jainism's view of X" answer scores extra marks for correctly framing Jainism as a
  systematic, non-theistic, pluralist *darśana* in its opening lines.
- **Probable framing.** Introductory lines of 15/20-mark answers; occasionally an explicit "Is Jainism a
  religion or a philosophy?" / "Examine Jainism's position on the existence of God" comparative question in
  Paper II bridges.
- **Common traps.**
  1. Calling Mahāvīra the "founder" of Jainism rather than its most recent systematiser/renewer.
  2. Treating "heterodox" (*nāstika*) as equivalent to "denies the soul" — it only means rejection of Vedic
     authority.
  3. Treating non-theism as anti-religious or as equivalent to Cārvāka materialism.
- **Answer-use guidance.** Open a "Discuss Jainism's theory of X" answer with one line locating Jainism as a
  *śramaṇa*, non-theistic, pluralist-realist *darśana* grounded in the *Tattvārthasūtra*'s three jewels —
  this signals command of the whole system before the specific doctrine is unpacked.

## 1.11 Practice (Lesson 1)

**Conceptual MCQs**

1. The word "Jaina" is derived from:
   A. *jina* (conqueror)
   B. *jñāna* (knowledge)
   C. *jīva* (soul)
   D. *jyoti* (light)
   **Answer: A.** *Jina* means conqueror of passions and karmic bondage; the other three are genuine Jaina
   terms but not the etymological root of "Jaina."

2. Which Tīrthaṅkara is regarded as the twenty-fourth and most recent in the present cycle?
   A. Ṛṣabha
   B. Vardhamāna Mahāvīra
   C. Kundakunda
   D. Pārśva
   **Answer: B.** Ṛṣabha is first, Pārśva twenty-third; Kundakunda is a later Digambara philosopher, not a
   Tīrthaṅkara.

3. The *Tattvārthasūtra* is significant chiefly because it:
   A. is accepted only by the Digambara sect
   B. replaces the ratna-traya with a single vow of non-violence
   C. is the first systematic Sanskrit exposition of Jaina philosophy, authoritative across both major sects
   D. was composed after Mahāvīra founded Jainism from nothing
   **Answer: C.** Its cross-sectarian authority is precisely what the lesson highlights as unusual and
   important.

4. Jainism is classified as heterodox (*nāstika*) in Indian philosophy because it:
   A. denies the existence of the soul
   B. denies rebirth
   C. rejects the possibility of liberation
   D. rejects the authority of the Veda
   **Answer: D.** Heterodoxy tracks rejection of Vedic authority, not denial of soul, rebirth or
   liberation — all three of which Jainism affirms.

**Applied MCQ**

5. A candidate writes: "Jainism denies a creator God, and therefore has no basis for ethical motivation."
   The strongest Jaina-internal reply is that:
   A. the self-executing logic of material karma supplies causal, person-specific moral consequence without
      requiring an external administrator
   B. ethical motivation is unnecessary once liberation is understood as extinction
   C. Jainism secretly accepts a creator God at a higher level
   D. ethics in Jainism applies only to ascetics, not to the metaphysics of karma
   **Answer: A.** This is exactly the reply built in §1.8; B misstates Jaina *mokṣa* (Lesson 7 shows
   liberation is not extinction), and C and D are false.

**Remedial MCQ (targets a predictable error)**

6. A student assumes that because Jainism has no creator God, it must be a materialist philosophy like
   Cārvāka. The correct correction is that:
   A. Jainism and Cārvāka are identical in denying God, soul and karma alike
   B. Jainism denies only the creator; it affirms real souls, real material karma and a real path to
      liberation, unlike Cārvāka which denies soul, karma and liberation altogether
   C. Jainism is theistic in its later Digambara phase
   D. Cārvāka accepts karma but rejects only the creator
   **Answer: B.** This is the precise contrast drawn in the comparative table at §1.7.

**Mains practice (10 marks)**

*Question:* "Jainism is non-theistic but not anti-religious." Examine.

*Model answer.* Jainism rejects a creator God (*anīśvaravāda*) because its six substances are beginningless
and eternal in substance, with only their modes arising and ceasing; karmic matter, not divine allocation,
explains embodied inequality; and liberation is achieved through self-effort, not grace. This removes the
explanatory need for a creator without removing religious content: Jainism retains twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras
as objects of reverence, a systematic soteriology culminating in *mokṣa*, and an elaborate ethical
discipline (vows, austerities) whose purpose is spiritual purification. The reverence given to a Jina or
siddha tracks *achieved perfection*, not creative or governing power — a structurally different basis for
religious authority than in theistic traditions, but a religious basis nonetheless. Hence Jainism is
correctly described as a religion of self-effort within an uncreated moral cosmos: non-theistic about
creation, but neither materialist nor spiritually negative. *(Why this earns marks: it defines
anīśvaravāda precisely, gives the causal argument in brief, and directly answers the "not anti-religious"
half of the quotation rather than only the non-theism half.)*

```text
                                     ▼        ▼           ▼
                    Example: a GOLD ORNAMENT reshaped from bracelet to ring

     BEFORE                          DURING                          AFTER
  [ bracelet-mode ]  ---vyaya (bracelet ceases)--->            [ ring-mode ]
        │                                                            ▲
        │                                                            │
        └──────────────── dhrauvya: THE GOLD PERSISTS ────────utpāda (ring arises)
                                     throughout

  guṇa (quality): "being gold", weight, colour  → ABIDE, do not change
  paryāya (mode): "being a bracelet" / "being a ring" → ARISE AND CEASE
```

**Caption.** The diagram shows why Jainism needs *three* terms, not one: pure permanence alone cannot
explain the transformation (bracelet → ring), and pure change alone cannot explain why we still call it
"the same gold." Reality must be read as **both at once, in different respects** — origination and decay
at the level of mode, permanence at the level of substance.

## 2.1 Simple intuition

Two rival simplifications tempt every metaphysician. One says: "underneath all appearances there is one
changeless reality; change is illusion." The other says: "everything is in constant flux; there is no
enduring thing at all, only a series of momentary events." Jainism's founding intuition in ontology is that
**both simplifications throw away something obviously true.** We do experience continuity (the same
ornament, the same person from yesterday to today) *and* we do experience real change (bracelet becomes
ring, child becomes adult). A metaphysics that denies either fact is paying for elegance with realism.
Jainism's answer is to build "the real" out of *three* ingredients rather than one.

## 2.2 Technical doctrine: *anekāntavāda* as a metaphysical thesis ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Anekāntavāda** (English-first: **the doctrine of many-sided reality**) is the Jaina
doctrine that reality is many-sided; no single, unqualified proposition exhausts the nature of a real
thing.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Real things endure through change and exhibit multiple aspects.
2. Human cognition usually grasps only one aspect at a time.
3. Dogmatic, one-sided claims (**ekānta**) therefore distort reality.
4. A sound metaphysics must allow plurality of aspects within one real.
5. Hence reality is non-one-sided, or **anekānta**.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Being is richer than any single finite judgement can capture.

**Crucial distinction (do not blur this in an answer).** ✅ Anekāntavāda is **not** mere indecision or
fence-sitting. It is a **metaphysical** claim about the objective complexity of reality — it is not yet the
logical form of predication (that is *syādvāda*, Lesson 4) nor the standpoint-taxonomy (that is *nayavāda*,
also Lesson 4) nor the formal sevenfold schema (that is *saptabhaṅgī*, Lesson 5). Holding these four terms
apart is one of the single highest-yield distinctions in the entire Jainism syllabus item; a full
side-by-side table is given at the end of Lesson 4 once all four have been introduced.

**Example.** ✅ A jar is permanent as a substance across a short stretch of time, changing in modes,
describable by shape, use, material and temporal state. No single predicate says all of this at once.

**Objection (the sceptical objection).** ✅ If every object has indefinitely many aspects, knowledge may
become impossible — how can a finite mind ever "finish" describing anything?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jaina thought replies that knowledge is possible precisely *through* qualified standpoints, not
through absolutist simplification. Partiality is not the same as falsity; a claim becomes false only when
its partiality is denied. (This reply is developed fully with the standpoint machinery of Lesson 4.)

## 2.3 Technical doctrine: *dravya* as *sat* = *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The real (**sat**) is what possesses **utpāda** (origination), **vyaya** (decay) and
**dhrauvya** (persistence/permanence) together.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Experience shows change: forms arise and pass away.
2. Experience also shows continuity: something persists through those changes.
3. Pure permanence cannot explain transformation.
4. Pure flux cannot explain identity.
5. Therefore reality must include both persistence and modification.
6. This is articulated by saying substance is characterised by origination, decay and permanence, all
   together, in different respects.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Both common-sense identity and experiential change are philosophically significant
and must both be preserved by any adequate ontology — neither may be explained away.

**Distinction.** ✅ This differs from **Buddhist momentariness**, which gives primacy to flux and denies an
enduring substance, and from **strict monisms** (a certain reading of Advaita) that absorb change into mere
appearance (*vivarta*) atop one changeless reality. Jainism deliberately sits between these two extremes.

**Example.** ✅ The gold-ornament example above: modes (bracelet-hood, ring-hood) originate and cease, yet
the underlying substance (gold) persists.

**Objection (Buddhist/Nyāya-style objection).** ✅ How can opposite features — change and permanence —
belong to one and the same thing?

**Reply.** ✅ They belong in **different respects**: permanence as *dravya* (substance), change as *paryāya*
(mode). This is not a contradiction because it is not the assertion of opposite predicates in the *same*
respect.

## 2.4 Technical doctrine: *guṇa* and *paryāya* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Every *dravya* (substance) possesses **guṇa** (essential/abiding qualities) and **paryāya**
(modes or modifications).

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. A substance must have stable features, or it would not be identifiable as what it is.
2. It must also undergo modal change, or it could not participate in ordinary experience at all.
3. Therefore a substance is intelligible only as substance-with-qualities-and-modes — three linked
   concepts, not one.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Ontology must distinguish *what remains* from *how it appears or changes*.

**Distinction.** ✅ **Guṇa** is not the same as **paryāya**: qualities belong to the substance as abiding
characteristics (e.g. consciousness as a defining feature of *jīva*); modes are changing states or
manifestations in which the substance is found (e.g. pleasure, pain, delusion, or liberated omniscience as
modes/conditions of a *jīva*).

**Example.** ✅ Consciousness is a defining **guṇa** of *jīva*; pleasure, pain, delusion, or liberated
omniscience are **paryāyas** — modes or conditions in which the *jīva* appears at different times.

**Objection (Buddhist nominalist objection).** ✅ Why not just speak of changing qualities directly, rather
than introducing a persisting substance underneath them?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Because changing qualities still presuppose *something whose changes they are* — "the anger
changed" already implies a subject that was angry and is no longer so.

## 2.5 Dravya–guṇa–paryāya as one integrated system (visual)

```text
                    DRAVYA (substance) ── persists (dhrauvya)
                       │
                       ├── GUṆA (essential quality) ── ABIDES with the substance
                       │        e.g. consciousness (for jīva), extension (for pudgala)
                       │
                       └── PARYĀYA (mode) ── ARISES (utpāda) and CEASES (vyaya)
                                e.g. bracelet-shape, anger, pleasure, omniscience-as-attained-state

  KEY SENTENCE: "Sat = utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya-yuktam" — the real is what is endowed with
  origination, decay, AND permanence, together, in different respects (substance vs. mode).
```

**Caption.** This single diagram is the metaphysical engine of the entire Jainism paper: once a learner can
correctly place any Jaina claim onto this three-box structure, most "define X" and "distinguish X from Y"
questions in Lessons 3–7 become applications of this one model rather than new memorisation.

## 2.6 Comparative treatment: Jaina causation previewed, and the three-school causal map ✅/⚠️

Although the full doctrine of Jaina causation belongs to Lesson 3 (because it depends on the six
substances taught there), its **comparative shape** belongs here, immediately after *dravya-guṇa-paryāya*,
because the comparison is a direct application of permanence-and-change.

**Statement.** ⚠️ Jaina causation is described as both-existence-and-non-existence of the effect
(**sadasatkāryavāda**): the effect exists in the cause with respect to enduring substance, but does not yet
exist with respect to its new mode.

| School | Causal doctrine | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Sāṃkhya** | **Satkāryavāda** | The effect *pre-exists* in the cause (strong pre-existence) |
| **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** | **Asatkāryavāda** | The effect is a *genuinely new* production, not pre-existing |
| **Jainism** | **Sadasatkāryavāda** | The effect exists in the cause *qua* substance, and does not exist in the cause *qua* mode — mediating between the other two |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ Sāṃkhya wants to preserve the intelligibility of production (nothing utterly new
appears from nothing) and so makes the effect already latent. Nyāya wants to preserve the reality of
genuine novelty and so denies any pre-existence. Jainism argues both intuitions are correct but about
*different aspects* of the same causal event: the underlying substance is continuous (satisfying Sāṃkhya's
worry) while the mode is genuinely new (satisfying Nyāya's worry). This is *anekāntavāda applied to
causation* — the full example (the clay jar) and objection/reply are taught with the six substances in
Lesson 3, §3.10.

## 2.7 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (self-application objection, previewed here and resolved fully in Lesson 5).** ✅ If reality is
irreducibly many-sided, is the *claim* "reality is many-sided" itself many-sided, or is it being asserted
absolutely? If the latter, anekāntavāda seems to exempt itself from its own rule.

**Jaina reply (brief here; full development in Lesson 5, §5.9–§5.10).** ⚠️ Jainism accepts reflexive
qualification: the doctrine of anekāntavāda too is asserted **syāt** ("in some respect"), and its force
lies in disciplined anti-absolutism rather than in an unqualified dogma. This does not collapse the
doctrine into triviality, because it still rules out something specific — the ekānta habit of absolutising
one aspect as the whole truth.

## 2.9 Revision notes (Lesson 2)

1. *Anekāntavāda* = doctrine that reality is many-sided; a **metaphysical** thesis, not a logical form.
2. *Sat* (the real) = *utpāda* (origination) + *vyaya* (decay) + *dhrauvya* (permanence), all together.
3. Pure permanence ⇒ cannot explain transformation; pure flux ⇒ cannot explain identity; Jainism keeps both.
4. *Dravya* (substance) persists; *guṇa* (essential quality) abides with it; *paryāya* (mode) arises/ceases.
5. Example anchor: gold ornament, bracelet-mode → ring-mode, gold (substance) persists throughout.
6. Consciousness = *guṇa* of *jīva*; pleasure/pain/omniscience-as-state = *paryāyas* of *jīva*.
7. Contradiction is avoided because opposite predicates (permanent/changing) are indexed to *different
   respects* (substance vs. mode), not asserted of the same respect at the same time.
8. Jaina realism is a **middle path**: against Buddhist momentariness (denies enduring substance) and
   against strict monism/vivarta readings (denies real, plural, persisting substances).
9. Causation preview: *sadasatkāryavāda* mediates Sāṃkhya's *satkāryavāda* and Nyāya's *asatkāryavāda* by
   applying many-sidedness to the causal relation itself (full doctrine: Lesson 3, §3.10).
10. Self-application objection is previewed: is "reality is many-sided" itself many-sided? Answer developed
    fully in Lesson 5.
11. 🔑 Mnemonic: **"SAT needs three legs: Up, Vy, Dh"** — *utpāda, vyaya, dhrauvya* — remove any one leg and
    the table (reality) falls.
12. Trap: anekāntavāda ≠ relativism/subjectivism — aspects are objectively real *in* the object.

**Mains practice (15 marks)**

*Question:* "The Jaina definition of reality avoids both the extreme of static permanence and the extreme
of pure flux." Discuss.

*Model answer.* Jainism defines the real (*sat*) as that which possesses origination (*utpāda*), decay
(*vyaya*) and permanence (*dhrauvya*) together: *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya-yuktam sat*. Every substance
(*dravya*) has essential, abiding qualities (*guṇa*) and undergoes changing modes (*paryāya*). Against a
doctrine of static permanence (a strict monism in which change is mere appearance), Jainism insists that
modal change is objectively real — bracelet genuinely ceases and ring genuinely arises when a gold ornament
is reshaped. Against a doctrine of pure flux (as in Buddhist momentariness, which denies an enduring
substance beneath changing states), Jainism insists that the underlying substance — here, the gold — really
persists through the change; otherwise there would be no basis for saying "the same gold" took a new form.
The two claims do not contradict because they are indexed to different respects: permanence is asserted of
substance, change of mode, not both of the same respect at the same time. This dual commitment — real
persistence plus real change — is what licenses Jainism's characteristic pluralistic realism (developed
across Lessons 3–8): a world of many enduring, changing, interacting substances, known partially by finite
standpoints (Lesson 4) and only totally by the omniscient *kevala-jñāna* (Lesson 7). *(Why this earns
marks: it states the exact formula, names both rival extremes by name, resolves the apparent contradiction
through the substance/mode distinction, and links forward to the standpoint theory without conflating the
two doctrines.)*

**Caption.** This single census answers the question every ontology must answer: what fundamentally exists,
and how many fundamentally different *kinds* of thing are there? Jainism's answer is deliberately
pluralistic — six kinds, each doing an irreplaceable explanatory job — as previewed in Lesson 1, §1.4.

## 3A.1 Simple intuition

Imagine trying to describe a fish swimming in a pond. You need: (1) the fish itself (a living, aware
thing); (2) the water and the fish's own body (material stuff); (3) *something* that makes swimming
possible at all, as distinct from the water itself — call it a "condition for motion"; (4) something that
makes resting on the pond floor possible — a "condition for rest"; (5) the pond as a container that gives
everything room to be; and (6) the sequence of moments in which the swim happens. Jainism's six substances
are exactly an attempt to name every irreducible ingredient needed to describe *any* event in the cosmos,
without leaving any explanatory gap and without needlessly multiplying kinds beyond what is needed.

## 3A.2 Technical doctrine: *jīva* and *ajīva* — the master distinction ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The most fundamental ontological distinction in Jainism is between **jīva** (conscious) and
**ajīva** (non-conscious).

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Experience distinguishes living, aware beings from inert entities.
2. This distinction is ontologically basic because consciousness cannot be reduced to inert matter in Jaina
   thought.
3. Hence the universe divides exhaustively into *jīva* and *ajīva*.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Consciousness is real and fundamental, not epiphenomenal (not a mere by-product of
matter).

**Distinction from rival schools.** ✅ Unlike Advaita, this is not a distinction *within* one underlying
Brahman — the many *jīvas* are not modes of one universal consciousness. Unlike Cārvāka, consciousness is
not reducible to material organisation of the body.

**Example.** ✅ The body, speech and even karmic matter belong to *ajīva*; the conscious experiencer bound by
them is *jīva*.

**Objection (Nyāya-Vedānta objection).** ✅ If *jīva* is immaterial, how can material karma affect it?

**Reply.** ⚠️ This becomes the central challenge in Jaina bondage theory (Lesson 6) and is answered through
the doctrine of karmic influx and adhesion, though critics find the relation obscure — flagged again and
assessed in full in Lesson 8.

## 3A.3 Technical doctrine: the six *dravyas*, in full, with the astikāya distinction ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism classically recognises **six *dravyas***: **jīva**, **pudgala**, **dharma**,
**adharma**, **ākāśa** and **kāla**.

| Dravya | Basic sense | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Jīva** (soul) | conscious substance | experiencer, knower, bearer of bondage and liberation |
| **Pudgala** (matter) | matter | corporeality, karmic matter, bodies, sense-objects |
| **Dharma** (medium of motion) | medium of motion | condition making movement possible |
| **Adharma** (medium of rest) | medium of rest | condition making rest possible |
| **Ākāśa** (space) | space | accommodation for substances |
| **Kāla** (time) | time | succession, change, sequence |

**Astikāya table — extended vs. non-extended substances.**

| Dravya | Astikāya? | Function | Extension |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jīva** | ✅ Yes | knowing, experiencing, agency, bondage and liberation | ✅ Has *pradeśas* (space-points); in embodied existence it is co-extensive with the body |
| **Pudgala** | ✅ Yes | material bodies, sense-objects, speech, breath, karma-*pudgala* | ✅ Extended and aggregative |
| **Dharma-dravya** | ✅ Yes | medium enabling motion | ✅ Co-extensive with *lokākāśa* (the occupied part of space) |
| **Adharma-dravya** | ✅ Yes | medium enabling rest | ✅ Co-extensive with *lokākāśa* |
| **Ākāśa** | ✅ Yes | gives room/accommodation to substances | ✅ Extended as *lokākāśa* and *alokākāśa* (occupied and unoccupied space) |
| **Kāla** | ❌ **No** | succession, duration, before-and-after, change | ✅ A *dravya* but **an-astikāya**, because it lacks spatial extension across many *pradeśas* in the astikāya sense |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The classification accounts for consciousness, matter, motion, rest, spatial accommodation and temporal
   succession — every major category needed to describe an event.
2. Jainism is therefore pluralistic without being chaotic: each *dravya* has a distinct explanatory
   function; none is redundant.
3. Five of the six *dravyas* are **astikāyas** (extended substances with many space-points/*pradeśas*).
4. **Kāla** (time) is a real *dravya* in the mainstream sixfold presentation, but it is **not** an
   *astikāya*; hence Jainism speaks of **pañcāstikāya** (five extended substances) **plus** *kāla*.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Reality is irreducibly pluralistic, and extension must be sharply distinguished from
substantiality — something can be fully real without being spatially extended in the astikāya sense.

**Distinction (do not misread this).** ✅ **Dharma** and **adharma** here do **not** mean moral merit and
demerit; they mean media of motion and rest — one of the most-tested traps in this whole topic.

**Sectarian nuance.** ✅ The safe doctrinal point for an answer is: **kāla is a *dravya* but not an
astikāya**; the *pañcāstikāya* list has five members, while the *dravya* list has six. ❓ A common sectarian
nuance is that Digambara authors characteristically affirm real **kāla-aṇus** (time-atoms) associated with
the space-points of *lokākāśa*, whereas some Śvetāmbara discussions treat the substantial independence of
*kāla* as disputed or conventional — flag this as a contested nuance rather than a settled fact.

**Example.** ✅ A fish swims because water is the medium enabling motion; analogically, *dharma-dravya* is
the cosmic condition enabling movement, not the motion itself.

**Objection (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika-style objection).** ✅ *Dharma* and *adharma* as "media" look mysterious and
unparsimonious — why posit two extra substances just to explain motion and rest?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jaina metaphysics treats them not as pushes or forces but as **enabling conditions** required
for a coherent pluralistic cosmology in which motion and rest are objectively, not merely nominally,
possible.

## 3A.4 Nature of *jīva*: intrinsic perfection, empirical obscuration, and spatiality ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Every *jīva* is distinct, beginningless, and intrinsically endowed with **infinite
knowledge, perception, bliss and power**, though these are obscured by karma.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Liberation is meaningful only if the soul has a native capacity to realise perfection.
2. Bondage is then understood as *obscuration*, not total annihilation, of that nature.
3. Therefore *jīva* is intrinsically luminous but contingently covered — a doctrine that will matter
   enormously in Lesson 7 when *kevala-jñāna* is explained as *revealed*, not produced.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Spiritual progress presupposes a real self capable of purification.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jainism is pluralistic: there are many *jīvas*, not one universal self. It also differs
from Nyāya, where the self is conscious only through conjunction with mind; in Jainism, consciousness
belongs *intrinsically* to *jīva*.

**Living-being taxonomy.** ✅

| Class | Sanskrit term | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Liberated soul | **mukta-jīva** | Free from every karmic obstruction |
| Bound soul | **baddha-jīva** | Embodied and karmically conditioned |
| Immobile bound being | **sthāvara** | One-sense life, with touch only; classically includes earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied beings |
| Mobile bound being | **trasa** | Two-, three-, four-, or five-sense beings |

**Spatiality — the contraction-and-expansion doctrine.** ✅ An embodied soul has space-points (*pradeśas*)
and is co-extensive with its body, like a lamp illuminating the room it occupies: the ant's soul fills an
ant-body and the elephant's soul an elephant-body, without the soul itself becoming material. This doctrine
explains embodied awareness but creates a serious objection: **how can an immaterial substance possess
changing extension at all?**

**Objection (Sāṃkhya/Vedānta objection).** ✅ If the soul is naturally omniscient, ignorance becomes hard to
explain.

**Reply.** ✅ Karma as obscuring matter is introduced precisely to explain the discrepancy between intrinsic
capacity and empirical limitation (full mechanism: Lesson 6).

## 3A.5 *Pudgala*: matter, karmic matter, and atomic structure ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Pudgala** is the material *dravya* and the **only** *dravya* possessing **rūpa** (form);
it includes atoms, bodies, sense-objects and karmic matter.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Corporeality, extension and sensible quality require a material substrate.
2. Matter must therefore be treated as a real and irreducible category.
3. Since karmic bondage has quasi-physical efficacy, karmic particles too are classed under *pudgala* — the
   single most distinctive move in the whole system, developed fully in Lesson 6.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Materiality is not illusory; it is an objective domain with its own laws.

**Distinction.** ✅ *Pudgala* is uniquely important because, unlike *jīva*, it is divisible, aggregative and
form-bearing.

**Example.** ✅ The body one inhabits, the food one consumes, the dust in the air, and karmic accretions all
belong to *pudgala*.

**Objection (Nyāya/Mīmāṃsā-style objection).** ✅ To call karma "matter" seems strange — karma is usually a
moral category, not a physical one.

**Reply.** ✅ That strangeness is also Jainism's originality: bondage is not merely a moral description but a
real, quasi-physical entanglement.

**Atomic structure.** ✅
1. The indivisible material atom (**paramāṇu**) is the smallest unit of matter.
2. Material aggregates (**skandhas**) arise through combinations of atoms.
3. Matter possesses colour, taste, smell and touch; sound is treated as a **material modification** rather
   than a separate immaterial quality.
4. Jaina atoms are **not** divided into permanently different elemental kinds in the Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika manner;
   elemental differentiation belongs to developed modes and combinations, not to fixed atomic types.
5. Bodies, sense-organs, speech, breath, mind (as an embodied instrument), and karmic matter are all
   material configurations of *pudgala*.

**Etymological aid.** ⚠️ Matter (*pudgala*) is commonly explained through **integration and disintegration**
— filling or combining (*pūraṇa*) and disintegrating (*galana*). The etymology is useful because it
highlights matter's aggregative and transformable character.

## 3A.6 Jaina causation, in full: *sadasatkāryavāda* ✅/⚠️

**Statement.** ⚠️ Jaina causation is described as both-existence-and-non-existence of the effect
(**sadasatkāryavāda**): the effect exists in the cause with respect to enduring substance but does not yet
exist with respect to its new mode.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. If the effect were wholly identical with the cause, production would add nothing new.
2. If it were wholly non-existent and unrelated to the cause, anything could arise from anything.
3. In transformation, the underlying substance persists while an earlier mode ceases and a new mode
   originates.
4. Therefore the effect is continuous with the cause **as substance** and novel **as mode** — a direct
   application of *dravya-guṇa-paryāya* (Lesson 2) to the causal relation.

**Example.** ✅ A clay jar is not produced from sheer nothing: the clay persists. Yet the jar-form is
genuinely new and was not previously present as that completed mode.

**Comparison (full table already previewed in Lesson 2, §2.6; restated here with the worked example).** ✅
Jaina causation mediates between Sāṃkhya's strong pre-existence doctrine (**satkāryavāda**) and Nyāya's
new-production doctrine (**asatkāryavāda**) by applying many-sided reality to causal identity.

**Objection.** ✅ Saying the effect both exists and does not exist appears contradictory.

**Reply.** ✅ Existence and non-existence are indexed to **different respects** — substance and mode — so
the claim does not affirm an unqualified contradiction; it is the same manoeuvre used throughout Jaina
metaphysics.

## 3A.7 Comparative treatment: the six-*dravya* system versus rival ontologies ✅

| Axis | Jainism | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | Sāṃkhya | Advaita |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of basic irreducible kinds | Six *dravyas*, pluralist | Multiple categories (substance, quality, action, universal, etc.) | Two: *puruṣa* (many) and *prakṛti* (one) | One: Brahman (empirically, plural names/forms) |
| Self | Many, permanent, each with *pradeśas* | Many eternal selves | Many *puruṣas*, changeless witnesses | Ultimately one *ātman-Brahman* |
| Matter | *Pudgala*: atomic, aggregative, includes karmic matter | Atomic, four elements, permanently distinct kinds | *Prakṛti*: one evolving material principle | Empirically real, ultimately *mithyā* |
| Motion/rest | Explained by dedicated *dharma*/*adharma dravyas* | Explained by inherent motion (*karma* as a category) and contact/disjunction | Explained by *guṇa* interplay within *prakṛti* | Explained within *māyā*'s functioning |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika is comfortable multiplying categories (substance, quality, action,
universal, particularity, inherence) without positing dedicated substances for motion and rest, because it
explains movement through the category of *karma* (action) and contact/disjunction; Jainism instead insists
that motion and rest need their *own* enabling media as substances, which is a distinctively Jaina
economy-of-explanation choice. Sāṃkhya reduces all of non-conscious reality to a single evolving *prakṛti*,
whereas Jainism keeps matter, motion-medium, rest-medium, space and time as five genuinely separate
*ajīva* categories. Advaita, in its ultimate register, denies that plurality of substances is final at all
— exactly the point of maximal disagreement that Lesson 8 revisits when assessing Jainism's claim to be
"pluralistic and realistic."

## 3A.8 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (parsimony objection, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika-style).** ✅ Positing six distinct substances — including
two purely functional "media" for motion and rest — looks metaphysically extravagant compared with
explaining movement through contact and disjunction of already-accepted categories.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Extravagance is only a fault if the extra posits do no explanatory work; Jainism argues
that without dedicated media, the *possibility* of motion and rest as such (not merely particular motions)
is left brute and unexplained. The *dharma*/*adharma* *dravyas* are the conditions of possibility for
motion/rest in general, not causes of any particular motion.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ The reply is coherent within Jaina pluralism but is not obviously more
economical than the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika alternative; this is one of the genuine points on which reasonable
philosophers can differ, and a strong answer should present both sides rather than declare one obviously
right.

**Conceptual MCQs**

1. Which of the following is a *dravya* but **not** an *astikāya*?
   A. *Kāla*
   B. *Ākāśa*
   C. *Pudgala*
   D. *Jīva*
   **Answer: A.** *Kāla* lacks the many-space-point extension that defines an astikāya; the other three are
   all astikāyas.

2. In Jaina ontology, *dharma-dravya* refers to:
   A. moral merit accumulated through good deeds
   B. the medium that makes motion possible
   C. scriptural authority
   D. the medium that makes rest possible
   **Answer: B.** *Adharma-dravya* is the medium of rest; *dharma-dravya* is of motion — neither is an
   ethical category.

3. *Pudgala* is distinctive among the six *dravyas* because it alone possesses:
   A. *pradeśas*
   B. *guṇa*
   C. *rūpa* (form)
   D. permanence
   **Answer: C.** All *dravyas* have *guṇa*, *pradeśas* (except *kāla* in the astikāya sense) and a form of
   permanence; only *pudgala* has *rūpa*.

4. Jaina causation (*sadasatkāryavāda*) holds that the effect:
   A. is wholly non-existent in the cause
   B. is wholly pre-existent and unchanged in the cause
   C. has no relation to the cause at all
   D. exists in the cause as substance and does not exist in the cause as mode
   **Answer: D.** This is the precise mediating position between Sāṃkhya and Nyāya.

**Applied MCQ**

5. A student must explain why Jainism needs both *dharma-dravya* and *adharma-dravya* rather than just one
   "medium of change." The best Jaina-internal justification is that:
   A. motion and rest are each brute, unexplained possibilities unless each has its own enabling condition,
      distinct from any particular moving or resting object
   B. motion and rest are the same phenomenon described differently
   C. only motion needs an enabling medium; rest is simply the absence of motion
   D. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika already proved this distinction is unnecessary
   **Answer: A.** This is the Jaina reply to the parsimony objection in §3A.8.

**Remedial MCQ**

6. A student assumes that "five astikāyas plus kāla" and "six astikāyas" mean the same thing. The
   correction is that:
   A. they are indeed the same claim, phrased differently
   B. the *dravya* list has six members, the *astikāya* list has only five (kāla excluded), so the two
      phrases are not interchangeable
   C. *kāla* is one of the five astikāyas by another name
   D. there are actually seven *dravyas*, and the astikāya list has six
   **Answer: B.** This is the highest-yield trap named explicitly in §3A.3 and §3A.10.

**Mains practice (10 marks)**

*Question:* Distinguish between the six *dravyas* and the five *astikāyas* in Jaina ontology.

*Model answer.* Jainism recognises six *dravyas* (substances): *jīva* (soul), *pudgala* (matter), *dharma*
(medium of motion), *adharma* (medium of rest), *ākāśa* (space) and *kāla* (time). Of these, five —
*jīva*, *pudgala*, *dharma*, *adharma* and *ākāśa* — are *astikāyas*: extended substances possessing many
space-points (*pradeśas*). *Kāla* (time), while a fully real *dravya*, is **not** an astikāya, because it
lacks this multi-space-point spatial extension; it functions instead purely as the principle of succession
and change-order. Hence Jaina texts speak of *pañcāstikāya* (the five extended substances) *plus* *kāla*,
giving six *dravyas* in total but only five *astikāyas*. The distinction matters philosophically because it
shows Jainism separating *substantiality* (being real) from *extension* (occupying many space-points):
something can be fully a *dravya* — objectively real, with its own function — without being spatially
extended in the astikāya sense. This precision also guards against the common error of treating "dravya"
and "astikāya" as synonyms. *(Why this earns marks: it lists all six dravyas correctly, states the exact
five-member astikāya list, explains the philosophical point behind excluding kāla, and names the technical
term pañcāstikāya.)*

## 3B.0 Visual — the pratyakṣa-inversion trap

```text
                    ORDINARY CLASSROOM EXPECTATION (e.g. Nyāya):
        pratyakṣa (perception) = the PARADIGM of direct, most reliable knowledge

                    JAINA STRICT USAGE — INVERTED:
        pratyakṣa (strict sense) = knowledge the SOUL knows DIRECTLY, WITHOUT any
                                    mediation by sense-organs or mind at all

        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  Ordinary sense-perception  →  merely "sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa"  │
        │  ("empirical/conventional direct knowledge") — CLASSED AS PAROKṢA │
        │  in the strict sense, because it depends on sense-organs, mind,  │
        │  signs, and karmic limitation.                                    │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

        STRICT PRATYAKṢA (mukhya / pāramārthika) is achieved ONLY by:
              avadhi-jñāna, manaḥparyāya-jñāna, kevala-jñāna
```

**Caption.** This is one of the single highest-value UPSC traps in the whole topic: a candidate who assumes
Jaina *pratyakṣa* works like Nyāya *pratyakṣa* will misclassify ordinary sense-perception as strictly
direct, when Jainism strictly classifies it as mediated.

## 3B.1 Simple intuition

Ask yourself: when you see a mango with your eyes, is that the most direct kind of knowing something could
possibly have? Most schools say yes. Jainism says: **not quite** — your eye, your sense-organ, your mind,
and the residue of your own karmic limitation all stand *between* your soul and the mango. True,
unmediated, soul-direct knowing would require none of that scaffolding. So Jainism reserves the word
"direct" for a stricter, rarer kind of knowledge, and treats ordinary perception as a *practically*
direct — but *strictly* mediated — form of knowing. This single reclassification restructures the entire
Jaina theory of knowledge.

## 3B.2 Technical doctrine: the primary division — *pratyakṣa* and *parokṣa* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jaina epistemology distinguishes **pratyakṣa** (direct knowledge) and **parokṣa** (indirect
knowledge), but uses the word *pratyakṣa* in a high-yield, non-ordinary way.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. In the strict Jaina sense, **pratyakṣa** means knowledge that the soul knows directly, without mediation
   by senses or mind.
2. Ordinary sense-perception is therefore **not** ultimate direct knowledge; it is only
   **sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa** (empirical or conventional direct knowledge).
3. Strictly, ordinary sense-and-mind cognition belongs with mediated cognition, because it depends on
   sense-organs, mind, signs and karmic limitation.
4. ⚠️ This reverses the usual classroom expectation that perception is the primary direct *pramāṇa*.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Directness is measured by freedom from mediation and karmic obstruction, not merely
by sensory vividness.

**Distinction.** ✅ Do not map Jaina *pratyakṣa* mechanically onto Nyāya *pratyakṣa*; the same term carries a
different doctrinal weight in each system.

**Example.** ✅ Seeing a pot with the eyes is conventionally direct in ordinary dealings, but **kevala-jñāna**
is direct in the strict spiritual sense.

**Objection (Nyāya objection).** ✅ Nyāya may object that perception through sense-contact is the paradigm of
direct cognition.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that sense-contact is direct only *pragmatically*; metaphysically it is
mediated by organs, mind and knowledge-obscuring karma.

## 3B.3 Technical doctrine: the five kinds of *jñāna*, exactly and in graded order ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism classically enumerates **five kinds of jñāna** (knowledge): **mati**, **śruta**,
**avadhi**, **manaḥparyāya** and **kevala**.

| Jñāna | Status | Meaning | UPSC trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mati-jñāna** | ✅ *Parokṣa* / conventional | sense-and-mind cognition | ✅ Ordinary perception is not strict *pratyakṣa* |
| **Śruta-jñāna** | ✅ *Parokṣa* | scriptural, verbal or sign-mediated knowledge derived from *mati* | ✅ Not blind faith; depends on cognition and communication |
| **Avadhi-jñāna** | ✅ *Mukhya* or *pāramārthika pratyakṣa* | limited clairvoyant direct knowledge of material things | ✅ Direct but limited in range |
| **Manaḥparyāya-jñāna** | ✅ *Mukhya* or *pāramārthika pratyakṣa* | direct knowledge of others' mental modes | ✅ Higher than *avadhi*, still not omniscience |
| **Kevala-jñāna** | ✅ *Mukhya* or *pāramārthika pratyakṣa* | unlimited, unmediated omniscience | ✅ The full overcoming of cognitive obstruction |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. *Mati* and *śruta* are mediated and therefore indirect in the strict classification.
2. *Avadhi*, *manaḥparyāya* and *kevala* are direct because the soul knows without ordinary sense-and-mind
   mediation.
3. The series shows a **graded release from karmic veiling** — the more obstruction is removed, the more
   direct and complete the knowledge.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Knowledge is graded according to the degree to which karmic obscuration is removed.

**Distinction.** ✅ The five *jñānas* are **not** five independent *pramāṇas* in the Nyāya sense; they are
Jaina modes of knowledge mapped onto the process of bondage and purification.

**Example.** ✅ A student's study of doctrine uses *mati* and *śruta*; the *kevalin*'s omniscience is
immediate and total.

**Objection (Cārvāka-style objection).** ✅ *Avadhi*, *manaḥparyāya* and *kevala* appear unverifiable to
ordinary experience.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism treats them as necessary consequences of its soul-karma theory; a critic may still
reject them if the ontology of *jīva* and karmic obstruction is denied — this external critique is assessed
in full in Lesson 8.

## 3B.4 Wrong cognition: *ajñāna* under *mithyā-darśana* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism recognises **mati-ajñāna** (or *kumati*), **śruta-ajñāna** (or *kuśruta*), and
**vibhaṅga-jñāna** as **wrong cognition**.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Wrongness is not always the mere absence of content.
2. Cognition becomes spiritually wrong when held under **mithyā-darśana** (false vision or wrong
   orientation).
3. **Kumati** is misoriented sensory-mental cognition; **kuśruta** is misoriented verbal/scriptural
   cognition; **vibhaṅga-jñāna** is perverted *avadhi*.
4. ⚠️ The content may have some descriptive accuracy, but its *orientation* is false because it is governed
   by delusion.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Epistemology and ethics interpenetrate: how one is disposed affects how one knows.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jaina error is not only a logical mistake; it can be a **karmically conditioned mode of
seeing**.

**Example.** ✅ A learned person may quote doctrine correctly but use it for pride, sectarian contempt or
violence; the verbal cognition then functions as *kuśruta* despite its correct-looking content.

**Objection (Nyāya objection).** ✅ Calling cognition wrong because of spiritual orientation seems to confuse
truth with moral psychology.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that bondage affects the knower as a whole; epistemic correctness without
right vision remains incomplete for liberation.

## 3B.5 Later Jaina logic and indirect *pramāṇas* ⚠️

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism admits **anumāna** (inference) and **śabda** (testimony/verbal authority) within
*parokṣa*, and later Jaina logicians give a richer account of indirect knowledge.

**Argument, step by step.** ⚠️
1. ✅ **Akalaṅka**, **Māṇikyanandi**, **Hemacandra** and **Yaśovijaya** are the important names for the
   developed Jaina *pramāṇa* tradition (previewed in Lesson 1, §1.3).
2. ⚠️ Later Jaina logicians accept **smṛti** (recollection), **pratyabhijñā** (recognition), **tarka**/*ūha*
   (reasoning that grasps pervasion or *vyāpti*), and **anumāna** as valid indirect knowledge in appropriate
   contexts.
3. ✅ This is a notable contrast with Nyāya, which does **not** treat memory as an independent *pramāṇa*.
4. ⚠️ The Jaina move protects continuity, recognition and inferential preparation as genuine cognitive
   achievements without making them omniscient knowledge.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Valid cognition need not be restricted to fresh sense-contact; mediated cognition can
still disclose real features of the object.

**Distinction.** ✅ **Śabda** is accepted as mediated knowledge, **not** as a creator-God's revelation;
Jainism remains non-theistic about cosmic creation (Lesson 1, §1.6).

**Example.** ✅ Recognising "this is the same teacher I met earlier" is not mere raw perception; it involves
memory and recognition.

**Objection (Nyāya objection).** ✅ Nyāya can argue that memory merely reproduces past knowledge and is not a
new *pramāṇa*.

**Reply.** ⚠️ The Jaina reply is that recollection and recognition can be **valid cognition** when they
successfully connect present awareness with a real object or past experience.

## 3B.6 The critical link: *pramāṇa* → *nayavāda* → *syādvāda* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ *Anekāntavāda* is not an arbitrary logical device; it is the **epistemological consequence**
of Jaina metaphysics plus Jaina theory of knowledge — this is the single most important argument in the
entire Jainism topic, because it shows *why* the logic of Lessons 4–5 is forced on Jainism rather than
merely stipulated.

**Argument, step by step (the full derivation).** ✅
1. Reality is **ananta-dharmātmakaṃ vastu**: the real object has infinitely many aspects, properties and
   modes.
2. Ordinary human cognition is **kṣāyopaśamika** (arising from partial destruction-cum-subsidence of
   knowledge-obscuring karma), and is therefore necessarily partial.
3. A judgment made by a non-omniscient knower captures an aspect, not the whole object.
4. A partial judgment is true only relative to its standpoint; this is **nayavāda** (Lesson 4).
5. Absolutising a partial standpoint yields **ekāntavāda** (one-sidedness), the root error of rival schools
   according to Jainism.
6. Therefore assertion must carry the operator **syāt** ("from a certain standpoint"); this is **syādvāda**
   (Lesson 4).
7. The sevenfold schema of qualified predication is the disciplined expression of that standpoint-
   relativity; this is **saptabhaṅgī** (Lesson 5).
8. Only **kevala-jñāna** grasps all aspects at once, because only omniscience is no longer karmically
   partial (Lesson 7).

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Finite cognition is not false simply because it is partial; it becomes false only
when it claims unconditioned finality.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jaina relativity is **not** subjectivism: the aspects are really in the object, but finite
knowers access them partially — restated here because §3B.6 is the exact point where epistemology proves
this realist reading is forced, not merely asserted.

**Example.** ✅ "The jar exists" is true with respect to its present substance-mode-place-time complex, but
false with respect to another place, another time, or another incompatible mode.

**Objection (Śaṃkara-style objection, previewed here, fully assessed in Lesson 5 and Lesson 8).** ✅ If all
assertions are standpoint-relative, the assertion of standpoint-relativity may also lose absolute force.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism accepts reflexive qualification: *syādvāda* too is asserted **syāt**, and its force lies
in disciplined anti-absolutism rather than in an unqualified dogma.

## 3B.8 Comparative treatment: Jaina *pramāṇa* scheme against rival schools ✅

| School | *Pramāṇas* admitted | Treatment of memory | Perception's status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jainism** | *Pratyakṣa* and *parokṣa*; developed accounts add *anumāna*, *śabda*, *smṛti*, *pratyabhijñā*, *tarka* | ✅ Accepted as valid cognition (*smṛti*, *pratyabhijñā*) | Ordinary perception is only *sāṃvyavahārika* (conventional) *pratyakṣa* — strictly *parokṣa* |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ Nyāya keeps "direct" tied to sense-contact because its ontology does not include a
soul capable of unmediated cognition once karmic obstruction is removed; Jainism's inverted use of
*pratyakṣa* is only intelligible once its soul-karma metaphysics (Lesson 3A, Lesson 6) is granted — the two
usages of the *same word* are not comparable across the divide without first noting the differing
metaphysical commitments behind them.

## 3B.9 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (Cārvāka/empiricist objection).** ✅ *Avadhi*, *manaḥparyāya* and *kevala-jñāna* are
unverifiable by ordinary experience and should not be admitted into a serious epistemology.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Within Jaina metaphysics, these forms of knowledge are the necessary consequence of a
soul that is intrinsically omniscient but karmically obstructed by degrees; they are not ad hoc additions
but graded points on a single scale of obstruction-removal. A critic who denies the soul-karma ontology
will, consistently, deny these knowledges too — but that is then a dispute about ontology, not a special
weakness of Jaina epistemology alone.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ This reply is internally consistent, but it does concede that the higher
*jñānas* cannot be independently, externally verified — a genuine cost, assessed again in Lesson 8's
graded verdict.

**Mains practice (15 marks)**

*Question:* Explain the Jaina classification of knowledge into five kinds, and show how it grounds the
theory of standpoints.

*Model answer.* Jaina epistemology enumerates five kinds of *jñāna*: *mati* (sense-and-mind cognition),
*śruta* (verbal/scriptural knowledge derived from *mati*), *avadhi* (limited clairvoyant knowledge of
material things), *manaḥparyāya* (direct knowledge of others' mental states), and *kevala* (unlimited
omniscience). The first two are classed as *parokṣa* (indirect); the last three, remarkably, are classed
as strict *pratyakṣa* (direct) — because in Jainism, "direct" means knowledge the soul possesses without
mediation by sense-organs or mind, a criterion ordinary sense-perception fails, being only conventionally
direct (*sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa*). This graded scheme tracks the progressive removal of
knowledge-obscuring karma: the more obstruction is removed, the more direct and comprehensive the
knowledge. Because only *kevala-jñāna* is total, every judgement made by a non-omniscient (i.e., ordinary
embodied) knower necessarily captures one aspect of a many-aspected reality (*ananta-dharmātmakaṃ vastu*)
rather than the whole. Such a partial judgement is true only relative to the standpoint from which it is
made — this is precisely the doctrine of *nayavāda* — and honest assertion of a partial judgement requires
the qualifier *syāt* ("in some respect"), which is *syādvāda*. The five-fold classification of knowledge
therefore is not a free-standing curiosity; it is the epistemological ground that makes the entire theory
of standpoints and conditional predication necessary rather than arbitrary. *(Why this earns marks: it
lists all five jñānas with correct pratyakṣa/parokṣa status, explains the inversion, and completes the
full derivation into nayavāda/syādvāda rather than stopping at classification.)*

## 4.1 Simple intuition

Think of a very large object — say, a mountain — and seven photographers standing at seven different spots
around its base. Each photograph is genuine, accurate, and yet incomplete: none shows "the whole mountain
from everywhere at once." *Nayavāda* names exactly this situation for **judgements**: each *naya* is a
legitimate, standpoint-bound photograph of reality, true as far as it goes, false only if someone insists
their one photograph is the entire mountain. *Syādvāda* is the discipline of always captioning each
photograph with "from this angle..." so no photograph gets mistaken for the whole.

## 4.2 Technical doctrine: *nayavāda*, standpoint-theory ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Nayavāda** teaches that every ordinary judgement expresses a **naya**, a standpoint that
captures one aspect of the real.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Reality is many-sided (Lesson 2).
2. Finite knowers rarely grasp all sides at once (Lesson 3, Part B).
3. Therefore judgements are aspectual, not exhaustive.
4. A doctrine of standpoints is needed to explain how partial truth is possible without collapsing into
   either dogmatism or scepticism.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Partiality is not falsehood; it becomes falsehood only when the partial is
absolutised.

**Distinction.** ✅ Nayavāda is **not** scepticism. It permits truth, but *qualified* truth.

**Example.** ✅ A jar may be described as clay, as container, as round, as breakable, as existing-now. Each
statement is true from a standpoint.

**Objection (Nyāya-style objection).** ✅ Standpoints may proliferate without limit — how many "angles" are
there, really?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jaina thinkers classify **principal** standpoints (the seven *nayas* below) to discipline
rather than dissolve discourse — the list is a finite, teachable taxonomy of the *kinds* of partiality that
recur, not an open-ended catalogue.

## 4.3 Technical doctrine: the seven *nayas*, exactly and in order ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Classical Jainism enumerates **seven *nayas***, each marking a characteristic standpoint.

| # | Naya | Brief meaning |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Naigama naya** | common or teleological standpoint; mixed, unspecialised practical view |
| 2 | **Saṃgraha naya** | generic standpoint; emphasis on class-character |
| 3 | **Vyavahāra naya** | practical or particular standpoint; ordinary usage about specific things |
| 4 | **Ṛjusūtra naya** | momentary or present-state standpoint |
| 5 | **Śabda naya** | verbal standpoint; attention to linguistic expression |
| 6 | **Samabhirūḍha naya** | etymological or strictly differentiated verbal standpoint |
| 7 | **Evambhūta naya** | actuality-conditioned verbal standpoint; a term applies only when the function is actually being performed |

**Argument.** ✅ The list shows that partiality may arise from **pragmatic, generic, particular, temporal
and linguistic conditioning** — five distinct sources of one-sidedness, not a single undifferentiated
"perspective."

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Language itself contributes to one-sidedness if its scope is not critically examined
— hence the last three *nayas* (*śabda*, *samabhirūḍha*, *evambhūta*) are specifically **linguistic**
standpoints, progressively stricter about when a word may correctly apply.

**Distinction.** ✅ Do **not** confuse the seven *nayas* (standpoints) with the **seven predications** of
*saptabhaṅgī* (Lesson 5). The former are epistemic perspectives on how a judgement is formed; the latter is
a structured propositional/logical schema for how a judgement should be *asserted*.

**Example.** ✅ Calling a person "a singer" may be true generically (*saṃgraha*) or dispositionally
(*vyavahāra*), but **evambhūta naya** is stricter: the term applies only in the relevant
performance-actuality — i.e., only while the person is actually singing.

**Worked micro-example across all seven, using one word — "king":**

```text
naigama:        "He is a king" — said even while walking to be crowned (purposive/anticipatory usage)
saṃgraha:        "He is a king" — said as a member of the general class "rulers"
vyavahāra:       "He is THIS particular king, ruling THIS particular kingdom"
ṛjusūtra:        "He is king NOW, at this present moment of holding the office"
śabda:           "He is correctly called 'king' according to current grammatical/verbal usage"
samabhirūḍha:    "'King' and 'ruler' are NOT synonyms here — each word is held to its precise sense"
evambhūta:       "He is truly 'king' ONLY while actually performing the function of ruling right now"
```

**Objection (examiner's objection).** ✅ The linguistic *nayas* seem overly scholastic — do real disputes
actually turn on such fine verbal distinctions?

**Reply.** ⚠️ They matter because many philosophical disputes **are** verbal absolutisations of context-bound
usage; the finer *nayas* exist precisely to name and defuse these disputes rather than to multiply jargon
for its own sake.

## 4.4 Technical doctrine: *syādvāda* as the logical/linguistic expression of *anekāntavāda* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Syādvāda** is the doctrine that assertions should be made with the qualification
**syāt** — "in some respect," "from a certain standpoint," or "conditionally."

**Translation trap (very high-yield).** ✅ Although **syāt** can literally suggest "perhaps" or "may be," it
does **not** express mere probability, doubt, agnosticism or scepticism here. It marks a **definite**
assertion restricted by standpoint, substance, place, time and mode. "The jar conditionally exists" is
**not** a guess that the jar probably exists — it is a precise, qualified, and fully asserted claim.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Since reality is many-sided, categorical (unqualified) assertion is risky.
2. Since judgements arise from standpoints (§4.2), language must register conditionality.
3. The particle **syāt** prevents absolutisation.
4. Hence *syādvāda* is the logical-linguistic expression of *anekāntavāda*.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Good logic mirrors ontological complexity and epistemic modesty — the discipline of
language should track the actual structure of what is being described.

**Distinction (restated for emphasis — this is the master distinction of the whole topic).** ✅
*Anekāntavāda* = metaphysical doctrine (reality is many-sided); *nayavāda* = theory of standpoints (a
knower approaches reality from one angle at a time); *syādvāda* = mode of conditional predication (language
must register that angle explicitly).

**Example.** ✅ "The jar exists" becomes "*syād asti*" — it exists in some respect, e.g., as present on the
floor now.

**Objection (Śaṃkara-style objection, previewed here, fully assessed in Lesson 5 and Lesson 8).** ✅
Constant qualification may weaken assertoric force — if everything is hedged, has anything actually been
claimed?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jaina logic accepts that precision is preferable to dogmatic simplicity; a qualified claim is
still a genuine, testable, fully asserted claim — it is simply honest about its own scope.

## 4.5 How the seven *nayas* feed into *syādvāda* (the missing link most candidates never state explicitly)

**Argument.** ⚠️ A *naya* identifies *which* aspect a judgement is tracking (the generic aspect? the
particular aspect? the present-moment aspect? the strict-usage aspect?). *Syādvāda* then supplies the
*linguistic discipline* — the "*syāt*" qualifier — that makes that *naya*-bound judgement honestly stated
rather than silently absolutised. In other words: **naya selects the angle; syāt announces the angle.** A
judgement formed under *vyavahāra naya* ("this particular jar exists") becomes fully disciplined Jaina
assertion only when stated as "*syād asti*" — conditionally, this particular jar exists (now, here, in this
mode) — leaving room for other, equally true, differently-angled judgements about the same jar.

## 4.6 Comparative treatment ✅/⚠️

| Axis | Jainism (naya + syāt) | Nyāya | Advaita (adhyāropa-apavāda method) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handles partial viewpoints by... | naming a finite taxonomy of standpoints and qualifying assertion | treating each *pramāṇa*-yielded judgement as simply true or false, without a standpoint-relativising operator | provisionally affirming a lower-order description, then negating/superseding it at a higher order of understanding |
| Multiple "true" descriptions of one object? | ✅ Yes, provided each is standpoint-indexed | Generally no special apparatus for this; conflicting claims are adjudicated as true/false directly | ⚠️ Yes, but hierarchically — the lower description is finally sublated, not co-affirmed |
| Risk flagged by critics | dissolving assertoric force through constant qualification | naïve realism about which single description is "the" description | risk of making all finite descriptions ultimately unreal/*mithyā* |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ Nyāya's realism does not need a standpoint-operator because it does not accept
that reality itself has the kind of many-sidedness Jainism posits; conflicting descriptions, for Nyāya, are
adjudicated as simply true or false by the relevant *pramāṇa*. Advaita's method looks superficially similar
to *naya*-qualification (both allow provisional truths) but differs sharply in destination: Jaina
standpoints remain **co-equally real** descriptions of a real object, while Advaita's provisional
descriptions are eventually **sublated** in favour of non-dual realisation. This distinction is worth
stating explicitly in a "compare" question, because it is exactly the kind of point examiners reward and
most candidates miss.

## 4.7 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (proliferation objection, Nyāya-style, restated from §4.2 with full reply).** ✅ If standpoints
can multiply without limit, has Jainism actually disciplined anything, or merely renamed the problem of
perspectives?

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ The **seven-naya taxonomy is exactly the discipline**: rather than admitting unlimited,
unclassified "points of view," Jainism identifies the five *recurring sources* of partiality — purpose,
generality, particularity, temporality, and (in three graded strengths) language — and requires every
assertion to be traceable to one of these seven, prefixed with *syāt*. This converts an open-ended worry
about perspectives into a closed, teachable, seven-member taxonomy.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ The reply is methodologically strong, but a critic may still ask whether these
exact seven categories are *complete* (do they really exhaust every kind of partiality?) or merely
traditionally fixed — a genuinely open question that a strong answer should acknowledge rather than
suppress.

*Question:* Distinguish *anekāntavāda*, *nayavāda* and *syādvāda*.

*Model answer.* The three doctrines form a dependence relation rather than a list of synonyms.
*Anekāntavāda* is **ontological**: every real has multiple aspects because substance persists while modes
change (Lesson 2). *Nayavāda* is **epistemological**: a finite knower approaches that complex real from a
partial standpoint, one of seven recurring kinds — purposive, generic, particular, momentary, and three
graded linguistic standpoints. *Syādvāda* is **semantic-logical**: every finite assertion should be
qualified by *syāt* ("in some respect"), so that a standpoint-bound judgement is not mistaken for an
exhaustive one. A gold ornament illustrates the sequence: the real includes enduring gold and changing
ring/bracelet modes (anekāntavāda); a *vyavahāra*-naya judgement stresses "this particular ornament";
stated with full Jaina discipline, this becomes "*syād asti*" — the ornament conditionally exists, in this
respect, now. Confusing the three doctrines makes Jainism look like verbal relativism; correctly ordered,
metaphysics grounds standpoint, and standpoint grounds qualified predication. *(Why this earns marks: it
defines each level precisely, shows the causal/dependency order, uses one coherent worked example across
all three terms, and prevents the standard synonym error.)*

**Caption.** The seven predications are not seven unrelated sayings to memorise in isolation — they are the
*complete combinatorial expansion* of three primitives (is / is-not / inexpressible) under the standpoint
operator *syāt*. Seeing this generative structure is what allows exact, in-order recall under exam
pressure, rather than an unreliable list learned by rote.

## 5.1 Simple intuition

Suppose someone asks you, all at once and in one breath: "Does this jar exist?" — meaning simultaneously
*right now, as clay, as this shape, in this place, compared with every other time, shape, and place it
could be described in.* You cannot answer with a single "yes" or "no" without misrepresenting something.
*Saptabhaṅgī* is Jainism's disciplined answer to exactly this demand: instead of one flat "yes" or "no," it
supplies **seven precisely defined ways** of answering, each qualified, so that every legitimate aspect of
the "does it exist?" question gets a truthful, non-contradictory answer.

## 5.2 Technical doctrine: *saptabhaṅgī*, the seven predications, exactly and in order ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The **saptabhaṅgī** is the sevenfold schema of conditional predication produced by
combining affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under the qualifier **syāt**.

1. **syād asti** — in some respect, it is.
2. **syād nāsti** — in some respect, it is not.
3. **syād asti ca nāsti ca** — in some respect, it is and it is not.
4. **syād avaktavyam** — in some respect, it is indescribable.
5. **syād asti ca avaktavyam** — in some respect, it is and is indescribable.
6. **syād nāsti ca avaktavyam** — in some respect, it is not and is indescribable.
7. **syād asti ca nāsti ca avaktavyam** — in some respect, it is, is not, and is indescribable.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. A thing may be affirmed under one standpoint.
2. Negated under another.
3. Both affirmed and negated under different references or times (successively, not simultaneously in the
   same respect).
4. Called indescribable where simultaneous exhaustive predication fails.
5. Combinations of these three primitives yield exactly seven basic structured forms.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Contraries need not violate logic when they are indexed to standpoint, time,
relation, or mode.

**Distinction.** ✅ "Indescribable" (*avaktavya*) does **not** mean mystical silence, simpliciter. It means
that an **unqualified or simultaneous** statement is not straightforwardly expressible in one utterance —
it is a semantic-modal limitation of language attempting simultaneity, not an admission of total ignorance.

**Example.** ✅ A clay jar exists now on the table; it does not exist as a lump of raw clay; it both is and
is not under different modal descriptions; in a complex state of transition it may be called *avaktavya*.

**Objection (Śaṃkara/Rāmānuja objection).** ✅ Critics say this violates the law of non-contradiction.

**Reply.** ✅ The Jaina response is that contradiction would arise only if affirmation and negation were made
**in the same respect, at the same time, in the same sense**. *Syāt* blocks that absolutist reading — every
one of the seven forms carries an implicit "with respect to X" that the objection ignores.

## 5.3 Application 1: the parable of the blind men and the elephant ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The familiar illustration of the blind men and the elephant captures the spirit of
*anekāntavāda*: each grasped part yields a partial truth wrongly elevated into a whole truth.

**Argument.** ⚠️ The illustration dramatises the difference between **partial correctness** and **exclusive
completeness**.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Cognitive violence begins when a part is mistaken for the whole.

**Distinction.** ✅ The parable illustrates non-absolutism generally; it is not itself the formal doctrine of
*saptabhaṅgī* — it is pedagogy for the whole system (anekāntavāda + nayavāda + syādvāda), placed here
because it is most often invoked when *teaching* the sevenfold schema.

**Example.** ✅ One touches the trunk and says "the elephant is like a serpent"; another touches the leg and
says "like a pillar." Each is conditionally right and absolutely wrong.

**Objection (pedagogical objection).** ✅ The parable may trivialise complex metaphysics.

**Reply.** ⚠️ It is pedagogical, not probative; its use is to clarify the *structure* of partial truth, not
to substitute for the formal argument.

## 5.4 Application 2: worked example of all seven predications on one object

```text
OBJECT: a particular clay jar, on the table, right now

1. syād asti              — the jar EXISTS as itself, here, now, in this substance-place-time-mode
2. syān nāsti              — the jar does NOT exist AS cloth, or in another place, or at another time
3. syād asti ca nāsti ca   — successively, the jar IS (as itself here) and IS NOT (as cloth, elsewhere) —
                              two respects stated together, never the same respect at the same instant
4. syād avaktavyam         — asked to state existence AND non-existence SIMULTANEOUSLY in one unqualified
                              breath, the jar's status is INEXPRESSIBLE in that single utterance
5. syād asti ca avaktavyam — the jar exists (as itself) AND its simultaneous total status is inexpressible
6. syān nāsti ca avaktavyam— the jar does not exist (as cloth) AND its simultaneous total status is
                              inexpressible
7. syād asti ca nāsti ca avaktavyam — the jar is, is not, AND is inexpressible — the fully combined form
```

**Caption.** Predicates 5–7 are not new content; they are predicates 1, 2, and 3 respectively, **each
additionally combined with predicate 4** — this is why the schema is called a combinatorial "closure": once
you have asti, nāsti and avaktavyam, the remaining four bhaṅgas simply pair avaktavyam with each of the
first three.

## 5.6 Standard objection: is *anekāntavāda* self-refuting or trivial? ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Critics argue either that *anekāntavāda* is **self-refuting** — because the claim that "all
reality is many-sided" is itself made absolutely — or **trivial**, because everyone already admits
contextual variation.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. If every assertion must be qualified, then the doctrine itself must be qualified.
2. If qualified, it may lose universal force.
3. If unqualified, it violates its own rule.
4. Alternatively, if it merely says that things can be looked at in different ways, it seems
   philosophically thin.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ A doctrine of relativity must explain its own status.

**Reply.** ✅
1. Jainism need not deny that the doctrine itself is also expressible from a standpoint.
2. This does not collapse it; it exhibits the very discipline it teaches.
3. The thesis is not that all statements are equally true, but that truth is aspect-conditioned.
4. Its philosophical bite lies in reconciling plurality with realism, not in banal relativism.

## 5.7 2022 PYQ doctrine: does Jaina non-absolutism presuppose absolutism? ⚠️

**Statement.** ⚠️ A standard challenge says that to say reality is many-sided, one must already know
**absolutely** that it is so; therefore *anekāntavāda* covertly presupposes absolutism.

**Argument of the challenge.** ✅
1. The doctrine claims universal scope.
2. Universal scope appears absolute.
3. Therefore the doctrine seems to rely on the very absolutism it criticises.

**Jaina reply.** ✅
1. Non-absolutism does not deny more comprehensive knowledge; it denies **one-sided exclusivism** by finite
   knowers.
2. The doctrine is a methodological and ontological caution against *ekānta*, not a ban on all graded
   comprehensiveness.
3. Within Jainism, **kevala-jñāna** represents total knowledge, while ordinary discourse remains
   standpoint-bound.
4. Hence the charge succeeds only if one confuses finite judgement with omniscient cognition.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ This is a subtle and strong reply: Jainism can admit a difference between omniscience and
ordinary language without abandoning its critique of dogmatic partiality. This exact question is solved as
a full 15-mark PYQ answer in Lesson 8, §8.5 (2022 Q6(b)).

## 5.8 Comparative treatment: how rival logics handle the same seven-fold demand ✅/⚠️

| Approach | How it treats "is / is not / both / neither" | Contrast with Jaina *saptabhaṅgī* |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical bivalent logic (Aristotelian-style)** | Every proposition is either true or false; "P and not-P" is a contradiction, full stop | Jainism agrees within one unqualified respect, but denies that "is" and "is not" are ever asserted in the *same* unqualified respect — the disagreement is about how many respects a real proposition implicitly carries |
| **Buddhist catuṣkoṭi (four-cornered negation, used in some Madhyamaka contexts)** | Denies all four of "is," "is not," "both," "neither" of an ultimate — a strategy of systematic negation | Jainism's *saptabhaṅgī* is a strategy of systematic **qualified affirmation and negation**, not blanket denial; the destination differs sharply — Jainism keeps a real object standing, the *catuṣkoṭi* strategy in its Madhyamaka use empties the object of independent essence |
| **Jaina saptabhaṅgī** | Seven qualified forms, each standpoint-indexed, applied to a **really existing** substance-with-modes | Realist throughout: the different bhaṅgas describe one real object from different angles, never dissolving the object |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ The point of maximal contrast is with Madhyamaka-style fourfold negation: both
schemes look superficially "non-classical," but Jainism's seven forms are in the service of a **realist**
metaphysics (Lesson 2–3), while the Madhyamaka fourfold negation (where it is used to deny inherent
existence) serves an anti-essentialist, ultimately empty (*śūnya*) metaphysics. **Rubric (per the controlled
comparison convention used throughout this edition): this parallel is illustrative only and should occupy
at most one or two lines in an answer, after the Jaina case has been fully and independently established.**

## 5.9 Criticism and reply: the Śaṃkara/Rāmānuja pressure on the schema itself ✅

**Objection (Śaṃkara-style objection).** ✅ If every assertion must carry *syāt*, and *syāt* is itself
asserted only conditionally, has anything final actually been said? Constant qualification threatens to
dissolve assertoric force altogether.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Jainism accepts **reflexive qualification**: *syādvāda* too is asserted *syāt*; this
reflexive move is a **virtue**, not a weakness, because the doctrine then obeys its own rule rather than
claiming a privileged, unqualified exception for itself. ⚠️ A residual objection remains, honestly noted
here rather than argued away: if *syādvāda* is *always* qualified, a critic may still insist that it loses
final assertoric force in a way ordinary categorical logic does not — this residual tension is carried
forward, unresolved in full, to the graded verdict of Lesson 8.

**Mains practice (20 marks)**

*Question:* Does *saptabhaṅgī* violate the law of non-contradiction? Discuss with reference to the
standard objections and Jaina replies.

*Model answer.* The law of non-contradiction forbids asserting P and not-P of the same subject in the same
respect and at the same time. *Saptabhaṅgī* does not, on inspection, do this. *Syād asti* and *syān nāsti*
index predicates to different times, relations, places or modes: a jar exists here as clay-jar now; it does
not exist as cloth or as the destroyed future jar. The third predicate combines affirmation and negation
successively across different respects, not as an unqualified simultaneous contradiction. *Avaktavya* marks
the inability of one simple, unqualified utterance to express complex, differently-indexed aspects
together in a single breath — not a truth-value gap and not mystical silence. The remaining three
predicates (5, 6, 7) simply pair this expressive limitation with each of the first three forms, giving the
complete combinatorial closure of affirmation, negation and inexpressibility. Śaṃkara's and Rāmānuja's
objection remains relevant precisely when qualifications are *omitted*, or when Jainism is misread as
claiming that one subject literally possesses opposed essential attributes in the same respect. The Jaina
reply relies on the *dravya-paryāya* distinction established in Lesson 2: permanence belongs to substance,
change to mode — the very distinction that grounds the whole schema. A further, subtler objection asks
whether *syādvāda* itself, being always qualified, can ever assert anything with final force; Jainism
accepts reflexive qualification here too, treating this as disciplined consistency rather than weakness,
though the residual worry about assertoric force is not fully dissolved. *Saptabhaṅgī* is therefore
compatible with non-contradiction under explicit indexing; it becomes incoherent only when "*syāt*" is
reduced to decorative uncertainty rather than genuine standpoint-qualification. *(Why this earns marks: it
states the formal law precisely, works through all seven predicates rather than only the first three,
presents both the Śaṃkara/Rāmānuja pressure and the reflexive-qualification reply, and closes with a
qualified rather than one-sided verdict.)*

## 6.1 Simple intuition

In many traditions, "karma" is a bookkeeping metaphor — an invisible ledger of moral credits and debits.
Jainism refuses the metaphor and makes a much stronger claim: **karma just is a kind of matter.** Passion —
anger, greed, delusion — makes the soul physically "sticky," the way a damp surface gathers dust; and fine
material particles genuinely, physically adhere to it. This is Jainism's most distinctive and most
philosophically demanding move: not "your bad deeds are recorded somewhere," but "your bad deeds coat your
soul in real, subtle matter, and that coating is precisely what blocks your natural omniscience and bliss."

## 6.2 Technical doctrine: karma as subtle material substance ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jainism's distinctive doctrine is that **karma is a subtle material substance**, a form of
**pudgala** that flows toward and binds the soul.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The soul's intrinsic omniscience is obscured in empirical life.
2. This obscuration requires a real binding factor.
3. That factor is karma, conceived not merely as moral tendency but as **fine material accretion**.
4. Thus bondage is ontologically real, not merely metaphorical.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Ethical and cognitive defilement must have a concrete causal structure.

**Distinction (a crucial UPSC trap).** ✅ In Jainism, karma is **material** (*pudgala*), unlike many Hindu
presentations where karma is a moral force, merit-demerit ledger, or *adṛṣṭa* (an invisible, non-material
moral potency).

**Example.** ✅ Passions attract karmic particles just as a sticky surface gathers dust.

**Objection (Nyāya-Vedānta objection).** ✅ How can matter bind a conscious, immaterial soul?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism answers through intimate association and obscuration (the *jīva*'s *pradeśas* and
embodied co-extension, taught in Lesson 3A, §3A.4, are part of the mechanism), though critics often say the
mechanism remains conceptually difficult — assessed fully in Lesson 8.

**Five principal causes of bondage.** ✅

| Cause | Sanskrit term | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong vision | **mithyātva / mithyā-darśana** | Misapprehends soul, reality and the path |
| Non-restraint | **avirati** | Leaves conduct exposed to harmful desire and action |
| Carelessness | **pramāda** | Weakens vigilance even where doctrine is known |
| Passions | **kaṣāya** | Anger, pride, deceit/delusion and greed make karmic adhesion deep and durable |
| Activity | **yoga** | Activity of body, speech and mind opens the channel for influx |

**Causal ordering (do not present these five as an unordered list — the order carries philosophical
weight).** ⚠️ Wrong vision and ignorance *sustain* passions; activity *supplies* the influx-channel; passion
*supplies* the "stickiness." This layered ordering prevents the material account of karma from becoming
crude physicalism: **psychical disposition determines the depth and duration** of material bondage, even
though the bondage itself is genuinely material.

## 6.3 Technical doctrine: seven *tattvas* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The seven **tattvas** (real categories for liberation-analysis) are **jīva**, **ajīva**,
**āsrava**, **bandha**, **saṃvara**, **nirjarā**, and **mokṣa**.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. **Jīva** and **ajīva** identify the two broad ontological poles (Lesson 3A).
2. **Āsrava** explains how karmic matter flows toward the soul.
3. **Bandha** explains how that matter becomes bound.
4. **Saṃvara** explains the stopping of new influx.
5. **Nirjarā** explains the shedding or wearing away of accumulated karma.
6. **Mokṣa** names the final state when all karmic bondage is absent.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Metaphysics is organised around soteriology here: the list is not merely
classificatory but **therapeutic** — it exists to be *used*, not merely catalogued.

**Distinction.** ✅ The *tattva*-list moves from *what exists* to *what binds* and *what liberates* — a
progression from ontology to ethics to soteriology in seven steps.

**Example.** ✅ A person's anger is not merely a mental event; it opens *āsrava*, deepens *bandha*, and must
be countered by *saṃvara* and *nirjarā*.

**Objection (Buddhist-style objection).** ✅ The list seems to reify a permanent soul and material karma
beyond observable process.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that without *jīva* and karma-*pudgala*, bondage and liberation cannot be
explained as real transformation of an enduring knower.

## 6.4 Technical doctrine: nine *padārthas* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The nine **padārthas** are the same seven *tattvas* plus **puṇya** (merit) and **pāpa**
(demerit).

| Enumeration | Members | Exam significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Seven tattvas** | ✅ *jīva*, *ajīva*, *āsrava*, *bandha*, *saṃvara*, *nirjarā*, *mokṣa* | ✅ Standard liberation-sequence framework |
| **Nine padārthas** | ✅ the seven plus *puṇya* and *pāpa* | ✅ Makes moral valence explicit |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. *Puṇya* and *pāpa* are ethically important because meritorious and demeritorious states affect karmic
   bondage.
2. ⚠️ They can also be subsumed under *āsrava* and *bandha*, because both merit and demerit are forms of
   karmic involvement.
3. ⚠️ Hence some expositions emphasise seven for soteriological mechanics and nine for moral classification.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Even "good" karma remains karmic bondage if it keeps the soul within *saṃsāra* —
a genuinely counter-intuitive and high-value point.

**Distinction.** ✅ *Puṇya* is better than *pāpa* ethically, but **both** are to be transcended for *mokṣa*.

**Example.** ✅ Charity performed with attachment may produce *puṇya*, but liberation requires
non-attachment rather than merely pleasant rebirth.

**Objection (Mīmāṃsā-style objection).** ✅ If *puṇya* is morally valuable, why treat it as ultimately part
of bondage?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism distinguishes ethical improvement *within* *saṃsāra* from final release *beyond* all
karmic determination — the two goals are related but not identical.

## 6.5 Technical doctrine: *bhāvabandha* and *dravyabandha* — the 2024 PYQ distinction ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Bondage has two closely related forms: **bhāvabandha** (psychic bondage) and
**dravyabandha** (material bondage).

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The soul first undergoes affective-volitional corruption through passions, attachment, aversion,
   delusion and wrong disposition.
2. This inner deformation is **bhāvabandha**.
3. Corresponding karmic particles then attach materially to the soul.
4. This actual material adhesion is **dravyabandha**.
5. Thus psychic and material bondage are distinct yet interlinked — one causes the other, in that order.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Inner states are causally efficacious; moral psychology precedes material karmic
accretion — "bondage begins in thought" but does not remain merely mental.

**Distinction.** ✅
- **Bhāvabandha** = subjective, intentional, affective bondage.
- **Dravyabandha** = objective, material bondage by karmic *pudgala*.

**Example.** ✅ Anger or greed in the soul is *bhāvabandha*; the karmic matter drawn and fixed by that
passion is *dravyabandha*.

**Objection (analytical objection).** ✅ Why not collapse them into one process, since they always occur
together?

**Reply.** ✅ The distinction helps Jainism explain **both** moral agency and ontological consequence: one is
the soul's state, the other is the karmic material result — collapsing them would erase the causal
direction (psychic first, material second) that is philosophically central here.

## 6.6 Technical doctrine: karma is material *pudgala* — restated with its philosophical stakes ✅

⚠️ **Source-duplication flag.** The canonical file restates "karma is material" once in its bondage section
(§6.2 above) and again, nearly identically, in its dedicated karma-*prakṛti* section. This edition does not
re-teach the base claim a second time; instead, this sub-section adds only the material the source
introduces that is genuinely new at this point — the **bhāva-karma / dravya-karma** vocabulary pairing and
its precise contrast class.

**Statement.** ✅ **Bhāva-karma** (the soul's psychical state) attracts **dravya-karma** (material karmic
influx) — the same causal direction as *bhāvabandha*→*dravyabandha* in §6.5, now named with the alternative
technical vocabulary found in the karma-*prakṛti* literature.

**Distinction (this is genuinely new content, not a repeat).** ✅ This differs sharply from Nyāya
**adṛṣṭa**, Mīmāṃsā **apūrva**, Sāṃkhya **saṃskāra**, and Buddhist **vāsanā** — none of which are material
karmic particles; all are non-material moral/psychological residues or potencies.

**Objection (Nyāya-Vedānta objection, restated with its full residual difficulty).** ✅ How can material
karma bind an immaterial conscious soul?

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that the embodied *jīva* is not absolutely non-spatial: it has *pradeśas*, is
co-extensive with the body, and is modified by *bhāva*-karma; karmic matter is attracted through passions.
⚠️ A residual difficulty remains because interaction between consciousness and matter still demands
explanation — this is carried forward, unresolved, to Lesson 8's graded verdict.

## 6.7 Technical doctrine: eight *karma-prakṛtis* ✅

| Class | Karma-prakṛti | What it obscures or determines | Philosophical significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ghātiyā** (destructive) | ✅ **Jñānāvaraṇīya** | knowledge | ✅ Blocks the soul's intrinsic knowing |
| **Ghātiyā** | ✅ **Darśanāvaraṇīya** | perception or apprehension | ✅ Blocks direct awareness |
| **Ghātiyā** | ✅ **Mohanīya** | right vision and conduct | ✅ Most dangerous because delusion misorients the entire path |
| **Ghātiyā** | ✅ **Antarāya** | energy, power, capacity to act | ✅ Obstructs the soul's effective power |
| **Aghātiyā** (non-destructive) | ✅ **Vedanīya** | pleasure and pain | ✅ Determines felt experience without destroying essence |
| **Aghātiyā** | ✅ **Nāma** | body, form, embodiment | ✅ Determines the type of body and individuality of embodiment |
| **Aghātiyā** | ✅ **Gotra** | status or family-standing | ✅ Determines high or low status conditions |
| **Aghātiyā** | ✅ **Āyus** | lifespan | ✅ Determines duration of embodied life |

**Statement.** ✅ The eight karmas divide into **ghātiyā** karmas, which damage or obscure the soul's
essential qualities, and **aghātiyā** karmas, which determine embodiment without directly destroying those
qualities.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. *Jñānāvaraṇīya*, *darśanāvaraṇīya*, *mohanīya* and *antarāya* are *ghātiyā* because they obstruct
   knowledge, perception, right orientation and power.
2. *Vedanīya*, *nāma*, *gotra* and *āyus* are *aghātiyā* because they structure embodied experience, body,
   status and lifespan.
3. **Mohanīya** is the most dangerous because delusion corrupts both right vision and right conduct.
4. *Mohanīya* is commonly divided into **darśana-mohanīya** and **cāritra-mohanīya**.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Liberation requires not only moral improvement but precise removal of the karmic
veils that obstruct the soul's essential nature.

**Distinction.** ✅ The *arhat* at the **sayoga-kevalī** stage (fully taught in Lesson 7, §7.7) has destroyed
*ghātiyā* karmas but still lives with remaining *aghātiyā* karmas until final release.

**Example.** ✅ A *kevalin* has omniscience because knowledge-obscuring karma is destroyed, but embodied life
continues until lifespan-determining karma is exhausted — this single fact is the key to understanding how
"living omniscience" is even conceivable (Lesson 7 develops it fully).

**Objection (Buddhist objection).** ✅ The taxonomy seems metaphysically heavy compared with a
mental-volitional account of karma.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism accepts the heaviness because it wants a realist explanation of why cognition,
embodiment, status, pleasure and lifespan are all karmically structured, rather than treating some of these
as brute biological facts unrelated to moral causation.

## 6.8 Technical doctrine: the four parameters of *bandha* ✅

| Parameter | Meaning | Governed chiefly by |
|---|---|---|
| **Prakṛti** | ✅ type of karma bound | ✅ **Yoga** (activity of body, speech and mind) |
| **Sthiti** | ✅ duration of bondage | ✅ **Kaṣāya** (passions) |
| **Anubhāga / rasa** | ✅ intensity or fruition-strength | ✅ **Kaṣāya** |
| **Pradeśa** | ✅ quantity of karmic matter bound | ✅ **Yoga** |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. **Yoga** brings karmic matter into relation with the soul and determines type and quantity.
2. **Kaṣāya** makes the bondage deep, long-lasting and intense.
3. Activity **without** passion therefore has a radically different karmic effect from activity **with**
   passion.
4. This explains the contrast between **īryāpathika bandha** (momentary bondage from activity without
   passion) and **sāmparāyika bandha** (durable bondage from passion-coloured activity).

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The moral weight of an action depends not only on external movement but on passion,
attachment and delusion.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jainism is not crude physicalism: material karma binds through **psychical states**;
passion is doctrinally decisive, not merely incidental.

**Example.** ✅ A monk's careful walking may involve bodily activity, but if free from passion it binds only
momentarily (*īryāpathika*); an angry act binds deeply because *kaṣāya* fixes the karma (*sāmparāyika*).

**Objection (Mīmāṃsā-Nyāya objection).** ✅ This mechanism seems more elaborate than needed to explain moral
consequence.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that the elaboration is the point: it explains why intention, passion,
duration, intensity and embodiment are all linked in a single karmic science, rather than left as separate,
unconnected facts.

## 6.9 Comparative treatment: Jaina material karma against rival moral-causal theories ✅

| School | Name of the moral-causal residue | Is it material? | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jainism** | Karma-*pudgala* (material karmic particles) | ✅ Yes | Adheres to soul via passion + activity; classified into 8 *prakṛtis* with 4 parameters |
| **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** | *Adṛṣṭa* | ❌ No | An unseen moral potency inhering in the self, producing future results |
| **Mīmāṃsā** | *Apūrva* | ❌ No | A potency generated by ritual action, linking act to future fruit |
| **Sāṃkhya** | *Saṃskāra* | ❌ No | Psychological impression/disposition carried in the subtle body |
| **Buddhism** | *Vāsanā* | ❌ No | Karmic "perfuming"/imprint within a stream of momentary mental events, no substance carrying it |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ All five traditions need *some* mechanism to connect past action with future
consequence across time — the shared philosophical problem is exactly the same. Jainism alone insists this
mechanism is **literally material**, because it wants karma to explain not only moral consequence but also
concrete embodiment, felt pleasure/pain, and lifespan — facts it treats as requiring a physical, not merely
psychological or ritual, substrate. The other schools keep the residue non-material because their own
ontologies (Nyāya's discrete self, Mīmāṃsā's ritual-centred cosmos, Sāṃkhya's *puruṣa*-*prakṛti* dualism,
Buddhism's substance-free stream) do not require — or, in Buddhism's case, positively forbid — a persisting
material carrier.

## 6.10 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (Nyāya-Vedānta objection, the deepest one in this lesson).** ✅ How can matter bind an immaterial
conscious soul? This is not a rhetorical difficulty; it is a genuine category-crossing puzzle.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Jainism answers through intimate association and obscuration: the embodied *jīva* has
*pradeśas* and is co-extensive with the body (Lesson 3A, §3A.4); karmic matter is drawn in through
passion-coloured activity and adheres because of this embodied, spatially-extended condition of the soul.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ Critics still find the mechanism conceptually difficult, because even granting
the *jīva* spatial co-extension with the body, the deeper question — *how can consciousness as such be
causally affected by matter as such* — is not fully dissolved, only relocated. This is one of the two or
three genuinely hardest objections in the entire syllabus item and is treated with full weight, without
being explained away, in Lesson 8's graded verdict.

## 6.12 Revision notes (Lesson 6)

1. Karma = subtle material substance (*pudgala*), **not** a moral ledger or *adṛṣṭa*-style invisible
   potency.
2. Five causes of bondage, in causal order: *mithyātva* → *avirati* → *pramāda* → *kaṣāya* → *yoga*.
3. Seven *tattvas*: *jīva*, *ajīva*, *āsrava*, *bandha*, *saṃvara*, *nirjarā*, *mokṣa*.

5. *Bhāvabandha* (psychic) causes *dravyabandha* (material) — causal direction matters; do not collapse

7. Contrast class: Nyāya *adṛṣṭa*, Mīmāṃsā *apūrva*, Sāṃkhya *saṃskāra*, Buddhist *vāsanā* — all

9. *Mohanīya* is the most dangerous karma; splits into *darśana-mohanīya* and *cāritra-mohanīya*.
10. Four *bandha* parameters: *prakṛti* & *pradeśa* ← *yoga*; *sthiti* & *anubhāga* ← *kaṣāya*.
11. 🔑 Mnemonic: **"Yoga brings the Type and Amount; Kaṣāya sets the Time and Strength"** — for the four
    parameters.
12. Trap: *sayoga-kevalī* has destroyed *ghātiyā* karma but retains *aghātiyā* karma — omniscient yet still
    embodied (full doctrine: Lesson 7).

**Mains practice (15 marks)**

*Question:* What is the distinction between *bhāvabandha* and *dravyabandha*, according to the Jainas?
Discuss. *(2024 Q6(c), reproduced here as guided practice; the fully sourced, exam-final version is solved
again in Lesson 8, §8.10, for cross-reference.)*

*Model answer.* Jainism distinguishes the psychic cause of bondage from its material embodiment.
**Bhāvabandha** is the internal state of the soul: wrong vision, ignorance, attachment and the four
*kaṣāyas* of anger, pride, deceit and greed. **Dravyabandha** is the actual binding of subtle karmic matter
to the soul. The relation is causal rather than merely classificatory: wrong vision, passion and activity
first corrupt the soul's inner disposition (*bhāvabandha*); this psychic "stickiness" then draws in and
fixes material karmic particles (*dravyabandha*). The distinction explains why bondage begins "in thought"
while still producing real bodies, capacities and experiences — it is not merely metaphorical to say karma
is material, because the eventual result is genuinely physical embodiment, felt pleasure and pain, and
determinate lifespan. The distinction also structures release: right vision and conduct remove the
affective conditions, producing *saṃvara*; austerity and equanimity promote *nirjarā* of already-bound
karma. The four parameters of material bondage — type and quantity (from *yoga*), duration and intensity
(from *kaṣāya*) — show that inner passion shapes the exact karmic result, so that ethics is not external
obedience but causal transformation of the soul's condition. Critically, the two-level theory avoids
reducing morality either to mental intention alone or to physical mechanism alone. Its weakness, honestly
stated, is the unexplained bridge by which a non-material affect attracts and fixes material particles — a
difficulty carried forward to Lesson 8. *(Why this earns marks: it defines both terms precisely, shows
their direction of causal dependence, connects them to the release-mechanism, and closes with an honest
evaluation of the category-crossing mechanism rather than a false resolution.)*

## 7.1 Simple intuition

If bondage is a matter of accumulated dust (Lesson 6's sticky-surface analogy), liberation must have two
distinct jobs: **stop new dust from settling**, and **clean off the dust that is already there**. Neither
job alone suffices — a monk who stops all new karma but never works through the old accumulation stays
bound; someone who "burns off" karma through suffering but keeps generating fresh karma through passion
never makes net progress. Jainism's entire ethical and ascetic programme is organised around doing both
jobs at once, systematically.

## 7.2 Technical doctrine: the fivefold soteriological sequence, completed ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The Jaina path is commonly described through the sequence **āsrava → bandha → saṃvara →
nirjarā → mokṣa** (introduced as a full chain already in Lesson 6, §6.0; this lesson supplies the discipline
behind the final three stages).

| Stage | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Āsrava** | influx of karmic matter into the soul |
| **Bandha** | bondage; karmic matter adheres to the soul |
| **Saṃvara** | stoppage of fresh inflow |
| **Nirjarā** | shedding or exhaustion of accumulated karma |
| **Mokṣa** | complete release from karma |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Wrong belief, passions and activity open the soul to karmic influx.
2. Influx produces bondage.
3. Discipline blocks new inflow.
4. Austerity and purification burn off old karma.
5. When all karmic matter is gone, liberation occurs.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Spiritual progress is both preventive and purgative — stopping new harm and repairing
existing harm are two genuinely different tasks.

**Distinction.** ✅ *Saṃvara* and *nirjarā* are **not** the same: one stops new karma; the other removes old
karma.

**Example.** ✅ Closing a muddy stream into a tank is like *saṃvara*; cleaning the sediment already deposited
is like *nirjarā*.

**Objection (Buddhist/Vedānta-style objection).** ✅ The process may seem mechanically moralistic — a
"karmic accounting" exercise rather than genuine insight.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Its strength is exactly its causal clarity: vice has ontological consequence; discipline has
ontological remedy — this is presented as a virtue of the system, not a concession.

## 7.3 The complete discipline of *saṃvara* and *nirjarā* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Liberation requires both stoppage of new karma (*saṃvara*) and shedding of accumulated
karma (*nirjarā*). The five vows (§7.4) are central but do **not** exhaust the standard discipline.

| Discipline | Sanskrit term | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Restraint of body, speech and mind | **gupti** | Controls the three channels of activity |
| Carefulness in movement, speech, alms, handling objects and bodily functions | **samiti** | Prevents negligent injury and fresh influx |
| Ten virtues, including forgiveness, humility, truthfulness, purity, restraint, austerity, renunciation, non-attachment and celibacy | **dharma** | Replaces passion with disciplined character |
| Contemplation on the self, impermanence, solitude, influx, stoppage and related truths | **anuprekṣā / bhāvanā** | Reorients cognition and weakens attachment |
| Patient conquest of afflictions | **pariṣaha-jaya** | Prevents pain, hunger, heat, cold and insult from producing passion |
| Right conduct | **cāritra** | Integrates vision, knowledge, vows and equanimity |
| Austerity | **tapas** | Stops passion and accelerates the wearing away of stored karma |

**Two forms of shedding.** ✅ **Natural fruition** wears karma out when it matures on its own, whereas
**disciplined austerity and purification** accelerate shedding. External austerities regulate bodily
consumption and exposure; internal austerities include repentance, humility, service, study, detachment,
meditation and abandonment of bodily identification.

**Distinction.** ✅ Stoppage is preventive; shedding is eliminative. Vows primarily close the channels of new
influx, while austerity and purified conduct also exhaust already-bound karma.

## 7.4 Technical doctrine: the three jewels and the five *mahāvratas* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Liberation requires the **three jewels (ratna-traya)**: **samyag-darśana** (right
faith/vision), **samyag-jñāna** (right knowledge), and **samyak-cāritra** (right conduct) — first named in
Lesson 1, §1.3, now developed in full.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. One must first see reality correctly.
2. One must know it correctly.
3. One must live in accordance with that insight.
4. Mere knowledge without conduct does not stop karmic influx.
5. Mere conduct without right view lacks orientation.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Cognitive, affective and practical transformation are inseparable — none of the three
jewels alone is sufficient.

**Distinction.** ✅ The three jewels are not sequential in a crude external sense; they are **mutually
reinforcing dimensions** of one path.

**Example.** ✅ One who knows non-violence conceptually but continues harmful conduct has not completed the
path.

**Objection (Cārvāka-style objection).** ✅ Is "faith" (*samyag-darśana*) here blind belief?

**Reply.** ✅ No. *Samyag-darśana* is right apprehension or trustful orientation to truth, not irrational
fideism.

**Right conduct is classically articulated through the five *mahāvratas*:**

| Vow | Basic meaning | Soteriological relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Ahiṃsā** | non-violence | central, because injury binds karma deeply |
| **Satya** | truthfulness | purifies speech and intention |
| **Asteya** | non-stealing | curbs acquisitive harm |
| **Brahmacarya** | chastity/control of sensuality | disciplines desire |
| **Aparigraha** | non-possession/non-attachment | reduces grasping and karmic adhesion |

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Ethical discipline is metaphysically effective, not merely socially good.

**Distinction.** ✅ In Jainism *ahiṃsā* is not only a moral ideal but a central **soteriological technology**.

**Example.** ✅ Extreme care toward even one-sense beings (Lesson 3A's living-being taxonomy) follows
directly from the pluralistic doctrine of *jīva*.

**Objection (householder-pragmatic objection).** ✅ Such rigour seems too severe for ordinary life.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism distinguishes full vows for ascetics and limited observances for householders,
preserving the ideal while allowing graded practice.

## 7.5 Technical doctrine: fourteen *guṇasthānas* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ The **guṇasthānas** are fourteen ordered stages marking the soul's gradual movement from
delusion to omniscience and final liberation.

| No. | Guṇasthāna | Brief gloss |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Mithyā-dṛṣṭi** | false vision; complete wrong orientation |
| 2 | **Sāsvādana / sāsādana** | falling condition with a lingering taste of right vision |
| 3 | **Miśra / samyag-mithyā-dṛṣṭi** | mixed right and wrong orientation |
| 4 | **Avirata samyag-dṛṣṭi** | right vision without full restraint |
| 5 | **Deśavirata** | partial restraint, especially lay vows |
| 6 | **Pramatta-saṃyata** | full restraint with negligence |
| 7 | **Apramatta-saṃyata** | full restraint without negligence |
| 8 | **Apūrva-karaṇa / nivṛtti-bādara** | unprecedented purification; gross passions being transformed |
| 9 | **Anivṛtti-bādara** | further advanced purification of gross passions |
| 10 | **Sūkṣma-samparāya** | only subtle greed remains |
| 11 | **Upaśānta-moha** | delusion suppressed, not destroyed |
| 12 | **Kṣīṇa-moha** | delusion destroyed |
| 13 | **Sayoga-kevalī** | omniscient embodied *arhat* with activity still present |
| 14 | **Ayoga-kevalī** | activity ceases; momentary final state before *siddhatva* |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The first three stages describe wrong, falling or mixed vision.
2. Stage 4 is decisive because **samyag-darśana** first becomes stable enough to orient the path.
3. Stages 5–7 discipline conduct through partial and full restraint.
4. Stages 8–10 refine passions from gross to extremely subtle.
5. Stages 11–12 mark the crucial difference between **suppression** and **destruction** of delusion.
6. Stages 13–14 explain how omniscience and final disembodiment are related.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Liberation is gradual, psychologically exact and karmically measurable — not a single
instantaneous flash of insight.

**Distinction.** ✅ Stage 4 is a breakthrough in right vision; it is **not yet** complete ascetic conduct.

**Example.** ✅ A layperson with right vision and partial vows belongs doctrinally closer to stage 5 than to
complete monastic purification.

**Objection (Advaita-style objection).** ✅ A fourteen-stage ladder may seem to turn liberation into a
mechanical sequence rather than insight.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism replies that insight, conduct and karmic purification must be integrated; right vision
without progressive destruction of karma is incomplete.

## 7.6 Two ladders and *jīvanmukti*: is embodied liberation possible for Jainism? ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Jaina doctrine distinguishes **upaśama-śreṇi** (ladder of suppression) and
**kṣapaka-śreṇi** (ladder of annihilation); only the annihilation ladder reaches irreversible destruction
of delusion.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. **Upaśama-śreṇi** suppresses delusion and can reach stage 11.
2. Stage 11 necessarily falls back because suppressed *moha* is not destroyed.
3. **Kṣapaka-śreṇi** destroys delusion and reaches stage 12 and beyond.
4. At stage 13 the **sayoga-kevalī** has *kevala-jñāna* but still retains embodied activity because
   *aghātiyā* karmas remain.
5. Stage 14, **ayoga-kevalī**, is the momentary cessation of activity immediately before **siddhatva**.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Suppression is spiritually unstable; destruction alone is final.

**Distinction.** ✅ The *sayoga-kevalī* is the Jaina answer to "is *jīvanmukti* possible?": the embodied
omniscient *arhat* is liberated-in-life in the Jaina sense, though the final *siddha*-state follows only
after remaining *aghātiyā* karmas are exhausted.

**Example.** ✅ A lamp with its obscuring cover removed shines fully even before the lamp is relocated;
similarly the *arhat* is omniscient while still embodied.

**Objection (Rāmānuja-style objection).** ✅ Omniscience while embodied may seem unintelligible if
embodiment itself implies limitation.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism distinguishes *ghātiyā* karmas, which obstruct essential knowledge, from *aghātiyā*
karmas, which sustain embodiment without blocking omniscience — the same distinction taught fully in Lesson
6, §6.7, now applied to answer the *jīvanmukti* question directly.

## 7.7 Technical doctrine: *kevala-jñāna* and *mokṣa* ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Kevala-jñāna** is perfect knowledge or omniscience, attained when the obstructive karmas
are fully destroyed. **Mokṣa** is the final state in which the soul is freed from all karmic matter,
regains infinite knowledge, bliss, power and perception, and rises to **siddha-śilā**.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The soul is intrinsically omniscient (Lesson 3A, §3A.4).
2. Ignorance is due to karmic obstruction.
3. Remove the obstruction and the native luminosity manifests completely — *kevala-jñāna* is
   **revelation, not production**.
4. Complete stoppage and exhaustion of karma eliminate bondage entirely.
5. The soul then manifests its intrinsic excellences unobstructed.
6. Being no longer weighed down by karmic matter, it rises to the topmost region of the cosmos.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Liberation is individual and cosmologically locatable within the Jaina universe —
not a merger into any single undifferentiated reality.

**Distinction.** ✅ Unlike Advaita *mokṣa*, this is **not** merger into impersonal Brahman. Unlike Buddhist
*nirvāṇa*, it is liberation **of** a distinct, enduring *jīva*, not the extinguishing of a self that was
never truly substantial.

**Example.** ✅ The liberated soul is a **siddha**, no longer embodied, no longer karmically entangled.

**Objection (modern philosophical objection).** ✅ Spatial ascent of a liberated soul to a specific cosmic
location may seem mythic to a modern reader.

**Reply.** ⚠️ In doctrinal exposition, one should report the cosmological picture faithfully while separating
canonical doctrine from modern philosophical evaluation — a UPSC answer should present the doctrine
accurately, and may add a brief evaluative remark, but should not silently edit out the cosmology to make
it sound more "modern."

## 7.8 Visual — liberation is subtraction, not acquisition

```text

          │
          ▼
     ĀSRAVA  (influx of karma-pudgala)
          │
          ▼
     BANDHA  (adhesion through yoga + kaṣāya)
          │
          ├──► SAṂVARA  (stops new influx)

  KEY IMAGE: cleaning a sooted lamp does NOT create light — it allows light already
  present to shine unobstructed. Mokṣa is REVELATION of the jīva's intrinsic nature,
  not the ACQUISITION of a new one.
```

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. The soul does not manufacture omniscience from nothing.
2. It removes what obscures its intrinsic cognitive and blissful capacity.
3. *Kevala-jñāna* is therefore revealed rather than produced.
4. *Mokṣa* is the purified manifestation of what the *jīva* essentially already is.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The self is intrinsically capable of perfection, but empirically veiled by
**beginningless** karma.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jaina *mokṣa* is not acquisition of God's grace, not merger into Brahman, and not
annihilation of self; it is the release of an individual *jīva* from karmic matter.

**Objection (Sāṃkhya/Vedānta objection).** ✅ If the soul is intrinsically omniscient, why was it obscured
at all — what began the obscuration?

**Reply.** ✅ The *jīva*-karma relation is **anādi** (beginningless) but **not endless**; like the
seed-and-tree or ore-and-gold analogies, there is no first temporal term, but purification can terminate
the bondage — a doctrine that blocks the demand for a first cause without fully dissolving the puzzle of
why an intrinsically perfect soul could ever have been obscured in the first place (assessed again in
Lesson 8).

## 7.9 Sallekhanā / Santhārā: death without passion ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Sallekhanā** or **santhārā** is the voluntary, gradual, ritually regulated relinquishment
of food and passions undertaken when death is unavoidable or bodily decline is incurable.

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. Bondage tracks *kaṣāya* (Lesson 6, §6.8): passion fixes karmic matter more deeply than bare activity.
2. A death faced with equanimity minimises new influx and prevents panic, attachment and aversion from
   creating fresh bondage.
3. The aim is not to end life as such but to end **passion** at the moment when life is already naturally
   or medically approaching its close.
4. The practice must be free from desire for death, desire to continue living, attachment to friends,
   recollection of pleasures, and hope of reward.
5. If such motives enter, the act becomes an **aticāra** (transgression) of the vow's proper discipline.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The karmic quality of dying depends on the inner state with which death is faced —
a direct application of the *bhāvabandha* doctrine (Lesson 6, §6.5) to the moment of death itself.

**Distinction.** ✅ Jainism distinguishes *sallekhanā* from **ātma-ghāta** (suicide): suicide is
passion-driven, sudden, violent, often concealed, and motivated by despair or aversion; *sallekhanā* is
passion-free, non-violent, gradual, supervised and public.

**Example.** ✅ An elderly ascetic or lay practitioner facing irreversible decline may gradually reduce
intake under religious supervision while cultivating forgiveness, detachment and equanimity.

**Objection (modern legal-ethical objection).** ❓ Critics in India have disputed whether *sallekhanā* is a
protected religious practice or an impermissible form of self-destruction.

**Reply.** ⚠️ The Jaina reply is that legal and ethical evaluation must distinguish intention, procedure,
publicity, non-violence and the absence of death-desire; the controversy is a useful Paper II bridge to
religion and morality, **but specific case names, holdings, dates and statutory details should not be
asserted unless separately verified** — this edition follows the canonical source's own explicit caution
here rather than reproducing an unverified citation.

## 7.10 Comparative treatment: three liberations compared ✅

| Axis | Jaina *mokṣa* | Advaita *mokṣa* | Buddhist *nirvāṇa* |
|---|---|---|---|
| What happens to individuality | ✅ Preserved — the *siddha* remains a distinct, individual, perfected *jīva* | ✅ Ultimately dissolved into non-dual Brahman | ✅ No enduring self-substance to preserve or dissolve in the first place |
| Mechanism | ✅ Subtraction: removal of material karmic obstruction | ⚠️ Removal of ignorance (*avidyā*) revealing already-existing non-duality | ⚠️ Extinguishing of craving/ignorance within a substance-free stream of events |
| Cosmological location | ✅ Rises to *siddha-śilā*, a specific topmost region | ⚠️ Not a place; a state of realisation | ⚠️ Not a place in the Jaina/Advaita sense |
| Role of grace/other-power | ✅ None — pure self-effort | ⚠️ Some traditions admit devotional grace as an aid, though jñāna is decisive in Advaita proper | ⚠️ Self-effort (with some devotional variants in later Buddhism) |

**Why they disagree.** ⚠️ The disagreement over individuality is the deepest: Jainism's entire pluralist
ontology (Lesson 3A) makes the *survival* of the individual soul the whole point of liberation, whereas
Advaita's non-dualism makes the *dissolution* of individuality (at the ultimate level) the whole point, and
Buddhism denies there was ever a substantial individual self to either preserve or dissolve. These are not
minor terminological differences — they follow necessarily from each school's prior commitment on the
metaphysics of the self (Lesson 3A vs. Advaita's *ātman-Brahman* vs. Buddhist *anātman*).

## 7.11 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (Cārvāka/empiricist objection).** ✅ A Cārvāka or modern empiricist objection rejects invisible
souls, karmic particles, omniscience and *siddha-śilā* as unverifiable.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Jainism combines testimony, inference, moral phenomenology and the internal logic of
purification to defend these doctrines, but it cannot fully satisfy a strict materialist who admits only
gross perception as a means of knowledge.

**Balanced assessment.** ⚠️ Use this objection only as an **external** critique (from outside the shared
premises of Indian soul-and-karma metaphysics); do not let it replace the **named internal** classical
objections (Śaṃkara, Rāmānuja, Nyāya, Sāṃkhya/Vedānta) that are assessed in full, with their residual
force explicitly graded, in Lesson 8.

**Conceptual MCQs**

**Mains practice (20 marks)**

*Question:* "Jaina liberation is a metaphysics of purification rather than transformation." Analyse.

*Model answer.* The statement captures the Jaina view that the soul is intrinsically endowed with infinite
knowledge, perception, energy and bliss (Lesson 3A, §3A.4). Bondage does not create a new essence; subtle
karmic matter obscures the existing capacities of *jīva*. Liberation therefore removes obstruction rather
than converting the soul into another substance. The mechanism is systematic: psychic bondage — wrong
vision, ignorance and passion — attracts material karma (*bhāvabandha* → *dravyabandha*, Lesson 6, §6.5).
*Āsrava* produces influx and *bandha* attachment. Right vision, knowledge and conduct generate *saṃvara*,
while austerity and passionlessness (*tapas*, the ten virtues, *anuprekṣā*) produce *nirjarā*. Destruction
of obscuring (*ghātiyā*) karmas reveals *kevala-jñāna*; destruction of *all* karma, including the remaining
*aghātiyā* karmas at the *ayoga-kevalī* stage, yields *mokṣa*. Purification is nevertheless transformative
at the level of *modes*: the embodied soul contracts or expands with bodies, experiences pleasure and pain,
and advances through fourteen *guṇasthānas*. The liberated *siddha* is bodiless, omniscient and beyond
rebirth, though numerically the same soul throughout — substance persists while modes are radically altered,
exactly the Jaina formula for reality established in Lesson 2 (*sat = utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya*) now applied to
the soul's own trajectory. The theory integrates ontology, ethics and soteriology without God. Its costs,
honestly stated, are the obscure interaction of soul and karmic matter, the unexplained beginninglessness
of bondage, and dependence on the contested possibility of omniscience. *(Why this earns marks: it
interprets the quotation directly, reconstructs the full causal sequence, uses *guṇasthānas* and
*kevala-jñāna* correctly, links back to the Lesson 2 reality-formula, and ends with a qualified synthesis
rather than an uncritical endorsement.)*

## 8A.1 Simple intuition for this lesson

Every philosophical system eventually has to answer the question: "why should I believe any of this, given

## 8A.2 Comparative treatment: three focused inter-school debates ✅

**8A.2.1 Jainism vs. Buddhism on reality**

| Axis | Jainism | Buddhism |
|---|---|---|
| Self | Many enduring *jīvas* | *Anātman* / no enduring self-substance |
| Reality | Substance with permanence-in-change | Strong tendency to momentariness in many schools |
| Logic of judgement | Conditional many-sided predication | Often sharper critiques of substantial identity |
| Karma | Material karmic accretion | Moral-causal continuity without material karmic particles |
| Liberation | Distinct soul freed from karma | *Nirvāṇa* without eternal self |

**8A.2.2 Jainism vs. Advaita Vedānta**

| Axis | Jainism | Advaita |
|---|---|---|
| Ultimate reality | Pluralistic | Non-dual Brahman |
| World | Real | Empirically real, ultimately dependent / *mithyā* |
| Souls | Many real *jīvas* | Ultimately one *ātman/Brahman* |
| Ignorance/bondage | Karmic material obscuration | *Avidyā* / *adhyāsa* |
| Liberation | Distinct *siddhas* | Realisation of non-duality |

**8A.2.3 Jainism vs. Cārvāka**

| Axis | Jainism | Cārvāka |
|---|---|---|
| Ontology | Pluralist realism with soul and karma | Materialist minimalism |
| Karma | Material and real | Rejected |
| Liberation | Central | Denied or emptied |
| Logic | Conditional predication | Suspicion of inference and testimony |
| Ethics | Austerity and *ahiṃsā* | This-worldly pleasure and prudence |

**Focused synthesis: Jainism vs. Buddhism (the closest and most instructive comparison).** ✅ Both are
*śramaṇa* traditions, both challenge Vedic ritual supremacy, and both reject a creator God. ✅ Jainism,
however, affirms many permanent *jīvas* and permanent-cum-changing substances, whereas Buddhism rejects
substance-self. ✅ Jaina *ahiṃsā* is far more radical because even minute living beings are *jīvas*. ✅ Jaina
karma is material karma-*pudgala*, while Buddhist karma is primarily volitional or mental-causal
continuity. ⚠️ Thus the two are close historically but sharply opposed metaphysically.

## 8A.3 Named criticisms and Jaina replies, in full ✅

**8A.3.1 Śaṃkara's critique of anekāntavāda**

**Statement.** ✅ Śaṃkara, in the *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya*, criticises Jaina *anekāntavāda* by arguing that
contradictory attributes cannot belong to the same thing in the same way.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. If the same object is said to be both existent and non-existent, determinate knowledge is threatened.
2. If everything is "somehow yes and somehow no," practical action loses clear guidance.
3. If *anekāntavāda* asserts itself absolutely, it contradicts itself.
4. If it asserts itself only conditionally, it seems to lose universal force.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ A viable metaphysics must preserve non-contradiction and assertoric clarity.

**Jaina reply.** ✅
1. Opposed predicates are indexed to distinct standpoints: **dravya** (substance), **kṣetra** (place),
   **kāla** (time) and **bhāva** (mode).
2. There is no contradiction unless affirmation and negation are made in the same respect, place, time and
   mode.
3. *Syādvāda* is itself asserted **syāt**; this reflexive qualification is a virtue because the doctrine
   obeys its own rule.
4. ⚠️ A residual objection remains: if *syādvāda* is always qualified, critics may say it loses final
   assertoric force.

**8A.3.2 Rāmānuja's critique: opposed real attributes**

**Statement.** ✅ Rāmānuja presses the charge that a real substance with really opposed attributes is
unintelligible.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. A thing cannot be genuinely both F and not-F in the same meaningful sense.
2. If the attributes are real and opposed, the substance becomes incoherent.
3. If they are not real, Jaina realism weakens.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Real predication must avoid contradiction at the level of the object, not merely at
the level of language.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ The Jaina answer again uses standpoint-indexing: the same *dravya* persists while
*paryāyas* arise and cease, so the opposition is between aspects, not within one unqualified predication.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ The reply works best if one accepts the *dravya–paryāya* distinction; it is less
persuasive to schools that reject such aspect-realism.

**8A.3.3 Dharmakīrti/Buddhist critique of a permanent-yet-changing soul**

**Statement.** ✅ A Buddhist critique associated with Dharmakīrti's causal-efficacy style of argument
challenges the Jaina soul as permanent yet changing.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. If the soul expands and contracts with the body, it undergoes real change.
2. What undergoes real change cannot be absolutely permanent.
3. A permanent entity that changes faces the **arthakriyā** (causal efficacy) dilemma: if it acts, it
   changes; if it does not change, it cannot act.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Causal efficacy and permanence are difficult to combine in one substance.

**Jaina reply.** ✅ Jainism replies through **utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya** (Lesson 2, §2.3): origination, cessation
and permanence are simultaneously real features of every *sat*; the substance is permanent *qua dravya* and
changing *qua paryāya*.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ The Buddhist objection remains strong if one denies the real distinction between
substance and modes.

**8A.3.4 Nyāya critique of the soul's expansion and material karma**

**Statement.** ✅ Nyāya criticises the Jaina view that the soul is co-extensive with the body and expands
or contracts like a lamp filling a room.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. An eternal self should not literally expand and contract.
2. If the self has parts or *pradeśas*, its eternality seems threatened.
3. Material karma is less parsimonious than an unseen moral potency such as *adṛṣṭa*.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ A permanent self should be partless or at least not spatially variable.

**Jaina reply.** ✅ Jainism invokes **pradeśas** and the lamp simile (Lesson 3A, §3A.4): consciousness can
pervade the body without being identical with gross matter, and karmic *pudgala* explains concrete
embodiment better than a merely invisible potency (Lesson 6, §6.9).

**Assessment.** ⚠️ The reply is internally coherent within Jaina pluralism, but the interaction of conscious,
*pradeśa*-bearing *jīva* and matter remains a debated point.

**8A.3.5 Sāṃkhya/Vedānta critique of intrinsic omniscience and beginningless bondage**

**Statement.** ✅ Sāṃkhya and Vedānta-style objections ask why karmic matter obscures an intrinsically
omniscient soul and what began the obscuration.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅
1. If the soul is intrinsically pure and omniscient, obscuration appears accidental and inexplicable.
2. If karma began at some time, a first cause of bondage is required.
3. If karma never began, liberation may seem impossible.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ A theory of bondage must explain both its origin and its removal.

**Jaina reply.** ✅ The *jīva*-karma relation is **anādi** (beginningless) but not endless; like
seed-and-tree or ore-and-gold analogies, there is no first temporal term, but disciplined *saṃvara* and
*nirjarā* can terminate the relation (Lesson 7, §7.8).

**Assessment.** ⚠️ The reply blocks a first-cause demand but leaves a metaphysical puzzle about why
beginningless bondage is possible for an intrinsically perfect soul.

**8A.3.6 Cārvāka/empiricist critique of omniscience and liberation**

**Statement.** ✅ A Cārvāka or modern empiricist objection rejects invisible souls, karmic particles,
omniscience and *siddha-śilā* as unverifiable.

**Argument of the critic.** ✅ Ordinary perception does not disclose karmic matter, disembodied *siddhas*,
or omniscient cognition.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Only publicly verifiable empirical entities should be admitted.

**Jaina reply.** ⚠️ Jainism combines testimony, inference, moral phenomenology and the internal logic of
purification to defend these doctrines, but it cannot satisfy a strict materialist who admits only gross
perception.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Use this objection only as an external critique; do not let it replace the named
classical objections above.

## 8A.4 Common UPSC traps — consolidated master list ✅

1. **Anekāntavāda ≠ syādvāda ≠ saptabhaṅgī.** ✅ Metaphysics / epistemic-logical method / formal sevenfold
   predication (Lessons 2, 4, 5).
2. **Karma in Jainism is material (*pudgala*).** ✅ Do not reduce it to generic moral force or *adṛṣṭa*
   (Lesson 6).
3. **Six *dravyas*, but only five *astikāyas*.** ✅ *Kāla* is a *dravya* but not an *astikāya* (Lesson 3A).
4. **Jainism rejects God as creator but not spiritual excellence.** ✅ Liberated souls and Tīrthaṅkaras are
   worthy of reverence, but there is no creator-God (Lesson 1).
5. **Bhāvabandha ≠ dravyabandha.** ✅ Psychic bondage is not identical with material karmic bondage, though
   they are linked (Lesson 6).
6. **Dharma / adharma in ontology do not mean moral merit / sin.** ✅ They are media of motion and rest
   (Lesson 3A).
7. **Seven *nayas* are not the same as seven *bhaṅgas*.** ✅ One is standpoint taxonomy; the other is
   predicational schema (Lessons 4, 5).
8. **Jainism is realist despite epistemic relativity.** ⚠️ Partial knowledge does not imply unreal objects
   (Lessons 2, 8A.9).
9. **Jaina *pratyakṣa* is inverted.** ✅ Strict *pratyakṣa* is soul-direct knowledge; ordinary
   sense-perception is only *sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa* (Lesson 3B).
10. **Puṇya is not mokṣa.** ✅ Merit may improve *saṃsāric* condition but still belongs to karmic involvement
    (Lesson 6).
11. **Ghātiyā ≠ aghātiyā karma.** ✅ *Ghātiyā* karmas obstruct essential qualities; *aghātiyā* karmas
    determine embodiment (Lesson 6).
12. **Sayoga-kevalī = Jaina jīvanmukta.** ✅ Omniscience can occur while embodied because *aghātiyā* karmas
    remain until final *siddhatva* (Lesson 7).

## 8A.9 Is Jaina philosophy pluralistic and realistic? — the 2025 PYQ doctrine, in full ⚠️

**Statement.** ⚠️ Yes, Jainism is strongly **pluralistic** and broadly **realistic**, though both labels
require nuance — this is the definitive final-synthesis question of the whole topic, and its full doctrine
belongs here rather than earlier, because it draws on every prior lesson at once.

**Why pluralistic.** ✅
1. It accepts many real souls (Lesson 3A).
2. It accepts multiple irreducible *dravyas* (Lesson 3A).
3. It rejects monistic reduction (contrast with Advaita, §8A.2.2).
4. Liberation is of many individual souls, not absorption into one absolute (Lesson 7, §7.10).

**Why realistic.** ✅
1. It treats *dravyas*, qualities and modes as objectively real (Lesson 2).
2. The external world is not illusion.
3. Knowledge aims at real objects, though only under standpoints (Lesson 4).

**Nuance.** ⚠️ Its realism is **qualified realism**, because judgement is standpoint-indexed. But
qualification of *knowledge* does not imply unreality of the *object* — this is the single sentence that
resolves most confusion about this question.

**Objection (realist objection).** ✅ If all knowledge is relative to standpoint, realism weakens.

**Reply.** ⚠️ Jainism separates ontological realism from epistemic non-absolutism: reality is objective, but
our finite access to it is partial — restated here for the last time as the capstone of the entire
metaphysics-epistemology arc built across Lessons 2 through 5.

## 8A.12 Graded verdict on Jainism ⚠️

**Strong ✅**
1. ✅ Jainism gives one of the clearest Indian accounts of how permanence and change can both be real.
2. ✅ Anekāntavāda, nayavāda and syādvāda form a disciplined sequence from ontology to epistemology to
   language.
3. ✅ Its material karma doctrine makes ethics causally serious and connects metaphysics with ascetic
   practice.
4. ✅ Its *ahiṃsā* follows directly from plural *jīvas* and karmic bondage, not from sentiment alone.

**Weak ⚠️**
1. ⚠️ Material karma binding a conscious soul remains difficult for non-Jaina metaphysics.
2. ⚠️ Omniscience and higher direct knowledges are hard to verify externally.
3. ⚠️ The system can seem over-taxonomic in karma-types, *guṇasthānas* and *nayas*.
4. ⚠️ Reflexively qualified *syādvāda* may appear to weaken its own universal claim.

**Genuinely contested ❓**
1. ❓ Sectarian nuances about *kāla*'s substantial independence differ in presentation.
2. ❓ Modern legal-ethical evaluation of *sallekhanā* requires case-specific verification before citing
   details.
3. ❓ Historical dating of several Jaina thinkers should not be asserted unless separately verified.

**Ready verdicts.** ⚠️
- **10 marks:** Jainism is a pluralistic realism that avoids both pure permanence and pure flux by making
  every real a unity of *dravya* and *paryāya*.
- **15 marks:** Its genius lies in connecting many-sided reality with conditional judgement, but its
  material karma and omniscience doctrines remain philosophically contestable.
- **20 marks:** Jainism is best evaluated as a rigorous qualified realism: internally systematic, ethically
  powerful and logically subtle, though vulnerable to external critiques of contradiction, soul-matter
  interaction and unverifiable higher knowledge.

## 8B.2 All solved PYQs, 2018–2026 (complete)

### Solved PYQ 1 — 2018 Q6(c), 15 marks

**Question:** How do the Jaina philosophers explain 'bondage'? What, according to them, is the distinction
between 'liberated soul' and 'bound soul'? What do the Jainas think about the condition of the 'liberated
soul'? Discuss.

**Model solution.** Jainism explains bondage as the real association of conscious *jīva* with subtle
material karma-*pudgala*. The soul is intrinsically endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, energy and
bliss, but passions and activities obstruct these powers (Lesson 3A, §3A.4; Lesson 6, §6.2). The mechanism
has two inseparable dimensions. **Bhāvabandha** is the psychic condition of delusion, attachment and the
four *kaṣāyas* — anger, pride, deceit and greed. It makes the soul adhesive. **Dravyabandha** is the actual
influx and binding of karmic particles to the soul (Lesson 6, §6.5).

| Bound soul | Liberated soul |
|---|---|
| embodied and co-extensive with a body | bodiless *siddha* |
| knowledge obscured by karmas | *kevala-jñāna* fully manifest |
| subject to birth, pleasure, pain and death | beyond rebirth and suffering |
| contracts or expands with embodiment | rises to *siddha-śilā* |
| possesses active karmic accretions | all karmas destroyed |

Liberation requires stopping new influx through *saṃvara* and exhausting old karma through *nirjarā*,
guided by the three jewels (Lesson 7, §7.2–§7.4). The liberated soul retains numerical individuality;
*mokṣa* is not merger into Brahman or extinction of the person (Lesson 7, §7.7, §7.10). **Critical
qualification:** the account preserves moral continuity through a permanent agent, but the interaction of
non-material consciousness with material karma remains difficult (Lesson 8A, §8A.3.4). *(Why this earns
marks: it answers all three limbs, distinguishes psychic and material bondage, names the causal sequence,
and states the liberated condition without importing Vedāntic merger.)*

### Solved PYQ 2 — 2019 Q6(a), 20 marks

**Question:** How is reality defined by the Jainas? How is this theory of reality reflected in their view
on judgements?

**Model solution.** Jaina philosophy defines the real as what possesses **origination, decay and
permanence** together: *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya-yuktaṃ sat* (Lesson 2, §2.3). A real substance persists
through changing modes. This avoids the extremes of immutable monism and Buddhist pure flux. Reality
consists of six irreducible *dravyas*: conscious *jīva* and the five forms of *ajīva* — matter, media of
motion and rest, space and time (Lesson 3A). Every substance has stable *guṇas* and changing *paryāyas*. A
gold ornament, for example, persists materially while bracelet-mode ceases and ring-mode originates. Hence
reality is *anekānta*, many-sided.

Finite cognition normally grasps one aspect at a time, because ordinary knowledge is *kṣāyopaśamika* —
karmically partial (Lesson 3B, §3B.6). **Nayavāda** identifies legitimate but partial standpoints; an error
occurs when a partial truth is absolutised. **Syādvāda** therefore prefixes predication with *syāt*, "in
some respect" or "conditionally" (Lesson 4). Its sevenfold expansion (Lesson 5, §5.2) states: (1) somehow it
is; (2) somehow it is not; (3) somehow it is and is not, successively and in different respects; (4) somehow
it is inexpressible; (5) somehow it is and is inexpressible; (6) somehow it is not and is inexpressible; (7)
somehow it is, is not and is inexpressible. The jar exists as its present substance and mode, does not exist
as another object or at another time, and becomes inexpressible when incompatible temporal-modal aspects
are demanded in one simple utterance.

Śaṃkara objects that opposite predicates cannot belong to one real and that conditional truth presupposes
an unconditional doctrine (Lesson 8A, §8A.3.1). The Jaina reply is that predicates are indexed to
standpoint, respect and time; contradiction arises only when these qualifications are suppressed.
Omniscient knowledge apprehends the whole, while ordinary statements remain partial. **Verdict:** Jaina
judgement is not sceptical relativism but the linguistic discipline demanded by a pluralistic realism. Its
strength is anti-dogmatism; its vulnerability is explaining the absolute status of *anekānta* and
omniscience. *(Why this earns marks: it links metaphysics to logic, reproduces all seven predications in
order, uses a worked example, and adjudicates the absolutism objection.)*

### Solved PYQ 3 — 2020 Q5(a), 10 marks

**Question:** Examine the concept of Karma according to Jainism. How does it bear upon their conception of
Liberation?

**Model solution.** Jainism treats karma not merely as moral law but as **subtle material pudgala**
attracted to the soul by bodily, verbal and mental activity under passion (Lesson 6, §6.2). Delusion and
the four *kaṣāyas* make the soul receptive; karmic matter then obscures its intrinsic knowledge, perception,
energy and bliss. The doctrine directly structures liberation: *āsrava* is influx, *bandha* binding,
*saṃvara* stoppage of new karma, and *nirjarā* shedding of accumulated karma (Lesson 6, §6.3; Lesson 7,
§7.2). Right vision, knowledge and conduct, together with vows and austerity, weaken passion, stop fresh
influx and mature or burn existing karmas (Lesson 7, §7.3–§7.4). When all eight basic karma-types are
destroyed, *kevala-jñāna* manifests and the soul becomes a liberated *siddha* (Lesson 6, §6.7; Lesson 7,
§7.7). The theory gives ethical acts an objective continuity without a creator God (Lesson 1, §1.6). Its
difficulty is explanatory: material particles affecting immaterial consciousness look category-crossing,
and beginningless bondage is asserted rather than historically explained (Lesson 7, §7.8; Lesson 8A,
§8A.3.5). **Verdict:** karma is the material mechanism that makes Jaina soteriology a liberation by
subtraction. *(Why this earns marks: it defines material karma, supplies the five-stage sequence, connects
practice to metaphysics, and includes a precise criticism.)*

### Solved PYQ 4 — 2021 Q7(c), 15 marks

**Question:** Explain the Jaina view of seven-fold (sapta-bhaṅgī) Naya.

**Model solution.** The sevenfold judgement is the logical expression of Jaina many-sided realism (Lesson
3B, §3B.6). *Naya* is a partial standpoint (Lesson 4, §4.2); *syāt* qualifies a proposition by respect,
time, relation or mode (Lesson 4, §4.4). Since a finite judgement cannot exhaust a real, predication must
remain conditional. The seven forms are (Lesson 5, §5.2): *syād asti* (in some respect, it is); *syān
nāsti* (in some respect, it is not); *syād asti ca nāsti ca* (in different respects/successive moments, it
is and is not); *syād avaktavyam* (in some respect, it is inexpressible); *syād asti ca avaktavyam ca* (it
is and is inexpressible); *syān nāsti ca avaktavyam ca* (it is not and is inexpressible); *syād asti ca
nāsti ca avaktavyam ca* (it is, is not and is inexpressible). A jar exists as a jar here and now, does not
exist as cloth or in a destroyed future state, and is inexpressible when its mutually indexed aspects are
demanded simultaneously in one unqualified assertion. The theory does not assert brute contradictions.
"Is" and "is not" concern different aspects. Nor is *avaktavya* mere ignorance: it marks the inability of
one simple sentence to state complex, temporally differentiated reality at once. Critics call the scheme
redundant or self-defeating (Lesson 5, §5.9); the Jaina reply is that seven possibilities systematically
combine affirmation, negation and inexpressibility, disciplining speech rather than multiplying truth-values
arbitrarily. **Verdict:** *saptabhaṅgī* is best read as standpoint-conditioned predication grounded in
realism, not as unrestricted relativism. *(Why this earns marks: it defines the doctrinal chain, gives the
exact ordered seven, applies an example, and blocks the contradiction caricature. Note: this question's own
printed title conflates "naya" and "bhaṅgī" — a strong answer gently disambiguates the two, per Lesson 4,
§4.8, rather than treating them as literally the same term.)*

### Solved PYQ 5 — 2022 Q5(d), 10 marks

**Question:** How does Jaina view of Karma bear upon their soteriology? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** Jaina soteriology depends on its material conception of karma (Lesson 6). The soul is
intrinsically omniscient and blissful, yet passions attract subtle karmic matter that obscures these
powers. Thus liberation is not divine grace or knowledge of an already non-dual identity; it is the actual
removal of material obstruction. The sequence is *āsrava → bandha → saṃvara → nirjarā → mokṣa*. Ethical
vows, non-violence, restraint and the three jewels stop fresh influx; austerity and passionlessness shed
accumulated karma (Lesson 7). Destruction of knowledge-obscuring karma reveals *kevala-jñāna*, and the fully
purified soul rises as an individual *siddha*. The theory strongly integrates metaphysics, ethics and
personal responsibility without a creator. Yet material karma binding a conscious soul is obscure, and if
bondage is beginningless the transition from original purity to obstruction is never fully explained
(Lesson 8A, §8A.3.5). **Verdict:** karma gives Jainism a rigorous technology of self-purification, though
its literal materialism creates the system's deepest interaction problem. *(Why this earns marks: it
focuses on "bearing," names the operational sequence, links vows to mechanisms, and balances explanatory
gain with cost.)*

### Solved PYQ 6 — 2022 Q6(b), 15 marks

**Question:** "The doctrine of Relativism of Jain Philosophy cannot be logically sustained without
postulating 'Absolutism'." Critically examine this view and give reasons in favour of your answer.

**Model solution.** The objection targets a possible self-reference problem (Lesson 5, §5.7). If every
claim is only relatively true, the claim "every claim is relative" must itself be relative and cannot govern
all judgements. Moreover, Jaina philosophy treats *anekāntavāda*, the reality of substances, and
*kevala-jñāna* as more than merely optional perspectives. C.D. Sharma therefore argues that Jainism makes an
exception for its own teaching. The criticism is powerful against a crude reading of Jainism as "all views
are equally true." Classical Jainism, however, is a **realist non-absolutism**, not sceptical relativism.
Reality objectively has many aspects; a *naya* is true insofar as it tracks one of them and false when
absolutised. *Syāt* indexes a statement to conditions; it does not dissolve its truth. The Jaina may
concede two levels: finite discursive cognition is partial because karmic obstruction limits the knower;
omniscient *kevala-jñāna* comprehends all aspects together. The unconditional thesis required is therefore
minimal — reality exceeds every finite unqualified judgement — not an absolutist proposition exhaustively
describing reality. Still, the reply carries a cost: if an omniscient standpoint validates the system, its
existence needs independent support, or it risks being introduced merely to rescue relativity; and the
assertion that every real has substance and modes appears itself universal. **Verdict:** Jainism cannot
avoid all absoluteness, but it need not avoid it entirely to remain coherent. Its defensible position is
qualified realism: objective many-sidedness plus the fallibility of finite predication, not the absolute
thesis that "nothing is absolute." *(Why this earns marks: it reconstructs the self-reference objection,
distinguishes relativism from non-absolutism, presents the omniscience reply, and concedes the residual
burden.)*

### Solved PYQ 7 — 2023 Q5(a), 10 marks

**Question:** "All human knowledge is empirical and therefore relative." Critically examine Jaina theory of
sevenfold judgement (saptabhaṅgīnaya) in the light of the above statement.

**Model solution.** Jainism agrees that ordinary embodied knowledge is limited and standpoint-bound, but it
does not reduce all knowledge to sensory empiricism (Lesson 3B). Its fivefold scheme includes *mati* and
*śruta* as indirect ordinary knowledge, while *avadhi*, *manaḥparyāya* and *kevala* transcend normal sense
mediation. Sevenfold judgement governs finite discursive assertion (Lesson 5). Because reality includes
enduring substance and changing modes, "the jar exists" is true from one time, place and respect; "the jar
does not exist" is true relative to another object, time or mode. The seven combinations of affirmation,
negation and inexpressibility prevent a partial empirical judgement from becoming absolute. The proposition
in the question is therefore only partly Jaina. Human embodied cognition is commonly relative, but
*kevala-jñāna* is direct, complete and non-relative. Critics may say this exception is ad hoc; the Jaina
replies that omniscience is the manifestation of the soul's intrinsic cognition after karmic obstruction
ends (Lesson 7, §7.7). **Verdict:** *saptabhaṅgī* is a discipline for finite empirical knowledge, not the
final theory of every possible cognition. *(Why this earns marks: it tests the quotation rather than
accepting it, links knowledge classification to judgement, and identifies the *kevala* qualification.)*

### Solved PYQ 8 — 2024 Q6(c), 15 marks

**Question:** What is the distinction between Bhāvabandha and Dravyabandha, according to the Jainas?
Discuss.

**Model solution.** *(Full model answer already given in Lesson 6, §6.13, as the guided Lesson 6 mains
practice; reproduced here in a slightly extended, exam-final form for the consolidated PYQ record.)*
Jainism distinguishes the psychic cause of bondage from its material embodiment. **Bhāvabandha** is the
internal state of the soul: wrong vision, ignorance, attachment and the *kaṣāyas* of anger, pride, deceit
and greed. **Dravyabandha** is the actual binding of subtle karmic matter to the soul (Lesson 6, §6.5). The
relation is causal rather than merely classificatory: *mithyātva* + passion + activity → *bhāvabandha*
(psychic adhesiveness) → karma *āsrava* → *dravyabandha* (material particles bind to the *jīva*). The
distinction explains why bondage begins "in thought" while still producing real bodies, capacities and
experiences. It also structures release: right vision and conduct remove the affective conditions,
producing *saṃvara*; austerity and equanimity promote *nirjarā* of already bound karma (Lesson 7, §7.3).
The four parameters of material bondage — type, duration, intensity and quantity (Lesson 6, §6.8) — show
that inner passion shapes the exact karmic result. Thus ethics is not external obedience but causal
transformation of the soul's condition. Critically, the two-level theory avoids reducing morality either to
mental intention or physical mechanism. Its weakness is the unexplained bridge by which a non-material
affect attracts material particles (Lesson 8A, §8A.3.4). *(Why this earns marks: it defines both terms,
shows their direction of dependence, connects them to release, and evaluates the category-crossing
mechanism.)*

### Solved PYQ 9 — 2025 Q7(c), 15 marks

**Question:** Is Jaina philosophy pluralistic and realistic? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** *(Full doctrinal grounding for this answer is taught in Lesson 8A, §8A.9.)* Jaina
philosophy is pluralistic because it recognises infinitely many individual *jīvas* and five irreducible
forms of *ajīva*, classified as six *dravyas* (Lesson 3A). It is realistic because these substances, their
qualities and modes exist independently of being known. *Anekāntavāda* does not make reality a construction
of language; it states that the real itself has many aspects (Lesson 2). Pluralism is disciplined rather
than chaotic: each substance has a distinct function — *pudgala* is material, *dharma* and *adharma* enable
motion and rest, space accommodates, and time orders succession. Every real combines persistence and change
through *dravya-guṇa-paryāya*. Hence Jainism rejects both monistic reduction and momentary flux. Its theory
of judgement follows the realism: *nayas* select objective aspects; *syādvāda* qualifies assertions;
sevenfold judgement prevents one-sided predicates from masquerading as complete descriptions. Truth is
perspective-conditioned but not merely subjective. Critics raise three costs: Śaṃkara argues opposed
predicates threaten non-contradiction; Buddhist critics reject an enduring substance beneath modes; C.D.
Sharma argues that combining partial truths does not automatically yield whole truth and that Jainism
absolutises its own relativity (Lesson 8A, §8A.3, §8A.7). Jaina replies distinguish respects and times, and
reserve total cognition for *kevala-jñāna*. **Verdict:** Jainism is best classified as **pluralistic realism
with epistemic non-absolutism**. Its ontology is robustly objective; relativity belongs chiefly to finite
predication. Its residual burden is to justify the omniscient standpoint and the unity of permanence with
change. *(Why this earns marks: it answers both labels, proves them through the six dravyas and objective
modes, connects logic to ontology, and engages three named criticisms.)*

### Solved PYQ 10 — 2026 Q7(b), 15 marks (newly verified)

**Question:** Explain the meaning of seven steps of judgement (Saptabhanginaya) and its application in
Jaina's Syadvada for advocating a philosophy of non-absolutism (Anekantavada).

**Model solution.** This question asks for the doctrine in the **reverse** dependency direction from how it
is usually taught, and a high-scoring answer should explicitly note this: rather than starting from
*anekāntavāda* and deriving *saptabhaṅgī* (the order used in Lessons 2→5 of this edition), the question asks
the candidate to explain *saptabhaṅgī* first and then show how it is *applied within syādvāda* to
*advocate for* *anekāntavāda* — i.e., to argue the chain in the direction of *use*, not *derivation*.

The **seven steps of judgement (saptabhaṅgī)** are the sevenfold schema of conditional predication produced
by combining affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under the qualifier *syāt* (Lesson 5, §5.2): (1)
*syād asti* — in some respect, it is; (2) *syān nāsti* — in some respect, it is not; (3) *syād asti ca
nāsti ca* — in some respect, it is and is not; (4) *syād avaktavyam* — in some respect, it is
indescribable; (5) *syād asti ca avaktavyam* — it is and is indescribable; (6) *syād nāsti ca avaktavyam*
— it is not and is indescribable; (7) *syād asti ca nāsti ca avaktavyam* — it is, is not, and is
indescribable.

**Application in syādvāda, for advocating non-absolutism.** *Syādvāda* is the doctrine that every assertion
must carry the qualifier *syāt* ("in some respect") to avoid absolutising a partial standpoint (Lesson 4,
§4.4). The seven-step schema is *syādvāda* worked out to its full, disciplined, combinatorial completion:
rather than leaving "in some respect" as a single vague hedge, *saptabhaṅgī* specifies **exactly seven**
precise, non-arbitrary ways in which a qualified assertion can be made about any real object, covering every
combination of affirmation, negation and the limits of simultaneous expression. This completeness is
precisely what makes the doctrine an effective *advocacy tool* for *anekāntavāda*: a critic who claims
Jainism's "many-sidedness" is a vague evasion can be shown a closed, seven-membered, fully specified logical
apparatus, not an open-ended shrug. Each of the seven forms is a *demonstration*, applied to a concrete case
(e.g., a jar: it exists here-now, does not exist as cloth, both in different respects, and is inexpressible
when both are demanded at once), that reality's many-sidedness can be spoken about rigorously rather than
merely gestured at. In this way, *saptabhaṅgī* is not a separate doctrine sitting beside *anekāntavāda*; it
is the **proof-by-construction** that *anekāntavāda* is a workable, disciplined, non-self-contradictory
philosophy of non-absolutism (*ekānta*-negation), rather than an empty slogan (Lesson 3B, §3B.6–§3B.7;
Lesson 5, §5.0).

A complete answer should also note the standard defence against the non-contradiction objection (Lesson 5,
§5.2, §5.9): the seven steps never assert affirmation and negation of the *same* respect at the *same* time;
each *bhaṅga* is indexed to substance, place, time or mode, so *anekāntavāda*'s "non-absolutism" is
logically earned, not merely asserted. **Verdict:** *saptabhaṅgī* is the sevenfold logical machinery by
which *syādvāda* makes good on *anekāntavāda*'s metaphysical promise — it is the *demonstration*, not merely
an illustration, of disciplined non-absolutism. *(Why this earns marks: it correctly reverses the usual
teaching order to match the question's own "explain X, then show its application for Y" structure, states
all seven predications exactly and in order, and explicitly argues why the schema functions as advocacy
for non-absolutism rather than merely restating the definitions.)*

**Original Mains practice (10 marks)**

*Question:* "Jaina realism survives its logical critics but not its metaphysical ones." Comment.

*Model answer.* This statement requires testing Jainism's logical defences (against Śaṃkara, Rāmānuja, and
the 2022 self-reference charge) separately from its metaphysical defences (against the Nyāya/Sāṃkhya-Vedānta
soul-matter interaction problem). On the logical side, Jainism's standpoint-indexing (dravya/kṣetra/kāla/
bhāva) and its acceptance of reflexive qualification give it a genuinely strong, internally consistent
answer to the non-contradiction and self-reference objections (Lesson 5, §8A.3.1, §8A.7) — these objections
are answered, not merely deflected. On the metaphysical side, however, the claim that subtle material
particles (karma-*pudgala*) can bind an immaterial, conscious *jīva* remains a genuine, unresolved
difficulty even after the *pradeśa*/lamp-simile reply (Lesson 3A, §3A.4; Lesson 8A, §8A.3.4) — the reply
relocates the puzzle (from "can matter affect mind at all" to "how does an embodied, spatially-extended
soul specifically interact with karmic matter") without dissolving it. The statement is therefore broadly
correct: Jainism's logic is more thoroughly defended than its metaphysics of the soul-karma relation.
*(Why this earns marks: it tests the quotation by separately assessing the logical and metaphysical
defences rather than treating "Jainism's critics" as one undifferentiated group.)*

**Original Mains practice (15 marks)**

*Question:* Compare Jainism's response to relativity-based objections with Advaita's response to
plurality-based objections.

*Model answer.* Both Jainism and Advaita face a structurally similar challenge: each must explain why its
central doctrine (Jaina non-absolutism; Advaitic non-duality) does not undermine itself or collapse into
incoherence when applied reflexively. Jainism's central challenge is the 2022-PYQ-style objection: if all
judgement is relative/standpoint-bound, is the claim "all judgement is relative" itself relative, and if so
does it lose universal force? Jainism replies by distinguishing finite, karmically-partial
(*kṣāyopaśamika*) judgement from omniscient (*kevala-jñāna*) cognition — the universal claim about
many-sidedness is licensed by the possibility of total, non-partial knowledge, not asserted as an
unqualified exception (Lesson 5, §5.7; Lesson 8A, §8A.7). Advaita's structurally parallel challenge is: if
ultimate reality is non-dual, how can the empirical plurality of souls, teachers, scriptures, and even the
process of instruction (which presupposes a plural teacher-and-student relation) be accounted for at all?
Advaita's classical reply distinguishes the empirical (*vyāvahārika*) level, where plurality and teaching
are fully operative and necessary, from the ultimate (*pāramārthika*) level, where non-duality alone is
final — a two-level distinction structurally analogous to Jainism's finite/omniscient distinction. The
disanalogy is instructive: Jainism's two levels (finite and omniscient cognition) are both cognitions of
the *same*, objectively plural and real world; Advaita's two levels ultimately downgrade the empirical
level's plurality to *mithyā* (dependently real, not finally real). Hence both schools use a
"two-level" reply to save their central doctrine from reflexive self-undermining, but only Jainism's
two-level reply preserves full ontological parity between the two levels, because only Jainism's finite
judgements are non-absolutist descriptions of a truly plural world rather than provisional descriptions of
what is ultimately non-plural. *(Why this earns marks: it identifies the shared logical structure of the
challenge across two different schools, states each school's reply precisely using its own vocabulary, and
draws a substantive disanalogy rather than a superficial one.)*

   The correction is:
   A. the two schools use the term identically.
   B. Nyāya rejects *pratyakṣa* as a valid *pramāṇa*.
   C. In Jainism only *kevala-jñāna* (and the other higher *jñānas*) is unconditionally *pratyakṣa*; Nyāya
      treats ordinary sense-perception as paradigmatically direct.
   D. Jainism has no concept of *pratyakṣa* at all.
   **Answer: C.**

4. **Error:** assuming *puṇya* (merit) is equivalent to liberation.
   The correction is:
   A. *puṇya* is the same as *mokṣa*.
   B. Only *pāpa*, not *puṇya*, needs to be transcended.
   C. Neither *puṇya* nor *pāpa* has any karmic status.
   D. Both *puṇya* and *pāpa* are forms of karmic involvement that must ultimately be transcended for
      *mokṣa*.
   **Answer: D.**

5. **Error:** believing *sallekhanā* is doctrinally identical to suicide.
   The correction is:
   A. Jainism explicitly distinguishes *sallekhanā* (passion-free, gradual, supervised, public) from
      *ātma-ghāta*/suicide (passion-driven, sudden, violent, concealed).
   B. *sallekhanā* is passion-driven, sudden and concealed, exactly like suicide.
   C. Jainism does not discuss the ethics of dying at all.
   D. *Sallekhanā* is available only to Tīrthaṅkaras.
   **Answer: A.**

6. **Error:** thinking *saptabhaṅgī*'s "*avaktavya*" means the object is entirely unknowable.
   The correction is:
   A. *avaktavya* is a full admission of total ignorance.
   B. *Avaktavya* applies to every object at every time without exception.
   C. *Avaktavya* is a synonym for *asti*.
   D. *Avaktavya* marks only the failure of one simple, unqualified, simultaneous utterance to express
      complex indexed aspects together — not total unknowability of the object.
   **Answer: D.**

7. **Error:** assuming the seven *nayas* and the seven *bhaṅgas* are the same list.
   The correction is:
   A. they are the same list, renamed for two different audiences.
   B. the seven *nayas* are standpoints from which a judgement is formed; the seven *bhaṅgas* are the
      formal predicative forms in which a judgement is asserted — related but distinct lists.
   C. only the *bhaṅgas* are genuinely Jaina; the *nayas* are a Buddhist import.
   D. there are fourteen items total, seven standpoints combined arbitrarily with seven predications.
   **Answer: B.**

8. **Error:** believing Jaina *mokṣa* means merger into a universal consciousness, as sometimes read in
   Advaita.
   The correction is:
   A. Jaina *mokṣa* and Advaitic realization of non-duality are identical in every respect.
   B. Jainism denies that liberation is achievable at all.
   C. the liberated *siddha* becomes a new creator God governing the cosmos.
   D. Jaina *mokṣa* preserves the *jīva*'s numerical individuality as a distinct *siddha*; there is no
      merger into any single universal reality.
   **Answer: D.**

## F.6 Original Mains practice, final consolidated set (model answers)

**Question (10 marks).** Why does Jainism classify *kāla* as a *dravya* but not as an *astikāya*?

*Model answer.* Jainism recognises six *dravyas* — *jīva*, *pudgala*, *dharma*, *adharma*, *ākāśa* and
*kāla* — but only the first five are *astikāyas*, extended substances possessing many space-points
(*pradeśas*). *Kāla* is fully real and objectively explains succession, duration and the sequence of
change, but it lacks the multi-space-point spatial extension that defines an *astikāya*. This is not an
arbitrary exclusion: Jainism is making the philosophical point that *substantiality* (being a real,
functionally irreducible *dravya*) and *extension* (occupying many space-points) are logically separable
properties. A sectarian nuance exists — Digambara authors characteristically affirm real *kāla-aṇus*
(time-atoms) tied to the space-points of *lokākāśa*, while some Śvetāmbara discussions treat *kāla*'s
substantial independence as more conventional — but the core, examinable point is safe across both:
*pañcāstikāya* (five extended substances) *plus kāla* equals six *dravyas* in total. *(Why this earns
marks: it states the exact six-dravya/five-astikāya split, explains the philosophical rationale
(substantiality ≠ extension), and correctly flags the sectarian nuance as contested rather than settled.)*

**Question (20 marks).** "Every criticism of Jainism examined in this course targets either its logic or
its metaphysics of the soul, and Jainism's reply always trades one difficulty for another." Critically
evaluate this statement with reference to at least four named objections.

*Model answer.* This statement is a strong, testable generalisation and substantially correct, though it
requires refinement about exactly what is "traded." Consider four named objections. First, Śaṃkara's
logical objection — that opposed predicates cannot belong to one real without contradiction — is answered
by indexing predicates to *dravya, kṣetra, kāla* and *bhāva*; the "trade" here is that Jainism must accept
a reflexively self-qualifying logic (*syādvāda* asserted *syāt* of itself), which purchases internal
consistency at the cost of a possible loss of final assertoric force. Second, Dharmakīrti's *arthakriyā*
dilemma — that a permanent-yet-changing soul cannot coherently both act and remain unchanged — is answered
through *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya*, trading the difficulty of "how can permanence and change coexist" for
acceptance of a substance/mode distinction that a Buddhist metaphysics of momentary events simply refuses
to grant. Third, the Nyāya objection to the soul's material entanglement — invoking *pradeśas* and the lamp
simile — trades the parsimony of an invisible, non-interacting *adṛṣṭa* for the concreteness of material
karma-*pudgala*, at the cost of a genuine, unresolved consciousness-matter interaction problem. Fourth, the
Sāṃkhya/Vedānta objection to beginningless bondage of an intrinsically pure soul is answered by declaring
the *jīva*-karma relation *anādi* but terminable, trading the demand for a first cause for an accepted,
unexplained metaphysical brute fact. In every case, the statement is right that a difficulty is not
dissolved so much as relocated or exchanged for a different, more internally manageable one. The
qualification worth adding is that not all four "trades" are equally costly: the logical trade (reflexive
qualification) is widely judged the least damaging, because it is a feature many sophisticated logical
systems share; the metaphysical trade (soul-matter interaction) is judged the most damaging, because unlike
the others it has no comparably strong analogue accepted elsewhere and remains, even by Jainism's own
graded self-assessment (Lesson 8A, §8A.12), a genuinely "weak" point rather than a merely "contested" one.
*(Why this earns marks: it tests the quotation against four distinct, correctly named objections and
replies, identifies exactly what is "traded" in each case rather than asserting the claim loosely, and ends
with a differentiated rather than uniform verdict.)*

## F.7 Consolidated register notes — Jainism (final structural section before the coverage and source ledgers)

*(Topic-specific headings, not a fixed generic template, per the register-note convention used throughout

### Foundations, pluralism and non-theism

- "Jaina" ← *jina* (conqueror); Tīrthaṅkara = ford-maker, not creator; 24 this cycle; Ṛṣabha (1st), Pārśva
  (23rd), Mahāvīra (24th, systematiser not founder).
- *Tattvārthasūtra* (Umāsvāti/Umāsvāmī) — shared by both sects; ratna-traya stated there.
- Heterodox (*nāstika*) = rejects Vedic authority only, not soul/rebirth/liberation.
- *Anīśvaravāda*: substances eternal, only modes arise/cease; karma explains inequality; liberation by
  self-effort; Jina/siddha reverence tracks achieved perfection, not creative power.

### Reality: persistence without stasis

- *Sat* = *utpāda* + *vyaya* + *dhrauvya*, all together, in different respects.
- Every *dravya* has *guṇa* (abiding) and *paryāya* (changing); consciousness = *guṇa* of *jīva*.
- Middle path: against Buddhist momentariness (no enduring substance) and against strict monism/*vivarta*
  readings (denies real change).
- Causation: *sadasatkāryavāda* — effect exists qua substance, does not exist qua mode; mediates Sāṃkhya's
  *satkāryavāda* and Nyāya's *asatkāryavāda*.

### Six substances and knowledge

- Universe = *jīva* + *ajīva* (5 kinds); six *dravyas* total; five *astikāyas* (all but *kāla*).
- *Dharma*/*adharma dravyas* = media of motion/rest, **not** ethical merit/demerit.
- *Jīva*: intrinsically infinite knowledge/perception/bliss/power, obscured (not destroyed) by karma; has
  *pradeśas*, co-extensive with body.
- *Pudgala*: only *dravya* with *rūpa*; atoms (*paramāṇu*) → aggregates (*skandhas*); includes karmic
  matter, speech, breath.
- Jaina *pratyakṣa* is inverted: only *avadhi/manaḥparyāya/kevala* are strict *pratyakṣa*; ordinary
  perception is *sāṃvyavahārika* (conventional), grouped with *parokṣa*.
- Five *jñānas*: *mati, śruta* (parokṣa) → *avadhi, manaḥparyāya, kevala* (pratyakṣa); wrong cognition =
  *kumati/kuśruta/vibhaṅga-jñāna* under *mithyā-darśana*.
- Derivation: infinite-aspect reality → *kṣāyopaśamika* (karmically partial) cognition → *naya* → *syāt* →
  *saptabhaṅgī* → only *kevala-jñāna* is total.

### Standpoints and conditional predication

- *Anekāntavāda* (metaphysics) → *nayavāda* (standpoint) → *syādvāda* (qualified language) →
  *saptabhaṅgī* (formal schema) — the master dependency chain.
- Seven *nayas*: *naigama, saṃgraha, vyavahāra, ṛjusūtra* (practical/general/particular/momentary);
  *śabda, samabhirūḍha, evambhūta* (three graded linguistic standpoints).
- *Syāt* ≠ doubt/probability — marks definite, standpoint-qualified assertion.
- "Naya selects the angle; *syāt* announces the angle."

### Sevenfold judgement

- Seven *bhaṅgas*, in order: *asti / nāsti / asti-nāsti / avaktavyam / asti-avaktavyam /
  nāsti-avaktavyam / asti-nāsti-avaktavyam* — predicates 5–7 = 1–3 each paired with 4.
- *Avaktavya* = failure of simultaneous unqualified expression, **not** total unknowability.
- Non-contradiction preserved by respect/time/place/mode-indexing.
- Self-refutation objection answered by reflexive qualification (*syādvāda* is itself asserted *syāt*) plus
  the finite/omniscient distinction (2022 PYQ).

### Karma and bondage

- Karma = material *pudgala*, **not** *adṛṣṭa*/moral ledger/*saṃskāra*/*vāsanā*.
- Five causes: *mithyātva* → *avirati* → *pramāda* → *kaṣāya* → *yoga*.
- Seven *tattvas* (jīva, ajīva, āsrava, bandha, saṃvara, nirjarā, mokṣa); nine *padārthas* = + *puṇya* +
  *pāpa* (both ultimately transcended).
- *Bhāvabandha* (psychic) → *dravyabandha* (material) — causal direction matters.
- Eight *karma-prakṛtis*: 4 *ghātiyā* (jñānāvaraṇīya, darśanāvaraṇīya, mohanīya, antarāya) + 4 *aghātiyā*
  (vedanīya, nāma, gotra, āyus); *mohanīya* most dangerous.
- Four *bandha* parameters: *prakṛti* & *pradeśa* ← *yoga*; *sthiti* & *anubhāga* ← *kaṣāya*.

### Liberation and spiritual discipline

- *Saṃvara* (stop) vs. *nirjarā* (shed); full discipline: *gupti, samiti*, ten *dharma*-virtues,
  *anuprekṣā/bhāvanā, pariṣaha-jaya, cāritra, tapas*.
- Ratna-traya (right vision/knowledge/conduct) mutually reinforcing; five *mahāvratas* (ahiṃsā, satya,
  asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha).
- Fourteen *guṇasthānas*; stage 4 = stable right vision; stages 11 vs. 12 = suppression vs. destruction of
  delusion; *upaśama-śreṇi* (unstable) vs. *kṣapaka-śreṇi* (irreversible).
- *Sayoga-kevalī* (13) = Jaina *jīvanmukti*; *ayoga-kevalī* (14) = momentary final activity-cessation.
- *Kevala-jñāna* revealed, not produced; *mokṣa* = subtraction, preserves individuality (unlike Advaita
  merger, unlike Buddhist no-self *nirvāṇa*).
- *Sallekhanā* ≠ suicide: passion-free/gradual/supervised/public vs. passion-driven/sudden/concealed; do
  not cite unverified legal case details.

### Criticism and final synthesis

- Three charges: metaphysics (contradiction) / logic (self-reference) / soteriology (soul-matter
  interaction, unverifiability); two reply-types: respect-indexing / finite-vs-omniscient distinction.
- Named critics: Śaṃkara, Rāmānuja, Dharmakīrti/Buddhist, Nyāya, Sāṃkhya/Vedānta, Cārvāka — each with a
  specific reply and a graded residual force.
- Final classification: **pluralistic realism with qualified, non-absolutist predication** — strong on
  permanence-change synthesis and causally serious ethics; weak on soul-matter interaction and
  unverifiable omniscience; contested on *kāla*'s status, *sallekhanā*'s legal status, exact historical
  dates.
- All ten verified PYQs (2018–2026) solved in Lesson 8B; answer architecture: 10 marks = define + 3 points
  + 1 distinction + 1 verdict; 15 marks = frame + body + objection/reply + verdict; 20 marks = full
  metaphysics→logic→soteriology→criticism→verdict arc.
