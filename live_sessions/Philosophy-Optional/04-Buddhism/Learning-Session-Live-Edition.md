## 1.1 Simple intuition — what problem is Buddhism solving?

Ordinary experience presents two temptations. One is to say: underneath all this change, there must be
some permanent "me," a soul, a fixed essence that owns my experiences and survives them — call this
**eternalism**. The other is to say: if nothing is permanent, then when "I" die or when this moment ends,
there is simply nothing left, no continuity, no consequence — call this **annihilationism**. Buddhism's
founding intuition is that *both* temptations are mistakes, and that suffering itself is caused by falling
into one or the other. If you believe in a fixed self, you cling to it and suffer when it changes anyway.
If you believe in sheer nothingness, morality and effort seem pointless. Buddhism's entire technical
apparatus — dependent origination, momentariness, no-self, the four schools — is built to hold a **middle
position**: things really do happen, causes really do have effects, effort really is worthwhile, but
nothing has to be an eternal fixed substance for any of that to be true. Before any Sanskrit or Pali
vocabulary is introduced, hold on to this plain question, because every later lesson answers a piece of it:
*if nothing is permanent, how can experience still be orderly, moral and eventually free of suffering?*

## 1.2 Early Buddhism, Theravāda, the Hīnayāna label and Mahāyāna — the tradition map ✅

**Plain-language definition.** ✅ Before any of the four philosophical schools can be placed correctly, a
UPSC candidate needs a historically careful map of the broader traditions, because the syllabus schools
(Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra, Mādhyamika) belong to different branches of this map and must not be
casually merged.

**Technical doctrine — four categories, none interchangeable.** ✅

| Category | Historically careful description | Exam-safe caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Early Buddhism** | The earliest recoverable discourse and discipline traditions before the later philosophical systems fully differentiated. The Four Noble Truths, Middle Path, dependent origination, impermanence and no-self form the common diagnostic core. | Do not identify early Buddhism exclusively with any one later school. Early discourses survive in more than one recension — chiefly the Pāli Nikāyas and parallel Chinese Āgamas, with further fragmentary witnesses. |
| **Theravāda** | The surviving "Teaching of the Elders" tradition, with the Pāli Tipiṭaka and its own Abhidhamma and commentarial history. It centrally honours the liberated-saint (arhat) ideal. | Theravāda is one historical school and living tradition — it is not the name for every early or non-Mahāyāna school. |
| **The historically loaded "Lesser Vehicle" label (Hīnayāna)** | A Mahāyāna-origin classificatory, often polemical, label applied to non-Mahāyāna or disciple-vehicle traditions. It was **not** their own self-designation. Older Indian-philosophy textbooks retain it as a broad doxographic category. | Do not casually equate Hīnayāna with Theravāda. In a modern answer, either avoid the label entirely or place it in quotation marks with its historical use explained, exactly as done here. |
| **Mahāyāna** | A diverse "Great Vehicle" family of texts, practices and philosophies foregrounding the bodhisattva path, complete Buddhahood for the benefit of all beings, compassion and wisdom. Yogācāra and Mādhyamika are its two major philosophical formations in the standard four-school scheme. | Mahāyāna is not one uniform doctrine. Do not turn its bodhisattva orientation into a crude "selfish arhat vs altruistic bodhisattva" contrast. |

**Why this ordering matters.** ⚠️ A candidate who writes "Hīnayāna philosophy holds Vaibhāṣika realism and
Theravāda is another name for it" commits two errors at once: treating a polemical label as a neutral
synonym, and collapsing a living Pāli tradition into a Sanskrit Abhidharma school it is not identical with.
This lesson exists specifically to prevent that compound error before Lessons 5–7 introduce the four
schools.

## 1.3 The Four Noble Truths as the existential frame ✅

**Technical doctrine.** ✅ Buddhism states the problem before it states any metaphysics:

1. **Duḥkha** (suffering / unsatisfactoriness) — life as ordinarily lived is pervaded by suffering, in
   gross pain, in the anxiety of impermanence, and in the subtle dissatisfaction built into conditioned
   existence.
2. **Samudaya** (origin) — suffering has a dependent origin: craving (**tṛṣṇā**) rooted in ignorance.
3. **Nirodha** (cessation) — because the origin is conditioned, it can cease.
4. **Mārga** (path) — a practical discipline, the Noble Eightfold Path, makes cessation achievable.

**Argument structure.** ⚠️ Dependent origination (taught fully in Lesson 2) is the philosophical grammar of
these four truths: the first truth states the fact of suffering; the second gives its dependent arising;
the third gives dependent cessation; the fourth is the practical discipline for breaking the chain. This is
why Buddhism is often called a *diagnostic and therapeutic* system before it is a metaphysical one — it
follows the structure of a physician: identify the disease, identify its cause, confirm that a cure is
possible, prescribe the treatment.

## 1.4 The Middle Path (madhyamā pratipad; Pali: majjhimā paṭipadā) — practical and doctrinal senses ✅

### Practical meaning

- ✅ In the Buddha's first-sermon framework, the Middle Path rejects two practical extremes: sensual
  indulgence and self-mortification. It is not an average compromise between them; it is a disciplined
  route that avoids the defects of both.
- ✅ Its positive content is the **Noble Eightfold Path (āryāṣṭāṅgamārga)**:

| Training | English-first path factors |
|---|---|
| Wisdom (**prajñā**) | right view (samyag-dṛṣṭi); right intention (samyak-saṃkalpa) |
| Ethical discipline (**śīla**) | right speech (samyag-vāc); right action (samyak-karmānta); right livelihood (samyag-ājīva) |
| Meditative discipline (**samādhi**) | right effort (samyag-vyāyāma); right mindfulness (samyak-smṛti); right concentration (samyak-samādhi) |

- ✅ The path operationalises the fourth Noble Truth: because suffering arises conditionally, disciplined
  changes in understanding, conduct and attention can interrupt its causes.

### Doctrinal meaning

- ✅ At the metaphysical level, dependent origination is a middle between **eternalism (śāśvatavāda)** and
  **annihilationism (ucchedavāda)**. Buddhism denies an unchanging substance or self without reducing
  causal continuity, karma and liberation to sheer nothingness.
- ⚠️ The practical and doctrinal senses reinforce each other: the path avoids harmful modes of living, while
  dependent origination avoids harmful extremes of metaphysical grasping.

### Exam use

- ⚠️ In an answer on dependent origination, no-self, momentariness or liberation, use the Middle Path as the
  bridge: conditioned continuity explains how Buddhism can reject permanence without rejecting causation,
  responsibility or the possibility of release.
- ❌ Do not define the Middle Path merely as "moderation." Its examinable content is the Noble Eightfold
  Path **plus** the philosophical avoidance of eternalism and annihilationism. A one-line answer that
  mentions only moderation loses half the available content.

## 1.5 The three marks (trilakṣaṇa; Pali: tilakkhaṇa) — impermanence kept distinct from momentariness ✅

| English-first mark | Doctrinal meaning | Relation to the syllabus |
|---|---|---|
| **Impermanence (anitya; Pali: anicca)** | All conditioned formations arise, alter and cease. | Supplies the foundation that later Buddhist logicians radicalise into momentariness (Lesson 3). |
| **Suffering / unsatisfactoriness (duḥkha; Pali: dukkha)** | What is impermanent and appropriated as "mine" cannot provide secure satisfaction. | Connects metaphysics to the first Noble Truth and to the diagnosis of craving. |
| **No-self (anātman; Pali: anattā)** | No permanent, independent owner can be found in the changing aggregates or conditioned processes. | Directly prepares the no-self doctrine (nairātmyavāda), taught fully in Lesson 4. |

**Historical caution — keep these two ideas separate.** ⚠️ **Impermanence** is the early, general teaching
that conditioned things change. **Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda)**, taught in Lesson 3, is a much stronger,
later scholastic thesis: that whatever is real exists for exactly one indivisible moment. Every conditioned
thing that is momentary is impermanent, but the early texts teaching impermanence do not yet assert the
full one-moment-only logic that Buddhist logicians later derive from causal efficacy. A UPSC answer that
uses the two words as if they were always synonymous loses the "historical caution" that examiners reward.

**Scope caution.** ⚠️ The three marks formula applies impermanence and suffering to *conditioned*
formations, while not-self is used more widely. Do not infer that the cessation of conditioned suffering
(**nirvāṇa**), classified as unconditioned (**asaṃskṛta**) in important Buddhist traditions, is just another
momentary conditioned event — this distinction becomes important again in Lessons 4 and 10.

## 1.6 Liberated-saint and awakening-being ideals — arhat and bodhisattva ✅

- ✅ The **liberated saint ideal (arhat; Pali: arahant)** is central to early disciple traditions and
  Theravāda: the practitioner follows the Buddha's path and extinguishes the causes of rebirth and
  suffering for themselves.
- ✅ The **awakening-being ideal (bodhisattva)** is foregrounded by Mahāyāna: the practitioner cultivates
  wisdom and compassion toward complete Buddhahood for the benefit of *all* beings.
- ⚠️ "The bodhisattva simply postpones nirvāṇa" is an oversimplification. The stronger, exam-safe
  formulation is: the bodhisattva does not treat arhatship as the final aim, but follows the longer path to
  complete Buddhahood.
- ⚠️ The contrast identifies dominant normative *ideals*, not mutually exclusive vocabulary. Theravāda
  recognises the *bodhisatta* career of a future Buddha, and Mahāyāna texts still know the arhat category.
  **Do not** reproduce the old polemical caricature that arhatship is merely "selfish" and the bodhisattva
  path merely "altruistic" — this trap resurfaces in Lesson 4's discussion of nirvāṇa.

## 1.7 How the four philosophical schools map — and do not map — onto the traditions ✅

> ✅ **Taxonomy control:** the four-school scheme (Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra, Mādhyamika) is the
> standard philosophical teaching matrix used in Indian and Tibetan doxographies. It is **not** a complete
> institutional history of Buddhism.

| Philosophical school | Broad historical placement | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| **Vaibhāṣika** (Lesson 5) | A Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma formation, especially associated with the Kashmir *Mahāvibhāṣā* tradition; non-Mahāyāna / disciple-vehicle in the older classification. | It is not a branch of Theravāda merely because an older chart places both outside Mahāyāna. |
| **Sautrāntika** (Lesson 5) | A critical current in the Sarvāstivāda orbit that privileges sūtra reasoning over Vaibhāṣika Abhidharma claims; non-Mahāyāna / disciple-vehicle in the older classification. | It is not identical with Theravāda and need not be treated as one perfectly unified monastic institution. |
| **Yogācāra** (Lesson 6) | A major Mahāyāna philosophical tradition centred on consciousness-only (representation-only) analysis, the three natures and, in important formulations, store-consciousness. | "Mahāyāna" is wider than Yogācāra, and consciousness-only must not be reduced to arbitrary private fantasy. |
| **Mādhyamika** (Lesson 7) | A major Mahāyāna philosophical tradition that develops emptiness, dependent designation and the two truths. | "Mahāyāna" is wider than Mādhyamika, and emptiness is not sheer non-being. |

> 🔑 **Exam-safe mapping:** Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika are non-Mahāyāna scholastic positions associated with
> the Sarvāstivāda world; Yogācāra and Mādhyamika are Mahāyāna philosophical traditions. Do not equate
> Vaibhāṣika/Sautrāntika with Theravāda — they share only a broad, older "non-Mahāyāna" classification, not
> an institutional or canonical identity.

**Canon and language — the accurate minimum.** ✅ Theravāda preserves the **Pāli Tipiṭaka**: discipline
basket (Vinaya Piṭaka), discourse basket (Sutta Piṭaka) and higher-teaching basket (Abhidhamma Piṭaka).
Early Buddhist discourse is not historically reducible to "Pāli Buddhism" — parallel early materials
survive in Chinese Āgamas and in Sanskrit or other fragmentary witnesses. Sarvāstivāda, Vaibhāṣika and
Sautrāntika materials are substantially preserved through Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan textual histories;
they do not share the Theravāda Pāli canon. Many Mahāyāna sūtras and treatises were transmitted in Sanskrit
or Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit and through Chinese and Tibetan translations — "Mahāyāna equals Sanskrit" is
therefore only a broad orientation, not an exhaustive linguistic rule.

## 1.9 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection.** ❓ A common undergraduate-level objection is that "early Buddhism is just ethical advice
without a philosophical system," so the elaborate four-school apparatus of Lessons 5–8 looks like a later
invention with no connection to the founding teaching.

**Reply.** ✅ The Four Noble Truths already contain a causal claim (suffering has a dependent origin) and a
metaphysical claim (the origin is conditioned and therefore removable). Every later school — realist,
idealist or anti-essentialist — is an attempt to work out precisely *what kind of thing* is arising and
ceasing when dependent origination occurs. The philosophical schools are elaborations of, not additions
external to, the diagnostic core stated in this lesson. ⚠️ The residual, genuinely debated point (returned
to in Lesson 9) is exactly how much of the later technical apparatus (twelve links in a fixed order, formal
momentariness, full-blown apoha theory) is already implicit in the earliest texts versus a later scholastic
development — historians of Buddhism do not fully agree on this, and a candidate should state the caution
rather than assert false certainty either way.

## 1.11 Revision notes (Lesson 1)

1. Four Noble Truths: duḥkha, samudaya, nirodha, mārga — diagnosis-cause-cure-treatment structure.
2. Middle Path has a practical sense (avoids indulgence/mortification; positive content = Noble Eightfold
   Path) and a doctrinal sense (avoids eternalism/annihilationism).
3. Three marks: anitya, duḥkha, anātman — impermanence is NOT yet full momentariness.

6. Theravāda preserves the Pāli Tipiṭaka (Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma Piṭaka) and centres the arhat ideal.
7. Mahāyāna is a diverse family foregrounding the bodhisattva ideal, compassion and wisdom.
8. Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika are non-Mahāyāna, Sarvāstivāda-linked scholastic positions — not Theravāda.
9. Yogācāra and Mādhyamika are the two major Mahāyāna philosophical formations.
10. Arhat (self-liberation) and bodhisattva (Buddhahood for all beings) are ideals, not rigid vocabularies —

11. Buddhism is heterodox (nāstika) for rejecting Vedic authority, not for denying ethics or liberation.

**Caption.** The same twelve-term chain supports three legitimate readings. The three-life reading explains

explicitly uses at least two of these three readings, because that is what distinguishes a mechanical list
from genuine understanding.

## 2.1 Simple intuition

Why does anything happen at all, and why does suffering in particular keep recurring? Buddhism's answer
is deliberately unglamorous: nothing happens by itself, and nothing happens by the will of an outside
creator either. Everything that arises does so **because something else, specific and identifiable,
supported its arising** — and when that support is withdrawn, the thing ceases. Suffering is not a
metaphysical fate handed down from a soul or a god; it is a process with traceable causes, and a process
with traceable causes can, in principle, be stopped. This is the single idea that keeps the whole of
Buddhist philosophy — momentariness, no-self, the four schools — pointed toward one soteriological goal
rather than becoming abstract metaphysics for its own sake.

## 2.2 Technical doctrine: statement of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) ✅

- ✅ **Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda)** means that whatever arises, arises depending on causes
  and conditions; whatever ceases, ceases when those conditions cease.
- ✅ The standard formula, in substance, runs: *"imasmiṃ sati idaṃ hoti; imass' uppādā idaṃ uppajjati;
  imasmiṃ asati idaṃ na hoti; imassa nirodhā idaṃ nirujjhati"* — this being, that becomes; from the arising
  of this, that arises; this not being, that is not; from the cessation of this, that ceases.
- ✅ It is not merely a cosmological thesis. It is the key to the arising of **duḥkha** and therefore the key
  to its cessation (Lesson 1, §1.3).

## 2.3 Technical doctrine: the twelve links (dvādaśa-nidāna), in order ✅

- ✅ The classical chain of dependent origination is expressed through the **twelve-link sequence
  (dvādaśa-nidāna)**:
  1. ignorance (**avidyā**)
  2. formations or dispositions (**saṃskāra**)
  3. consciousness (**vijñāna**)
  4. name-and-form (**nāma-rūpa**)
  5. six sense-bases (**ṣaḍāyatana**)
  6. contact (**sparśa**)
  7. feeling (**vedanā**)
  8. craving (**tṛṣṇā**)
  9. clinging or grasping (**upādāna**)
  10. becoming or existence (**bhava**)
  11. birth (**jāti**)
  12. old age and death (**jarā-maraṇa**)
- ✅ In visual Buddhist pedagogy, this sequence is often represented through the **bhava-cakra** (wheel of
  becoming), showing how ignorance and craving keep cyclic existence in motion.

## 2.4 Three exam-useful interpretations of the twelve links ✅

| Interpretation | How it reads the chain | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-life pedagogic reading** | Past causes: ignorance and formations; present results: consciousness through feeling; present causes: craving, clinging and becoming; future results: birth, ageing and death. | This common Abhidharma and textbook arrangement explains rebirth and karmic continuity, but it is not the only legitimate reading. |
| **Present-process / psychological reading** | In each lived episode, ignorance conditions habitual construction, consciousness and name-and-form organise experience, contact and feeling arise, and craving turns feeling into appropriation and renewed becoming. | "Psychological" does not mean merely private or imaginary — the point is the conditioned construction of suffering here and now. |
| **Structural and soteriological reading** | The chain identifies the conditions that maintain suffering and can be read forward as arising and in reverse as cessation. It diagnoses a pattern, not an absolutely first cause. | Early formulations vary in order and number before the twelve-link list becomes standard; do not force every occurrence into one rigid linear chronology. |

**⚠️ Answer use:** state the standard twelve-link sequence, then add one sentence that the three-life model
explains rebirth while the present-process model shows why dependent origination is immediately practical.
This single move converts a merely descriptive list into an analytically complete answer.

## 2.5 Step-by-step argument structure ✅

1. ✅ What is experienced is not self-grounding; it occurs only in dependence.
2. ✅ If a thing were self-existent by its own nature, it would not depend on conditions.
3. ✅ But all conditioned phenomena arise and cease in dependence on conditions.
4. ✅ Therefore conditioned phenomena are neither eternal substances nor sheer nothingness; they are
   dependent processes.
5. ⚠️ The existential point is that suffering too is a process, not a metaphysical fate. Therefore it is
   removable.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The doctrine presupposes that explanation should be sought through **conditional
relations**, not through a static substratum like ātman or Brahman and not through a creator-God standing
outside the chain (the creator-God argument is developed fully in Lesson 9). It also presupposes that what
matters philosophically is not an absolutely first beginning but the structure by which bondage is
maintained here and now.

## 2.6 Distinction: the middle way between extremes ✅

- ✅ Buddhism presents dependent origination as the **middle way** between:
  - **eternalism (śāśvatavāda):** some enduring self or substance exists unchanged.
  - **annihilationism (ucchedavāda):** things are sheer nullities, or persons are simply obliterated without
    causal continuity.
- ⚠️ Why is this a middle? Because Buddhism denies permanent substance, yet also denies that phenomena are
  sheer nothing. What exists does so **dependently**.

## 2.7 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ A flame persists only because wick, oil, oxygen and ignition remain coordinated. Remove the conditions
  and the flame ceases. Yet the flame is not absolutely unreal while it burns. This approximates dependent
  origination better than the language of substance. **Limit of the analogy:** a flame example illustrates
  conditioned continuity well but must not be over-read as proving momentariness by itself — that stronger
  conclusion needs the separate causal-efficacy argument of Lesson 3.

## 2.8 Objection and reply ✅

**Objection.** ❓ If everything depends on something else, does Buddhism explain nothing ultimately? Does it
create an infinite regress of conditions?

**Reply.** ✅ Buddhism need not posit an unconditioned substance to make conditioning intelligible. Its
primary concern is soteriological: to show that suffering is conditioned and therefore stoppable. ⚠️ UPSC
answers should stress that dependent origination is not a failed search for a first cause; it is a
diagnosis of dependent becoming.

## 2.9 How momentariness follows from dependent origination (previewed here, derived fully in Lesson 3) ✅

- ✅ What depends on conditions cannot remain numerically identical through all times, because it changes
  with the rise and fall of those conditions.
- ✅ Therefore what is conditioned is **impermanent (anitya)**; Buddhist logicians radicalise this into
  **momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda)**, according to which what is real is momentary.
- ⚠️ Thus a candidate can move from *pratītyasamutpāda* to *kṣaṇikavāda* by arguing: the dependently arisen
  has no independent enduring essence; it is event-like, not substance-like. **This preview is completed,
  with the full causal-efficacy proof, in Lesson 3, §3.3 — it is not re-taught in full here to avoid
  duplication.**

## 2.10 How 2024's "everything is void" and "everything is real" both arise from dependent origination (previewed here, completed in Lessons 5 and 7) ✅

#### (a) Mādhyamika: dependent origination implies emptiness

- ✅ Nāgārjuna's central move is that what arises dependently cannot possess **svabhāva** (self-being /
  own-nature). Therefore all dharmas are **śūnya** — empty of independent essence. In substance, Nāgārjuna
  identifies dependent origination and emptiness: what is dependently arisen is what is called empty. ⚠️
  This does **not** mean sheer non-being; it means lack of intrinsic, self-subsistent reality. *(Full
  treatment: Lesson 7.)*

#### (b) Vaibhāṣika / Sautrāntika: dependent origination implies conditioned reality

- ✅ The realist Buddhist schools agree that things arise dependently. But instead of inferring emptiness of
  all dharmas in the Mādhyamika sense, they say: precisely because things arise causally, they are **real
  as conditioned entities**. Vaibhāṣika treats dharmas as real and directly knowable; Sautrāntika treats
  them as real though not directly perceived externally. *(Full treatment: Lesson 5.)*

| School move | What is denied | What is affirmed |
|---|---|---|
| Mādhyamika | intrinsic nature (svabhāva) | conventional dependent existence |
| Vaibhāṣika / Sautrāntika | eternal substance and self | reality of conditioned dharmas |

- ⚠️ Thus the same causal thesis can support two ontological emphases: absence of self-nature, or reality of
  conditioned events. This is the exact demand of the 2024 PYQ (Q6(b), 15 marks — solved in full in
  Lesson 10, §10.4).

## 2.11 Cessation and knowledge — the 2023 PYQ doctrine ✅

- ✅ Ignorance of dependent origination is bondage; insight into it is release.
- ⚠️ Therefore the 2023 question — "ignorance of dependent origination is suffering while its knowledge is
  cessation" — should be answered not as a mere epistemic slogan, but as a claim that wrong metaphysical
  grasping fuels craving and hence suffering. *(Fully solved as PYQ 11 in Lesson 10, §10.4.)*

## 2.12 Comparative treatment: dependent origination against rival causal theories ✅/⚠️

| Causal theory | School | Core claim | Contrast with dependent origination |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ārambhavāda** | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | The effect is a genuinely new substance, produced by the combination of causes, not pre-existing in them. | Nyāya still needs an enduring substance-bearer; Buddhism denies any substance persists through the process. |
| **Satkāryavāda / pariṇāma** | Sāṃkhya | The effect pre-exists in its material cause and is only manifested (transformed), not newly created. | Sāṃkhya presupposes an eternal, really-transforming Prakṛti; Buddhism denies any single eternal substrate undergoes the change. |
| **Vivartavāda** | Advaita Vedānta | The effect is only an apparent transformation (superimposition) on an unchanging Brahman. | Advaita's Brahman is absolutely real and unchanging; Buddhism has no such unconditioned substantial ground behind the process. |
| **Pratītyasamutpāda** | Buddhism | The effect arises dependent on conditions, and both cause and effect are themselves conditioned, without an underlying substance. | Explicitly denies both an enduring material substratum and a creator standing outside the chain. |

**Caption.** This is the single most important argument diagram in the whole syllabus clause, because it is

## 3.1 Simple intuition

If dependent origination says everything depends on conditions, how long does a "thing" actually last once
its conditions are in place? Buddhist logicians pushed this question to its most radical possible answer:
not a lifetime, not a day, not even a second in the ordinary sense — but exactly one indivisible instant.
Their reasoning is not mystical; it is a very ordinary-sounding test of what it means to be *real* at all: a
real thing is one that can actually **do** something, produce some effect. Once you ask carefully when,
exactly, an unchanging thing could produce its effect, you run into a dilemma that seems to force the
conclusion that nothing genuinely unchanging can be causally active — and if only causally active things
are real, then reality itself must be momentary. The philosophically difficult part comes next: if reality
really does reset every instant, what happens to *memory* (which needs a past to reach back to), to
*personal identity* (which needs someone who was there before to still be here now), and to *karma* and
*moral responsibility* (which need the person who acted to be, in some sense, the person who is punished or
rewarded)? This lesson gives Buddhism's answer to all three, and ends with the exact 2026 UPSC question
that asks you to critically test that answer.

## 3.2 Technical doctrine: statement of momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) ✅

- ✅ **Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda)** holds that whatever is real is momentary.
- ✅ A standard scholastic formulation runs, in substance: **whatever is truly real is momentary**
  (*sattve satyeva kṣaṇikatvam*).
- ⚠️ The doctrine is not that nothing exists; it is that reality is **evental** rather than **substantial**.

## 3.3 Technical doctrine and full argument: causal efficacy (arthakriyākāritva) ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Causal efficacy (arthakriyākāritva)**, the capacity to perform a function, is the
Buddhist means-of-knowledge school's criterion of the real: **sat = arthakriyā-samartha** — the real is
what is capable of producing an effect. Momentariness follows because whatever is causally effective must
be moment-specific; a wholly permanent entity cannot explain why an effect appears now rather than always.
⚠️ This is the bridge between Buddhist epistemology (taught fully in Lesson 8) and ontology: the same test
that ranks **svalakṣaṇa** (unique particular) as ultimately real also grounds the thesis **yat sat tat
kṣaṇikam** — whatever is real is momentary.

**Full argument, step by step.** ✅
1. To be real is to be causally efficacious; an entity that makes no difference is indistinguishable from a
   non-entity for Buddhist analysis.
2. Causal efficacy must occur either **simultaneously** or **successively**.
3. If a permanent entity produces its effect all at once, nothing remains for later production — this is
   the **yaugapadya** (simultaneity) horn.
4. If it produces effects successively, then it functions differently at different moments; what functions
   differently is not wholly permanent — this is the **krama** (succession) horn.
5. If Nyāya says it acts only when auxiliary conditions (**sahakārin**, helper-conditions) are present, the
   Buddhist reply is the **sahakāri-anapekṣā** dilemma: either the permanent thing already has the power and
   does not need auxiliaries, or the decisive causal power lies in the changing auxiliaries rather than in
   the permanent thing.
6. Therefore a permanent entity cannot coherently be causally efficient.
7. Therefore whatever is real is momentary: **yat sat tat kṣaṇikam**.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The argument presupposes a pragmatic-realist criterion: reality is not inert
self-identity but effect-producing capacity. It also presupposes that a single unchanged entity cannot have
genuinely different causal profiles at different times without ceasing to be unchanged.

**Distinction from Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika.** ✅

| Issue | Buddhist logician | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika opponent |
|---|---|---|
| Test of reality | causal efficacy (**arthakriyā**) | substance, quality, action, universal, inherence and absence as categories |
| Enduring thing | only a conventional series | real substance enduring through changing qualities |
| Effect at different times | requires momentary causal phases | explained by auxiliary conditions acting on an enduring substance |

**Example.** ⚠️ A seed is real because it can issue in a sprout. But the seed that has exercised that precise
sprout-producing capacity is no longer the same causal phase; the series has advanced. **Limit of the
analogy:** the seed/sprout image conveys causal succession vividly but should not be read as proving, by
itself, that seed and sprout share zero continuity — that apparent continuity is exactly what §3.5's
*santāna* device is built to explain.

## 3.4 Technical doctrine: argument from destruction (vināśitvānumāna / kṣaṇabhaṅga line) ✅

- ✅ Another line of reasoning holds that destruction does not require a second, separate destroyer added
  from outside.
- ✅ The very nature of the conditioned is to perish; arising already contains ceasing.
- ⚠️ Hence things are not first fully stable entities and later destroyed; their transience belongs to what
  they are. This argument is independent of the causal-efficacy proof in §3.3 — it attacks the very idea
  that "destruction" is a distinct further event requiring its own separate cause, rather than proving
  momentariness from causal function.

## 3.5 Technical doctrine: apparent continuity — the causal continuum (santāna) ✅

- ✅ If everything is momentary, why does the world look continuous?
- ✅ Buddhism answers through **santāna** — a causal series or stream.
- ✅ Each moment gives rise to the next sufficiently similar moment, producing the appearance of
  persistence.
- ✅ Standard illustrations are a **flame** or a **stream**: continuity without strict identity.

## 3.6 Objection, reply and 2026 PYQ: memory, personal identity, karma and responsibility ✅

### Objection — Nyāya (Vātsyāyana / Uddyotakara / Udayana line), on memory and identity (2021 PYQ)

- ✅ If every mental state perishes instantly, then who remembers a past event?
- ✅ If the rememberer is numerically different from the experiencer, how can there be personal identity or
  moral responsibility?

### Buddhist replies on memory and identity

1. ✅ **Santāna**: memory belongs to the same causal stream, not to a permanent self.
2. ✅ **Pratisandhāna**: reconnection/relinking explains continuity across moments and lives.
3. ✅ **Ālaya-vijñāna** in Yogācāra: a store-consciousness carries latent impressions (**bīja**, seeds),
   explaining the preservation of karmic and mnemonic potential. *(Fully taught in Lesson 6.)*

**Philosophical point.** ⚠️ The Buddhist answer is deliberately middle-path: the later subject is neither
strictly the same nor wholly other than the earlier one.

### Objection — action and moral consequence (karma) without a permanent self (2022 PYQ)

- ✅ Karma seems to require a persisting moral subject: the doer should receive the fruit.

### Buddhist answer on karma

- ✅ The stream receives the fruit of what the earlier stream-stage performed.
- ✅ The relation is **neither identity nor utter difference**.
- ⚠️ If it were strict identity, Buddhism would collapse into eternalism; if it were total difference,
  responsibility would collapse. The doctrine of series protects moral continuity without substantial
  selfhood.

### ✅ 2026 PYQ — how momentariness problematises action and responsibility (Q5(b), 10 marks)

> **Verified question (2026 Paper I, Q5(b), 10 marks):** "How does the Buddhist theory of momentariness
> problematize the issue of human action and responsibility? Critically discuss." — cross-checked against
> the repository's own `paper-1\_PYQ-Indian-Philosophy-2026.md`, itself sourced to the official OCR of
> `QP-CSM-26-010926-Optional-PHILOSOPHY-PAPER_I.pdf`.

**Why this question is precisely a problematisation, not a simple exposition.** ⚠️ Unlike the 2022 question
(which asks for compatibility and the Buddhist response), the 2026 wording asks the candidate to first show
*how momentariness creates the problem* for action and responsibility, before critically assessing whether
the Buddhist reply actually resolves it. The demand has three layers:

1. **State the problem precisely.** If every mental and physical event lasts only one moment, then (a) the
   agent who forms an intention has already perished by the time the action is completed; (b) the agent who
   acts has already perished by the time any consequence — praise, blame, karmic fruit — could attach to
   anyone; and (c) there is, strictly, no single continuing "doer" to be responsible at all. This is often
   sharpened through two classical difficulties:
   - **Kṛtanāśa** ("loss of the done"): if the doer perishes instantly, the deed appears to vanish without
     ever generating its fruit for anyone.
   - **Akṛtābhyāgama** ("arrival of the fruit of the undone"): if the fruit ripens in a later moment
     numerically distinct from the moment that acted, that later moment appears to suffer or enjoy a
     result it never itself brought about — an innocent party receiving another's deserts.
2. **Present the Buddhist resolution.** Buddhism does not restore a permanent self to solve this. It uses
   **santāna** (causal stream) so that intention, action and fruition are causally connected phases of one
   continuously conditioned series, not of one substantial owner. Sautrāntika models this through seed-like
   causal potency transmitted moment to moment; Yogācāra (Lesson 6) refines it through **ālaya-vijñāna**
   storing **bīja** that mature into the appropriate karmic result. The relation between actor-moment and
   fruit-moment is **neither strict identity nor total difference** — exactly the Middle Path move of
   Lesson 1 applied to moral psychology.
3. **Critically assess the resolution ("critically discuss").** The causal-stream answer explains why event
   B reliably *follows* event A (causal transmission is lawful, not arbitrary), but it is less clear that it
   explains why B's suffering or reward is genuinely **B's own**, in the first-person sense that
   responsibility seems to need. Nyāya presses exactly this residual gap: causal succession may be
   sufficient for prediction but insufficient for desert unless some persisting subject "owns" the series.
   A fully critical answer must state this residual pressure explicitly rather than presenting *santāna* as
   a costless solution.

**Model answer (10 marks).** Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) holds that every real, causally efficacious entity
exists for exactly one moment (§3.3). Applied to human action, this immediately problematises
responsibility: the moment that intends an act perishes before the act is completed, and the moment that
acts perishes before any consequence can be assigned. Classical difficulties sharpen the point — *kṛtanāśa*
(the deed appears lost with the perishing doer) and *akṛtābhyāgama* (an unconnected later moment appears to
inherit an unearned fruit). Buddhism resolves this not by reviving a permanent self but by treating
intention, action and fruition as causally continuous phases of one **santāna** (causal stream): each
moment transmits a disposition (in Sautrāntika, a causal seed; in Yogācāra, a **bīja** stored in
**ālaya-vijñāna**) to its successor, so the later fruit-bearing moment is neither strictly identical with
nor wholly unrelated to the earlier acting moment — the same Middle Path structure used throughout
Buddhist metaphysics. This blocks the crude charges of "lost action" and "undeserved result." **Critical
qualification:** the account explains causal transmission convincingly but is weaker at explaining
first-person *ownership* of the fruit — Nyāya's objection that recognition and desert seem to require one
persisting subject retains real force, and Buddhism's reply (that "mine-ness" is itself a further conceptual
construction within the stream) is coherent but not universally regarded as fully decisive. **Verdict:**
momentariness genuinely problematises naive notions of agency and desert, and Buddhism's causal-continuum
reply preserves moral practice at the conventional level without fully closing the ownership gap that
rival realist schools press.

## 3.7 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ One lamp lights another. The second flame is not numerically the first, yet it is causally connected to

  lamp-to-lamp transfer conveys causal dependency clearly but risks implying that something (a "flame-stuff")
  literally moves across; the intended point is only lawful causal succession, not the transfer of a
  substance.

## 3.8 Comparative treatment: Buddhist momentariness against rival persistence theories ✅/⚠️

| Issue | Buddhist momentariness | Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | Sāṃkhya | Advaita Vedānta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What persists | nothing substantially; only a causal series (santāna) | an enduring substance bearing changing qualities | an eternal, unchanging puruṣa distinct from a transforming prakṛti | an unchanging Brahman/Ātman beneath apparent change |
| How is change explained | new momentary causal phases replace prior ones | one substance undergoes qualitative change through time | prakṛti really transforms (pariṇāma); puruṣa itself never changes | change is only apparent (vivarta), superimposed on the changeless |
| Cost of the position | continuity of memory/identity/karma becomes harder to explain | must explain how a strict substance nonetheless "changes" without losing identity | must explain the causal relation between an inactive puruṣa and an active prakṛti | must explain why an unchanging Brahman appears to change at all (māyā) |

## 3.9 Objection — Nyāya recognition (pratyabhijñā) argument, and reply ✅

**Objection.** ❓ Recognition such as "this is the same person I met before" appears to require enduring
identity, not merely stream-likeness.

**Reply.** ✅ Buddhism can say recognition tracks practical continuity and similarity (**sādṛśya**) within a
series, not metaphysical identity of a substance. ⚠️ The language of sameness belongs to conventional truth
(taught fully in Lesson 7); ultimate analysis reveals only a flow of conditioned moments. The Nyāya
rejoinder retains force: a mere series seems unable to own memories or deserts unless something owns the
series — this exact tension is why the 2026 PYQ above asks for a *critical* discussion rather than a mere
exposition.

## 4.1 Simple intuition

If there is no fixed "me" running the show — no unchanging inner controller behind experience — why does
everything not simply fall apart into chaos? And if there really is no self, is Buddhist religious life (an
follower "taking refuge," meditating, seeking liberation) just an elaborate contradiction? Buddhism's answer
is that the mistake is not in denying a fixed self; the mistake is in assuming that only a fixed self could
explain order, practice and liberation. An organised, causally connected process — like a well-run
institution with no single permanent director, or a river that is never "the same river" and yet is
recognisably one river — can be orderly, morally accountable within limits (Lesson 3), and even capable of
a designed transformation (liberation), without any component of it being a permanent substance.

## 4.2 Technical doctrine: statement of no-self (nairātmyavāda; anātmavāda) ✅

- ✅ **No-self (nairātmyavāda; anātmavāda)** denies a permanent, unchanging self (**ātman**) behind
  experience.
- ✅ What is conventionally called a person is only an aggregate of changing factors.

## 4.3 Technical doctrine: the five aggregates (skandhas) ✅

- ✅ The person is analysed into five **skandhas**:
  1. form or material body (**rūpa**)
  2. feeling or sensation (**vedanā**)
  3. perception or recognition (**saṃjñā**)
  4. dispositions or formations (**saṃskāra**)
  5. consciousness (**vijñāna**)
- ⚠️ The philosophical force of the doctrine lies in exhaustive analysis: if what we call self reduces to
  these aggregates and no extra owner is found, the self is a conceptual imputation, not an independently
  real substance.

## 4.4 Example: the chariot analogy (Milinda-pañha) ✅

- ✅ The classic **chariot analogy** argues that "chariot" is merely a name for the arrangement of axle,
  wheels, yoke and other parts.
- ✅ Likewise, "person" is a conventional designation for the aggregate of skandhas.
- ⚠️ **Limit of the analogy:** the point is not that the person is uselessly unreal — it is that the person
  is conventionally valid but not ultimately self-subsistent. Do not over-read the analogy as proving that
  persons are complete illusions with no practical reality at all.

## 4.5 Technical doctrine: personal no-self (pudgala-nairātmya) and phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairātmya) — 2021 PYQ ✅

### Personal no-self (pudgala-nairātmya)

- ✅ Denial of a permanent personal self (**pudgala**, person).
- ✅ This is the minimum no-self thesis common to Buddhism.

### Phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairātmya)

- ✅ Denial that even dharmas possess intrinsic self-nature.
- ✅ This is developed most strongly in Mahāyāna, especially Mādhyamika (Lesson 7).

### School difference

- ✅ Early Buddhism and non-Mahāyāna Abhidharma traditions share the denial of a permanent personal self.
  Several realist Abhidharma schools (Lesson 5) nevertheless treat dharmas as ultimately real.
- ✅ Mahāyāna, especially Mādhyamika, develops the denial of intrinsic nature in dharmas themselves.
- ⚠️ This is a difference of philosophical scope, not permission to equate Theravāda with the historically
  loaded Hīnayāna label (Lesson 1, §1.2).

### Distinction table

| Thesis | What is denied | Who strongly stresses it |
|---|---|---|
| Personal no-self (pudgala-nairātmya) | enduring person/self | shared across Buddhist traditions |
| Phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairātmya) | intrinsic nature of dharmas | developed emphatically in Mahāyāna, especially Mādhyamika |

## 4.6 The Buddhist person-theory foil: Puggalavāda ✅

- ✅ Some Buddhist groups, often associated with the **Vātsīputrīyas**, proposed a **pudgala** that is
  neither the same as nor different from the skandhas.
- ✅ Mainstream Buddhists criticised this as a relapse toward self-theory.
- ⚠️ In answers, use Puggalavāda as a foil: it shows that the pressure to preserve moral continuity was
  real, but orthodox Buddhist thought refused to answer it by reintroducing a substantial person. This is
  the clearest internal illustration of causal personhood under pressure: even Buddhists who felt the pull
  of the Nyāya-style objections of Lesson 3 (memory, karma) declined to solve them by smuggling in a
  disguised self.

## 4.7 Technical doctrine: the Three Jewels (Triratna) and no-self — 2022 PYQ ✅

**Problem.** ✅ If there is no self, who takes refuge in the **Triratna** — Buddha, Dharma, Saṅgha?

**Reply.** ✅ Buddhism can answer: the refuge-taker is the **conventional person**, a **prajñapti-puruṣa** or
designated person, not an ultimately real soul. Ethical and spiritual practice proceeds at the level of
conventional truth without requiring metaphysical ātman. ⚠️ Thus anātman does not abolish discipline; it
purifies it of ego-metaphysics.

## 4.8 Technical doctrine: cessation of conditioned suffering (nirvāṇa) ✅

- ✅ **Cessation of conditioned suffering (nirvāṇa)** extinguishes the three fires: attachment (**rāga**),
  aversion (**dveṣa**) and delusion (**moha**).
- ✅ It is not best understood as a substantial place or eternal self-state gained by an entity.
- ⚠️ It is the cessation of the very process that produces bondage.

## 4.9 Is nirvāṇa consistent with momentariness and no-self? — 2025 PYQ ✅

### Objection — Śaṃkara-type Advaita and Nyāya realist (previewed here, developed fully in Lesson 9)

- ✅ If there is no self, who attains nirvāṇa?
- ✅ If everything is momentary, what persists in liberation?

### Reply

1. ✅ The question "who attains?" assumes the very self Buddhism rejects.
2. ✅ Nirvāṇa is not an entity possessed by a soul.
3. ✅ It is the cessation of craving, ignorance and the suffering-series grounded in them.
4. ⚠️ So consonance exists: no-self removes the false claimant to liberation; momentariness explains why the
   chain can in fact cease.

### Theravāda and Mahāyāna nuance ✅

- ✅ **Theravāda:** foregrounds the arhat (Pali: arahant) ideal and describes cessation of suffering as
  *nibbāna*; important Theravāda classifications treat *nibbāna* as **unconditioned**, without making it the
  state of an eternal self.
- ✅ **Mahāyāna:** foregrounds the bodhisattva path toward complete Buddhahood for the benefit of all beings.
- ⚠️ Do not reproduce the old polemical caricature that arhatship is simply selfish or that the bodhisattva
  ideal is merely delayed personal salvation (recall Lesson 1, §1.6).
- ✅ In Nāgārjuna's radical language (Lesson 7), **saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two independent realms**; the
  difference lies in how dependent reality is grasped.

**Scope reminder (from Lesson 1, §1.5).** ⚠️ Nirvāṇa, as unconditioned (*asaṃskṛta*) in important Buddhist
classifications, is not simply "one more momentary conditioned event" — momentariness as a thesis applies
strictly to conditioned reals, and this distinction is exactly what makes the 2025 PYQ's "critically
discuss" demand answerable without contradiction.

## 4.10 Comparative treatment: rebirth and personhood without a permanent self ✅/⚠️

| Question | Buddhist answer | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| What links one life to the next, if not a soul? | causal continuum (santāna), reconnection (pratisandhāna), and (in Yogācāra) ālaya-vijñāna carrying karmic seeds | Jainism: an eternal jīva itself migrates and is really bound/freed (Lesson 9, §9.6). Advaita: the individual jīva's bondage is ultimately an appearance on one unchanging Ātman-Brahman. |

## 4.11 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (Śaṃkara/Advaita and Nyāya realist, fully developed in Lesson 9).** ❓ Without a self, there is
no one to be reborn, no one to suffer, and no one to be liberated — anātman appears to make the entire
soteriological project (rebirth, karma, nirvāṇa) unintelligible.

**Reply.** ✅ Causal continuity (santāna, pratisandhāna) is offered as sufficient for rebirth and karmic
continuity without requiring numerical identity of a substantial self; the conventional person is sufficient
for religious practice (§4.7); and nirvāṇa is reframed as process-cessation rather than soul-survival
(§4.8–§4.9). ⚠️ The residual, honestly-stated weakness (returned to fully in Lesson 9, §9.2 and §9.3) is
that critics still press whether a "conventional person" with no ultimate owner can really be the subject of
first-person suffering and first-person liberation, or whether talk of "conventional" selfhood quietly
smuggles back exactly what was denied.

**Caption.** Both schools accept the full causal/momentary apparatus of Lessons 2–3; their disagreement is
narrow and precise — not "is the world real?" (both say yes) but "is the external object grasped directly,
or only inferred from an internal representation?" and "do past and future dharmas exist in the same sense
as present ones?" Getting this narrow, precise axis of disagreement right is exactly what separates a
strong UPSC answer from a vague "one school is more realist than the other."

## 5.1 Simple intuition

Suppose you accept everything taught so far: nothing is permanent, everything is conditioned, reality is
momentary. A further question remains completely open: when you see a tree, is the tree itself — an
external, mind-independent momentary object — directly present to your awareness, the way a photograph
directly shows what is in front of the camera? Or is what you are aware of really only an internal image or
representation, caused by an external tree you never directly touch with your awareness, the way a
detective infers a suspect's presence from footprints rather than seeing the suspect? Vaibhāṣika answers
"directly present"; Sautrāntika answers "only inferred." Both remain thoroughgoing Buddhist realists —
external, causally efficacious matter genuinely exists for both — but they disagree sharply on the
*epistemic route* by which we know it. This disagreement about the object of knowledge is the exact seed
from which Yogācāra's later, much more radical idealism (Lesson 6) grows.

## 5.2 Technical doctrine: Vaibhāṣika direct realism ✅

- ✅ Usually associated with **Sarvāstivāda** realism, especially the Kashmir *Mahāvibhāṣā* tradition.
- ✅ Holds **bāhya-pratyakṣa-vāda**: external objects are directly perceived.
- ✅ Affirms **sarvam asti** in the technical sense that dharmas of past, present and future have some mode
  of existence.
- ⚠️ The doctrine does not mean that all three times are causally active in exactly the same way; present
  dharmas have the distinctive present efficacy.

**Philosophical significance.** ⚠️ Vaibhāṣika preserves Buddhist anti-self doctrine while still maintaining a
robust realism about dharmas. It is therefore a useful contrast both to Yogācāra idealism (Lesson 6) and to
Mādhyamika anti-essentialism (Lesson 7).

## 5.3 Technical doctrine: Sautrāntika representational realism ✅

- ✅ Holds **bāhyānumeya-vāda**: external objects exist, but are known only inferentially through mental
  representations.
- ✅ Rejects the Sarvāstivāda thesis that past and future dharmas exist in the same sense as present ones.
- ✅ Strongly privileges the present moment.
- ⚠️ Historically, "Sautrāntika" can designate critical currents rather than one simple institutional
  school; the exam-relevant doxographic contrast is its appeal to sūtra and inferred external objects
  against Vaibhāṣika direct realism.

### Distinction from Vaibhāṣika

| Issue | Vaibhāṣika | Sautrāntika |
|---|---|---|
| External object | real | real |
| How known | directly perceived | inferentially known via representation |
| Temporal ontology | past-present-future dharmas exist | only present dharmas fully exist |

## 5.4 Full derivation: how dependent origination supports the realist conclusion (completing Lesson 2, §2.10) ✅

- ✅ The realist Buddhist schools agree that things arise dependently.
- ✅ But instead of inferring emptiness of all dharmas in the Mādhyamika sense (Lesson 7), they say:
  precisely *because* things arise causally, they are **real as conditioned entities**.
- ✅ Vaibhāṣika treats dharmas as real and directly knowable; Sautrāntika treats them as real though not
  directly perceived externally.
- ⚠️ Thus the same causal thesis that Mādhyamika uses to derive emptiness of self-nature is used by the
  realist schools to derive the reality of conditioned, causally efficacious dharmas — the disagreement is
  not about whether dependent origination is true (both accept it) but about what follows from accepting
  it.

| School move | What is denied | What is affirmed |
|---|---|---|
| Mādhyamika (Lesson 7) | intrinsic nature (svabhāva) | conventional dependent existence |
| Vaibhāṣika / Sautrāntika | eternal substance and self | reality of conditioned dharmas |

**Argument, step by step.** ✅
1. All dharmas arise dependent on conditions (Lesson 2).
2. What arises dependently and functions causally satisfies the criterion of the real, arthakriyā (Lesson
   3, §3.3).
3. Dharmas do function causally — they produce effects, sustain the causal continuum, and are not sheer
   fictions.
4. Therefore conditioned dharmas are real, though momentary and non-substantial — this is the Vaibhāṣika/
   Sautrāntika realist conclusion.

## 5.5 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ A patch of blue seen in ordinary perception, for Vaibhāṣika, is the direct presentation of an external,
  momentary colour-dharma to visual consciousness — nothing stands "between" awareness and the object. For
  Sautrāntika, the same patch of blue is a mental image (**ākāra**) caused by an external, momentary
  colour-dharma that itself is never directly grasped — awareness infers the external cause from the
  presence of the image, much as one infers fire from smoke.

## 5.6 Comparative treatment: Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika against Theravāda and against each other ✅

| Question | Vaibhāṣika | Sautrāntika | Theravāda (for contrast — NOT one of the four schools) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Institutional lineage | Sarvāstivāda / Kashmir Mahāvibhāṣā, non-Mahāyāna | critical current in the Sarvāstivāda orbit, non-Mahāyāna | its own surviving tradition with the Pāli Tipiṭaka |
| External object | real, directly perceived | real, inferred via representation | affirmed within its own Abhidhamma realism, but not framed in Sanskrit Vaibhāṣika/Sautrāntika technical vocabulary |
| Past/future dharmas | exist in some mode (sarvam asti) | only the present is fully real | not organised around this particular Sanskrit Abhidharma dispute |

> Theravāda. They share only the broad, older "non-Mahāyāna" doxographic classification with it; canon,
> language and institutional history differ.

lesson supplies the first two rungs of that ladder.

## 5.8 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (internal Buddhist, from Sautrāntika against Vaibhāṣika).** ✅ If dharmas of all three times
exist in some sense, momentariness (Lesson 3) is weakened and the ontology becomes needlessly overpopulated
— why posit the existence of a past dharma that no longer performs any present causal function?

**Reply (Vaibhāṣika).** ⚠️ A technical distinction is drawn between a dharma's own-nature (which, in the
Sarvāstivāda sense, persists across the three times in some mode) and its distinctive causal function
(**kāritra**), which belongs only to the present moment. This preserves the letter of "sarvam asti" while
still granting present dharmas a unique operative status — though many later Buddhist logicians, including
Sautrāntika, judged this distinction to be an uneasy compromise rather than a fully stable position, which
is precisely why the more austere Sautrāntika presentism gained ground.

**Objection (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika realist, from outside Buddhism, previewed here and developed in Lesson 9).** ❓
If Sautrāntika external objects are never directly perceived, only inferred from representations, how can
one ever be sure an external object corresponding to the representation exists at all, rather than the
representation arising on its own?

**Reply.** ✅ Sautrāntika treats the systematic, lawful correlation between representation and inferred
external cause as sufficient for a valid inferential pramāṇa (Lesson 8) — inference from smoke to fire is
similarly indirect yet counts as valid knowledge. ⚠️ This very worry, pressed further, is exactly the seed of
Yogācāra's argument (Lesson 6) that the inferred external object can be dispensed with altogether.

**Caption.** Yogācāra is best taught as the next rung of the escalation begun in Lesson 5: it takes
Sautrāntika's admission that we never directly touch the external object and asks why an external object
needs to be posited at all. Its two central arguments (dream, atom/whole) attack the inference to an
external cause; its positive doctrine (ālaya-vijñāna, seeds, three natures) then has to do all the work
that "the external world" used to do — explaining order, regularity and the appearance of a shared world —
without literally re-admitting matter.

## 6.1 Simple intuition

You have almost certainly had a dream in which a whole convincing world appeared — people, places, physical
sensations — with no corresponding external object anywhere. If a fully structured experience of an
"external" world can occur with nothing external causing it, then the sheer vividness or structure of an
experience can never, by itself, prove that something external caused it. Yogācāra generalises this insight
from dreams to waking life: perhaps waking experience, too, is entirely a matter of how consciousness
presents itself, without anything genuinely outside consciousness being needed to explain it. The hard
question this immediately raises is: if there is no shared external world, why do different people's
experiences line up so consistently — why can two people apparently see "the same" table? Yogācāra's
answer is a sophisticated internal mechanism (store-consciousness and seeds) that produces coordinated
experience without an external world.

## 6.2 Technical doctrine: statement of consciousness-only (vijñaptimātratā) ✅

- ✅ Yogācāra advances **consciousness-only or representation-only (vijñaptimātra)**.
- ✅ What appears as external object is inseparable from modes of consciousness.

## 6.3 Step-by-step argument: two lines against external realism ✅

1. ✅ **Dream argument:** dream-objects are vividly experienced despite lacking corresponding external
   objects; therefore experienced objecthood does not prove external independent matter.
2. ✅ **Atom critique:** if atoms are partless, they cannot combine spatially; if they have parts, they are
   not ultimate atoms. Hence external atomism is unstable.
3. ⚠️ (Cross-checked against the existing repository session for completeness): a further supporting
   consideration sometimes added is that a strictly momentary external object (per Lesson 3's
   momentariness) would already have ceased before the cognition it supposedly causes becomes available —
   sharpening the difficulty of external causation of experience even on the realist schools' own
   momentariness commitments.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The dream argument presupposes that the *phenomenal character* of an experience
(how vivid or structured it feels) cannot by itself settle whether an external cause exists — a
presupposition many realists (Lesson 9) directly contest.

## 6.4 Technical doctrine: store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) ✅

- ✅ **Store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna)** preserves karmic seeds (**bījas**), explaining continuity,
  memory and rebirth without a permanent self.
- ⚠️ Store-consciousness is a conditioned, transforming stream, not an eternal ātman in disguise — it
  supplies the mechanism previewed in Lesson 3, §3.6 for how memory and karmic fruition are transmitted
  across momentary phases.

## 6.5 Technical doctrine: the three natures (tri-svabhāva) ✅

- ✅ imagined nature (**parikalpita-svabhāva**)
- ✅ dependent nature (**paratantra-svabhāva**)
- ✅ perfected or consummate nature (**pariniṣpanna-svabhāva**)
- ⚠️ These explain how deluded objectification arises and how it is overcome.

| Nature | Meaning | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Parikalpita | imagined subject-object duality | the deluded, constructed appearance of an external world confronting an internal subject |
| Paratantra | dependently arisen flow of cognition | the actual causal flow of consciousness-moments on which the imagined duality is superimposed |
| Pariniṣpanna | perfected realisation of the absence of duality | insight that removes the imagined duality from the dependent flow, revealing things as they are |

## 6.6 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ Two people asleep in the same room may each dream a vivid, fully structured world with no relation to
  each other's dream and no external object at all — yet each dream is internally regular and rule-governed
  (objects behave consistently within one dream). Yogācāra treats ordinary waking experience analogously:
  internally regular and apparently shared, without needing an external object, because **ālaya-vijñāna**
  and shared karmic seed-maturation, not a mind-independent external world, produce the appearance of
  intersubjective regularity. **Limit of the analogy:** ordinary waking experience is far more stable and
  cross-checkable between independent persons than any two individuals' private dreams ever are — this gap
  is exactly what critics press in §6.8 below.

## 6.7 Comparative treatment: Yogācāra against the realist schools and against Mādhyamika (previewed) ✅/⚠️

| Issue | Vaibhāṣika/Sautrāntika (Lesson 5) | Yogācāra | Mādhyamika (Lesson 7, previewed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| External object | affirmed (directly or inferentially) | denied as independently established | neither object nor consciousness has intrinsic nature (svabhāva) |
| What explains continuity | causal series of external and internal dharmas | ālaya-vijñāna and bīja | conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya), without reifying any level |
| Risk each position runs | naive realism about an unverifiable "external" cause | reifying consciousness as a subtle substance | apparent nihilism if two truths are not carefully stated |

⚠️ **Escalation payoff (continuing Lesson 5, §5.7):** Yogācāra's own siddhānta — that consciousness-only
explains appearance, karma and continuity through ālaya-vijñāna — itself becomes a pūrvapakṣa for
Mādhyamika, which objects that consciousness is being reified as a subtle substance. Mādhyamika's reply,
completed in Lesson 7, is that even consciousness lacks svabhāva; all dharmas, including ālaya-vijñāna
itself, are empty.

## 6.8 Criticism and reply — 2019 PYQ context ✅

**Objection (realist, e.g. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika and Sautrāntika holdouts).** ❓ If there is no external world,
why do independent persons' experiences agree so precisely and so testably (two people can jointly measure
the same table, disagree with each other about a hallucination, and correct errors through shared
experiment)? Private dream-coherence is a poor model for this degree of intersubjective, correctable
regularity.

**Reply (Yogācāra).** ✅ The shared regularity is explained by structurally similar **bīja** (karmic seeds)
maturing in parallel across different streams of **ālaya-vijñāna**, producing coordinated experience without
requiring a literal external world. ⚠️ This is a genuine and non-trivial explanatory proposal, but it is
also the weakest point of the school: critics reasonably ask whether "structurally similar seeds in
different streams, by coincidence of karmic history, produce coordinated experience" is doing real
explanatory work, or is simply relabelling the very intersubjective regularity that needed explaining.
❓ The tradition itself does not fully close this debate, and a UPSC answer should state the objection and
reply candidly rather than presenting Yogācāra idealism as costlessly victorious.

**Safest exam-answer framing.** ⚠️ Present Yogācāra as a **critique of independent objecthood while
preserving cognitive order**, not as the crude claim "nothing exists" — this is the exact remedial point

## 7.1 Simple intuition

If everything depends on something else for its existence (Lesson 2), does anything have its *own*,
built-in, independent nature at all — the kind of nature a thing would keep even if every relation to
everything else around it were stripped away? Mādhyamika's radical answer is: no, nothing does, and this is
what it means to call something "empty" (**śūnya**). This sounds, at first hearing, exactly like saying
"nothing exists" — but that is precisely the misreading Mādhyamika works hardest to block. The intuition to
hold onto is this: a family, a government, a river or a friendship can be completely real and effective in
the ordinary sense while having *no* fixed, independent, self-contained essence apart from its constituent
relations. Mādhyamika claims that *everything whatsoever*, without exception, is like this — dependently
real, but never independently, intrinsically real.

## 7.2 Technical doctrine: statement of emptiness (śūnyatā) ✅

- ✅ Associated especially with **Nāgārjuna**.
- ✅ The central thesis is **emptiness (śūnyatā)**: absence of **intrinsic nature or own-being (svabhāva)**,
  not sheer nothingness.
- ✅ In substance, Nāgārjuna identifies dependent origination and emptiness: what is dependently arisen is
  empty of independent essence.

## 7.3 Step-by-step derivation from dependent origination (completing Lesson 2, §2.10(a)) ✅

1. ✅ Whatever has **svabhāva** would be independent, unproduced by anything else, and incapable of
   alteration by conditions.
2. ✅ But all dharmas arise dependent on causes and conditions (Lesson 2).
3. ✅ Whatever is dependently produced cannot be independent, unproduced or unalterable.
4. ✅ Therefore no dharma possesses svabhāva.
5. ✅ Absence of svabhāva is what is meant by **śūnyatā**, emptiness.
6. ⚠️ This is not a further, separate metaphysical discovery layered on top of dependent origination; it is
   the same fact restated at the level of intrinsic-nature analysis rather than mere causal description.

## 7.4 Technical doctrine: the four-cornered analysis (catuṣkoṭi) ✅

| Corner | Sanskrit | Basic claim |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **asti** | It is. |
| 2 | **nāsti** | It is not. |
| 3 | **asti ca nāsti ca** | It both is and is not. |
| 4 | **naivāsti naiva nāsti** | It neither is nor is not. |

- ✅ **Early / Nikāya use:** the *avyākata/avyākṛta* questions (fully taught in Lesson 9, §9.4) are often
  framed through four alternatives, and all four are set aside because they do not conduce to liberation and
  rest on misleading presuppositions.
- ✅ **Madhyamaka use:** Nāgārjuna negates all four alternatives when they are applied to things imagined as
  possessing svabhāva.
- ⚠️ Thus the early use is primarily therapeutic and soteriological; the Madhyamaka use is also
  logical-ontological, because it exposes the incoherence of intrinsic nature.

## 7.5 Crucial logical nuance: why this is not simple contradiction ✅

- ✅ Madhyamaka negation is best read as **prasajya-pratiṣedha** (non-implicative / commitment-free
  negation), not **paryudāsa-pratiṣedha** (implicative negation that asserts a positive alternative).
- ✅ The four corners are denied **of things taken as having svabhāva**; once svabhāva is dropped, the
  denial does not install a fifth positive thesis.
- ✅ Hence Nāgārjuna can claim, in the spirit of the *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, that he has no independent
  **pratijñā** (thesis) of his own.
- ⚠️ This means: "I deny that x has intrinsic nature" is not meant to assert a new metaphysical substrate
  called emptiness.

## 7.6 Technical doctrine: the two truths as the anti-nihilist frame ✅

| Truth | Meaning | Exam use |
|---|---|---|
| **saṃvṛti-satya** | Conventional truth: language, persons, karma, compassion and practice function
  dependently. | Prevents Madhyamaka from abolishing ordinary discourse. |
| **paramārtha-satya** | Ultimate truth: no dharma possesses svabhāva. | Prevents conventional truth from
  being mistaken for substance. |

- ✅ Conventional truth is not mere falsehood; it is the domain within which practical life and Buddhist
  discipline function.
- ✅ Ultimate truth is insight into the emptiness of all reified positions.
- ✅ Nāgārjuna warns that emptiness wrongly grasped destroys the dull-witted, like a snake wrongly seized.
- ⚠️ The two-truths doctrine keeps catuṣkoṭi from collapsing into nihilism: conventional functioning is
  preserved, ultimate intrinsic being is denied.

## 7.7 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ A rainbow is a genuine, dependently-arisen visual phenomenon — sunlight, water droplets and an observer
  at a specific angle jointly produce it. It has no independent "rainbow-substance" apart from these
  conditions, yet it is not a mere hallucination; under the right conditions, it reliably, predictably
  appears. Mādhyamika treats *every* phenomenon, including persons, karma and even nirvāṇa itself, this way:
  conventionally functional, ultimately without intrinsic own-nature. **Limit of the analogy:** a rainbow's
  dependence on external optical conditions is easy to see; Mādhyamika's more radical claim is that even
  apparently solid, persisting things (a person, a mountain, a moment of consciousness) are exactly this
  kind of dependently-arisen, svabhāva-empty phenomenon.

## 7.8 Objection — Nyāya/Uddyotakara-type and modern irrationalism charge — and the *prasaṅga* reply ✅

**Objection.** ✅ The opponent says: if all four alternatives are denied, the Mādhyamika either says nothing
or contradicts himself. The opponent adds: the denial itself is a thesis, so the claim "I have no thesis"
is self-refuting.

**Reply — Nāgārjuna and later Madhyamaka.** ✅ Nāgārjuna's reply is the "my words are empty too" move: his
statements do not possess svabhāva and therefore do not create a positive ontological commitment. The
traditional image is that one magically created person (**nirmitaka**) may counteract another magically
created person; similarly, empty words may cure empty reifications. The Mādhyamika uses arguments as
medicine, not as a new metaphysical substance. ⚠️ Whether this reply fully succeeds is genuinely debated.

**The internal Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika split.** ❓ This debate marks an internal division: **Candrakīrti** is
associated with using reductio (**prasaṅga** — deriving an absurd or unwanted consequence from the
opponent's own premises, without asserting any independent counter-thesis), while **Bhāvaviveka** allows
independent (**svatantra**) inferences in debate, formulating positive Madhyamaka syllogisms of his own.
⚠️ The Prāsaṅgika method is the more thoroughgoing application of the "no independent thesis" principle: it
refutes the opponent purely by exposing the opponent's own premises' unacceptable consequences, never
committing Madhyamaka itself to a rival positive claim that could, in turn, be attacked as reifying
emptiness.

## 7.9 Emptiness (śūnyatā) is not nihilism ✅

- ✅ To say things are empty is to say they lack **svabhāva**.
- ✅ It is not to say they are absent in every sense.
- ✅ Since conventional truth remains operative, compassion, karma and the path are not denied.
- ⚠️ Emptiness undercuts metaphysical grasping, not dependent functioning.
- ✅ **Emptiness of emptiness:** śūnyatā itself must not be reified into a new Absolute or hidden substrate —
  this single qualification is the sharpest available reply to the "crypto-Advaita" trap developed fully in
  Lesson 9, §9.7.

## 8.1 Simple intuition

How many fundamentally different *ways* of coming to know something are there? Nyāya famously says four:
perception, inference, comparison and testimony. Buddhist epistemologists, led by **Dignāga** and
**Dharmakīrti**, insist there are exactly **two**, and their reasoning is elegant: a way of knowing should be
individuated by the *kind of object* it delivers, and there are only two fundamentally different kinds of
object in the world — momentary, unrepeatable, causally active particulars (this exact patch of blue, right
now) and constructed, shareable, describable general concepts (the concept "blue" itself, as something you
can talk about and apply to many different particulars). Perception delivers the first kind; inference and
concept-use deliver the second. Once this two-object, two-pramāṇa framework is understood, testimony,
comparison and the other candidate "extra" pramāṇas can each be shown to reduce, in the end, to one or the
other.

## 8.2 Technical doctrine: the two-pramāṇa thesis ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Dignāga and Dharmakīrti accept only two independent **pramāṇas** (means of valid
knowledge): **pratyakṣa** (perception) and **anumāna** (inference). Other candidates — **upamāna**
(comparison), **arthāpatti** (postulation), **anupalabdhi** (non-apprehension) and **śabda** (testimony) —
are reduced to inference or to perception-plus-inference.

**Argument.** ✅
1. A pramāṇa must be individuated by the kind of object it reveals.
2. There are exactly two object-types: **svalakṣaṇa** (unique particular / self-characterised particular)
   and **sāmānyalakṣaṇa** (general character / constructed universal-aspect).
3. Perception grasps the svalakṣaṇa.
4. Inference grasps the sāmānyalakṣaṇa.
5. Therefore there are exactly two pramāṇas.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ The number of knowledge-sources is fixed by the object-side, not by a mere list of
human cognitive habits. There is no third kind of object beyond the unique causal particular and the
constructed general character.

**Contrast with Nyāya.** ✅

| Issue | Buddhist pramāṇa school | Nyāya |
|---|---|---|
| Number of pramāṇas | Two: perception and inference | Four: perception, inference, comparison, testimony |
| Testimony | reducible to inference from reliable signs / speaker-function | independent pramāṇa |
| Universals | conceptual constructions | real universals inhering in particulars |

## 8.3 Technical doctrine: unique particular (svalakṣaṇa) and general character (sāmānyalakṣaṇa) ✅

| Feature | Svalakṣaṇa | Sāmānyalakṣaṇa |
|---|---|---|
| Ontological status | paramārtha-sat (ultimately real) | saṃvṛti-sat (conventionally real) |
| Temporal status | momentary | enduring only as a construct |
| Cognition | non-conceptual perception | conceptual inference / judgment |
| Language | ineffable as a unique particular | expressible and shareable |
| Causal role | causally efficient | causally inert as a universal construct |

- ✅ The svalakṣaṇa is unique, momentary, causally efficient, ineffable and non-conceptual.
- ✅ The sāmānyalakṣaṇa is shareable, expressible and stable only as a conceptual construction.
- ⚠️ This is why Buddhist realism about momentary particulars (Lessons 3, 5) can coexist with nominalism
  about universals (§8.4 below).

## 8.4 Technical doctrine: perception as non-conceptual (kalpanāpoḍha) and non-erroneous (abhrānta) ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Dignāga defines perception as **kalpanāpoḍha** (free from conceptual construction).
Dharmakīrti adds **abhrānta** (non-erroneous), so perception is non-conceptual and non-mistaken.

**Why the addition matters.** ✅ Mere vivid presentation is not enough for valid perception. Illusions such
as a double moon or the circle produced by a whirling firebrand can be vivid while still mistaken. ⚠️
Dharmakīrti's addition protects Buddhist epistemology from counting every non-conceptual appearance as valid
knowledge.

**Fourfold division of perception.** ✅

| Type | Meaning | Philosophical point |
|---|---|---|
| **indriya-pratyakṣa** | sense perception | non-conceptual contact with the particular through sense-faculty |
| **mānasa-pratyakṣa** | mental perception | immediate mental awareness following sensory presentation |
| **svasaṃvedana** | self-awareness of cognition | cognition manifests itself without a second knower |
| **yogi-pratyakṣa** | yogic perception | disciplined direct insight into subtle truths relevant to liberation |

⚠️ **Svasaṃvedana** connects Buddhist epistemology with Yogācāra idealism (Lesson 6) because cognition is
treated as self-revealing; this is why Nyāya attacks it as collapsing object, cognition and knower into
one.

## 8.5 Technical doctrine: exclusion theory of meaning (apoha) and universals ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Apoha** (exclusion) says a general term does not denote a real universal (**jāti**)
inhering in particulars. A word functions through **anya-apoha** (exclusion of the other): "cow" means
"not-non-cow."

**Argument.** ✅
1. Only unique particulars are ultimately real.
2. Words and concepts are general.
3. If generality were real, one would need real universals; but universals are not perceived and produce
   the Nyāya problems of inherence and location.
4. Therefore generality must be constructed negatively through exclusion.

**Presupposition.** ⚠️ Apoha presupposes that a negative/differential function can do the work usually
assigned to a positive shared property.

**Objection — Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Uddyotakara (previewed here, fully developed in Lesson 9).** ✅ The
exclusion seems circular: to exclude non-cows one must already know what a cow is. A merely negative content
seems unable to explain the positive determinate cognition "cow," and seems unable to explain the felt
similarity among cows.

**Reply — Dharmakīrti's line.** ✅ Similarity is grounded not in a shared universal but in a **shared causal
capacity**: many distinct particulars produce the same effect — the same conceptual judgment and the same
successful practical activity. Apoha is a conceptual construct erected upon that causal convergence, not
upon a prior grasp of a real universal. ⚠️ The circularity objection retains residual force; many critics
hold that the Buddhist reply does not fully explain positive determination — this residual tension is
carried forward, fully assessed, into Lesson 9.

## 8.6 Technical doctrine: causal efficacy (arthakriyākāritva) as the criterion of the real — completing Lesson 3 ✅

**Statement.** ✅ **Sat = arthakriyā-samartha**: to be real is to be capable of producing an effect. This
criterion ranks the svalakṣaṇa as ultimately real and the sāmānyalakṣaṇa as conventional.

**Argument connecting epistemology to kṣaṇikavāda.** ✅
1. To be real is to be causally efficacious.
2. Causal efficacy must occur either successively or simultaneously.
3. A permanent entity can do neither coherently: if it produces everything at once, nothing remains for
   later moments; if it produces successively, it changes; if it needs auxiliary conditions, the decisive
   efficacy lies in the changing conditions.
4. Therefore whatever is real is momentary — **yat sat tat kṣaṇikam** (fully derived in Lesson 3, §3.3).

⚠️ **Exam payoff:** this is the clean bridge from pramāṇa theory to ontology — what perception ultimately
presents is the momentary causally efficient particular; what inference and language manage is the
conventional general construction.

## 8.7 Technical doctrine: means of valid knowledge and its result (pramāṇa and pramāṇaphala) ✅

**Statement.** ✅ Dignāga's distinctive thesis is that **pramāṇa** (instrument of knowledge) and
**pramāṇaphala** (result of knowledge) are not two ultimately different things. The resultant cognition —
the determination of the object and the self-awareness of cognition — is non-different from the cognitive
event that functions as pramāṇa.

**Argument.** ✅ On a momentary and non-substantialist ontology, there is no enduring agent-instrument-result
triad. The analysis into **karaṇa** (instrument) and **phala** (result) is a conceptual overlay on one
momentary cognitive event. ⚠️ This shows that Buddhist epistemology is internally driven by **anātman**
(Lesson 4) and **kṣaṇikavāda** (Lesson 3), not by free-standing formal logic.

**Contrast with Nyāya.** ✅

| Factor | Nyāya | Buddhist pramāṇa school |
|---|---|---|
| **pramātṛ** (knower) | real enduring subject | constructed stream, not ultimate self |
| **pramāṇa** (instrument) | distinct means | non-different from cognition as event |
| **prameya** (object) | independently knowable object | ultimately svalakṣaṇa; conventionally constructed object |
| **pramiti/pramāṇaphala** (result) | distinct resultant knowledge | not ultimately separate from pramāṇa |

## 8.8 Example ✅/⚠️

- ⚠️ Seeing a flash of red and then judging "that is a ripe apple" are, for the Buddhist epistemologist, two
  different phases: the bare, non-conceptual, momentary perception of the unique colour-particular
  (svalakṣaṇa) is pratyakṣa; the conceptual, language-ready judgment "apple," applicable to many particulars
  across time, is the work of anumāna/apoha operating on the sāmānyalakṣaṇa constructed from that and prior
  experiences. **Limit of the analogy:** ordinary language makes it feel like one seamless act of "seeing an
  apple," but the Buddhist analysis insists these are two distinct cognitive functions with two distinct
  kinds of object — collapsing them is exactly the error apoha theory is designed to prevent.

## 8.9 Comparative treatment: Buddhist pramāṇa school against Nyāya-Mīmāṃsā (previewed, completed in Lesson 9) ✅

| School | Pramāṇas | Universals | Testimony |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddhist (Dignāga-Dharmakīrti) | two: perception, inference | apoha; no real jāti | reduced to inference |
| Nyāya | usually four in classical Nyāya | real universals | independent pramāṇa; Veda accepted |
| Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila) | expands pramāṇas; several including arthāpatti and anupalabdhi | real universals | Veda intrinsically authoritative |

## 8.10 Criticism and reply ✅

**Objection (Kumārila Bhaṭṭa, previewed here, fully developed in Lesson 9).** ✅ Mīmāṃsā defends a
persisting self as the subject of cognition, duty, memory and Vedic action, and resists apoha by arguing
that general words and recognition require positive universals, not merely exclusion.

**Reply (Dignāga/Dharmakīrti).** ✅ The self is reduced to a constructed unity imposed on a stream of
momentary cognitions (Lesson 3, §3.9); apoha explains generality as exclusion grounded in causal
convergence, so a concept is formed without a real universal. ⚠️ Residual force remains: critics argue that
the exclusion must already know what to exclude, making apoha circular — this is assessed fully, alongside
all other named pūrvapakṣa-siddhānta pairs, in Lesson 9.

## 9.1 Simple intuition

Every philosophical system looks strongest when explained from the inside. The real test of Buddhism's
coherence is what happens when its strongest rivals — Nyāya's robust realism about the self and substance,
Mīmāṃsā's defence of an enduring ritual agent and real universals, Advaita's positive, self-luminous
Brahman, and ordinary realist common sense — are allowed to press their hardest objections, one at a time,
and Buddhism is required to answer without quietly smuggling back exactly what it claims to deny (a
permanent self, a real universal, an unconditioned substance). This lesson does that work systematically,
named objector by named objector, and honestly reports where the objection still retains force even after
the best available Buddhist reply.

## 9.2 Nyāya criticism: memory, recognition and moral desert require a permanent self ✅

### Objection — Nyāya (Vātsyāyana / Uddyotakara / Udayana line)

- ✅ **Pratyabhijñā** (recognition) — "this is that same jar" or "I am the same person who saw before" —
  appears to disclose identity through time.
- ✅ Memory seems to require that the subject who remembers be identical with the subject who experienced.
- ✅ Moral desert seems to require an enduring agent: the doer of an act must be the enjoyer or sufferer of
  its fruit.

### Reply — Buddhist schools

- ✅ **Santāna/santati** (causal series, Lesson 3, §3.5) explains continuity without an identical substance.
- ✅ **Sādṛśya** (similarity) explains why recognition mistakes a later similar causal phase for the
  numerically same object.
- ✅ The flame and river examples show continuity without strict identity: a later flame or river-stage is
  neither wholly the same nor wholly other.
- ✅ Yogācāra adds **ālaya-vijñāna** (store-consciousness, Lesson 6, §6.4) and **bīja** (seeds) as the
  carrier of karmic and mnemonic potential without accepting an ātman.
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** Nyāya's rejoinder is that a series may explain causal succession, but it is less
  clear how a series can "own" memories unless some subject owns the series — exactly the pressure point
  named without evasion in the 2026 PYQ (Lesson 3, §3.6).

## 9.3 Nyāya realist criticism: a permanent substance can act via auxiliary conditions ✅

### Objection

- ✅ Nyāya asks: why can a permanent substance not act at one time because relevant auxiliary conditions
  (**sahakārin**) are then present?

### Reply — Buddhist logicians

- ✅ The Buddhist reply is that if the permanent thing remains wholly unchanged, then the decisive factor
  lies elsewhere; its own efficacy is redundant (the **sahakāri-anapekṣā** dilemma, fully derived in Lesson
  3, §3.3).
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** Nyāya's substance-quality model remains intuitively powerful and continues to
  attract those who find "changing auxiliary conditions alone did all the causal work" counter-intuitive
  for ordinary macroscopic objects.

## 9.4 Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Mīmāṃsā criticism: pramāṇa supports self and universals ✅

### Objection — Kumārila Bhaṭṭa

- ✅ Mīmāṃsā defends a persisting self as the subject of cognition, duty, memory and Vedic action.
- ✅ Kumārila also resists **apoha** by arguing that general words and recognition require positive
  universals, not merely exclusion.
- ✅ A merely negative meaning such as "not-non-cow" seems unable to explain the positive cognition "cow."

### Reply — Buddhist pramāṇa school

- ✅ Dignāga and Dharmakīrti reduce the self to a constructed unity imposed on a stream of momentary
  cognitions (Lesson 8, §8.7).
- ✅ Apoha explains generality as exclusion grounded in causal convergence: many distinct particulars
  produce the same practical judgment and action, so a concept is formed without a real universal (Lesson
  8, §8.5).
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** the circularity objection — that exclusion must already know what to exclude —
  persists, and many critics hold that the Buddhist reply does not fully explain positive determination.

## 9.5 Śaṃkara/Advaita criticism: momentariness and emptiness make liberation unintelligible ✅

### Objection — Śaṃkara-type Advaita

- ✅ If everything is momentary, bondage and liberation cannot belong to one continuing subject.
- ✅ If there is no self, the unity of the knower is unintelligible.
- ✅ If **śūnyatā** is ultimate, the very denial seems to presuppose a self-luminous witnessing consciousness
  that makes denial possible.

### Reply — Buddhist two-truths response

- ✅ Buddhism denies that liberation requires an eternal witness; liberation is the cessation of ignorance,
  craving and appropriation in a causal stream (Lesson 4, §4.8–§4.9).
- ✅ At **saṃvṛti-satya** (conventional truth), persons, practice and compassion are valid designations.
- ✅ At **paramārtha-satya** (ultimate truth), neither person nor witness has intrinsic nature (Lesson 7,
  §7.6).
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** the Advaitin's strongest pressure point is that Buddhist analysis must explain the
  unity of experience — how a stream of discrete moments feels, from the inside, like *one* continuous
  experiencer — without quietly importing a witness. This is the single sharpest cross-school criticism of
  Buddhism in the entire syllabus, and a top-scoring answer states it in exactly these terms.

## 9.6 Nyāya/realist criticism: emptiness collapses into nihilism ✅

### Objection — Nyāya and common realist charge

- ✅ If all dharmas are empty, nothing seems to remain for knowledge, ethics or liberation.
- ✅ If the Mādhyamika denies all theses, the denial appears self-refuting.

### Reply — Madhyamaka

- ✅ Emptiness means emptiness of own-being, not absence of dependent functioning (Lesson 7, §7.9).
- ✅ The two truths preserve conventional causation, karma and path-practice while denying intrinsic nature.
- ✅ Nāgārjuna's "empty words" strategy treats philosophical utterance as a therapeutic instrument rather
  than a final ontology (Lesson 7, §7.8).
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** the debate over whether non-implicative negation can avoid becoming a thesis
  persists internally (Prāsaṅgika vs Svātantrika, Lesson 7, §7.8) and externally.

## 9.7 Criticism: karma without self is unjust ✅

### Objection — Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta

- ✅ If doer and enjoyer are not strictly identical, moral desert appears to disappear.
- ✅ If they are strictly different, punishment or reward would fall on another — this is precisely the
  **akṛtābhyāgama** ("arrival of the fruit of the undone") difficulty already named in the 2026 PYQ (Lesson
  3, §3.6).

### Reply — Buddhist middle position

- ✅ Buddhism rejects both strict identity and strict difference.
- ✅ The stream that receives the fruit is causally continuous with the stream-stage that acted.
- ✅ This avoids eternalism while preserving moral responsibility at the conventional level.
- ⚠️ **Residual force:** the unresolved pressure is whether causal continuity alone is enough for desert,
  rather than merely for causal explanation — this is the same residual gap already flagged honestly in the
  2026 PYQ's model answer (Lesson 3, §3.6).

## 9.8 Buddhism vs Jainism — high-yield distinction (2024 cross-school context) ✅

- ✅ Both are non-Vedic śramaṇa traditions and both reject a creator God as necessary for liberation.
- ✅ Jainism affirms **anekāntavāda** (many-sidedness): a substance can be permanent in substance and
  changing in modes.
- ✅ Buddhism denies substance: what looks like persistence is a conditioned series without an underlying
  **dravya**.
- ✅ The Jaina **jīva** is real and omniscient by nature when freed from karmic obstruction; the Buddhist
  self is a construct designated on skandhas (Lesson 4, §4.3).
- ⚠️ **Trap to avoid:** do not say both deny the soul. Buddhism denies ātman; Jainism strongly affirms jīva.

## 9.9 Buddhism vs Advaita — avoiding the "crypto-Advaita" trap ✅

- ✅ Both deny that ordinary world-experience is ultimately real in the naive realist sense.
- ✅ Advaita's ultimate is positive, self-luminous **Brahman/ātman**.
- ✅ Madhyamaka **śūnyatā** is absence of **svabhāva**, not a hidden substrate.
- ✅ Advaita sublates the world into Brahman; Madhyamaka refuses to replace empty phenomena with a
  metaphysical ground.
- ⚠️ **Trap to refute:** "Buddhist emptiness is just Advaita Brahman under another name." The Buddhist case
  is anti-substantialist; Advaita is absolute-substantialist. This is the precise reply owed whenever
  Yogācāra idealism (Lesson 6) or Mādhyamika emptiness (Lesson 7) is casually equated with Advaita
  non-dualism.

## 9.10 Cārvāka's rejection of self vs Buddhist rejection of ātman — 2024 PYQ ✅

- ✅ **Cārvāka** denies self beyond body and tends toward materialist reduction.
- ✅ **Buddhism** denies permanent self but does not reduce personhood simply to gross body; it analyses the
  person into skandhas (Lesson 4, §4.3) and preserves karmic continuity without substance.
- ⚠️ Therefore both reject an eternal ātman, but for very different reasons and with very different
  moral-metaphysical consequences: Cārvāka's denial destroys post-mortem moral continuity altogether, while
  Buddhism's denial is compatible with — indeed structures — karmic continuity via santāna.

## 9.11 Internal Buddhist pūrvapakṣa chain — complete (assembling Lessons 5–7) ✅

| Stage | Siddhānta claimed | Becomes pūrvapakṣa for | Next Buddhist reply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vaibhāṣika/Sarvāstivāda | Dharmas are real and, in the Sarvāstivāda sense, have existence across past, present and future. | Sautrāntika objects that this weakens momentariness and overpopulates ontology. | Only the present moment is fully operative; external objects are inferred through representations. |
| Sautrāntika | External objects exist but are known inferentially, not directly. | Yogācāra objects that an inferred external object is unnecessary because all that is given is cognition. | Vijñapti-mātra: objecthood is inseparable from consciousness-appearance. |
| Yogācāra | Consciousness-only explains appearance, karma and continuity through ālaya-vijñāna. | Madhyamaka objects that consciousness is being reified as a subtle substance. | Even consciousness lacks svabhāva; all dharmas are empty. |
| Madhyamaka | Śūnyatā prevents every reification, including reification of emptiness. | Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā and Advaita object that this risks nihilism or self-refutation. | Two truths preserve conventional practice while ultimate analysis denies intrinsic nature. |

an argumentative escalation — realism of dharmas → presentist representationalism → idealist
consciousness-only → anti-essentialist emptiness — not a mechanical list.

## 9.12 Optional cross-paper enrichment: Buddhist arguments against a creator God ✅

> ⚠️ This is a cross-paper theme, most useful when linked with Paper II's Proofs for God and Religion
> without God topics; it is included here as complete optional enrichment because the syllabus clause for
> Buddhism does not separately print it, but examiners have tested Buddhism's non-theism as part of broader
> comparative or philosophy-of-religion questions.

**Core lines of criticism.** ✅
1. If God is eternal and unchanging, genuine creation becomes difficult to explain, because willing or
   producing seems to involve change.
2. If God creates from desire or purpose, that suggests lack, preference or incompleteness.
3. The presence of suffering and evil sits uneasily with an omnipotent benevolent creator.
4. Dependent origination (Lesson 2) already explains the arising of phenomena through conditions, making a
   creator explanatorily unnecessary.

**Philosophical force.** ⚠️ Buddhist critique is not mere atheistic denial; it is a systematic refusal of
explanatory surplus. If conditioned arising explains the world-process, God is not needed as a further,
additional explanatory posit.

## 9.13 Undeclared questions (avyākata/avyākṛta) — pragmatic silence ✅

### The undeclared questions

- ✅ **Avyākata/avyākṛta** (undeclared/unanswered) questions are questions the Buddha declines to answer in
  the expected metaphysical format.
- ✅ They include whether the world is eternal or not eternal, finite or infinite; whether the **jīva**
  (life-principle/self) is the same as or different from the body; and whether the **Tathāgata** exists
  after death, does not exist, both, or neither.

```text
AVYĀKṚTA CLUSTER
World: eternal? not eternal? finite? infinite?
Self/body: jīva same as body? different from body?
Tathāgata after death: exists? does not exist? both? neither?
```

### Why the Buddha declined

**Statement.** ✅ The refusal is not a lack of doctrine; it is a disciplined refusal to answer malformed or
soteriologically useless questions.

**Argument.** ✅
1. The questions are **na arthasaṃhita** (not conducive to the goal/not connected with welfare and
   liberation).
2. The arrow parable in the **Cūḷamālukya** teaching compares the metaphysical questioner to a man shot with
   a poisoned arrow who refuses treatment until he learns the archer's caste, name, bow-type and
   arrow-fletching; he dies before treatment.
3. The questions rest on a false presupposition: they assume a substantial self or substantial world about
   which the alternatives can be predicated.
4. ⚠️ The structure resembles the loaded question "Have you stopped beating your wife?"; any direct answer
   concedes the presupposition.
5. Conditioned cognition cannot settle what is framed beyond the conditions of meaningful experience and
   liberation-oriented practice.

### Fire-going-out simile

- ✅ For the Tathāgata-after-death question, early Buddhist teaching uses the fire-going-out simile: when a
  fire has gone out, it cannot be said to have gone east, west, north or south.
- ⚠️ The point is not that a hidden self travels elsewhere; the categories of directional predication no
  longer apply once the sustaining conditions have ceased.

### Three readings of the silence

| Reading | Claim | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| ❓ Agnostic | The Buddha did not know, or the matter is unknowable. | Explains non-answering simply. | Fits poorly with the strong positive diagnosis of suffering and cessation. |
| ✅ Pragmatic-therapeutic | The questions are useless for liberation. | Best fits the arrow parable's stated reason. | May understate the logical problem in the questions. |
| ⚠️ Logical-semantic | The questions are malformed because their presupposition of substantial self/world fails. | Best fits anātman and dependent origination. | Not always explicitly formulated as a formal semantic theory in early sources. |

- ❓ The tradition does not settle the issue in a single uncontested interpretation.
- ⚠️ For UPSC, combine the second and third readings: the questions are both therapeutically useless and
  presuppositionally defective.

### Objection and reply

**Objection — realist/metaphysical critic.** ✅ Silence looks like evasion and leaves Buddhism
metaphysically indeterminate.

**Reply — Buddhist middle way.** ✅ The Buddha's positive teaching is precisely what he declared: Four Noble
Truths, dependent origination, path and cessation (Lessons 1–2). The middle way avoids both śāśvatavāda
(eternalism) and ucchedavāda (annihilationism). Answering the avyākṛta questions in any of the four corners
would concede the grammar of one of those extremes. ⚠️ Therefore silence is not emptiness of teaching; it is
refusal to let the wrong question control the answer — the exact scope-correct treatment this internal
subtopic requires, distinguishing it from a general claim that Buddhism "has no answer to metaphysics."

## 10.3 Graded verdict on Buddhism ✅/⚠️/❓

### 10.3.1 What is strong

- ✅ Buddhism gives a powerful process-based account of suffering, causation and liberation through
  pratītyasamutpāda (Lesson 2).
- ✅ It avoids both eternalism and annihilationism by using causal continuity without an eternal self
  (Lessons 1, 3, 4).
- ✅ Kṣaṇikavāda is supported by a sharp causal-efficacy argument, especially in Dharmakīrti's pramāṇa
  framework (Lessons 3, 8).
- ✅ Mādhyamika's two truths allow it to deny intrinsic nature without abolishing ordinary practice
  (Lesson 7).

### 10.3.2 What is weak

- ⚠️ The denial of a persisting subject makes memory, recognition and moral desert philosophically difficult
  (Lessons 3, 9).
- ⚠️ Apoha explains universals economically but struggles to account for positive determinate cognition
  (Lesson 8).
- ⚠️ Catuṣkoṭi and "no thesis" replies can look self-referentially unstable unless the non-implicative-
  negation nuance is clearly stated (Lesson 7).

### 10.3.3 What is genuinely contested

- ❓ Whether **svasaṃvedana** proves self-awareness or merely reifies cognition is debated between Buddhist
  epistemologists and Nyāya (Lesson 8).
- ❓ Whether Madhyamaka's empty statements avoid self-refutation is debated both internally (Prāsaṅgika vs
  Svātantrika) and by opponents (Lesson 7, Lesson 9).
- ❓ Whether a causal series is sufficient for moral responsibility without an owner remains the core
  cross-school dispute — this is exactly the tension the 2026 PYQ asks candidates to adjudicate (Lesson 3,
  §3.6).

### 10.3.4 Ready one-line verdicts

- ⚠️ **10 marks:** Buddhism replaces substance with conditioned process: this makes suffering removable, but
  raises the problem of continuity without self.
- ⚠️ **15 marks:** Its doctrines of dependent origination, momentariness and no-self form a coherent
  anti-substantialist system, though Nyāya and Vedānta press hard on memory, agency and liberation.
- ⚠️ **20 marks:** Buddhism is strongest as a therapeutic and causal philosophy of liberation; its deepest
  unresolved issue is whether causal continuity and two truths can fully replace the metaphysical work done
  by self, substance and universals in rival systems.

### Solved PYQ 1 — 2018 Q5(b), 10 marks

**Question:** How do the Mādhyamika Buddhists apply the notion of Pratītyasamutpāda to establish their
doctrine of Śūnyatā? Discuss.

**Model solution.** For Mādhyamika, **pratītyasamutpāda** means that every phenomenon arises only through
causes, conditions, conceptual designation and relations (Lesson 2). A thing possessing **svabhāva**
(self-existent own-nature) would be independent, unproduced and incapable of alteration. Dependently
originated things cannot satisfy that condition (Lesson 7, §7.3).

```text
dependent arising → absence of independent own-nature → emptiness of svabhāva
                                                       → conventional, relational existence
```

Therefore Nāgārjuna treats dependent origination and **śūnyatā** as two descriptions of the same fact.
Emptiness does not mean non-existence. A chariot, person or flame works conventionally, but none exists as
a self-grounded substance apart from components and conditions (Lesson 4, §4.4; Lesson 2, §2.7). The
doctrine is protected from nihilism by the **two truths**: at *saṃvṛti-satya*, causation, language, karma
and compassion operate; at *paramārtha-satya*, no entity survives analysis as intrinsically existent.
Śūnyatā is itself empty and must not be reified into an Absolute (Lesson 7, §7.9). **Verdict:** Mādhyamika
moves from causal dependence to ontological anti-essentialism, not from dependence to sheer nothingness.
*(Why this earns marks: it gives the exact derivation, defines svabhāva, uses the two truths, and answers
the standard nihilism objection within a compact 10-mark structure — see §10.5 below for the template.)*

### Solved PYQ 2 — 2018 Q6(b), 15 marks

**Question:** Is it consistent for the Buddhists to admit the theory of Nairātmyavāda and the doctrine of
Nirvāṇa simultaneously? Give reasons in favour of your answer.

**Model solution.** **Nairātmyavāda** denies a permanent, unchanging owner behind the five skandhas
(Lesson 4, §4.2–§4.3). **Nirvāṇa** is the cessation of ignorance, craving and the causal process that
reproduces suffering (Lesson 4, §4.8). The two are consistent if liberation is understood as transformation
and cessation within a causal stream, not as the release of an eternal soul.

```text
self-grasping → craving → clinging → becoming → suffering
insight into no-self → non-grasping → cessation → nirvāṇa
```

An opponent may argue that without a self there is nobody who suffers, practises or becomes liberated.
Buddhism replies through **santāna**, causal continuity (Lesson 3, §3.5): the later liberated stream is
neither numerically identical with nor wholly unrelated to the earlier bound stream. Conventional agency
remains adequate for discipline and responsibility. Theravāda accounts commonly stress cessation of greed,
hatred and delusion; Mahāyāna adds that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two self-existent realms, because both
are empty of svabhāva (Lesson 4, §4.9). Neither account requires a substantial ātman. **Verdict:** the
denial of a permanent self is not an obstacle to nirvāṇa; it is the cognitive condition for ending the
grasping that sustains bondage. The residual pressure concerns whether an ownerless stream fully explains
first-person liberation (Lesson 9, §9.5). *(Why this earns marks: it distinguishes self from causal
continuity, connects no-self to suffering, adds school nuance, and gives a balanced critical verdict.)*

### Solved PYQ 3 — 2019 Q5(b), 10 marks

**Question:** How do the Yogācāra Buddhists deny the existence of the external world? Discuss.

**Model solution.** Yogācāra advances **vijñaptimātratā**: what is given in experience is cognition or
representation, not an independently established external object (Lesson 6, §6.2). Dream experience shows
that a structured subject-object world can appear without corresponding external things (Lesson 6, §6.3).
The object in waking cognition is likewise inseparable from the form in which consciousness presents it.
Yogācāra also argues that a material object cannot coherently be identified as one indivisible atom, an
aggregate of atoms, or a whole distinct from its parts (the atom/whole critique). Further, a momentary
external object would have ceased before the cognition allegedly caused by it became available.

The positive account uses **ālaya-vijñāna**, the store-consciousness carrying karmic seeds. Seeds mature
into coordinated experiences, explaining apparent stability and intersubjective regularity (Lesson 6,
§6.4, §6.8).

| Nature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Parikalpita | imagined subject-object duality |
| Paratantra | dependently arisen flow of cognition |
| Pariniṣpanna | perfected realisation of the absence of duality |

**Verdict:** Yogācāra denies external objects as independently established causes of experience, but not
the ordered causal flow of consciousness. Its weakness is explaining shared experience without covertly
rebuilding an external world (Lesson 6, §6.8). *(Why this earns marks: it combines the dream and atom
arguments with ālaya-vijñāna and tri-svabhāva rather than merely asserting "mind-only.")*

### Solved PYQ 4 — 2019 Q7(c), 15 marks

**Question:** What is Pratītyasamutpāda? How do the Buddhists apply this concept to prove that everything
is momentary?

**Model solution.** Pratītyasamutpāda is the law that conditioned phenomena arise when their conditions
arise and cease when those conditions cease (Lesson 2, §2.2). Its existential expression is the twelve-link
chain from avidyā to jarā-maraṇa (Lesson 2, §2.3); its general philosophical form is conditional
dependence.

Momentariness follows because a conditioned thing cannot remain unchanged while its conditioning complex
changes. Buddhist logicians reinforce this through **arthakriyākāritva**, causal efficacy (Lesson 3, §3.3):

1. The real is what performs a causal function.
2. A wholly permanent thing would possess the same causal capacity at every time.
3. It should therefore produce its effect always or never.
4. If it acts only with auxiliaries, the changed causal situation, not an unchanged substance, explains the
   effect.
5. Real causal operation belongs to moment-specific events.
6. Hence, *yat sat tat kṣaṇikam* — whatever is real is momentary.

The apparent endurance of a flame, person or seed is explained as **santāna**, a succession of causally
connected but numerically distinct moments (Lesson 3, §3.5). Nyāya objects that enduring substances act
when helper-conditions arrive and that memory and recognition require identity (Lesson 9, §9.2–§9.3).
Buddhism replies that each causal phase inherits capacities from its predecessor and recognition tracks
similarity within a stream. Yet ownership of memory remains a serious pressure point. **Verdict:** dependent
origination supplies impermanence; causal efficacy turns it into the stricter doctrine of momentariness.
*(Why this earns marks: it separates the early causal doctrine from the later logical proof, handles the
auxiliary-condition objection, and concludes critically.)*

### Solved PYQ 5 — 2020 Q6(a), 20 marks

**Question:** How is Kṣaṇikavāda a logical derivative of Pratītyasamutpāda in Buddhism? Explain.

**Model solution.** Pratītyasamutpāda states that no conditioned event is self-grounded: "this being, that
arises; with the cessation of this, that ceases" (Lesson 2, §2.2). Kṣaṇikavāda radicalises this
impermanence into the claim that every causally real entity exists only for a moment (Lesson 3, §3.2).

The derivation has three layers. First, an independently permanent substance would not need conditions.
Since phenomena arise and cease with conditions, they lack unchanging substantial existence. Second, a
cause that produces an effect has exercised a determinate capacity; the causal phase after production
cannot be strictly identical to the phase before it. Third, the arthakriyā dilemma states:

```text
If permanent X causes E:
├─ simultaneously → X should produce all effects at once
└─ successively   → X has different powers at different times
                    and is therefore not wholly unchanged
```

Nyāya invokes **sahakārin**, auxiliary conditions. The Buddhist asks whether X already has the relevant
power. If yes, delay is unexplained; if no, the new causal complex bears the explanatory work (Lesson 3,
§3.3; Lesson 9, §9.3). Reality thus belongs to momentary causal particulars.

Continuity is preserved as **santāna**, a causal succession. This supports memory, karma and rebirth
without a permanent bearer, although critics argue that resemblance and causation do not fully explain
ownership. Mādhyamika adds that momentary dharmas must not themselves be reified: if they arise dependently,
they too are empty of svabhāva (Lesson 7). **Verdict:** kṣaṇikavāda is a logical derivative when
pratītyasamutpāda is joined to causal efficacy — conditionality establishes impermanence; arthakriyā
establishes moment-specific reality. *(Why this earns marks: it provides a layered derivation, formal
dilemma, Nyāya objection and reply, santāna, and Mādhyamika refinement appropriate to a 20-marker.)*

### Solved PYQ 6 — 2020 Q7(b), 15 marks

**Question:** How does Buddhism explain Self in terms of Pañcaskandhas? What is Liberation for Buddhism if
there is no Soul?

**Model solution.** Buddhism analyses the person into five aggregates: **rūpa** (form), **vedanā**
(feeling), **saṃjñā** (recognition), **saṃskāra** (formations) and **vijñāna** (consciousness) (Lesson 4,
§4.3). None is permanent, fully controllable or an adequate self; nor is a self found outside or as owner
of them.

The *Milinda-pañha* chariot analogy clarifies the person's status (Lesson 4, §4.4). "Chariot" designates an
organised set of parts; likewise "person" is a conventional designation for an aggregate-stream. This
avoids both eternalism and annihilationism: no immutable soul exists, yet a causally continuous process
remains.

Liberation is **nirvāṇa**, the cessation of avidyā, craving, clinging and production of suffering (Lesson
4, §4.8). The Eightfold Path (Lesson 1, §1.4) transforms the stream through morality, concentration and
wisdom. The person who begins and the liberated person are causally continuous without being one enduring
substance. An objection says practice is pointless if no one receives its result; the reply is that moral
succession requires causal continuity, not numerical identity (Lesson 4, §4.7). **Verdict:** pañcaskandha
analysis removes the imagined owner; liberation removes the causes of bondage within the conventional
continuum. *(Why this earns marks: it names all five skandhas, uses the chariot, defines liberation
positively, and answers the agency objection.)*

### Solved PYQ 7 — 2021 Q5(d), 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish between pudgala-nairātmyavāda and dharma-nairātmyavāda with reference to
Buddhist Philosophy.

**Model solution.**

| Doctrine | What it denies | Primary scope | Typical association |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pudgala-nairātmya | permanent personal self | person composed of five skandhas | shared broadly across Buddhism |
| Dharma-nairātmya | intrinsic self-nature of dharmas | elements constituting person and world | developed emphatically in Mahāyāna |

**Pudgala-nairātmya** says no owner exists apart from the aggregates. The person is a conventional
designation upon a causal stream. Some Abhidharma realists (Lesson 5) nevertheless treat the constituent
dharmas as ultimately real. **Dharma-nairātmya** extends the critique: even dharmas do not possess
independent svabhāva. Mādhyamika grounds this in dependent origination (Lesson 7, §7.3); Yogācāra connects
it with absence of subject-object duality (Lesson 6). The second doctrine prevents analysis of the person
from turning dharmas into metaphysical atoms. Together they remove selfhood at personal and elemental
levels. *(Why this earns marks: it gives the exact object of denial, identifies the school-level
development, and explains why the second doctrine is necessary — see Lesson 4, §4.5 for the full teaching.)*

### Solved PYQ 8 — 2021 Q7(b), 15 marks

**Question:** If everything is momentary then how do the Buddhists explain the problem of memory and
personal identity? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** Momentariness denies an enduring mental subject, apparently making memory impossible:
the experiencer has perished before remembering occurs (Lesson 3, §3.6). Buddhism responds with **santāna**,
a causal continuum. An experience leaves a disposition in the next moment; transmission produces a later
memory. Sautrāntika speaks of causal seeds or traces; Yogācāra uses **ālaya-vijñāna**, whose bīja preserve
karmic and mnemonic potential (Lesson 6, §6.4). Personal identity is conventional continuity based on
causal connection, bodily organisation, resemblance and inherited dispositions:

```text
experience → impression → transmitted seed → occasioning condition → memory
```

The later rememberer is neither strictly the same as nor wholly other than the earlier experiencer. Strict
identity would reintroduce an ātman; total difference would destroy responsibility. Nyāya objects that
memory has the form "I experienced this," requiring one subject to connect the cognitions (Lesson 9, §9.2).
Similarity may explain resemblance but not ownership. Buddhism replies that ownership is itself constructed
within a causally integrated stream. **Verdict:** Buddhist models explain informational transmission
economically, but remain vulnerable if first-person appropriation is treated as irreducible. *(Why this
earns marks: it addresses both memory and identity, names school-specific mechanisms, and gives the
strongest Nyāya objection and reply.)*

### Solved PYQ 9 — 2022 Q6(a), 20 marks

**Question:** How compatible is Buddhist theory of momentariness with their theory of Karma? In this
regard how do Buddhists respond to objections raised by their opponents? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** Karma appears to require a persisting agent who acts and later receives the fruit.
Kṣaṇikavāda denies such an enduring subject. Buddhism reconciles them by replacing substance-identity with
causal continuity (Lesson 3, §3.6):

```text
intention → karmic impression → causal transmission → maturation → fruit
```

Sautrāntika uses seed-like causal potency; Yogācāra locates bīja in ālaya-vijñāna; Abhidharma traditions
employ causal relations within the dharma-series. All reject an unchanging owner. Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā raise
three objections (Lesson 9, §9.7):

1. **Kṛtanāśa:** the doer's destruction seems to make the deed lost.
2. **Akṛtābhyāgama:** a later different moment seems to receive an unperformed act's fruit.
3. Praise, blame and recognition seem to require one subject.

Buddhism replies that these objections assume only absolute identity or absolute difference. A causally
continuous stream is sufficiently connected for responsibility. A child and adult differ profoundly, yet
ordinary morality treats them as one practical continuum. The pressure remains: causal inheritance explains
why B follows A, but does it explain why the fruit is *mine*? Ālaya-vijñāna improves continuity but may
resemble a quasi-self. Mādhyamika avoids reifying even the stream and treats karma as conventionally valid
(Lesson 7). **Verdict:** karma and momentariness are compatible if moral responsibility needs causal and
conventional identity rather than a numerically identical substance. *(Why this earns marks: it states the
compatibility mechanism, names classical objections, differentiates schools, and reaches a genuinely
critical verdict. Compare directly with the 2026 PYQ, §10.4's final entry and Lesson 3, §3.6, which asks
the same underlying question from the opposite direction — "problematise" rather than "assess
compatibility.")*

### Solved PYQ 10 — 2022 Q7(b), 15 marks

**Question:** Explain Buddhist concept of Triratna and their internal relation. Critically examine the
consistency of Triratnas with the Buddhist concept of No-soul (Nairātmyavāda).

**Model solution.** The **Triratna** are Buddha, Dharma and Saṅgha (Lesson 4, §4.7). Buddha is the awakened
teacher and exemplar; Dharma is truth and path; Saṅgha is the community preserving and embodying the path.

```text
Buddha discovers and teaches Dharma
Dharma makes awakening intelligible
Saṅgha preserves and practises Dharma
Saṅgha produces further exemplars
```

No-soul does not contradict refuge in Buddha because Buddha is not an eternal ātman or creator. Nor does it
contradict Saṅgha, a conventional community of causal persons. Dharma explicitly includes insight into
anātman. The objection is that refuge and discipline require stable agents and authorities. Buddhism answers
through two levels: conventionally, teachers and practitioners exist; ultimately, none possesses independent
selfhood. **Verdict:** Triratna and no-self are consistent when refuge is practical orientation rather than
submission of one immortal soul to another. *(Why this earns marks: it defines each jewel, shows their
internal relation, and resolves the issue through conventional and ultimate levels.)*

### Solved PYQ 11 — 2023 Q8(a), 20 marks

**Question:** "Ignorance of dependent origination is suffering while its knowledge is cessation of
suffering." Present an account of Buddhist soteriology in the light of above statement.

**Model solution.** Buddhist soteriology treats suffering as a dependently sustained process (Lesson 2,
§2.11). **Avidyā** misconstrues impermanent, conditioned and selfless phenomena as permanent, satisfactory
and owned by a self. This error generates craving and clinging.

```text
avidyā → saṃskāra → vijñāna → nāma-rūpa → ṣaḍāyatana
→ sparśa → vedanā → tṛṣṇā → upādāna → bhava → jāti
→ jarā-maraṇa
```

Knowledge reverses the chain's practical force. Feeling need not become craving; craving need not become
clinging. Insight into impermanence and no-self removes appropriation, while śīla and samādhi weaken habits
reproducing ignorance.

| Division | Path factors | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Prajñā | right view, right resolve | sees dependence and no-self |
| Śīla | speech, action, livelihood | stops fresh harmful conditioning |
| Samādhi | effort, mindfulness, concentration | transforms attention and craving |

Abhidharma analyses the causal stream into dharmas (Lesson 5). Yogācāra purifies seeds in consciousness
(Lesson 6). Mādhyamika sees dependent phenomena as empty, preventing attachment even to doctrine and
nirvāṇa (Lesson 7). Mere verbal knowledge cannot liberate; wisdom must become non-grasping perception
supported by conduct and meditation. **Verdict:** ignorance is suffering because it constructs appropriation
in the causal chain. Knowledge is cessation when embodied as a path that removes renewed craving's
conditions. *(Why this earns marks: it integrates the quotation, twelve links, Eightfold Path, school
nuance, and the distinction between intellectual and transformative knowledge.)*

### Solved PYQ 12 — 2024 Q6(b), 15 marks

**Question:** How do the two schools of Buddhism arrive at two opposed conclusions, namely "everything is
void" and "everything is real" from the same doctrine of Pratītyasamutpāda? Answer with arguments.

**Model solution.** The opposition arises because schools ask different questions of dependent origination
(Lesson 2, §2.10; Lesson 5, §5.4; Lesson 7, §7.3).

**Mādhyamika:** if a thing arises dependently, it lacks svabhāva. What lacks self-nature is **śūnya**.
"Everything is void" means no dharma is intrinsically existent; conventional causation remains valid.

**Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika:** causal interaction shows conditioned dharmas are real. Vaibhāṣika holds
external dharmas directly perceived; Sautrāntika infers external momentary particulars from
representations. "Everything is real" means causally efficacious dharmas are not illusions, though
impermanent.

| Shared premise | Realist inference | Mādhyamika inference |
|---|---|---|
| things arise through conditions | causal efficacy establishes conditioned reality | dependence disproves intrinsic nature |
| no permanent substance | momentary dharmas are real | even momentary dharmas are empty |
| ordinary practice works | realism explains causal order | convention explains causal order |

The conclusions conflict only if "real" and "void" are univocal. Realists affirm causal reality; Mādhyamika
denies ultimate self-existence. **Verdict:** one doctrine supports realism about causal functioning and
emptiness about self-nature. The real dispute is whether momentary dharmas remain ultimately real. *(Why
this earns marks: it identifies the schools, distinguishes two senses of reality, and dissolves superficial
contradiction without erasing genuine disagreement.)*

### Solved PYQ 13 — 2025 Q8(c), 15 marks

**Question:** Is Buddhist notion of Nirvāṇa in consonance with their conception of Kṣaṇikavāda
(momentariness) and Nairātmyavāda (no-soul theory)? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** Nirvāṇa is consonant with momentariness and no-self if understood as cessation of
suffering's causes, not an eternal state possessed by a self (Lesson 4, §4.9).

```text
conditioned arising of suffering
          ↓ insight and path
cessation of its causes
          ↓
nirvāṇa
```

An opponent argues that if nirvāṇa is real and permanent, it contradicts momentariness; if momentary, it
cannot be final; and without a self, nobody attains it. Buddhism replies that kṣaṇikavāda applies to
conditioned entities. Nirvāṇa is **asaṃskṛta** (unconditioned), or more cautiously the cessation of the
conditioned process. No subject-substance is required for cessation. Mādhyamika refuses to reify nirvāṇa:
saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are empty of svabhāva; their distinction concerns ignorance and insight, not two
substances (Lesson 7). **Verdict:** the doctrines are consonant within process ontology. Tension appears
only if nirvāṇa is interpreted as an eternal positive entity or personal attainment is assumed to require a
substantial self. *(Why this earns marks: it answers both compatibility conditions, distinguishes
conditioned from unconditioned, adds Mādhyamika nuance, and identifies the exact source of possible
inconsistency.)*

### Solved PYQ 14 — 2026 Q5(b), 10 marks (newly integrated)

**Question:** How does the Buddhist theory of momentariness problematize the issue of human action and
responsibility? Critically discuss.

**Model solution.** *(This is the complete, verified answer for the newest PYQ in the syllabus clause. The
full derivation and problem-statement are taught in Lesson 3, §3.6; it is repeated here in full so that all
fourteen PYQs stand together for final revision.)* Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) holds that every real,
causally efficacious entity exists for exactly one moment (Lesson 3, §3.3). Applied to human action, this
immediately problematises responsibility: the moment that intends an act perishes before the act is
completed, and the moment that acts perishes before any consequence can be assigned. Classical difficulties
sharpen the point — *kṛtanāśa* (the deed appears lost with the perishing doer) and *akṛtābhyāgama* (an
unconnected later moment appears to inherit an unearned fruit). Buddhism resolves this not by reviving a
permanent self but by treating intention, action and fruition as causally continuous phases of one
**santāna** (causal stream): each moment transmits a disposition (in Sautrāntika, a causal seed; in
Yogācāra, a **bīja** stored in **ālaya-vijñāna**) to its successor, so the later fruit-bearing moment is
neither strictly identical with nor wholly unrelated to the earlier acting moment — the same Middle Path
structure used throughout Buddhist metaphysics (Lesson 1, §1.4). This blocks the crude charges of "lost
action" and "undeserved result." **Critical qualification:** the account explains causal transmission
convincingly but is weaker at explaining first-person *ownership* of the fruit — Nyāya's objection that
recognition and desert seem to require one persisting subject retains real force (Lesson 9, §9.2), and
Buddhism's reply (that "mine-ness" is itself a further conceptual construction within the stream) is
coherent but not universally regarded as fully decisive. **Verdict:** momentariness genuinely problematises
naive notions of agency and desert, and Buddhism's causal-continuum reply preserves moral practice at the
conventional level without fully closing the ownership gap that rival realist schools press. *(Why this
earns marks: it states the problem precisely with both named classical difficulties, presents the
resolution by name with both school-specific mechanisms, and delivers the required "critical" qualification
— the exact three-layer structure demanded by the 2026 wording and taught in full in Lesson 3, §3.6.)*

### 10.6.3 Original Mains Practice — complete model answers

**Original Mains Practice 1 (10 marks, from Lesson 1).** "Theravāda is simply another name for the
Hīnayāna school." Critically examine this statement.

*Model solution.* This statement conflates two distinct historical categories (Lesson 1, §1.2). Theravāda
is a specific, surviving historical school and living tradition, preserving the Pāli Tipiṭaka (Vinaya,
Sutta and Abhidhamma Piṭaka) and centring the arhat ideal. "Hīnayāna" ("Lesser Vehicle") is, by contrast, a
Mahāyāna-origin classificatory and often polemical label applied broadly to non-Mahāyāna or
disciple-vehicle traditions; it was never these traditions' own self-designation. Older Indian-philosophy
textbooks sometimes retain "Hīnayāna" as a broad doxographic category covering multiple non-Mahāyāna
schools (including the Sarvāstivāda-linked Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika, Lesson 1, §1.7), which is precisely
why the two terms get carelessly merged — but Theravāda is only one surviving school among several the
label was historically used to cover, not its synonym. A historically careful answer should either avoid
the label "Hīnayāna" altogether or place it in quotation marks with its polemical Mahāyāna origin explained,
exactly as this lesson does. **Verdict:** the statement is false as an identity claim; it survives only as
the weaker, historically qualified claim that Theravāda was one of the traditions an outside label once
covered. *(Why this earns marks: it defines both terms precisely, explains the origin and function of the
label, and gives the only defensible qualified verdict.)*

**Original Mains Practice 2 (10 marks, from Lesson 2).** Explain why *pratītyasamutpāda* is simultaneously
a theory of causation and a method of liberation.

*Model solution.* Pratītyasamutpāda explains causation by showing that conditioned phenomena arise and
cease with supporting conditions (Lesson 2, §2.2). It is soteriological because suffering is one such
conditioned process (Lesson 1, §1.3): avidyā generates formations, craving and clinging; removing these
conditions prevents renewed becoming. Its practical significance is that the chain is interruptible,
especially at the movement from feeling to craving (Lesson 2, §2.3–§2.4). Wisdom sees impermanence and
no-self; morality and meditation alter the affective conditions of the stream (Lesson 1, §1.4). **Verdict:**
it is not a neutral cosmology later applied to salvation. Its causal grammar reveals both why suffering
arises and how it can cease. *(Why this earns marks: it states both dimensions, identifies an intervention
point, and concludes with their internal unity.)*

**Original Mains Practice 3 (15 marks, from Lesson 5).** Compare Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra and
Mādhyamika as answers to the problem of the object of knowledge.

*Model solution.*

| School | Object position | Knowledge relation | Main pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vaibhāṣika | external dharmas are real | directly perceived | direct access to momentary objects |
| Sautrāntika | external objects are real | inferred from mental forms | copy cannot prove unknown original |
| Yogācāra | independent external object denied | cognition presents subject and object | shared experience |
| Mādhyamika | mind and object lack svabhāva | claims operate conventionally | self-refutation charge |

The movement is not a ladder in which one school simply wins (Lesson 5, §5.7; Lesson 9, §9.11). Vaibhāṣika
protects causal realism; Sautrāntika recognises mediation; Yogācāra exposes experience's mind-structured
character; Mādhyamika criticises reification of both object and consciousness. **Verdict:** the debate
shifts from *which object is known* to *whether knower or known possesses intrinsic nature*. Mādhyamika is
most radical but depends on conventional truth to preserve inquiry. *(Why this earns marks: it compares all
four on one axis, gives each strength and objection, and avoids presenting the sequence as mere
chronology.)*

**Original Mains Practice 4 (15 marks, from Lesson 8).** Explain the Buddhist theory of *pramāṇas* and
show how it is internally connected to the doctrines of momentariness and no-self.

*Model solution.* Dignāga and Dharmakīrti accept exactly two pramāṇas, perception and inference, because
there are exactly two kinds of object: the unique causal particular (svalakṣaṇa) and the constructed
general character (sāmānyalakṣaṇa) (Lesson 8, §8.2–§8.3). This is not a free-standing logical preference;
it follows directly from the ontology already established. Kṣaṇikavāda (Lesson 3) holds that only momentary
events are causally efficacious, and it is exactly this momentary causal particular that perception
non-conceptually grasps (Lesson 8, §8.4). Nairātmyavāda (Lesson 4) denies an enduring knower, and Dignāga's
thesis that pramāṇa and pramāṇaphala are non-different phases of one momentary cognitive event (Lesson 8,
§8.7) is precisely what a no-self ontology requires — there is no room for a persisting knower standing
apart from the instrument and result of knowledge. Apoha (Lesson 8, §8.5) supplies the matching
anti-realism about universals: since only momentary particulars are ultimately real, general words cannot
denote real universals and must instead work by exclusion, grounded in shared causal capacity rather than a
shared essence. **Verdict:** Buddhist epistemology is not "Nyāya logic with fewer pramāṇas"; every one of
its distinctive theses — two pramāṇas, svalakṣaṇa/sāmānyalakṣaṇa, apoha, non-difference of pramāṇa and
pramāṇaphala — is derived from, and would collapse without, momentariness and no-self. *(Why this earns
marks: it does not merely describe Buddhist logic in isolation, but explicitly derives each of its four
distinctive theses from the two ontological doctrines the question names.)*

**Original Mains Practice 5 (20 marks, from Lessons 3 and 9).** "Buddhist process ontology secures change
more successfully than it secures responsibility." Critically examine.

*Model solution.* Buddhist process ontology explains change powerfully. Pratītyasamutpāda replaces static
substance with conditional arising (Lesson 2); kṣaṇikavāda explains causal novelty through moment-specific
efficacy (Lesson 3, §3.3); nairātmyavāda removes an unchanging owner incompatible with experiential flux
(Lesson 4). Responsibility is harder. Karma requires that an intentional act and its later fruit belong
together. Buddhism answers through santāna, transmitted dispositions, pratisandhāna and, in Yogācāra,
ālaya-vijñāna with karmic seeds (Lesson 3, §3.6; Lesson 6, §6.4). The later recipient is neither identical
with nor unrelated to the agent. This blocks the crude charges of lost action (kṛtanāśa) and undeserved
result (akṛtābhyāgama) — exactly the difficulties named in the verified 2026 PYQ (Lesson 3, §3.6; §10.4
above). Ordinary responsibility already tracks psychological and causal continuity despite bodily and
mental change; Mādhyamika argues that agency operates conventionally and never required ultimate substance
(Lesson 7). Yet causal connection explains why event B follows A more readily than why B's suffering is
*my* fruit. Nyāya treats memory and appropriation as evidence of an enduring subject (Lesson 9, §9.2);
ālaya-vijñāna improves continuity but risks resembling a disguised self. The Buddhist reply is that "mine"
is itself constructed within an integrated stream. **Verdict:** Buddhism explains change better than
substance theories that leave alteration mysterious. Its account of responsibility is coherent but less
decisive because the move from causal continuity to ownership remains contestable — the exact residual
tension the 2026 examiner has now made a direct, named UPSC question. *(Why this earns marks: it evaluates
both halves, names multiple continuity devices, includes Nyāya's strongest challenge, cites the verified
2026 PYQ as live confirmation of the tension, and reaches a qualified verdict.)*

## FINAL CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES — SCHOOLS OF BUDDHISM

### Tradition Map

- Early Buddhism → Theravāda (Pāli Tipiṭaka, arhat ideal) is NOT the same as the historically loaded
  "Hīnayāna" label (Mahāyāna-origin, polemical, never a self-designation) → Mahāyāna (bodhisattva ideal;
  Yogācāra and Mādhyamika as its two major philosophical formations).
- Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika: non-Mahāyāna, Sarvāstivāda-linked — never call them "Theravāda."

### Causal Core

- Pratītyasamutpāda: conditioned arising and conditioned cessation — "this being, that becomes."
- Four Noble Truths give its soteriological frame: duḥkha, samudaya, nirodha, mārga.
- Twelve links: avidyā → saṃskāra → vijñāna → nāma-rūpa → ṣaḍāyatana → sparśa → vedanā → tṛṣṇā → upādāna →
  bhava → jāti → jarā-maraṇa. Three readings: three-life, present-process, structural/soteriological.
- Middle Path: practical (avoids indulgence/mortification; Noble Eightfold Path) and doctrinal (avoids
  eternalism/annihilationism).
- Three marks: anitya, duḥkha, anātman — impermanence is the seed of, but not identical with, momentariness.

### Derivation Spine

```text
DEPENDENCE → IMPERMANENCE → MOMENTARINESS
           → NO SUBSTANTIAL SELF
           → CRAVING CAN CEASE
           → NIRVĀṆA
```

### Momentariness

- Real = causally efficacious: *sat = arthakriyā-samartha*.
- Permanent-cause dilemma: all effects simultaneously (yaugapadya) or different powers successively (krama).
- Sahakāri-anapekṣā objection: unchanged cause contributes no temporally specific explanation.
- Destruction argument: perishing is intrinsic to the conditioned, not a separately-caused event.
- Santāna explains continuity without numerical identity.
- 2026 PYQ: momentariness problematises action/responsibility via kṛtanāśa and akṛtābhyāgama; santāna
  resolves transmission but leaves ownership contested.
- Main criticism: memory, recognition and ownership appear to require one persisting subject.

### No-Self

- Five skandhas: rūpa, vedanā, saṃjñā, saṃskāra, vijñāna.
- Chariot analogy: person is conventionally designated upon parts/processes.
- Pudgala-nairātmya denies personal substance; dharma-nairātmya denies intrinsic nature even in dharmas.
- Puggalavāda/Vātsīputrīya foil: rejected as a relapse into self-theory.
- Triratna: refuge-taker is the conventional person (prajñapti-puruṣa).
- Nirvāṇa: cessation of rāga, dveṣa, moha — cessation of process, not survival of a soul; unconditioned
  (asaṃskṛta) in important classifications, so momentariness (which applies to conditioned reals) does not
  contradict it.
- No-self is not non-existence of conventional persons; it is not annihilationism.

### Four Schools Matrix

| School | External world | Key formula | Main objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vaibhāṣika | directly perceived and real | direct realism (bāhya-pratyakṣa-vāda) | access to momentary dharmas |
| Sautrāntika | real but inferred | representational realism (bāhyānumeya-vāda) | copy cannot prove original |
| Yogācāra | independent external object denied | vijñaptimātratā | intersubjective order |
| Mādhyamika | empty of svabhāva | śūnyatā and two truths | nihilism/self-refutation charge |

### Yogācāra

- Ālaya-vijñāna stores bīja and vāsanā.
- Dream and atom/whole arguments challenge external objects.
- Three natures: parikalpita, paratantra, pariniṣpanna.
- Safer verdict: critique of independent objecthood, not "nothing exists"; NOT identical with Advaita.

### Mādhyamika

- Dependently arisen = empty of svabhāva.
- Śūnyatā does not deny conventional efficacy.
- Two truths: saṃvṛti and paramārtha.
- Catuṣkoṭi: asti / nāsti / asti ca nāsti ca / naivāsti naiva nāsti — prasajya-pratiṣedha (non-implicative).
- Emptiness of emptiness blocks reification; Prāsaṅgika (Candrakīrti) vs Svātantrika (Bhāvaviveka).

### Buddhist Epistemology

- Dignāga-Dharmakīrti: exactly two pramāṇas — perception and inference.
- Perception is non-conceptual (kalpanāpoḍha) and non-errant (abhrānta); fourfold division.
- Svalakṣaṇa: unique causally effective particular. Sāmānyalakṣaṇa: conceptual/general construction.
- Apoha: meaning by exclusion of the other; grounded in shared causal capacity (Dharmakīrti's reply to
  Kumārila's circularity charge).
- Pramāṇa and pramāṇaphala are phases of one cognitive event, not two separate things.

### Karma, Memory and Responsibility

- Experience leaves dispositions in the stream; Sautrāntika: seed-like potency; Yogācāra: ālaya-vijñāna/bīja.
- Across moments: neither strict identity nor complete difference.
- Kṛtanāśa (loss of the deed) and akṛtābhyāgama (undeserved fruit) are the two classical difficulties.
- Nyāya pressure: causal succession may not equal ownership — the exact 2026 PYQ tension.

### Criticisms — Named Objector Map

|---|---|---|---|
| Nyāya (Vātsyāyana/Uddyotakara/Udayana) | memory, recognition, desert need an enduring self | santāna, sādṛśya, ālaya-vijñāna | series may not "own" memories |
| Nyāya realist | permanent substance can act via auxiliaries | sahakāri-anapekṣā dilemma | substance-quality model remains intuitive |
| Kumārila Bhaṭṭa | self/universals needed for pramāṇa and word-meaning | constructed self, apoha via causal convergence | circularity objection persists |
| Śaṃkara-type Advaita | momentariness/emptiness make liberation, unity of experience unintelligible | two truths, no eternal witness needed | unity of experience is Advaita's strongest pressure point |
| Nyāya/realist | emptiness = nihilism/self-refuting | two truths, empty words cure empty reifications | Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika internal unease |
| Nyāya/Mīmāṃsā/Vedānta | karma without self is unjust | neither identity nor difference; causal continuity | ownership of desert remains contested |

### Creator God and Avyākata (Optional Enrichment)

- Creator-God arguments: change objection, desire/purpose objection, problem of evil, explanatory
  redundancy given dependent origination.
- Avyākata questions: world eternal/finite, jīva=body?, Tathāgata after death? — declined as non-conducive
  and presuppositionally defective; arrow parable; fire-going-out simile; three readings (agnostic,
