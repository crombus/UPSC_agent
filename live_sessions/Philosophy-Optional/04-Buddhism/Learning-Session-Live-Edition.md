# MUST-NEEDED / CORE LEARNING SESSION

## Lesson 1 — The diagnostic frame: traditions, suffering and two Middle Paths

**Progress:** 1/11 · **Stage:** Foundation · **Owned range:** orientation, path and three marks

### Visual first: the complete starting architecture

```text
SUFFERING (duḥkha)
      │ diagnosed by
      ▼
FOUR NOBLE TRUTHS
fact → conditioned origin → possible cessation → executable path
      │
      ├── PRACTICAL MIDDLE PATH
      │   indulgence ✗ ── Eightfold Path ── self-mortification ✗
      │
      ├── DOCTRINAL MIDDLE PATH
      │   eternal unchanged self ✗ ── conditioned continuity ── sheer annihilation ✗
      │
      └── THREE MARKS
          impermanence → insecure when grasped → no permanent owner
```

*The practical discipline and the metaphysical middle reinforce one another: transformation is
possible because bondage is conditioned rather than fixed.*

### 1. Historical and school placement

| Category | Exact minimum | Examiner trap |
|---|---|---|
| **Early Buddhism** | Earliest recoverable discourse traditions; common diagnostic core of Four Truths, Middle Path, dependent origination, impermanence and no-self | Do not identify it exclusively with a later school |
| **Theravāda** | Living “Teaching of the Elders,” Pali Tipiṭaka, arahant ideal | Do not use it as a synonym for every non-Mahāyāna school |
| **Historically loaded “Hīnayāna”** | Old Mahāyāna polemical/doxographic label | It was not a shared self-designation; avoid casual use |
| **Mahāyāna** | Diverse family foregrounding the bodhisattva path, wisdom and compassion | It is wider than Yogācāra or Madhyamaka |
| **Vaibhāṣika / Sautrāntika** | Sarvāstivāda-related non-Mahāyāna scholastic positions | Neither is simply Theravāda |
| **Yogācāra / Madhyamaka** | Major Mahāyāna philosophical formations | They do not exhaust Mahāyāna |

The **liberated saint (arhat; Pali: arahant)** extinguishes the causes of suffering. The
**awakening-being (bodhisattva)** pursues complete Buddhahood for the benefit of beings. The safe
contrast concerns dominant path ideals, not the caricature “selfish arhat versus altruistic
bodhisattva.”

### 2. Four Noble Truths as one causal argument

1. ✅ There is suffering or pervasive unsatisfactoriness.
2. ✅ Suffering has conditioned origin, centrally ignorance and craving.
3. ✅ What depends on removable conditions can cease.
4. ✅ The Noble Eightfold Path is the disciplined removal-route.

This is not a four-item list. It is an inference: **conditioned origin makes cessation and practice
intelligible**.

### 3. The practical Middle Path

| Training | Path factors | Causal work |
|---|---|---|
| Wisdom (prajñā) | right view, right intention | corrects ignorance and orientation |
| Ethical discipline (śīla) | right speech, action, livelihood | stops conduct that feeds craving and harm |
| Meditative discipline (samādhi) | right effort, mindfulness, concentration | transforms attention and habitual reaction |

The path is not a numerical average between indulgence and mortification. It is a positive
discipline avoiding the defects of both.

### 4. The doctrinal Middle Path and three marks

| Concept | Claim | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Impermanence (anitya; Pali: anicca) | conditioned formations arise and cease | early impermanence is not verbally identical with the later one-moment thesis |
| Unsatisfactoriness (duḥkha; Pali: dukkha) | what changes cannot secure lasting satisfaction when appropriated | pain alone does not exhaust duḥkha |
| No-self (anātman; Pali: anattā) | no permanent independent owner is found | conventional agency is not thereby erased |

### Argument anatomy

- **Claim:** Buddhism rejects both eternalism and annihilationism.
- **Reason:** a permanent self is not found, yet causal continuity remains operative.
- **Consequence:** karma, practice and liberation can function without an unchanging substance.
- **Qualification:** the adequacy of causal continuity for moral desert remains disputed.

### Bounded example

A medical diagnosis links symptom, cause, cure and treatment. The analogy clarifies the Four
Truths’ structure. It stops short of making liberation merely clinical: Buddhist diagnosis also
changes the metaphysical appropriation of “I” and “mine.”

### Strongest objection → reply → residual

**Objection:** Without a permanent subject, nobody could walk the path or enjoy liberation.

**Reply:** The conventional person—a causally connected aggregate-stream—learns, chooses and
changes. Practice does not require an immutable owner.

**Residual:** The reply secures practical continuity more easily than strict identity or desert.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Elucidate:** define both Middle Paths and connect them to the Four Truths.
- **Distinguish:** early Buddhism / Theravāda / four-school taxonomy; arhat / bodhisattva.
- **Discuss:** doctrine → reason → practical consequence → boundary.
- **Critical use:** never present moderation, no-self or Mahāyāna as slogans.

### Local practice

**MCQ 1.** Which statement best expresses the doctrinal Middle Path?

A. It denies an eternal self while preserving conditioned causal continuity.
B. It compromises halfway between realism and idealism.
C. It says every view is equally true.
D. It replaces all discipline with metaphysical silence.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** It avoids both eternalism and annihilationism.
- **B — Incorrect:** The middle is not an arithmetic compromise.
- **C — Incorrect:** Buddhism advances definite causal and soteriological claims.
- **D — Incorrect:** The Eightfold Path supplies positive discipline.

**MCQ 2.** Which classification is accurate?

A. Vaibhāṣika is another name for Theravāda.
B. Yogācāra and Madhyamaka are major Mahāyāna philosophical formations.
C. “Hīnayāna” is the preferred self-name of all early schools.
D. Mahāyāna is identical with Madhyamaka.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Vaibhāṣika belongs to the Sarvāstivāda-related scholastic world.
- **B — Correct:** Both are major Mahāyāna philosophies without exhausting Mahāyāna.
- **C — Incorrect:** The term is historically loaded and polemical.
- **D — Incorrect:** Mahāyāna is broader than one school.

## Lesson 2 — Dependent origination: arising, maintenance and reverse cessation

**Progress:** 2/11 · **Stage:** Foundation/Core · **Dependency:** Lesson 1

### Visual first: the twelve-link causal wheel

```text
ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six bases → contact
     ▲                                                            │
     │                                                            ▼
ageing/death ← birth ← becoming ← grasping ← craving ← feeling

FORWARD: maintained suffering
REVERSE: insight/discipline weakens craving → grasping → becoming → renewed suffering
```

### 1. Formula and philosophical point

**Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda)** means: this being, that becomes; from this arising,
that arises; this not being, that is not; from this ceasing, that ceases. It explains conditions,
not creation from an absolute first cause.

### 2. Twelve links in order

1. ignorance (avidyā)
2. formations (saṃskāra)
3. consciousness (vijñāna)
4. name-and-form (nāma-rūpa)
5. six sense-bases (ṣaḍāyatana)
6. contact (sparśa)
7. feeling (vedanā)
8. craving (tṛṣṇā)
9. grasping (upādāna)
10. becoming (bhava)
11. birth (jāti)
12. ageing-and-death (jarā-maraṇa)

### 3. Three legitimate readings

| Reading | Kernel | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-life | past causes → present fruits/causes → future fruits | rebirth without transmigrating self | not the only interpretation |
| Present-process | contact and feeling become craving and appropriation now | immediate psychological-existential use | “psychological” does not mean imaginary |
| Structural-soteriological | conditions maintain suffering and can be reversed | strongest link to Four Truths | not a search for the universe’s first event |

### 4. Wrong appropriation and cessation

Ignorance does not mean absence of information only. It is the practical misreading of changing
aggregates as enduring “I” and “mine.” Feeling becomes craving; craving becomes grasping; grasping
sustains becoming. Wisdom, ethical restraint and meditation interrupt that sequence. Thus
knowledge of dependent origination is performative: it changes appropriation.

### 5. What follows—and what does not

- Dependence directly supports conditioned **impermanence**.
- It supports **no independent substance**.
- Madhyamaka draws **emptiness of intrinsic nature** from it.
- Bare dependence does **not** by itself prove that every real lasts exactly one moment; that needs
  the causal-efficacy argument taught next.

### Argument anatomy

- **Claim:** suffering is neither fate nor an uncaused accident.
- **Ground:** it is maintained through identifiable conditions.
- **Application:** remove or transform decisive conditions, especially craving and ignorance.
- **Conclusion:** cessation is intelligible without positing a permanent liberated self.

### Bounded example

A flame depends on wick, fuel and oxygen. Remove the conditions and it ceases. This illustrates
conditional existence, but it cannot by itself prove all Buddhist claims about consciousness,
karma or exact moment-duration.

### Objection → reply → residual

**Objection:** An endless chain of conditions explains nothing because it lacks a first cause.

**Reply:** The doctrine answers a different question: what presently maintains suffering and how
can it stop? It need not supply an absolute cosmological origin.

**Residual:** A realist may still ask whether conditional relations require some stable relata;
later Buddhist schools answer differently.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- For **“What is?”**, give formula, twelve links and causal purpose.
- For **“soteriology”**, run Four Truths → forward chain → reverse cessation → Eightfold practice.
- For **“prove momentariness”**, state the extra causal-efficacy premise; do not jump.
- For **“void/real”**, preserve the school-specific conclusions taught in Lessons 7–9.

### Local practice

**MCQ 3.** Which sequence correctly begins the twelve links?

A. craving → feeling → contact → ignorance
B. consciousness → ignorance → formations → name-and-form
C. ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form
D. name-and-form → birth → feeling → craving

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** It reverses and relocates the early links.
- **B — Incorrect:** Ignorance and formations precede consciousness in the standard sequence.
- **C — Correct:** This is the canonical opening order.
- **D — Incorrect:** Birth belongs near the end, not between name-and-form and feeling.

**MCQ 4.** Why does dependent origination not fail merely because it lacks a first cause?

A. Buddhism proves a creator elsewhere.
B. The links are timeless substances.
C. First causes are logically impossible in every system.
D. Its primary task is to diagnose and end the conditions maintaining suffering.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Buddhism does not add a creator to this analysis.
- **B — Incorrect:** The links are conditioned processes.
- **C — Incorrect:** The claim is not a universal proof against every first-cause theory.
- **D — Correct:** Its explanatory target is maintained suffering and cessation.

## Lesson 3 — Momentariness: from causal efficacy to event-ontology

**Progress:** 3/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** dependent origination

### Visual first: the two-horn test for permanence

```text
REAL = causally efficacious (arthakriyā)
                 │
       Can a wholly permanent cause act?
          ┌──────┴──────┐
          ▼             ▼
   all at once       successively
   (yaugapadya)      (krama)
 all effects now     different profile over time
          │             │
          └──── permanent unchanged cause fails ────┘
                         ▼
        causally operative reality is momentary
```

### 1. Thesis and historical caution

**Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda)** holds that whatever is genuinely real and causally operative is
momentary. This is stronger than the early doctrine that conditioned things are impermanent.

### 2. Causal-efficacy argument

1. To be real is to be capable of producing an effect
   (**sat = arthakriyā-samartha**).
2. A permanent cause must produce either simultaneously or successively.
3. If fully efficacious simultaneously, it should produce all effects at once.
4. If it produces successively, its causal profile differs across moments.
5. A wholly unchanged thing cannot possess genuinely different temporal profiles.
6. Therefore causal reality is moment-specific: **whatever is real is momentary
   (yat sat tat kṣaṇikam)**.

### 3. Auxiliary-condition challenge

Nyāya proposes an enduring cause that acts only when helper-conditions (sahakārin) arrive. The
Buddhist replies: if the cause already has complete power, why wait; if the helpers add the
decisive power, the change belongs to them rather than to an inert permanent substrate.

### 4. Destruction argument

Conditioned arising already bears cessation; a thing does not first become wholly stable and later
require an independent destroyer. This does not mean “nothing has causal conditions.” It means
perishing belongs to the nature of conditioned occurrence.

### 5. Particular and concept

The **unique particular (svalakṣaṇa)** is momentary and causally efficacious. A general character
(sāmānyalakṣaṇa) is a conceptual construction used by thought and language. Thus a changing series
can be conventionally called “the same jar” without an ultimately enduring jar-substance.

### Argument anatomy

- **Hidden premise:** reality is measured by causal work, not inert self-identity.
- **Rival model:** substance endures while properties and helpers change.
- **Buddhist pressure:** the changing phase does explanatory work.
- **Residual:** Nyāya can insist that differentiated powers belong to one enduring substance.

### Bounded example

A seed-series issues in a sprout. The example shows phase-specific efficacy. It does not itself
prove that the duration of every phase is literally infinitesimal; the philosophical argument must
carry that burden.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Prove:** display both horns and the helper-condition reply.
- **Logical derivative:** distinguish direct impermanence from the added scholastic premise.
- **Critically discuss:** reconstruct Nyāya’s best enduring-cause model before judging.
- **Trap:** momentary does not mean causally chaotic or unreal.

### Local practice

**MCQ 5.** What is the decisive added premise needed to derive momentariness from dependence?

A. Reality is causal efficacy, and an unchanged entity cannot explain different effects over time.
B. Whatever is named is eternal.
C. Every conditioned thing is spatially atomic.
D. Memory is infallible.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** It supplies the bridge beyond bare impermanence.
- **B — Incorrect:** Buddhist nominalism rejects this inference.
- **C — Incorrect:** The argument is not restricted to spatial atoms.
- **D — Incorrect:** Memory is a challenge, not the derivational premise.

**MCQ 6.** What is Nyāya’s strongest reply to the simultaneity/succession dilemma?

A. Causes never need conditions.
B. An enduring substance acts differently when changing auxiliary conditions are present.
C. Effects are unreal.
D. Every cause produces all effects immediately.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Nyāya expressly invokes auxiliaries.
- **B — Correct:** This is the rival model the Buddhist must answer.
- **C — Incorrect:** Nyāya is realist about effects.
- **D — Incorrect:** That is the Buddhist pressure against a fully efficacious permanent cause.

## Lesson 4 — Continuity without identity: memory, karma and responsibility

**Progress:** 4/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** momentariness

### Visual first: neither the same nor wholly other

```text
experience₁ → trace → disposition → memory₂ → choice₃ → consequence₄
    │                                                   │
    └──────────── one causal continuum (santāna) ──────┘

STRICT IDENTITY ✗  (would invite permanence)
TOTAL DIFFERENCE ✗ (would destroy memory and desert)
CAUSAL CONTINUITY ✓ (Buddhist middle position)
```

### 1. Causal continuum

A **causal continuum (santāna/santati)** is a sequence in which an earlier moment conditions a
later sufficiently connected moment. Continuity does not require numerical identity. Flame and
river analogies illustrate this; they do not substitute for the causal account.

### 2. Memory and recognition

Memory is a later cognition caused by a trace from an earlier experience in the same stream.
Recognition reports conventional continuity and similarity (sādṛśya), not an infallible grasp of
substantial identity. Mere resemblance between unrelated streams would not suffice.

### 3. Karma and rebirth

Intentional action (cetanā) conditions later dispositions and consequences. The recipient is
neither numerically the identical permanent agent nor an unrelated other. This avoids eternalism
and moral discontinuity.

### 4. Yogācāra’s special mechanism

Store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) carries conditioned seeds (bīja) that mature as later
experience. It is a changing stream, not an eternal soul, and it is not the pan-Buddhist answer.

### 5. Human action and responsibility

Momentary agents can deliberate, restrain harm, express remorse and follow right action and right
livelihood. Causal connection explains why later phases bear consequences. Yet **causal succession
and moral desert are not identical questions**: why a later phase deserves blame remains the
strongest realist pressure.

### Argument anatomy

- **Agency:** intentions are causally efficacious events.
- **Continuity:** dispositions and effects remain within an appropriately connected stream.
- **Normativity:** reasons and path-discipline distinguish voluntary harm from restraint.
- **Qualification:** practical responsibility is stronger than a fully demonstrated identity-based
  theory of retributive desert.

### Bounded example

One lamp lights another: no numerically identical flame travels, yet the second depends on the
first. The analogy clarifies transmission, but moral responsibility additionally needs intention,
norms and an account of ownership.

### Objection → reply → residual

**Nyāya objection:** The experiencer, rememberer, doer and enjoyer must be one enduring self.

**Buddhist reply:** Trace, causal stream and conventional designation explain continuity without a
substance; Yogācāra adds conditioned seeds.

**Residual:** A stream may connect events without obviously being the subject that owns them.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Memory:** experience → trace → later cognition → conventional recognition → Nyāya residual.
- **Karma:** intention → stream-conditioning → neither identity nor difference → desert objection.
- **Responsibility:** separate agency, causal consequence and deserved blame.
- **Trap:** never turn store-consciousness into a hidden ātman.

### Local practice

**MCQ 7.** Which claim best explains Buddhist memory?

A. A permanent witness stores every cognition.
B. Similar events anywhere count as one person’s memory.
C. A later cognition is conditioned by an earlier trace within one causal stream.
D. Memory proves that momentariness is false.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** It imports the self Buddhism rejects.
- **B — Incorrect:** Causal linkage, not mere resemblance, is required.
- **C — Correct:** It states the stream-and-trace account.
- **D — Incorrect:** Memory is treated as an objection answered conventionally.

A. Whether intentions can have effects.
B. Whether Buddhism permits ethical discipline.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Intentional efficacy is central to karma.

## Lesson 5 — No-self: aggregates, designation and two scopes

**Progress:** 5/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** continuity

### Visual first: search for the owner

```text
PERSON
 ├─ form/body (rūpa)
 ├─ feeling (vedanā)
 ├─ recognition (saṃjñā)
 ├─ formations/dispositions (saṃskāra)
 └─ consciousness (vijñāna)
          │
          ▼
no sixth permanent owner found
          │
          ├─ conventional person: valid designation
          └─ ultimate self-substance: denied
```

### 1. Five-aggregate argument

No-self (nairātmyavāda/anātmavāda) denies a permanent, independent self behind experience. The
person is analysed into form, feeling, recognition, formations and consciousness. Each is
conditioned and changing; none can be securely appropriated as an eternal “I,” and no additional
owner appears.

### 2. Chariot and designation

“Chariot” designates arranged parts and functions; it is not a separate substance beyond them.
Likewise, “person” is a valid conventional designation on an aggregate-process. This is not the
claim that persons are useless fictions.

### 3. Two no-self scopes

| Thesis | Denial | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Personal no-self (pudgala-nairātmya) | enduring personal substance | shared Buddhist minimum |
| Phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairātmya) | intrinsic nature even in dharmas | stressed by Mahāyāna, especially Madhyamaka |

Several Abhidharma realist schools deny a person-self while treating dharmas as ultimately real.
Madhyamaka extends anti-essentialism to dharmas themselves.

### 4. Puggalavāda as internal foil

Puggalavādins proposed a person (pudgala) neither identical with nor different from the aggregates
to secure continuity. Mainstream critics feared a disguised self. Its importance is diagnostic:
the continuity pressure was serious inside Buddhism itself.

### Argument anatomy

- **Analysis:** experience yields conditioned aggregates.
- **Negative conclusion:** no permanent independent owner is found.
- **Positive account:** a functional person exists conventionally through causal organisation.
- **Limit:** absence of a found owner is strongest against substantial selfhood, not against every
  possible account of subjectivity.

### Bounded example

A university is not one extra object beside buildings, people and practices, yet it is not nothing.
The analogy helps with designation; it stops before proving that consciousness has no irreducible
unity.

### Objection → reply → residual

**Objection:** Enumeration of parts does not show that no whole or subject exists.

**Reply:** Buddhism asks for evidence of an independent, unchanging owner beyond the conditioned
functions and finds none.

**Residual:** Nyāya and Advaita argue that unified cognition itself supplies such evidence.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Explain:** name all five aggregates and state the anti-appropriation argument.
- **Distinguish:** personal no-self versus phenomenon no-self with school scope.
- **Critically discuss:** conventional designation → rival subject-unity objection → graded verdict.
- **Trap:** no-self is not gross-body materialism.

### Local practice

**MCQ 9.** Which set contains the five aggregates?

A. form, feeling, recognition, formations, consciousness
B. earth, water, fire, air, ether
C. self, intellect, ego, mind, senses
D. substance, quality, action, universal, inherence

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** These are the five aggregates.
- **B — Incorrect:** This is an element-list, not the Buddhist person analysis.
- **C — Incorrect:** It resembles other Indian ontological schemes.
- **D — Incorrect:** These are Vaiśeṣika categories.

**MCQ 10.** What distinguishes phenomenon no-self from personal no-self?

A. It affirms an eternal person.
B. It denies intrinsic nature even to dharmas, not only a permanent person.
C. It is identical with bodily materialism.
D. It belongs exclusively to Nyāya.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Both positions reject substantial selfhood.
- **B — Correct:** It widens anti-essentialism to phenomena.
- **C — Incorrect:** Buddhist analysis is not Cārvāka reduction.
- **D — Incorrect:** Madhyamaka strongly develops it.

## Lesson 6 — Three Jewels and cessation without a soul

**Progress:** 6/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** no-self

### Visual first: the soteriological relation

```text
AWAKENED EXEMPLAR
Buddha
   │ teaches
   ▼
PATH / REALISED TEACHING
Dharma
   │ practised, tested and transmitted by
   ▼
COMMUNITY
Saṅgha
   │ transforms the conventional practitioner
   ▼
cessation of greed + aversion + delusion = nirvāṇa
```

### 1. Internal relation of the Three Jewels

| Refuge | Function | Relation |
|---|---|---|
| Buddha | awakened teacher and exemplar | discovers and teaches the path |
| Dharma | teaching and realised truth of suffering, conditioning and cessation | makes awakening communicable |
| Saṅgha | practising and noble community | embodies, tests and transmits Dharma |

The relation is pedagogical and soteriological, not identity among three eternal substances.

### 2. Consistency with no-self

A conventional changing practitioner can take refuge, cultivate insight and cease appropriation.
The Saṅgha is not a collective self, and the Buddha is not a creator who saves an immortal soul.

### 3. Cessation of suffering

Cessation (nirvāṇa; Pali: nibbāna) extinguishes attachment, aversion and delusion. It is not a
place possessed by a soul. The process producing suffering ends.

### 4. Momentariness and cessation

The one-moment thesis applies to conditioned causally operative formations. Important Theravāda
and Abhidharma accounts classify nibbāna as unconditioned; do not call it another momentary event.
An arahant can be liberated while aggregates remain; final passing means no renewed conditioned
arising, not the travel of an owner.

Madhyamaka denies independently substantial saṃsāra and nirvāṇa alike: liberation is the ending of
reifying grasping, not entry into a second self-existing realm.

### Argument anatomy

- No-self removes the supposed permanent beneficiary.
- Momentariness allows conditioned bondage to cease.
- Conventional personhood preserves practice and attainment-language.
- The realist residual asks whether cessation without a persisting subject is genuinely liberation.

### Bounded example

Extinguishing a fire means ending the sustaining conditions, not transporting a fire-substance
elsewhere. The analogy clarifies cessation; it must not be turned into a complete ontology of
post-mortem existence.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Consistency:** define both doctrines, expose the apparent conflict, use designation and
  cessation, then retain the subject objection.
- **Triratna:** explain all three functions and their internal relation before discussing no-self.
- **2025 qualifier:** distinguish conditioned momentariness from unconditioned cessation.
- **Trap:** liberation is neither annihilation of a soul nor eternal experience by one.

## Lesson 7 — Buddhist realism: Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika

**Progress:** 7/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** dependent origination and momentariness

### Visual first: two external realisms

```text
EXTERNAL OBJECT
      │
      ├── Vaibhāṣika: directly perceived
      │      + Sarvāstivāda three-time existence
      │      + present has distinctive efficacy
      │
      └── Sautrāntika: representation directly known
             + external cause inferred
             + presentist pressure against past/future dharmas
```

### 1. Vaibhāṣika direct realism

Vaibhāṣika holds that external objects are directly perceived
(bāhya-pratyakṣa-vāda). Its Sarvāstivāda background affirms some mode of existence for past,
present and future dharmas (sarvam asti), while present dharmas possess distinctive present causal
efficacy. Real dharmas do not amount to a permanent personal self.

### 2. Sautrāntika representational realism

Sautrāntika holds that the mental representation is directly known and the external object is
inferred as its cause (bāhyānumeya-vāda). It rejects the stronger three-time ontology and privileges
the present.

### 3. Bilateral comparison

| Axis | Vaibhāṣika | Sautrāntika |
|---|---|---|
| External world | real | real |
| Access | direct | mediated by representation |
| Past/future dharmas | some mode of existence | not fully existent like present |
| Main pressure | how direct access works | whether inference needs the external posit |

### Argument anatomy

The pair proves that “Buddhism” cannot be equated with idealism or emptiness. Both defend external
reality while differing about access and temporal ontology.

### Bounded example

Seeing smoke and inferring fire approximates Sautrāntika mediation, but the school’s claim concerns
all external perception, not merely occasional inference.

### Objection → reply → residual

**Yogācāra objection:** If only representation is given, an external cause is explanatory surplus.

**Sautrāntika reply:** patterned, resistant and shared experience is best explained by independent
external causes.

**Residual:** The inference remains defeasible because representation never exits itself to compare
with the external object.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Compare:** shared realism first, then access and temporal difference.
- **“Everything real”:** name Vaibhāṣika/Sarvāstivāda and define the qualified sense.
- **Trap:** sarvam asti does not mean all times are equally causally active.

### Local practice

## Lesson 8 — Yogācāra: consciousness-only without private fantasy

**Progress:** 8/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** representational realism

### Visual first: three natures and the continuity mechanism

```text
IMAGINED NATURE (parikalpita)
independent subject/object duality projected
                 │ corrected within
                 ▼
DEPENDENT NATURE (paratantra)
conditioned flow of cognition + seeds in store-consciousness
                 │ insight reveals
                 ▼
CONSUMMATE NATURE (pariniṣpanna)
absence of the imagined duality in that dependent flow
```

### 1. Consciousness-only

Consciousness-only or representation-only (vijñaptimātra) says that object-appearance is
inseparable from cognition and that an independently external object is not established merely by
appearance. It does not mean a private ego freely invents the world.

### 2. Arguments

- **Dream/illusion:** object-like appearance can occur without a matching external object.
- **Access:** cognition directly encounters presentations, not an object defined as outside every
  presentation.
- **Atom dilemma:** partless atoms cannot combine spatially; atoms with parts are not ultimate.

The dream argument defeats “appearance guarantees exteriority”; it does not alone prove that no
waking external reality exists.

### 3. Store-consciousness and shared experience

Store-consciousness is a conditioned stream bearing karmic seeds. Beginningless dispositions and
shared karmic conditioning explain patterned intersubjective appearances. It solves continuity only
at the cost of appearing close to the substantial continuity Buddhism rejects.

### 4. Realist reply

Sautrāntika argues that representations are best explained as effects of external causes. Public
agreement, resistance and surprise support realism. Yogācāra must show why the external cause is
unnecessary, not merely unknowable.

### Argument anatomy

- **Target:** inference from appearance to independent external matter.
- **Positive model:** dependent cognition-stream structured by seeds.
- **Liberative point:** subject/object duality is imagined within conditioned representation.
- **Residual:** intersubjectivity and the status of store-consciousness remain pressure-points.

### Bounded example

A dream shows that vivid objecthood does not entail a matching external object. It does not show
that waking tables are impossible; further argument is required.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Discuss denial:** dream + access + atom critique + three natures + realist reply.
- **Trap:** avoid solipsism and “mind creates whatever it wishes.”
- **Critical verdict:** strong epistemic challenge; metaphysical denial needs the positive seed-model.

### Local practice

A. All waking objects are certainly mental.
B. Store-consciousness is eternal.
C. Object-like appearance does not guarantee an independently external object.
D. Shared experience is impossible.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** That conclusion exceeds the evidence.
- **B — Incorrect:** Store-consciousness is conditioned.
- **C — Correct:** This is the argument’s bounded force.
- **D — Incorrect:** Yogācāra offers a seed-based account of shared patterns.

**Answer: D**

- **B — Incorrect:** This confuses the imagined and dependent levels.

## Lesson 9 — Madhyamaka: emptiness, four corners and two truths

**Progress:** 9/11 · **Stage:** Core · **Dependency:** dependent origination and school contrasts

### Visual first: dependence to emptiness without nihilism

```text
depends on causes, parts, concepts and designation
                         │
                         ▼
cannot exist by intrinsic nature (svabhāva)
                         │
                         ▼
empty (śūnya) of self-being
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             ▼                       ▼
conventional truth              ultimate truth
causation, persons,             no intrinsic nature
ethics and path function        in any dharma
```

### 1. Core derivation

If something arose through its own independent nature, it would not depend on causes and
conditions. Whatever is dependently originated therefore lacks intrinsic nature (svabhāva).
Emptiness (śūnyatā) is this absence, not sheer non-being.

### 2. Four-corner analysis

The four alternatives are: it is; it is not; it both is and is not; it neither is nor is not.
Madhyamaka denies all four when they are asserted of an intrinsically existing thing. The denial is
non-implicative: rejecting own-being does not install a fifth hidden substance.

Early Buddhist use of the four alternatives is primarily therapeutic and soteriological: all four
answers to certain unanswered questions are set aside because their presuppositions do not conduce
to liberation. Madhyamaka turns the four-corner analysis into a logical-ontological critique by
denying every alternative asserted of anything imagined to possess svabhāva.

### 3. Two truths

Conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) is the domain of dependent language, persons, karma, compassion
and path-practice. Ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) is the absence of intrinsic nature. Convention
is not sheer falsehood, and ultimacy is not another entity.

### 4. Emptiness of emptiness

Emptiness itself is dependently designated and must not be reified. Nāgārjuna’s no-independent-
thesis strategy treats argument as therapeutic removal of reification.

### 5. Nihilism and self-refutation objections

The realist asks: if all is empty, what knows, acts or liberates? If “all theses are empty” is a
thesis, is it self-defeating? Madhyamaka replies that empty discourse functions conventionally and
denies intrinsic commitments rather than all assertion.

### Argument anatomy

- **Claim:** dependence excludes intrinsic self-existence.
- **Consequence:** all dharmas are empty of svabhāva.
- **Protection:** two truths preserve conventional functioning.
- **Residual:** whether non-implicative denial avoids every positive commitment remains contested.

### Bounded example

Money functions through institutions, practices and recognition without an intrinsic monetary
essence. The analogy illuminates dependent designation but cannot reduce every dharma to social
convention.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Establish:** dependence → contradiction with svabhāva → emptiness.
- **Discuss:** add two truths and nihilism reply.
- **“Void versus real”:** compare Madhyamaka with Vaibhāṣika, not with an unnamed Buddhism.
- **Trap:** emptiness is neither nothingness nor a permanent Absolute.

### Local practice

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** This is Nāgārjuna’s central inference.

## Lesson 10 — Epistemology, unanswered questions and rival pressure

**Progress:** 10/11 · **Stage:** Core synthesis · **Dependency:** Lessons 1–9

### Visual first: Buddhist reasoning in one circuit

```text
PERCEPTION → unique particular (svalakṣaṇa) → causal efficacy → momentariness
INFERENCE  → general construction (sāmānyalakṣaṇa) → language via exclusion (apoha)
                         │
                         ▼
no enduring knower / universal / instrument-result substance
                         │
                         ▼
named rivals: Nyāya · Mīmāṃsā · Advaita · Cārvāka
```

### 1. Two means of valid knowledge

Dignāga and Dharmakīrti accept perception and inference. Perception is non-conceptual
(kalpanāpoḍha) and, in Dharmakīrti’s refinement, non-erroneous (abhrānta). It presents the unique
particular. Inference handles constructed generality.

### 2. Exclusion theory of meaning

Exclusion (apoha) explains “cow” as “not-non-cow,” avoiding a real universal. Kumārila and
Uddyotakara object that exclusion presupposes positive identification. The Buddhist replies that
causal convergence supports common practical classification; the positive-content problem remains.

### 3. Means and result

On a momentary event-ontology, the means of knowing (pramāṇa) and its result (pramāṇaphala) are not
ultimately separate stages owned by an enduring knower. This shows how epistemology expresses
momentariness and no-self.

### 4. Unanswered questions

The Buddha declines questions about the world’s eternity/finitude, self-body identity and the
Tathāgata after death when framed through misleading alternatives. The poisoned-arrow parable
supports a pragmatic reading: solve suffering before liberation-irrelevant speculation. A
logical-semantic reading adds that the questions presuppose substantial self or world.

### 5. High-yield rival matrix

| Rival | Objection | Buddhist reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nyāya | memory, recognition and desert need enduring self | stream, trace, similarity, conventional person | who owns the stream? |
| Nyāya | permanent cause can act with helpers | helpers carry decisive change | enduring substance remains intuitive |
| Mīmāṃsā | duty and word-meaning need self and universals | constructed stream and apoha | positive determination |
| Advaita | unity and liberation need witnessing consciousness | two truths / self-revealing cognition without ātman | unity of experience |
| realist | emptiness is nihilist or self-refuting | conventional function and non-implicative negation | status of the denial |
| Puggalavāda | a person is needed for continuity | designation on aggregates | pressure motivated the theory |
| Cārvāka | only body-self; no post-mortem stream | Buddhism preserves non-substantial karmic continuity | depends on broader causal theory |

### 6. Cārvāka comparison

Both reject an immortal ātman. Cārvāka tends to identify person with the living body and rejects
post-mortem karmic continuity. Buddhism analyses person into aggregates and preserves conditioned
continuity. This comparison is supporting material; the 2024 exact comparison is Cārvāka-owned.

### Argument anatomy

The Buddhist system is integrated: ontology of momentary particulars shapes perception; denial of
universals shapes language; denial of self shapes the knower; soteriology determines which
questions deserve pursuit.

### Bounded example

Calling several animals “cow” enables reliable action despite their numerical difference. This
helps apoha’s practical side but does not by itself prove that no positive shared feature exists.

### Directive, retrieval and application

- **Distinguish:** object, cognition, word and school scope.
- **Critically explain:** opponent’s strongest premise before reply.
- **Unanswered questions:** therapeutic usefulness + defective presupposition, not mere ignorance.
- **Trap:** later Buddhist epistemology is not automatically every early school’s doctrine.

### Local practice

# VERIFIED PYQS — EXACT WORDING, RETRIEVAL AND SOLVED ANSWERS

## 1. 2018 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Exact question:** How do the Mādhyamika Buddhists apply the notion of Pratītyasamutpāda to establish their doctrine of Śūnyatā? Discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—“apply dependent origination” · kernel—dependence excludes svabhāva · use—derive,
then protect conventional functioning · remediation—if you write “nothing exists,” restore two truths.

### Model answer

Mādhyamika applies dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) as an anti-essentialist argument.
Whatever arises through causes, parts and conceptual designation cannot exist through independent
intrinsic nature (svabhāva). If it possessed svabhāva, it would be self-grounded and would not await
conditions. Therefore dependently arisen dharmas are empty (śūnya) of own-being.

Emptiness does not mean non-existence. At conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya), causes, persons,
karma and the path function; at ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya), none possesses intrinsic
existence. Emptiness itself is also dependently designated and cannot become a hidden Absolute.

The realist objection is that universal emptiness destroys knowledge and ethics. Nāgārjuna replies
that dependence makes functioning possible: only a non-fixed process can arise, change and cease.
Thus śūnyatā is the philosophical implication of dependent origination, not nihilism, though the
status of Madhyamaka’s own denial remains debated.

## 2. 2018 Q6(b) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** Is it consistent for the Buddhists to admit the theory of Nairātmyavāda and the doctrine of Nirvāṇa simultaneously? Give reasons in favour of your answer.

**Retrieval:** cue—“no self, who attains?” · kernel—designated person + cessation of conditions ·
use—show consonance · remediation—do not make nirvāṇa a soul’s possession.

### Model answer

No-self (nairātmyavāda) denies a permanent owner behind the five aggregates; cessation
(nirvāṇa) ends attachment, aversion and delusion. The doctrines appear inconsistent only if
liberation must be an enduring self’s acquired state.

Buddhism distinguishes the conventional person from an ultimate self-substance. A causally
connected aggregate-stream practises the Eightfold Path, develops insight and ceases craving.
Nirvāṇa is therefore not a destination possessed by ātman but the cessation of the conditions
producing suffering. No-self removes the false claimant to liberation; dependent origination
explains both bondage and its reversal.

The flame-going-out analogy clarifies that cessation need not mean a substance travelling
elsewhere. Important traditions classify nibbāna as unconditioned, while Madhyamaka denies that
either saṃsāra or nirvāṇa is an independently existing realm.

Nyāya and Advaita may still object that liberation without a persisting beneficiary is
unintelligible. The Buddhist answer is practically coherent cessation in a continuous stream,
though it does not satisfy every identity-based account of attainment. The two doctrines are thus
internally consistent on Buddhist premises.

## 3. 2019 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Exact question:** How do the Yogācāra Buddhists deny the existence of the external world? Discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—appearance versus exteriority · kernel—dream/access/atom critique + three
natures · use—deny unwarranted external posit · remediation—add Sautrāntika reply and avoid solipsism.

### Model answer

Yogācāra’s consciousness-only thesis (vijñaptimātra) argues that what is directly given is
cognition-as-object-appearance, not a thing established outside cognition. Dreams and illusions
show that vivid objecthood can occur without a matching external object; therefore appearance does
not guarantee exteriority. Yogācāra also attacks atomism: partless atoms cannot combine spatially,
while atoms with parts are not ultimate.

Its positive account is not private fantasy. The dependent nature (paratantra) is a conditioned
stream structured by seeds (bīja) in store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna). The imagined nature
(parikalpita) is independent subject-object duality; consummate nature (pariniṣpanna) is insight
into that duality’s absence.

Sautrāntika replies that shared, resistant experience is best explained by external causes of
representations. Hence dream evidence alone does not disprove all realism. Yogācāra powerfully
undermines the inference from appearance to independent matter, but its full denial depends on the
adequacy of its seed-based account.

## 4. 2019 Q7(c) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** What is Pratītyasamutpāda? How do the Buddhists apply this concept to prove that everything is momentary?

**Retrieval:** cue—definition plus proof · kernel—dependence gives impermanence; causal efficacy
gives one-moment thesis · use—show extra premise · remediation—never call bare dependence a complete proof.

### Model answer

Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) states that phenomena arise and cease through conditions:
“this being, that becomes; this ceasing, that ceases.” It explains suffering through the twelve
links and makes reverse cessation possible.

Dependence first establishes impermanence: what varies with conditions cannot remain eternally
self-identical. Later Buddhist logicians strengthen this into momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) through
causal efficacy (arthakriyā). A real thing must produce an effect. A wholly permanent cause cannot
produce all effects simultaneously, since later production would be unexplained; nor can it
produce successively without acquiring different causal profiles. If auxiliary conditions supply
the difference, they, rather than an unchanged substrate, perform the decisive work. Therefore the
causally real is momentary.

Nyāya replies that an enduring substance acts when helpers are present. Buddhism answers that a
causal stream of changing phases explains apparent persistence. Thus momentariness is a powerful
scholastic development of dependent origination, but not a theorem from the bare dependence
formula alone.

## 5. 2020 Q6(a) · 20 marks

**Exact question:** How is Kṣaṇikavāda a logical derivative of Pratītyasamutpāda in Buddhism? Explain.

**Retrieval:** cue—“logical derivative” · kernel—conditionality → impermanence + arthakriyā horns ·
use—reconstruct rival auxiliaries · remediation—state the qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) claims that whatever is causally real is momentary. Dependent
origination (pratītyasamutpāda) supplies its starting point: a conditioned thing arises and ceases
with conditions and therefore cannot be an eternal self-grounded substance.

However, dependence directly proves non-eternity, not exact one-moment duration. The stronger
derivation uses causal efficacy (arthakriyākāritva). First, the real must make a causal difference.
Second, a wholly permanent cause must act either simultaneously or successively. If simultaneously,
its entire effect-capacity should be exhausted at once. If successively, it has different powers at
different times and is not wholly unchanged. Third, invoking auxiliary conditions creates a
dilemma: if the permanent cause already has complete power, helpers are redundant; if helpers add
the operative difference, changing factors carry the efficacy.

Thus the apparently persisting object is analysed as a causal continuum (santāna) of momentary
phases. Recognition tracks similarity within that stream rather than strict numerical identity.

Nyāya’s strongest reply is that one substance can endure while qualities and conditions change.
This model explains ordinary recognition but risks making the substrate causally idle. Hence
kṣaṇikavāda is logically derivative when dependent origination is joined to the Buddhist criterion
of reality; it is not entailed by dependence alone.

## 6. 2020 Q7(b) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** How does Buddhism explain Self in terms of Pañcaskandhas? What is Liberation for Buddhism if there is no Soul?

**Retrieval:** cue—five aggregates plus liberation · kernel—designation, no sixth owner, cessation ·
use—name each aggregate · remediation—avoid annihilation and hidden soul.

### Model answer

Buddhism analyses the person into five aggregates (pañcaskandha): form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā),
recognition (saṃjñā), formations (saṃskāra) and consciousness (vijñāna). Each is conditioned,
impermanent and unfit to be appropriated as an eternal “I.” Exhaustive analysis finds no sixth
independent owner. The person is therefore a conventional designation on an organised causal
process, like “chariot” designating functioning parts.

Liberation does not require a soul that escapes the aggregates. Nirvāṇa is the cessation of
ignorance, craving, attachment and the suffering-producing process. The conventional person
follows the Eightfold Path; insight removes the appropriation of aggregates as “I” and “mine.”
Momentary causal continuity preserves practice without substantial identity.

The realist objection asks who is liberated if no subject persists. Buddhism replies that the
question presupposes the self under rejection: release is cessation, not a property acquired by
ātman. This account avoids both eternalism and annihilationism, though critics may doubt whether
liberation without an enduring beneficiary is fully intelligible.

## 7. 2021 Q5(d) · 10 marks

**Exact question:** Distinguish between pudgala-nairātmyavāda and dharma-nairātmyavāda with reference to Buddhist Philosophy.

**Retrieval:** cue—two scopes · kernel—person-self denied versus intrinsic nature denied ·
use—add school distribution · remediation—do not make every Abhidharma school Madhyamaka.

### Model answer

Personal no-self (pudgala-nairātmyavāda) denies a permanent person or ātman behind the five
aggregates. It is the common Buddhist minimum: “person” remains a conventional designation for a
causally ordered stream.

Phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairātmyavāda) widens the denial. It holds that even dharmas lack
intrinsic self-nature (svabhāva). Mahāyāna, especially Madhyamaka, develops this thesis through
dependent origination: what depends on causes and designation cannot exist independently.

The distinction is historically important. Vaibhāṣika-type Abhidharma realism can reject a
permanent person while treating dharmas as ultimately real. Madhyamaka denies intrinsic reality at
both levels. Thus the difference concerns the scope of anti-essentialism, not two rival immortal
self-theories. Confusing them erases real school disagreement within Buddhism.

## 8. 2021 Q7(b) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** If everything is momentary then how do the Buddhists explain the problem of memory and personal identity? Critically discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—rememberer and experiencer · kernel—trace + stream + conventional identity ·
use—add Yogācāra as school-specific · remediation—retain ownership objection.

### Model answer

Momentariness appears to separate the experiencer from the later rememberer. Buddhism answers
through a causal continuum (santāna): an experience leaves a trace that conditions a later memory
within the same connected stream. The later cognition is neither numerically identical with nor
wholly unrelated to the earlier cognition. Recognition similarly tracks causal continuity and
similarity, not an enduring substance.

Yogācāra gives a detailed but school-specific mechanism: karmic and mnemonic seeds (bīja) persist
as dispositions in changing store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna). The store is not an eternal self.
Conventional personal identity is therefore valid for language, responsibility and practice even
though ultimate analysis finds only momentary events.

Nyāya objects that memory has the first-person form “I experienced this,” which seems to require
one subject rather than connected events. Mere similarity between unrelated people would not
suffice. Buddhism correctly answers the latter problem by requiring one causal stream, but the
further question—what makes the stream an owner rather than a sequence—retains force. The theory
secures continuity more convincingly than strict numerical identity.

## 9. 2022 Q6(a) · 20 marks

**Exact question:** How compatible is Buddhist theory of momentariness with their theory of Karma? In this regard how do Buddhists respond to objections raised by their opponents? Critically discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—doer and fruit · kernel—intention conditions same stream · use—identity/difference
dilemma · remediation—separate causal explanation from desert.

### Model answer

Karma appears to require an enduring doer who later receives the fruit, whereas momentariness
denies any persisting entity. Buddhism locates karma primarily in intention (cetanā). An
intentional event conditions dispositions and later experiences in one causal continuum
(santāna). The later recipient is neither strictly identical with nor wholly different from the
earlier agent: identity would approach eternalism, total difference would make fruition arbitrary.

The flame-to-flame analogy illustrates transmission without numerical identity. Yogācāra adds
conditioned seeds in store-consciousness, while other traditions rely on causal series without
that mechanism. Conventional persons can deliberate, restrain conduct and bear consequences.

Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta object that only the identical doer can deserve the fruit. Nyāya also
uses memory and recognition to infer an enduring self. Buddhism replies that causal linkage and
appropriately inherited dispositions, not substance, ground responsibility.

The reply is strong against the claim that momentariness makes consequences impossible. Yet
causal production does not automatically prove moral desert: a later phase may be caused by an
earlier act without being numerically its agent. Therefore momentariness and karma are compatible
as causal-practical doctrines, while a fully retributive identity-based theory remains contested.

## 10. 2022 Q7(b) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** Explain Buddhist concept of Triratna and their internal relation. Critically examine the consistency of Triratnas with the Buddhist concept of No-soul (Nairātmyavāda).

**Retrieval:** cue—three functions and relation · kernel—teacher → path → community · use—conventional
practitioner · remediation—do not merely define three nouns.

### Model answer

The Three Jewels (Triratna) are Buddha, Dharma and Saṅgha. Buddha is the awakened teacher and
exemplar; Dharma is the teaching and realised path of dependent suffering and cessation; Saṅgha is
the practising community that learns, embodies and transmits Dharma. Their internal relation is
therefore soteriological: awakened exemplar → communicable path → community of realisation.

No-self (nairātmyavāda) denies a permanent soul, not conventional practitioners or institutions.
A designated person constituted by changing aggregates can take refuge, cultivate ethics and
insight, and cease ego-appropriation. Buddha is not a creator-self, Dharma is not an eternal
substance and Saṅgha is not a collective ātman.

The objection is that refuge and benefit require a persisting recipient. Buddhism replies through
causal continuity: later phases inherit the transformation initiated earlier. This makes Triratna
fully consistent with no-self at the conventional and practical level. The residual question is
whether a stream without an owner can literally receive benefit, but that is the general identity
objection, not a special inconsistency among the Three Jewels.

## 11. 2023 Q8(a) · 20 marks

**Exact question:** “Ignorance of dependent origination is suffering while its knowledge is cessation of suffering.” Present an account of Buddhist soteriology in the light of above statement.

**Retrieval:** cue—quote controls structure · kernel—Four Truths + forward/reverse links + path ·
use—show knowledge as transformation · remediation—do not equate liberation with information.

### Model answer

The statement condenses Buddhist soteriology. Ignorance (avidyā) misreads conditioned aggregates
as enduring “I” and “mine.” Through formations, consciousness, contact and feeling, this error
enables craving, grasping, becoming, birth and ageing-and-death. Thus ignorance of dependent
origination is not merely intellectual absence; it is the appropriation that sustains suffering.

The Four Noble Truths express the same logic: suffering has a conditioned origin; therefore
cessation is possible; the Eightfold Path executes it. Right view understands conditionality,
ethical discipline restrains craving-driven conduct, and meditation transforms attention.
Knowledge becomes liberating when feeling is experienced without conversion into craving and
grasping. Read in reverse, the cessation of ignorance weakens formations and the entire
suffering-process.

The practical Middle Path avoids indulgence and self-mortification; the doctrinal middle avoids an
eternal self and nihilistic discontinuity. No-self prevents appropriation, while momentariness
makes transformation possible.

A critic may say knowledge alone cannot end embodied suffering. Buddhism agrees if “knowledge”
means verbal information. Liberating wisdom is cultivated insight integrated with conduct and
meditation. Cessation is therefore the practical reversal of dependent origination, not escape by
an immortal soul.

## 12. 2024 Q6(b) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** How do the two schools of Buddhism arrive at two opposed conclusions, namely “everything is void” and “everything is real” from the same doctrine of Pratītyasamutpāda? Answer with arguments.

**Retrieval:** cue—name both schools · kernel—Madhyamaka anti-essentialism versus Vaibhāṣika dharma
realism · use—different senses of real · remediation—do not call either conclusion unqualified.

### Model answer

The opposed conclusions arise because Madhyamaka and Vaibhāṣika ask different questions of
dependent origination.

Madhyamaka argues that whatever arises through conditions cannot possess intrinsic nature
(svabhāva). Dependence and self-existence are incompatible; hence all dharmas are empty (śūnya).
“Void” means empty of own-being, not causally absent. The two truths preserve conventional
functioning while denying ultimate essence.

Vaibhāṣika, by contrast, treats causal efficacy as evidence that dharmas are real. External objects
are directly perceived, and the Sarvāstivāda formula “all exists” grants past, present and future
dharmas modes of existence, though the present has distinctive efficacy. Dependence therefore
shows the ordered reality of conditioned factors rather than their substantial personhood.

The disagreement turns on “real.” Vaibhāṣika means irreducible causally functioning dharmas;
Madhyamaka denies only independent intrinsic reality and accepts conventional efficacy. The
positions are opposed at the ultimate level but share rejection of a permanent self. Madhyamaka
better blocks reification; Vaibhāṣika offers a more determinate causal ontology. Neither should be
reduced to “nothing exists” or “everything is eternally substantial.”

## 13. 2025 Q8(c) · 15 marks

**Exact question:** Is Buddhist notion of Nirvāṇa in consonance with their conception of Kṣaṇikavāda (momentariness) and Nairātmyavāda (no-soul theory)? Critically discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—three-doctrine consistency · kernel—conditioned stream ceases; no permanent
beneficiary · use—state unconditioned nuance · remediation—do not call nirvāṇa momentary.

### Model answer

Nirvāṇa is the cessation of attachment, aversion, delusion and the suffering-process.
Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) holds that conditioned causally operative realities are momentary;
no-self (nairātmyavāda) denies an enduring owner of the aggregates. Their consonance lies in a
process account: because bondage is conditioned and impermanent, it can cease; because there is no
permanent self, liberation is not a soul’s acquired property.

A conventional person practises through one causal continuum. An arahant may extinguish
defilements while aggregates remain; final passing involves no renewed conditioned arising.
Important Theravāda/Abhidharma accounts classify nibbāna as unconditioned, so it should not be
treated as another momentary formation. Madhyamaka likewise refuses to reify either saṃsāra or
nirvāṇa.

Nyāya and Advaita object that no one remains to be liberated and no unified subject connects
bondage with release. Buddhism replies that cessation of suffering, not possession by a substance,
is the relevant achievement. The doctrines are therefore coherent within Buddhist process
metaphysics, though the persisting-beneficiary objection remains a genuine external challenge.

## 14. 2026 Q5(b) · 10 marks

**Exact question:** How does the Buddhist theory of momentariness problematize the issue of human action and responsibility? Critically discuss.

**Retrieval:** cue—action versus later agent · kernel—intention + stream + conventional normativity ·
use—separate consequence from desert · remediation—do not answer only with mechanical causation.

### Model answer

Momentariness makes agent, intention and bodily act perish as they arise. A later recipient of
consequences therefore seems not to be the actor, while no permanent owner is available under
no-self.

Buddhism replies that intentional action (cetanā) conditions later dispositions within one causal
continuum (santāna). The later phase is neither numerically identical with nor wholly disconnected
from the acting phase. Conventional persons can deliberate, respond to reasons, restrain harm and
follow right action; Yogācāra’s conditioned seeds provide one school-specific continuity model.

This grounds practical responsibility and differential consequences without an eternal self.
Nevertheless, causal connection alone may not prove that a later phase deserves blame or reward.
Nyāya’s identity-of-doer-and-enjoyer objection therefore retains force. Buddhism gives a coherent
forward-looking account of agency and transformation, but its retributive theory of desert remains
philosophically contested.

# ORIGINAL SOLVED MAINS PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish early Buddhist impermanence from the later theory of momentariness. Why
does the distinction matter? Answer in about 150 words.

### Model answer

Impermanence (anitya) is the early teaching that conditioned formations arise, alter and cease.
Momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) is the stronger scholastic thesis that whatever is causally real lasts
only a moment. The first follows from dependence on changing conditions; the second requires the
causal-efficacy argument.

Dharmakīrti’s line defines the real through causal work. A wholly permanent cause cannot produce
successively without changing and cannot produce all effects simultaneously without exhausting
its capacity. What appears stable is therefore a causal continuum of momentary phases.

The distinction matters because a rival can accept that a jar is impermanent yet claim it endures
for an interval. Writing that dependent origination alone proves one-moment duration hides the
central premise and ignores Nyāya’s enduring-substance-plus-auxiliaries reply. Momentariness is thus
a reasoned radicalisation of impermanence, not its mere synonym.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** “Buddhist no-self abolishes neither agency nor ethical cultivation.” Critically
examine. Answer in about 200 words.

### Model answer

No-self denies a permanent independent owner, not the conventionally designated person. The five
aggregates form a causally ordered stream in which intentions, habits and consequences arise.
Intentional action (cetanā) therefore remains efficacious: right speech, action and livelihood can
weaken craving, while harmful choices condition later suffering.

The Noble Eightfold Path presupposes practical agency. A learner hears Dharma, forms intentions,
trains attention and changes conduct. Memory and responsibility are explained through traces in
one continuum; Yogācāra’s store-consciousness and seeds offer a detailed but non-universal model.

Nyāya objects that deliberation and desert require one enduring agent. Buddhism answers that
strict identity would reintroduce eternalism, whereas total difference would destroy continuity;
the stream is neither. This secures forward-looking responsibility and cultivation. Yet the reply
more clearly explains causal transformation than why a later phase deserves retributive blame for
an earlier phase. No-self therefore preserves agency as conditioned practical functioning, while
leaving identity-based desert contested.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** Compare Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra and Madhyamaka on external reality,
cognition and intrinsic nature. Answer in about 300 words.

### Model answer

The four-school matrix is best read as an argumentative sequence, not a complete institutional
history.

Vaibhāṣika defends direct external realism: external dharmas are directly perceived. Its
Sarvāstivāda setting gives past, present and future dharmas modes of existence, although present
dharmas possess distinctive efficacy. It preserves no-self while granting robust dharma-reality.

Sautrāntika remains an external realist but makes access representational. Cognition directly knows
a mental presentation and infers its external cause. It rejects the stronger three-time ontology
and privileges present causal occurrence. Its challenge is explaining how representation warrants
the external inference.

Yogācāra argues that the external posit is unnecessary. Dreams show that appearance does not
guarantee exteriority; atom-analysis pressures material reduction. Store-consciousness and seeds
explain patterned, shared experience, while the three natures distinguish imagined duality,
dependent cognition and its consummate non-duality. The residual problem is avoiding solipsism or
a disguised substantial store.

Madhyamaka then criticises reification of consciousness itself. Whatever arises dependently lacks
intrinsic nature; hence objects, cognitions and emptiness are empty. Two truths preserve
conventional causation and practice while denying ultimate svabhāva. The realist charges nihilism
and self-refutation; Madhyamaka replies through non-implicative negation.

The sequence moves from direct realism to representational realism, consciousness-only and
anti-essentialism. Each gains critical reach while facing a corresponding explanatory cost.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Diagnostic and path spine

- Four Truths form one causal sequence: suffering → conditioned origin → cessation → path.
- Practical Middle Path rejects indulgence and self-mortification through the Eightfold Path.
- Wisdom: right view/intention; ethics: right speech/action/livelihood; meditation: right
  effort/mindfulness/concentration.
- Doctrinal Middle Path rejects eternalism and annihilationism.
- Three marks: conditioned impermanence, unsatisfactoriness when grasped, no permanent owner.
- Early impermanence must not be equated without argument with later one-moment duration.

## 2. Dependent origination and cessation

- Formula: this being, that becomes; this ceasing, that ceases.
- Twelve links: ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six bases → contact →
  feeling → craving → grasping → becoming → birth → ageing-and-death.
- Three readings: three-life, present-process, structural-soteriological.
- Ignorance is wrong appropriation of changing aggregates as “I/mine.”
- Reverse cessation targets craving, grasping and becoming through insight and discipline.
- The doctrine diagnoses maintained suffering; it need not discover a first cosmic cause.

## 3. Momentariness and causal reality

- Real = causally efficacious (arthakriyā); whatever is real is momentary.
- Simultaneity horn: a fully efficacious permanent cause should produce all effects at once.
- Succession horn: different effects over time require different causal profiles.
- Auxiliary objection: enduring cause acts with helpers.
- Buddhist reply: changing helpers carry the operative difference.
- Qualified verdict: momentariness is a scholastic development of dependence plus causal efficacy,
  not a direct synonym for impermanence.

## 4. Stream, memory, karma and responsibility

- Causal continuum (santāna) = continuity without strict identity.
- Memory = later cognition caused by earlier trace in the same stream.
- Recognition is conventionally valid; similarity alone across unrelated streams is insufficient.
- Intention (cetanā) conditions later dispositions and consequences.
- Later recipient is neither strictly identical nor wholly different.
- Store-consciousness and seeds are a conditioned Yogācāra mechanism, not an eternal soul.
- Practical agency survives; identity-based moral desert remains the strongest residual problem.

## 5. No-self and personhood

- Five aggregates: form, feeling, recognition, formations, consciousness.
- No sixth independent owner is found.
- Person = valid conventional designation, not sheer fiction.
- Personal no-self denies enduring person-substance.
- Phenomenon no-self denies intrinsic nature in dharmas; Madhyamaka stresses it.
- Realist Abhidharma can accept person-no-self while affirming dharma-reality.
- Puggalavāda is an internal continuity pressure-point, not the orthodox default.

## 6. Three Jewels and cessation

- Buddha = awakened exemplar; Dharma = communicable path; Saṅgha = practising/transmitting community.
- Internal relation: exemplar → teaching/realisation → community.
- Conventional practitioners can take refuge without an eternal soul.
- Nirvāṇa ends greed, aversion and delusion; it is not a place possessed by a self.
- Important traditions classify nibbāna as unconditioned; do not call it a momentary formation.
- Attainment while alive can coexist with remaining aggregates; final passing brings no renewed
  conditioned arising.
- Madhyamaka denies independent substantiality to both saṃsāra and nirvāṇa.

## 7. Four-school comparison

| School | External world/access | Distinctive ontology | Primary trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vaibhāṣika | real and directly perceived | dharma realism; qualified three-time existence | “all exists” ≠ equal causal activity |
| Sautrāntika | real but inferred through representation | presentist causal realism | representation does not automatically prove cause |
| Yogācāra | independent external object denied/not established | cognition-stream, seeds, three natures | not private fantasy |
| Madhyamaka | conventional function affirmed | no dharma has svabhāva | emptiness is not nihilism |

- Argumentative sequence: direct realism → representational realism → consciousness-only →
  anti-essentialism.
- Use as a philosophical escalation, not a simple institutional genealogy.

## 8. Madhyamaka answer spine

- Dependence is incompatible with independent intrinsic nature.
- Therefore dependently arisen things are empty of svabhāva.
- Four corners are denied when intrinsic predication is assumed.
- Non-implicative negation need not posit a fifth entity.
- Conventional truth preserves causation, persons, ethics and path.
- Ultimate truth denies intrinsic nature.
- Emptiness itself is empty; never turn it into Brahman-like ground.
- Residual: self-reference and the status of Madhyamaka discourse remain contested.

## 9. Yogācāra answer spine

- Appearance does not guarantee exteriority.
- Dream/illusion provides a limited underdetermination argument.
- Atom critique attacks a particular material reduction.
- Three natures: imagined duality, dependent cognition, consummate absence of that duality.
- Store-consciousness carries conditioned seeds and supports shared patterns.
- Sautrāntika reply: independent causes best explain resistance and intersubjectivity.
- Verdict: powerful critique of naïve realism; full metaphysical idealism requires the positive
  seed-model.

## 10. Epistemology and unanswered questions

- Two means: perception and inference.
- Perception: non-conceptual and non-erroneous; object is unique particular.
- Inference handles conceptual general character.
- Exclusion (apoha) explains general terms without real universals.
- Mīmāṃsā/Nyāya objection: exclusion cannot yield positive determination without circularity.
- Means and result are one momentary cognitive event rather than a permanent agent-instrument-result
  triad.
- Unanswered questions are liberation-irrelevant and often presuppositionally defective.
- Poisoned-arrow lesson: do not let speculative framing displace treatment of suffering.

## 11. Rival objections and ready replies

- **Nyāya memory:** stream and trace; residual ownership problem.
- **Nyāya causation:** helpers do decisive work; residual substance model.
- **Mīmāṃsā universals:** apoha and causal convergence; residual positive content.
- **Advaita witness:** conventional stream/two truths; residual unity of experience.
- **Nihilism:** empty of own-being, not of function; residual self-reference.
- **Desert:** causal connection and intention; residual identity-based justice.
- **Cārvāka comparison:** both deny immortal ātman; Buddhism preserves non-bodily karmic continuity.
