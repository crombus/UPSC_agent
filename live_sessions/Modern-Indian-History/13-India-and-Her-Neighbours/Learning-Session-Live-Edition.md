## Lesson 1 - Why a Frontier Became a System

Progress: 1/16 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: British Indian frontier and foreign-policy logic

### Visual: the outward-moving security perimeter

```text
Company conquest inside India
          |
political consolidation + roads + surveys + larger standing army
          |
official demand for a "secure" geographical edge
          |
neighbouring kingdom treated as a strategic zone
          |
mission / treaty / subsidy / resident / war / annexation
          |
new outer edge creates a new security problem
```

The central intuition is simple: once the Company-state controlled a large part of the subcontinent, officials stopped seeing Nepal, Burma or Afghanistan as merely foreign countries. They began to see each as part of the defence of British India. That change did not make aggression inevitable, but it made intervention easier to justify.

### 1. Three motives that worked together

**First, security.** Officials wanted defensible approaches, reliable passes and governments that would not assist a rival. "Security" could mean protecting an existing border, but it could also mean pushing influence beyond it.

**Second, commerce.** Burma's teak, river routes, markets and possible route to western China mattered. Himalayan treaties also promised trade routes. Economic interests rarely acted alone, but they shaped where coercion became attractive.

**Third, imperial rivalry and prestige.** London and Calcutta feared that France or Russia might acquire influence near India. Prestige mattered because colonial authority depended partly on the belief that an Asian state could not defy British demands without consequence.

### 2. Why this was not the foreign policy of a free India

Bipan Chandra's key distinction is between a country's foreign policy serving its people and a colonial government's policy serving the ruling power. British Indian policy was directed by imperial needs. Indian soldiers fought, Indian taxpayers paid, and London gained strategic advantages.

This does not mean every border dispute was fabricated. Borders were often undefined; neighbouring states also expanded; merchants faced real restrictions; rulers sought rival alliances. The analytical question is who defined the remedy and whose resources bore its cost.

### 3. Expansion as a security chain

```text
uncertain border
   -> demand for influence across it
   -> resistance by neighbouring authority
   -> coercive settlement
   -> British responsibility for a wider perimeter
   -> fresh uncertainty
```

This is the recurring frontier paradox: policy described as defensive could be expansionist in effect.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The British simply reacted to expanding neighbours and dangerous border disorder.

**Reply:** Some threats were real, especially where two expanding powers met. Yet the chosen response often exceeded border settlement. Commercial privileges, control of foreign relations, indemnities, residents and annexation show that defence was one motive within a wider imperial programme.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims trap:** "Forward policy" is not identical with annexation. It may involve missions, posts, subsidies or control of diplomacy.
- **Mains thesis:** British frontier policy was defensive in stated purpose, differentiated in method and frequently expansionist in consequence.
- **Boundary caution:** Do not use present-day borders as if they already existed in fixed national form during the nineteenth century.

### Revision notes

1. Frontier policy joined security, commerce, prestige and European rivalry.
2. Colonial foreign policy served imperial rather than representative Indian priorities.
3. Indian troops and revenues sustained policy beyond India's settled provinces.
4. Undefined frontiers created disputes but did not dictate aggressive remedies.
5. A buffer preserved formal autonomy while limiting strategic freedom.
6. A resident represented continuing political influence.
7. Annexation was only one instrument among several.
8. Defensive language could produce an outward-moving perimeter.
9. Regional rulers retained choices and were not passive pieces.
10. Present legal status must not be inferred from a colonial treaty.

- **C — Incorrect:** Commercial demands often became connected to political pressure, treaty enforcement and strategic influence.
- **D — Incorrect:** A settled line could generate new concerns about passes, communities and rival influence beyond it.

**B.** Imperial priorities prevailed, while Indian revenue and soldiers bore much of the burden.

**D.** Indian councils controlled campaign finance, although the Governor-General retained authority over diplomatic negotiations.

**A.** Commercial treaties, reciprocal consulates, neutral arbitration, jointly supervised border patrols and equal access to frontier markets
**B.** Temporary expeditions, followed by renunciation of residents, subsidies and influence over external relations
**C.** War, political residents, subsidies, diplomatic restrictions, boundary agreements and selective territorial annexation in practice
**D.** Annexation and ordinary provincial administration as the standard outcome in every neighbouring region

- **B — Incorrect:** Expeditions were often followed by continuing political obligations rather than a consistent renunciation of influence.
- **C — Correct:** British policy combined coercion, indirect influence, line-making and territorial absorption in different theatres.
- **D — Incorrect:** Nepal and Afghanistan remained separate states, while Tibet, Sikkim and Bhutan followed other arrangements.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why British Indian frontier policy was "defensive in conception but expansionist in effect."

**Model answer:**
British officials treated neighbouring territories as an outer defence ring for the Indian empire. The stated objective was to prevent hostile influence, settle uncertain borders and protect routes. Yet each security measure enlarged British commitments. The Anglo-Nepalese settlement carried British influence to the Himalayas; the Burmese wars converted disputed approaches and commercial demands into staged annexation; intervention in Afghanistan sought a compliant buffer and produced repeated occupation; and missions in Tibet attempted to exclude rival influence. The pattern was therefore circular: an uncertain frontier encouraged intervention, intervention created a wider perimeter, and the wider perimeter generated another demand for control. This did not make every threat imaginary. Burmese expansion, Afghan instability and Russian movement in Central Asia were real factors. However, defence cannot alone explain residents, indemnities, commercial privileges, diplomatic restrictions and annexations. Colonial frontier policy was thus defensive in vocabulary, imperial in decision-making and expansionist in its consequences.

**Scoring guidance:** 2 marks for the paradox; 4 for a causal chain with at least three theatres; 2 for qualification; 2 for a balanced verdict.

## Lesson 2 - Great Game, Buffers and Competing Doctrines

Progress: 2/16 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Great Game context, forward policy and close-border alternatives

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: three policy choices

```text
defend the settled edge ---- support an independent buffer ---- move influence outward
      close-border                 buffer policy                    forward policy
        lower cost               strategic distance              greater leverage
           |                            |                                |
   less outer control          partner retains agency          longer, exposed commitments
```

| Policy model | Basic intuition | Principal gain | Principal cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Close-border | Hold the administered edge and punish raids when necessary | Lower permanent commitment | Influence beyond the edge may pass to a rival |
| Buffer support | Keep a friendly, sufficiently strong neighbour between India and a rival | Distance without occupation | The buffer retains agency and may resist control |
| Forward policy | Move missions, posts, troops or political influence toward the outer strategic line | Earlier warning and leverage | Costly supply lines, resistance and recurring warfare |

The Great Game was the conventional name for Anglo-Russian competition in Central Asia. It explains British threat perception, but it must not become a magic word that explains every event.

### 1. The buffer dilemma

A useful buffer had to be strong enough to resist Russia but not so independent that it rejected British wishes. That was an unstable demand. Afghan rulers wanted arms, money and guarantees while preserving equality; British officials often wanted influence without reciprocal obligations.

### 2. Forward versus close-border

The **forward school** argued that waiting at the Indian edge surrendered initiative. It favoured political agents, strategic posts, influence over passes and, in some phases, control of a neighbour's external relations.

The **close-border school** argued that the mountains, deserts and tribal zones punished occupying armies. It preferred a defended settled border, mobile retaliation and limited relations with rulers beyond it.

"Masterly inactivity," associated especially with the Lawrence-era preference for non-interference in Afghan succession struggles, was not indifference. It meant refusing costly intervention while strengthening a friendly buffer when useful.

### 3. Persia and Russia: necessary context, not a second syllabus

Persian pressure on Herat and Russian diplomacy mattered because Herat was imagined as an approach to Afghanistan and India. Russian expansion in Central Asia later sharpened British anxiety. But Afghan politics cannot be reduced to two European players: succession, tribal coalitions, relations among Kabul, Kandahar and Herat, and the policies of Afghan rulers shaped outcomes.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since Russia did expand across Central Asia, forward policy was rational anticipation rather than imperial overreach.

**Reply:** Anticipation can be rational without making every intervention prudent. The First Afghan War damaged the very buffer it claimed to secure. Threat assessment, feasible logistics and regional legitimacy mattered as much as the existence of a rival.

### UPSC application

- Distinguish **threat**, **threat perception** and **policy choice**.
- Do not say "close-border meant no action"; it could include punitive expeditions.
- Do not say "forward policy meant annexation"; Afghanistan and Tibet show influence-seeking without intended provincial absorption.

### Revision notes

1. Great Game describes Anglo-Russian rivalry, not a single official blueprint.
2. Afghanistan was valued for strategic depth and Central Asian access.
3. A buffer must possess capacity as well as formal independence.
4. British support often came with demands for diplomatic influence.
5. Close-border policy limited permanent commitments beyond the settled edge.
6. Forward policy moved influence outward through varied instruments.
7. Masterly inactivity was selective non-interference, not passivity.
8. Russian expansion was real; its immediate danger was debated.
9. Persian and Herat questions belong only as supporting context here.
10. Afghan and tribal agency prevents a two-player deterministic account.

**D.** It could extend missions, posts and diplomatic control without annexing the neighbouring state.

- **B — Incorrect:** Forward policy concerned the outward location of influence, not a transfer of decision-making from India to London.
- **C — Incorrect:** Some forward policies sought influence or access without permanent occupation of the entire outer line.
- **D — Correct:** Forward policy described outward strategic influence and did not necessarily require formal annexation.

**A.** Selective non-interference in Afghan succession struggles while supporting a viable independent buffer
**B.** Recognition of an Afghan ruler only after he accepted a permanent British mission in Kabul
**C.** Withdrawal of subsidies and diplomacy until Afghanistan established control over the Khyber frontier

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The Great Game explains British anxiety, but not British policy by itself." Discuss.

**Model answer:**
The Great Game supplies an essential geopolitical background to British Indian policy. Russian expansion in Central Asia, Persian pressure around Herat and the possibility of rival diplomatic influence in Kabul or Lhasa made the north-western and Himalayan approaches appear vulnerable. Afghanistan consequently acquired value as a buffer, while Tibet drew Curzon's attention as a possible channel of Russian influence.

Yet rivalry did not dictate one inevitable policy. British officials alternated among intervention, non-interference, subsidy, diplomatic control and defended-border approaches. Auckland invaded Afghanistan in 1839 to install Shah Shuja; Lawrence later preferred non-interference and support for a viable Afghan ruler; Lytton returned to coercion in 1878; Ripon again withdrew from an intrusive resident system. The same external rivalry therefore produced opposing doctrines.

Regional agency also mattered. Dost Muhammad sought a reciprocal alliance, Sher Ali resisted unilateral demands, Afghan resistance destroyed occupation, and Tibetan authorities tried to preserve isolation. Logistics, finance, prestige and British commercial ambition shaped decisions alongside Russia.

The Great Game should therefore be treated as a field of perceived danger, not as a deterministic command. It explains why Afghanistan and Tibet mattered; it does not by itself explain why particular officials chose war rather than negotiation or why those choices repeatedly failed.

**Scoring guidance:** 3 marks for defining the Great Game; 5 for policy variation; 4 for regional agency and other motives; 3 for a qualified conclusion.

## Lesson 3 - Nepal and the Gorkha Encounter

### Visual: from competing expansion to negotiated relationship

```text
Gorkha unification and expansion
Bhutan ---------------- Nepal ---------------- Sutlej
                         |
              southward pressure into Tarai
                         |
British acquisition of Gorakhpur + ill-defined border
                         |
1814-16 war -> hard campaigning -> Sugauli settlement
                         |
territorial cessions + resident + recruitment + durable coexistence
```

### 1. Why war began

The Gorkha kingdom had expanded east and west after conquering the Kathmandu valley in 1768. British power was also moving north after acquiring Gorakhpur. Two expanding systems met across poorly marked terrain. A police clash in 1814 triggered war, but the deeper cause was overlapping frontier expansion.

### 2. Why the campaign surprised the British

British officials expected easy victory along a very long front. Gorkha troops used difficult terrain, defended forts and demonstrated discipline. Initial reverses challenged assumptions of effortless military superiority. British advantages in men, finance and material eventually prevailed; Kumaon was occupied and Amar Singh Thapa surrendered in 1815.

### 3. Treaty of Sugauli, 1816

Use **Sugauli** as the preferred spelling; **Sagauli** appears as a common variant.

| Settlement feature | Historical meaning |
|---|---|
| Nepal accepted a British resident | Continuing diplomatic influence, not annexation |
| Garhwal and Kumaon were ceded | British Indian reach extended to the Himalayan belt |
| Claims to parts of the Tarai were abandoned | Border and revenue consequences |
| Nepal withdrew from Sikkim | Reordered eastern Himalayan relations |

The settlement also opened access to hill-station sites and improved British possibilities for trans-Himalayan trade.

### 4. Gurkha recruitment and the post-war compact

British admiration for Gorkha fighting capacity became a recruitment relationship. This was not simply "friendship after bravery." Recruitment served the manpower needs of the colonial army, while Nepal preserved state independence and obtained a relatively stable external relationship.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling Nepal a buffer understates its continued sovereignty and diplomatic calculation.

**Reply:** Correct. "Buffer" describes British strategic use, not Nepal's whole identity. Nepal retained its dynasty, internal authority and room for choice. The term is useful only when paired with regional agency.

### UPSC application

- **Treaty trap:** Sugauli - Nepal - 1816; Yandabo - Burma - 1826; Gandamak - Afghanistan - 1879.
- **Comparison:** Nepal was treaty-managed and not annexed like Burma.
- **Analytical line:** Defeat produced unequal adjustment, but also a durable relationship based on mutual recognition of military capacity.

### Revision notes

1. Gorkha expansion and British expansion met across an uncertain border.
2. The war lasted from 1814 to 1816.
3. Terrain and Gorkha resistance defeated British expectations of a quick campaign.
4. Material superiority eventually favoured the British.
5. Sugauli brought territorial cessions and a British resident.
6. Nepal remained a sovereign kingdom rather than a British Indian province.
7. British India reached Kumaon and Garhwal.
8. Nepal withdrew from Sikkim.
9. Gurkha recruitment strengthened the colonial army.
10. Buffer is a British strategic description, not a denial of Nepalese agency.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Assess the significance of the Treaty of Sugauli in reshaping British-Nepal relations.

**Model answer:**
The Treaty of Sugauli ended the Anglo-Nepalese War of 1814-16 and converted a violent encounter between two expanding powers into an unequal but durable relationship. Nepal accepted a British resident, ceded Garhwal and Kumaon, withdrew claims from parts of the Tarai and from Sikkim, while British India reached the Himalayan belt. These gains improved strategic depth, access to hill regions and prospects for trans-Himalayan trade. The settlement did not annex Nepal. Its monarchy and internal administration survived, allowing the kingdom to remain a strategically useful but politically active neighbour. A further consequence was Gurkha recruitment into the British Indian army, through which battlefield respect was transformed into a manpower relationship serving colonial warfare. The treaty should therefore be read neither as equal accommodation nor as complete subjugation. It established British predominance while preserving a Nepalese state whose cooperation had to be cultivated.

## Lesson 4 - First Anglo-Burmese War: Border Collision and Yandabo

### Visual: converging expansion on the eastern frontier

```text
Burmese consolidation under Alaungpaya's dynasty
      -> Arakan (1785) -> Manipur (1813) -> Assam (1822)
                              |
British Bengal -> Chittagong -> Cachar and Jaintia influence
                              |
refugees + cross-border pursuit + Shahpuri dispute
                              |
First Anglo-Burmese War, 1824-26
                              |
Treaty of Yandabo: cessions, compensation, recognition, resident
```

### 1. A real frontier conflict, enlarged by imperial aims

Burma and British India became neighbours as both expanded. Arakanese refugees operated from British territory; Burmese pursuit crossed uncertain boundaries; Manipur, Assam, Cachar and Jaintia became contested strategic spaces. The Shahpuri island dispute sharpened tension.

Yet British policy was not only reactive. Officials had long sought a commercial treaty, exclusion of French traders and the weakening of a strong eastern neighbour. Burmese rulers also misjudged British capacity and expected wider Indian resistance.

### 2. Course and cost of war

War was declared in February 1824. A seaborne force occupied Rangoon, while operations expelled Burmese forces from Assam, Cachar, Manipur and Arakan. Burmese resistance, jungle warfare, monsoon conditions and disease made victory extremely costly. Disease killed far more British-led troops than battlefield action.

### 3. Treaty of Yandabo, February 1826

| Term | Consequence |
|---|---|
| One crore rupees indemnity | Heavy fiscal burden on Burma |
| Arakan and Tenasserim ceded | Burma lost much of its coastal reach |
| Claims to Assam, Cachar and Jaintia abandoned | British influence expanded in the north-east |
| Manipur recognised as independent | A strategic intermediate polity was restored |
| Commercial treaty and British resident accepted | Continuing access and influence |

Yandabo did not annex all Burma. It created a coastal and political base for later expansion.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Burmese expansion reached Assam and threatened British interests, the war was simply defensive.

**Reply:** Burmese expansion and border incidents were substantial causes. But British commercial objectives, rejection of compromise and the scale of treaty extraction show how defence and expansion fused.

### UPSC application

- Remember **Yandabo = First Burmese War = 1826**.
- Do not attach Yandabo to the annexation of Upper Burma in 1885.
- For Mains, structure: border collision -> commercial strategy -> military cost -> treaty transformation.

### Revision notes

1. Burma and British India became neighbouring expanding powers.
2. Burmese control extended into Arakan, Manipur and Assam.
3. Refugee raids and pursuit complicated the Chittagong-Arakan frontier.
4. Shahpuri island became an immediate dispute.
5. War began in 1824.
6. Rangoon was occupied by a seaborne expedition.
7. Terrain, rain and disease imposed severe losses.
8. Yandabo was signed in 1826.
9. Arakan and Tenasserim were ceded.
10. The treaty created a platform for later British penetration.

### 1. Second war, 1852: coercion without a peace treaty

British merchants complained of restrictions and treatment at Rangoon. Dalhousie sent Commodore Lambert with warships. The Burmese court recalled the governor and offered investigation, but Lambert escalated the crisis through blockade and attack. A British expedition captured Rangoon, Bassein, Pegu and Prome.

There was no formal peace treaty. Britain annexed Pegu, completing control of Burma's coastline and sea trade. Popular guerrilla resistance continued for years. Indian soldiers did much of the fighting and Indian revenues met the expense.

### 2. Third war, 1885: sovereignty treated as defiance

Upper Burma sought commercial and political relations with France and tried to regulate foreign concessions. British commercial groups wanted fuller access to teak, river trade and the route toward China. The Bombay-Burma Trading Corporation dispute supplied a pretext. British demands included control over Burma's external relations - a demand incompatible with independent statehood.

King Thibaw surrendered quickly in November 1885, but conquest was not pacification. Tens of thousands of troops were needed for years against widespread resistance.

### 3. Annexation and administration

Upper Burma was added to the Indian empire. Burma remained administratively connected to British India until separation under the 1935 constitutional settlement took effect in 1937. Separation was not independence; Burma remained under British rule.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Burmese rulers provoked intervention by restricting commerce and seeking French support.

**Reply:** An independent state possessed the right to regulate trade and conduct diplomacy. British treatment of these acts as offences reveals the unequal imperial assumption that commercial access and strategic deference were enforceable rights.

Dost Muhammad was not simply "pro-Russian." He wanted support against multiple threats and preferred British friendship if it came on acceptable terms. Britain wanted Russian influence excluded but did not want to empower an equal Afghan partner or commit unconditionally against Ranjit Singh.

### 2. The Shah Shuja solution

Auckland chose to replace Dost Muhammad with Shah Shuja, a former ruler living under British protection. The 1838 arrangement with Ranjit Singh and Shah Shuja sought to install a ruler whose external choices would be constrained. ### 3. Occupation without legitimacy

Kabul fell, but Shah Shuja's authority rested visibly on foreign arms. Bribed coalitions and garrison power could secure entry, not durable consent. Rising costs encouraged reductions in payments to tribal intermediaries, while interference and resentment widened opposition. The Kabul uprising of November 1841 destroyed the occupation's political foundation.

### 4. Retreat and failure

The January 1842 retreat became a symbol of disaster. A later force reoccupied Kabul and demonstrated military capacity, but Britain evacuated and recognised Dost Muhammad. The war cost India heavily in money and lives and deepened Afghan distrust.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Auckland had to act once Dost Muhammad received a Russian envoy.

**Reply:** Diplomatic contact did not prove Afghan subordination to Russia. Britain's refusal of reciprocal support helped create the opening it then treated as proof of hostility.

### 1. The 1855 accommodation

The friendship treaty with Dost Muhammad promised peaceful relations, territorial respect and non-interference. Dost Muhammad remained loyal during the revolt of 1857. The relationship showed that a stable Afghan buffer could be maintained without occupation.

### 2. Masterly inactivity in practice

Lawrence resisted choosing winners in Afghan succession conflicts. He favoured recognition of effective authority, limited assistance and avoidance of permanent political agents inside Afghanistan. This was not altruistic equality; it was an economical imperial strategy after learning the cost of invasion.

### 3. Why the doctrine lost influence

From the 1870s, Russian advances, rivalry in West Asia and a wider revival of imperial expansion altered British politics. The forward school argued that a merely friendly Amir was insufficient. Britain should obtain a mission in Kabul and control Afghan foreign relations before Russia did.

Sher Ali was willing to cooperate against external danger but wanted reciprocal commitments. Britain demanded unilateral access and influence. The mismatch resembled the earlier Dost Muhammad episode.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Non-interference worked only because Russia had temporarily slowed after the Crimean War.

**Reply:** External conditions helped, but the doctrine's success also came from aligning British aims with Afghan legitimacy. It reduced the contradiction between a secure buffer and an occupied, hostile society.

- Use the word **oscillation**: policy changed with threat perception and British domestic politics.
- Do not present Lawrence's policy as anti-imperial.
- Show continuity: both schools sought imperial security; they disagreed over means and cost.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the Treaty of Gandamak as both a high point and a failure of forward policy.

**Model answer:**

### 1. The fiscal argument

Bipan Chandra emphasises that frontier and overseas policy served British imperial interests while its implementation was charged substantially to India. He records that military expenditure absorbed nearly 52 per cent of Indian revenues in 1904. This figure should be used as a source-specific illustration, not generalised to every year.

### 2. War-specific evidence

The First Afghan War cost India more than one and a half crore rupees in the book's account and caused enormous losses. The Second Burmese War was fought largely by Indian soldiers and paid from Indian revenue. Suppression after the 1885 annexation also fell on the Indian exchequer. These examples show incidence, not merely abstract "drain."

### 3. Opportunity cost and army use

Money assigned to distant campaigns could not simultaneously finance famine protection, health, education or productive works. Indian troops were also deployed as instruments of wider British power in Asia and Africa. The army defended not only inhabitants of India but the routes and prestige of a global empire.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A secure frontier benefited India, so charging Indian revenue was reasonable.

**Reply:** Some security benefit was real. The issue is proportionality and political accountability: India did not control the imperial objectives, rivalries or expansion decisions for which it paid.

- Distinguish **benefit**, **decision power** and **incidence**.
- Use exact numbers only with source qualification.

Book context: Queried - all canonical sections and Bipan Chandra's regional narratives

CA found: None used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: policy made through interaction, not unilateral design

```text
imperial plan
   + ruler's diplomacy
   + court faction
   + merchant pressure
   + local military resistance
   + terrain and disease
   + treaty wording
   + uneven enforcement
             |
actual frontier outcome
```

### 1. Regional rulers as strategists

Dost Muhammad balanced threats; Abdur Rahman bargained over autonomy; Amanullah chose a post-war moment. The Burmese court recalled officials, negotiated commerce and sought French ties. Nepal resisted hard enough to shape a post-war compact. Tibetan authorities used isolation and Chinese suzerainty defensively. These decisions did not always succeed, but they changed imperial costs and outcomes.

### 2. Treaties are settlements, not self-executing facts

Sugauli, Yandabo and Gandamak followed wars but produced different orders. A treaty's meaning depends on:

1. who signed and under what coercion;
2. whether the ruler could secure compliance;
3. which clauses were implemented or revised;
4. how local communities experienced the line or obligation;
5. what later agreements changed.

Gandamak's resident clause collapsed; Yandabo opened a path rather than completing annexation; Sugauli produced a durable state relationship.

### 3. Frontier knowledge

Maps, surveys, ethnographic labels, route reports and intelligence made territory legible to the colonial state. Knowledge could improve navigation and administration, but it also simplified fluid authority into categories useful for control. "Tribe," "pass," "buffer" and "natural frontier" were not neutral descriptions when they guided coercive policy.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Emphasising agency understates the overwhelming asymmetry of British military and fiscal power.

**Reply:** Agency is not equality. It means weaker actors still altered timing, legitimacy, implementation and cost. Recognising it explains why superior force repeatedly failed to secure intended political outcomes.

### UPSC application

- Avoid both extremes: imperial omnipotence and romantic local invincibility.
- Interpret treaties dynamically.
- Use regional agency as analysis, not decorative balance.

### Revision notes

1. Regional agency operated under unequal power.
2. Diplomacy could redirect imperial choices.
3. Resistance raised costs and damaged legitimacy.
4. Court divisions could facilitate conquest.
5. Treaties differed in durability and institutional effect.
6. Signature did not guarantee implementation.
7. Maps converted zones into administrative objects.
8. Surveys served knowledge and power together.
9. Colonial categories could simplify diverse societies.
10. Strong answers connect agency to causal outcomes.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How do regional agency and treaty interpretation revise a simple story of British frontier expansion?

### Visual: one empire, several frontier models

| Theatre | Principal British objective | Main instrument | Result | Central limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nepal | Himalayan security and access | War, cession, resident, recruitment | Unequal but durable state relationship | Nepal retained agency and sovereignty |

| Sikkim/Bhutan | Himalayan belt and routes | Cession, treaty and protectorate-style influence | Strategic predominance without uniform annexation | Continued local institutions |

### 1. Why methods differed

Where a European rival appeared near a strong or difficult-to-occupy state, Britain generally preferred buffers and external control. Where conquest seemed feasible and commercial value high, as in Burma, annexation advanced in stages. Terrain, logistics, local legitimacy and expected revenue shaped the instrument.

### 2. The strongest overall judgement

British frontier policy was not one doctrine. It was a repertoire unified by imperial priorities. Security concerns were often genuine, but "security" expanded to include commercial access, exclusion of rivals, prestige and control over a neighbour's diplomacy.

### 3. Legacy without presentism

Colonial policy left:

- mapped lines designed for imperial strategy;
- exceptional frontier administrations;
- military recruitment relationships;
- routes and provinces tied to British India;
- memories of intervention and unequal treaty;
- fiscal and military burdens borne from India.

These inheritances influenced later states, but no present boundary, sovereignty or diplomatic claim is decided merely by citing a colonial agreement.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A broad imperial interpretation makes every policy outcome look predetermined.

**Reply:** The comparative record shows contingency. Britain changed doctrine after defeat, accepted buffers where occupation failed, annexed where conditions favoured it and revised treaties under resistance and European realignment.

### UPSC application

Use a four-step answer:

```text
define the strategic problem
   -> compare instruments by theatre
   -> weigh cost, agency and failure
   -> give a bounded legacy verdict
```

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark model

**Question:** Why was Afghanistan treated differently from Burma in British Indian frontier policy? (150 words)

**Model answer:**
Afghanistan and Burma served different imperial functions. Afghanistan stood between British India and Russian expansion in Central Asia. Its difficult terrain, strong resistance and value as strategic depth made a formally independent buffer preferable to annexation. British efforts therefore centred on subsidies, rulers, missions and control of external relations. Direct occupation in 1839-42 and 1878-80 proved politically and logistically costly, forcing retreat to indirect management.

Burma offered ports, teak, markets and a possible commercial route toward China. No comparable need existed to preserve it as a buffer against a land great power. British control advanced through three wars: coastal cessions at Yandabo, annexation of Pegu in 1852 and absorption of Upper Burma in 1885. French influence supplied strategic urgency, but the outcome was provincial incorporation.

Thus policy varied because geography, rival-power context, commercial value and cost produced buffer management in Afghanistan and staged annexation in Burma.

**Scoring guidance:** 2 marks for a direct distinction; 3 each for Afghanistan and Burma; 2 for causal comparison.

## 15-mark model

**Question:** "British frontier policy solved immediate problems by creating new frontiers." Analyse. (250 words)

**Model answer:**
British frontier policy repeatedly converted local security concerns into wider commitments. The Anglo-Nepalese War settled overlapping expansion through Sugauli, but British India then acquired a direct Himalayan frontier, new trade ambitions and deeper involvement in Sikkim. Yandabo removed Burmese pressure from Assam and Manipur, yet coastal acquisitions became bases for demands that led to Pegu and finally Upper Burma.

In Afghanistan, the desire to exclude Russian influence produced the most obvious cycle. Installing Shah Shuja was meant to secure a buffer but created an occupation vulnerable to revolt. Gandamak sought greater diplomatic control, but the Kabul resident became a focus of resistance. The modified Abdur Rahman settlement was more durable precisely because Britain retreated from permanent internal presence. The Durand agreement reduced ambiguity at one level while leaving a socially complex tribal frontier requiring agents, allowances, roads and expeditions. The NWFP institutionalised rather than eliminated exceptional frontier governance.

Curzon's Tibetan mission similarly obtained immediate concessions, but wider Anglo-Russian diplomacy in 1907 limited the unilateral gain and redirected relations through China.

The proposition should not imply that every danger was invented. Expanding neighbours, uncertain borders and rival diplomacy were real. The problem was the elasticity of the remedy: security came to mean influence beyond the border, and influence generated fresh liabilities. British frontier policy was therefore tactically adaptive but strategically self-expanding.

**Scoring guidance:** 3 marks for explaining the proposition; 8 for three causal chains; 2 for qualification; 2 for verdict.

## 20-mark model

**Question:** Critically examine the role of security, commerce, imperial rivalry and regional agency in shaping British relations with India's neighbours. (250 words)

**Model answer:**
British relations with neighbouring states were shaped by four interacting forces rather than one frontier doctrine.

**Security** supplied the common language. Nepalese and Burmese expansion reached uncertain borders; Afghanistan was imagined as strategic depth; tribal routes and Tibet appeared as vulnerable approaches. **Commerce** influenced the form of intervention: Burmese teak, markets, river navigation and access toward China encouraged annexation, while Himalayan treaties sought trade routes and favourable transit.

**Imperial rivalry** raised the urgency of control. Russian expansion affected Afghan policy and Curzon's Tibetan mission; French influence mattered in Upper Burma. Yet rivalry was not deterministic. The same Russian problem produced Auckland's invasion, Lawrence's non-interference, Lytton's forward war and Ripon's indirect buffer.

**Regional agency** determined whether imperial plans survived. Gorkha resistance produced respect and a post-war compact. Afghan opposition destroyed Shah Shuja's regime and the Gandamak resident system; Amanullah secured external independence. Burmese courts negotiated and sought alternative partners, while guerrilla resistance made annexation expensive. Tibetan closure and Chinese claims constrained British penetration.

The resulting repertoire included annexation, buffer, subsidy, resident, boundary line, protectorate-style treaty and exceptional administration. Indian troops and revenue paid heavily for these policies.

Security concerns were often genuine, but colonial power expanded their meaning. Commerce identified opportunities, rivalry magnified threats and regional agency imposed limits. British policy was therefore imperial in purpose, differentiated in instrument and contingent in outcome.

**Scoring guidance:** 2 marks each for security, commerce, imperial rivalry and regional agency; 5 for interaction and named examples; 3 for Indian cost; 4 for critical synthesis and conclusion.

## Core thesis

British Indian frontier policy formed the external face of empire. It treated neighbouring territories as part of India's defence, but defined defence through British strategic, commercial and prestige interests. The result was a varied repertoire - buffer, treaty, subsidy, resident, line, mission, exceptional administration and annexation - funded heavily by Indian revenue and manpower.

## Country-wise recall

### Nepal

- Gorkha expansion met British expansion across an uncertain border.
- British setbacks exposed Gorkha military quality.
- Sugauli brought cessions, a resident and withdrawal from Sikkim.
- Nepal remained independent and became a recruitment source.

### Burma

- First War: border collision plus commercial strategy; Yandabo.
- Second War: merchant pressure and Dalhousie's escalation; no formal peace treaty; Pegu annexed.
- Third War: teak, markets, China route and French ties; Upper Burma annexed.
- Royal defeat was followed by prolonged resistance.

### Afghanistan

- Dost Muhammad wanted reciprocal support, not subordination.
- Shah Shuja's restoration lacked legitimacy.
- First War exposed political and logistical failure.
- Lawrence-era policy supported a buffer without occupation.
- Gandamak imposed external control; the resident system collapsed.
- Abdur Rahman preserved internal autonomy under external constraint.
- Amanullah secured full external independence after the 1919 war.

### North-west frontier

- Durand agreement marked spheres but did not erase social complexity.
- Agents, allowances, levies, roads and punitive expeditions created graded rule.
- NWFP was created in 1901.
- Exceptional administration economised on occupation but weakened accountability.

### Tibet

- British motives combined trade and fear of Russian influence.
- Younghusband reached Lhasa in 1904 through armed force.
- Convention terms included indemnity, Chumbi occupation and Gyantse access.
- The 1907 Anglo-Russian accommodation limited unilateral British gains.

### Sikkim and Bhutan

- Sikkim: Darjeeling 1835, coercion, 1861 treaty, 1890 recognition of British predominance.
- Bhutan: duars, 1864-65 war and cession for annual payment.
- Both reveal protectorate-style influence rather than Burmese-style annexation.

## Ten analytical lines

1. Defensive intent did not prevent expansionist consequence.
2. A strong buffer and a compliant buffer were contradictory British desires.
3. Forward policy extended influence; annexation was only one possible result.
4. Military entry was easier than political legitimacy.
5. Treaties signed under coercion required implementation and could be revised.
6. Great-power rivalry shaped perception but did not dictate one policy.
7. Regional agency altered cost, timing and institutional outcome.
8. Frontier knowledge made space legible while simplifying layered authority.
9. Indian resources paid for priorities defined by an alien state.
10. Colonial legacies matter historically without settling present legal claims.
