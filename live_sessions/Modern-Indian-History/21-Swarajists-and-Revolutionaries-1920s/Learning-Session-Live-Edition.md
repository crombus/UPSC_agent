# Swarajists and Revolutionaries of the 1920s - Learning Session Live Edition

The central problem of the 1920s was not whether colonial rule should end. It was how to continue the struggle after a mass movement had been suspended, Gandhi was imprisoned, constitutional reforms had disappointed nationalists, communal tensions were increasing, and many young activists doubted both patient construction and council politics. This session follows the three principal strategic responses in dependency order: constructive work outside the state, obstruction inside colonial legislatures, and revolutionary action against the state.

### Visual: the post-1922 strategic fork

```text
Non-Cooperation withdrawn, February 1922
                 |
 Gandhi imprisoned + mass momentum recedes
                 |
     How should nationalism remain active?
                 |
       +---------+------------------+
       |                            |
NO-CHANGERS                    PRO-CHANGERS
construct society              enter councils

prepare next struggle          expose constitutional fraud
       |                            |
       +-------- remain within Congress --------+
```

The withdrawal of Non-Cooperation created a strategic vacuum, not an ideological surrender. Congress leaders still sought Swaraj. They disagreed over the arena in which anti-colonial work could remain effective while a new mass struggle was not immediately possible.

### 1. Why the disagreement became urgent

The movement had raised expectations and then stopped before the planned Bardoli escalation. Gandhi was arrested in March 1922. Volunteers faced demoralisation, institutions created during Non-Cooperation needed support, and the legislatures established under the Government of India Act of 1919 continued to function.

The question was therefore practical:

```text
Stay outside councils
and build social capacity?
             OR
Enter councils
and turn them into arenas of resistance?
```

### 2. The Gaya Congress split

At the Gaya Congress in December 1922, C.R. Das advocated ending the boycott of legislative councils. His proposal was defeated. Das resigned the Congress presidency, and the disagreement hardened into two recognised tendencies.

The **Pro-changers**, led especially by C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru, favoured council entry. They argued that emptying the political field allowed loyalists and collaborators to occupy it. Nationalists should instead enter, obstruct official business, expose the limitations of the reforms and keep political controversy alive.

The **No-changers**, including Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and M.A. Ansari, defended the existing boycott. They feared that legislative routines, offices, patronage and personal rivalry would dilute mass nationalism. They preferred khadi, spinning, temperance, national education, Hindu-Muslim unity and removal of untouchability.

### 3. Tactical disagreement, not two nationalisms

Both sides accepted the need for independence-oriented national work and remained within the Congress. Their dispute concerned the means appropriate to a non-mass-struggle phase.

| Question | No-changer answer | Pro-changer answer |
|---|---|---|
| Where is political capacity built? | Society, villages and constructive institutions | Legislatures and public constitutional conflict |
| Main danger | Careerism and absorption by colonial institutions | Political passivity and surrender of public platforms |
| Immediate task | Prepare people for the next struggle | Discredit the reforms through obstruction |
| Relation to Congress | Work through its constructive programme | Form a party within the Congress |

**Strongest objection to Pro-change:** Entering a colonial legislature might legitimise the institution and convert agitators into parliamentarians.

**Pro-changer reply:** Participation was instrumental, not loyalist. The aim was to demonstrate that an elected Indian bloc could obstruct but could not exercise responsible power.

**Strongest objection to No-change:** Quiet constructive work could look like retreat while the government regained initiative.

**No-changer reply:** Mass struggle without disciplined organisation had already shown its danger. Durable capacity mattered more than continuous spectacle.

### UPSC application

Do not describe the split as moderates versus extremists or constitutionalists versus nationalists. State the common anti-imperialist goal, then compare arena, mechanism, risk and expected result.

**Model answer (139 words):** The dispute was tactical because both groups remained committed to Swaraj and to the Congress. The disagreement produced the Swaraj Party, altered Congress's boycott policy, created a nationalist parliamentary bloc and tested whether colonial institutions could be used against colonial rule. At the same time, retaining both wings within Congress prevented a strategic dispute from becoming an organisational rupture. It was tactical in object but consequential in institution-building and future strategy.

### Visual: entry as an anti-system tactic

```text
Contest elections
      |
enter legislatures
      |
form disciplined nationalist blocs
      |
reject or amend official proposals
      |
expose executive override and dyarchy
      |
demonstrate: representation without responsibility is inadequate
```

The apparent paradox is the key: the Swarajists entered institutions they considered fundamentally defective. Entry did not necessarily mean acceptance. Their stated purpose was to use the limited representative space to reveal the limits of representation.

### 1. Formation and identity

In December 1922, following the Gaya defeat, C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru organised the **Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party**. The working date conventionally retained is **1 January 1923**. Das served as president; Motilal Nehru was a principal secretary.

The party was not designed as a rival national congress. It accepted the Congress programme except for the boycott of legislatures and functioned as a group within Congress.

The institutional compromise developed in stages. At the **Special Congress in Delhi in September 1923**, Congress suspended propaganda against council entry and allowed Congressmen to stand as candidates and exercise the franchise. The **Swarajist manifesto of 14 October 1923**, issued before the November elections, made the party's anti-imperialist reasoning explicit: colonial reforms masked British economic and political control, so elected members would obstruct the sham system rather than administer it loyally.

### 2. Aim: wreck the reforms from within

The Swarajists proposed to:

1. contest elections;
2. enter central and provincial legislatures;
3. obstruct budgets and measures that sustained irresponsible government;
4. resist attempts to legitimise dyarchy;
5. press nationalist demands and civil liberties;
6. expose the contradiction between elected debate and executive supremacy.

The expression **"wreck the reforms from within"** did not mean physically destroy institutions. It meant make their political inadequacy visible through organised non-cooperation inside them.

### 3. Why council entry could remain anti-colonial

Under the 1919 constitutional structure, legislatures contained elected Indian members, but the executive retained decisive safeguards, certification powers and official authority. A nationalist majority could embarrass or delay government without becoming the government.

```text
Election victory ≠ responsible government
Debate power      ≠ executive control
Budget criticism ≠ sovereignty over finance
```

This gap was the Swarajist opportunity. If the government ignored elected opposition, the reforms stood exposed. If it conceded, nationalists gained limited political ground.

### 4. The built-in risk

Obstruction depended on cohesion. Once some members sought selective benefits, offices or cooperation, the party could lose its distinctive purpose. The tactic also required explaining why electioneering did not replace mass struggle.

**Strongest objection:** A party cannot indefinitely participate in an institution while denying its legitimacy.

**Reply:** Transitional tactics often use limited openings without accepting the enclosing system. The real test is whether participation changes the balance of political education or instead changes the participants.

### UPSC application

Define Swarajist council entry as **instrumental participation for obstruction and exposure**, not conventional parliamentary government and not constitutional loyalism.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** How did the Swaraj Party seek to convert constitutional limitation into anti-colonial political education?

**Model answer (162 words):** The Swaraj Party treated the legislatures created by the 1919 Act neither as sovereign institutions nor as useless spaces. C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru argued that nationalists could enter them, organise disciplined blocs, reject budgets and oppose official measures. The resulting confrontations would reveal that elected representatives could debate and even defeat proposals while the colonial executive retained decisive power.

This was political education through contradiction. If the government overrode an elected majority, the hollowness of dyarchy became visible. If it conceded, nationalists obtained a limited victory and public platform. Council speeches also kept civil liberties, fiscal criticism and self-government in official debate during a period without nationwide satyagraha.

The tactic carried risks. Election work could narrow politics to representatives, patronage could erode discipline, and obstruction without a mass movement could become repetitive. Its significance therefore lay less in transforming government than in demonstrating why the constitutional settlement was inadequate. Entry was anti-colonial only while it remained subordinate to that larger purpose.

### Visual: powers, openings and ceilings

| Legislative instrument | Swarajist use | Colonial ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Budget discussion and voting | reject grants, expose priorities | executive safeguards and restoration powers |
| Resolutions and motions | publicise nationalist criticism | often non-binding |
| Questions and speeches | demand information, record grievances | administration remained executive-controlled |
| Elections to offices | capture institutional positions | office did not transfer sovereignty |
| Alliances with other groups | outvote official supporters | coalitions could be unstable |

Obstruction was not a single dramatic vote. It was a sequence of procedural acts intended to make irresponsible government difficult and politically costly.

### 1. The logic of denying cooperation

Outside the council, Non-Cooperation had asked citizens to withdraw assistance. Inside the council, the Swarajists adapted the same principle: do not make an unrepresentative system run smoothly.

Their legislative conduct included rejecting or reducing grants, opposing government-backed bills, moving adjournments, demanding release of political prisoners, defending civil liberties and combining with independent Indian members.

### 2. Why coalition mattered

No single bloc could defeat official measures alone. The franchise was exceptionally narrow: about **6.2 million people, less than three per cent of the population**, could vote, and eligibility rules varied across provinces. Even within that restricted arena, the November 1923 result was regionally uneven. Swarajists won **42 of 101 elected Central Assembly seats**, secured a clear majority in the Central Provinces, became the largest party in Bengal, performed well in Bombay and U.P., and did poorly in Madras and Punjab amid strong caste and communal alignments.

In the Central Legislative Assembly they built a common front with **M.A. Jinnah's Independents, Liberals and individuals such as Madan Mohan Malaviya**; related coalitions appeared in several provinces. Coalition enlarged leverage but also required compromise with partners who did not share a programme of uniform, continuous and consistent obstruction.

### 3. Obstruction versus responsive cooperation

**Obstruction** meant refusing to facilitate colonial governance so long as responsible government was denied.

**Responsive cooperation** meant supporting the government where it responded to Indian demands or where cooperation might protect a sectional or practical interest. In principle, this could be a conditional tactic. In practice, it weakened the clarity of the original programme when some leaders moved closer to government.

```text
Pure obstruction: no cooperation without political advance
Conditional response: cooperate after a concrete concession
Drift into accommodation: cooperation becomes the routine
```

Madan Mohan Malaviya, Lala Lajpat Rai and N.C. Kelkar were associated with a responsive tendency that increasingly diverged from Motilal Nehru's line. Communal claims also entered these divisions.

### 4. Obstruction's democratic ambiguity

Obstruction can expose an undemocratic constitution, but permanent obstruction may also prevent useful public measures. Swarajists therefore had to distinguish resistance to executive domination from opposition for its own sake.

**Strongest objection:** Blocking budgets harmed administration and the public more than the colonial executive.

**Reply:** Under an unaccountable executive, voting supply was one of the few ways elected representatives could challenge authority. The defence is strongest when linked to a clear demand and weakest when obstruction becomes theatrical routine.

### UPSC application

Use a mechanism chain: **limited franchise and dyarchy -> elected bloc -> procedural obstruction -> executive override -> public exposure -> diminishing returns**.

Its supporters were willing to assist government when it responded to Indian demands or where cooperation appeared necessary to protect practical or sectional interests. Such flexibility could secure limited gains, but it also blurred the anti-system purpose of council entry. Once cooperation became routine or linked to communal competition, the party risked absorption into the constitutional order it had entered to expose.

The distinction is therefore one of threshold: obstruction made political advance a condition of cooperation; responsive politics lowered or varied that threshold.

### Visual: achievement at three levels

```text
ELECTORAL
strong 1923 entry into central and provincial councils
       |
PROCEDURAL
defeat or delay official business; win Assembly presidency
       |
POLITICAL
show that representation could embarrass government but not control it
```

The Swarajists performed strongly in the November 1923 elections. Bipin Chandra's text records **42 of 101 elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly**. The number is useful when carefully sourced, but the analytical point is larger: they became the leading organised nationalist bloc and, with allies, repeatedly outvoted government.

### 1. Central and provincial leverage

Their strongest positions were in the Central Legislative Assembly and in important provincial councils, including Bengal and the Central Provinces. They opposed budgets and executive proposals, supported nationalist resolutions, demanded civil liberties and demonstrated that official authority could survive elected rebuke only because the constitution protected it.

### 2. Vithalbhai Patel's election: date reconciliation

The official Lok Sabha Secretariat/Sansad profile records that **Vithalbhai J. Patel was elected President of the Central Legislative Assembly on 22 August 1925**, becoming the first Indian to hold the office. This official date controls the chronology in this session. A permitted secondary source, Bipin Chandra's *Modern India*, prints **March 1925**. That conflict is disclosed rather than silently harmonised: use **22 August 1925** for factual recall and retain March 1925 only as a source-variance note.

An Indian nationalist presiding over the chamber demonstrated that coordinated elected members could capture procedural authority even while the executive remained beyond their control.

### 3. Three issue families and measurable votes

At the centre, Swarajists repeatedly organised debate around three families:

1. **constitutional advance** toward self-government;
2. **civil liberties**, repeal of repressive laws and release of political prisoners;
3. **development of indigenous industry**.

In the first Central Assembly session, Motilal Nehru moved the national demand for framing a constitution that would transfer real power to India. It passed **64 votes to 48**. The constitutional demand was reiterated in **September 1925** and passed **72 votes to 45**. These were not cotton-excise votes: they measured legislative support for constitutional advance. Their practical ceiling was equally important—the executive did not become responsible to the Assembly.

Swarajist and wider Indian commercial pressure also formed part of the campaign against the cotton excise duty. The Government suspended its levy with effect from **1 December 1925**. Use this only as a bounded indigenous-industry example. The precise permitted record is the official *Legislative Assembly Debates*, **3 February 1926, Vol. VII, No. 9**, which refers to the Governor-General's notification suspending the levy: `https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/762736/1/clad_02_04_03-02-1926.pdf`. It does not prove that Swarajists alone caused the fiscal decision.

### 4. Municipalities and local bodies

During 1923-24 Congressmen also captured many municipalities and local bodies. Das became Mayor of Calcutta, with Subhas Bose as Chief Executive Officer; Vithalbhai Patel headed Bombay Corporation; Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and Jawaharlal Nehru led Ahmedabad, Patna and Allahabad municipalities respectively. No-changers joined this work because local institutions could support education, sanitation, health, anti-untouchability and khadi. Their powers were limited, but this was an important bridge between electoral politics and construction.

### 5. What counted as success?

| Criterion | Result |
|---|---|
| Replace colonial executive | No |
| Destroy dyarchy legally | No |
| Keep nationalist politics visible | Yes |
| Demonstrate reform limitations | Yes |
| Train legislators and procedural leadership | Yes |
| Mobilise masses through council work alone | Weak |
| Maintain permanent party unity | No |

The party's real achievement was **demonstrative rather than governmental**. It converted an abstract criticism - that the reforms were irresponsible - into repeated institutional evidence.

**Strongest objection:** If no policy transformation followed, obstruction was merely symbolic.

**Reply:** Symbols backed by votes, recorded defeats and executive overrides can change public understanding. Yet the objection identifies a real ceiling: exposure produces diminishing returns unless followed by wider mobilisation or constitutional change.

### UPSC application

Use a graded verdict. The Swarajists succeeded as critics, parliamentary organisers and exposers of dyarchy; they failed to convert electoral strength into responsible government or sustained mass mobilisation.

### Revision notes

1. Elections were held in November 1923.
2. Bipin Chandra records 42 of 101 elected Central Assembly seats.

No single event ended Swarajism. Its decline came from the exhaustion of a tactic, leadership loss, internal division and a changing political environment.

### 1. The ceiling became repetitive

Government could be defeated in a vote and still retain policy control. Once this fact was demonstrated, repeating the demonstration produced fewer returns. In March 1926, the Swarajists found it necessary to walk out of the Central Assembly.

### 2. Death of C.R. Das

Das died in June 1925. His authority had connected Bengal strength, all-India strategy and the party's place within Congress. His death removed the leader best placed to manage the contradiction between disciplined obstruction and tactical flexibility.

### 3. Agrarian cleavage, Responsivists and communal fracture

In Bengal, the majority within the Swaraj Party failed to support tenants against zamindars and lost pro-tenant allies, many of them Muslim. The episode shows how an anti-imperialist coalition could fracture when social interests inside it diverged.

The **Responsivists** wanted to work the reforms and hold office wherever possible. They joined government in the Central Provinces; their ranks expanded to include **N.C. Kelkar and M.R. Jayakar**, while Lala Lajpat Rai and Madan Mohan Malaviya also separated from the main party on Responsivist and communal grounds. These developments weakened the cohesion required for sustained obstruction.

### 4. Mass disconnection

Council speeches reached newspapers and political publics, but the party did not by itself bring masses or middle classes into sustained active politics. The No-changers also struggled to reverse political apathy. This is important: decline was not proof that the rival route had achieved immediate mobilisation.

### 5. Withdrawal, weak election and later walkout

To arrest parliamentary corruption and disintegration, the main leadership **decided to withdraw from the legislatures in March 1926**. This was not the final 1930 walkout and did not prevent the party from contesting again. In **November 1926** it entered the elections as a weaker, demoralised force: it won forty seats at the centre and half the Madras seats but was badly mauled in U.P., the Central Provinces, Punjab and elsewhere, and could not recreate its 1923 nationalist coalition.

Legislative resistance nevertheless continued. In **1928** the Government was defeated on the Public Safety Bill, which sought power to deport allegedly subversive foreigners; opposition ranged from nationalist militants to moderates and commercial representatives. The **final walkout in 1930** followed the Lahore Congress resolution and the launch of Civil Disobedience. Thus March 1926 marks a tactical withdrawal amid decay; 1930 marks disciplined alignment with a new mass struggle.

**Strongest objection:** Decline proves council entry was a mistake from the beginning.

**Reply:** A tactic can produce real but time-bound gains. Swarajism preserved nationalist visibility and exposed dyarchy, even though its institutional ceiling eventually exhausted it.

No-changers were not inactive. They treated the period between mass campaigns as one of social preparation. Khadi, spinning, national education, temperance, communal harmony and anti-untouchability work aimed to sustain cadres, reduce dependence and connect Swaraj with everyday conduct.

Constructive work performed three functions:

1. kept volunteers active when direct confrontation receded;
2. maintained contact with towns and villages;
3. linked political freedom to social reform and self-reliance.

Gandhi was released on health grounds on **5 February 1924**. He could not eliminate the strategic disagreement, but he encouraged both groups to remain inside Congress and work through their respective methods.

Revolutionary revival was not simply a return to pre-war methods. The new generation inherited clandestine organisation and heroic sacrifice, but increasingly sought an all-India structure, a republican political programme and later a socialist explanation of freedom.

### 1. Why two revolutionary streams re-emerged

The revolutionary revival did not arise only from disappointment after the suspension of Non-Cooperation. **Two distinct streams** developed—one across Punjab, U.P. and Bihar, the other in Bengal—and both absorbed three wider forces:

1. the postwar upsurge of working-class trade unionism;
2. the Russian Revolution and the consolidation of the Soviet state;
3. newly emerging Communist groups stressing Marxism, socialism and the proletariat.

These influences did not instantly create a common socialist doctrine. They widened the social field in which young revolutionaries thought about power, organisation and the possible agency of workers and peasants.

### 2. Formation of the HRA

The Hindustan Republican Association was formed at Kanpur in October 1924. Important organisers included Sachindranath Sanyal, Ram Prasad Bismil and Jogesh Chatterjee. Chandrashekhar Azad later provided continuity after repression.

### 3. Programme before the explicitly socialist name

The HRA aimed to overthrow colonial rule by organised armed revolution and establish a **Federal Republic of the United States of India** based on **universal adult franchise**. Its 1925 organ, *The Revolutionary*, also proposed:

- abolition of systems enabling exploitation of one person by another;
- nationalisation of railways, other major transport and communication, mines, steel, shipbuilding and other large industries;
- the electors' right to recall representatives;
- legislative control over the executive;
- organisation of labour and peasants;
- cooperative organisation in economic life.

The founding council had already resolved in October 1924 to preach social-revolutionary and communistic principles. The HRA was therefore more than a narrowly nationalist conspiracy. The 1928 socialist turn intensified, clarified and systematised tendencies already present; it did not create every social idea from nothing.

### 4. Method

```text
secret organisation
       |
arms, funds and trained cadres
       |
symbolic attacks on colonial resources
       |
attempt to inspire wider uprising
       |
republican political order
```

The HRA did not possess a mass army. Its leaders therefore relied on small conspiratorial groups and dramatic actions. The strategy assumed that courage, sacrifice and disruption could awaken broader resistance.

### 5. Bismil's reassessment and ethical distance

From the death cell, Ram Prasad Bismil urged young people to give up fixation on revolvers and pistols, leave conspiratorial work, join the **open movement**, build Hindu-Muslim unity and unite political groups under Congress leadership. He also affirmed an egalitarian, communistic outlook. This reassessment matters: internal criticism of the old method began within the Kakori generation itself.

Colonial officials commonly used "terrorist" for revolutionary organisations. Historians must identify the term as an administrative or historiographical label, explain the actors' republican and social objectives and still assess violence without romanticisation.

**Strongest objection:** A secret minority cannot claim to create a democratic republic through violence.

**Revolutionary reply:** Colonial rule itself excluded democratic consent; exemplary action could break fear, expose coercion and prepare revolt.

**Residual problem:** The method offered no reliable mechanism by which a small armed group would become accountable mass sovereignty.

### UPSC application

Separate **goal, organisation and method**. HRA was republican in declared end, conspiratorial in organisation and armed in method. Do not project HSRA's fully explicit socialist name backward to 1924.

The government used the investigation to break the organisation. Arrests extended beyond those immediately present. Bipin Chandra records seventeen long prison terms, four transportation-for-life sentences and four executions.

The four executed in 1927 were:

| Revolutionary | Chronological caution |
|---|---|
| Rajendra Nath Lahiri | executed first, on 17 December 1927 |
| Ram Prasad Bismil | executed on 19 December 1927 |
| Ashfaqulla Khan | executed on 19 December 1927 |
| Roshan Singh | executed on 19 December 1927 |

### 3. Hindu-Muslim comradeship without decorative symbolism

Bismil and Ashfaqulla are often remembered as symbols of Hindu-Muslim unity. The historical point is stronger when stated concretely: they worked within the same clandestine republican organisation, shared responsibility and faced execution in the same prosecution. Avoid turning comradeship into sentimental decoration detached from programme.

### 4. Achievement and cost

The action demonstrated courage, organisation and willingness to target colonial resources. The trial and executions widened public sympathy. Yet the state arrested or executed much of the leadership, exposing the vulnerability of conspiratorial organisations to surveillance and prosecution.

The 2025 centenary tribute shows Kakori's durable place in public memory. Commemoration must not flatten chronology or transform a failed fund-raising operation into a military uprising. Its larger political effect came through trial, sacrifice and memory.

**Strongest objection:** Martyrdom is evidence of repression, not proof that the strategy was effective.

**Reply:** Correct. Political effect must be separated from operational success. Kakori weakened the HRA organisationally while strengthening the moral and symbolic appeal of revolutionary nationalism.

### UPSC application

Use a double ledger:

```text
Political result: publicity, martyrdom and recruitment value
```

### 1. Naujawan Bharat Sabha

The **Naujawan Bharat Sabha**, founded at Lahore in **March 1926** with Bhagat Singh as founding secretary, was the open wing of revolutionary politics. It was designed for political work among **youth, peasants and workers**, and it proposed branches in villages. Under its auspices Bhagat Singh delivered political lectures using magic-lantern slides. Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev also organised the **Lahore Students Union** for open, legal work among students.

These bodies were not decorative fronts for a fixed conspiracy. They show movement toward a mass revolutionary organisation. Bhagat Singh increasingly argued that broad-based popular movements alone could make a successful revolution—revolution had to be made by the masses for the masses.

### 2. HSRA formation

On **9-10 September 1928**, revolutionaries met at Feroz Shah Kotla in Delhi and reorganised the HRA as the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association**. Chandrashekhar Azad, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Bhagwati Charan Vohra were prominent.

The added word **Socialist** represented an ideological trajectory:

| HRA emphasis | HSRA development |
|---|---|
| armed overthrow of British rule | anti-imperial revolution linked to social transformation |
| federal republic | socialist republic |
| heroic and conspiratorial action | increasing attention to workers, peasants and political propaganda |
| nationalism as primary vocabulary | class exploitation added to imperial exploitation |

This was a trajectory, not an overnight conversion of every member into an identical Marxist thinker.

### 3. Saunders action

The Simon Commission protest and the police lathi-charge associated with the death of Lala Lajpat Rai belong fully to Topic 22. Topic 21 owns the revolutionary response. In December 1928, an HSRA group intended to punish police superintendent James A. Scott. Assistant Superintendent **J.P. Saunders** was killed instead.

Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Azad were associated with the action. The revolutionaries presented it as retaliation for national humiliation and as part of a struggle against an exploitative order.

### 4. Retaliation's political problem

**Strongest defence:** Colonial violence was institutionally protected; a targeted action shattered impunity and demonstrated that repression carried a cost.

**Strongest objection:** Individual retaliation depended on mistaken identification, exposed the network and risked replacing political mobilisation with vengeance.

**Balanced assessment:** The action increased revolutionary prestige but did not create a scalable strategy for freedom. It also shows why historical explanation must not become romantic celebration.

### UPSC application

1. Naujawan Bharat Sabha was an open youth front.
2. HSRA marked a socialist-republican turn.
3. Saunders and the Assembly bomb were separate actions with different immediate purposes.

### 1. The Assembly bomb action

On **8 April 1929**, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly while protesting repressive legislation, especially the Public Safety and Trade Disputes measures. The bombs were deliberately intended not to cause indiscriminate death. The purpose was to “make the deaf hear.”

They did not use the opportunity to escape. Arrest was part of the strategy: the courtroom and press would carry their political message farther than the explosion.

This is commonly described as **propaganda by deed**. The action was designed as communication through dramatic illegality. It still involved violence and risk. Non-lethal intention should not be converted into a claim that no one could have been harmed.

### 2. Keep the proceedings separate

The **Assembly Bomb Case** arose from the April 1929 action. The **Lahore Conspiracy Case** concerned the Saunders killing and the wider HSRA network. Bhagat Singh appeared in both historical sequences, but they were not one undifferentiated trial.

### 3. Hunger strike and Jatin Das

Revolutionary prisoners demanded treatment appropriate to political prisoners, including equality in food, clothing, reading material and conditions. Hunger strike converted the prison into another political arena.

**Jatin Das died on 13 September 1929 after a 63-day fast.** His funeral procession and public mourning enlarged the issue beyond revolutionary circles. The political claim shifted from the legitimacy of a bomb to the colonial state's unequal and degrading treatment of prisoners.

### 4. Bhagat Singh's ideas

Bhagat Singh should not be reduced to a martyr portrait. His mature political thought included:

- complete independence from imperial rule;
- socialism and an end to exploitation by landlords and capitalists;
- a republic grounded in workers and peasants rather than a change of rulers;
- secular opposition to communal politics;
- rationalism and atheism as intellectual positions;
- criticism of unthinking faith and cults of personality;
- recognition that revolution meant social transformation, not perpetual bomb-throwing.

```text
Revolution for Bhagat Singh
not simply: transfer from British rulers to Indian elites
but: political freedom + social emancipation + end of exploitation
```

The famous writings and statements should be interpreted, not quoted at length without textual verification. His ideas developed under prison conditions and through engagement with socialist literature.

### 5. Strategy: achievement and limit

| Revolutionary achievement | Strategic limit |
|---|---|
| kept anti-colonial defiance visible | organisations remained numerically small |
| attracted politically restless youth | secrecy limited mass accountability |
| used trials and prison protest as public platforms | arrests repeatedly destroyed leadership |
| popularised socialist and secular vocabulary | exemplary action did not organise workers and peasants at scale |
| challenged colonial moral authority | violence enabled severe repression and public controversy |

Martyrdom broadened sympathy, but martyrdom is not itself a theory of victory.

### 6. Bengal's distinct revolutionary stream

Bengal was not an appendix to HSRA. Its revolutionaries reorganised underground while many simultaneously worked inside Congress. That two-way relationship gave them access to Congress's mass base in small towns and the countryside, while revolutionary cadres strengthened local Congress organisation. They cooperated with C.R. Das in Swarajist work. After his death, Bengal Congress divided: **Yugantar aligned with Subhas Chandra Bose's wing**, while **Anushilan aligned with J.M. Sengupta's**.

In **January 1924**, Gopinath Saha attempted to assassinate Calcutta Police Commissioner Charles Tegart but mistakenly killed another Englishman, Day. The government answered with arrests under a new ordinance, including Subhas Bose and other Congressmen; Saha was executed despite popular protest. Factional rivalry between Anushilan and Yugantar then contributed to stagnation, while younger revolutionaries formed new groups with links to active elements of both.

### 7. Surya Sen's Congress-linked organisation

Surya Sen had participated in Non-Cooperation, taught in a national school and continued Congress work despite imprisonment from 1926 to 1928. In 1929 he was secretary of the Chittagong District Congress Committee and five associates were committee members. His group—organisationally distinct from HRA-HSRA—planned a small-scale rebellion to demonstrate that coordinated armed challenge to the colonial state was possible.

### 8. The 18 April 1930 plan and action

```text
Police Armoury + Auxiliary Force Armoury
                 |
seize arms and form an armed detachment
                 |
cut telephone and telegraph links
                 |
disrupt rail communication with Bengal
                 |
hoist the national flag and proclaim provisional revolutionary authority
```

At 10 p.m. on **18 April 1930**, Ganesh Ghosh's group captured the Police Armoury and Lokenath Paul's group took the Auxiliary Force Armoury. The latter found rifles and Lewis guns but not the ammunition—a decisive operational failure. Revolutionaries disrupted communications, assembled under Surya Sen, hoisted the national flag and proclaimed a provisional revolutionary government in the name of the **Indian Republican Army, Chittagong Branch**. About sixty-five people participated.

### 9. Jalalabad and the dispersed aftermath

Unable to hold the town against expected troops, the group moved toward the hills. At **Jalalabad on 22 April**, several thousand troops surrounded them; after a fierce battle, survivors dispersed into neighbouring villages. Small groups continued attacks on government personnel and property. Villagers—many of them Muslim—provided food and shelter despite repression, helping the underground survive for years. Surya Sen was arrested on 16 February 1933 and executed on 12 January 1934.

### 10. Women, regional distinctness and limits

Women were not merely symbolic supporters. They sheltered activists, carried messages, guarded arms and fought. **Pritilata Waddedar died while leading a later raid; Kalpana Datta was arrested, tried with Surya Sen and sentenced to life imprisonment.** The Chittagong method advanced beyond individual assassination by coordinating group action against institutions and communications, though exemplary sacrifice and inspiration of youth remained central. Bengal groups also retained social limits: they did not develop an equally systematic socioeconomic programme, and revolutionaries working with Swarajists failed to support Muslim tenants against zamindars.

Chronologically, Chittagong overlaps with Civil Disobedience. Thematic ownership remains here because it completes Bengal's revolutionary development; Topic 22 uses it only to illustrate the varied regional setting of the 1930 movement.

### 11. Three strategies without false equivalence

| Strategy | Primary arena | Theory of pressure | Democratic strength | Central limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No-changer construction | society and organisation | self-discipline, alternatives, cadre | open participation and social reform | weak immediate coercive pressure |
| Swarajist obstruction | elected institutions | deny cooperation and expose constitutional limits | public debate and accountable procedure, though franchise was narrow | executive remained insulated |
| revolutionary action | clandestine cells, courts and prisons | exemplary sacrifice, disruption and propaganda | challenged fear and widened radical imagination | small-group violence lacked mass authorisation and scalability |

These methods responded to the same colonial order but were not morally or institutionally equivalent. Comparing them requires common criteria - participation, coercion, accountability, reach and result - while preserving their differences.

### 12. Legacy and the Simon bridge

By the later 1920s, nationalism had a larger strategic repertoire: social construction, legislative obstruction, youth mobilisation, revolutionary socialism and growing labour-peasant currents. The Simon Commission's exclusionary composition supplied the next unifying provocation. Topic 22 begins there.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Explain the council-entry logic of the Swaraj Party.

**Model answer (127 words):** The Swaraj Party entered colonial legislatures not because it accepted dyarchy, but because it wanted to expose its limitations. C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru feared that continued boycott would surrender public institutions to loyalists during a period without mass struggle.

Swarajists contested elections, formed blocs, opposed budgets, moved resolutions and cooperated with other Indian members. If government ignored an elected majority, executive irresponsibility became visible; if it conceded, nationalists gained limited space. Thus, entry was intended as institutional non-cooperation.

The strategy had a ceiling. Legislatures could embarrass government but not control the executive, and electoral routine encouraged coalition compromise, responsive cooperation and office-oriented politics. Council entry was therefore an effective method of political exposure and parliamentary training, but not a substitute for mass mobilisation or responsible government.

**Rubric (10):** council-entry logic 2 + manifesto/settlement evidence 2 + legislative mechanism 2 + ceiling 2 + judgement 2 = 10.
## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Compare constructive work, Swarajist obstruction and revolutionary action as responses to the political conditions of the 1920s.

**Model answer (167 words):** All three responses emerged from a common problem: sustaining anti-colonial politics after Non-Cooperation was withdrawn. They differed in arena, method and theory of change.

No-changers worked in society through khadi, education, temperance, communal harmony and anti-untouchability. Their strength was open participation and durable cadre; their weakness was limited immediate pressure on the state. Swarajists entered legislatures to reject budgets, expose dyarchy and maintain public controversy. They gained procedural victories and trained legislators, but executive safeguards and restricted franchise limited their power.

HRA and HSRA revolutionaries used clandestine organisation, targeted action, trials and prison protest to break fear and publicise republican and socialist ideas. Their sacrifice inspired youth, yet small-group violence lacked mass authorisation and repression repeatedly destroyed leadership.

These were not morally identical methods. Constructive work minimised coercion, council politics used representative procedure, and revolutionaries accepted political violence. Still, each revealed a dimension the others lacked: social preparation, institutional exposure and radical critique. The decade's significance lies in strategic diversification without a single route yet achieving freedom.

**Rubric (15):** common context 2 + constructive stream 3 + Swarajist stream 3 + revolutionary streams 3 + comparison criteria 2 + verdict 2 = 15.
## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** The revolutionary movement of the 1920s evolved from republican conspiracy toward socialist political communication. Critically examine.

**Model answer (187 words):** HRA and HSRA combined ideological continuity with important development. HRA, founded in October 1924, sought armed overthrow and a federal republic based on adult franchise. Its programme already envisaged nationalisation of major transport, mines and industry, recall, legislative control over the executive, and organisation of labour and peasants. Bismil's later appeal for open work and political unity shows reassessment within the Kakori generation.

HSRA's 1928 reorganisation made socialism and collective leadership more explicit. Naujawan Bharat Sabha worked openly among youth, workers and peasants, planned village branches, and complemented the Lahore Students Union. Bhagat Singh increasingly described revolution as mass social transformation, not permanent bomb action. Saunders revealed continuity with retaliatory violence; the Assembly action, trial and hunger strike converted sacrifice into political communication.

Bengal followed a separate organisational route. Anushilan-Yugantar cadres retained Congress links, while Surya Sen's Chittagong group attempted coordinated seizure of armouries and communications in April 1930. Jalalabad, village shelter, Pritilata Waddedar and Kalpana Datta reveal a regional experiment not commanded by HSRA.

The decade therefore produced ideological radicalisation and new public techniques, but clandestine action still lacked a reliable path to accountable mass power.

**Rubric (20):** HRA social programme 4 + Bismil and open work 3 + HSRA intensification 4 + mass communication 3 + Bengal comparison 3 + limits and judgement 3 = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Strategic setting

- Non-Cooperation's withdrawal ended an immediate campaign, not the demand for Swaraj.
- Gandhi's imprisonment and political demoralisation created a problem of continuity.
- Gaya Congress, December 1922, crystallised council entry versus continued boycott.
- Pro-changers feared inactivity; No-changers feared absorption into colonial constitutionalism.
- The disagreement remained tactical because both stayed within Congress.

## 2. Swaraj Party

- Formed after Gaya; working date 1 January 1923.
- Original name: Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party.
- C.R. Das: president; Motilal Nehru: principal secretary.
- Purpose: enter councils, obstruct official business, expose dyarchy and sustain political controversy.
- Entry was instrumental participation, not acceptance of colonial legitimacy.
- Principal tools: elections, budget votes, motions, questions, speeches and alliances.
- Responsive cooperation meant conditional assistance after government response; it later diluted cohesion.

## 3. Achievements

- Strong November 1923 electoral performance.
- Bipin Chandra records 42 of 101 elected Central Assembly seats.
- Government was repeatedly outvoted with support from other Indian groups.
- Official Sansad evidence dates Vithalbhai J. Patel's election as Assembly president to 22 August 1925; the permitted Bipan passage prints March 1925.
- Legislative pressure contributed to public exposure of dyarchy and selected policy gains.
- Parliamentary procedure trained a generation of nationalist legislators.

## 4. Limits and decline

- Legislatures lacked control over the colonial executive.
- Repeated obstruction produced diminishing returns.
- Council politics did not mobilise masses at the scale of national satyagraha.
- C.R. Das's death in June 1925 weakened leadership.
- Responsive cooperation and office incentives blurred purpose.
- Communal competition divided the party and its coalitions.
- Central Assembly walkout in March 1926 marked strategic exhaustion.
- New anti-Simon and Left currents changed the political cycle.

## 5. No-changers and accommodation

- Programme: khadi, spinning, national education, temperance, communal harmony and anti-untouchability.
- Theory: build disciplined social capacity before the next mass confrontation.
- Strength: durable cadre, alternatives and village contact.
- Limit: weak immediate pressure and risk of quietism.
- Gandhi was released on 5 February 1924.
- He helped preserve both streams within Congress.
- Belgaum 1924 symbolised organisational accommodation.
- “Division of labour” is a useful retrospective description, not proof of prior harmony.

## 6. HRA

- Founded in October 1924 after an all-India revolutionary conference.
- Leaders included Sachindranath Sanyal, Ram Prasad Bismil and Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee.
- Goal: armed overthrow and a federal republic of the United States of India.
- The Revolutionary supplied manifesto-level political articulation.
- Organisation remained clandestine and cadre-based.
- Democratic limit: no clear transition from secret minority to accountable mass sovereignty.

## 7. Kakori

- Date: 9 August 1925.
- Target: government treasury carried by train near Kakori.
- Purpose: finance revolutionary organisation.
- Belonged to HRA, not HSRA.
- Government prosecution badly damaged the network.
- Rajendra Lahiri executed 17 December 1927.
- Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan and Roshan Singh executed 19 December 1927.
- Operational setback and symbolic victory must be separated.
- Official centenary tribute: PIB Release ID 2154621, 9 August 2025.

## 8. Naujawan Bharat Sabha and HSRA

- Postwar trade unionism, the Russian Revolution and Communist groups reshaped both revolutionary streams.
- HRA already demanded adult franchise, nationalisation of major industries and transport, labour-peasant organisation, recall and an end to exploitative systems.
- Bismil's death-cell reassessment urged open work, unity and an end to conspiratorial weapon-fixation.
- Naujawan Bharat Sabha was founded at Lahore in March 1926 as an open youth-peasant-worker platform with planned village branches.
- Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev organised the Lahore Students Union for legal student work.
- HRA reorganised at Ferozeshah Kotla on 9-10 September 1928.
- HSRA made socialism more explicit and systematic; it intensified rather than invented the lineage's social programme.
- Bhagat Singh increasingly argued for revolution by organised masses in villages and factories.

## 9. Saunders and Assembly bomb

- Saunders killed in December 1928; Scott had been intended target.
- The action followed Lajpat Rai's death after the anti-Simon lathi-charge.
- Full Simon sequence belongs to Topic 22.
- Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt carried out the Assembly bomb action on 8 April 1929.
- The action protested repressive legislation and sought political publicity.
- Deliberate surrender made the courtroom part of the strategy.
- Symbolic intention does not erase the risk inherent in explosives.

## 10. Trials, prison and martyrdom

- Assembly Bomb Case and Lahore Conspiracy Case were separate proceedings.
- Prisoners demanded equal food, clothing, reading materials and dignified political treatment.
- Jatin Das died on 13 September 1929 after a 63-day fast.
- Hunger strike widened revolutionary politics into a civil-liberties and prison-justice issue.
- Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were executed on 23 March 1931.
- Chandrashekhar Azad died in a police encounter in February 1931.

## 11. Bhagat Singh's political ideas

- Complete independence was insufficient without social emancipation.
- Socialism meant opposition to landlord, capitalist and imperial exploitation.
- Workers and peasants were central to the future republic.
- Secularism opposed communal division.
- Rationalism and atheism challenged unexamined authority.
- Revolution meant transformation of social relations, not permanent bomb action.
- Trial and prison were used as communication platforms.
- Martyrdom amplified the thought; it must not replace the thought.

## 13. Bengal and Chittagong

- Two revolutionary streams developed: Punjab-U.P.-Bihar and Bengal; shared influences did not erase organisational distinctness.
- Bengal revolutionaries combined underground reorganisation with Congress and Swarajist work.
- After C.R. Das's death, Yugantar aligned with Subhas Bose's Bengal Congress wing and Anushilan with J.M. Sengupta's.
- Gopinath Saha's January 1924 attempt targeted Charles Tegart but killed Day by mistake; repression followed.
- Surya Sen remained Congress-linked and organised the Chittagong group independently of HSRA.
- The 18 April 1930 plan combined two armoury seizures, communications disruption, armed detachment and provisional authority.
- Failure to secure ammunition limited the operation; the 22 April Jalalabad battle led to dispersed village-supported resistance.
- Pritilata Waddedar died in a later raid; Kalpana Datta was tried with Surya Sen and received life imprisonment.
- Topic 21 owns this revolutionary sequence; Topic 22 retains only the overlapping Civil Disobedience setting.

## 14. Final comparative verdict

- No-changers built capacity outside the state.
- Swarajists exposed constitutional limitation inside the state.
- Revolutionaries attacked state legitimacy through exemplary action, trial and sacrifice.
- The methods responded to a common crisis but were not ethically or institutionally identical.
- The 1920s were not an empty lull. They widened nationalism's methods, ideas and constituencies.
- None of the three routes independently solved the problem of converting anti-colonial mobilisation into sovereign mass power.
- The Simon Commission supplied the next common provocation and begins Topic 22.
