# World War I, Home Rule and the Lucknow Pact — Live Session Edition

World War I did not suddenly create Indian nationalism. It changed the conditions in which nationalism operated. India was made to serve an imperial war, nationalists expected political repayment, everyday life became harsher, and the colonial state combined repression with a carefully limited promise. Between these pressures, the Home Rule Leagues made self-government a continuous public demand and the Lucknow settlement produced unity with a constitutional cost.

### Visual: loyalty becomes leverage, then disappointment

```text
Britain enters an imperial war
          |
India is committed without popular consent
          |
leaders offer wartime cooperation
          |
expectation: loyalty -> constitutional advance
          |
wartime extraction + slow/controlled concession
          |
the moral contradiction sharpens:
"democracy abroad, subjection in India"
```

The key is a change in political expectation. Many nationalists did not love British imperial power; they calculated that visible cooperation during Britain's danger would strengthen India's claim to self-government.

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine being required to help fight a war described as a defence of liberty while you are denied control over your own government. Cooperation may initially look like bargaining power: “We helped; now honour the principle you invoked.” If the ruler accepts the help but postpones the right, loyalty turns into evidence against the ruler.

This is the **imperial contradiction**: Britain used democratic language in war while governing India without responsible government.

### 1. What changed in 1914?

1. India entered the war as part of the British Empire; Indians did not make a sovereign decision to join.
2. Indian soldiers served overseas, and Indian resources sustained an imperial military system.
3. Moderate and Extremist leaders, including Tilak after his June 1914 release, publicly supported the war effort.
4. The support was largely strategic: a grateful Britain was expected to permit a major constitutional advance.
5. The war also displayed European powers fighting one another for imperial interests, weakening the aura of a naturally united or superior West.

### 2. Expectation was political, not simply emotional loyalty

The nationalist calculation may be written as:

```text
extraordinary Indian contribution
        + British language of democracy
        + earlier demand for colonial self-government
        = stronger claim to post-war responsible government
```

Home Rule meant self-government on the model of self-governing parts of the Empire. It did **not** yet mean the later Congress declaration of complete independence.

### 3. Why the contradiction mattered

War joined three arguments that had previously appeared separately:

| Existing nationalist argument | Wartime sharpening |
|---|---|
| India was taxed without responsible control | Indian revenue now bore extraordinary wartime pressure |
| Indians were politically capable | Indian soldiers and workers sustained a global war |
| British rule claimed a civilising mission | European warfare exposed coercion and imperial rivalry |
| Reform should follow loyal cooperation | Delay after contribution appeared as breach of expectation |

The war therefore matured nationalism not by one automatic shock but by making old criticism easier to connect with lived experience.

### Example and its limit

The service of Indian soldiers abroad made empire tangible in villages when servicemen left and later returned from Africa, Asia and Europe. Their experience could widen horizons and confidence.

**Limit:** military service did not automatically make every soldier anti-colonial. Service, livelihood, loyalty and political learning could coexist. Avoid inventing an aggregate troop total.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If leaders supported Britain, their later agitation was opportunistic or inconsistent.

**Reply:** Political cooperation can be conditional. The leaders expected a constitutional exchange and simultaneously knew that pressure would be necessary. Their position contained tension, but not necessarily hypocrisy: public loyalty was a tactic within a bargaining strategy. The deeper error was overestimating how far an imperial power would reward colonial participation.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims trap:** World War I began in 1914; Home Rule was self-government, not Purna Swaraj.
- **Mains use:** organise the answer around contribution, expectation, contradiction and mobilisation.
- **Boundary:** the military conspiracy response belongs to Topic 16; this lesson owns the constitutional-political response.

### Revision notes

1. India entered World War I through imperial subordination, not an Indian popular decision.
2. Nationalist support was often strategic rather than affectionate loyalty.
3. Leaders expected constitutional reward after wartime cooperation.
4. Home Rule meant self-government within the imperial framework.
5. Allied democratic language exposed colonial inconsistency.
6. Inter-imperial warfare weakened myths of uniform European superiority.
7. Wartime contribution strengthened the moral claim to political rights.
8. Expectation became anger when concession remained slow and controlled.
9. Soldiers' overseas experience could widen rural political horizons.
10. The war accelerated an existing nationalism; it did not create it from nothing.

### Local Mains practice
**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how World War I converted nationalist cooperation into a stronger demand for self-government.

**Model answer (132 words):** World War I intensified an existing nationalist argument rather than creating Indian nationalism. India was committed to an imperial war and supplied soldiers, finance and materials without responsible political control. Moderate and Extremist leaders, including Tilak after his release in June 1914, publicly supported the war effort because they expected a grateful Britain to permit a major constitutional advance. The Allies' language of democracy and self-determination strengthened this expectation. Yet the war was also an inter-imperial struggle, while India experienced taxation, recruitment pressure, inflation and coercive law. The contrast between liberty proclaimed abroad and subjection maintained in India weakened the moral defence of empire. Nationalists therefore moved from conditional cooperation to organised pressure for Home Rule. The shift was neither instantaneous nor uniform, but wartime contribution made indefinite political tutelage increasingly indefensible.

**Rubric (10):** context 2 + conditional support 2 + democratic contradiction 2 + material pressure 2 + qualified conclusion 2 = 10.

### Visual: the wartime extraction chain

```text
imperial war demand
   |------------|--------------|----------------|
   v            v              v                v
soldiers     defence cost    war loans       recruitment
overseas     and taxes       and levies      pressure
   |            |              |                |
absence and  consumer       financial        rural anger
return       burden         mobilisation     and coercion
   \____________|______________|_______________/
                        |
               uneven regional experience
```

The war was not experienced as one national average. It reached different people through enlistment, family separation, taxes, prices, loans, requisitions and administrative pressure.

### 1. Indian troops and overseas service

Indian soldiers served the Empire in theatres across Africa, Asia and Europe. The safe historical point is qualitative: their service made India's contribution global and brought returning soldiers into contact with worlds beyond the village.

Bipan Chandra notes that returning soldiers carried confidence and wider knowledge into rural areas. This could loosen the belief that European power was naturally invincible.

**Limit:** do not equate return from war with automatic nationalist conversion, and do not supply an unsupported total for troops.

### 2. Finance and taxation

War produced a phenomenal rise in defence expenditure. It was met through:

- war loans;
- higher customs duties;
- income tax;
- super tax on companies and undivided Hindu business families;
- excess profits taxation;
- indirect burdens transmitted through prices.

The OCR line claiming that national debt rose by “more than Rs 3 million” between 1914 and 1923 is omitted because the wording may reflect an edition or OCR anomaly. The secure conclusion is that defence expenditure, loans and taxation rose sharply.

### 3. Recruitment as administration inside society

Sekhar Bandyopadhyay records forced recruitment continuing from 1914 to 1923 and accumulating rural resentment. “Forced” does not mean every enlistment was physically compelled. It points to administrative pressure and unequal bargaining power within recruitment drives.

```text
district target Rect
   -> pressure on local officials and notables
   -> pressure on villages and families
   -> enlistment, avoidance or resentment
```

### 4. Regional and social effects

| Group or location | Wartime experience | Political possibility |
|---|---|---|
| Soldiers and families | overseas service, absence, danger, return | widened horizons, claims based on sacrifice |
| Peasants | recruitment, taxes, costly goods, crop distress | resentment shaped by local intermediaries |
| Traders | war taxation and unstable exchange conditions | support for political protection varied by scale |
| Urban educated | employment pressure and frustrated expectations | attraction to sustained agitation |
| Provinces with recruitment drives | visible official intrusion | intense local memories of coercion |

The same policy could produce different reactions. A prosperous contractor might benefit while a cultivator household lost labour and paid more for necessities.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Economic and recruitment hardship cannot explain political mobilisation because suffering does not automatically produce nationalism.

**Reply:** Correct. Hardship is a condition, not a political organisation. It becomes nationalist when activists supply language, networks and a target. Home Rule's importance lies precisely in joining scattered burdens to the demand for self-government.

### UPSC integration

- Use a **burden map**, not unsupported national totals.
- Distinguish Indian contribution from Indian consent.
- Link returning soldiers to widened political horizons cautiously.
- In a Mains answer, add the mediation step: hardship → organisation → political claim.

### Revision notes

1. Indian soldiers served in Africa, Asia and Europe.
2. No unsupported aggregate troop figure should be used.
3. Defence expenditure rose sharply during and after the war.
4. War loans and taxation helped finance imperial needs.
5. Indirect taxation transmitted the burden to ordinary consumers.
6. Recruitment pressure reached villages through officials and local intermediaries.
7. Sekhar records forced recruitment across 1914-23.
8. Returning soldiers could carry confidence and wider knowledge to rural India.
9. Wartime burdens varied by class and region.
10. Material distress required political organisation before becoming nationalist action.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse the social and regional channels through which World War I affected India.

**Model answer (187 words):** World War I entered Indian society through several unequal channels. Indian troops served overseas in Africa, Asia and Europe, while their families experienced absence, danger and recruitment pressure. Returning soldiers could carry wider knowledge and confidence into rural society, though service did not automatically produce nationalism. The fiscal channel was equally important: defence expenditure, war loans, customs duties, income tax, company super tax and excess profits taxation expanded, while indirect burdens reached ordinary consumers through prices. Sekhar Bandyopadhyay records forced recruitment from 1914 to 1923, making district administration an intrusive presence in many villages.

The effects varied socially. Poor peasants faced costly necessities and recruitment; richer cultivators were hurt when commercial-crop markets weakened; small traders resented taxation and exchange instability; some larger industrial interests gained from wartime demand. Regions affected by crop failure or intensive recruitment therefore experienced the war differently from industrial centres.

These pressures were not self-executing causes of nationalism. Grievances became political only when newspapers, meetings and Home Rule workers connected household burdens to colonial irresponsibility. The war's significance lay in creating a broad field of discontent that a renewed nationalist organisation could interpret.

**Rubric (15):** military channel 3 + fiscal channel 3 + recruitment 3 + social/regional variation 3 + mediation and qualification 3 = 15.

## Lesson 3 — Inflation, food, industry and labour: growth without welfare

Progress: 3/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: The uneven wartime economy

### Visual: why output growth did not mean shared prosperity

```text
foreign imports fall + wartime demand rises
                    |
            some Indian industries expand
                    |
         profits and paid-up capital increase
                    |
      wages rise much less than living costs
                    |
food shortfall + transport dislocation + taxation
                    |
   growth for some, falling real income for many
```

The central intuition is simple: an economy can produce more industrial goods while workers and peasants become poorer. What matters is not only production, but prices, wages, food and distribution.

### 1. The inflation shock

Sekhar cites the official all-India price index rising from **147 in 1914 to 281 in 1920**, with **1873 as the base year**.

| Indicator | Earlier point | Later point | Meaning |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| All-India price index | 147 in 1914 | 281 in 1920 | severe rise in the cost environment |
| Worker cost of living in Lahore/Bombay | — | +60-70% | household expenses surged |
| Wages in the same comparison | — | +15-25% | nominal wage rise lagged far behind |

When living costs rise by 60-70 per cent but wages only 15-25 per cent, **real wages**—what wages can buy—fall.

### 2. Food dislocation

Two extraordinary crop failures occurred in **1918-19** and **1920-21**, affecting large areas of the United Provinces, Punjab, Bombay, Central Provinces, Bihar and Orissa. Underproduction, transport dislocation and continued supply for forces abroad tightened domestic availability. Influenza compounded distress.

This lesson does not use the disputed mortality estimate because the requested core is the economic mechanism, not a contested demographic total.

### 3. Industrial expansion and uneven gains

Reduced imports and wartime demand aided industries such as textiles, steel and jute. One useful indicator is jute paid-up capital:

| Year | Paid-up capital |
|---|---:|
| 1914-15 | 79.3 million |
| 1918-19 | 106.4 million |
| 1922-23 | 179.4 million |

These figures show investment expansion, not general welfare. Much jute capital was British, while Indian capital had a larger role in Bombay and Ahmedabad textiles.

### 4. Labour and the distribution problem

The number of workers in organised industries increased by **575,000 between 1911 and 1921**. Expansion created a larger working class, but high prices eroded wages. Wartime and post-war business profits could therefore coexist with worker impoverishment and strike activity.

```text
nominal wage rises
        -
larger rise in cost of living
        =
declining purchasing power
```

### 5. Agrarian differentiation

Poor cultivators suffered high consumer prices. Richer peasants could also suffer because prices of exported agricultural raw materials did not always rise as fast, contributing to stockpiles, reduced acreage and market crisis in 1917-19. Land transfer toward non-cultivating classes became especially visible in Madras and the United Provinces between 1914 and 1922.

### Example and limit

Jute capital is an example of wartime industrial opportunity. It establishes that war could accelerate sectoral investment.

**Limit:** it cannot prove that all industries, owners, regions or workers prospered. Paid-up capital is not the same as wages, output or household welfare.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Industrial growth means the war modernised India and therefore benefited it overall.

**Reply:** Industrial capacity mattered, but distribution and control matter too. Expansion under wartime scarcity produced profits for some owners while food shortages, inflation and lagging wages reduced mass welfare. A balanced answer concedes structural industrial growth without calling the wartime economy broadly beneficial.

### UPSC integration

- Quote the **147 → 281** price-index movement with the 1873 base.
- Use **cost of living +60-70% versus wages +15-25%** to explain real wages.
- Treat **575,000 additional organised workers** as class expansion, not proof of good conditions.
- Use jute capital only as a sector-specific indicator.

### Revision notes

1. The all-India price index rose from 147 in 1914 to 281 in 1920.
2. The index used 1873 as its base.
3. Crop failures occurred in 1918-19 and 1920-21.
4. Affected areas included U.P., Punjab, Bombay, C.P., Bihar and Orissa.
5. Transport dislocation and taxation contributed to inflation.
6. Organised industrial workers increased by 575,000 between 1911 and 1921.
7. Worker living costs in Lahore and Bombay rose 60-70 per cent.
8. Wages in that comparison rose only 15-25 per cent.
9. Jute paid-up capital rose from 79.3 million to 106.4 million and later 179.4 million.
10. Industrial growth and falling real wages can occur together.
11. Poor and richer peasants could be hurt through different mechanisms.
12. Wartime hardship was socially uneven, not uniform.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “World War I produced industrial opportunity and social distress at the same time.” Discuss.

**Model answer (184 words):** The wartime economy was expansionary in selected industries but regressive in its distribution. Foreign manufactured imports declined and imperial demand increased, helping textiles, steel and jute. Poor peasants faced costly necessities, while some richer cultivators suffered weak commercial-crop markets. Influenza deepened the crisis.

Thus the war did encourage industrial capacity and a larger working class, but gains accrued unevenly. Its political importance lay precisely in this divergence: visible profits and production coexisted with declining mass purchasing power, making colonial economic management vulnerable to nationalist criticism.

### Visual: emergency power and political space

```text
wartime security claim
        |
Defence of India Act and related regulations
        |
detention / internment / restrictions / prosecution
        |
radicals and revolutionaries suppressed
        |
open constitutional agitation also faces surveillance
        |
nationalists connect civil liberty with self-government
```

Emergency law did more than punish a particular act. It enlarged executive discretion and changed the environment in which all political organising occurred.

### 1. Proper boundary

Topic 16 teaches Ghadar, overseas conspiracies and planned wartime revolt. This lesson does not retell those organisations. It asks what their existence and the war allowed the colonial state to do politically.

Topic 19 teaches the Rowlatt legislation and the 1919 movement. The wartime powers here are the antecedent; Rowlatt's attempted peacetime continuation is the later breach.

### 2. The Defence of India context

The **Defence of India Act, 1915** and related regulations enabled extraordinary executive action during war. Bipan Chandra notes that large numbers of radical nationalists and revolutionaries were jailed or interned under wartime law.

The mechanism was:

1. define dissent through wartime security;
2. expand preventive and summary powers;
3. disrupt networks before open proof of a completed offence;
4. make ordinary political association riskier;
5. combine coercion with selective constitutional promise.

### 3. Coercion and concession were complementary

| “Stick” | “Carrot” |
|---|---|
| emergency legislation | promise of reform |
| internment and restrictions | dialogue with selected leaders |
| control of activists and students | declaration of gradual progress |
| deterrence | incorporation |

Colonial rule did not simply choose repression or reform. It used both to divide opponents, isolate radicals and retain control over the pace of change.

### 4. Civil-liberty effect

For Home Rule activists, a demand for self-government became inseparable from the right to speak, meet, publish and travel. When speakers were restricted or leaders interned, the government's response supplied evidence that political tutelage and civil restriction belonged together.

### Example and limit

The later restrictions on Tilak's travel and Besant's internment show how executive measures could turn a constitutional campaign into a civil-liberty issue.

**Limit:** not every restriction was imposed under the same legal instrument. Use “wartime coercive apparatus” unless the exact order and statute are verified.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A government at war was entitled to extraordinary security powers.

**Reply:** Security needs can explain emergency power but do not settle its legitimacy or scope. The political issue is whether vague and preventive authority suppressed lawful nationalist organisation and whether wartime discretion was allowed to define dissent itself as danger.

### UPSC integration

- Do not merge the Defence of India Act with the Rowlatt Act.
- Use “coercion plus concession” as the analytical frame.
- Distinguish legal context from the separate organisational history of Ghadar.
- Explain why civil liberty became part of the Home Rule claim.

### Revision notes

1. The Defence of India Act belongs to the wartime coercive context.
2. It enabled extraordinary executive action.
3. Radical nationalists and revolutionaries faced detention and internment.
4. Topic 16 owns the organisations targeted in the conspiracy story.
5. Topic 19 owns the Rowlatt continuation and mass response.
6. Wartime coercion affected open political association as well as underground activity.
7. Preventive power acts before ordinary adjudication of a completed offence.

### Visual: Tilak's strategic sequence

```text

                |

                |
wartime loyalty posture + reassurance to Moderates
                |

                |
```

Tilak did not simply resume the politics of 1907. He first rebuilt legitimacy inside the national organisation, reduced the government's pretext for repression and then launched sustained agitation.

### Plain-language intuition

A leader returning after six years in prison faces two problems: the state expects danger, and former allies remain divided. If he immediately escalates, repression may isolate him. If he becomes passive, he loses political relevance. Tilak's answer was tactical reassurance without surrendering the objective of self-government.

### 1. Public rejection of revolutionary violence

Tilak stated that he sought reform of administration, comparable to Irish Home Rule, rather than overthrow of government. He described acts of violence as repugnant and politically retarding.

This should be read as:

- a genuine public rejection of individual violence in this phase;
- an attempt to reassure Moderate Congress leaders;
- a means of reducing the government's justification for renewed repression;
- a shift toward open, continuous political education.

It must not be rewritten as an embrace of British imperial objectives.

### 2. Why Congress readmission came first

Tilak believed Congress sanction remained necessary because it symbolised the national movement. In May 1915 his followers proposed an agency to explain Congress objectives to villagers. Associations formed in Maharashtra emphasised Congress unity.

The December 1915 Congress decision permitting Extremists to return followed:

1. growing Moderate dissatisfaction with inactivity;
2. Annie Besant's sustained pressure;
3. weakening of the Bombay Moderate opposition after Pherozeshah Mehta's death;
4. recognition that division harmed both sides.

### 3. A secular and linguistic politics

Tilak linked Swaraj with linguistic provinces and vernacular education. His argument was not Marathi exclusivism: he defended Kannada speech and envisaged Marathi, Telugu and Kannada provinces.

He also argued that “alienness” should be judged by whether conduct served Indian interests, not by skin colour or religion. On untouchability he made an emphatic rejection of divine sanction for the practice.

### 4. Social positioning and its limit

Tilak urged patience toward the non-Brahman memorandum and argued that education, not an inherent caste entitlement, explained Brahman advantage in jobs.

**Limit:** a conciliatory speech does not erase the social composition of the League. Later non-Brahman opposition to Brahman dominance reveals that inclusive language and inclusive organisational power were not identical.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Tilak's wartime loyalty and constitutional language prove ideological retreat.

**Reply:** The objective remained Indian self-government, but the chosen route changed. Re-entry into Congress, open organisation and mass political education were strategic adaptations after repression and the failure of isolated militancy. Tactical legality is not the same as political moderation in purpose.

- Sequence: release → reassurance → Congress readmission → League.
- Distinguish rejection of revolutionary violence from abandonment of assertive nationalism.
- Use linguistic and secular statements to complicate a one-dimensional portrayal of Tilak.
- Qualify inclusive rhetoric with the League's social limits.

- B.P. Wadia;
- George Arundale.

Reuniting people in one institution is not the same as making that institution capable of continuous action. Lucknow repaired legitimacy; it did not fully repair machinery.

### 1. From readmission to reunion

The December 1915 decision changed Congress rules so that Tilak's followers could return. At Lucknow in December 1916, Moderates and Extremists met as a reunited Congress.

The reunion mattered because:

- Congress again represented a broader nationalist spectrum;
- Home Rule workers could use Congress legitimacy;
- factional conflict no longer consumed the annual session;
- the Congress-League agreement appeared as part of a wider unity moment.

### 2. The delegate mobilisation

The Leagues mobilised supporters to attend Lucknow. Tilak and Besant dominated proceedings sufficiently to demonstrate that constitutional agitation had moved beyond old Moderate caution.

Yet domination of a session is not the same as control of a durable institution.

### 3. Tilak's Working Committee proposal

Tilak proposed a small committee that could conduct Congress affairs throughout the year. The proposal failed.

|---|---|
| annual session-centred | continuous executive direction |
| large deliberative gathering | small accountable working body |
| resolutions | implementation and coordination |
| episodic mobilisation | sustained organisation |

### 4. Why the failure matters

The Leagues partially filled Congress's organisational gap through branches and year-round propaganda. But because they remained separate and uneven, they could not simply substitute for a central national executive.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The failed Working Committee proposal was procedural and therefore historically minor.

**Reply:** Procedure structures political capacity. Without a year-round executive, Congress could declare a programme but struggled to coordinate implementation, resolve local disputes and respond quickly to repression. Institutional form helps explain both Home Rule's opportunity and its later fragility.

## Lesson 11 — The Lucknow Pact: terms and constitutional bargain

Progress: 11/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Common reform demands, separate electorates and weightage

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the double unity at Lucknow

```text
UNITY WITHIN CONGRESS              UNITY BETWEEN ORGANISATIONS
Moderates + Extremists             Congress + Muslim League
          \                             /
           \                           /
            common constitutional demand
                         +
 Congress acceptance of separate electorates,
 weightage and minority safeguards
```

Lucknow contained two different achievements. Congress reunion restored an internal coalition; the Pact created an inter-organisational constitutional bargain.

### 1. The common reform demand

Congress and the Muslim League jointly pressed for:

- provincial and imperial legislative councils with **four-fifths elected** and one-fifth nominated members;
- direct election of provincial councillors on as broad a franchise as possible; for the Imperial Council, a widened franchise in which elected provincial legislators would also form an electorate;
- effective legislative authority over budgets, taxation and provincial administration, subject to specified executive vetoes;
- autonomous provincial governments with provincial revenue heads separated from imperial finance;
- executive councils in which at least half the members would be Indians elected by the elected legislators;
- a Government of India made, as far as possible, independent of the Secretary of State in legislative and administrative matters;
- abolition of the Secretary of State's Council for India and movement toward self-government.

The agreement did not merge the two bodies. It created a negotiated common platform.

### 2. Separate electorates: the chronological distinction

Separate electorates for Muslims were **introduced by the colonial reforms of 1909**. They were not invented at Lucknow.

What changed in 1916 was that Congress **accepted** them within a bargain with the League.

```text
1909: colonial introduction
             |
1916: Congress acceptance in negotiated pact
```

This distinction prevents two errors: blaming Lucknow for legal origination, or ignoring its role in nationalist legitimisation.

### 3. Representation and weightage

The Pact provided that **one-third of the Indian elected members of the Imperial Legislative Council would be Muslims elected by separate Muslim electorates**. At the provincial level, negotiated weightage did not simply mirror population:

- in several Muslim-minority provinces, representation was above the Muslim population share;
- in Muslim-majority Punjab and Bengal, Muslim representation was below population share.

This was reciprocal bargaining. Minority provinces received assurance; majority provinces accepted under-representation to make an all-India settlement possible.

No unverified fine-grained provincial percentages are used here.

### 4. Minority safeguards

The scheme specified a communal blocking safeguard, not merely a general promise of consultation. A bill, clause or non-official resolution affecting one community was not to proceed if **three-fourths of that community's members in the imperial or provincial council opposed it**. Members of the community concerned would determine whether the measure affected that community.

The political logic was that formal majority voting alone could not reassure a large, geographically uneven minority. The mechanism was narrower than a general executive veto: it applied within a legislature to a measure identified as affecting one community and required opposition from three-fourths of that community's council members.

### 5. What each side gained

| Congress calculation | League calculation |
|---|---|
| wider anti-colonial unity | recognition as representative negotiating body |
| common constitutional pressure | safeguards against permanent majority |
| reduced colonial divide-and-rule leverage | central and provincial representation guarantees |
| broader legitimacy | cooperation without organisational merger |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Congress acceptance merely ratified a colonial device and cannot be called unity.

**Reply:** It did ratify a problematic communal representation principle, but unity need not mean institutional sameness. In 1916 leaders considered assurance necessary for joint action. The analytical task is to recognise both the immediate coalition gain and the long-run cost of making political community the basis of representation.

```text
             /        \

  immediate coalition   institutional cost

  - Congress reunion    - elite spokesmen empowered

            \          /
             \        /
       non-teleological verdict:
       a real achievement with real risk
```

Historical judgement fails when it turns one consequence into the whole event. Lucknow was neither pure harmony nor an inevitable first step to Partition.

### 1. The achievement case

The Pact:

- ended the Congress Moderate-Extremist split;
- produced Congress-League cooperation;
- demonstrated capacity for negotiated concessions;
- reduced the government's ability to present Indians as incapable of agreement;
- strengthened a joint demand for constitutional change.

For leaders confronting colonial divide-and-rule, minority assurance could appear necessary rather than irrational.

### 2. The constitutional cost

Congress acceptance gave nationalist legitimacy to communal electorates. It encouraged political negotiation through accredited communal elites and helped convert flexible identities into representation categories.

This did not create communalism from nothing. Topic 17 owns the earlier origins, organisations and theories. The Pact's role was to **institutionalise and legitimise** one political form of community.

### 3. Elite bargain and mass disjunction

```text
leaders negotiate seats and safeguards
                 |
        agreement at the top
                 X
no equivalent everyday integration of social interests
                 |
unity vulnerable when elite incentives change
```

The Pact coordinated organisations, not populations. It did not integrate peasant, worker, caste, regional and religious interests through a shared mass programme.

### 4. Three historiographical positions

| Reading | Strength | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|
| nationalist triumph | captures joint pressure and reunion | may understate communal institutionalisation |
| fatal communal mistake | identifies long-run representation risk | reads later outcomes backward as inevitable |
| negotiated transitional bargain | holds security and cost together | can become vague unless terms and actors are specified |

The strongest verdict is the third, provided it does not evade judgement: the bargain was rational in its immediate setting but structurally fragile.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Any criticism of separate electorates ignores genuine minority fear under majority rule.

**Reply:** Minority fear was real and required institutional response. The criticism concerns the chosen mechanism, not the need for assurance. Reserved or negotiated representation, rights guarantees, federal design and cross-community political programmes are conceptually distinct possibilities. In 1916, leaders selected community electorates because they were already embedded and politically available.

- Avoid the teleology: “Lucknow caused Partition.”
- Avoid the opposite romanticism: “unity solved communal politics.”
- Name both actors' incentives.
- Distinguish minority-security problem from the separate-electorate solution.

Minority insecurity was genuine; the criticism concerns the chosen institutional response, not the need for protection.

Lucknow should therefore be understood as a rational but brittle transitional bargain: effective for immediate anti-colonial cooperation, costly in its legitimisation of community-based political representation, and dependent on elite incentives that could later change.

### Visual: promise and reservation in one declaration

```text
20 August 1917
          |
increasing association of Indians in administration
          +
gradual development of self-governing institutions
          +
progressive realisation of responsible government
          |

          +

          |

```

The declaration mattered because responsible government entered official policy. It was limited because the ruler retained the authority to decide when, how far and at what speed that policy would be realised.

### 1. Context

By mid-1917, wartime pressure, Home Rule agitation, internment protests and wider nationalist unity made simple refusal costly. Edwin Montagu, Secretary of State for India, announced British policy on **20 August 1917**.

### 2. The wording that matters

Its aim was:

- increasing association of Indians in every branch of administration;
- gradual development of self-governing institutions;
- progressive realisation of responsible government in India;
- continued status of India as an integral part of the British Empire.

Successive stages and the timing of advance remained subject to British judgement.

### 3. Why it was a gain

Previously, reform could be defended as better administration without admitting responsible government as the destination. The declaration made that destination official language. Nationalists could now hold Britain against its own promise.

### 4. Why it was limited

| Nationalist reading | Imperial reservation |
|---|---|
| responsible government acknowledged | only progressively realised |
| self-governing institutions promised | development explicitly gradual |
| Indian association to increase | British government judged timing and pace |
| political direction conceded | imperial integrity preserved |

The statement offered no deadline and no Indian control over the transition.

### 5. Proper boundary

The detailed Montagu-Chelmsford proposals, the Government of India Act 1919 and dyarchy are not taught here. They are later constitutional substance. Topic 18 supplies the pressure, promise and community-representation context needed to approach them.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because the declaration lacked a deadline, it changed nothing.

**Reply:** It did not transfer power, but official recognition of responsible government altered the argumentative terrain. A promise can be politically consequential when a movement uses it as a benchmark. Its importance is doctrinal and strategic, not equivalent to immediate constitutional delivery.

### UPSC integration

- Date: 20 August 1917.
- Use the tension “responsible government promised; pace retained.”
- Do not smuggle dyarchy into Topic 18.
- Connect Home Rule pressure without claiming it was the sole cause.

## Lesson 14 — Decline, legacy and a non-teleological transition

### Visual: decline of an organisation, survival of political capacity

```text

   |

```

A movement can decline organisationally while altering the repertoire of later politics. Legacy is neither total disappearance nor automatic succession.

### 1. Why decline set in

The 1918 reform proposals divided nationalist opinion. Many Moderates were satisfied enough to step back or were alarmed by talk of passive resistance. Besant vacillated over whether the reforms should be accepted or opposed.

Tilak left for England to pursue his libel case against Valentine Chirol. The movement thereby lost its most consistent advocate of escalation at a critical moment.

### 2. Structural weaknesses

- the two Leagues were not fused;
- Besant's loose branches often depended on central publicity;
- Congress still lacked a compact year-round executive;
- the social base remained educated and urban-heavy;
- non-Brahman opposition limited reach in important southern and western areas;
- no common strategy existed once reform divided leaders.

### 3. What survived

Home Rule normalised the language of self-government, created trained local workers, revived Congress politics, widened activity into new districts and linked towns with smaller centres. Its methods—public meetings, pamphlets, petitions, vernacular explanation and continuous local work—remained available.

### 4. Gandhi and the non-automatic transition

Gandhi interacted with Home Rule networks during the internment protest. Later nationalism could draw on their contacts and skills. But Gandhi did not simply inherit a complete mass machine.

```text
Home Rule infrastructure
       + Gandhi's methods and authority
       + new local grievances and post-war anger
       + organisational reconstruction
       = later mass mobilisation
```

Topic 19 owns Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda, Rowlatt and Jallianwala. They should not be narrated as the inevitable unfolding of Home Rule.

### 5. Overall assessment

Sekhar regards the movement as a major step toward democratising Congress politics, but one with limited grassroots reach. That balance is persuasive: its great contribution was political activation and organisation; its limit was the incompleteness of social mobilisation and central discipline.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the movement collapsed by 1918, calling it a major phase exaggerates failure.

**Reply:** Institutional endurance is only one measure. The Leagues altered vocabulary, geography, personnel and expectations. Yet legacy should not become a success myth: later mobilisation required new grievances, new methods and new leadership.

## 10 marks — Political education

**Question:** How did the Home Rule Leagues convert self-government from an annual Congress resolution into a continuous political demand? (150 words)

**Model answer (135 words):** The Home Rule Leagues changed the rhythm and geography of nationalism. Congress had remained centred on annual sessions, resolutions and limited local committees. ## 15 marks — War and Home Rule

**Question:** To what extent did World War I create the conditions for the Home Rule movement? (250 words)

**Model answer (199 words):** World War I created a favourable conjuncture for Home Rule, but organisation and leadership converted opportunity into movement. India was committed to an imperial war without responsible consent. Indian troops served overseas, defence expenditure and taxation rose, recruitment pressure entered villages, and returning soldiers brought wider experience. At the same time, leaders who publicly supported Britain expected constitutional reward. The Allied language of democracy made colonial subjection increasingly contradictory.

The material crisis widened potential audiences. The price index rose from 147 in 1914 to 281 in 1920; worker living costs in Lahore and Bombay rose 60-70 per cent while wages rose only 15-25 per cent. Crop failures and transport dislocation intensified food distress. Yet selective industrial growth benefited some capital, demonstrating that wartime effects were uneven.

These conditions did not automatically produce Home Rule. Tilak rebuilt Congress legitimacy after his 1914 release, while Besant used Theosophical networks, *New India*, *Commonweal* and pressure for reunion. Their separate Leagues then supplied pamphlets, branches, meetings and a language connecting scattered grievance to colonial irresponsibility.

Thus war was an accelerator rather than an autonomous cause. It sharpened contradiction, expectation and hardship; political entrepreneurs and organisations turned them into a sustained demand for self-government.

## 20 marks — A transitional political formation

**Question:** “The Home Rule-Lucknow moment widened Indian nationalism while carrying forward institutional and social limits.” Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer (232 words):** The Home Rule-Lucknow moment rebuilt nationalist capacity without resolving representation and social-base problems. Tilak and Besant helped reunite Congress. Two coordinated Leagues made self-government a continuous demand through pamphlets, newspapers, branches, libraries, lectures and petitions. The June 1917 internment of Besant, Wadia and Arundale drew hesitant Moderates into protest and placed passive resistance on the national agenda.

Lucknow added Congress-League unity through four-fifths elected councils, wider legislative authority and provincial autonomy. Congress accepted separate electorates—introduced in 1909—and provided that one-third of the Indian elected members of the Imperial Legislative Council would be Muslims elected by separate Muslim electorates, alongside provincial weightage. Three-fourths of an affected community's council members could block a relevant measure. The bargain answered a genuine minority-security problem and challenged colonial claims of Indian incapacity.

The gains carried limits. Tilak's proposed year-round Congress executive failed. Many branches were loose; students, professionals and businessmen predominated; non-Brahman criticism exposed uneven social representation. At Lucknow, agreement among leaders did not integrate mass socio-economic interests, while Congress acceptance legitimised religious community as a political constituency.

The Montagu Declaration acknowledged progressive responsible government but retained British control over pace. Reform then divided leaders; Besant vacillated and Tilak left for England. Home Rule declined, yet left cadres, political vocabulary and regional connections. Later Gandhian mobilisation drew on these resources but required new grievances, methods and organisational reconstruction. The moment therefore widened politics without predetermining its successor.

**MCQ 37: A**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. India and World War I

### Core proposition

World War I accelerated Indian nationalism by joining **imperial contribution**, **political expectation**, **material burden** and **coercive governance**. It did not independently or uniformly create nationalist politics.

### Troops, finance and recruitment

- India entered the war through imperial subordination.
- Indian soldiers served overseas in Africa, Asia and Europe.
- Returning soldiers could bring wider knowledge and confidence to rural society.
- This effect was possible, not universal; no unsupported aggregate troop total should be supplied.
- Defence expenditure rose dramatically.
- War loans, higher customs duties, income tax, super taxes and excess profits taxation increased extraction.
- Indirect burdens reached people who did not pay direct taxes.
- Recruitment pressure operated through officials and local notables.
- Sekhar records forced recruitment extending from 1914 to 1923.
- Omit the OCR phrase that national debt rose by “more than Rs 3 million” unless an edition is independently verified.

### Wartime economy

| High-yield fact | Exact evidence | Analytical use |
|---|---|---|
| prices | index 147 in 1914 → 281 in 1920; 1873 base | scale of inflation |
| labour force | organised industrial workers +575,000, 1911-21 | industrial-class expansion |
| worker welfare | Lahore/Bombay living costs +60-70%; wages +15-25% | falling real wages |
| jute investment | paid-up capital 79.3m in 1914-15; 106.4m in 1918-19; 179.4m in 1922-23 | sectoral expansion, not general welfare |
| food shock | crop failures in 1918-19 and 1920-21 | scarcity and agrarian distress |
| affected regions | U.P., Punjab, Bombay, C.P., Bihar, Orissa | uneven geography of crisis |

**Flow logic:** reduced imports and wartime orders aided some industries; shortages, taxes, transport disruption and crop failure pushed prices upward; wages lagged; therefore growth and distress coexisted.

### Political expectation

- Moderate and Extremist leaders supported the war effort conditionally.
- They expected political advance in return for loyalty and contribution.
- Allied democratic rhetoric sharpened the contradiction of colonial subjection.
- European warfare weakened claims of natural European unity or invincibility.
- Home Rule meant self-government, not yet complete independence.

## B. Wartime coercion

- Defence of India Act, 1915: wartime extraordinary-power context.
- It enabled detention, internment and preventive control.
- Topic 16 owns Ghadar and revolutionary conspiracy.
- Topic 19 owns Rowlatt's post-war continuation and its consequences.
- Colonial strategy combined coercive “stick” with reformist “carrot.”
- Legal restrictions made civil liberty part of the self-government demand.
- Do not state that every externment or security order arose under the identical statute.

## C. Tilak's repositioning

### Chronology

```text
16 June 1914 release
-> rejection of revolutionary violence
-> wartime reassurance
-> Congress unity campaign
-> December 1915 readmission decision
-> April 1916 League
```

- The loyalty posture was tactical, not surrender of the nationalist objective.
- Congress legitimacy was important to national-scale action.
- Tilak defended linguistic provinces and vernacular education.
- He used a political-interest rather than racial-religious test of “alienness.”
- He publicly rejected untouchability.
- His conciliatory position did not eliminate non-Brahman concern over Brahman organisational predominance.

## D. Annie Besant and pre-League infrastructure

- Besant's background included British Radical, Fabian, Free Thought and Theosophical activity.
- She came to India in 1893.
- Adyar supplied a Theosophical headquarters and educated network.
- *New India* and *Commonweal* built a sustained press campaign.
- She pressed Congress to readmit Extremists and revive propaganda.
- The 1914 effort failed; Congress moved in December 1915.
- She could launch independently if Congress remained inactive by September 1916.
- Inherited networks sped diffusion but did not guarantee disciplined political branches.

## E. Two Home Rule Leagues

| Recall hook | Tilak | Besant |
|---|---|---|
| month | April 1916 | September 1916 |
| zone | intended field: Maharashtra except Bombay city; Karnataka; C.P.; Berar | intended remainder, including Bombay city; same source later lists a Tilak branch there |
| branch design | six defined units | three members could form a branch |
| leading organisers | Tilak and provincial colleagues | Besant, Arundale, Wadia, C.P. Ramaswamy Aiyar |
| strength | dense, vernacular, more supervised | expansive, press-enabled, flexible |

### Methods

- pamphlets and newspapers;
- lectures, conferences and public meetings;
- libraries and discussion circles;
- classes for students;
- petitions and protest resolutions;
- fundraising and social work;
- local-government participation;
- friend-to-friend persuasion.

### Numbers with dates

- Tilak: six branches; 47,000 copies of six Marathi and two English pamphlets; 14,000 members by April 1917.
- Besant: 200 branches; 7,000 members by March 1917.
- Besant pre-launch propaganda: 300,000 copies of 26 English pamphlets.
- A broader estimate around sixty thousand members in 1917-18 measures a different aggregate and date.

### Social base and limits

- strong among students, professionals, lawyers and businessmen;
- some reach to agriculturists, especially through public/market meetings;
- reach through print and meetings exceeded membership;
- some branches existed largely on paper;
- non-Brahman critique targeted Brahman leadership dominance;
- not yet an all-India peasant-worker mass movement.

## F. Government response

### Tilak

- 23 July 1916: government demanded Rs 60,000 security.
- Magistrate ruled against him.
- High Court exonerated him in November 1916.
- M.A. Jinnah led the defence team.
- February 1917: Tilak was excluded from Punjab and Delhi.

### Besant and associates

- November 1916: Besant excluded from Central Provinces and Berar.
- Madras banned students from political meetings.
- June 1917: Besant, B.P. Wadia and George Arundale interned.
- The detailed Home Rule narrative supports June; an isolated Sekhar OCR reference to April is treated as inconsistent and not adopted.
- Moderates joined in protest; S. Subramania Aiyar renounced his knighthood.
- 28 July 1917 AICC: passive resistance debated.
- Berar and Madras favoured immediate action; most provincial committees preferred waiting.
- Gandhi supported signature preparation for defiance and wider petitioning.
- Besant was released in September 1917 and became Congress president in December.

## G. Congress reunion

- December 1915: Congress rules permitted Extremist readmission.
- December 1916: reunion publicly enacted at Lucknow.
- The Leagues mobilised delegates and renewed Congress energy.
- Tilak's small year-round Working Committee proposal failed.
- Congress retained weak continuous executive capacity.
- Comparable reform in 1920 is a later bridge, not Topic 18 substance.

## H. Lucknow Pact

### Double unity

1. Moderates and Extremists reunited within Congress.
2. Congress and the Muslim League agreed on a common constitutional programme.

### Terms and logic

- four-fifths elected provincial and imperial legislative councils;
- direct election of provincial councillors on a broad franchise; a widened Imperial franchise also using elected provincial legislators as an electorate;
- stronger legislative authority over budgets, taxation and provincial administration;
- autonomous provinces with provincial revenues separated from imperial finance;
- at least half of executive councillors to be Indians elected by elected legislators;
- greater Government of India independence from the Secretary of State and abolition of his Council;
- Congress acceptance of separate electorates;
- one-third of the Indian elected members of the Imperial Legislative Council to be Muslims elected by separate Muslim electorates;
- negotiated provincial weightage;
- above-population representation in several Muslim-minority provinces;
- below-population Muslim representation in Muslim-majority Punjab and Bengal;
- a bill, clause or non-official resolution affecting a community could not proceed if three-fourths of that community's council members opposed it.

### Chronology trap

> **1909 introduced separate electorates; 1916 secured Congress acceptance.**

### Balanced evaluation

**Achievement**
- common front;
- negotiated assurance;
- wider constitutional pressure;
- challenge to colonial claims of Indian incapacity.

**Criticism**
- legitimised community as political constituency;
- strengthened accredited communal elites;
- elite agreement lacked a mass integrative programme;
- institutional segmentation made cooperation fragile.

**Verdict**
- Do not say Lucknow created communalism.
- Do not say it inevitably caused Partition.
- It was an immediately rational coalition with long-run institutional risk.

## I. Montagu Declaration

- Date: 20 August 1917.
- Context: war, nationalist expectation, Home Rule pressure and internment protest.
- Promised increasing Indian association in administration.
- Promised gradual development of self-governing institutions.
- Named progressive realisation of responsible government as policy.
- Kept India integral to the British Empire.
- Left stages, pace and timing under British judgement.
- Importance: official recognition of direction.
- Limit: no deadline or immediate responsibility.
- Do not teach the 1919 Act or dyarchy here.

## J. Decline and legacy

### Decline by 1918

- reform proposals divided nationalists;
- Moderates were reassured or deterred by passive-resistance talk;
- Besant vacillated;
- Tilak went to England for the Valentine Chirol libel case;
- loose/separate structures lost direction;
- narrow social reach and non-Brahman opposition limited depth.

### What survived

- trained political workers;
- wider geographic contacts;
- normalised vocabulary of self-government;
- vernacular propaganda practice;
- links among town, district and some rural audiences;
- revived Congress legitimacy.

### Non-teleological transition

Later Gandhian mobilisation used some networks but required new grievances, satyagraha methods, leadership authority and Congress organisation. Topic 19's Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda, Rowlatt and Jallianwala are bridges, not inevitable products of Home Rule.

## K. Ten answer-writing traps

1. Do not invent troop totals.
2. Do not use the anomalous OCR debt figure as secure fact.
3. Do not treat all war support as ideological loyalty.
4. Do not equate industrial capital growth with mass prosperity.
5. Do not merge the two Home Rule Leagues.
6. State the intended Bombay-city demarcation and disclose the source's contrary branch-list entry.
7. Do not say Lucknow introduced separate electorates.
8. Do not supply unverified provincial percentages.
9. Do not equate the Montagu Declaration with delivery of the 1919 reforms.
10. Do not present Gandhi as inheriting an automatic ready-made mass movement.
