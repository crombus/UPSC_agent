# Independence and Partition, 1946-1947 - Live Learning Session

> **Learner-first lock:** This session asks how a constitutional deadlock became territorial division, why political leaders accepted a settlement they had long resisted, and how hurried state-making exposed millions of ordinary people to displacement and violence. Constitutional mechanics and human consequences are taught together. Facts are distinguished from analytical inferences, disputed numbers remain qualified, and Partition is never treated as inevitable from the beginning.

*The compromise did not collapse because nobody had accepted it; it collapsed because acceptance rested on incompatible understandings of the same machinery.*

### Start with the intuition

A constitutional compromise can survive disagreement if the parties disagree about policy inside a shared institution. It cannot survive when they disagree about what the institution itself is allowed to become. Congress treated the proposed Constituent Assembly as a sovereign body capable of revising inherited limits. The Muslim League treated compulsory provincial grouping as the minimum guarantee that the Muslim-majority zones would not be absorbed into a powerful all-India centre.

✅ **Fact:** The League accepted the Cabinet Mission Plan on **6 June 1946**, seeing in compulsory grouping the basis of Pakistan without an immediate sovereign partition.

✅ **Fact:** Congress agreed to enter the Constituent Assembly but did not accept that the Assembly would remain permanently bound by every prior limitation. At the AICC on **7 July 1946**, Jawaharlal Nehru emphasised the Assembly's freedom to decide its procedure.

✅ **Fact:** On **29 July 1946**, the League withdrew its acceptance and called for Direct Action.

⚠️ **Inference:** Nehru's statement was an important trigger, but it is too simple to make one speech the sole cause of collapse. The Plan contained a real ambiguity: it described grouping as optional in principle while prescribing compulsory section meetings in operation. The parties had accepted different constitutional futures, not a common settlement.

### The disagreement, step by step

1. Britain sought a united India with a weak centre controlling defence, foreign affairs and communications.
2. Congress accepted entry into constitution-making but feared that compulsory groups would paralyse the Union and trap Assam and the North-West Frontier Province.
3. The League accepted because grouping gave Muslim-majority provinces collective constitutional weight.
4. Each side expected later procedure to confirm its own interpretation.
5. Nehru's sovereignty claim made the League fear that Congress would revise grouping after entering the Assembly.
6. League withdrawal destroyed the only all-India compromise then available.
7. Yet Partition was still not mechanically predetermined: negotiations, Interim Government participation and the Assembly's future remained open questions.

### What the 1945-46 elections proved - and did not prove

| Supported conclusion | Unsupported leap |
|---|---|
| Congress dominated general constituencies | Every adult Indian voted |
| League became the electorally certified representative of most Muslim electorates | The election was a territorial plebiscite on a precisely defined Pakistan |
| Any settlement excluding the League lacked political viability | Boundaries, population transfers and the constitutional form of Pakistan were settled |
| Separate electorates organised representation communally | Every Muslim voter held one uniform view |

The franchise covered only a minority of the population. The election altered bargaining power, but it did not make a detailed partition plan inevitable.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Once the League won Muslim seats and called for Pakistan, a united India was already impossible.

**Reply:** The League accepted the Cabinet Mission arrangement after the elections. That fact does not prove unity would have survived, but it proves that a united constitutional structure remained negotiable. The proper conclusion is **mandate without inevitability**.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** 6 June acceptance, 7 July Congress interpretation, 29 July League withdrawal.
- **Mains:** Explain collapse through ambiguity, rival sovereignty claims and altered electoral legitimacy.
- **Trap:** The Cabinet Mission did not partition India.
- **Answer use:** Treat this lesson as the short-term constitutional cause, not as a complete explanation of Partition.

### Revision notes

1. The Mission tried to preserve one India through a weak Union and provincial grouping.
2. Congress and the League accepted it through incompatible interpretations.
3. Congress stressed Constituent Assembly sovereignty.
4. The League treated compulsory grouping as its minimum safeguard.
5. The League accepted on 6 June 1946.
6. Nehru's AICC statement came on 7 July.
7. The League withdrew acceptance on 29 July.
8. Elections changed representative claims but were not a universal plebiscite.
9. The Plan's ambiguity was structural, not merely verbal.
10. Breakdown made Partition more likely without proving it had always been inevitable.

#### Local Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the Cabinet Mission settlement fail even though both Congress and the Muslim League had accepted it in some form?

**Model answer:**
The Cabinet Mission settlement failed because acceptance concealed incompatible constitutional projects. Congress entered the Constituent Assembly believing that a sovereign constitution-making body could decide its own procedure and resist compulsory grouping, especially for Assam and the North-West Frontier Province. The League accepted on 6 June 1946 because compulsory Sections B and C appeared to preserve Muslim-majority blocs within a weak Union.

Nehru's 7 July statement that the Assembly was not bound beyond its decision to enter intensified the League's fear that Congress would revise grouping after gaining institutional control. The League withdrew acceptance on 29 July and chose Direct Action.

The speech was therefore a trigger, not a sufficient explanation. The Plan itself combined optional grouping in principle with compulsory sectional procedure. The 1945-46 elections had also hardened rival representative claims. Thus a shared text failed because it did not embody a shared constitutional future.

             |

             |

*Participation in the executive did not produce agreement on the state being created.*

### Why an Interim Government mattered

An interim government was supposed to prepare Indians to exercise central power before the British departure. It was not yet a sovereign national cabinet: the Viceroy remained central to the constitutional structure. But it shifted Indian leaders from demanding power to administering defence, finance, external affairs and other portfolios under transitional constraints.

✅ **Fact:** The Interim Government was formed on **2 September 1946** initially with Congress members, with Nehru as its de facto head.

✅ **Fact:** The League joined on **26 October 1946**. Its entry did not mean acceptance of the Cabinet Mission's long-term settlement or withdrawal of Direct Action.

✅ **Fact:** **Liaquat Ali Khan** held the Finance portfolio. League and Congress members repeatedly clashed over appointments, policy and collective working.

⚠️ **Inference:** The executive became a rehearsal for two rival sovereignties. Each side could use office to prevent the other from converting temporary control into a permanent constitutional advantage.

### Constituent Assembly: formation versus participation

The Assembly's members were indirectly elected by provincial legislatures under the Cabinet Mission scheme; princely-state representatives were to be nominated through a separate process. It was not elected by universal adult suffrage.

✅ **Fact:** The Constituent Assembly first met on **9 December 1946**.

✅ **Fact:** The Muslim League boycotted its opening and later demanded its dissolution.

✅ **Fact:** Congress and other participating members continued constitution-making. This created a practical centre of constitutional authority for the territories represented, even before the final partition decision.

| Institution | Congress objective | League objective | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interim Government | Demonstrate capacity to govern and accelerate transfer | Prevent exclusive Congress control and bargain from inside | Conflict rather than cabinet solidarity |
| Constituent Assembly | Build a sovereign constitution-making body | Avoid entering a body that might override grouping and Pakistan claims | Assembly proceeds without League |

### Why this was more than personality conflict

1. Cabinet government requires collective responsibility.
2. Congress and League ministers did not agree on the future sovereign unit.
3. Administrative decisions therefore carried constitutional meaning.
4. Finance, appointments and procedure became instruments of bargaining.
5. An executive divided over the identity of the state could not behave like a normal coalition.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** League entry proves that cooperation was restored after Direct Action.

**Reply:** Entry reduced the danger of total exclusion but did not settle the constitutional dispute. The League joined the executive while boycotting constitution-making. Participation and acceptance must therefore be distinguished.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** 2 September, 26 October and 9 December are separate thresholds.
- **Mains:** Use the phrase "executive participation without constitutional partnership."
- **Trap:** The Assembly was not formed by universal adult franchise.
- **Boundary:** Detailed Constitution-making belongs to Polity; this lesson explains only its role in the Partition sequence.

### Revision notes

1. Interim Government began on 2 September 1946.
2. It initially contained Congress members without the League.
3. League entered on 26 October.
4. League entry did not end Direct Action or secure cabinet solidarity.
5. Liaquat Ali Khan controlled Finance.
6. Executive conflict reflected rival ideas of sovereignty.
7. Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December.
8. League boycotted the opening.
9. The Assembly was indirectly elected, not based on universal adult suffrage.
10. Congress proceeded with constitution-making despite incomplete participation.
11. The combination created executive coexistence and constitutional separation.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** "The Interim Government was less a coalition than a constitutional conflict conducted through administration." Examine.

**Model answer:**
The Interim Government was designed as a bridge from colonial rule to responsible Indian power. It began on 2 September 1946 with Congress members and Nehru as de facto head. The Muslim League entered on 26 October, but its participation did not imply acceptance of the Cabinet Mission's long-term scheme or abandonment of Direct Action.

Normal coalition government presupposes agreement on the political unit being governed. That foundation was absent. Congress sought to demonstrate the viability of one central authority and to accelerate transfer. The League feared that exclusive Congress control would prejudice Pakistan and therefore entered to contest power from within. Liaquat Ali Khan's Finance portfolio, disputes over appointments and separate consultations turned administrative procedure into constitutional bargaining.

The division was sharper because the Constituent Assembly met on 9 December without League participation. The League could therefore share temporary executive office while refusing partnership in the institution framing the permanent state.

Yet the arrangement was not meaningless. It transferred experience and departments to Indian hands and kept communication open. Its failure lay in expecting cabinet solidarity before agreement on sovereignty. It became executive coexistence without constitutional partnership, revealing why the Cabinet Mission compromise could not stabilise the transfer.

### Visual: political mobilisation becomes reciprocal mass violence

```text
League withdrawal, 29 July
          |
call for Direct Action
          |
16 August public mobilisation in Calcutta
          |
administrative failure + inflammatory politics + organised groups
          |
initial attacks and street confrontation
          |

          |

          |

```

*The chain has identifiable political and administrative causes, but no single community-level essence explains it.*

### What "Direct Action" meant

The League's decision marked a shift from reliance on British-mediated constitutional bargaining to mass political pressure for Pakistan. "Direct Action" did not itself specify one uniform operational method across India. In Calcutta, however, the declared day became the setting for catastrophic communal violence.

✅ **Fact:** **16 August 1946** was observed as Direct Action Day.

✅ **Fact:** Calcutta experienced several days of mass killing, arson and looting, often called the Great Calcutta Killings.

✅ **Fact:** The Bengal ministry was headed by **H. S. Suhrawardy** of the Muslim League. Questions about governmental preparation, policing and response are central to historical assessment.

✅ **Fact with source qualification:** The consulted Bipan Chandra account records about **5,000 deaths** in the Calcutta violence. Casualty totals differ across histories; the number must be attributed rather than presented as an exact final count.

### A careful causal model

| Level | Mechanism | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| High politics | League withdrawal and pressure for Pakistan | Supplied the mobilisation context |
| Provincial administration | Holiday, policing and response failures remain contested | Shaped opportunity and containment |
| Organised politics | Communal groups mobilised crowds and retaliatory action | Converted slogans into targeted violence |
| Urban structure | Mixed neighbourhoods, economic rivalry and dense streets | Made local escalation rapid |
| Information | Rumour magnified fear and revenge | Extended violence beyond initiating incidents |
| Reciprocal agency | Muslim and Hindu communal organisations participated in attack and retaliation | Prevents one-directional or community-essentialist explanation |

⚠️ **Inference:** Responsibility can be assigned to decisions, organisations and officials without treating entire religious communities as collective perpetrators.

### Non-monocausal framing

Three errors must be avoided:

1. **Ancient-hatred error:** It erases modern political organisation, colonial institutions and immediate administrative choices.
2. **Single-speech error:** Political calls matter, but violence required mobilisation, opportunity, local actors and failed restraint.
3. **Symmetry-as-evasion error:** Recognising retaliation by multiple organisations does not eliminate the need to examine who initiated particular phases or who held official responsibility.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling the violence reciprocal excuses the political decision that created the day.

**Reply:** No. The League's call and the Bengal government's conduct require scrutiny. Reciprocal analysis adds the later dynamics of retaliation and local agency; it does not erase the initiating political context.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Direct Action Day = 16 August 1946.
- **Mains:** Separate trigger, enabling conditions, escalation and responsibility.
- **Trap:** Do not write "communities naturally turned violent."
- **Evidence rule:** Attribute casualty figures and acknowledge variation.

### Revision notes

1. League withdrew Cabinet Mission acceptance on 29 July.
2. Direct Action Day was 16 August.
3. Calcutta violence lasted several days.
4. Suhrawardy headed the Bengal ministry.
5. Political mobilisation supplied the immediate context.
6. Administrative preparation and response affected escalation.
7. Organised communal groups had agency.
8. Rumour and neighbourhood fear widened violence.
9. Retaliation made the violence reciprocal without cancelling initiating responsibility.
10. The Bipan account records about 5,000 deaths; totals vary.
11. Assign responsibility to actors and institutions, not entire communities.
12. Calcutta deepened distrust but did not mechanically dictate every later decision.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain the Great Calcutta Killings through a non-monocausal framework.

**Model answer:**
The Great Calcutta Killings followed the Muslim League's withdrawal from the Cabinet Mission Plan on 29 July 1946 and its call for Direct Action on 16 August. The political decision supplied the immediate mobilisation context, but it does not by itself explain several days of urban mass violence.

The League ministry under H. S. Suhrawardy faced serious questions concerning preparation, policing and containment. Organised Muslim communal groups participated in attacks; Hindu communal groups retaliated. Dense mixed neighbourhoods, local economic antagonisms, looting, rumour and fear converted political demonstration into cycles of revenge. The Bipan Chandra account records about 5,000 deaths, though totals vary and must remain attributed.

A balanced explanation therefore rejects both "ancient communal hatred" and responsibility-free symmetry. The Direct Action call, provincial administrative conduct and initiating mobilisation require scrutiny. At the same time, later retaliation and local organisation had independent causal force.

The event's wider significance was political: constitutional conflict now appeared capable of producing mass insecurity. It hardened community fears and made subsequent bargaining occur under the shadow of violence, while still not making the precise form or timing of Partition inevitable.

*The common element was communal mobilisation; the local social structure, state response and sequence differed sharply.*

### Noakhali and Bihar: retaliation is not explanation enough

✅ **Fact:** Violence in **Noakhali** in October 1946 included killings, forced displacement, coercion and assaults directed largely against Hindu communities in affected rural areas.

✅ **Fact:** In **Bihar**, retaliatory violence targeted Muslims on a large scale.

⚠️ **Inference:** Calling Bihar merely "revenge for Noakhali" explains the language used by perpetrators, not the legitimacy or full causation of the massacres. Retaliation remains a choice organised through local leadership, rumour and state failure.

### Punjab: why 1947 became different

Punjab combined four volatile conditions:

1. Muslims, Sikhs and Hindus were territorially intermingled.
2. The province was strategically central and heavily militarised.
3. The Unionist coalition's collapse removed an important cross-communal political structure.
4. A possible boundary transformed neighbours into minorities on an unknown side of a future border.

The resignation of **Khizr Hayat Khan Tiwana's** ministry in March 1947 intensified the crisis. Violence in the Rawalpindi division, including attacks on Sikh villages, fed fear and preparations for retaliatory force. Sikh leaders faced the prospect that undivided Punjab could place major Sikh populations and sacred centres inside Pakistan.

### Gandhi's method: peace as presence

```text

    |
    v
Gandhi travels to affected localities
    |
    +-- walking and village contact
    +-- prayer meetings and moral appeal
    +-- pressure on local leaders
    +-- insistence on minority security
    +-- refusal to justify retaliatory violence
    |
    v
local calming possible, structural settlement unresolved
```

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi worked in Noakhali and Bihar, rejecting the logic that one massacre could justify another.

✅ **Fact:** At independence he was in Bengal, engaged in peace work rather than participating in Delhi's principal celebrations.

⚠️ **Inference:** Gandhi's presence could restore local confidence where parties accepted his moral authority, but it could not by itself rebuild a collapsing all-India constitutional compact or replace police and administrative capacity.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Gandhi's peace tours were symbolic because they did not prevent Partition.

**Reply:** Preventing Partition was not the only measure of effectiveness. Local de-escalation, minority protection and refusal of collective guilt mattered. The limit is real: moral intervention could not substitute for functioning institutions across Punjab and Bengal.

*The announcement did not itself partition India; it transformed delay from a British option into a shrinking political resource.*

### The exact constitutional change

✅ **Fact:** On **20 February 1947**, Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced Britain's "definite intention" to transfer power into responsible Indian hands by a date **not later than June 1948**.

✅ **Fact:** If a fully representative constitution had not emerged, Britain would decide how and to whom power should be transferred. That contingency made transfer to more than one authority possible without yet announcing the final partition plan.

✅ **Fact:** Attlee also announced that **Lord Mountbatten** would replace Wavell as Viceroy.

### Why a deadline mattered

1. It made British departure credible to Congress.
2. It warned the League that the British would not mediate indefinitely.
3. It allowed provincial and communal actors to calculate where power would lie after withdrawal.
4. It risked encouraging obstruction: a party might expect the deadline to deliver its minimum demand.
5. It exposed an administrative problem. Authority could decline faster than successor institutions could be prepared.

⚠️ **Inference:** Deadlines can compel compromise only when actors fear the costs of non-agreement more than they value their separate fallback. In 1947, the League increasingly believed its fallback was Pakistan, while Congress increasingly believed its fallback was a strong but partitioned India.

### Withdrawal date versus transfer date

| Date or phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Not later than June 1948 | Outer deadline announced by Attlee |
| 30 June 1948 | Calendar date associated with the original terminal schedule in contemporary planning |
| 15 August 1947 | Earlier date ultimately selected for dominion transfer |

Do not collapse these into one date. The February statement set the outer limit; the June Plan accelerated actual transfer into 1947.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The deadline solved the political crisis because all parties now knew Britain would leave.

**Reply:** It solved uncertainty about whether Britain intended to leave, not disagreement about the successor state. Indeed, a credible exit could intensify the race to control territory, institutions and security before departure.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** 20 February 1947; transfer no later than June 1948; Mountbatten's appointment.
- **Mains:** Analyse a deadline as both commitment device and source of acceleration.
- **Trap:** The announcement was not the 3 June Plan.
- **Answer use:** Connect declining imperial authority to the risks of hurried transfer without claiming that Britain had instantly lost coercive power.

6. Mountbatten was announced as the new Viceroy.
7. The deadline increased bargaining pressure.
8. It also encouraged actors to prepare separate fallbacks.
9. Credible exit did not equal constitutional agreement.
10. Actual transfer was later advanced to 15 August 1947.

*Mountbatten accelerated and shaped the settlement, but he did not act as an unconstrained personal sovereign.*

### Arrival and mandate

✅ **Fact:** Mountbatten assumed office as Viceroy on **24 March 1947**.

✅ **Fact:** His task was to secure transfer by the announced deadline. He consulted Congress, League and Sikh leaders, provincial officials and the British government.

✅ **Fact:** He concluded rapidly that the Cabinet Mission framework could not be revived on terms acceptable to both Congress and League.

⚠️ **Inference:** Speed was partly a response to deteriorating authority and violence, but the decision to compress transfer into roughly ten weeks after the 3 June announcement was still a policy choice with consequences.

### What he could and could not decide

| Mountbatten could influence | Mountbatten could not simply dictate |
|---|---|
| Consultation sequence and presentation | Party acceptance |
| Advice on timing and plan design | A viable united settlement rejected by principal parties |
| Recommendation to London | British legislation without Cabinet and Parliament |
| Administrative coordination | Social peace in every locality |
| Public communication | League and Congress objectives |

The Viceroy had greater room for manoeuvre than many predecessors, but he repeatedly referred proposals to London. The image of a single man independently writing the whole settlement is therefore misleading.

### The "limited Pakistan" logic

The League demanded Pakistan. Congress insisted that if India were divided, **Punjab and Bengal must also be divided**; Muslim-majority provincial governments could not take large non-Muslim-majority regions into Pakistan as undivided units. Sikh leaders similarly opposed inclusion of the whole Punjab in Pakistan.

This produced a harsh symmetry:

```text
League argument: Muslim-majority zones may leave India
                         |
Congress/Sikh reply: non-Muslim-majority zones may leave those provinces
                         |
                         v
Pakistan conceded, but Punjab and Bengal divided
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Mountbatten alone caused Partition by imposing a personal plan.

**Reply:** His acceleration and boundary-publication decisions deserve scrutiny, but the underlying deadlock predated him. League insistence on sovereignty, Congress rejection of a weak paralysed Union, Sikh opposition to an undivided Punjab in Pakistan and spreading violence constrained the available settlement.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Mountbatten assumed office on 24 March 1947.
- **Mains:** Distinguish agency from omnipotence.
- **Trap:** Do not treat the 3 June Plan as an unreviewed personal decree.
- **Historiography:** Evaluate "great man" claims against institutional and party constraints.

## Lesson 7 - The 3 June Plan, provision by provision

Progress: 7/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Exact political machinery of Partition

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried - official Commons statement of 3 June 1947 and canonical distinctions

CA found: None; the official plan text controls the provisions
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: political plan, not yet statute or boundary

```text
3 JUNE PLAN
   |
   +-- Existing Constituent Assembly continues
   +-- Areas unwilling to accept it may choose a separate Assembly
   +-- Bengal and Punjab assemblies vote in Muslim/non-Muslim sections
   +-- partition follows if either section votes for it
   +-- Sind Assembly decides its constitutional route
   +-- NWFP referendum
   +-- Sylhet referendum if Bengal is partitioned
   +-- boundary commissions if Punjab/Bengal are divided
   +-- negotiations on defence, finance, communications, assets and services
   +-- transfer advanced to 1947 on dominion-status basis
```

*The Plan created a decision procedure. Parliament would later create the dominions, and Radcliffe would later locate provincial boundaries.*

### First principle: no imposed final constitution

The official announcement stated that Britain would not frame India's ultimate constitution. The existing Constituent Assembly could continue, but its constitution could not bind territories unwilling to accept it. Those areas could form a new Constituent Assembly.

### Bengal and Punjab procedure

✅ **Fact:** Each provincial legislative assembly, excluding European members, would meet in two sections:

- members from Muslim-majority districts;
- members from the rest of the province.

✅ **Fact:** If a simple majority of either section voted for partition, the province would be divided.

✅ **Fact:** If partition occurred, each part would decide which Constituent Assembly would frame its constitution.

### Territorial choice outside the two provinces

| Area | Procedure under the Plan |
|---|---|
| Sind | Provincial Legislative Assembly decision |
| North-West Frontier Province | Referendum among the existing provincial electorate on the constitutional route |
| British Baluchistan | Opportunity to reconsider which Constituent Assembly to join |
| Sylhet | Referendum on remaining in Assam or joining Eastern Bengal if Bengal was partitioned |
| Rest of Assam | Continued in the existing Constituent Assembly |

### Boundary and administrative provisions

✅ **Fact:** If Punjab or Bengal voted for partition, a Boundary Commission would demarcate contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims while taking "other factors" into account.

✅ **Fact:** Successor authorities were to negotiate division of central subjects, including **Defence, Finance and Communications**.

✅ **Fact:** Partitioned provinces also required division of assets and liabilities, police, services, High Courts and provincial institutions.

✅ **Fact:** The Plan offered transfer during **1947** on a **Dominion status** basis, without prejudicing the later right of constituent assemblies to decide their Commonwealth relationship.

### What the Plan did not do

- It did not itself enact the dominions.
- It did not draw the final boundaries.
- It did not require an exchange of population.
- It did not grant princely states an operational licence to ignore geography and successor-state realities.
- It did not guarantee peaceful implementation.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The Plan democratically settled every territorial question.

**Reply:** It used elected provincial institutions and limited-franchise referendums, not universal votes across every disputed locality. District majorities guided boundary work, but "other factors" and mixed populations made demarcation contentious.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Distinguish assembly vote, referendum and boundary commission.
- **Mains:** Organise the Plan under political choice, territorial procedure and administrative consequence.
- **Trap:** NWFP and Sylhet had referendums; Punjab and Bengal used sectional legislative votes followed by commissions.
- **Instrument rule:** Plan decides procedure; Act gives legal force; Award draws lines.

- League refusal to join the existing Constituent Assembly;

The League secured the principle of a sovereign Pakistan. It accepted a Pakistan smaller than its maximum claim because rejecting the Plan risked losing a definite transfer timetable and internationally recognised successor state.

The cost was division of Punjab and Bengal, limiting the territorial reach that an undivided-province formula might have produced.

Sikh populations, land and sacred centres were distributed across Punjab. An undivided Punjab joining Pakistan could place much of the community under a Muslim-majority state. ⚠️ **Inference:** "Inevitable" is a counterfactual claim. It cannot be proved merely because Partition occurred. A better formulation is:

Districts were not internally homogeneous. A district majority could contain tehsils, villages, canal colonies, cities or transport links with a different composition. ### Core provisions

✅ **Fact:** The **Indian Independence Act 1947** received Royal Assent on **18 July 1947**.

✅ **Fact:** It provided that from **15 August 1947**, two independent dominions, **India and Pakistan**, would be established.

✅ **Fact:** The constituent assemblies of the two dominions could make laws for their territories and frame their constitutions.

✅ **Fact:** British suzerainty or paramountcy over Indian states lapsed, as did treaty relationships dependent on it.

### Dominion status: dependence or sovereign transition?

Dominion status retained the Crown as the formal head represented by a Governor-General. Yet the new dominion legislatures possessed full law-making authority, including constitutional authority. India could and did move to a republic through its own Constituent Assembly.

| Misreading | Correction |

| India became a republic on 15 August 1947 | India became an independent dominion; the republic began on 26 January 1950 |
| British paramountcy transferred to India | Paramountcy lapsed |
| The Act drew every district boundary | Radcliffe awards performed that function |
| The Act completed princely integration | It created the legal opening; political integration followed |

### Transfer as constitutional discontinuity and administrative continuity

The Act ended British legislative supremacy, but government could not be rebuilt overnight. Adaptation of the 1935 Act, continuation of officials and temporary institutional arrangements supplied continuity. Independence therefore combined a legal break with administrative inheritance.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling India independent on 15 August is inaccurate because it remained a dominion.

**Reply:** Dominion status retained a constitutional link to the Crown, but legislative sovereignty had transferred and Britain could no longer legislate automatically for India. The later republic changed the head-of-state framework; it did not retroactively create independence.

- **Trap:** The 3 June Plan was political; the Act legal; the Award territorial.
- **Boundary:** Detailed accession and integration belong to Topic 28.

*Refugee policy was not only charity; it was an early test of who the new state recognised, protected and resettled.*

### Immediate response

India and Pakistan inherited administrations already divided and overwhelmed. Emergency responses included refugee camps, medical aid, food distribution, escorted convoys and special evacuation arrangements. Military and civilian agencies attempted to secure trains and road columns, though capacity and conduct varied.

In India, central, provincial and local authorities worked with voluntary organisations. Delhi and East Punjab faced an immediate western influx; Bengal and eastern India faced longer and repeated migration.

### Rehabilitation was more than relief

| Relief | Rehabilitation |
|---|---|
| food, shelter and urgent medicine | permanent housing and settlement |
| safe transit and evacuation | land, employment, credit and training |
| camp registration | compensation and property claims |
| immediate family tracing | schools, colonies and social integration |

⚠️ **Inference:** Rehabilitation redistributed opportunity as well as hardship. Refugees could become entrepreneurs, workers, farmers and political constituencies, but outcomes differed by class, caste, gender, skill, region and access to claims machinery.

### Evacuee property and compensation

People left houses, farms, businesses, tools, debts and documents on both sides. Successor states developed custodial and claims systems for "evacuee property." Property abandoned in one dominion could not simply be returned across a hardened border, so compensation became linked to assets available in the receiving state.

The apparent symmetry concealed unequal cases:

- urban house and shop versus irrigated agricultural land;
- documented ownership versus customary possession;
- wealthy claimant versus person who fled without papers;
- western one-time flight versus repeated eastern movement.

### Abducted-person recovery

India and Pakistan created inter-dominion arrangements and later legislation for locating and restoring abducted women and children. Recovery teams, police and social workers crossed a politically charged field of missing-person lists, family claims and contested identities.

✅ **Fact with qualification:** Frequently cited official compilations record roughly **20,000 Muslim women and children recovered from India** and about **9,000 Hindu and Sikh women recovered from Pakistan** by the mid-1950s. Definitions, periods and incomplete reporting mean these figures should be treated as administrative records, not a complete count of abduction or survival.

The ethical problem was profound. A woman might have formed a new household, borne children or rejected the family claiming her. State agents often defined her "proper" national and religious location without treating her present consent as decisive.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Criticising coercive recovery ignores the need to rescue abducted women.

**Reply:** Rescue was essential. The criticism concerns procedures that treated women as recoverable community property rather than rights-bearing persons. A policy can have a justified protective purpose and still reproduce patriarchal control in implementation.

- **Mains:** Move from rescue to rehabilitation to citizenship and institutional capacity.
- **Comparison:** Western and eastern refugee experiences differed in tempo, property and policy.
- **Ethics:** Ask whose consent defined "restoration."
- **Trap:** Do not present administrative recovery totals as the total number abducted.

*A line on a map could not operate until money, people, files, weapons and networks were assigned.*

### The Partition Council function

Representatives of the successor authorities used a Partition Council and specialised machinery to supervise division. The tasks ranged from major financial liabilities to office furniture and printing equipment. The apparent triviality of dividing typewriters reveals a deeper truth: every administrative function required physical capacity.

### Armed forces

The British Indian armed forces had been recruited, trained and commanded as one system. Partition required:

- allocation of units and personnel;
- options and transfers shaped partly by religious composition and domicile;
- division of equipment, stores, training establishments and records;
- creation of separate command structures;
- arrangements for frontier and internal security during migration.

Field Marshal **Claude Auchinleck**, as Supreme Commander, oversaw the reconstitution machinery during transition.

⚠️ **Inference:** The same forces were being divided while expected to suppress violence and protect migrants. Institutional partition therefore reduced the coherence of the principal instrument meant to manage partition's consequences.

### Finance, services and communications

Assets and liabilities had to be apportioned between successor governments. Cash balances became politically contentious. Civil servants chose or were allocated between administrations, while shortages were especially serious for the new Pakistan government. Railways, postal networks, telegraphs, audit systems, records and river-management arrangements crossed the new border.

Joint mechanisms were created because immediate separation was technically impossible. They were fragile. Bipan Chandra notes that joint defence machinery did not survive beyond **December 1947**, by which time armed conflict over Kashmir had begun. The Kashmir conflict itself belongs to Topic 28.

### Princely states: the exact boundary of this session

✅ **Fact:** The Independence Act caused British paramountcy to lapse.

✅ **Fact:** States were not automatically transferred as provinces to either dominion.

✅ **Fact:** In practice, accession was structured through the new dominions, geography and the limited subjects of **defence, external affairs and communications** in the Instrument of Accession framework.

⚠️ **Inference:** Legal lapse did not make hundreds of durable independent sovereign states a practical answer. Geography, economy, popular politics and the power of successor governments constrained nominal choice.

**Forward route:** The integration strategy, Instruments of Accession, Standstill Agreements, States Department and the cases of Kashmir, Hyderabad and Junagadh are taught in Topic 28.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Administrative division was secondary because political sovereignty had already been decided.

**Reply:** Sovereignty without records, revenue, communications, personnel and force cannot function. Administrative division was the material construction of the two states and directly affected their capacity to protect citizens.

Units, personnel, equipment and commands were being separated under transitional machinery associated with Auchinleck precisely when troops were required to protect convoys and suppress communal violence. Joint arrangements were necessary but fragile; joint defence machinery did not last beyond December 1947.

The lapse of paramountcy created another institutional frontier. States did not become ordinary provinces automatically; accession arrangements had to connect them to successor dominions, a process treated separately in Topic 28.

Thus sovereignty was not complete when a flag rose or a statute commenced. It became effective through control of money, files, communications, personnel and force. The speed of division weakened that capacity at the moment citizens needed it most.

**Model length:** 200 words.
**Component rubric:** 2 marks for the material-statehood thesis; 3 for finance, records and services; 3 for armed-force division and Auchinleck; 2 for communications and joint machinery; 2 for Pakistan's capacity problem; 2 for the princely-state boundary; 1 for the conclusion. Total: 15.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the constitutional functions of the 3 June Plan, the Indian Independence Act and the Radcliffe awards.

**Model answer:**
The **3 June Plan** was a political procedure. It allowed the existing Constituent Assembly to continue, provided a separate constitutional route for unwilling areas, prescribed sectional votes in Punjab and Bengal, and arranged referendums in the North-West Frontier Province and Sylhet.

The **Indian Independence Act**, receiving Royal Assent on 18 July 1947, was the legal instrument. It created India and Pakistan as independent dominions from 15 August, empowered their constituent assemblies to legislate and frame constitutions, continued adapted 1935 Act machinery temporarily, and ended British paramountcy.

The **Radcliffe awards** were territorial determinations. Separate commissions demarcated divided Punjab and Bengal using contiguous religious majorities and other factors. Their publication on 17 August came after transfer.

Thus the Plan decided the route, the Act created sovereign authorities, and the awards located provincial borders. Confusing them hides the fragmented timing that aggravated administrative disorder.

**Model length:** 141 words.
**Component rubric:** 3 marks for the 3 June Plan's political function; 3 for the Independence Act's legal function; 3 for the Radcliffe awards' territorial function; 1 for explaining the consequence of separated timing. Total: 10.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Why did the principal political leaderships accept Partition in June 1947 despite its foreseeable risks?

**Model answer:**
Acceptance resulted from different calculations under a rapidly narrowing deadline.

Congress had opposed the two-nation theory, but the Cabinet Mission compromise had collapsed. The Interim Government functioned as an arena of Congress-League struggle, while the League boycotted the Constituent Assembly. Congress leaders feared that compulsory grouping would preserve only nominal unity under a weak centre. Spreading violence and declining authority made immediate control appear essential. They therefore accepted a smaller India with a stronger Union and treated dominion status as temporary.

The Muslim League gained the decisive principle of sovereign Pakistan. It accepted division of Punjab and Bengal, and therefore a smaller state than its widest claim, because the Plan offered rapid and recognised statehood.

Sikh leaders feared that an undivided Muslim-majority Punjab would place Sikh populations, lands and sacred centres inside Pakistan. They sought provincial division despite knowing that no line could protect all interests.

Britain preferred an agreed and rapid exit that preserved future relations and reduced imperial responsibility. Mountbatten's timetable reinforced urgency.

The risks were visible but poorly mastered. Leaders underestimated the likelihood of mass transfer and the administrative consequences of announcing boundaries after independence. Acceptance was therefore neither enthusiastic consensus nor pure inevitability. It was a set of consequential choices made because each leadership judged its alternative worse.

**Model length:** 212 words.
**Component rubric:** 4 marks for Congress calculations; 3 for League calculations; 2 for Sikh calculations; 2 for Britain and timing; 2 for foreseeable risks; 2 for a constraint-with-agency verdict. Total: 15.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Examine how the speed and sequencing of the 1947 transfer converted a political settlement into an administrative and humanitarian crisis.

**Model answer:**
The transfer separated four connected tasks onto dangerously compressed clocks. The 3 June Plan created procedures for provincial division and successor assemblies. The Indian Independence Act followed on 18 July and fixed dominion transfer for 15 August. Boundary commissions then worked under severe time pressure, while the Punjab and Bengal awards were disclosed only on 17 August.

This sequence meant that residents and officials entered independence without final knowledge of jurisdiction. Mixed districts, canals, railways and cities could not be allocated by census arithmetic alone. Rumour and fear encouraged flight; organised militias and partisan or overwhelmed local institutions converted movement into massacre.

Meanwhile, the machinery needed to manage the crisis was itself being divided. Armed forces, police, civil services, treasuries, records, railways and communications had to be apportioned. Joint arrangements were temporary. Refugee flows therefore outran camp, transport, medical and security capacity.

The consequences were regionally unequal. Punjab underwent a rapid near-total population exchange; Bengal experienced recurrent, longer migration. Roughly 10-15 million people were displaced, while mortality estimates vary from about 200,000 to 2 million. Women faced abduction and sexual violence; later recovery could rescue but also override autonomy.

The political deadlock made some rapid settlement understandable, but urgency does not erase policy choice. Accelerated transfer and delayed boundary publication did not alone cause violence; they magnified the consequences of prior communal mobilisation and institutional collapse.

**Model length:** 227 words.
**Component rubric:** 3 marks for the instrument sequence; 3 for boundary timing and jurisdictional uncertainty; 4 for institutional division and weakened capacity; 3 for displacement and violence mechanisms; 3 for regional and gender differentiation; 2 for causal qualification; 2 for the verdict. Total: 20.

- Never attribute collective guilt to an entire religious community.

## 4. Noakhali, Bihar, Punjab and Gandhi

- Noakhali violence in October 1946 targeted Hindu communities in affected rural areas.
- Bihar witnessed major retaliatory massacres of Muslims.
- Revenge explains perpetrator language, not moral legitimacy.
- Punjab's mixed population, militarisation and coalition collapse made boundary fear acute.
- Khizr Hayat Khan Tiwana resigned in March 1947.
- Rawalpindi violence intensified Sikh fear and retaliatory preparation.
- Gandhi worked through village presence, prayer and defence of minorities.
- He rejected one massacre as justification for another.
- Gandhi was in Bengal at independence.
- Moral intervention had real local value but could not replace state capacity.

## 5. Attlee and Mountbatten

- Attlee announcement: 20 February 1947.
- Britain intended transfer no later than June 1948.
- If no representative constitution emerged, recipient authority remained open.
- Mountbatten assumed office on 24 March.
- He consulted major Indian and British actors and abandoned revival of the Mission framework.
- League wanted sovereign Pakistan.
- Congress preferred immediate power and a strong centre.
- Sikh leaders resisted whole Punjab entering Pakistan.
- Mountbatten shaped and accelerated the settlement but required London approval.
- Speed was understandable under crisis but remained a consequential policy choice.

## 6. 3 June Plan

- Existing Constituent Assembly continued.
- Unwilling areas could choose a separate Constituent Assembly.
- Bengal and Punjab assemblies met in Muslim-majority and non-Muslim-majority sections.
- A simple majority for partition in either section triggered provincial division.
- Sind decided through its Legislative Assembly.
- NWFP held a referendum.
- Sylhet held a conditional referendum on Assam or Eastern Bengal.
- Boundary commissions followed a decision to divide Punjab or Bengal.
- Criteria: contiguous religious-majority areas plus other factors.
- Defence, finance and communications required successor negotiation.
- Provincial division included assets, police, services, High Courts and institutions.
- Transfer was advanced to 1947 on a dominion-status basis.

## 7. Political acceptance

- Congress accepted Partition reluctantly but actively.
- Its calculations: Interim Government paralysis, Assembly boycott, violence, strong centre and immediate power.
- Dominion status was accepted as transitional.
- League accepted a smaller Pakistan to secure sovereign statehood.
- Sikh leaders sought Punjab division to avoid entire inclusion in Pakistan.
- No actor received a costless outcome.
- Leadership agreement did not equal universal popular consent.
- Explain constraint and agency together.

## 8. Historiography

- Imperial mission accounts understate colonial strategy and institutional responsibility.
- Pure divide-and-rule accounts understate Indian political agency.
- High-politics accounts clarify negotiation but can marginalise lived experience.
- Social histories restore locality, rumour, gender, property and migration.
- Long-term causes: communal representation and ideology.
- Medium-term causes: 1937 conflict, Lahore Resolution, wartime political change and League growth.
- Short-term causes: Mission breakdown, Direct Action, institutional conflict, violence, deadline and accelerated transfer.
- Cabinet Mission acceptance is decisive evidence against early inevitability.
- Best verdict: Partition became the chosen exit from an accumulated crisis; it was not timeless destiny.

## 9. Radcliffe boundaries

- Separate Punjab and Bengal commissions; Cyril Radcliffe chaired both.
- 1941 census district majorities supplied the demographic base.
- "Other factors" allowed consideration beyond population.
- Mixed tehsils, canals, railways, cities and sacred geography complicated Punjab.
- Riverine and commercial networks complicated Bengal.
- No line could create complete homogeneity.
- Transfer occurred 15 August.
- Awards were published 17 August.
- Bipan Chandra states they were ready by 12 August.
- Publication delay aggravated uncertainty and preparation failure.
- Commissions drew lines; they did not order universal population exchange.

## 10. Indian Independence Act

- Royal Assent: 18 July 1947.
- Appointed day: 15 August.
- Created independent dominions of India and Pakistan.
- Constituent assemblies could legislate and frame constitutions.
- Adapted Government of India Act 1935 supplied transitional machinery.
- British automatic legislative supremacy ended.
- Old imperial governmental responsibility ended.
- Paramountcy over Indian states lapsed.
- Dominion status retained the Crown formally but transferred effective sovereignty.
- India became a republic only on 26 January 1950.
- The Act created neither every boundary nor completed princely integration.

## 11. Migration and violence

- Roughly 10-15 million displaced is a responsible common range; some estimates are higher.
- Death estimates vary from about 200,000 to 2 million.
- State the range and uncertainty; do not invent precision.
- Punjab underwent rapid, near-total exchange.
- Bengal's migration was longer and recurrent.
- Violence, fear, expulsion and administrative failure turned partition into migration.
- Trains, road columns and villages became sites of rescue and massacre.
- Militias and local institutions had agency.
- Police and military conduct varied by place.

## 12. Women, abduction and recovery

- Gendered violence included rape, abduction, forced conversion, marriage, mutilation and killing.
- Patriarchal honour made women's bodies symbols of community.
- Family violence and pressured suicide also occurred.
- Women exercised survival, negotiation and protective agency.
- India and Pakistan created recovery machinery.
- Commonly cited estimates place abductions around 75,000-100,000.
- Frequently cited official recovery totals are roughly 20,000 Muslim women and children and 9,000 Hindu and Sikh women.
- These are incomplete administrative records, not total victim counts.
- Recovery could rescue while also overriding consent, new families and children.

## 13. Refugees and state response

- Immediate relief: evacuation, camps, food, medicine, sanitation and registration.
- Rehabilitation: housing, land, work, credit, education and compensation.
- Evacuee-property systems administered abandoned assets.
- Documentation and class shaped claim success.
- Western settlement was rapid; eastern rehabilitation had repeated waves.
- Voluntary organisations supplemented government.
- Refugees rebuilt farms, markets, neighbourhoods and political life.
- Rehabilitation defined citizenship and tested state legitimacy.

## 14. Institutional division and forward boundary

- Partition Council and committees divided assets and liabilities.
- Treasury, debt, cash, buildings, stores, records and equipment mattered.
- Civil services, railways, post and telegraph required separation.
- Armed forces were divided while being used for peacekeeping.
- Auchinleck oversaw transitional military reconstitution.
- Pakistan had to construct central capacity rapidly.
- Joint machinery was necessary but fragile.
- Joint defence arrangements ended by December 1947.
- Paramountcy lapsed; it did not transfer automatically.
- Accession focused initially on defence, external affairs and communications.
- Detailed Kashmir, Hyderabad and Junagadh cases belong to Topic 28.

## Bounded uncertainties

- Mortality, displacement and abduction totals vary because definitions, territorial coverage, reporting periods and records differ. Ranges are retained instead of false precision.
- The frequently cited recovery totals represent recorded administrative operations, not all abducted persons or every survivor's experience.
- The Radcliffe ready date is attributed to Bipan Chandra's account; the public announcement date of 17 August is the firm threshold used in chronology.
- The 2021 objective question is adjacent rather than directly owned because its historical language-movement context develops after 1947.
- Detailed accession disputes, military conflict over Kashmir and the Hyderabad and Junagadh cases are deliberately deferred to Topic 28.
