# Growth of Communalism and the Muslim League - Live Session Edition

This session explains the early growth of communal politics without treating religious communities as timeless political blocs. Its core period runs from the late nineteenth century to the eve of the Lucknow Pact. Topic 16 retains revolutionary nationalism; Topic 18 retains the First World War, Home Rule and the detailed Lucknow settlement.

The first task is to separate **religion** from **communalism**. Religion concerns belief and practice. Communalism is a political way of imagining society: it treats a religious category as if it were a single political community with one set of economic, social and political interests.

### 1. The three-stage model

Bipan Chandra presents communal ideology as a progression:

| Stage | Proposition | Political consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Common interests | Followers of one religion possess common secular interests | A religious population is imagined as one political body |
| Divergent interests | Its interests differ from those of other religious groups | Bargaining is organised between communities |
| Hostile interests | The interests are treated as incompatible | Compromise appears unnatural and conflict appears permanent |

In Chandra's terminology, **stages 1 and 2 constitute liberal or mild communalism**, while **stage 3 is extreme or fascistic communalism**. The labels describe degrees within his ideological model: even the "mild" form remains communal because it assigns common or divergent secular interests to religious communities. "Fascistic" marks the hostile, exclusionary logic of the third stage; it should be attributed to Chandra and not used as a legal classification or as a claim that every organisation at that stage possessed an identical structure.

The stages are analytical, not a rigid chronology. Different arguments can coexist, actors can move in either direction, and constitutional language or professions of nationalism can accompany the first two stages. The third turns presumed difference into permanent antagonism.

### 2. Why communalism is described as modern

Religious difference existed long before colonial rule, but the political machinery required for communalism was modern:

1. a state capable of classifying and counting large populations;
2. representative institutions in which seats had to be allocated;
3. newspapers, associations and public meetings that could claim to speak for a community;
4. modern education and professions that created competition for scarce opportunities;
5. popular politics in which leaders needed a constituency larger than kin, locality or estate.

Pre-modern conflicts could employ religious language, but that does not prove the continuous existence of modern communal ideology. Projecting twentieth-century political identities backward turns changing alliances, classes and regions into an imagined permanent Hindu-Muslim conflict.

#### How communal history-writing produced a political effect

Communal history-writing did more than select unpleasant episodes. It reorganised the past through a misleading religious frame:

```text
medieval centuries described uniformly as "Muslim rule"
        |
Muslims cast collectively as rulers; Hindus collectively as the ruled
        |
dynastic, regional, class and institutional conflicts recoded as religious conflict
        |
composite culture and shared political loyalties pushed out of view
        |
modern communities taught to inherit opposing historical roles
        |
present claims for safeguards, retaliation or political dominance appear natural
```

The simplification suppresses the composite culture formed through shared languages, artistic traditions, devotional movements, local economies and shifting alliances. It also ignores that rulers and subjects were internally divided by dynasty, class, caste, region and political interest. Its political effect was to give modern competition a ready-made story: one community could be portrayed as the historical victor and the other as the historical victim. History then became evidence for present communal mobilisation rather than an inquiry into a complex past.

The qualification matters. Criticising communal history-writing does not require denying conquest, discrimination or religious conflict. It requires explaining each through its specific institutions and context instead of treating an entire medieval period as one unchanging Hindu-Muslim confrontation.

### 3. Historiographical positions

| Approach | Main claim | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primordialist | Ancient religious difference naturally produced modern conflict | Notices inherited symbols and memories | Mistakes difference for inevitable political antagonism |
| Colonial-instrumentalist | Colonial classification and divide-and-rule manufactured communal politics | Explains state incentives and timing | Can reduce Indians to passive objects |
| Socio-economic | Scarcity, uneven education and elite competition produced communal claims | Explains jobs, seats and class leadership | Cannot alone explain ideology or cultural mobilisation |
| Political-cultural | Print, language, history-writing and symbols shaped public identities | Explains how political meaning was communicated | May understate institutions and material interests |
| Multi-causal | Colonial structures interacted with Indian agency, class, region and political choices | Best fits variation and change over time | Requires careful weighting rather than one slogan |

The most defensible conclusion is multi-causal. Colonial rule created powerful incentives and categories, but Indian elites, associations, journalists and organisations made choices within that structure. Neither "the British invented everything" nor "ancient hatred made conflict inevitable" survives close scrutiny.

### 4. Evidence limits and anti-essentialism

- A communal organisation did not represent every member of the religion named in its title.
- A riot does not by itself prove a settled communal ideology among an entire population.
- Muslim political opinion included Congress supporters, Deobandi nationalists, regional leaders, professionals, landlords and loyalists.
- Hindu political opinion ranged across Congress nationalism, social reform, loyalism, caste movements and communal organisations.
- Regional, class, linguistic and agrarian interests often crossed religious boundaries.

> **Core rule:** analyse ideologies, institutions, organisations and interests; never assign one timeless mind or motive to a whole religious community.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If religious symbols were already powerful, why deny that communalism was ancient?

**Reply:** The issue is not whether religion mattered. It is whether people were organised as permanent political blocs with supposedly uniform secular interests. That form depended on modern public politics, representative institutions and organised claim-making. Religious inheritance supplied a vocabulary; modern conditions changed its political function.

### UPSC application

For a conceptual answer, begin with the distinction between religion and political representation. Then explain modern institutions, social competition and political agency. End by rejecting inevitability.

### Revision notes

1. Communalism is an ideology, not a synonym for religion.
2. It converts religious identity into claims about secular interests.
3. Chandra's stages move from common to divergent to hostile interests.
4. Modern mass politics and representation are necessary parts of the explanation.
5. Primordialism mistakes inherited difference for inevitable antagonism.
6. Colonial policy was powerful but not the only cause.
7. Indian elites and organisations possessed agency.
8. Regions and classes prevent any homogeneous-community explanation.
9. Communal violence is a possible consequence, not the definition.
10. Pakistan must not be projected backward into the League's birth.

**Local Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words**

**Question:** Explain why communalism in colonial India should be treated as a modern political ideology rather than an ancient inheritance.

**Model answer:** Communalism was modern because it transformed religious affiliation into a claim that co-religionists possessed common secular interests. Colonial authorities strengthened this process by recognising communal spokesmen and later separate electorates. Indian elites also exercised agency: associations, newspapers and leaders translated material or political anxieties into community claims. Yet the process was neither uniform nor inevitable. Muslim and Hindu opinion remained internally divided, regional cultures crossed religious lines, and nationalist organisations repeatedly secured cooperation. Thus inherited symbols supplied political language, but modern institutions and choices converted that language into communal ideology.

### Visual: the institutional feedback loop

```text
enumeration and administrative categories
                 +
limited councils, jobs and educational openings
                 +
official search for recognised intermediaries
                 v
leaders claim to speak for a religious community
                 v
state grants safeguards or separate channels
                 v
future competition is expressed through the same category
```

The colonial state did not merely observe identities. Through censuses, administrative reports, recruitment practices and constitutional representation, it made categories legible and politically consequential. Enumeration did not automatically create hostility, but repeated official comparison encouraged people to ask: What is our number, share and representation?

### 1. Classification and representation

Counting is not the same as communalism. The political effect arose when counted categories became units for allocating:

- legislative seats;
- government employment;
- educational concessions;
- municipal influence;
- titles, patronage and official consultation.

An identity becomes politically hardened when advantages are repeatedly negotiated in its name. Leaders then gain status by presenting themselves as authentic spokesmen, while the state gains intermediaries who can be consulted without democratising power.

### 2. Scarcity under colonial underdevelopment

Bipan Chandra connects communal politics to a restricted colonial economy. Government service, law, education and a narrow professional sector carried unusual importance because industrial and commercial opportunities were limited. Educated applicants therefore competed intensely for a small pool of posts.

```text
restricted economic growth
        -> few salaried and professional openings
        -> intense educated-middle-class competition
        -> demand for communal or provincial shares
        -> elite claims presented as community interests
```

This mechanism must be qualified. Relative educational disadvantage was real in some regions and strata, but it did not make communal politics inevitable. One response was to expand education and oppose colonial underdevelopment; another was to seek protected shares within the existing order.

### 3. Elite politics and popular translation

Early communal claims commonly emerged among landlords, retired officials, professionals and educated aspirants. These groups could convert narrow interests into wider language:

| Immediate concern | Communal translation |
|---|---|
| Government appointments | "Our community is excluded" |
| Council seats | "Numbers alone will produce domination" |
| Landlord-debtor conflict | "One religion exploits another" |
| Language employment | "A script or language belongs exclusively to us" |

The translation could obscure class relations. In Punjab, for example, landlord, debtor, trader and moneylender interests did not align neatly with a single religious bloc. In Bengal, agrarian conflict could be narrated communally even when its material structure was landlord-tenant inequality.

### 4. Colonial responsibility and its limit

Colonial authorities:

- treated religious groups as distinct political entities;
- extended patronage to selected communal leaders;
- accepted some communal claims rapidly;
- recognised organisations as community spokesmen;
- institutionalised separate electorates;
- tolerated communal propaganda more readily than anti-colonial mobilisation.

Yet official encouragement worked because it met real anxieties, inequalities and ambitions. The state created channels and incentives; Indian actors supplied organisations, programmes and rhetoric.

**Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words**

**Question:** Examine how colonial political structures and socio-economic competition interacted in the growth of communalism.

**Model answer:** Colonial rule supplied both scarcity and a political grammar for managing it. Underdevelopment limited industrial and professional expansion, making government posts, education and council seats unusually valuable to the educated middle classes. Uneven access generated real anxieties, but those anxieties became communal when elites claimed that a religious population possessed one interest and deserved a fixed share. Enumeration, administrative comparison and official consultation made religious categories visible. Patronage and recognition rewarded leaders who claimed community authority, while separate representation later gave such claims an electoral payoff. Nevertheless, colonial policy was not mechanically decisive. Landlords, professionals, journalists and associations chose how to articulate interests; many members of the same communities adopted nationalist, regional or class politics. Regional examples also varied: agrarian relations mattered strongly in Punjab and Bengal, while service competition was especially visible in the United Provinces. Communalism therefore grew through an interaction between colonial structures and indigenous agency, not from either alone.

### Visual: when a shared practice becomes a political boundary

```text
LANGUAGE OR RELIGIOUS PRACTICE
        |
legitimate cultural demand
        |
exclusive ownership claim
        |
employment/status competition + public propaganda
        |
"our gain requires their loss"
        |
communal boundary hardens
```

Neither language advocacy nor reverence for the cow was automatically communal. The decisive shift occurred when a cultural preference was defined as the exclusive property of one religious community and directed against another.

### 1. The Hindi-Urdu controversy

In north India, especially the United Provinces and Bihar, disputes over the language and script of courts and administration acquired political significance. Hindi in Devanagari and Urdu in the Perso-Arabic script had overlapping linguistic worlds, but organisations increasingly presented them as the natural possessions of Hindus and Muslims respectively.

The controversy had several layers:

- **cultural:** script, literary tradition and educational identity;
- **material:** access to clerical and administrative employment;
- **institutional:** which language the state recognised in courts and offices;
- **political:** who could claim to defend a community.

The qualification is essential. Support for Hindi was not inherently anti-Muslim, and support for Urdu was not inherently separatist. Communalisation occurred through exclusive identification, zero-sum rhetoric and the conversion of language policy into proof of permanent religious conflict.

### 2. Cow-protection mobilisation

Cow-protection associations and propaganda expanded in parts of north India in the late nineteenth century. A religious concern became communal when campaigns focused hostility upon Muslims rather than consistently opposing all slaughter, including supplies connected with British cantonments.

The campaigns could:

1. create a broad Hindu public across caste and locality;
2. give local notables and preachers a mobilising issue;
3. turn processions, sacrifice and public space into tests of power;
4. intensify rumours and produce riot situations.

Again, avoid a simple chain from belief to violence. Local political rivalry, routes, markets, festivals, policing and rumour affected outcomes. Many Hindus did not join such campaigns, and inter-community cooperation continued.

### 3. Why symbols matter

Symbols compress complex anxieties. A job dispute requires explanation; a threatened language or sacred animal can be narrated immediately as collective danger. This emotional efficiency helps political entrepreneurs widen an elite dispute, but it does not prove that the resulting constituency was socially uniform.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling these controversies communal dismisses genuine cultural concerns.

**Reply:** The concern may be genuine. The analytical question is how it is framed. A plural demand seeks accommodation; a communal demand asserts exclusive ownership, treats another community as the obstacle and claims that all co-religionists share the same political interest.

### Visual: one career, changing political priorities

```text
post-1857 reform problem
        |
modern education + scientific outlook + social reform
        |
Hindu-Muslim cooperation and shared country
        |
fear that agitation may endanger education and official support
        |
loyalism + opposition to Congress + minority safeguards
        |
followers develop a more explicitly communal political language
```

Sir Syed Ahmad Khan cannot be understood through a single label. His educational and intellectual work preceded and exceeded his later political conservatism.

### 1. Reformist Aligarh

Sir Syed sought to reconcile Islamic thought with reason and modern science. He promoted translation, founded educational institutions and established the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh in 1875. The college admitted non-Muslims and received Hindu financial support. His early language stressed a shared country and common social life.

His project responded to a real problem: sections of the Muslim upper and middle classes lagged in English education and modern professions after 1857. Education, not political separatism, was the original centre of his programme.

### 2. The political turn

By the later 1880s, Sir Syed opposed Congress agitation and representative democracy as then proposed. Several considerations shaped this shift:

- fear that confrontation with the Raj would damage the Aligarh educational project;
- reliance on official and landed support;
- belief that British withdrawal or majority voting could expose Muslims to domination;
- preference for gradual social and educational preparation over agitation;
- distrust of Congress's social base and democratic implications.

His language increasingly represented Hindus and Muslims as possessing divergent political interests. He advised Muslims to remain loyal to British rule and aloof from Congress politics.

### 3. How to judge the change

Two errors must be avoided:

1. **Erasure:** treating his early composite nationalism and reformism as insincere.
2. **Exoneration by reform:** assuming educational achievement cancels the consequences of later communal argument.

A historical judgment can recognise both. His educational work challenged intellectual stagnation, while his later political strategy helped formulate themes of minority safeguards, official protection and suspicion of representative majorities.

### 4. Aligarh was not all Muslim politics

Badruddin Tyabji, R.M. Sayani, A. Bhimji and other Muslim leaders participated in Congress. Muslim delegates increased in its early sessions. Bombay's commercial and educational context differed from the landed politics of parts of north India. Later, young Muslim nationalists and Deobandi scholars challenged loyalist Aligarh politics.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Sir Syed's concern about majority rule was merely communal fear.

**Reply:** Minority security was a legitimate constitutional problem. The communal element lay in treating religious populations as permanently separate political interests and British protection as their necessary safeguard. A democratic answer would protect rights while building common citizenship, not freeze political identity by religion.

### Revision notes

Partition did not divide political responses neatly by religion. It intensified competing projects of nationalism, loyalism, regional advantage and communal representation.

### 1. Official policy and political consequence

The partition was presented administratively, but nationalists saw it as an effort to weaken Bengal's political mobilisation. Officials also appealed to the prospect of a Muslim-majority province in Eastern Bengal and Assam. The Nawab of Dacca and some elite supporters backed partition, while many Muslim intellectuals joined the anti-partition movement.

The evidence therefore supports neither "all Muslims supported partition" nor "religion was irrelevant." Official patronage and provincial advantage influenced some elites; anti-colonial nationalism attracted others.

### 2. Swadeshi's inclusive achievement

Swadeshi widened political action through boycott, public meetings, national education and local organisation. Muslim participants included Abdul Rasul, Liaquat Husain and other activists. Their participation shows that a common anti-colonial programme could cut across religious identity.

### 3. Swadeshi's limitations

Parts of militant nationalist mobilisation used Hindu symbols, sacred geography and historical imagery. This did not make the movement as a whole anti-Muslim. Many militant nationalists favoured unity, and their boycott programme was modern and anti-colonial. Yet an idiom that assumed Hindu cultural familiarity could make entry harder for some Muslims and offered loyalist propagandists material for depicting nationalism as Hindu rule in waiting.

The correct causal statement is limited: Hindu colouring was less the original cause of Muslim communalism than a weakness that obstructed nationalist counter-mobilisation.

### 4. From mass politics to organised communal politics

Swadeshi demonstrated that politics could mobilise wider publics. Loyalist elites could no longer rely only on political passivity. They needed organisation, representation and constitutional claims. This is the bridge from the Bengal crisis to the Simla Deputation and League foundation.

### 5. Annulment in 1911 and loyalist reassessment

The British government annulled the partition of Bengal in **1911**. The reversal answered a central anti-partition demand, but it did not restore the political position of 1905.

For League leaders and Muslim notables who had supported Eastern Bengal and Assam, annulment weakened confidence that loyal cooperation would preserve a valued provincial arrangement. It encouraged reassessment of exclusive dependence on official patronage and strengthened the case made by younger Muslim nationalists for a more self-governing, cooperative constitutional strategy. The consequence was not an immediate abandonment of communal safeguards: the League survived, and the separate-electorate framework created in 1909 remained intact.

```text
        |

        |

```

Thus administrative reversal and political reversal were different. Bengal was reunited, but the organisations, representative claims and electoral incentives produced during the controversy continued.

### Visual: the institutional sequence of 1906

### 1. Why the deputation mattered

The Simla Deputation transformed an argument into a constitutional demand. Muslim notables asked that representation should not rest only on numerical strength. They sought separate political recognition and weight reflecting what they described as Muslim political importance.

The deputation was led by the Aga Khan and received by Viceroy Minto in October 1906. Its importance lies less in ceremony than in the precedent: the colonial executive dealt with selected notables as spokesmen for an all-India religious community.

### 2. Evidence and conspiracy caution

Official encouragement is clear in the favourable reception and later constitutional concession. It is unsafe, however, to describe the deputation simply as a British script unless direct evidence proves every step. Muslim elites had their own concerns about representation, status and Congress politics. Colonial strategy and elite agency converged.

### 3. Foundation at Dacca

The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dacca in December 1906. Prominent names included the Aga Khan, Nawab Salimullah of Dacca and Nawab Mohsin-ul-Mulk. Its early leadership came substantially from:

- large landlords and aristocrats;
- retired officials and title-holders;
- upper-class professionals;
- Aligarh-associated loyalist networks.

It was not founded as a mass anti-colonial movement and did not begin with a Pakistan demand.

### 4. Initial objectives

The League's early purposes may be understood as:

1. promoting loyalty to the British government;
2. protecting and advancing what its leaders described as Muslim political rights and interests;
3. preventing hostility toward other communities, but within a framework that assumed separate communal representation.

The tension in the third aim is important. Formal inter-community amity could coexist with an ideology that treated communities as separate bargaining units.

### 5. Historical significance

The League supplied organisational continuity. A deputation ended after presenting its memorial; a party could hold sessions, develop leadership, negotiate reforms and adapt its programme. Organisation did not guarantee mass support, but it created a durable political claimant.

### 1. Early programme

The League sought to protect Muslim political interests as defined by its leadership, cultivate loyalty to British rule and secure access to services and representation. It supported the partition of Bengal and opposed many Congress demands during its initial phase.

Its early claim to speak for Muslims must be treated as a political claim, not a demographic fact. The organisation lacked a broad mass base and was strongest among sections of landlords, aristocrats, retired officials and educated elites.

### 2. Social base: what it explains and what it does not

An elite base helps explain:

- preference for constitutional petition over mass agitation;
- concern with office, seats, status and safeguards;
- willingness to work with the colonial state;
- suspicion of democratic mobilisation and Congress pressure.

It does not prove that every member had identical motives or that elite leadership could never change. Organisations respond to external crises and internal generational conflict.

### 3. Regional diversity

| Region or setting | Important political context |
|---|---|
| United Provinces | Aligarh networks, language politics and competition for education and government employment |

| Bombay | Earlier commercial and professional development and prominent Muslim Congress leaders |

Deobandi scholars, the Ahrar current, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan and younger Muslim intellectuals criticised loyalism.

M.A. Jinnah joined the League in the same year while remaining a Congress constitutional nationalist and seeking cooperation between the organisations. Younger nationalists had gained influence, but the change did not erase the League's commitment to communal safeguards.

### 5. No backward projection

The early League's politics contained communal premises: separate interests, safeguards and representation. But the future remained open. Its later mass politics, Lahore Resolution and Pakistan demand arose under different conditions and cannot be treated as the automatic unfolding of 1906.

## Lesson 8 - Morley-Minto Reforms and Separate Electorates

Progress: 8/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Constitutional design and political incentives

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Bipan Chandra OCR accounts of the Indian Councils Act 1909
CA search: "not run - bounded historical generation pass"
CA found: none used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: how separate electorates changed political incentives

```text
ordinary territorial contest
candidate must address a mixed electorate

SEPARATE ELECTORATE
only Muslim voters -> Muslim candidates -> Muslim-reserved contest
        |
electoral reward for speaking as the community's representative
        |
fewer incentives for cross-community programmes inside that contest
        |
community identity becomes a constitutional route to office
```

### 1. What the 1909 reforms did

The Indian Councils Act 1909, associated with Morley and Minto, enlarged legislative councils but did not establish responsible government. Most authority remained with the colonial executive. Its decisive communal provision introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

Under the system, Muslim voters elected Muslim representatives from designated constituencies. This differed from:

- **reservation in a joint electorate:** a protected seat elected by a common electorate;
- **nomination:** selection by an authority rather than voters;
- **weightage:** representation greater than a group's numerical proportion.

### 2. Why the mechanism mattered

Separate electorates did not create religious identity. They altered the route by which political office was obtained. A candidate's legal electorate was confined by religion, so community-specific appeals could be electorally rational. The state also strengthened organisations able to nominate, endorse or claim representative authority.

### 3. Protection and partitioning: the debate

**Defence:** A numerical minority required credible protection from majority domination. Separate electorates ensured a political voice that a common electorate might dilute.

**Critique:** The device treated religious identity as the primary basis of political interest, reduced incentives for shared programmes and allowed the colonial state to arbitrate between communities while withholding responsible government.

**Balanced assessment:** Minority protection was a real constitutional need; separate electorates were one particular remedy, not the only possible one. Rights, federal arrangements, reserved seats in joint electorates and institutional checks could address insecurity without making religious separation the normal route to representation.

### 4. Causal caution

Separate electorates strengthened communal politics, but they did not make later Partition automatic. Their effect depended on party organisation, franchise limits, provincial politics, nationalism and later constitutional crises.

### 1. From issue campaigns to organisation

Hindu communal politics developed through language agitation, cow-protection campaigns, communal readings of history and demands for a "Hindu share" in employment and representation. The Punjab Hindu Sabha, founded in 1909, criticised Congress for subordinating Hindu interests to national unity.

### 2. Hindu Mahasabha in chronological scope

The first session of the All-India Hindu Mahasabha was held in 1915 under the presidency of the Maharaja of Kasim Bazar. ### 3. Similar logic, different histories

Hindu and Muslim communal politics shared a basic logic: religious communities were treated as political units with separate interests. But they were not identical organisations and did not possess equal state support, social bases or regional trajectories.

Avoid two errors:

- **reaction theory:** justifying one communalism as a mere defence against the other;
- **false equivalence:** assuming similar ideology means identical power, chronology or consequence.

Communalisms could feed one another. Each side cited the other's claims to strengthen its own leadership, while colonial arbitration became more valuable.

### 4. Boundary discipline

Savarkar's *Hindutva* (1923) and the RSS (1925) belong to a later phase. They may be named only as forward chronology. Their doctrines and politics should not be imported into an account ending at the pre-Lucknow threshold.

### 1. Congress's early response

Early Congress leaders argued that economic and political grievances were common to Indians. These measures showed awareness of minority insecurity. Their limitation was that reassurance by elites could not by itself dissolve uneven education, official patronage, symbolic exclusion or the new constitutional rewards attached to communal organisation.

### 2. Muslim participation in nationalism

Muslim participation in Congress and Swadeshi demonstrates that the political field was open:

- Badruddin Tyabji presided over Congress in 1887;
- R.M. Sayani and other Muslim leaders worked within Congress;
- Abdul Rasul and other Bengali Muslims supported Swadeshi;

### 3. Why relations improved before Lucknow

Several developments brought the League closer to Congress:

1. younger Muslim leaders rejected passive loyalism;
2. international crises involving the Ottoman Empire encouraged anti-British feeling;
3. the League adopted colonial self-government as its objective in 1913;
4. Jinnah and Tilak helped build constitutional cooperation;

**Achievements of the pre-1916 nationalist response**

- included important Muslim leaders;
- offered minority safeguards;

By 1916, communal representation had become a powerful constitutional fact, but the future was not closed. Congress-League cooperation, nationalist Muslim politics and regional alternatives remained viable. Sir Syed Ahmad Khan promoted modern science, rational interpretation and social reform, and founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College in 1875. His early language stressed Hindu-Muslim cooperation, while the institution accepted Hindu support and students. Aligarh therefore cannot be reduced to separatism.

Its political direction changed in the later 1880s. Sir Syed feared that confrontation with the Raj would endanger educational advance and believed representative politics could permit majority domination. Dependence on official and landed support reinforced his opposition to Congress. He increasingly presented Hindu and Muslim political interests as divergent, defended loyalism and favoured safeguards in employment and representation. Followers developed these themes more explicitly, and Aligarh networks contributed personnel and ideas to early League politics.

Yet Aligarh is an incomplete explanation. Muslim politics differed by class and region. Tyabji, Sayani and Jinnah worked in Congress; Bengali Muslims joined Swadeshi; Deobandi and younger nationalist currents opposed loyalism. Colonial patronage, separate electorates, economic scarcity, language politics and Bengal partition also mattered. Aligarh should thus be judged as a modernising educational movement whose later defensive politics supplied an influential, but contested, vocabulary of communal representation.

**Scoring note:** Reward chronological change, educational achievement, political consequence, Muslim counter-currents and rejection of a monocausal verdict. **Rubric (15 marks; 250-word ceiling):** direct thesis - 2; reform and 1875 evidence - 3; later political shift - 3; consequence for communal politics - 3; counter-currents and causal limits - 2; balanced conclusion - 2. **Total: 15.**

## 20 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Trace the growth of communalism in India from the late nineteenth century to the eve of the Lucknow Pact. Was it primarily a colonial construction?

**Model answer:** Communalism emerged when religious identity was converted into a claim about shared secular interests. Its growth was modern, linked to public politics, representation and organisation rather than to timeless religious hostility.

Colonial responsibility was substantial. Census and administrative classification made communities legible; underdevelopment restricted jobs and professional opportunity; officials cultivated selected notables and treated them as communal spokesmen. Language policy in the United Provinces, patronage during Bengal partition, the favourable reception of the Simla Deputation and separate electorates in 1909 gave community claims institutional rewards.

However, construction was interactive rather than unilateral. Sir Syed's later loyalism translated educational anxiety and fear of majority rule into opposition to Congress. Hindi-Urdu and cow-protection activists converted cultural issues into zero-sum politics. Landlords, professionals and associations used communal claims in contests over status, employment and seats. The League, founded at Dacca in 1906, organised these claims through elite constitutional politics. Punjab Hindu Sabha and the 1915 Hindu Mahasabha session marked parallel Hindu organisation.

Counter-evidence prevents determinism. Muslim leaders participated in Congress and Swadeshi; regional and class interests divided every community; the League revised its creed toward colonial self-government in 1913 and moved toward Congress cooperation. Colonial institutions created a highly favourable field, but Indian agency shaped its use. By 1916 communal representation was entrenched, not destiny: cooperation remained possible, though increasingly negotiated through communal categories.

**Scoring note:** Reward conceptual definition, chronology, both causal sides, regional diversity, counter-evidence and a non-teleological conclusion. **Rubric (20 marks; 250-word ceiling):** concept and thesis - 3; late-nineteenth-century causes - 3; 1905-1909 institutional chronology - 4; colonial construction argument - 3; indigenous agency and cultural politics - 3; counter-evidence and regional diversity - 2; pre-1916 verdict - 2. **Total: 20.**

## Error pattern 1: religion equals communalism

Which case is least sufficient by itself to prove communalism?

**A.** Members of a faith publicly observe a religious festival without claiming a distinct share of political power or economic resources.
**B.** An educational association seeks additional grants by presenting unequal access as a grievance of one religious community.
**C.** A political body argues that representatives from another religion cannot defend its community's secular interests.
**D.** A campaign links recurring local disputes to an enduring conflict between two religious political communities.

**MCQ 37: A**

- **A — Correct:** Religious observance alone does not assert a uniform secular interest or a separate political constituency.
- **B — Incorrect:** The claim translates an educational issue into a religious community's distributive entitlement.
- **C — Incorrect:** Exclusive representation directly applies the communal premise that secular interests follow religious identity.
- **D — Incorrect:** Turning contingent disputes into permanent inter-community conflict approaches the hostile stage of communal ideology.

## Error pattern 2: treating all safeguards as identical

Which arrangement specifically defines a separate electorate?

**A.** The executive selects a minority member after consulting recognised associations and elected provincial representatives.
**B.** Voters and legally eligible candidates in a designated legislative contest belong to the specified communal category.
**C.** A community receives legislative weightage above its population share while candidates use the ordinary electorate.
**D.** Members of a minority vote on a panel whose final representative is appointed by the colonial executive.

**MCQ 38: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Consultative executive selection remains nomination, even when community organisations participate in recommending names.
- **B — Correct:** A separate electorate uses a communally delimited voter roll to elect a representative from that category.
- **C — Incorrect:** Weightage changes a community's seat share; it does not create the communally restricted voter roll that defines a separate electorate.
- **D — Incorrect:** A mixed election-and-appointment arrangement is not the direct communal election created by a separate electorate.

## Error pattern 3: assuming a homogeneous Muslim political response

Which evidence most effectively corrects that assumption?

**A.** League influence varied between provinces because landed, commercial and professional interests differed sharply.
**B.** Congress safeguards and League petitions offered competing constitutional routes for addressing minority insecurity.
**C.** Muslim leaders and organisations appeared in Congress, Swadeshi, loyalist and nationalist currents.
**D.** The League changed its creed in 1913 as younger leaders sought closer cooperation with Congress.

**MCQ 39: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Regional variation weakens homogeneity, but it does not display the full range of organised political alternatives.
- **B — Incorrect:** Different constitutional routes show contestation, yet they do not capture the wider ideological plurality listed in the key.
- **C — Correct:** Participation across rival movements directly disproves the assumption of one uniform Muslim political response.
- **D — Incorrect:** Organisational change demonstrates flexibility within the League, not the complete plurality of Muslim politics outside it.

## Error pattern 4: reading 1940 backward into 1906

Which statement avoids teleology?

**A.** Early safeguard politics supplied a continuous constitutional path that later culminated in separatist negotiation.
**B.** League loyalism gave way to self-government in 1913, beginning a steady expansion toward territorial politics.
**C.** Separate electorates increasingly marginalised regional parties and nationalist Muslims until their alternatives became politically residual.
**D.** Early communal premises mattered, but later choices and crises produced Pakistan politics.

**MCQ 40: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** A continuous culminating path erases reversals, rival programmes and the later crises needed to produce separatism.
- **B — Incorrect:** The 1913 change moved toward Congress cooperation rather than starting a steady territorial progression.
- **C — Incorrect:** Regional and nationalist alternatives retained substantial agency; their later fortunes were not settled by 1909.
- **D — Correct:** It recognises path dependence while preserving the causal work of later wars, elections, negotiations and organisational change.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core definition

- Communalism is a modern political ideology that treats followers of a religion as a single community with common secular interests.
- Stage 1: common interests; Stage 2: divergent interests; Stage 3: hostile and irreconcilable interests. In Chandra's terms, stages 1-2 are liberal/mild communalism and stage 3 is extreme/fascistic communalism; these are attributed analytical labels, not legal categories.
- Religion, religiosity, communal ideology and communal violence are distinct.
- Do not assign collective guilt or homogeneous agency to Hindus, Muslims or Sikhs.

## B. Why modern, not primordial

- Modern state enumeration and classification made large communities administratively visible.
- Representative institutions created a problem of allocating seats and safeguards.
- Print, associations and public meetings enabled claims of community leadership.
- Colonial underdevelopment restricted education, professions and government jobs.
- Modern mass politics required leaders to mobilise publics.
- Earlier religious conflict cannot be read as proof of continuous communal ideology.
- Communal history-writing portrayed the medieval centuries uniformly as Muslim rule, simplified Muslims into rulers and Hindus into the ruled, and suppressed composite culture to legitimise present antagonism.

## C. Causal framework

1. Colonial state power and divide-and-rule.
2. Census and administrative categorisation.
3. Uneven educational and economic development.
4. Middle-class competition for jobs, seats and status.
5. Landed and bureaucratic elite interests.
6. Language, history-writing and religious symbols.
7. Nationalist failures of outreach.
8. Political organisation and constitutional incentives.

## D. Hindi-Urdu and cow protection

- Hindi-Urdu controversy linked script and official recognition to culture and employment.
- It became communal when languages were treated as exclusive religious possessions.
- Cow-protection belief became communal politics where campaigns targeted another religious group.
- Local mobilisation depended on organisations, processions, rumours, policing and political rivalry.
- Neither Hindi advocacy nor cow reverence was communal by definition.

## E. Sir Syed and Aligarh

- Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College founded at Aligarh in 1875.
- Sir Syed promoted modern education, science, rational interpretation and social reform.
- His early language stressed a shared country and Hindu-Muslim cooperation.
- Later he opposed Congress, favoured loyalism and feared majority domination.
- Educational priorities, official patronage and landed support shaped the shift.
- His reformism must not erase his later political consequence; his later politics must not erase his reformism.
- Muslim Congress participation proves that Aligarh was not the only political path.

## F. Bengal partition and Swadeshi

- Partition in 1905 was treated by nationalists as an attack on Bengal's political strength.
- Officials encouraged support among some Muslim notables by stressing an eastern Muslim-majority province.
- Nawab Salimullah of Dacca supported partition.
- Abdul Rasul, Liaquat Husain and other Muslims joined Swadeshi.
- Hindu symbols in parts of militant rhetoric created a limitation, not a total definition of the movement.
- The crisis accelerated demand for permanent Muslim elite organisation.
- Annulment in 1911 disappointed League-aligned supporters of Eastern Bengal, weakened loyalist confidence and encouraged strategic reassessment, while the League and 1909 separate electorates survived.

## G. Simla Deputation and League

- Simla Deputation: October 1906, led by Aga Khan, received by Minto.
- It sought separate Muslim representation and political weight.
- Favourable official recognition strengthened communal spokesman politics.
- All-India Muslim League founded at Dacca in December 1906.
- Key early names: Aga Khan, Nawab Salimullah and Nawab Mohsin-ul-Mulk.
- Early base: landlords, aristocrats, retired officials and upper-class professionals.
- Early aims: loyalty, safeguards and protection of Muslim interests as defined by its leadership.
- No Pakistan demand at foundation.

## H. Early League development

- Supported Bengal partition and separate electorates.
- Initially opposed Congress and mass agitation.
- Never represented all Muslim opinion.
- Younger nationalists, Deobandi-linked figures and Congress Muslims contested loyalism.
- League adopted colonial self-government as its objective in 1913.
- Jinnah joined the League in 1913 while remaining a Congress constitutional nationalist and seeking inter-organisational cooperation.
- Topic 17 stops before the detailed Lucknow Pact settlement.

## I. Morley-Minto 1909

- Indian Councils Act 1909 enlarged councils but retained executive control.
- Introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
- Separate electorate: communally restricted voters elect a candidate from the same designated category.
- Different from reservation in a joint electorate, nomination and weightage.
- Gave an electoral payoff to community-specific appeals.
- Addressed a genuine security concern through a divisive institutional mechanism.
- Strengthened communal politics but did not make Partition inevitable.

## J. Early Hindu communal organisation

- Hindu communal politics grew through historical narratives, language, cow protection and demands for shares.
- Punjab Hindu Sabha founded in 1909.
- Lal Chand and U.N. Mukherji were associated early ideologues.
- First All-India Hindu Mahasabha session held in 1915 under the Maharaja of Kasim Bazar.
- It remained weaker than the League in the early period.
- Similar communal logic did not mean identical social base or state support.
- Later *Hindutva* and RSS developments fall beyond Topic 17.

## K. Regional diversity

- United Provinces: education, Aligarh, language and government service.
- Bengal: partition, agrarian structures and divided Muslim responses.
- Punjab: landed and debtor-creditor relations with cross-cutting religious patterns.
- Bombay: commercial-professional development and strong Muslim Congress leadership.
- Regional, linguistic and class interests often exceeded religious commonality.

## L. Final analytical verdicts

- **Concept:** communalism made faith the language of supposedly uniform secular interests.
- **Colonial role:** the state created categories, rewards and representative channels.
- **Agency:** Indian elites and organisations actively adopted, modified or resisted those channels.
- **Institution:** separate electorates converted ideology into a recurring constitutional incentive.
- **Nationalist record:** common politics achieved real successes but suffered symbolic and organisational limits.
- **Legacy:** by 1916 communal representation was entrenched, yet cooperation and alternative futures remained open.
