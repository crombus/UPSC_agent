# Simon Commission, Nehru Report, Civil Disobedience and Round Table Conferences — Learning Session Live Edition

The central problem of 1927–34 was not simply whether Indians wanted freedom. It was how an exclusionary British review, an Indian attempt to draft a constitution, unresolved questions of representation, a declaration of complete independence, mass law-breaking and repeated negotiations interacted. The sequence below treats constitutional bargaining and street-level struggle as one movement cycle without pretending that either automatically produced the other.

### Visual: why an early review still looked illegitimate

```text
Government of India Act, 1919
          |
promised statutory review after ten years
          |
British Conservative government anticipates possible electoral defeat
          |
commission appointed in November 1927, before the expected 1929 point
          |
SEVEN BRITISH MEMBERS; NO INDIAN
          |
question changes from "When will reform be reviewed?"
to "Who has authority to design India's political future?"
```

The issue was not that Indians opposed constitutional review. Nationalists had long argued that the 1919 reforms were inadequate and wanted an earlier reconsideration. The insult lay in Britain claiming sole competence to judge Indian readiness while excluding Indians from the judging body.

### 1. The statutory background

The 1919 Act envisaged a commission after ten years to examine the working of the constitutional system. By 1927 Indian criticism of dyarchy, restricted responsibility and executive safeguards was already mature. The British Conservative government appointed the Indian Statutory Commission early, fearing that a future Labour government might handle an imperial question differently.

Sir John Simon chaired the seven-member body. Its all-British composition converted a technical review into a question of political equality.

### 2. Why exclusion mattered

Three claims collided:

1. Britain claimed trusteeship and the right to determine the pace of advance.
2. Indian political organisations claimed competence to frame their own constitutional future.
3. The all-white membership visibly ranked British judgment above Indian consent.

The commission therefore produced a **legitimacy crisis**. A review can be procedurally authorised yet politically illegitimate when those governed are denied meaningful authorship.

### 3. Broad initial response

The boycott crossed older political boundaries. Congress, the Liberal Federation led by Tej Bahadur Sapru, commercial bodies and the Hindu Mahasabha supported opposition. The Muslim League divided, but Jinnah carried its larger section toward boycott. This was substantial unity, not unanimity.

### 4. The report of 1930: recommendation without representative legitimacy

The Commission published its report in 1930. Its principal recommendations were not a simple defence of the 1919 arrangement:

- provincial dyarchy should end, so one unitary provincial cabinet would answer to the legislature across the provincial field, including law and order;
- provincial self-government should widen, but governors would retain overriding and emergency safeguards for order and minority protection;
- provincial legislatures and the franchise should expand rather than remain frozen at 1919 levels;
- British India should move in a federal direction, with an eventual wider federation involving the Indian States;
- responsible government at the centre was not conceded on the same basis as in the provinces, so central defence, security and imperial supervision remained protected;
- communal representation and minority safeguards remained embedded rather than being replaced by the Nehru Report's common-electorate approach;
- Burma should be separated from the projected Indian polity.

The consequence was paradoxical. Provincial autonomy and federation entered the official reform vocabulary, but the report arrived after an all-white inquiry had already lost nationalist trust and after Indian leaders had produced their own constitutional draft. The report therefore influenced the RTC and later White Paper process without recovering political legitimacy for the Commission itself.

### Comparison: 1919 reform versus 1927 review

| Dimension | 1919 arrangement | 1927 commission |
|---|---|---|
| British claim | gradual constitutional advance | expert review of readiness |
| Indian criticism | dyarchy and safeguards denied responsibility | Indians excluded from judging their own future |
| Political arena | councils and constitutional debate | boycott plus mass protest |
| Deeper issue | inadequate power | denial of constituent agency |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A British parliamentary statute created the commission; therefore British membership was legally normal.

**Reply:** Legal origin did not settle political legitimacy. The body claimed to recommend India's future after decades of Indian constitutional debate. Excluding every Indian made its evidentiary and representative claim inherently weak. Still, boycott did not itself supply an agreed Indian alternative; that challenge generated the All Parties Conference.

### UPSC application and traps

- Distinguish **appointment in 1927** from **arrival in 1928**.
- Do not say Indians opposed any review; they opposed a review conducted without Indians.
- Do not call the boycott perfectly unanimous; important Muslim League division remained.
- Use the chain: statutory promise → early appointment → all-white composition → legitimacy crisis → boycott → Indian constitution-making response.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the Simon Commission become a crisis of constitutional legitimacy rather than merely a dispute about timing?

**Model answer (144 words):** The Government of India Act of 1919 supplied a ten-year review framework, while Indian nationalists themselves wanted earlier reconsideration because dyarchy and executive safeguards had proved inadequate. Timing alone therefore cannot explain the opposition.

The decisive issue was composition. The Conservative government appointed the commission in 1927 with seven British members and no Indian. A body claiming to judge India's readiness for constitutional advance thus denied Indians any role in judging their own political future. Congress, Liberals, commercial organisations and the Hindu Mahasabha supported boycott; the Muslim League divided, with Jinnah's larger section also opposing it.

The commission was legally authorised but representatively deficient. It converted reform from a question of concessions into one of constituent agency. Its limit as a unifying issue was that boycott did not settle what constitution Indians themselves wanted, which produced the All Parties Conference and the Nehru Report.

**Scoring note:** Highest marks require the distinction between legal authority and political legitimacy, plus the link to an Indian constitutional counter-project.

**Component rubric (10 marks):** statutory setting 2 + exclusion and legitimacy mechanism 4 + breadth/qualification of boycott 2 + counter-project link and verdict 2 = 10/10.

The safe causal formulation is: Rai was injured in the Lahore lathi-charge and died weeks later; contemporaries and revolutionaries treated the assault as responsible. Avoid reducing a medically complex death to a single simplistic sentence, and avoid claiming that retaliation was an inevitable consequence.

### 1. A nationwide protest repertoire

When the commission arrived on 3 February 1928, major towns observed hartals. Black flags, processions, meetings, kites, balloons and the slogan “Simon Go Back” turned exclusion into a public spectacle. Youth supplied much of the energy. Police firing in Madras and lathi-charges in Lucknow and Lahore showed that even constitutional protest could meet coercion.

### 2. Lahore sequence

Lala Lajpat Rai led the Lahore procession on 30 October. Police struck demonstrators; Rai received blows to the chest. He died on 17 November. His age, public stature and Punjab symbolism magnified the event.

The analytical consequence was twofold:

- the colonial state appeared unwilling to tolerate even black-flag protest;
- revolutionary youth interpreted the episode as national humiliation requiring retaliation.

The Saunders action belongs to the revolutionary sequence already taught. Here it is only the consequence bridge.

### Comparison: symbolic and coercive repertoires

| Nationalist action | Colonial response | Political effect |
|---|---|---|
| hartal | policing and disruption | collective withdrawal from normality |
| black flags and slogans | lathi-charge | exclusion made visible |
| mobile, creative protest | surveillance and route control | youth politicisation |
| senior leaders in procession | physical assault | broader moral outrage |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The protest was negative: it rejected the commission without offering a constitution.

**Reply:** That criticism explains the next step rather than invalidating the boycott. Public refusal established that externally designed reform lacked consent; the All Parties process attempted to supply the missing positive alternative. The limitation remained that unity against exclusion was easier than agreement on federal power and minority representation.

### UPSC application and traps

- Arrival: 3 February 1928.
- Lahore lathi-charge: 30 October; Rai's death: 17 November.
- Do not merge Reginald Dyer, Michael O'Dwyer and the Lahore police episode.
- Do not develop HSRA history here; use one sentence to connect protest to retaliatory action.
- “All-white” refers to membership, not a claim that every British person supported it.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain how the anti-Simon agitation combined constitutional protest, mass symbolism and political radicalisation.

**Model answer (148 words):** The Simon Commission was a constitutional body, but its all-British membership turned protest into a mass issue. On its arrival on 3 February 1928, hartals, black flags, processions and “Simon Go Back” demonstrations converted the abstract denial of representation into visible public action. Youth organisations gained experience and leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru emerged more strongly.

Colonial policing intensified radicalisation. At Lahore on 30 October, Lala Lajpat Rai led a procession and received serious blows in a lathi-charge. He died on 17 November. It is safest to state that the assault was widely treated as causally significant, not to reduce the death to a simplistic medical formula. HSRA revolutionaries later sought retaliation, though that response was chosen rather than inevitable.

Thus the agitation linked constitutional exclusion, popular mobilisation and coercive backlash. Its unresolved problem was positive agreement: unity against Simon did not guarantee unity on an Indian constitution.

### Visual: an Indian constitutional counter-project

```text
Birkenhead's challenge:
"Indians cannot agree on a workable constitution"
              |

              |

              |

              |

```

The Report can look federal and centralising at the same time. Federalism describes the distribution of constitutionally recognised levels; centralisation describes where decisive unlisted powers are placed. These are different questions.

### 1. From challenge to drafting

Secretary of State Lord Birkenhead repeatedly argued that Indians could not produce a constitutional scheme supported by wide opinion. All Parties Conference meetings in February, May and August 1928 took up that challenge. A committee chaired by Motilal Nehru, with members including Tej Bahadur Sapru, Ali Imam and Subhas Bose, submitted the Report in August.

The draft was therefore both an answer to imperial exclusion and an attempt to prove Indian capacity for constitution-making. It must not be reduced to a protest memorandum.

### 2. Governing framework

The Report treated **Dominion Status** as the next immediate constitutional objective. It proposed parliamentary government in which the executive would be responsible to the legislature.

Its territorial and federal design included:

- a federation built on provinces;
- provincial autonomy and the end of dyarchy;
- reorganisation of provinces on linguistic lines;
- separation of Sind from Bombay when financially workable;
- constitutional reform in the North-West Frontier Province;
- residuary powers assigned to the centre;
- a Supreme Court and a common constitutional framework.

The apparent tension is real but intelligible: federalism preserved constitutionally recognised provincial fields, while central residuary power protected national cohesion.

### 3. Rights, citizenship and franchise

The Report recommended adult suffrage, equal rights for women, freedom of conscience and association, protection of religion, culture and language, and dissociation of the state from religion. These provisions made the draft an early attempt to connect responsible government with enforceable civic equality rather than merely redistribute offices among elites.

### 4. Representation formula

The Report rejected separate communal electorates. It preferred **joint electorates**, but this did not mean unqualified majoritarianism:

- minority seats could be reserved for a limited period;
- Muslims would receive reservation at the centre and in provinces where they were minorities;
- no Muslim reservation was proposed in Punjab or Bengal, where Muslims already formed majorities;
- cultural and religious safeguards accompanied the electoral design.

### Comparison: federal design and central allocation

| Question | Federal feature | Centralising feature |
|---|---|---|
| territorial units | provinces retained autonomous fields | the common constitution bound the units |
| legislative distribution | provincial subjects were recognised | unlisted powers went to the centre |
| representation | provincial and central legislatures remained distinct | one citizenship-oriented electoral design was preferred |
| practical implication | local government gained constitutional space | the federation was not designed as a weak confederacy |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Dominion Status made the Report timid and obsolete before adoption.

**Reply:** The objection captures youth dissatisfaction but understates the Report's constitutional innovation: responsible government, adult suffrage, equal citizenship, linguistic provinces and a rights catalogue were substantive. Its deeper failure was political consent. A sophisticated draft could not settle minority insecurity merely by institutional elegance.

### UPSC application and traps

- Motilal Nehru chaired the committee; Jawaharlal did not.
- Dominion Status was not Purna Swaraj.
- Joint electorates coexisted with temporary reservation and cultural safeguards.
- Federal form did not place residuary powers in provinces; that was one of Jinnah's Calcutta amendments.
- Sind and NWFP concessions answered two Delhi Proposals but did not settle the entire representation dispute.

Its federal character lay in recognised provincial governments, linguistic reorganisation and a centre-province distribution of authority. Yet it was not a loose federation. Residuary powers—subjects not expressly allocated—were assigned to the centre. The Report can therefore be federal in structure and centralising in residual competence without contradiction.

Its broader significance was considerable. It recommended adult suffrage, equal rights for women, freedom of association, state neutrality in religion and protection of minority culture and language. It rejected separate electorates but retained reservations for minorities in joint electorates.

Its limits were political and strategic. Dominion status disappointed younger nationalists seeking complete independence. Muslim, Sikh and Hindu communal objections showed that institutional safeguards did not dissolve mistrust. The Report was therefore a major exercise in Indian constitutional authorship, but not a consensual settlement.

These were rival constitutional risk models. Treating the Fourteen Points as proof that Partition was already inevitable converts later history into destiny and prevents analysis of the still-negotiable disagreements of 1928–29.

### 1. The Delhi Proposals of December 1927

Before the Nehru Report, Muslim leaders meeting at Delhi framed a four-part compromise:

1. Sind should be separated from the Bombay Presidency.
2. The North-West Frontier Province should receive constitutional treatment on the same footing as other provinces.
3. Muslims should receive one-third representation in the central legislature.
4. Muslim representation in Punjab and Bengal should follow population so that existing Muslim majorities were preserved, while reservations would continue where Muslims were minorities.

The significance is often missed: these proposals offered movement toward joint electorates in return for specified territorial and representational safeguards.

### 2. Nehru Report response and the December 1928 amendments

The Nehru Report accepted the first two territorial demands—Sind and NWFP reform—but not the full central and majority-province formula. At the Calcutta All-Parties Convention in December 1928, Jinnah proposed three amendments:

1. one-third Muslim representation in the central legislature;
2. population-based Muslim representation in Punjab and Bengal without reducing those majorities;
3. residuary powers vested in the provinces rather than the centre.

The amendments were rejected. Congress leaders resisted the weak-centre implication; Hindu Mahasabha and Sikh objections narrowed room for concessions; and the Shafi-Aga Khan League current would not surrender separate electorates. Jinnah's attempted amendment therefore came before his consolidated counter-programme.

### 3. Fourteen Points, March 1929

The Fourteen Points gathered the Delhi demands, the rejected Calcutta amendments and additional safeguards into a single programme:

**Federal and provincial power**

1. The constitution should be federal, with residuary powers vested in provinces.
2. Every province should receive a uniform measure of autonomy.
3. Territorial redistribution must not disturb Muslim majorities in Punjab, Bengal and the NWFP.
4. No constitutional amendment by the central legislature should occur without concurrence of the federating units.

**Representation and minority adequacy**

5. Minorities should receive adequate and effective representation without reducing a majority to a minority or even equality.
6. Muslims should hold at least one-third of the central legislature.
7. Representation should continue through separate electorates, but any community could voluntarily choose joint electorates.
8. No bill or resolution should pass if three-fourths of the members of a community in a legislature considered it injurious to that community.

**Territorial and administrative safeguards**

9. Sind should be separated from Bombay.
10. Reforms should be introduced in the NWFP and Baluchistan on the same footing as in other provinces.
11. Muslims should receive an adequate share in state services and local self-governing bodies.
12. Central and provincial cabinets should contain at least one-third Muslim ministers.

**Religious and cultural safeguards**

13. Full religious liberty should protect belief, worship, observance, propaganda, association and education.
14. The constitution should safeguard Muslim culture, education, language, religion, personal law, charitable institutions and an appropriate share of grants-in-aid.

### Comparison: rival constitutional risk models

| Issue | Nehru Report | Jinnah's counter-position |
|---|---|---|
| federation | provinces within a common responsible system | federal constitution with stronger provincial security |
| residuary power | centre | provinces |
| electorate | joint, with bounded reservations | separate, with voluntary community conversion to joint |
| central Muslim share | reservation, amount disputed | at least one-third |
| Punjab/Bengal/NWFP | no reservation that altered existing majorities | majority position protected against redistribution |
| administration | common citizenship framework | adequate services and one-third ministry safeguards |
| communal legislation | ordinary legislative process plus rights | three-fourths community veto against injurious measures |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Treating the Points as a constitutional counter-position sanitises communal politics.

**Reply:** Separate electorates and communal vetoes could strengthen identity-based leadership and impede common citizenship. Historical explanation must nevertheless identify the problem they claimed to solve: permanent minority insecurity under a strong centre. Criticising the remedy and understanding the represented fear are compatible.

### 1. Irwin Declaration and its ambiguity

On 31 October 1929 Irwin linked the 1917 declaration to Dominion Status as the natural issue of constitutional progress and promised a Round Table Conference after the Simon Report. The wording sounded conciliatory but supplied neither a timetable nor a binding commitment about the conference's purpose.

### 2. Delhi Manifesto, 2 November 1929

Two days later major Indian leaders issued the Delhi Manifesto. They asked that:

- the RTC meet to frame a Dominion constitution for implementation, not to debate whether or when Dominion Status might arrive;
- Congress receive effective representation appropriate to its political weight;
- political prisoners receive amnesty and the administration adopt a conciliatory spirit;

The date matters: **2 November 1929**, following the 31 October declaration. It is distinct from Gandhi's later eleven-demand publication of 31 January 1930 and his 2 March letter.

Irwin could not give the requested assurance at the 23 December interview. The failure showed that a declared direction was not an enforceable constitutional destination.

### 3. Lahore Congress and Purna Swaraj

Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency symbolised youth pressure and generational transfer, although Gandhi remained strategically decisive. Congress adopted Purna Swaraj, authorised preparation for civil disobedience and called on legislators to resign. At midnight on 31 December the tricolour was unfurled on the Ravi.

Purna Swaraj ended Dominion Status as Congress's operative goal. It meant severing the compulsory British connection, not merely gaining more offices within it.

### 4. The 26 January pledge

Public meetings across India affirmed the Independence Pledge on 26 January 1930. Its argument connected political subjection with economic exploitation, cultural damage, compulsory disarmament and civil-liberty denial. It committed participants to non-violent withdrawal of voluntary cooperation and prepared them for tax refusal.

The pledge transformed a Congress resolution into repeated public commitment.

### 1. The 31 January publication: a negotiated minimum

On **31 January 1930**, Gandhi published an eleven-point minimum programme before selecting the opening act of civil disobedience. The demands covered:

1. total prohibition of intoxicants;
2. reduction of the rupee-sterling ratio from **1s. 6d. to 1s. 4d.**;
3. **50 per cent reduction of land revenue**, with legislative control over assessment;
4. abolition of the salt tax and government salt monopoly;
5. **50 per cent reduction of military expenditure**;
6. substantial reduction of the highest civil-service salaries;
7. protective tariff treatment for foreign cloth;
8. passage of the coastal-shipping reservation measure for Indian interests;
9. release of political prisoners and withdrawal of political prosecutions or restrictive orders covered by the demand;
10. abolition or elected control of the Criminal Investigation Department;
11. easier arms licences for citizens for self-defence under popular control.

The list was not a replacement for independence. It tested whether the Raj would concede a cross-class minimum touching peasants, industry, fiscal policy, civil liberties and moral reform.

### 2. The 2 March letter: explanation and notice

On **2 March 1930**, Gandhi addressed Irwin directly. The letter:

- explained why he regarded British rule as economically, politically, culturally and spiritually destructive;
- recalled the failed Delhi interview and the absence of a Dominion assurance;
- stated the logic of non-violent civil disobedience;
- notified Irwin that Gandhi would begin breaking the salt laws on the eleventh day unless the substance of the appeal was met.

The distinction is exact: **31 January = publication of the eleven-demand minimum; 2 March = personal letter, indictment and notice of the intended salt-law breach.**

### 3. Why salt?

Salt was universally consumed, burdened the poor disproportionately, made monopoly visible and allowed a simple act of decentralised violation. Women and local groups could manufacture, collect, sell or possess it without needing access to legislatures.

Its direct fiscal effect was limited. Its strategic value lay in making the state defend an unpopular law over a necessity of life.

### 4. March as communication

Gandhi left Sabarmati on 12 March with seventy-eight selected followers and broke the law at Dandi on 6 April. The route through Gujarat allowed nightly speeches, volunteer preparation, resignations by village officials and continuous press coverage. The march was a moving school of civil resistance rather than transport to a beach.

### 5. Organisational logic

Congress workers enrolled volunteers, formed committees, collected funds, selected salt sites and prepared replacement leadership. Public notice accepted the legal consequence of disobedience and denied the government any plausible claim that the campaign was a secret conspiracy.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Salt was theatrically powerful but economically minor.

**Reply:** Its purpose was catalytic rather than fiscal destruction. Once one unjust law could be broken openly, cloth and liquor boycott, tax refusal, forest-law defiance and resignations could follow. Symbolism worked because organisation translated it into local programmes.

After Gandhi's arrest on 4 May, Sarojini Naidu and other leaders guided volunteers toward Dharasana Salt Works. On 21 May, non-resisting columns faced severe lathi blows. International reporting made disciplined suffering visible. Dharasana demonstrated that non-violence was organised confrontation, not passivity.

### 3. Peshawar and the Frontier

Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's years of local work supported the Khudai Khidmatgars, or Red Shirts. Arrests on 23 April produced an unprecedented Peshawar demonstration; for more than a week the city largely escaped ordinary state control. Garhwali soldiers refused to fire on an unarmed crowd and faced court martial. Qualification matters: this was a specific refusal by units in a specific confrontation, not a general army mutiny.

### 4. Sholapur

After Gandhi's arrest, textile workers and other residents struck, attacked symbols of authority and briefly created a near-collapse of ordinary administration. Martial law restored control. Sholapur illustrates a limit: mass anger could exceed the movement's prescribed non-violent form.

### 5. Repression

The state used arrests, ordinances, censorship, bans, lathi-charges, firing, confiscation and attachment. Repression varied by place and phase. A careful account names coercion without converting suffering into spectacle.

### Comparison: three iconic theatres

| Theatre | Main actor/form | What it proves | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dharasana | disciplined salt volunteers | moral-publicity power | severe suffering did not itself transfer power |
| Peshawar | Khudai Khidmatgars and mass crowd | deep Frontier organisation; military disaffection | specific, not general army revolt |
| Sholapur | workers and urban residents | state authority could collapse locally | violence and martial-law repression |

### Boundary: Chittagong is an overlap, not a transferred owner

Surya Sen's Indian Republican Army carried out the Chittagong Armoury Raid on 18 April 1930, within the same movement year. Topic 22 may mention that simultaneous anti-colonial escalation, but **Topic 21 owns Surya Sen, the raid, Jalalabad, the Bengal revolutionary network and its later women participants**. Chittagong was not a Congress salt sub-campaign and is not retaught here.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Iconic scenes exaggerate isolated moments and conceal the ordinary organisational labour of the movement.

**Reply:** Correct if they replace the wider field. Their proper use is diagnostic: Dharasana reveals disciplined suffering, Peshawar prior organisation and Sholapur the control problem. They become misleading only when treated as the whole movement.

### UPSC application and traps

- Gandhi was arrested before he could lead the Dharasana raid.
- Sarojini Naidu was prominent at Dharasana.
- Khudai Khidmatgars were a locally built Frontier organisation.
- Garhwali refusal was specific and qualified.
- Sholapur was not a stable sovereign parallel state.
- Repression should be described accurately, not sensationally.

## Lesson 10 - First RTC and Gandhi-Irwin Pact: movement-negotiation cycle

Progress: 10/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Congress absence, reciprocal concessions and contested truce

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — First RTC agenda and exact Pact inclusions/exclusions were checked
CA search: "not used — bounded historical session limited to source-permitted evidence"
CA found: no source-permitted current anchor
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual

```text
mass pressure -> First RTC without Congress -> limited legitimacy
      -> negotiation with Gandhi -> Pact, March 1931
Government: releases non-violent prisoners; relief on unsold confiscated property,
uncollected fines, coastal salt and peaceful picketing
Congress: suspends CDM; attends Second RTC
      -> Karachi ratification amid criticism
```

The Pact was a truce between unequal adversaries, not independence and not unconditional surrender.

### 1. First Round Table Conference: representation without Congress

The First RTC met in London from **12 November 1930 to 19 January 1931**. It included representatives of the three main British parties, the Indian princely states and a wide range of British Indian constituencies: Liberals, Muslim League and Hindu Mahasabha figures, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans, Depressed Classes, labour, landholders, commerce and women. Congress was absent while its leadership was imprisoned and Civil Disobedience continued.

Nine principal committees or sub-committees examined:

1. federal structure;
2. provincial constitutions;
3. minorities;
4. franchise;
5. defence;
6. services;
7. Sind;
8. the North-West Frontier Province;
9. Burma.

The important result was broad acceptance in principle of an all-India federation joining British Indian provinces and princely states, together with provincial responsibility and minority safeguards. The unresolved questions were decisive: the terms of princely accession, responsibility at the centre, British safeguards and communal representation. Without Congress, the settlement lacked the organisation claiming the largest mass mandate.

### 2. Gandhi-Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931

The settlement granted bounded relief:

- release of political prisoners not convicted of violence;
- remission of uncollected fines;
- return of confiscated property not already sold to third parties;
- lenient treatment of officials who had resigned;
- limited manufacture or collection of salt for personal use in coastal areas;
- peaceful, non-aggressive picketing.

Congress agreed to suspend Civil Disobedience and participate in the Second RTC. The government did not concede the proposed inquiry into police excesses, a transfer-of-power timetable or a general release covering violence convictions. Commutation of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru was not written into the Pact and was not secured.

### 3. Karachi Congress, 29 March 1931

The session met six days after the three revolutionaries were executed on 23 March. Gandhi encountered black-flag demonstrations from angry youth. Congress nevertheless endorsed the Pact, passed a resolution admiring the bravery and sacrifice of the executed men while dissociating itself from political violence, and **reaffirmed Purna Swaraj**.

Karachi then defined what Swaraj should mean.

**Fundamental Rights cluster**

- freedom of speech and press;
- peaceful assembly and association;
- freedom of conscience, profession and practice of religion subject to public order and morality;
- protection of minority culture, language and script;
- equality before law irrespective of caste, creed or sex;
- equal access to public employment and public places;
- personal liberty and security of dwelling and property according to law;
- state neutrality toward religions;
- universal adult franchise;
- free and compulsory primary education;
- freedom of movement, residence, property and occupation within India;
- no state-conferred titles and opposition to capital punishment in the adopted rights programme.

**National Economic Programme cluster**

- economic organisation directed toward justice and a decent standard of life;
- living wage, healthy work, limited hours and machinery for industrial disputes;
- protection against old age, sickness and unemployment;
- freedom from serfdom, maternity protection and prohibition of child labour of school-going age;
- labour and peasant organisation;
- substantial reduction of rent and land revenue, exemption for uneconomic holdings, debt relief and control of usury;
- progressive taxation of agricultural income and graduated inheritance tax;
- major reductions in military and civil expenditure;
- protection of indigenous cloth, prohibition of intoxicants and removal of the salt burden;
- state ownership or control of key industries, mines and transport.

### Comparison: conference, truce and programme

| Stage | Achievement | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| First RTC | federation and provincial responsibility entered a shared agenda | Congress absent; minorities and central safeguards unresolved |
| Gandhi-Irwin Pact | prisoner and civil-disobedience relief; Congress recognised as negotiating actor | no sovereignty timetable or police inquiry |
| Karachi | Pact ratified; Purna Swaraj and a rights-economic vision joined | execution controversy and unequal truce gains remained |

**Objection:** The Pact betrayed the movement's sacrifice.

**Reply:** It did not achieve Purna Swaraj, but it secured relief and recognised Congress as indispensable. The stronger criticism is precise: Congress received no enforceable constitutional guarantee, and the execution context made ratification politically painful.

### Visual

```text
Gandhi: sole Congress representative; Congress speaks for territorial nation
Princes: federation without surrendering protected authority
Communal delegates: group safeguards before majoritarian transfer
British: safeguards and imperial control
                    -> no common constitutional sequence -> deadlock
```

### 1. Exact setting and represented constituencies

The Second RTC met from **7 September to 1 December 1931**. Gandhi attended as the **sole Congress representative**, not the sole Indian delegate. Alongside British ministers and party representatives were princes, Liberals, Muslim, Sikh, Indian Christian, Anglo-Indian, European and Depressed Classes representatives, women, labour, landholders and commercial interests.

Congress participation repaired the First Session's most visible absence, but it did not create a shared mandate among the many represented constituencies.

### 2. Federal Structure Committee

The committee debated an all-India federation of British Indian provinces and princely states:

- princes accepted federation in principle but sought favourable accession terms and protected authority;
- Congress wanted responsible government at the centre and a national political framework;
- Britain reserved safeguards over defence, external affairs, finance and executive stability;
- the relation between elected British Indian representation and ruler-nominated state representation remained unequal.

Federal agreement in principle therefore did not settle the democratic content of the federation.

### 3. Minorities Committee

The committee brought into direct conflict several claims:

- Muslim representatives sought communal safeguards and political weight;
- Sikh representatives sought protection, especially in Punjab;
- Ambedkar argued for independent political representation of the Depressed Classes;
- Anglo-Indian, Indian Christian and European representatives defended specific guarantees;
- Gandhi argued that Congress represented the territorial nation and resisted multiplying permanent communal electorates, especially for the Depressed Classes.

No agreed minority settlement emerged. Gandhi's national claim had a mass basis, but other delegates denied that Congress could speak conclusively for communities whose institutional security remained unsettled.

### 4. Why the Second Session failed

The deadlock was structural rather than merely personal:

```text
Congress -> national responsibility before prolonged safeguards
communities -> enforceable protection before majority rule
princes -> federation without democratic erosion of rulership
Britain -> federation under strategic, financial and executive reservations
```

The Prime Minister closed the session without a common solution. Gandhi returned to India after the conference had enlarged Congress's formal recognition but failed to secure freedom, agreed federation or minority consent.

| Claim | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Congress represents the nation | mass organisation and anti-colonial legitimacy | incomplete communal and social consent |
| group delegates represent minorities | voiced concrete insecurity | could freeze identity and elite mediation |
| princes complete all-India federation | territorial reach | rulers lacked popular electoral mandates |
| British safeguards preserve stability | administrative continuity | retained imperial veto over responsibility |

**Objection:** Gandhi's sole status made him personally responsible for failure.

**Reply:** His claim could understate minority and Ambedkarite autonomy, but no personal compromise could by itself reconcile princely accession, democratic legitimacy, communal safeguards and British retained power.

### 1. Resumption under pre-emption

After Gandhi returned, the Working Committee resolved on 29 December 1931 to resume struggle. The government acted before another Dandi-like public sequence could form. Gandhi was arrested on 4 January 1932; Congress organisations were outlawed; offices and property were seized; communication was censored; ordinances widened detention and executive power.

### 2. Third Round Table Conference: exact scale and composition

The Third RTC met from **17 November to 24 December 1932**. Congress and the British Labour Party were absent. The standard attendance statement is **46 of 112**. The official proceedings independently list the forty-six delegates as:

- 12 British representatives;
- 12 Indian States' representatives;
- 22 British Indian representatives.

The reduced British Indian group still included Muslim, Sikh, Depressed Classes, Liberal, labour, commercial and women's voices, but not Congress. The conference therefore had technical breadth without broad nationalist authority.

### 3. Agenda and result

The Third Session filled details left by the earlier conferences. Its agenda covered:

- the Franchise Committee report and the size/election of federal chambers;
- legislative and administrative relations between the federal centre and units;
- special powers of the Governor-General and governors;
- financial and commercial safeguards;
- defence finance;
- federal finance and states' financial relations;
- fundamental rights and constituent powers;
- instruments of accession;
- Anglo-Indian education and a Supreme Court.

The conference refined machinery rather than resolving legitimacy. It accepted no Congress-backed transfer settlement and did not cure the minority or princely mandate problems.

The constitutional discussions fed the **March 1933 White Paper**, then the Joint Select Committee and the Government of India Act, 1935. Topic 22 owns only this bridge. **Topic 24 owns the detailed White Paper-to-Act provisions, the federal scheme, provincial autonomy under the Act, the 1937 elections and Congress ministries.**

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did salt succeed as the opening issue of Civil Disobedience?

**Model answer (110 words):** Salt joined material grievance, legal clarity and universal symbolism. The tax burdened the poor disproportionately, while monopoly made colonial authority visible in an everyday necessity. Unlike a council boycott, salt law could be broken by ordinary people; unlike a uniform revenue strike, it did not depend on one land relation.

Gandhi integrated it into a calibrated escalation after the eleven demands. The Sabarmati-Dandi march publicised intent, trained volunteers and used villages as communication nodes. Coastal manufacture could then coexist with inland boycott, tax refusal and forest action.

Its limitation was economic: salt alone could not disable imperial finance. Its success was catalytic—turning law-breaking into a legitimate, repeatable national act.

**Scoring note:** Award the highest band for universality, legal symbolism, organisational route and the fiscal-limit qualification.

**Component rubric (10 marks):** universality and distributive burden 3 + visible legal breach 2 + march organisation 3 + fiscal limitation and catalytic verdict 2 = 10/10.

## 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Compare the Nehru Report and the Round Table Conference process as attempts to solve India's constitutional problem.

**Model answer (137 words):** The Nehru Report was an Indian-authored counter to exclusion. It proposed dominion status, responsible government, adult franchise, rights, linguistic provinces and a federation with central residuary powers. Joint electorates with reservations sought common citizenship plus minority protection.

Its weakness was consent: Muslim, Sikh and Hindu communal objections exposed competing security models. The Round Table process widened formal participation to princes, communal representatives and British parties. It placed federation and minority safeguards at the centre, but the First RTC lacked Congress and the Second contained incompatible claims. Princes protected authority, minorities sought guarantees, Congress claimed national representation and Britain retained safeguards.

The Report was programmatically coherent but politically insufficient; the conferences were more plural but imperially structured and unable to agree on sequence. Both demonstrate that responsible government, federal power and minority security had to be resolved together.

**Scoring note:** High marks require method, content and failure comparison—not two separate narratives.

**Component rubric (15 marks):** Nehru Report method/content 4 + RTC representation/process 4 + comparative failure analysis 4 + integrated constitutional verdict 3 = 15/15.

## 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Trace the movement-negotiation-repression cycle from 1930 to 1934 and assess its historical significance.

**Model answer (154 words):** The eleven-demand ultimatum and Dandi march launched open illegality in 1930. Salt, boycott, tax refusal and forest defiance spread through differentiated regional organisations. Repression produced arrests and confiscation but also legitimacy costs, helping make Congress indispensable after the weak First RTC.

The Gandhi-Irwin Pact traded suspension and Second-RTC participation for prisoner, property, salt and picketing relief. It secured recognition but no sovereignty guarantee or police inquiry. The Second RTC deadlocked over federation, princes, minorities and British safeguards. Congress resumed struggle; Gandhi's arrest on 4 January 1932 and sweeping ordinances prevented a second Dandi-style preparation.

The Communal Award and Poona Pact shifted attention to representation under unequal social power. The Third RTC lacked Congress and authority. Limited phases continued before suspension and final withdrawal in 1934.

The cycle did not transfer power, but it widened mobilisation, exposed coercion, accumulated cadres and changed bargaining legitimacy. These effects were consequential, not sufficient or inevitable causes of independence.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional arc

- The 1919 review promise created the statutory setting; the 1927 all-white appointment created the legitimacy crisis.
- The Simon Report of 1930 proposed ending provincial dyarchy, widening provincial responsibility under governor safeguards and moving toward federation, while withholding equivalent central responsibility.
- The December 1927 Delhi Proposals demanded Sind separation, NWFP reform, one-third Muslim central representation and protection of Muslim majorities in Punjab and Bengal.
- Motilal Nehru's committee answered Birkenhead with Dominion Status, responsible government, adult franchise, rights, linguistic provinces and joint electorates with bounded reservations.
- Federal levels coexisted with central residuary powers in the Nehru design.
- Jinnah's three amendments were rejected in December 1928; the March 1929 Fourteen Points consolidated federal, minority, territorial, service, ministry, veto and cultural safeguards.
- The 2 November Delhi Manifesto demanded implementation-oriented talks; the 23 December failure of assurance cleared the path from the Calcutta ultimatum to Lahore.

## Movement arc

- Gandhi published eleven minimum demands on 31 January 1930 and sent a separate letter and salt-law notice on 2 March.
- The programme included reduction of the exchange ratio to 1s. 4d. and 50 per cent cuts in land revenue and military expenditure.
- Salt combined universal consumption, poor people's burden, clear law and replicability.
- Dandi was a moving organisational school from 12 March to 6 April.
- Programme: salt, cloth/liquor boycott, conditional no-tax/no-revenue/no-rent, chowkidari refusal, forest defiance, resignations and non-cooperation.
- Dharasana revealed disciplined suffering; Peshawar prior Frontier organisation and a specific Garhwali refusal; Sholapur mass anger and control limits.
- Chittagong and Surya Sen belong to Topic 21; Topic 22 records only the chronological overlap.
- Regional motive mattered: revenue, tenancy, village policing, forest access and coastal monopoly were not interchangeable.

## Social coalition

- Women entered direct action but patriarchy persisted.
- Workers produced powerful local peaks, not one national wave.
- Students multiplied communication.
- Business support joined nationalism and commercial interest, then became settlement-oriented.
- Muslim participation was lower overall than in Non-Cooperation but neither absent nor predetermined; NWFP was exceptional.

## Negotiation and closure

- First RTC, 12 November 1930-19 January 1931, represented British parties, princes and organised British Indian constituencies but lacked Congress.
- The Pact released defined non-violent prisoners and offered bounded property, fine, salt and picketing relief; it did not guarantee sovereignty or a police inquiry.
- Karachi on 29 March 1931 met after the executions and black-flag protests, endorsed the Pact, reaffirmed Purna Swaraj and adopted full rights and economic clusters.
- Second RTC, 7 September-1 December 1931, failed over federal responsibility, princely accession, minorities and British safeguards.
- The Communal Award-Poona Pact sequence is retained only to the minimum needed for chronology and the direct 2025 PYQ; the Pact date is 24 September 1932.
- Third RTC, 17 November-24 December 1932, had 46 of 112 expected participants; the official list divides them into 12 British, 12 States and 22 British Indian delegates.
- The March 1933 White Paper leads forward to Topic 24, which owns the detailed 1935 Act and provincial-ministry consequences.
- Resumed struggle faced pre-emptive ordinances and ended finally in 1934.
