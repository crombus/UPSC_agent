# Left, Peasant, Workers' and States' Peoples' Movements - Learning Session Live Edition

The national movement did not become socially radical through one organisation or one ideology. Socialist language, communist organisation, trade-union action, peasant mobilisation and democratic struggles in princely states grew through different routes. They sometimes converged with the Congress, sometimes pushed it from within, and sometimes exposed the limits of its broad social coalition.

This session asks one governing question:

> How did organised pressure from workers, peasants, the Left and princely-state subjects change both the meaning and the social reach of Indian nationalism?

The Left did not invent Indian poverty. It supplied languages and organisations that linked poverty to structures: imperial extraction, landlord power, capitalist exploitation and unequal ownership. This changed the political question. Independence could no longer be discussed only as a transfer of authority from British to Indian hands.

### 1. What "the Left" means here

The term covers related but non-identical currents:

| Current | Main emphasis | Organisational location |
|---|---|---|
| Communist | class struggle, Marxism, worker-peasant organisation and anti-imperialism | communist groups and, eventually, the CPI |
| Congress socialist | socialism combined with work inside the national movement | Congress Socialist Party |
| Left nationalist | planning, anti-imperial militancy and social transformation without identical party discipline | Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose and other radicals |
| Peasant and labour radicalism | immediate material demands that could become anti-imperialist | unions, kisan sabhas and regional movements |

These currents disagreed about organisation, timing, international strategy and the relationship between class struggle and national unity. "Left influence" therefore does not mean that the Congress became communist.

### 2. Why the interwar decades mattered

Four forces reinforced one another:

1. **The Russian Revolution of 1917** showed that an old regime could be overthrown and that workers and peasants could be described as political makers, not merely followers.
2. **Colonial economic experience** made structural critiques plausible: low wages, insecure tenancy, debt and high rent were lived realities.
3. **The Great Depression** damaged the prestige of capitalism and sharply reduced agricultural prices after 1929.
4. **Mass nationalism** created networks, public meetings, newspapers and cadres through which socialist ideas could travel.

The Depression did not automatically produce socialism. It made existing inequality more visible and weakened confidence in orthodox economic arrangements.

### 3. Nehru and Bose as transmitters, not identical ideologues

Jawaharlal Nehru popularised socialism, international anti-imperialism and the idea that political freedom required economic reconstruction. Subhas Chandra Bose stressed planning, rapid transformation and a more confrontational anti-imperial strategy. Both helped move socialist vocabulary into national politics, but neither should be reduced to the organisational line of the CPI or the CSP.

Nehru did not formally join the CSP. Bose's later organisational conflict with the Congress leadership arose from a mixture of ideology, strategy and authority, not from a simple communist-versus-Gandhian split.

### 4. Institutional signs of a wider agenda

The Karachi Resolution of 1931 linked civil liberties with socio-economic commitments. The Faizpur Congress of 1936 foregrounded agrarian demands. These moments show pressure entering Congress doctrine, but resolutions were not the same as completed redistribution.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Socialist influence was mostly elite language used by educated leaders.

**Reply:** Elite mediation mattered, but class organisations were not merely rhetorical. Trade unions, kisan sabhas and regional struggles created autonomous demands and leadership. The qualification is equally important: industrial workers were concentrated in limited centres, and many peasants mobilised around local grievances rather than a settled socialist programme.

### UPSC application and traps

- Distinguish socialist influence from communist organisational control.
- Treat Nehru, Bose, CSP and CPI as overlapping but distinct.
- Explain the Depression through prices, fixed obligations and debt, not as an ideological slogan.
- Use Karachi and Faizpur as evidence of agenda change, not proof that social revolution had occurred.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the 1930s make economic justice a central test of Indian nationalism?

**Model answer (141 words):** The 1930s joined ideological change to material crisis. The Russian Revolution had already made socialist reconstruction an imaginable alternative, while colonial poverty, insecure tenancy and industrial exploitation supplied Indian grievances. The Great Depression then reduced agricultural prices without proportionately reducing rents, revenue or debt obligations, intensifying rural distress.

Nehru, Bose, communists and Congress socialists translated these conditions into competing programmes. Trade unions and kisan sabhas made workers and peasants visible as organised constituencies rather than an undifferentiated "mass." The Karachi Resolution linked civil liberties with socio-economic commitments, and the Faizpur Congress gave agrarian questions greater prominence.

Yet nationalism did not become uniformly socialist. Congress remained a cross-class coalition, industrial labour was regionally concentrated, and many peasant struggles pursued immediate local demands. The decisive change was therefore agenda-setting: independence increasingly had to promise social and economic transformation, even where implementation remained cautious.

**Component rubric (10 marks; 150-word ceiling):** causal context 2 + Depression mechanism 3 + named organisational/programme evidence 3 + cross-class qualification and verdict 2 = 10/10.

The date question is not solved by choosing one date and erasing the other. The Tashkent initiative of 17 October 1920 was an expatriate formation associated with M.N. Roy and the Communist International. The Kanpur conference beginning on 26 December 1925 brought Indian groups together on Indian soil; the CPI recognises this as its foundation point. Standard UPSC answers should state both and explain what each date represents.

### 1. M.N. Roy and the international route

M.N. Roy became an important theorist of colonial revolution and participated in Communist International debates. His importance lies not in single-handedly creating Indian communism but in connecting anti-imperial revolution with international Marxist strategy.

The early movement also grew independently inside India through small circles among workers, intellectuals and radical nationalists. Bombay, Bengal, Punjab and Madras did not simply receive a finished doctrine from abroad.

### 2. Kanpur and the attempt at all-India organisation

The Kanpur conference of December 1925 connected dispersed groups in an all-India organisation. S.V. Ghate later emerged as a general secretary. Communists sought influence in unions, worker-peasant organisations and the Congress, but their line toward the national movement changed over time.

This matters because the CPI was neither continuously legal nor organisationally stable. Colonial bans, arrests and international strategic shifts repeatedly altered its capacity.

### 3. Repression as a political mechanism

The Peshawar and Kanpur conspiracy prosecutions preceded the largest intervention. On **20 March 1929**, the government arrested **thirty-one** leading communists, trade unionists and other left-wing leaders. The later Meerut proceedings ultimately named **thirty-two accused**, including three British communists. The figures describe different stages, not rival totals: thirty-one people were arrested in the coordinated 20 March swoop, while thirty-two were ultimately accused and tried as the case was constituted. The Meerut Conspiracy Case then lasted for years.

```text
radical influence in labour
          |
Public Safety and Trade Disputes measures
          |
leadership arrests in March 1929
          |
long Meerut trial
       /       \
movement loses organisers
trial publicises communist arguments
```

Repression therefore had a double effect. It damaged organisational continuity but made the accused and their ideas nationally visible. Nationalists including Jawaharlal Nehru, M.A. Ansari and M.C. Chagla participated in the defence, while Gandhi visited the prisoners.

### 4. Why conspiracy cases were not ordinary criminal trials

The colonial state treated organisation itself as a security threat. The aim was to separate communists from the national mainstream and weaken labour leadership. The state simultaneously used coercion and selective reform, such as labour inquiry, to channel worker politics into controlled forms.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because communists were influenced by an international movement, their politics was external to Indian nationalism.

**Reply:** International influence was real, but Indian communism acquired roots through Indian workplaces, agrarian struggles and anti-imperial networks. Its vulnerability was the opposite problem: international line changes could produce strategic positions poorly matched to Indian nationalist sentiment.

### UPSC application and traps

- Write **17 October 1920, Tashkent** as the expatriate genesis.
- Write **26 December 1925, Kanpur** as the all-India organisational foundation recognised by CPI.
- The coordinated Meerut arrests occurred on **20 March 1929**: thirty-one were arrested, while thirty-two were ultimately accused and tried.
- The case continued into 1933; always identify which procedural stage a number describes.
- Do not treat every socialist as a CPI member.
- Repression weakened leadership but also publicised communist ideas.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Colonial repression both weakened and advertised the communist movement in India. Discuss with reference to the conspiracy cases.

**Model answer (198 words):** The colonial state viewed communist influence in labour and anti-imperial politics as a security threat. Early prosecutions at Peshawar and Kanpur were followed by the coordinated arrest of thirty-one leading communists, trade unionists and other left-wing leaders on 20 March 1929; the constituted Meerut case ultimately had thirty-two accused. The prolonged Meerut Conspiracy Case sought to remove organisers, disrupt union networks and isolate communists from the national movement. Repressive labour legislation and the trial together deprived a young movement of experienced leadership.

Yet Meerut also became a cause celebre. Defence statements circulated in nationalist newspapers, familiarising wider audiences with communist critiques. Jawaharlal Nehru, M.A. Ansari and M.C. Chagla joined the defence effort, and Gandhi visited the prisoners. The state therefore failed fully to draw a boundary between communist activists and mainstream anti-imperial opinion.

This publicity did not cancel the organisational loss. A small movement operating under bans could not easily replace arrested leaders, and international tactical shifts later complicated its nationalist relationship. The conspiracy cases thus reveal a colonial paradox: criminalisation could fragment institutions while converting a courtroom into a political platform. Repression constrained communist capacity but could not prevent class language from entering Indian nationalism.

The Congress Socialist Party was formed at Bombay in October 1934 under leaders including Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev and Minoo Masani. Ram Manohar Lohia, Achyut Patwardhan, Yusuf Meherally and others became important in its wider work.

### 1. Four linked propositions

Congress socialists broadly held that:

1. the immediate primary struggle was national freedom;
2. nationalism was a necessary stage toward socialism;
3. socialists should work inside Congress because it led the anti-imperial movement;
4. workers and peasants should be organised around their own economic demands and become a stronger social base for nationalism.

This was not timidity alone. It was a judgment that leaving the largest anti-colonial organisation would isolate socialists from the people they hoped to mobilise.

### 2. What "within Congress" did and did not mean

The CSP tried to radicalise Congress policy, build class organisations and resist excessive compromise. But membership inside a broad coalition imposed limits. Congress included capitalists, landlords, middle classes and peasants; an immediate programme of class war could fracture anti-imperial unity.

| Advantage of internal work | Cost of internal work |
|---|---|
| access to national cadre and legitimacy | pressure to preserve coalition unity |
| influence on programmes and resolutions | diluted or delayed class demands |
| bridge to peasants and workers | tension with conservative Congress leaders |
| participation in mass struggle | dependence on Congress strategic timing |

### 3. Nehru's relationship to the CSP

Nehru's socialist vision, Congress presidencies in 1936 and 1937, anti-fascism and planning advocacy gave legitimacy to the Left. Yet he never formally joined the CSP. He preferred to influence the Congress as a whole and feared factional confinement.

This distinction prevents a common error: ideological sympathy is not organisational membership.

### 4. Bose and the left-nationalist route

Bose shared planning and anti-imperial radicalism but was not simply a CSP leader. His conflict with the Gandhian core involved the pace of struggle, the presidency's authority, the composition of the Working Committee and later the response to impending war. His 1939 break requires its own lesson.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** By remaining inside Congress, the CSP surrendered socialism to coalition discipline.

**Reply:** It did accept constraints, but internal work helped move agrarian, labour and planning questions into national debate. The unresolved tension was real: influence increased, yet an independent socialist programme remained subordinate to Congress unity.

### UPSC application and traps

- CSP formation: Bombay, October 1934.
- It was formed **within** Congress.
- Nehru sympathised but did not formally join.
- Do not merge CSP, CPI and Bose's later Forward Bloc.
- The central analytical phrase is **radicalisation without organisational separation**.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the Congress Socialist Party choose to work inside the Indian National Congress?

**Model answer (160 words):** The CSP's October 1934 decision reflected a strategic judgment. Congress was the principal organisation of the anti-imperial struggle and possessed mass legitimacy, provincial networks and the capacity to launch nationwide action. Socialists such as Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya Narendra Dev therefore regarded national freedom as a necessary stage toward socialism and considered withdrawal from Congress politically isolating.

Internal work offered access to workers, peasants and nationalist cadres. The CSP could press for agrarian reform, labour organisation and a socialist direction while preserving a common front against colonial rule. It also helped connect class organisations with national politics.

The strategy imposed costs. Congress was a cross-class coalition containing landlords and capitalists, so radical demands could be diluted in the name of unity. CSP action also remained affected by Congress leadership and timing. Its choice was therefore neither capitulation nor complete autonomy: it exchanged organisational independence for wider influence, successfully radicalising parts of nationalist discourse without converting Congress into a socialist party.

AITUC was founded at Bombay on 31 October 1920. Lala Lajpat Rai was its first president and Dewan Chaman Lal its first general secretary. Its importance lay in giving scattered unions an all-India platform and connecting economic freedom with political freedom.

### 1. Nationalists and worker self-organisation

Early AITUC included leading nationalists such as C.R. Das, Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Bose and others. Lajpat Rai linked imperialism with organised capital, yet also anticipated that workers would ultimately produce leaders from their own ranks.

This creates a useful distinction:

- nationalist leaders helped create legitimacy and coordination;
- workers' interests were not identical to the priorities of a cross-class Congress.

### 2. Industrial centres and major actions

Bombay textile labour became a major centre of militancy, especially in the strike wave of 1928. Railway workers also organised significant actions; the Great Indian Peninsular Railway system was an important field. Jute, railways, textile mills, plantations and ports produced different labour regimes, so one strike model cannot explain all industries.

```text
factory concentration
      |
shared hours, wage and discipline grievances
      |
strike committees and unions
      |
rapid collective leverage
      |
equally rapid exposure to lockout, police and dismissal
```

The strength of workers came from concentration at strategic nodes. The limit was equally structural: industrial labour was a minority, unions were uneven, employment insecurity was high and sustained national coordination was difficult.

### 3. Law: recognition and control

The Trade Unions Act of 1926 provided a framework for registration and legal protection for registered unions. Recognition did not mean freedom from control. The Trade Disputes Act of 1929 restricted certain strikes and created state mechanisms for industrial disputes. Public-safety measures and Meerut prosecutions targeted radical leadership.

| Instrument | Function | Political meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Trade Unions Act, 1926 | registration and limited legal status | channel organisation into recognised forms |
| Trade Disputes Act, 1929 | regulation of industrial conflict and restrictions | constrain disruptive collective power |
| Royal Commission on Labour, 1929 | inquiry and recommendations | concession and incorporation alongside coercion |
| Meerut prosecution | remove radical organisers | separate labour militancy from nationalist-left leadership |

### 4. Labour and Congress: convergence without identity

Congress benefited when strikes weakened colonial normalcy, but it feared that unrestricted class conflict might alienate Indian capitalists or escape national discipline. Gandhi preferred regulation of capital-labour relations to the destruction of capitalists. Nehru, while presiding over AITUC in 1929, also acknowledged that Congress represented many classes and was not itself a labour organisation.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because union density was limited, labour was marginal to nationalism.

**Reply:** Numbers alone miss strategic location and ideological effect. Workers could disrupt major industries, dramatise colonial-capital relations and force labour rights into nationalist programmes. Yet it remains inaccurate to describe workers as the uniform leading force of the freedom struggle.

- Keep labour action distinct from peasant revenue or rent struggles.
- Do not equate legal registration with unrestricted strike freedom.
- Use the phrase **strategic concentration, limited national density**.

Book context: queried - the fall in agricultural prices and persistence of fixed obligations were checked

CA found: no current anchor needed
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: why falling prices could deepen rather than relieve distress

```text
agricultural prices fall sharply after 1929
                    |
peasant cash income contracts
                    |
rent + land revenue + old debt remain sticky
                    |
more produce must be sold to meet the same cash claims
                    |
arrears, eviction, land transfer and renewed borrowing
                    |
rent reduction + debt relief + tenure security become political demands
```

The key Depression mechanism is a **price-obligation mismatch**. A cultivator received less money for crops while rent, revenue and debt claims did not fall proportionately. Manufactured goods also did not become cheaper to the same degree. The result was not simply "poverty" but a tighter transfer of risk onto cultivators.

### 1. The countryside was not one class

| Rural position | Typical interest under crisis |
|---|---|
| landlord or zamindar | preserve rent, levies and control over land |
| occupancy tenant | reduce rent, stop eviction, restore lost holdings |
| insecure tenant or sharecropper | secure tenure and a larger crop share |
| owner-cultivator under state revenue | obtain reassessment, remission or suspension |
| agricultural labourer | improve wages, resist forced labour and gain access to land |
| moneylender-creditor | recover debt and interest |

Because interests differed, "peasant unity" was often temporary. A prosperous tenant could oppose a zamindar while employing landless labour. A movement might radicalise over rent but remain cautious about wages or caste hierarchy.

### 2. Three recurring claim systems

```text
STATE CLAIM            LANDLORD CLAIM          CREDITOR CLAIM
land revenue           rent + illegal dues     principal + interest
     |                       |                        |
remission/reduction     tenancy security        moratorium/scaling down
```

The target depended on land relations. In a ryotwari-type area, cultivators often confronted the state revenue demand directly. Under zamindari or taluqdari arrangements, conflict more readily focused on landlords, rent-farmers, eviction and illegal cesses.

### 3. From grievance to organisation

Depression did not mechanically create a movement. Organisation required:

1. a shared interpretation of distress;
2. local leaders and village committees;
3. demands precise enough to mobilise around;
4. links to wider kisan, socialist or Congress networks;
5. confidence that collective action could reduce retaliation.

Civil Disobedience supplied confidence and political space, but its mechanics remain with the preceding topic. Here the important result is that economic grievance acquired durable class organisation.

### 4. Bardoli as an organisational bridge, not a template for every countryside

Bardoli preceded the Depression-era federation covered in the next lesson, but it supplies the clearest anatomy of disciplined agrarian organisation. The issue was a proposed enhancement of state land revenue in a ryotwari-type setting. Patel divided the taluqa into **thirteen workers' camps (*chhavanis*)**, each under an experienced leader. **One hundred political workers** from across Gujarat, assisted by about **1,500 volunteers**, many of them students, formed the movement's operating cadre.

```text
thirteen chhavanis
        |
100 political workers + about 1,500 volunteers
        |
daily Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika
        |
intelligence on wavering payers and coming confiscations
        |
meetings + door-to-door persuasion + rapid village warning
        |
disciplined refusal of enhanced state revenue
```

A publications bureau issued the daily ***Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika*** with movement reports, speeches and images of *jabti* or confiscation proceedings. Volunteers carried it across the taluqa. An **intelligence wing** identified wavering cultivators, monitored government moves and warned villages of likely confiscations, allowing households to lock property or move temporarily toward neighbouring Baroda. Organisation therefore joined persuasion, information and collective discipline; it was not simply Patel issuing a no-tax call.

#### Bardoli compared with Awadh and Eka

| Dimension | Bardoli, Gujarat | Awadh Kisan mobilisation and Eka, United Provinces |
|---|---|---|
| agrarian relation | relatively prosperous peasant proprietors confronted state revenue enhancement | tenants and some small zamindars confronted taluqdars, rent-farmers, excess rent, eviction and forced labour |
| principal target | colonial revenue assessment | landlords, *thekedars* and local coercive relations, alongside the colonial order |
| organisation | Congress-linked command, camps, trained workers, volunteers, daily publication and intelligence | stronger grassroots autonomy; Eka's contact with nationalists diminished as local leadership and methods diverged |
| political fit | state demand and nationalist target could be aligned | direct anti-landlord action risked conflict inside Congress's cross-class coalition |
| limit | success in one taluqa of comparatively prosperous proprietors could not represent tenants or landless labourers everywhere | wider social antagonism made discipline, coalition and settlement more difficult |

The comparison explains variation through land relations rather than through a claim that Gujarat peasants were inherently more disciplined. Bardoli's prosperous-proprietor base supplied resources and cohesion, but also limited how far its model could travel. This session uses Bardoli only to explain agrarian organisation and Congress-class relations; the mechanics of Civil Disobedience remain in Topic 22.

### 5. Anti-zamindari, rent and debt demands

The peasant programme increasingly included:

- reduction of rent and land revenue;
- abolition of illegal levies and forced labour;
- moratorium or scaling down of debt;
- protection against eviction;
- occupancy rights;
- restoration of land lost through arrears;
- recognition of peasant unions;
- eventually, abolition of zamindari.

These demands formed a ladder. Debt relief could be reformist; abolition of zamindari directly challenged a major property structure.

### Moplah/Mappila: a necessary warning against single-cause labels

The 1921 Mappila rebellion grew from an agrarian and anti-colonial setting in Malabar. Mappila tenants faced insecure tenure, renewal fees, high rents and other landlord exactions. The Manjeri conference, tenant associations and the Khilafat mobilisation overlapped; colonial prohibition, leadership arrests and the Tirurangadi mosque raid intensified confrontation. In its first phase, rebels frequently attacked unpopular *jenmi* landlords and symbols of government authority.

The qualification is indispensable. As martial law and severe repression deepened desperation, attacks, murders and forced conversions of Hindus increased. What had been largely anti-government and anti-landlord acquired **grave communal violence and strong communal overtones**. A sound answer therefore holds both propositions together:

```text
agrarian insecurity + anti-colonial/Khilafat mobilisation
                         |
                         v
repression and rebellion against landlords and the state
                         |
                         v
communalisation, forced conversions and violence against Hindus
```

Neither "pure class revolt" nor "only communal riot" explains the full trajectory. Its agrarian and anti-colonial origins do not excuse communal violence; the later communal rupture does not erase the tenancy context.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Economic distress explains mobilisation by itself.

**Reply:** Similar distress could produce migration, private borrowing or submission rather than collective action. Organisation, political opportunity, leadership and local social relations determined whether hardship became a movement.

- Use Bardoli's camps, volunteers, daily paper and intelligence wing as evidence of organisation, then state its prosperous-proprietor and single-taluqa limits.

- Describe Mappila through both agrarian/anti-colonial context and grave communal violence.

These movements are often grouped as "radical peasant struggles." That label is useful only if their differences are preserved.

### 1. Bakasht: reform versus landlord compromise

In Bihar, former occupancy tenants demanded restoration of land lost to zamindars, particularly during Depression arrears. Congress ministry legislation offered partial restoration under conditions, but kisan leaders judged the landlord compromise inadequate. The issue exposed the difference between administrative relief and a movement's claim to agrarian justice.

### 2. Warli movement

The Warli Adivasi movement in western India grew in the mid-1940s around exploitative landlord, creditor and labour relations, including forced labour. Left organisation helped convert dispersed subordination into collective bargaining and resistance.

The category "peasant" must be expanded here: tribal cultivators and labourers faced land, debt, wage and social-power questions simultaneously.

### 3. Tebhaga in Bengal, 1946

Bengal sharecroppers or **bargadars** generally surrendered half the crop to jotedars. Tebhaga demanded that cultivators retain two-thirds and that the crop be stored in the bargadar's own **khamar** before division.

```text
existing practice: crop -> jotedar's store -> half share
movement claim:   crop -> bargadar's store -> two-thirds to cultivator
                         |
                    control of grain
                    + larger share
```

The location of storage mattered because control over measurement and possession shaped the real division.

Women participated in mobilisation and defended harvested crop. Their role should be stated as political agency, not added as a decorative sentence.

### 4. Telangana from 1946

The Telangana struggle began under the Hyderabad state against landlord domination, illegal exactions and **vetti** or forced labour. It developed an armed dimension and village-level challenges to established authority. Because it continued after independence, it cannot be narrated as a struggle whose meaning ended automatically in August 1947.

Its combined target distinguished it:

- local landed power;
- coercive structures of the Hyderabad state;
- later, conflict shaped by accession and postcolonial state consolidation.

### 5. Tribal-response bridge for routed PYQs

Warli belongs directly to this late-colonial social movement. Earlier uprisings are a necessary cross-period comparison because the 2023 GS-I demand asks how colonial rule affected tribal societies and how tribals responded.

#### Munda *ulgulan*: from common land to an anti-Raj horizon

For more than thirty years, Munda *sardars* resisted the destruction of a **system of common landholding** in Chota Nagpur. Colonial incorporation enabled the intrusion of **jagirdars, thikadars or revenue farmers, and merchant moneylenders**. These outsiders were grouped as ***dikus*** because they converted customary control into rent, debt and alienation.

Birsa Munda did not stop at demanding the removal of individual intermediaries. His religious-messianic message mobilised followers around a lost moral order, but the 1899-1900 *ulgulan* also developed an explicit political horizon: end the British Raj and establish Munda or Birsa rule. That movement from local land defence to an anti-Raj project makes the uprising answerable as political resistance rather than as a pre-political disturbance.

```text
common landholding weakened
          |
jagirdars + thikadars + moneylenders enter as dikus
          |
rent, debt, alienation and loss of customary authority
          |
Birsa's religious mobilisation
          |
Munda rule / Birsa Raj and an anti-British horizon
```

The movement retained limits and discriminations. It used a religious idiom, was regionally bounded and was militarily suppressed; non-tribal poor were not to be attacked. These facts prevent both romantic homogenisation and the colonial label of indiscriminate "primitive rebellion."

#### Distributed comparison: Kol, Santhal, Rampa and Munda

| Movement | Region and period | Colonial disruption foregrounded | Response and analytical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kol risings | Chhotanagpur, 1820-37 | outsider penetration and erosion of local authority | prolonged armed resistance; shows an early regional response before later all-India nationalism |

The comparison is not a claim that every tribal movement had one programme. It supplies a distributed answer: land alienation dominates Santhal and Munda evidence; state-backed intermediaries and forest control are especially clear at Rampa; the Kol sequence shows the longer chronology of resistance. Colonial rule altered land, forest access, authority and credit in different combinations, and tribal responses ranged from local armed defence to a more explicit political project.

### Women, caste and uneven representation

Women acted as organisers, picketers, couriers, defenders of crop and participants in village mobilisation. Yet formal leadership remained disproportionately male. Similarly, anti-zamindari politics did not automatically end caste domination. Landless Dalit labourers could have interests different from tenant or middle-peasant leaders.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** These movements were local economic conflicts, not part of nationalism.

**Reply:** Their immediate targets were often landlords or princely authorities rather than the colonial state. Yet those powers were embedded in a colonial order, and left networks connected material struggle with anti-imperial politics. Their autonomy should be preserved: they were not merely local branches of Congress campaigns.

**Model answer (193 words):** Tebhaga and Telangana both challenged entrenched rural power in the transfer-of-power years, but arose from different agrarian and political structures.

They should therefore be compared as parallel radicalisations, not collapsed into one model. Their common significance was to place control over land, labour and produce at the centre of freedom.

### Visual: why Congress could support and restrain agrarian action

```text
Congress needs mass mobilisation
            |
peasants supply numbers, networks and legitimacy
            |
Congress also contains landlords, rich peasants and business supporters
            |
radical rent or land struggle threatens coalition unity
            |
support in principle + negotiation + selective restraint
```

Congress and peasant organisations were neither naturally identical nor permanently opposed. Their relationship changed by region, leadership, demand and political phase.

### 1. Four possible relationships

| Relationship | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Congress-led | national leadership defines timing and discipline |
| Congress-linked | activists overlap, but local organisation shapes demands |
| autonomous convergence | movement joins anti-imperial politics without accepting full Congress control |
| open tension | class demand or repertoire conflicts with Congress coalition strategy |

Bardoli represented disciplined Congress-linked revenue struggle. Eka developed greater autonomy from Congress. AIKS worked through overlapping cadres while pressing beyond cautious agrarian reform. Telangana moved into a conflict that could not be contained within ordinary Congress discipline.

### 2. Why Congress needed peasants

India was overwhelmingly rural. No mass nationalism could succeed without peasant participation. Agrarian mobilisation expanded:

- village organisation;
- fundraising and volunteer networks;
- boycott capacity;
- moral legitimacy;
- the social meaning of swaraj.

Peasants did not enter politics as a blank mass. They brought rent, revenue, land and debt claims that could redirect the national programme.

### 3. Why Congress often hesitated

Congress sought a broad anti-imperial front. Direct attacks on landlords risked dividing supporters and provoking social conflict inside the coalition. Leaders also feared uncontrolled violence, local coercion or demands that outran national strategy.

The tension can be stated as:

> Congress wanted peasant mobilisation against colonial rule but could not always accept the full social consequences of mobilisation against Indian landed power.

### 4. Caste and gender limits

Many peasant organisations spoke in universal kisan language. Yet land ownership, tenancy, caste and gender shaped who led and who bore the heaviest burdens. Landless Dalits and agricultural labourers could remain underrepresented when middle peasants dominated organisations. Women performed movement labour but rarely received equal formal authority.

Recognition of these limits does not erase mobilisation. It makes the social base visible rather than treating "the peasant" as a single person.

### 5. Achievement without capture

The Left did not capture Congress. It made Congress answer questions it might otherwise have postponed:

- What would happen to zamindari?
- Would tenants receive security?
- Would labour obtain protection?
- Would freedom include economic rights?

Karachi, Faizpur and later planning discourse were institutional responses to this pressure.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Congress restraint merely betrayed peasants.

**Reply:** Conservative interests and fear of class conflict clearly limited reform. Yet national coalition management, non-violent discipline and the need to sustain anti-imperial unity were genuine strategic concerns. A balanced judgment distinguishes explainable caution from the unequal social costs of that caution.

### UPSC application and traps

- Avoid both extremes: "Congress created every peasant movement" and "Congress had no role."
- Identify the exact relationship in each case.
- Use caste, gender and landlessness to qualify class unity.
- Distinguish programme adoption from implementation.

### Visual: the triangular structure of power

```text
                 BRITISH PARAMOUNTCY
        external protection + ultimate supremacy
                 /                         \
                /                           \
        PRINCE / DARBAR  -----------  STATE SUBJECTS
       internal authority             taxes, services,
       and court politics             limited civil rights
                \                           /
                 \---- Praja Mandal -------/
                   responsible government
                   + civil liberties
```

Princely states were not fully sovereign countries and were not ordinary British Indian provinces. Their rulers possessed internal authority under an imperial system of paramountcy. Britain protected dynasties and retained superior power, while subjects often lacked representative institutions and enforceable civil liberties.

### 1. Why paramountcy mattered

British paramountcy created a layered order:

1. princes ruled internally but acknowledged British supremacy;
2. Residents or Political Department officials could influence succession, external relations and major political questions;
3. British protection reduced the ruler's dependence on popular consent;
4. constitutional reforms in British India did not automatically apply within the states.

The phrase "princely sovereignty" therefore needs qualification. Rulers claimed autonomy against Congress intervention, yet their position rested within an imperial guarantee.

### 2. Common grievances

Conditions varied greatly, but recurring demands included:

- responsible government;
- representative assemblies;
- civil liberties and press freedom;
- accountable use of state revenue;
- reduction of burdensome taxation;
- an end to forced labour or arbitrary exactions;
- freedom to organise.

Some states pursued reform, and no single description fits all rulers. The movement targeted autocratic structure, not the biological identity of princes.

### 3. Why Congress initially hesitated

Congress was created in British India and had to avoid allowing princes or the colonial government to depict it as an external invader. It also believed that durable movements should grow from local subjects rather than be imposed from outside.

This produced a distinction:

| Congress concern | Democratic cost |
|---|---|
| respect local initiative | subjects could remain isolated |
| avoid premature confrontation | princes gained time to repress |
| preserve anti-imperial focus | autocracy could be treated as secondary |
| prevent organisational overextension | all-India solidarity remained limited |

### 4. Nationalism's sovereignty dilemma

If a prince claimed freedom from Congress while depending on British paramountcy, whose sovereignty mattered: the ruler's or the people's? States' peoples' movements answered that political legitimacy belonged to subjects, not merely to treaty-recognised dynasties.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Congress intervention violated the separate constitutional identity of the states.

**Reply:** Imposed control from British India would have been problematic, but support for civil liberties and responsible government did not require denying local agency. The strongest strategy was local organisation linked to all-India solidarity.

### UPSC application and traps

- Paramountcy meant superior British authority, not full princely independence.
- States' peoples' movements primarily challenged autocracy and rights denial.
- Do not apply a British-province constitutional provision automatically to a state.
- Congress caution reflected both strategy and a democratic limitation.

### Revision notes

**Model answer (157 words):** Paramountcy placed princely states in a layered constitutional order. States' peoples therefore confronted two connected structures: local autocracy and an imperial system that sustained it. Their demands for civil liberties, accountable revenue, representative institutions and responsible government asserted popular sovereignty against a treaty-centred view of princely authority.

Paramountcy also complicated Congress policy. Direct intervention could be portrayed as violating a state's separate identity, yet non-interference left local movements isolated. The eventual solution was locally rooted Praja Mandal action coordinated through an all-India platform and supported increasingly by Congress.

Thus paramountcy did not remove the states from colonialism. It mediated colonial power through princes and made democratic mobilisation inside the states an essential extension of the national movement.

AISPC coordinated state-level organisations without replacing them. Its creation in Bombay in December 1927 gave democratic struggles a national forum.

### 1. What coordination achieved

AISPC helped:

- exchange information across isolated states;
- formulate common demands for responsible government and liberties;
- publicise repression;
- connect princely-state subjects to all-India nationalism;
- train cadres later relevant to accession and democratic integration.

It did not command every Praja Mandal uniformly. Local language, ruler, land system and religious composition continued to shape each struggle.

### 2. Regional diversity

Major movements arose in Jaipur, Kashmir, Rajkot, Patiala, Hyderabad, Mysore, Travancore and the Orissa states.

| State or region | Illustrative political problem |
|---|---|
| Rajkot | promised reform, court politics and Gandhi's intervention |
| Hyderabad | ban on a state-wide political organisation and the Nizam's autocracy |
| Travancore | responsible-government agitation and severe repression |
| Mysore | representative demands in a relatively administratively developed state |
| Kashmir | autocracy, social inequality and a distinctive mass political setting |
| Orissa states | local Praja Mandals confronting small-state autocracy and exactions |

The examples show why "states' movement" must remain plural.

### 3. The 1938-39 Congress shift

At Haripura in 1938, Congress still stated that movements should not be launched in its name and should rely on local strength. This was not indifference: it was a non-intervention rule combined with sympathy.

Mass upsurges soon demonstrated that subjects were capable of sustained action. Gandhi publicly explained a change in attitude in January 1939. At Tripuri, Congress moved toward a more active relationship, and Jawaharlal Nehru became AISPC president in 1939.

The accurate formulation is therefore:

> Haripura retained organisational caution; the experience of 1938-39 accelerated active Congress support.

### 4. Integration without teleology

AISPC helped create political links later useful in integrating princely India. It would be teleological, however, to say every 1927 activist was consciously executing the accession policy of 1947. Their immediate goals were rights and responsible government.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Congress support merely absorbed local movements and erased their agency.

**Reply:** National support could increase visibility and protection, but local organisations had already created their own leaders and demands. Absorption was a risk, not a complete description.

- Nehru became AISPC president in 1939.

Tripuri was more than a personality clash. It tested whether an elected Congress president could pursue an independent strategy without the confidence of Gandhi and the dominant Working Committee.

### 1. Haripura and planning

Bose's 1938 presidency highlighted national planning, industrial development and a stronger state role in economic transformation. The National Planning Committee was established under Nehru's chairmanship. Planning was a shared left-nationalist language, even where Nehru and Bose differed strategically.

### 2. Tripuri: election versus organisational authority

Bose won re-election in 1939 against Pattabhi Sitaramayya, who was supported by Gandhi. Victory demonstrated his appeal, but the presidency did not command the organisation alone. The Pant resolution effectively required a Working Committee acceptable to Gandhi. Ill health and political isolation compounded the crisis.

### 3. Why "the Left" did not unite behind Bose

The Congress Left was divided. Communists, Congress socialists, Royists and other radicals assessed unity, strategy and organisational consequences differently. Some preferred preserving Congress unity to building a Bose-led bloc. This is a critical correction to the assumption that one left label produced one vote.

### 4. Forward Bloc

After resigning from the presidency, Bose formed the Forward Bloc in May 1939, initially as a grouping within Congress intended to consolidate radical and anti-imperialist forces. Later disciplinary action further separated him from the Congress organisation.

The sequence is:

```text
re-election -> Working Committee impasse -> resignation
-> Forward Bloc within Congress -> disciplinary separation
```

### 5. Ideology, strategy and institution

| Dimension | Bose position or concern | Gandhian-majority concern |
|---|---|---|
| anti-imperial timing | greater urgency and readiness for confrontation | leadership control and disciplined timing |
| planning | rapid industrial and state-led transformation | accepted planning but differed over political command |
| presidency | electoral mandate should carry authority | Working Committee confidence remained indispensable |
| unity | consolidate radical forces | prevent organisational rupture |

The disagreement cannot be reduced either to ideology alone or to personal rivalry alone.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Bose won the election, so his resignation proves Congress rejected internal democracy.

**Reply:** His mandate was real, and the leadership's resistance exposed limits on presidential autonomy. Yet Congress operated through a Working Committee and an informal moral authority centred on Gandhi. The crisis concerned competing sources of legitimacy, not the absence of any organisational rule.

### UPSC application and traps

- Haripura presidency: 1938.
- Tripuri re-election and resignation: 1939.
- Forward Bloc: May 1939, initially within Congress.
- Do not say the entire Congress Left united behind Bose.
- Do not project Bose's later wartime path backward as the sole cause of Tripuri.

### Visual: four levels of historical impact

```text
IMMEDIATE
relief, wage, rent, tenure, liberties, organisation
        |
POLITICAL
new cadres, repertoires and constituencies
        |
IDEOLOGICAL
freedom linked to social and economic justice
        |
INSTITUTIONAL LEGACY
planning, labour protection, land reform and democratic integration
```

The movements did not overthrow colonialism by themselves or complete a social revolution. Their central achievement was to alter the social content, organisational reach and future expectations of nationalism.

### 1. Achievements

**Agenda transformation:** Swaraj increasingly required an answer on labour protection, land relations, debt, planning and civil liberties.

**Organisation from below:** Unions, kisan sabhas and Praja Mandals trained local leaders, built collective confidence and created durable political networks.

**Territorial expansion:** States' peoples' movements challenged the assumption that nationalism belonged only to British India.

**Social visibility:** Workers, tenants, sharecroppers, Adivasis, women participants and princely-state subjects became political agents rather than a passive national "mass."

**Anti-imperial linkage:** Movements exposed how landlords, employers, princes and colonial authority could form connected structures of power.

### 2. Limits

```text
repression and illegality
      +
ideological division
      +
uneven regional organisation
      +
cross-class Congress constraints
      +
caste and gender hierarchy
      +
worker and landless underrepresentation
      =
radical influence greater than uniform organisational control
```

Colonial prosecutions removed leaders. Left groups disagreed over Congress, international lines and war. Rural classes had conflicting interests. Trade unions were concentrated in selected industries. Women and subordinated castes participated without equal authority. Congress absorbed demands while often moderating them.

### 3. Historiographical lenses

| Lens | Contribution | Risk if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| nationalist | shows integration into anti-imperial struggle | can turn autonomous movements into Congress appendages |
| Marxist | reveals class relations and material demands | can understate culture, caste, gender and political contingency |
| subaltern | recovers local agency and non-elite initiative | can understate coordination and national institutions |
| gender history | makes hidden movement labour visible | needs integration with land, class and organisation |
| constitutional-democratic | explains Praja Mandal claims and popular sovereignty | can soften economic conflict into rights language alone |

A strong answer uses more than one lens and identifies what each reveals.

### 4. Integration into nationalism

Integration occurred through pressure, alliance and argument, not simple absorption:

```text
autonomous grievance
      |
class or local organisation
      |
contact with Congress and Left networks
      |
programme enters national debate
      |
compromise, conflict or partial adoption
```

This model explains why influence could be substantial even where a movement remained organisationally separate.

### 5. Overall verdict

The Left did not capture the national movement. Peasants and workers did not become one homogeneous revolutionary class. Praja Mandals did not immediately democratise every state. Yet these movements changed what independence had to mean. A merely constitutional transfer became increasingly inadequate without economic rights, social reform and popular sovereignty.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because many immediate demands remained unfulfilled, the movements failed.

**Reply:** Immediate outcomes matter, and repression or compromise often limited them. Historical achievement also includes organisation, agenda-setting, political training and the creation of claims that shaped postcolonial policy. Legacy must not be used to excuse unfinished justice.

### UPSC application and traps

- Grade achievements as immediate, organisational, ideological and institutional.
- Pair each achievement with a limit.
- Avoid inevitability: later policy was contested, not predetermined.
- Preserve the autonomy of regional and class movements.
- Do not treat "Left" as a single disciplined actor.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark practice - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the organisational strategies of the CPI, CSP and Forward Bloc in the interwar national movement.

**Model answer (159 words):** The CPI, CSP and Forward Bloc belonged to the broad Left but embodied different organisational strategies.

The CPI emerged through an expatriate Tashkent initiative in 1920 and an all-India Kanpur formation in 1925. It sought to build class organisations among workers and peasants and influence anti-imperial politics, while facing bans, conspiracy trials and shifts in international communist strategy.

The CSP was formed at Bombay in October 1934 explicitly within Congress. It treated national freedom as a necessary stage toward socialism and sought to give Congress a socialist direction while organising workers and peasants.

The Forward Bloc was formed by Subhas Bose in May 1939 after the Tripuri crisis. It initially attempted to consolidate radical anti-imperialist forces within Congress around a more militant strategy and stronger presidential initiative.

Thus the CPI developed as a communist party, CSP as an internal socialist caucus, and Forward Bloc as a Bose-led radical regrouping. Shared left vocabulary did not produce one institution or strategy.

**Component rubric (10 marks; 150-word ceiling):** CPI dual-origin and external strategy 3 + CSP internal strategy 3 + Forward Bloc post-Tripuri strategy 2 + comparative verdict 2 = 10/10.

## Final 15-mark practice - 250 words

**Question:** Explain how workers' and peasants' organisations expanded the social base of Indian nationalism while retaining autonomous interests.

**Model answer (220 words):** Workers' and peasants' organisations carried nationalism into workplaces and villages while refusing to reduce freedom to a purely constitutional transfer.

AITUC, founded in Bombay in 1920, linked wage, hours and workplace discipline to political freedom. Textile and railway action gave labour strategic leverage, and left organisers connected imperialism with capitalist power. Yet Congress was a cross-class body, not a labour party, and workers retained interests that could conflict with Indian employers and nationalist timing.

Peasant organisation developed through different land systems. Bihar kisan politics addressed zamindari, eviction and bakasht restoration; Andhra built trained cadres through ryot associations and N.G. Ranga's institute; Punjab demanded tax relief and a debt moratorium. AIKS, formed in 1936, nationalised demands for rent and revenue reduction, occupancy rights, union recognition and zamindari abolition. Tebhaga and Telangana later challenged sharecropping, forced labour and landlord-state power.

These organisations expanded nationalist networks, supplied cadres and pressured Congress toward Karachi, Faizpur and planning commitments. They nevertheless retained autonomous demands, leadership and timing. Congress often welcomed mobilisation against colonial rule while restraining class conflict against landlords or Indian capitalists.

Their relationship was therefore one of partial convergence. Nationalism gave class struggles an all-India horizon; class organisations made nationalism answerable to material injustice. The social base widened precisely because workers and peasants entered not as passive followers but as organised constituencies.

**Component rubric (15 marks; 250-word ceiling):** labour organisation and autonomy 4 + peasant regional evidence 4 + Congress programme effects 3 + convergence-conflict mechanism 2 + qualified social-base verdict 2 = 15/15.

## Final 20-mark practice - 250 words

**Question:** "Popular sovereignty, not merely anti-colonial sovereignty, was the common horizon of peasant, labour and states' peoples' movements." Critically examine.

**Model answer (233 words):** Anti-colonial sovereignty sought the end of British rule; popular sovereignty asked who would exercise power and in whose interests. Labour, peasant and states' peoples' movements deepened nationalism by raising the second question.

Workers organised through AITUC and strikes to assert economic voice inside factories. Peasants demanded rent and revenue reduction, debt relief, occupancy rights, land restoration and crop control. Tebhaga's two-thirds claim and Telangana's challenge to landlord-vetti relations addressed control over production, not only foreign rule. Praja Mandals and AISPC demanded civil liberties and responsible government from rulers protected by British paramountcy, locating legitimacy in subjects rather than dynasties.

The common horizon was therefore democratic participation and material accountability. These movements also widened Congress programmes and later expectations of labour protection, planning, land reform and integration.

The formulation requires qualification. Their social bases were unequal: richer peasants, tenants and landless labourers did not share identical interests; women and subordinated castes lacked equal authority; unions were regionally concentrated. Some movements primarily sought relief rather than a developed theory of sovereignty. Congress's cross-class coalition absorbed demands selectively, while repression and left divisions limited coordination.

Popular sovereignty was thus not a uniform doctrine consciously held by every participant. It is a defensible analytical thread connecting claims over workplace, land, revenue and princely government. These movements made independence more than the replacement of an imperial executive, even though the social redistribution implied by popular rule remained incomplete.

**Component rubric (20 marks; 250-word ceiling):** popular-sovereignty definition 3 + labour evidence 3 + peasant evidence 4 + states-peoples evidence 3 + social/organisational limits 4 + critical synthesis 3 = 20/20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core thesis

- Indian nationalism widened when freedom was forced to answer social and economic questions.
- The Left was ideologically diverse: communist, Congress socialist and left-nationalist currents overlapped without becoming one organisation.
- Workers, peasants and princely-state subjects acted through their own demands and institutions.
- Congress absorbed part of this pressure while limiting class conflict to preserve a broad anti-imperial coalition.

## B. Communist chronology and repression

- 17 October 1920, Tashkent: expatriate communist initiative associated with M.N. Roy.
- 26 December 1925, Kanpur: all-India organisational foundation on Indian soil recognised by CPI.
- Use both dates and state their different meanings.
- Regional communist groups existed independently before all-India coordination.
- Peshawar and Kanpur conspiracy prosecutions preceded Meerut.
- On 20 March 1929, thirty-one leading communists, trade unionists and other left-wing leaders were arrested.
- Thirty-two people were ultimately accused and tried at Meerut; the two totals refer to different procedural stages.
- The Meerut trial continued into 1933.
- Repression removed organisers but defence speeches publicised communist ideas.
- Nationalist participation in the defence complicated the state's attempt to isolate communists.

## C. Congress socialism and left nationalism

- CSP formed at Bombay in October 1934.
- Major leaders included Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, Minoo Masani, Ram Manohar Lohia, Achyut Patwardhan and Yusuf Meherally.
- CSP worked inside Congress because national freedom was considered a necessary stage toward socialism.
- It sought worker-peasant class organisations and a socialist direction for Congress.
- Nehru popularised socialism, planning and international anti-imperialism but never formally joined CSP.
- Bose shared planning and radical anti-imperialism but followed a distinct left-nationalist route.

## D. Labour

- AITUC founded at Bombay on 31 October 1920.
- Lala Lajpat Rai was first president; Dewan Chaman Lal first general secretary.
- Bombay textile and railway workers were major centres of industrial action.
- Worker concentration created leverage beyond demographic size.
- Trade Unions Act, 1926: registration and limited legal protection.
- Trade Disputes Act, 1929: state regulation and restrictions on industrial conflict.
- Royal Commission on Labour, 1929: inquiry and controlled incorporation.
- Congress cooperated with labour but was not a labour party.
- Labour's limits included uneven unionisation, insecurity, ideological division and concentration in selected industries.

## E. Depression and agrarian mechanism

- Agricultural prices fell sharply after 1929.
- Rent, revenue and debt claims remained comparatively rigid.
- Peasant cash income fell while obligations persisted.
- Manufactured-good prices did not fall equally.
- Result: arrears, borrowing, eviction and land transfer.
- Revenue is paid to the state; rent is paid through landlord relations.
- Major demands: remission, rent reduction, debt moratorium, end to illegal levies, tenure security, union recognition and zamindari abolition.
- Distress required organisation and political opportunity to become a movement.

## F. Regional peasant politics

- Bihar: Sahajanand Saraswati, provincial kisan organisation, zamindari abolition, illegal levies, eviction and bakasht restoration.
- Bakasht: holdings lost by occupancy tenants to zamindars, often during Depression arrears.
- UP: tenancy and landlord conflict; strong Congress-socialist networks.
- Andhra: Andhra Provincial Ryots Association, Andhra Zamin Ryots Association and N.G. Ranga's Nidubrolu training initiative from 1933.
- Punjab: Punjab Kisan Committee, 1937; tax reduction, debt moratorium and challenge to landed Unionist dominance.
- AIKS formed at Lucknow in April 1936; Sahajanand Saraswati first president.
- Faizpur, December 1936: agrarian questions gained national prominence.

## G. Late agrarian radicalism

- Bardoli, 1928: thirteen camps, 100 political workers, about 1,500 volunteers, the daily *Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika* and an intelligence wing made disciplined revenue refusal operational.
- Bardoli's relatively prosperous proprietor base and single-taluqa setting limit generalisation; Awadh/Eka involved sharper landlord-tenancy antagonism.
- Mappila, 1921: agrarian insecurity and anti-colonial/Khilafat mobilisation were followed by grave communal violence; neither dimension may be erased.
- Warli: Adivasi agrarian-labour struggle involving debt and forced-labour relations.
- Tebhaga, 1946: bargadars demanded two-thirds of produce and storage in their own khamars.
- Telangana, from 1946: challenged landlord oppression, illegal exactions and vetti within Hyderabad; acquired an armed dimension and continued after independence.
- Women acted as organisers, couriers and defenders of crop and village action.
- Landless labourers and subordinated castes could remain underrepresented in peasant leadership.
- Ulgulan: Birsa Munda's revolt of 1899-1900; Munda common landholding was threatened by jagirdars, thikadars and moneylenders treated as *dikus*.
- Birsa linked land recovery and religious mobilisation to an anti-Raj horizon of Munda/Birsa rule.
- Compare Kol 1820-37, Santhal 1855-56, Rampa 1879-80 and Munda 1899-1900 through their distinct land, intermediary and forest pressures.

## H. Congress and class

- Congress needed peasants for mass reach and legitimacy.
- Peasants entered with autonomous rent, revenue, debt and land demands.
- Congress contained landlords, rich peasants, business groups and workers.
- It could support anti-colonial mobilisation while restraining attacks on Indian property holders.
- Relationships ranged from Congress-led to linked, autonomous-convergent and openly tense.
- Karachi and Faizpur show programme change; they do not prove completed redistribution.

## I. Princely states and popular sovereignty

- Paramountcy meant superior British power combined with princely internal authority.
- British protection reduced rulers' dependence on popular consent.
- British Indian reforms did not automatically extend to princely states.
- Praja Mandals demanded civil liberties, accountable administration and responsible government.
- AISPC founded at Bombay in December 1927.
- It coordinated rather than erased local organisations.
- Major movements occurred in Rajkot, Hyderabad, Travancore, Mysore, Kashmir, Jaipur, Patiala and Orissa states.
- Haripura 1938 retained a local-strength and non-intervention principle.
- The 1938-39 upsurge shifted Congress toward active support.
- Nehru became AISPC president in 1939.
- These movements later aided democratic integration, but their immediate goal was popular rights.

## J. Bose and Forward Bloc

- Bose was Congress president at Haripura in 1938 and re-elected at Tripuri in 1939.
- Planning, anti-imperial timing and presidential authority shaped the conflict.
- Election victory did not produce a cooperative Working Committee.
- The Congress Left was divided; shared radicalism did not ensure support for Bose.
- Bose resigned and formed Forward Bloc in May 1939, initially within Congress.
- Tripuri must be explained through ideology, strategy and organisation.

## K. Achievements and limits

- Achievements: organisation, cadre formation, social visibility, agenda transformation, democratic territorial expansion and future policy expectations.
- Limits: repression, bans, ideological division, uneven regional reach, cross-class restraint, caste and gender hierarchy, and incomplete worker-landless representation.
- The movements did not uniformly capture Congress or complete social revolution.
- They made political independence without economic justice and popular sovereignty increasingly inadequate.
