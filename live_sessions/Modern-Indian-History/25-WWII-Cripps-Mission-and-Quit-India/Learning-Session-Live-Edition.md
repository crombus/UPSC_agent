# Second World War, Cripps Mission and Quit India — Live Learning Session

> **Learner-first lock:** This session follows the problem as contemporaries experienced it: a war entered without consent, constitutional offers that withheld present power, a carefully limited protest, an international military crisis, a failed negotiation, and finally a movement whose leadership was removed before it could direct events. It distinguishes Congress's declared non-violent strategy from the forms actually taken by the decentralised upsurge.

## Lesson 1 — War without consent: the constitutional rupture of 1939

Progress: 1/11 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Outbreak of war, resignations and rival political responses

*The quarrel was not simply whether fascism should be opposed. It was whether an unfree India could be treated as a voluntary participant in Britain's war.*

### 1. Begin with the democratic contradiction

Congress had opposed fascist aggression before the war. It had condemned Japanese aggression in China, criticised European appeasement and expressed sympathy for peoples threatened by fascism. Therefore its position cannot be reduced to “Britain's enemy is India's friend.”

The contradiction was sharper:

1. Britain claimed to fight for democracy and freedom.
2. India was committed to that war by an unelected colonial executive.
3. Indian elected representatives were denied control over defence and national policy.
4. Congress therefore connected anti-fascist cooperation with Indian freedom and an effective national government.

✅ **Fact:** The Government of India declared India at war without consulting Congress, the central legislature or the elected provincial ministries.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The event converted the limitations of the 1935 constitutional arrangement from a legal criticism into an immediate political fact. Provincial ministers could govern departments, but they could not decide whether the country entered a world war.

### 2. Why did the ministries resign?

Congress did not resign because it had lost provincial majorities. It resigned after Britain failed to link Indian cooperation to a credible declaration of freedom and responsible government.

| Congress demand | Imperial response | Political consequence |
|---|---|---|
| State the war aims as they applied to India | No commitment to immediate freedom | British democratic rhetoric looked selective |
| Establish genuinely responsible authority | Viceroy retained decisive power | Provincial office appeared constitutionally subordinate |
| Treat Indians as political partners | Communal and special-interest differences were emphasised | Congress read this as continuation of divide-and-rule |

The resignations involved a real cost. Congress surrendered offices through which it had released prisoners, widened civil liberties and attempted reforms. Yet remaining in office without influence over the central war decision could also have made Congress appear responsible for a war policy it did not control.

### 3. The aftermath: opportunity, not a single inevitable result

The Muslim League treated the resignations as a political opportunity and observed **22 December 1939 as the Day of Deliverance** from Congress ministries. This deepened the Congress–League conflict, but it needs qualification.

- The League's campaign drew on earlier complaints about Congress rule and its claim that Congress did not represent Muslims.
- Congress regarded the celebration as a divisive use of an anti-imperial protest.
- The Lahore Resolution followed in March 1940, but the resignation did not by itself cause the Pakistan demand.

> **Causation caution:** Write “the resignations altered political opportunity and intensified Congress–League competition,” not “the resignations caused Partition.”

### 4. Congress was internally divided over cooperation, timing and organisation

The wartime debate was not a choice between patriotism and indifference. Gandhi's first response was to favour virtually unconditional Allied cooperation on moral grounds: fascist aggression had to be resisted even though India remained unfree. His later turn towards struggle grew from British refusal to recognise Indian freedom, not from sympathy for the Axis.

The wider Congress leadership gave three connected reasons for delaying immediate mass struggle:

1. **The anti-fascist cause:** Britain should not be embarrassed merely because it was in danger when resistance to fascism was just.
2. **The unity problem:** without Hindu-Muslim unity, confrontation could deepen communal conflict rather than create national power.
3. **The organisation problem:** Congress and the public were not adequately prepared; indiscipline, opportunism and organisational weakness had to be corrected.

| Position | Strategic diagnosis | Organisational consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Gandhi | Initially favoured virtually unconditional Allied cooperation; later insisted that struggle remain morally disciplined | Delay mass action while preparing Congress and preserving a peaceful route |
| Nehru | Fascism was a global danger, but imperial rule made Indian cooperation politically untenable | Refuse participation without freedom while resisting opportunistic use of Britain's crisis |
| Subhas Chandra Bose | Britain's war crisis created a rare opening for immediate anti-imperial struggle | If the official leadership refused, organise a parallel Congress and launch independently |
| Congress Socialists | Delay risked demobilisation, but splitting Congress would weaken the broad anti-imperial platform | Remain inside Congress and press its leadership towards struggle |
| Communist Party in the war's first phase | Treated the conflict as an imperialist war while the Soviet Union remained outside it | Remain within the Congress arena and pressure for struggle rather than form Bose's parallel Congress |

The distinction was institutional as well as ideological. Bose proposed a rival organisational centre if Congress would not act. The Congress Socialists and CPI preferred to stay within Congress and change its line through persuasion and pressure. This comparison is bounded to the 1939–41 strategic debate; Bose's overseas wartime course belongs to Topic 26.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** By resigning, Congress abandoned the provinces and gave its rivals space to organise.

**Reply:** That strategic cost was real. But continued office would have implied that provincial administration compensated for India's exclusion from sovereign decisions. Congress chose to demonstrate that a ministry under the 1935 framework was not a national government.

**Balanced assessment:** The resignations were constitutionally principled and politically costly. Both parts belong in a strong answer.

- **Trap:** Do not portray Congress as supporting fascism or opposing defence of India.
- **Boundary:** Detailed ministry achievements and safeguards are revised in Topic 24, not retaught here.

#### Local Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the Congress ministries resign in 1939? Explain the political cost of the decision.

**Model answer:**
The Congress ministries resigned in October–November 1939 because the Viceroy had committed India to the Second World War without consulting elected Indian representatives. Congress opposed fascism, but argued that an enslaved country could not be described as a voluntary partner in a war for democracy. British refusal to promise immediate responsible government exposed the narrow limits of provincial autonomy under the 1935 framework.

Resignation therefore asserted that administrative office could not substitute for national sovereignty. It also showed that Congress participation in provincial government was conditional rather than acceptance of colonial supremacy.

The decision nevertheless carried costs. Provincial reform ceased, Congress vacated legal governmental space, and the Muslim League used the opening to observe the Day of Deliverance and intensify its claim that Congress rule endangered Muslim interests. Yet the resignations did not cause Partition; they altered the balance of political opportunity within a longer communal and constitutional conflict.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** unilateral war decision and constitutional trigger (3); provincial responsibility versus national sovereignty (3); political costs, including lost office and League opportunity (2); balanced anti-fascist and non-mechanical Partition qualification (2). Total: 10.

## Lesson 2 — August Offer and Individual Satyagraha: protest at controlled temperature

### Visual: offer, rejection and calibrated protest

```text
Congress resigns and demands present political power
                         │
                         ▼
                 August Offer, 1940
      future advance + wider consultation + safeguards
                         │
              but no national government now
                         │

                   Congress rejects
                         │

                         │

                         │

               Individual Satyagraha
       limited speech-based protest, 1940–41
```

*The limited form was deliberate: Congress wanted to deny that India freely supported the war without immediately obstructing the Allied war effort through a mass revolt.*

### 1. What did the August Offer attempt?

Viceroy Linlithgow's August 1940 statement tried to secure cooperation without transferring effective power. Its important elements included:

- expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council;
- creation of a War Advisory Council;
- recognition that Indians would frame a new constitutional arrangement after the war;
- restatement of dominion status as the goal;
- an assurance that no future constitution would be imposed on substantial elements of national life that withheld consent.

The last point strengthened the position of organised minorities and interests against any simple transfer based on Congress's majority claim.

| Offered | Withheld |
|---|---|
| More Indian consultation | A responsible national government |
| A post-war constitution-making process | Sovereign constitution-making during the crisis |
| Eventual dominion status | Immediate independence |
| Protection against coercing minorities | A method for reconciling competing representative claims |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The offer widened consultation while preserving the Viceroy's control. It postponed the central question—who governed India during the war.

### 2. Why did Congress reject it?

Congress did not reject constitution-making or minority protection as such. It rejected:

1. the absence of immediate responsible authority;
2. the postponement of substantive transfer until after the war;
3. a formulation that allowed organised opposition to block an all-India settlement without creating a democratic method for resolution.

The League also did not receive an explicit sovereign Pakistan from the offer. The British had acknowledged that Congress could not alone settle India's future, but had not accepted the League's complete demand.

### 3. Why Individual Satyagraha rather than a mass movement?

Gandhi chose a limited campaign beginning in October 1940. Its central act was the public assertion of the right to oppose participation in the war without consent.

The method served several purposes:

```text
Say publicly that India is not a voluntary war partner
                         +
avoid paralysing anti-fascist defence through mass upheaval
                         +
test and rebuild Congress discipline
                         +
give Britain another peaceful opportunity
                         =
Individual Satyagraha
```

✅ **Fact:** Vinoba Bhave was selected as the first individual satyagrahi and Jawaharlal Nehru as the second.

✅ **Fact:** By 15 May 1941, the cited book account records more than 25,000 convictions in the campaign.

### 4. The complete operating method

The campaign was individual, sequential and controlled, but “court arrest” is too compressed to describe its procedure.

```text
Congress selects an individual satyagrahi
                    │
advance notice to the district magistrate:
exact time and place of the anti-war speech
                    │
                    ▼
deliver the notified speech
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          │                   │
     arrested            not arrested
          │                   │
accept custody and       repeat the speech,
legal penalty            enter nearby villages
                              │
                              ▼
                    begin a Delhi-directed
                    village trek: “Delhi Chalo”
```

The intended procedure used openness rather than surprise. Prior notice made the issue the right to political speech; the expected arrest exposed coercion. If authorities withheld arrest, repetition and the village trek widened political education without converting the campaign into a general seizure of power.

**Intended political and organisational outcomes:**

- deny the claim that India voluntarily supported the war;
- give Britain another peaceful opportunity to recognise Indian freedom;
- maintain political activity under repression;
- educate and mobilise people through speech and village contact;
- test, reshape and discipline Congress organisation;
- identify opportunists and prepare reliable cadres.

**What it could not achieve:**

- force immediate transfer of power;
- answer the growing Japanese military threat;
- satisfy militants demanding a national mass struggle;
- resolve Congress–League disagreement.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Individual Satyagraha was too mild for an imperial government exploiting India's resources during a world war.

**Reply:** The campaign's modest scale was its strategic content. Congress was attempting to oppose imperial compulsion without appearing to facilitate fascist victory. It also bought time for organisational preparation.

**Residual problem:** A calibrated protest works only while delay appears safer than escalation. The military collapse in Southeast Asia would soon destroy that assumption.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** August Offer—Linlithgow, 1940; Individual Satyagraha—Vinoba first, Nehru second.
- **Mains:** Explain limitation as design, not as accidental weakness.
- **Trap:** The Individual Satyagraha did not begin as a mass no-tax or sabotage campaign.
- **Answer line:** “It was an anti-imperial protest constrained by anti-fascist responsibility.”

1. The August Offer belonged to 1940, before Individual Satyagraha and Cripps.
2. It promised constitutional advance after the war, not immediate responsible government.
3. It proposed wider Indian participation in executive and advisory bodies.
4. Minority non-consent became a major constitutional constraint.
5. Congress rejected postponement and lack of present power.
6. The League did not receive an explicit Pakistan settlement.
7. Individual Satyagraha began in October 1940.
8. Vinoba Bhave was first; Jawaharlal Nehru was second.
9. Each satyagrahi notified the district magistrate in advance of the speech's time and place.
10. Arrest normally followed the speech; otherwise it was repeated and a village trek towards Delhi began.
11. “Delhi Chalo” was the fallback continuation of notified protest, not the opening of a mass insurrection.

#### Local Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Individual Satyagraha was limited in form but substantial in political purpose. Explain.

**Model answer:**
Individual Satyagraha began in October 1940 after Congress rejected the August Offer. Gandhi deliberately avoided immediate mass action. Each selected volunteer gave the district magistrate advance notice of the anti-war speech's time and place, delivered it and accepted arrest. If no arrest followed, the satyagrahi repeated the speech, entered villages and began the Delhi-directed “Delhi Chalo” trek. Vinoba Bhave was first; Jawaharlal Nehru was second.

The campaign was not passive cooperation: it denied India's voluntary consent, sustained mobilisation and rejected postponed responsible government. Its controlled form also avoided an uncontrolled disruption of anti-fascist defence.

It gave Britain another peaceful opportunity while educating people, testing discipline and filtering opportunists from Congress. Yet it could neither secure immediate power nor answer the Japanese advance. It was therefore calibrated resistance and organisational preparation, not either acquiescence or a full mass revolt.

```text
                              │

      war's anti-fascist character gains wider acceptance
                              │
Japan advances through Southeast Asia
      Singapore falls ─ Burma collapses ─ refugees reach India
                              │

                              │

“British rule protects India” loses credibility
                              │
                              ▼

```

*By early 1942, waiting no longer looked neutral. It appeared to leave India's defence in the hands of a power visibly retreating across Asia.*

### 1. The war's political character changed

Germany's attack on the Soviet Union in June 1941 brought the USSR into the Allied camp. For Communists internationally, the conflict was now understood as a people's war against fascism. Congress leaders also felt strong solidarity with China and the Soviet Union, though they did not accept that this justified continued British rule.

The key distinction was:

```text
Support the defeat of fascism ≠ accept imperial control of India
```

At the end of December 1941, the Congress Working Committee adopted—over Gandhi's and Nehru's objections—an offer of full cooperation in India's defence if Britain promised full independence after the war and transferred the substance of power immediately. The vote shows that the CWC could move beyond both Gandhi's caution and Nehru's objection to the particular formula even while retaining their shared insistence that colonial command was untenable.

### 2. Why the Japanese advance mattered

The fall of Singapore and the British retreat from Burma destroyed the aura of imperial military invincibility. Refugees and reports from Southeast Asia conveyed that Europeans had often been evacuated with priority while Asian subjects were left exposed.

This produced three linked perceptions:

1. Britain might not be able to defend India.
2. Britain might abandon Indians during retreat.
3. A free Indian government could mobilise defence with greater legitimacy.

Gandhi rejected Japanese imperialism and the slogan “Asia for Asiatics” when used to legitimise Japanese expansion. The choice was therefore not Britain or Japan. Congress sought Indian freedom so that resistance to aggression would rest on Indian consent.

### 3. Material distress sharpened the crisis

War was experienced through daily disruptions:

- rising prices and shortages;
- military requisitioning;
- commandeering or destruction of boats in coastal Bengal and Orissa to deny their use to an invader;
- fear, hoarding and withdrawal of savings;
- refugee stories and visible military reverses.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Constitutional exclusion became inseparable from administrative insecurity. The Raj claimed exceptional wartime powers while appearing unable to protect livelihoods or territory.

### 4. International pressure on Churchill

The United States, China and British Labour opinion pressed for a settlement that would secure active Indian cooperation. President Franklin Roosevelt and Chiang Kai-shek were among those concerned with the political weakness created by continued imperial deadlock.

This pressure helped produce the Cripps Mission. It did not mean Britain had decided upon immediate withdrawal. The mission was intended to gain wartime cooperation while preserving essential wartime command.

### 5. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Launching pressure when Japan threatened India risked weakening defence.

**Reply:** Congress argued that defence without legitimacy was already weak. A responsible Indian government could mobilise people more effectively than a colonial government ruling through emergency powers.

**Qualification:** This did not eliminate genuine disagreement. Nehru remained reluctant to launch a movement until late because he took the fascist and Japanese dangers seriously.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Know the sequence—Soviet entry into war, Japanese advance, Cripps Mission, Quit India.
- **Mains:** Treat international reverses as both military events and changes in political credibility.
- **Trap:** Do not present Gandhi or Congress as supporters of Japanese conquest.
- **Causal phrase:** “The Asian retreat turned a constitutional demand into an urgent question of legitimate defence.”

2. Congress sympathy for China and the USSR remained strong.
3. Congress offered defence cooperation if real power was transferred.
4. Japanese victories destroyed British prestige in Southeast Asia.

6. Refugee experience exposed racial inequality in British evacuation.

9. Gandhi rejected both British imperialism and Japanese expansion.
10. Allied diplomatic pressure helped produce the Cripps Mission.
11. Nehru's hesitation reflected anti-fascist concern, not acceptance of colonial rule.

## Lesson 4 — Cripps Mission: read the proposal in four separate boxes

Progress: 4/11 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Dominion status, constitution-making, non-accession and defence

📚 Book context: Queried — canonical files; *India's Struggle for Independence*, PDF pages 483–484; UK National Archives Cripps educational record

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the four-box test

| Question | Cripps proposal | Exact distinction |
|---|---|---|
| What status? | A new Indian Union with dominion status after the war | Post-war status, not immediate independence |
| Who frames the constitution? | A constitution-making body after the war | Provincial members elected by provincial legislatures; princely-state members nominated by rulers |
| Must every province join? | No | A non-accepting province could remain outside and make a separate arrangement |
| Who controls wartime defence? | British authority | Present defence did not pass to a fully responsible Indian government |

*Most errors arise from merging these four questions. Keep status, composition, territorial choice and present executive power separate.*

### 1. Why was Stafford Cripps sent?

Cripps was a senior British politician with earlier sympathy for Indian aspirations. His mission in March 1942 was meant to secure Indian cooperation during a severe military crisis.

His personal reputation raised expectations, but he operated within a draft declaration and a British cabinet unwilling to transfer the decisive wartime powers Congress demanded.

### 2. Post-war dominion status

The offer envisaged a new Indian Union after the war with dominion status and the ability to shape its constitutional relationship with the Commonwealth.

**Dominion status** meant substantial self-government within the Commonwealth framework. Congress's objection in 1942 was not that dominion status had no content. It was that:

- it was promised after the war;
- Congress had already moved to complete independence as its goal;
- power during the decisive wartime emergency remained British.

> **Exam distinction:** “Post-war dominion status” answers the future-status question. It does not answer who governed India in 1942.

### 3. The constitution-making body

The constitution-making arrangement had two channels:

```text
British Indian provinces:
members elected by provincial legislatures

Princely states:
representatives nominated by their rulers

Both streams → constitution-making body after the war
```

This was not a Constituent Assembly elected directly by universal adult franchise. Nor were both groups nominated in the same manner.

The princely-state method created a democratic objection: rulers, not the states' peoples, selected their representatives.

### 4. Provincial non-accession

A province unwilling to accept the new constitution could remain outside the proposed Union and retain its existing constitutional position, with the possibility of a separate agreement with Britain regarding its future status.

This clause:

- did not itself draw Pakistan's borders;
- did not immediately create Pakistan;
- did make an all-India constitution non-binding on every province;
- therefore introduced a constitutional route by which the Union could fragment.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** It accommodated the logic of separation without satisfying the League's demand for a recognised sovereign Pakistan.

### 5. Princely states were a different problem from provinces

Do not merge provincial non-accession with princely-state participation.

| British Indian province | Princely state |
|---|---|
| Part of British India | Ruled under a treaty relationship and paramountcy |
| Legislature elected constitution-body members | Ruler nominated representatives |
| Could decline the new constitution | Participation depended on rulers and accession arrangements |

Both weakened a simple democratic union, but through different mechanisms.

### 6. Defence during the war

The British would retain control of India's defence during the war. Congress sought immediate effective power and a real share in defence responsibility. The disagreement was not solved by offering Indians advisory or limited executive roles while command remained under the existing authority.

This was the practical heart of failure:

```text
Britain: decide the future after victory
Congress: responsibility must begin during the danger
```

### 7. Strongest defence of the proposal—and the answer

**Defence:** In wartime, Britain could not risk reorganising command immediately; the offer nevertheless conceded Indian constitution-making and a future dominion.

**Answer:** The proposal expected Indian leaders to mobilise a population for war while denying them effective authority over that mobilisation. Its future concession was therefore undermined by its present structure.

**Balanced verdict:** Cripps was more explicit than earlier offers about constitution-making, but its timing, representation rules, non-accession clause and defence reservation prevented it from becoming a transfer-of-power settlement.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Provincial members were elected by provincial legislatures; princely representatives were nominated by rulers.
- **Prelims:** A province could decline the new constitution and negotiate separately.
- **Mains:** Analyse future promise and present power in separate paragraphs.
- **Trap:** Cripps did not offer immediate full independence or immediate responsible control of defence.

*“Both rejected” is not an explanation. UPSC expects the reasons to be separated.*

### 1. Congress objections

Congress objections operated at four levels:

1. **Status:** dominion status rather than the already-declared objective of full independence.
2. **Timing:** substantive transfer after the war rather than immediately.
3. **Defence:** British retention of sole effective control during the emergency.
4. **Unity and democracy:** provincial non-accession and ruler-nominated princely representatives.

Gandhi's remembered description of the proposal as a “post-dated cheque” expressed the timing problem: a future promise offered by a government whose wartime position looked increasingly uncertain.

### 2. The Muslim League's objection

The provincial opt-out provision moved towards the logic of separation, but it did not:

- recognise the League as the sole representative of Muslims;
- concede a sovereign Pakistan in advance;
- define the territorial units or boundaries of Pakistan;
- guarantee that Muslim-majority zones would act as the League required.

The League therefore rejected an offer that made separation possible without accepting its full political claim.

### 3. Princes and democratic representation

Princely rulers were offered representation by nomination. This protected their status but denied the subjects of the states an elected voice in constitution-making.

There was also uncertainty about whether and on what terms rulers would accede to a new Union. The proposals combined future constitution-making with a structure still dependent on undemocratic princely authority.

### 4. British constraints on Cripps

Cripps could not negotiate freely beyond the draft declaration. Churchill, Secretary of State Leo Amery, Viceroy Linlithgow and Commander-in-Chief Wavell opposed concessions that would transfer effective wartime command.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Failure cannot be reduced to either Indian intransigence or one negotiator's personality. The authorised bargaining space was too narrow to solve the central issue.

### 5. Three explanations tested

| Explanation | What it captures | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| British insincerity | Power and defence were not genuinely transferable | The offer did concede future constitution-making and provincial choice |
| Congress maximalism | Congress would not accept dominion status or deferred power | Present responsibility during invasion was a practical, not decorative, demand |
| Communal deadlock | Congress and League wanted incompatible national futures | Britain itself institutionalised separate routes rather than resolving representation democratically |

**Qualified verdict:** All three mattered, but the immediate failure arose because Britain sought cooperation without transferring effective authority, while Indian parties disagreed about the territorial and representative basis of the future state.

### 6. Consequence of failure

The mission's return in mid-April produced frustration among leaders who had held back mass struggle partly in hope of a settlement. The failure made continued silence look like acceptance of Britain's unilateral right to determine India's fate.

It did not automatically produce the Quit India resolution. Gandhi, Nehru and Congress still debated timing, invasion, non-violence and the shape of a movement. But the space for another limited constitutional response sharply narrowed.

- **Mains:** Use actor-wise objections before offering a weighted verdict.
- **Prelims:** Congress and League both rejected Cripps, but for different reasons.
- **Trap:** The provincial option neither equalled an accepted Pakistan nor preserved compulsory unity.
- **Answer discipline:** Do not describe the mission as offering “nothing”; identify its genuine concessions and decisive limitations.

### Visual: decision was a process, not a single slogan

```text

          │

          │

          │

          │

```

*The resolution authorised a struggle, but Gandhi still expected to approach the Viceroy. The government arrested the leadership before that controlled sequence could unfold.*

### 1. Why did Gandhi now favour withdrawal?

Several arguments converged:

- Cripps showed that Britain would not transfer effective power during the war.
- Japanese advance made colonial defence look unreliable.
- Continued passivity risked appearing to consent to imperial rule.
- Popular distress and expectation were rising.
- British withdrawal, Gandhi argued, would permit Indians to organise their own non-violent resistance to aggression.

Gandhi and Nehru were not identical in reasoning. Nehru feared harm to the anti-fascist struggle and remained opposed to launching a movement until late. Gandhi increasingly believed that British presence itself invited Japanese attack and prevented legitimate Indian mobilisation.

### 2. Wardha and Bombay must be distinguished

✅ **Fact:** The Congress Working Committee at Wardha accepted the idea of a struggle on **14 July 1942**.

✅ **Fact:** The All India Congress Committee ratified the Quit India resolution at **Gowalia Tank, Bombay, on 8 August 1942**.

Wardha was the Working Committee decision. Bombay was the broader AICC authorisation. Do not collapse the two dates or bodies.

### 3. What did “Do or Die” mean?

The phrase expressed refusal to accept continued slavery, not a command to kill. Gandhi's formulation was: India would either be freed or participants would die in the attempt rather than live to see slavery perpetuated.

The intended framework remained non-violent mass struggle. Gandhi's speech also indicated that the actual struggle did not begin at that instant; he expected to approach the Viceroy.

> **UPSC trap:** “Do or Die” signifies total moral commitment. It is not evidence that Congress officially adopted armed insurrection.

### 4. Instructions reveal the imagined social reach

Gandhi's guidance differentiated social groups:

- government servants were not immediately asked to resign, but were asked to declare allegiance to the national cause;
- soldiers were not told to leave their posts, but not to fire upon their own people;
- princes were asked to accept the sovereignty of their peoples;
- people in princely states were asked to identify with the Indian nation;
- committed students could leave studies;
- prepared peasants might refuse land revenue;
- the relationship between tenants and zamindars was treated differently depending on whether landlords sided with the people or government.

These instructions were not fully issued because leaders were arrested. They matter as evidence of intended direction, not as proof that every instruction became an operating programme.

### 5. The resolution anticipated disrupted command

The AICC recognised that instructions might not reach participants and committees might become unable to function. Individuals were told that within the broad instructions each participant might have to become his or her own guide.

This is crucial for interpreting what followed:

```text
decentralisation was anticipated
but exact local forms were not centrally designed
```

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** A call issued without a detailed operational programme irresponsibly invited disorder.

**Reply:** Congress expected further direction and Gandhi intended another approach to the Viceroy. Preventive arrests removed the mechanism that might have specified and disciplined the programme.

**Qualification:** The leadership also knew repression could sever communications and explicitly authorised local initiative within a general non-violent framework. Spontaneity was therefore neither wholly accidental nor centrally scripted.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Wardha—14 July; AICC Bombay—8 August; arrests—9 August.
- **Mains:** Use the 24-hour sequence to explain the later character of the movement.
- **Trap:** The slogan did not equal an official endorsement of lethal violence.
- **Answer line:** “The Congress authorised decentralised moral agency, while the Raj removed the leadership that might have bounded its expression.”

4. Wardha Working Committee decision came on 14 July 1942.
5. AICC ratification came at Bombay on 8 August.

*“Leaderless” describes the removal of national command; it does not mean the movement lacked aims, local organisers or accumulated preparation.*

### 1. The preventive strike

In the early hours of 9 August, the colonial government arrested Gandhi and the principal Congress leaders in a coordinated sweep. Orders for arrests and suppression had already been prepared.

The state's logic was to prevent a movement by removing its command. The result was paradoxical: the arrests prevented central control but triggered immediate public reaction.

### 2. The first phase: cities and visible protest

Bombay, Ahmedabad and Poona reacted on 9 August. Delhi and towns in U.P. and Bihar followed. Hartals, processions, flag-hoisting and clashes marked the opening days.

Students left schools and colleges, distributed illegal news-sheets and became communication links. Workers struck:

- Bombay workers stayed away for more than a week;
- Jamshedpur saw a thirteen-day strike;
- Ahmedabad's mill stoppage lasted roughly three and a half months;
- activity also occurred in Ahmednagar and Poona.

The urban phase showed immediate sympathy, but prolonged open action became difficult under concentrated police and military force.

### 3. The rural phase

Students and activists carried news from towns into villages. In Bihar and eastern U.P., large peasant crowds moved towards police stations, railway stations, post offices, courts and administrative headquarters.

The target pattern matters. Much of the violence was aimed at:

- communications;
- records and buildings of government;
- police and administrative symbols;
- the visible infrastructure of colonial command.

This does not make every act non-violent. It does show that the dominant political object was the state rather than indiscriminate private conflict.

### 4. Regional concentration

| Region | Characteristic evidence |
|---|---|
| Bihar | Intense rebellion; temporary loss of authority in several districts; communications disrupted |
| Eastern U.P. | Large rural crowds, attacks on state symbols; Ballia later formed a short-lived parallel authority |
| Midnapur, Bengal | Deep constructive-work base; mass action and later Tamluk Jatiya Sarkar |
| Satara, Maharashtra | Demonstrations, sabotage, regrouped underground and long-lived Prati Sarkar |
| Karnataka | Numerous telegraph-cutting incidents and attacks on communication offices |
| Gujarat and Maharashtra cities | Worker action, underground links and sabotage networks |

The movement did not spread with equal intensity everywhere. A good answer explains concentration through prior Congress work, student networks, local grievances, geography and the capacity of repression.

### 5. Spontaneous or organised?

The binary is misleading.

**Evidence for spontaneity:**

- top leaders were arrested before issuing a detailed programme;
- local crowds selected targets and tactics;
- communication with central authority collapsed;
- intensity and forms differed sharply by region.

**Evidence for organisation:**

- Congress had rebuilt networks since the mid-1930s;
- Individual Satyagraha had mobilised and tested cadres;
- Gandhi and provincial leaders had prepared public expectations;
- Congress Socialists had trained volunteers;
- underground leadership later restored information and morale.

**Verdict:** Quit India was **centrally authorised, preconditioned by years of organisation, and locally improvised after decapitation**.

### 6. Violence and non-violence

Congress's declared policy remained non-violent. Actual activity ranged from peaceful strikes and satyagraha to cutting wires, removing rails, burning buildings and attacks in which lives were lost.

Three distinctions prevent confusion:

1. official method is not identical to every participant's action;
2. sabotage of infrastructure is not identical to deliberate killing, though it could endanger life;
3. popular violence occurred within a setting of prior arrests, firing, collective punishment and censorship.

Qualification is not justification. The historian explains the relationship without erasing either popular violence or state coercion.

*The underground did not command every act. Its more defensible achievement was continuity—keeping communication, confidence and a sense of national struggle alive.*

### 1. Who sustained the underground?

Prominent underground figures included:

- Achyut Patwardhan;
- Aruna Asaf Ali;
- Ram Manohar Lohia;
- Sucheta Kripalani;
- Jayaprakash Narayan after his escape;
- Chhotubhai Puranik, Biju Patnaik and others in regional networks.

Congress Socialists were important, but the underground also drew Gandhian workers, Forward Bloc members, revolutionaries and unaffiliated Congress activists.

### 2. What did underground work involve?

The network's functions can be separated:

| Function | Examples | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | illegal bulletins, messages, couriers | uneven reach and constant surveillance |
| Morale | statements, broadcasts, continued symbols of resistance | could not recreate open mass mobilisation everywhere |
| Logistics | money, shelter, vehicles, movement of materials | depended on small trusted circles |
| Disruption | wires, bridges, rails and selected state targets | often nuisance value rather than sustained paralysis |
| Guidance | broad political direction | local groups retained operational initiative |

### 3. Congress Radio

The clandestine Congress Radio operated from changing locations in Bombay. Usha Mehta was a central member of the group, and Ram Manohar Lohia broadcast through it. It continued until November 1942, when the police located and confiscated it.

Its significance was not military. It challenged censorship by circulating news and assuring scattered participants that the movement continued.

### 4. Ballia: dramatic but brief

In Ballia in eastern U.P., a parallel authority was proclaimed in August 1942 under Chittu Pande. The Collector was compelled to release arrested Congress leaders and hand over authority, but the experiment survived only briefly. Troops restored colonial control within about a week.

**Lesson:** symbolic seizure of authority can be politically striking without becoming durable administration.

### 5. Tamluk: constructive work becomes alternative governance

The **Jatiya Sarkar** arose in Tamluk, Midnapur, on **17 December 1942** and lasted until September 1944.

It drew strength from prior Gandhian constructive work and local mobilisation. Its activities included:

- cyclone relief;
- grants to schools;
- arbitration courts;
- distribution of surplus paddy from better-off households to the poor;
- an armed volunteer wing known as the Vidyut Vahini.

The combination is important: parallel authority could mix welfare, adjudication, redistribution and coercive capacity.

### 6. Satara: the longest sustained experiment

Satara's first phase involved marches, sabotage and attacks on communications. After arrests reduced open action, activists regrouped. By mid-1943 the **Prati Sarkar** emerged, with **Nana Patil** as its most important leader.

Its activities included:

- Nyayadan Mandals or people's courts;
- punishment of informers and collaborators;
- “Robin Hood”-style seizures;
- prohibition;
- simple “Gandhi marriages” including formerly excluded groups;
- village libraries and education.

The princely state of Aundh provided refuge to activists. The Prati Sarkar continued until 1945.

### 7. Comparison: not three identical “governments”

| Dimension | Ballia | Tamluk | Satara |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duration | About a week | December 1942–September 1944 | Consolidated from 1943; continued to 1945 |
| Main character | Brief displacement of district authority | Relief and administrative functions | Underground-based alternative justice and social regulation |
| Key name | Chittu Pande | Jatiya Sarkar; local collective leadership | Nana Patil |
| Exam caution | Do not exaggerate durability | Do not omit constructive work | Do not reduce it to sabotage alone |

### 8. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Parallel governments were marginal local episodes and cannot prove that the Raj lost India.

**Reply:** They did not replace the colonial state nationally. Their importance lies in demonstrating that resistance could move from protest against authority to experiments in performing authority.

**Balanced assessment:** Their scale was limited, their forms uneven and their survival dependent on local conditions. Their symbolic and organisational significance was larger than their territorial reach.

- left schools and colleges;
- organised processions;
- wrote and distributed illegal patrikas;
- carried messages for underground groups;
- moved from towns to villages to spread news.

Banaras Hindu University students were among those who went into eastern U.P. villages. Youthfulness helps explain energy and mobility, but it also meant vulnerability to arrest and uneven strategic discipline.

### 2. Women

Women participated as organisers, couriers, broadcasters and public symbols of defiance.

- **Aruna Asaf Ali** became associated with the Gowalia Tank flag-hoisting and underground resistance.
- **Sucheta Kripalani** helped organise clandestine work.
- **Usha Mehta** was central to Congress Radio.
- School and college girls took part in protest and communication networks.

The named figures are anchors, not the whole story. Women also supplied shelter, moved messages and sustained households under repression.

### 3. Workers

Worker action was significant but geographically uneven. Ahmedabad, Bombay and Jamshedpur provide strong evidence of strikes. The Tata Steel stoppage in Jamshedpur lasted thirteen days; Ahmedabad's mills remained closed for a much longer period.

However, the Communist Party's People's War line encouraged industrial peace in areas where it had influence. Therefore “workers participated” is true, but “the organised working class acted uniformly” is not.

### 4. Peasants and agrarian society

Peasants of different strata formed the heart of the movement in eastern U.P., Bihar, Midnapur and Satara, with participation also in Andhra, Gujarat and Kerala.

Their action concentrated heavily on colonial symbols. The cited book account emphasises the notable absence of a general anti-zamindar turn even where state authority broke down. Some small zamindars participated; some large landlords remained neutral rather than actively assisting repression. The Raja of Darbhanga is cited as refusing use of his retainers against the movement and helping arrested tenants.

This evidence corrects two simplifications:

- Quit India was not merely an urban middle-class protest.
- It was not a general agrarian class war against landlords.

### 5. Officials and the erosion of loyalty

Lower-level police and administrative officials sometimes:

- warned activists of impending arrests;
- offered shelter;
- provided information or money;
- treated prisoners more sympathetically.

This is politically important because colonial rule depended on Indian intermediaries. Yet it must not be exaggerated. The civil service, police and military remained sufficiently functional to repress the movement. Erosion of loyalty was visible, not complete.

**Qualification:** Reach was regionally uneven, Muslim mass participation was limited, and the coercive apparatus did not disintegrate. “Broad but not universal” is the defensible formulation.

Yet the movement was not universal. Muslim mass participation was limited; the League, Communist Party and Hindu Mahasabha opposed or avoided the campaign; much organised business and many princes did not join; and the bureaucracy, police and army remained capable of repression.

Quit India was therefore a broad mass uprising without organisational unanimity. Its significance lies in diverse popular agency and regional depth, qualified by uneven participation and the survival of the colonial coercive structure.

*Suppression ended much open action. Gandhi's fast contested the government's claim that coercion had restored moral as well as administrative authority.*

### 1. The instruments of repression

The government used:

- mass preventive detention and prosecution;
- censorship and closure of nationalist newspapers;
- police and military firing;
- low-flying aircraft in some operations;
- collective fines;
- hostage-taking;
- whipping;
- burning or looting of villages during punitive action;
- extensive use of the Defence of India Rules.

The movement was not formally placed under nationwide martial law, but the book account judges that repression could be as severe as martial law in practice.

### 2. Use verified figures carefully

The cited book records:

- firing on unarmed crowds on **538 occasions**;
- by the end of 1942, **over 60,000 arrests**;
- **26,000 convictions**;
- **18,000 detentions** under the Defence of India Rules;
- collective fines totalling about **Rs 90 lakh**.

These figures describe state action and custody, not a complete casualty ledger.

This edition does **not** state a single death total because commonly repeated official and nationalist estimates vary and the permitted evidence reviewed here did not establish one uncontested figure. In an examination answer, “hundreds were killed and many more injured under severe firing and military repression” is safer unless a specifically sourced figure is supplied.

### 3. What repression achieved

Within roughly six or seven weeks, concentrated force largely ended the first open mass phase. This was a real operational defeat.

However, repression did not:

- eliminate underground work;
- erase parallel governments;
- restore Congress participation in legal politics;
- settle the freedom demand;
- convince national opinion that British rule enjoyed consent.

### 4. Gandhi's 21-day fast

Gandhi began a **21-day fast on 10 February 1943** while detained at the Aga Khan Palace. The government repeatedly pressed him to condemn popular violence. Gandhi refused to accept sole movement responsibility and argued that the state's “leonine violence” had provoked the eruption.

The fast was his available form of protest against detention, repression and the government's moral indictment.

### 5. Political effects of the fast

The response included:

- hartals, demonstrations and sympathetic fasts;
- demands for Gandhi's release;
- pressure from newspapers, organisations and international opinion;
- the resignation of three Indian members of the Viceroy's Executive Council: **M.S. Aney, N.R. Sarkar and H.P. Mody**.

The resignations are important because these men had supported suppression in 1942 but would not share responsibility for Gandhi's possible death.

Gandhi survived the fast. It revived political activity, raised morale and weakened the government's attempt to justify repression as a necessary response to Congress-instigated violence.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** A personal fast could not reverse military suppression and therefore had little political value.

**Reply:** Its purpose was not to defeat the army. It shifted the contest from physical control to legitimacy, mobilised public opinion and imposed costs on Indian cooperation with the Viceroy's government.

**Qualification:** The fast did not release the Congress leadership or revive a nationwide open movement. Its achievement was moral and political, not an immediate constitutional concession.

- **Trap:** The three Executive Council resignations followed the fast crisis, not the original ministry resignations of 1939.

## Lesson 11 — Political alignments, significance and the road beyond 1942

```text
    immediate suppression / long-term change in the terms of power
```

*The movement's scale cannot be inferred from party unanimity, and party positions cannot be inferred from the conduct of every local supporter.*

### 1. Communist Party of India

After Germany attacked the Soviet Union, the CPI treated the war as a People's War against fascism. Its official position opposed Quit India and encouraged support for production and the Allied war effort.

This requires two qualifications:

- the position followed a changed international and ideological assessment, not simple devotion to British imperialism;
- hundreds of local Communists participated despite the party line, showing that organisational directives did not perfectly determine grassroots behaviour.

### 2. Muslim League

The League did not join the Congress movement. It was pursuing the Pakistan demand and did not accept Congress's right to launch a national struggle on behalf of all Indians.

Muslim mass participation in Quit India was low. Yet the cited account stresses that League supporters generally did not become informers and that the movement was not accompanied by a general communal clash.

This distinction matters:

```text
low participation ≠ active popular hostility everywhere
```

The Congress's removal from legal politics until 1945 gave the League greater organisational space, but that opportunity alone did not create its later mandate. League mobilisation, wartime politics and the separate-electorate structure must also be considered.

### 3. Hindu Mahasabha

The Hindu Mahasabha did not join Quit India. Its leaders criticised or opposed the movement, and Mahasabha politicians continued participation in provincial coalition politics in places.

Use careful language:

- party leadership did not support the Congress campaign;
- this does not prove that every Hindu Mahasabha sympathiser acted identically;
- its position should not be merged with the League's ideology merely because both stayed out.

### 4. Princes and princely states

Most princely rulers did not join the movement. Gandhi appealed to princes to accept the sovereignty of their peoples and to subjects of the states to identify with the Indian nation.

Local variation existed. The princely state of Aundh, whose ruler had a pro-nationalist orientation, gave refuge to Satara activists. This exception shows why “all princes supported repression” is too categorical.

Detailed states' peoples movements belong to Topic 23, while integration after independence belongs to a later topic.

### 5. Did Quit India fail?

It failed in immediate operational terms:

- the leadership was imprisoned;
- the open movement was violently suppressed;
- no transfer of power occurred in 1942;
- political divisions remained unresolved;
- colonial administration and armed force survived.

It succeeded in changing the political threshold:

- immediate independence replaced deferred reform as the governing national demand;
- future negotiations concerned the manner and structure of transfer rather than whether India should remain indefinitely subject;
- local initiative revealed the depth of nationalist legitimacy;
- repression showed that the Raj required extraordinary coercion rather than routine consent;
- Congress's continuing popular standing survived years of prohibition.

### 6. Do not make 1942 the single cause of 1947

The strongest causal judgement is cumulative:

```text
Quit India
   + British wartime exhaustion
   + changed international environment
   + INA trials and post-war popular upsurge
   + armed-forces disaffection
   + 1945–46 electoral mandates and constitutional deadlock
   = transfer-of-power crisis
```

The later links belong to Topic 26. Here the conclusion is narrower: Quit India made a return to pre-war gradualism politically implausible and demonstrated that stable colonial government could not rely on enduring Indian consent.

### 7. Historiographical debate

**Nationalist emphasis:** The movement was the culminating mass struggle that placed immediate independence beyond retreat.

**Popular-agency emphasis:** Regional histories show that peasants, students and local organisers shaped aims and tactics rather than merely executing commands from above.

**Critical emphasis:** The movement was uneven, partly violent, organisationally isolated and unable to produce an immediate concession; Congress's imprisonment also left legal political space to its rivals.

**Synthesis:** Quit India was both a national call and a collection of regional insurgencies. It was defeated as an immediate campaign, but it altered the legitimacy, expectations and future agenda of imperial rule.

- **Mains:** Evaluate success in immediate, organisational, political and long-term registers.
- **Prelims:** CPI—People's War line; League and Mahasabha—did not join; local conduct requires qualification.
- **Trap:** Do not say Quit India alone caused British withdrawal.
- **Bridge:** INA, post-war unrest and the transfer negotiations are continued in Topic 26.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Why was the Individual Satyagraha neither passive cooperation nor a full mass movement?

**Model answer:**
Individual Satyagraha, launched in October 1940, challenged Britain's claim that India voluntarily supported the war. A selected satyagrahi notified the district magistrate of a speech's time and place, delivered the anti-war declaration and accepted arrest. If not arrested, the satyagrahi repeated the speech, entered villages and began the Delhi-directed “Delhi Chalo” trek. Vinoba Bhave was first; Jawaharlal Nehru was second.

It was not passive cooperation: it asserted political non-consent, sustained mobilisation and rejected the August Offer's postponement of responsible government. It was not a mass revolt because Gandhi wished to avoid uncontrolled disruption of anti-fascist defence.

The campaign also educated people, tested Congress discipline, removed opportunists and gave Britain another peaceful opportunity. Its limitation was deliberate: it could prepare cadres and expose coercion, but not answer the Japanese advance or secure immediate power. Those pressures later drove the transition to Quit India.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** purpose and constitutional protest (2); complete notified-speech/arrest/Delhi Chalo method (3); deliberate limits and anti-fascist concern (2); organisational outcomes (2); bridge to 1942 (1). Total: 10.

## 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Analyse the relationship between preventive arrests and the decentralised character of the Quit India Movement.

**Model answer:**
The government arrested Gandhi and the principal Congress leaders in the early hours of 9 August 1942, immediately after the AICC adopted the Quit India resolution. This preventive strike removed the leadership before Gandhi could approach the Viceroy or issue a detailed operational programme.

The arrests first triggered urban hartals, processions, student action and worker strikes. As news spread into rural areas, local crowds attacked police stations, courts, railway stations and telegraph links. Bihar and eastern U.P. experienced rebellion-like intensity. Because central communication had collapsed, tactics differed sharply across regions.

Decentralisation was nevertheless not wholly accidental. Congress had rebuilt its organisation, Individual Satyagraha had prepared cadres, provincial leaders had raised expectations, and the AICC resolution anticipated that participants might have to become their own guides when committees could not function.

Underground networks later restored partial continuity. Achyut Patwardhan, Aruna Asaf Ali, Ram Manohar Lohia, Sucheta Kripalani, Jayaprakash Narayan and others circulated information, maintained morale and supported local groups. Parallel governments at Ballia, Tamluk and Satara represented further local initiative.

Thus preventive arrests did not create political purpose, but they determined operational form. Quit India became leaderless at the national level, locally led in practice and connected by clandestine networks rather than directed through a continuous central chain of command.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** national authorisation and intended framework (3); preventive arrests and broken command (4); locally selected tactics and regional variation (3); prior preparation and underground linkage (3); balanced causal judgement (2). Total: 15.

## 20 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Assess Quit India as a movement of popular sovereignty rather than merely a campaign of protest.

**Model answer:**
Quit India began as a Congress demand for immediate British withdrawal, but preventive arrests transformed it into varied local challenges to colonial authority. Popular sovereignty appeared first as refusal: hartals, flag-hoisting, strikes, attacks on police stations, courts and communications, and non-cooperation with officials.

It also appeared through autonomous political agency. Students carried the movement from towns to villages; women organised clandestine work and radio; workers struck; peasants became central in Bihar, eastern U.P., Midnapur and Satara; lower officials sometimes supplied warning and shelter. The AICC's anticipation that participants might become their own guides gave this initiative a broad political frame.

The strongest evidence came from parallel governments. Ballia briefly displaced district authority. Tamluk's Jatiya Sarkar organised relief, schools, arbitration and distribution. Satara's Prati Sarkar created people's courts, social rules and action against collaborators. These experiments performed limited functions of government rather than only opposing government.

However, popular sovereignty remained incomplete. The movement was regionally uneven, major political organisations stayed out, violence complicated Congress's non-violent claim, and the colonial coercive apparatus restored control.

Quit India therefore did not create a rival Indian state. It demonstrated that national legitimacy could generate local authority outside the Raj and that colonial government increasingly depended on coercion rather than accepted sovereignty.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** definition of popular sovereignty and refusal of colonial legitimacy (3); autonomous mass agency (4); underground communication and continuity (3); Ballia, Tamluk and Satara governing evidence (5); territorial, institutional and political limits (3); reasoned overall judgement (2). Total: 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. The wartime constitutional break

- 3 September 1939: Britain entered war with Germany and the Government of India declared India a belligerent without consulting elected representatives.
- Congress had condemned fascist aggression; its objection was involuntary participation under colonial authority.
- Core contradiction: democracy abroad versus denial of responsible power in India.
- Congress ministries resigned in October–November 1939.
- Meaning: provincial autonomy allowed administration but not sovereignty over defence or war.
- Cost: provincial reforms ended and political space opened to rivals.
- Muslim League observed Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939.
- Lahore Resolution followed in March 1940; resignation was a context, not a single cause of Partition.
- Gandhi initially favoured virtually unconditional Allied cooperation; Congress leaders later cited the just anti-fascist cause, weak Hindu-Muslim unity and inadequate organisation as reasons to delay mass struggle.
- Bose proposed a parallel Congress and independent launch; the CSP and CPI preferred to remain within Congress and press its leadership.
- Route the detailed 1935 Act and ministry record back to Topic 24.

## 2. August Offer and calibrated resistance

- August Offer announced by Viceroy Linlithgow in 1940.
- Proposed expanded Executive Council and War Advisory Council.
- Promised post-war constitution-making and retained dominion status as the goal.
- Protected substantial minority non-consent, strengthening a veto-like constraint.
- Did not establish an immediate responsible national government.
- Congress rejected postponement and retained imperial power.
- League did not receive an explicit Pakistan settlement.
- Individual Satyagraha began in October 1940.
- Vinoba Bhave was first satyagrahi; Jawaharlal Nehru second.
- Method: advance notice to the district magistrate, notified anti-war speech and expected arrest.
- If no arrest followed: repeat the speech, enter villages and begin the Delhi-directed “Delhi Chalo” trek.
- Purpose: deny voluntary consent, give Britain another peaceful opportunity, educate the public, test cadres, remove opportunists and avoid immediate mass disruption of anti-fascist defence.
- More than 25,000 convictions were recorded by 15 May 1941 in the consulted book.

## 3. Why urgency rose in 1941–42

- Germany's attack on the USSR changed the ideological character of the war for Communists.
- Congress remained sympathetic to China and the Soviet Union.
- At the end of December 1941, the CWC adopted over Gandhi's and Nehru's objections an offer of full defence cooperation if Britain promised post-war full independence and transferred the substance of power immediately.
- Japanese victories, the fall of Singapore and retreat from Burma broke British prestige.
- Refugee accounts suggested unequal evacuation and possible imperial abandonment.
- Inflation, shortages, requisitioning and boat-denial measures widened distress.
- Gandhi opposed Japanese aggression; Quit India was not an invitation to Japan.
- U.S., Chinese and British Labour pressure encouraged a British initiative.

## 4. Cripps Mission — exact distinctions

| Box | Recall |
|---|---|
| Status | Post-war Indian Union with dominion status |
| Constitution body | Provincial members elected by legislatures; princely representatives nominated by rulers |
| Provinces | Could decline the new constitution and arrange future status separately |
| Princely states | Participation depended on rulers, not direct election by subjects |
| Defence | British control continued during the war |

- Cripps arrived in March 1942.
- The offer recognised Indian constitution-making more clearly than earlier statements.
- It did not create a directly elected adult-franchise assembly.
- Provincial non-accession made fragmentation possible without itself creating Pakistan.
- Congress objections: no immediate power, retained defence, dominion rather than complete independence, undemocratic princely nomination, danger to unity.
- League objection: no explicit sovereign Pakistan, fixed territorial settlement or exclusive League representative right.
- Cripps had little authority to move beyond the draft.
- Churchill, Amery, Linlithgow and Wavell resisted substantive wartime transfer.
- Best verdict: genuine future concession weakened by unchanged present authority and unresolved national structure.

## 5. From Cripps failure to Quit India

- Cripps returned in mid-April 1942.
- Failure made further silence appear to legitimise unilateral imperial rule.
- Working Committee at Wardha accepted struggle on 14 July 1942.
- AICC ratified Quit India at Gowalia Tank, Bombay, on 8 August.
- Gandhi's “Do or Die” meant freedom or sacrifice, not an official instruction to kill.
- Intended method remained non-violent mass struggle.
- Gandhi expected to approach the Viceroy before actual launch.
- Instructions differentiated officials, soldiers, princes, students, peasants and landlords.
- Many were never fully issued because the leadership was arrested.
- The resolution anticipated broken communication and local self-direction.

## 6. Movement phases

1. **Preventive decapitation:** leaders arrested in early hours of 9 August.
2. **Urban shock:** hartals, processions, student action, worker strikes and flag-hoisting.
3. **Rural rebellion:** crowds attacked police stations, post offices, courts, railways and telegraphs.
4. **Suppression:** open mass phase largely broken within six or seven weeks.
5. **Underground continuity:** bulletins, couriers, funds, shelter, sabotage and Congress Radio.
6. **Parallel authority:** Ballia, Tamluk and Satara.
7. **Moral counter-offensive:** Gandhi's February 1943 fast.

> **Answer line:** Quit India was nationally authorised, organisationally preconditioned and locally improvised.

## 7. Regional evidence

- Bihar and eastern U.P.: rebellion-like intensity, disrupted communications and temporary loss of authority.
- Midnapur: prior constructive work supported sustained resistance and Tamluk Jatiya Sarkar.
- Satara: marches, sabotage, regrouped underground and Prati Sarkar.
- Karnataka: extensive telegraph disruption and attacks on communication offices.
- Bombay, Ahmedabad and Jamshedpur: important worker action.
- Avoid describing the movement as geographically uniform.

## 8. Underground and parallel governments

- Achyut Patwardhan, Aruna Asaf Ali, Ram Manohar Lohia, Sucheta Kripalani and Jayaprakash Narayan were prominent underground figures.
- Underground role: morale, information, funds, shelter and broad guidance; local groups retained initiative.
- Congress Radio operated from Bombay; Usha Mehta was central and Lohia broadcast; it lasted until November 1942.
- Ballia: Chittu Pande; brief authority lasting about a week.
- Tamluk Jatiya Sarkar: 17 December 1942 to September 1944; relief, schools, arbitration, paddy distribution and Vidyut Vahini.
- Satara Prati Sarkar: Nana Patil; people's courts, action against informers, prohibition, simple marriages, libraries and education; continued to 1945.
- Parallel governments did not establish national dual power but performed limited sovereignty.

## 9. Social composition

- Students: early strikes, processions, patrikas, couriers and village mobilisation.
- Women: Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani and Usha Mehta; wider roles in shelter, messages and public resistance.
- Workers: major but uneven strikes in selected centres.
- Peasants: heart of the movement in eastern U.P., Bihar, Midnapur and Satara.
- Agrarian activity focused primarily on colonial symbols; no general anti-zamindar uprising.
- Some small zamindars joined; some large landlords remained neutral.
- Lower officials sometimes gave information, shelter and money.
- Erosion of loyalty did not equal collapse of bureaucracy, police or army.

## 10. Political alignments

- CPI officially opposed Quit India after the People's War turn; some local Communists participated.
- Muslim League stayed out; Muslim mass participation was low.
- Low Muslim participation did not amount to universal popular hostility or widespread communal clash.
- Hindu Mahasabha did not join and its politicians continued cooperation or office in some provinces.
- Most princes did not join; Gandhi appealed for popular sovereignty.
- Aundh was a local exception that sheltered Satara activists.
- Congress fought with broad popular reach but without party unanimity.

## 11. Repression and fast

- Censorship, preventive arrests, firing, military deployment, hostages, collective fines, whipping and village punishment were used.
- Source-verified figures: firing on 538 occasions; more than 60,000 arrests by end-1942; 26,000 convictions; 18,000 Defence of India Rules detentions; about Rs 90 lakh in collective fines.
- Do not state a disputed death total without a controlled source.
- Gandhi began a 21-day fast on 10 February 1943.
- Purpose: protest detention and state violence; reject the government's unilateral blame for popular violence.
- Hartals, appeals and international pressure followed.
- M.S. Aney, N.R. Sarkar and H.P. Mody resigned from the Viceroy's Executive Council.
- The fast challenged legitimacy but did not immediately reverse suppression.

## 12. Significance and limits

### Immediate limits

- Leadership imprisoned.
- Open phase suppressed.
- No 1942 transfer of power.
- Political unity absent.
- Colonial coercive apparatus survived.

### Durable significance

- Immediate independence displaced deferred constitutional reform.
- Local initiative displayed deep nationalist legitimacy.
- Parallel governments experimented with alternative authority.
- British rule increasingly appeared dependent on coercion.
- Future negotiations focused on how and to whom power would transfer.

### Final qualification

Quit India was a major cause in the end of empire, not the sole cause. British wartime exhaustion, international change, INA trials, post-war unrest, service disaffection, electoral mandates and constitutional negotiations must be added. Those developments are continued in Topic 26.
