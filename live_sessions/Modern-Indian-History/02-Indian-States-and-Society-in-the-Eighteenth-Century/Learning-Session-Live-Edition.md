# Modern Indian History Topic 02: Indian States and Society in the Eighteenth Century

## Learning promise

The eighteenth century is often taught as an empty corridor between Mughal decline and British conquest. That picture is misleading. The century contained political fracture, agrarian pressure and warfare, but it also contained vigorous state-building, commercial expansion, mobile military labour, banking networks, regional courts and new cultural centres. The central problem is therefore not, "Why did everything collapse?" It is:

> How did energetic regional polities emerge from an imperial order, and why could their individual strength not become durable collective resistance to the East India Company?

This live edition develops the answer from first principles. It begins with a map of state types, then opens the shared machinery of legitimacy, revenue, credit and force. Only after that foundation does it compare individual states, social groups, economic networks, culture, technology, historiography and the Company's opportunity.

## Lesson 1 — Stop Calling It a Vacuum: The Map of Eighteenth-Century State Formation

Progress: 1 / 17 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Typology, chronology and scope

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Concept focus:** State typology and regional variation checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; this lesson establishes a historical classification rather than a live policy claim.

### Visual first: one imperial language, several political trajectories

```text
                 WEAKENING EFFECTIVE IMPERIAL COMMAND
                                |
          +---------------------+----------------------+
          |                     |                      |
  SUCCESSOR STATES       NEW / REBEL STATES    INDEPENDENT KINGDOMS
  former Mughal          power built through   older regional polities
  provinces              resistance, mobility  becoming more assertive
          |                     |                      |
  Bengal, Awadh,         Marathas, Sikhs,       Mysore, Rajput states,
  Hyderabad              Jats, Rohillas         Travancore
          \_____________________|_____________________/
                                |
        Mughal titles, offices, revenue forms and prestige often survive
```

The diagram shows why "post-Mughal" does not mean "non-Mughal": political authority dispersed, but much of the inherited language of rule remained useful.

### 1. Begin with the ordinary intuition

Imagine a large organisation whose head office can no longer supervise its regional branches. Three things may happen. A regional manager may run a branch as a personal domain while retaining the old logo. A group previously resisting headquarters may construct a new organisation. An older local house may use the weakness of the centre to enlarge its room for action. Eighteenth-century India displayed all three patterns.

That analogy establishes **different routes to autonomy**. It does not imply that states were modern companies, or that territorial sovereignty was already uniform and exclusive. Rights overlapped; tribute, office, hereditary possession, conquest and imperial sanction could coexist.

### 2. The three-part typology

| Type | Route to power | Representative cases | What continued | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Successor state | A Mughal governor or high official consolidated a province | Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad | Titles, administrative vocabulary, imperial sanction, often coin/khutba symbolism | Appointments, taxation, diplomacy and war became locally controlled |
| New or rebel formation | Communities, chiefs or military entrepreneurs built power through conflict with imperial authority | Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Rohillas/Afghans | Selective use of Mughal grants, revenue practices and legitimacy | New ruling coalitions and military networks gained territorial control |
| Independent kingdom | A pre-existing regional polity asserted or expanded practical independence | Mysore, Rajput states, Travancore | Local dynastic and institutional traditions | Stronger armies, centralisation, territorial expansion or commercial strategy |

The categories are analytical aids, not sealed boxes. The Marathas challenged Mughal power but also sought Mughal *sanads* and used inherited administrative forms. Mysore preserved an older Wodeyar kingship while Haidar Ali exercised effective authority. Arcot developed from the Carnatic subah under Hyderabad's nominal superiority, but its later history became entangled with European rivalry.

### 3. Autonomy was practical before it was juridical

A frequent mistake is to imagine a clean declaration of independence. Regional rulers often found the opposite strategy more useful:

1. retain the emperor as a source of rank and legitimacy;
2. secure local control over appointments and revenue;
3. build a loyal coalition of officials, landholders, soldiers and financiers;
4. transmit less revenue to Delhi, or stop remittance in practice;
5. conduct war and diplomacy without awaiting imperial direction;
6. preserve symbolic deference when it lowered political costs.

Coins in an emperor's name or the emperor's name in the *khutba* could coexist with substantial local autonomy. Symbols answered, "Why may this ruler rule?" They did not prove that Delhi collected the province's revenue or commanded its army.

### 4. A century of regional variation

The political map was uneven. Bengal combined agricultural surplus, textile exports, zamindars and major banking houses. Awadh controlled a rich Gangetic zone but had to bargain with powerful taluqdars and military groups. Hyderabad governed a broad Deccan territory through dispersed authority and patron-client relations. Maratha power expanded through the fiscal claims of *chauth* and *sardeshmukhi* and through armies tied to powerful sardars. Sikh power grew through a religious-political community, agrarian support and armed bands. Mysore and Travancore built more centralised military-fiscal machines in parts of southern India.

Therefore, no single sentence such as "all states declined" or "all regions prospered" can carry the century.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If warfare, invasions and political fragmentation were widespread, calling this a transformation romanticises suffering.

**Reply:** Transformation is not praise. It identifies the relocation and recombination of power. Delhi, some routes and some populations suffered severely; peasants could face high extraction; cities could be sacked. Yet Bengal's commerce, Lucknow's court culture, Maratha expansion, Mysore's military innovation and Travancore's consolidation cannot be described as a universal void.

**Qualified assessment:** The most defensible starting formula is:

> Contraction of central command + regional state formation + uneven economic vitality + unequal social costs.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish origin categories and avoid treating symbolic Mughal allegiance as effective subordination.
- **Mains:** use the typology to organise an answer, but show overlaps instead of forcing every state into a rigid compartment.
- **Historical scope:** Use later conflicts only to test a state's structure; their detailed conquest sequences fall beyond this regional-state analysis.

### Revision notes

1. Political decentralisation was not automatically a power vacuum.
2. Successor states arose from Mughal provincial structures.
3. Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad are the core successor cases.
4. Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas followed new/rebel trajectories.
5. Mysore, Rajput states and Travancore had older regional bases.
6. Categories can overlap because institutions and legitimacies travelled.
7. Mughal symbolism could survive practical autonomy.
8. Regional variation is essential to any economic or social claim.
9. Transformation does not deny violence or peasant hardship.
10. Company supremacy was not the predetermined outcome of 1707.

- **B — Incorrect:** Rights remained overlapping and negotiated; modern exclusive sovereignty did not become the common pattern.

- **D — Incorrect:** Institutional vocabulary survived, but appointments, revenue and force increasingly moved away from imperial control.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**10 marks | 150 words**

**Question:** Classify the principal types of eighteenth-century Indian states. Why should this typology remain flexible?

#### Model answer

Eighteenth-century states can be classified by their route to power. **Successor states** such as Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad converted Mughal provincial office into dynastic regional autonomy while retaining imperial forms. **New or rebel formations** such as the Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas built territorial power through resistance, community mobilisation or military entrepreneurship. **Independent kingdoms** such as Mysore, the Rajput states and Travancore used older regional bases to enlarge practical sovereignty.

The typology clarifies genealogy, but it is not a set of sealed boxes. Marathas challenged Mughal command while seeking imperial *sanads*. Mysore belonged to the post-Vijayanagara political field, yet Haidar Ali later acquired effective authority within the existing kingdom. Hyderabad preserved coin and *khutba* conventions while independently controlling war and appointments.

Thus the categories identify dominant routes, not pure constitutional forms. Eighteenth-century authority remained layered, combining inherited legitimacy, local rights, tribute, conquest and negotiated power.

#### Distinct scoring guidance

- 2 marks for defining all three routes rather than merely listing states.
- 4 marks for placing representative states accurately.
- 2 marks for two examples of overlap or institutional borrowing.
- 2 marks for the qualified conclusion that typology explains genealogy, not rigid sovereignty.

### Transition

A typology tells us **what kinds of states appeared**. It does not yet explain **how any ruler converted a title into obedience**. That requires opening the common machinery of revenue, credit, coercion and negotiation.

## Lesson 2 — The State-Formation Engine: Legitimacy, Revenue, Credit and Force

Progress: 2 / 17 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: The common political mechanism

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Regional-state chapters and discussions of zamindars, revenue farming, bankers and military labour checked.
- **Concept focus:** Basic typology and Advanced fiscal-military framework checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; no modern analogy is used as historical evidence.
- **Prior dependency:** Lesson 1's typology is assumed.

### Visual first: authority had to circulate

```text
 LEGITIMACY
 title, dynasty,
 imperial sanction
       |
       v
 REVENUE -----> CREDIT -----> ARMY
 land demand    bankers,      troops, forts,
 customs        hundis        artillery
       ^                         |
       |                         v
 LOCAL ELITES <--- SECURITY --- TERRITORY
 zamindars,       routes,       villages,
 chiefs,          markets       towns, ports
 officials
```

The state survived only when this circuit kept moving. A broken link could turn nominal authority into helplessness.

### 1. Why revenue comes before glory

A ruler could inherit a title, win a battle or receive an imperial *farman*. None of these paid soldiers every month. Effective rule required predictable claims on agrarian surplus, customs and tribute. Collection, however, was rarely a direct conversation between ruler and peasant. It passed through zamindars, taluqdars, deshmukhs, revenue farmers, village headmen and accountants.

The ruler therefore faced a double problem:

- extract enough to finance government and war;
- avoid making intermediaries so autonomous, or cultivators so desperate, that the revenue base itself fractured.

This was not solved uniformly. Some rulers disciplined old landholders but confirmed them after submission. Some promoted a smaller number of large zamindars. Some farmed revenue to bidders. Some converted jagir land to *khalisa*, whose income went more directly to the ruler.

### 2. The fiscal-military idea

A **fiscal-military state** is not simply a warlike state. It is a political system that improves the collection and mobilisation of resources in order to sustain organised force.

```text
more reliable assessment
        -> more predictable cash
        -> larger or better-equipped forces
        -> stronger territorial enforcement
        -> wider or safer revenue base
```

The virtuous circle could become vicious. High military costs might produce harsher collection; harsh collection could encourage flight or rebellion; disorder could shrink receipts; unpaid troops could mutiny or plunder.

### 3. Why bankers entered politics

Revenue did not arrive at the same place or time as expenditure. A state might need cash for an army today while land revenue arrived after harvest. Banking houses:

- advanced money;
- transferred funds through *hundis*, or bills of exchange;
- guaranteed remittances;
- connected courts to merchant networks;
- sometimes handled mints or treasuries;
- assessed political risk.

Their role was not decorative. Credit converted future income into present military capacity. A ruler who alienated major financiers could face a cash crisis even in a wealthy province. Conversely, bankers who helped make and unmake rulers increased their influence but also exposed themselves to political retaliation.

### 4. Military labour and political entrepreneurship

Armies contained more than hereditary retainers. The century offered employment to cavalrymen, infantrymen, artillery specialists, Afghans, Arabs, local peasant-soldiers and European-trained units. A successful commander could turn a following into a territorial claim. Haidar Ali's rise in Mysore is a striking example; Afghan adventurers in Farrukhabad and Rohilkhand show another route.

Military modernisation was selective. Importing firearms, instructors or drill did not automatically create a modern state. Supply, payment, command discipline, manufacture, intelligence and political loyalty mattered just as much as weapons.

### 5. Negotiated sovereignty

Power was distributed among rulers and corporate or local groups. A zamindar might collect revenue, command armed followers and possess customary rights. A merchant might fund the court. A sardar might hold a territorial assignment and a private army. A ruler frequently governed not by eliminating these actors but by making them clients, partners or subordinate allies.

This is why eighteenth-century sovereignty is best seen as **layered and negotiated**. The ruler sought supremacy, but several actors retained enforceable claims.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The engine exaggerates money and reduces religion, identity and charisma to mere covers.

**Reply:** Religious community, dynastic memory and personal authority could mobilise loyalty. The Sikh Khalsa cannot be understood as a revenue device, nor Maratha identity as a bookkeeping fiction. But durable territorial rule still required supplies, collection, armed organisation and alliances.

**Qualified assessment:** Identity could create solidarity; fiscal and military institutions converted solidarity into sustained power.

### UPSC application: the four-question diagnostic

For any eighteenth-century state, ask:

1. **Legitimacy:** What authorised rule?
2. **Extraction:** Who collected what from whom?
3. **Coalition:** Which landed, commercial and military groups sustained it?
4. **Capacity:** Could it reliably turn resources into administration and force?

### Revision notes

1. Titles without revenue produced nominal power.
2. Agrarian surplus remained the main fiscal base.
3. Intermediaries were necessary but potentially centrifugal.
4. *Khalisa* revenue differed from assigned jagir income.
5. Revenue farming transferred collection rights for a contracted payment.
6. Credit bridged the timing gap between income and expenditure.
7. *Hundis* moved or promised funds across commercial networks.
8. Military labour was mobile and politically consequential.
9. Weapons alone did not create durable capacity.
10. Eighteenth-century sovereignty was layered and negotiated.
11. Identity and institutions should be studied together.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**10 marks | 150 words**

**Question:** Explain how revenue, credit and military power formed an interdependent circuit in eighteenth-century regional states.

#### Model answer

Regional rulers needed more than titles. Agrarian revenue, customs and tribute financed troops, forts and administration. Because receipts arrived unevenly after harvests and across distant territories, bankers used advances and *hundis* to make expected income available for immediate expenditure. Paid forces could enforce claims, secure routes and widen the taxable base.

The circuit depended on intermediaries. Zamindars, taluqdars, revenue farmers and village officers connected the court to producers, while merchants and financiers supplied cash and information. Their cooperation increased reach, but also gave them bargaining power.

A virtuous cycle linked predictable collection to credit, security and further revenue. A destructive cycle emerged when war costs caused excessive demands, disorder, falling receipts and unpaid troops.

The qualification is crucial: identity and legitimacy could mobilise loyalty, but neither sustained territorial rule without fiscal organisation. Conversely, greater treasury income did not by itself establish peasant welfare or administrative justice.

#### Distinct scoring guidance

- 3 marks for the complete revenue-credit-force sequence.
- 2 marks for naming both landed and financial intermediaries.
- 3 marks for contrasting virtuous and destructive cycles.
- 2 marks for separating state capacity from legitimacy and welfare.

## Lesson 3 — Hyderabad: Autonomy Through Incorporation

Progress: 3 / 17 | Stage: Successor states | Subtopic: The Deccan polity of the Nizam

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Bipan Chandra and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay accounts of Nizam-ul-Mulk, Hyderabad's administration and regional elite checked.
- **Concept focus:** Hyderabad's successor-state profile and fiscal limits checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; present-day Hyderabad is not used to infer eighteenth-century institutions.
- **Historical scope:** Arcot's genealogy matters here; the detailed Anglo-French and Carnatic conflict sequence does not.

### Visual first: control without uniform administration

```text
                 NIZAM / ASAF JAH
          war, treaty, appointment, patronage
                         |
       +-----------------+------------------+
       |                 |                  |
 semi-autonomous     revenue farmers    military and
 local rulers        and officials      financial elites
       |                 |                  |
 annual peshkash     land collection    cash, troops,
 and allegiance      with weak checks   expertise
       \_________________|__________________/
                         |
             WIDELY DIFFUSED REGIONAL POWER
```

Hyderabad's ruler was strong because he stood above a network, not because he replaced every local authority with a uniform bureaucracy.

### 1. From imperial noble to regional founder

Chin Qulich Khan, better known as Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I, was a leading Mughal noble and briefly *wazir*. Frustrated by court politics and failed reform, he returned to the Deccan. After defeating Mubariz Khan in 1724, he consolidated authority as subahdar. His permanent settlement in the Deccan is commonly associated with the practical crystallisation of Hyderabad's autonomy.

He did not issue a simple modern declaration of independence. Instead:

- coins continued in the Mughal emperor's name;
- the emperor's name remained in the *khutba*;
- the Nizam conducted wars and treaties independently;
- he conferred ranks, jagirs and offices;
- he subdued refractory zamindars and built a regional support base.

The distinction is central: **symbolic suzerainty above, operational autonomy below**.

### 2. Incorporation rather than administrative erasure

Hyderabad did not destroy every indigenous power structure. Local rulers could retain inherited territories in exchange for *peshkash*, an annual tribute, and political acknowledgement. Traders, moneylenders, military nobles and revenue specialists entered a patron-client network centred on the Nizam.

This arrangement offered reach at low administrative cost. Its limit was that power remained widely diffused. Revenue farmers and hereditary jagirdars could entrench themselves; supervision was weaker than an idealised central system; the ruler depended on negotiation and patronage.

### 3. Continuity with changed content

| Mughal-derived form | Hyderabad adaptation | Political result |
|---|---|---|
| Mansab and noble rank | Retained, but altered and less tightly integrated | Status survived without the same imperial control |
| Jagir | Increasingly hereditary in practice | Local power bases hardened |
| Provincial office | Became the foundation of dynastic rule | Delegated authority turned regional |
| Revenue intermediation | Greater reliance on powerful farmers and local elites | Collection expanded reach but reduced close supervision |
| Imperial ceremony | Coin and khutba symbolism continued | Legitimacy without daily obedience |

The case demonstrates that continuity of vocabulary can conceal transformation of power relations.

### 4. Social and political coalition

The Nizam's toleration toward Hindus was not merely private benevolence. Economically influential Hindu officials and financiers were important to administration; Puran Chand served as a notable diwan in Bipan Chandra's account. The state formed a regional elite from diverse social backgrounds, including older military aristocrats and newer specialists in finance and revenue management.

This does not prove equal access for all social groups. Elite inclusion should not be confused with democratic representation. It means that political recruitment was not reducible to a simple Hindu-Muslim binary.

### 5. Capacity and vulnerability

Asaf Jah built a recognisable Deccan power, but succession after his death in 1748 produced crisis. Maratha pressure, disputed succession and intervention by European companies exposed the costs of diffused authority. These later contests matter here only as a test of institutional resilience; their war sequence belongs elsewhere.

Hyderabad survived as a major polity, showing that fragmentation did not equal disappearance. Yet its reliance on negotiated authority made rapid unified mobilisation difficult when internal claimants and external powers interacted.

### 6. Strongest criticism and reply

**Criticism:** If the Nizam could wage war, appoint officers and compel tribute, describing authority as "diffused" understates centralisation.

**Reply:** Central supremacy and dispersed implementation can coexist. The Nizam possessed decisive patronage and military authority, but local rulers, jagirdars, revenue farmers and financiers retained substantial operational power.

**Qualified verdict:** Hyderabad was neither a loose collection with no centre nor a uniformly administered territorial state. It was a powerful regional hierarchy built through incorporation.

### UPSC integration

- Use Hyderabad to explain continuity-with-change.
- Distinguish the Nizam's practical independence from formal severance.
- In classification questions, distinguish a superior political framework from the later practical autonomy of a subordinate region.
- Do not convert that genealogy into a narrative of the Carnatic Wars.

### Revision notes

1. Nizam-ul-Mulk consolidated Hyderabad after defeating Mubariz Khan in 1724.
2. He had served within the Mughal imperial system.
3. Practical autonomy coexisted with Mughal coin and khutba symbolism.
4. Hyderabad incorporated local rulers through tribute and allegiance.
5. *Peshkash* was a fixed tribute, not the same as ordinary peasant land revenue.
6. Revenue farming and hereditary jagirs diffused operational power.
7. Hindu officials and financiers formed part of the ruling coalition.
8. Elite pluralism did not erase social hierarchy.
9. Succession disputes exposed the fragility of networked authority.
10. Arcot's genealogy must be separated from the later European-war sequence.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**10 marks | 150 words**

**Question:** How did Asaf Jah I combine autonomy with incorporation in building Hyderabad?

#### Model answer

After defeating Mubariz Khan in 1724, Asaf Jah I consolidated a Deccan state without a dramatic declaration of independence. Mughal-derived titles, coinage and *khutba* conventions preserved legitimacy, while the Nizam independently conducted diplomacy, made appointments and distributed ranks and jagirs.

Hyderabad's large and diverse territory made uniform direct administration impractical. The Nizam incorporated local rulers through *peshkash*, allegiance and patronage; revenue farmers, hereditary jagirdars, military nobles, merchants and financiers extended his reach. Recruitment across religious lines also widened the governing coalition.

This arrangement reduced the cost of displacing entrenched authorities and created a recognisable regional hierarchy. However, incorporation preserved substantial operational power below the centre. Hereditary claims, weak supervision and rival patronage networks could become active during succession disputes.

Hyderabad was therefore neither a centrally administered modern state nor a loose collection without authority. Its strength lay in negotiated supremacy; the same networked structure also limited rapid, uniform mobilisation.

## Lesson 4 — Bengal: A Revenue-Commercial State and Its Powerful Partners

Progress: 4 / 17 | Stage: Successor states | Subtopic: Murshid Quli Khan to Alivardi Khan

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Book accounts of Bengal's revenue settlement, zamindars, trade, Jagat Seths and military limits checked.
- **Concept focus:** Bengal's administrative and commercial profile checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; modern Bengal data is not projected backward.
- **Historical scope:** This lesson explains Bengal before Company conquest; the detailed Plassey-to-Diwani sequence is treated separately.

### Visual first: Bengal's power triangle

```text
                    NAWAB / NAZIM
                  order and patronage
                   /              \
                  /                \
       LARGE ZAMINDARS ---------- BANKERS
       collection, rural          credit, remittance,
       influence, security        treasury, mint links
                  \                /
                   \              /
              MERCHANTS AND PRODUCERS
          textiles, silk, inland and oceanic trade
```

Bengal's strength came from cooperation among the court, landed magnates and commercial-financial groups. The same partners could also constrain a nawab.

### 1. The unusual concentration of offices

Murshid Quli Khan first built authority as Bengal's diwan, responsible for revenue, and in 1717 became governor or *nazim*. Holding the revenue and executive offices together weakened the old check between them. He continued to remit revenue to Delhi and did not formally repudiate Mughal authority, yet he organised nearly dynastic rule in practice.

The mechanism was straightforward:

1. survey revenue-paying areas;
2. compel timely payment;
3. punish refractory holders;
4. transfer some jagirdars away;
5. convert parts of assigned land into *khalisa*;
6. rely on a smaller number of powerful zamindars capable of delivery.

This improved the state's receipts, but administrative success for the treasury did not necessarily mean welfare for cultivators.

### 2. The peasant must not disappear from the account

Bipan Chandra records *taccavi*, agricultural loans intended to assist cultivators and revenue payment. He also emphasises coercive collection and the pressure created by revenue farming. Sekhar Bandyopadhyay notes that the scale of increased oppression is difficult to establish conclusively while reporting a marked rise in revenue collection.

The careful inference is:

- Bengal's fiscal system became more effective;
- powerful intermediaries benefited;
- loans and order could support cultivation;
- high or coercive collection could transfer risk downward;
- evidence of state prosperity cannot be treated as proof of peasant prosperity.

### 3. Trade, bullion and production

Bengal exported cotton and silk textiles, sugar, oil and other goods through inland and oceanic networks. European companies brought bullion to purchase Bengal goods because the province maintained a favourable trade balance. Indian merchants included Hindus, Muslims and Armenians; magnates such as Umi Chand and Khoja Wajid operated at large scale.

Bullion did not sit passively in chests. It entered a monetised economy, financed transactions and supported revenue remittance. Export demand could stimulate production, but artisans might still face advances, price pressure or dependence on merchants.

### 4. Why the Jagat Seths mattered

The Jagat Seth banking house provided guarantees and remittance capacity, became closely linked to the provincial treasury and obtained strategic influence around the mint. Bankers helped make regular revenue transmission possible. Their resources could also affect court politics.

Alivardi Khan's rise against Sarfaraz Khan in 1739-40 was assisted by powerful bankers, zamindars and officials. This does not mean "bankers ruled Bengal." It means political authority rested on a coalition, and a nawab who lost major pillars of that coalition became vulnerable.

### 5. Administration and inclusion

Murshid Quli Khan and his successors employed both Hindus and Muslims in high civil and military posts. Local zamindars and *mahajans* gained importance. Again, this was elite incorporation rather than social equality. It shows that economic function, loyalty and administrative competence frequently cut across a simplistic religious explanation.

### 6. Capacity and its limit

Bengal offered peace, revenue and thriving trade, but its military establishment was not proportionate to the long-term threat posed by armed European companies. Alivardi faced repeated Maratha incursions and ultimately ceded Orissa and accepted a chauth arrangement in 1751. The point is not to preview Company conquest. It is to identify an imbalance: **high commercial-fiscal value without equivalent autonomous military transformation**.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Bengal's later conquest proves that its apparent prosperity was institutional weakness in disguise.

**Reply:** Later defeat cannot erase earlier administrative capacity or economic vitality. Prosperity created resources; it did not guarantee that rulers would identify, organise against or politically isolate every new threat.

**Qualified verdict:** Bengal was a viable and wealthy regional state whose coalition-based strength and military-political vulnerabilities existed simultaneously.

### UPSC integration

- Distinguish revenue efficiency from peasant welfare.
- Connect merchant-bankers to state capacity, not merely private trade.
- Use Bengal as the strongest case against a universal "dark age."
- Keep the focus on the nawabi state's own political economy rather than narrating the later conquest mechanism.

Awadh's rulers did not simply destroy local magnates. They made submission and regular payment the terms of continued possession.

### 1. Saadat Khan's initial problem

Appointed governor in 1722, Saadat Khan faced rajas and zamindars who withheld revenue, maintained forts and private forces, and defied provincial authority. He used military coercion to establish order. Yet many defeated zamindars were confirmed in their estates after agreeing to pay.

This pattern matters:

```text
revolt -> military suppression -> negotiated confirmation -> revenue payment
                                      |
                              possibility of renewed revolt
```

It was an equilibrium, not a final abolition of landed power.

### 2. Fiscal consolidation

Saadat Khan introduced a new revenue settlement. Sources differ in emphasis: one stresses a substantial increase in demand and the creation of a new regional elite; another credits him with more equitable assessment and protection against large zamindars. These claims can be reconciled only cautiously.

The state sought greater and more reliable receipts. Its impact varied by locality, status and enforcement. A stronger provincial treasury could protect some cultivators from arbitrary chiefs while simultaneously raising the total claim on agrarian output.

### 3. A diverse ruling coalition

Awadh's emerging elite included Indian Muslims, Afghans and Hindus. Punjabi Khatri officials handled important fiscal responsibilities. High office was not reserved by a single religious identity; Maharaja Nawab Rai held a leading position under Safdar Jung.

Political inclusion served state-building. It should not be inflated into modern secular citizenship, but neither should Awadh's politics be narrated as a religious bloc.

### 4. Imperial office as regional resource

Saadat Khan and Safdar Jung remained entangled in Mughal court politics. Safdar Jung became *wazir* in 1748. Imperial appointment offered titles, alliances and opportunities for territorial gain. Yet service at Delhi could also consume attention and generate enemies.

Awadh's rulers therefore used the empire while reducing their financial dependence on it. Formal recognition and practical autonomy reinforced one another until the connection became increasingly thin.

### 5. Trade, towns and Lucknow culture

The province benefited from rich agriculture and trade. Peace and elite prosperity supported the rise of a distinct court culture, especially after Lucknow became the seat of the nawabs in 1775. Arts, literature and handicrafts clustered around patronage.

The example shows a general rule: political decentralisation could **relocate** cultural production. Delhi's relative decline did not mean the end of court culture; artists, poets, officials and craftspeople moved toward regional patrons.

The limit is equally important. Courtly splendour reveals concentration of surplus and cultural production, not the average condition of every peasant or artisan.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Repeated zamindar resistance proves the Awadh state was administratively hollow.

**Reply:** Recurrent resistance demonstrates the bargaining power of landed groups, not the absence of government. Saadat Khan enlarged revenue and created a durable dynasty; local magnates remained difficult because they were embedded in rural society and possessed armed capacity.

**Qualified verdict:** Awadh was a viable negotiated state whose strength depended on managing, rather than permanently eliminating, taluqdari power.

### 1. Shared architecture

All three:

- originated in Mughal provincial government;
- avoided a dramatic early repudiation of the emperor;
- made offices or succession increasingly hereditary;
- controlled appointments and revenue locally;
- recruited across religious lines;
- relied on intermediaries rather than a fully direct bureaucracy;

Bengal's distinctive advantage was the density of production, bullion-linked trade and sophisticated finance. Awadh's challenge lay in governing a wealthy countryside populated by powerful landed chiefs. Hyderabad's scale and Deccan diversity favoured incorporation through tribute and patronage.

Efficiency can mean:

- more revenue reaches the treasury;
- roads and markets are protected;
- officials receive salaries;
- disputes are resolved;
- armies mobilise on time.

These goals can conflict. A system efficient at extraction may burden cultivators. A ruler who leaves local chiefs in place may preserve order cheaply but reduce direct control. A commercially open province may gain customs while exposing itself to armed companies.

UPSC answers improve when "efficient" is replaced by the precise function being discussed.

### 4. Shared strongest limit

None of the three escaped the structural dependence on landed surplus, intermediaries and personal-patronage politics. None built a political mechanism for sustained coordination with the other regional powers against a changing Company threat. That does not mean they were all equally weak or doomed.

**Reply:** Comparison is useful only when it explains divergence. The point is not that every box is the same; it is that a shared provincial inheritance was recombined with different ecologies, elite structures and commercial networks.

**Qualified verdict:** Successor states were variations on an imperial inheritance, not miniature copies of one another.

## Lesson 7 — Maratha Power I: From Shahu and the Peshwas to Northward Expansion

Progress: 7 / 17 | Stage: New formations | Subtopic: Political reconstruction and the Peshwa transition

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Bipan Chandra and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay accounts of Shahu, Tarabai, Balaji Vishwanath and Baji Rao I checked.
- **Concept focus:** Maratha genealogy and fiscal terminology checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Historical scope:** The later Anglo-Maratha war and annexation sequence falls beyond this lesson's state-formation analysis.

### Visual first: a kingdom becomes an expanding confederated power

```text
Shivaji's regional kingdom
        |
succession conflict: Shahu <-> Tarabai
        |
Balaji Vishwanath builds coalition and secures Mughal sanads
        |
Peshwa becomes centre of patronage and administration
        |
Baji Rao I expands claims into Malwa, Gujarat and Bundelkhand
        |
military success + delegated collection + stronger sardars
```

Expansion increased Maratha power and simultaneously empowered the chiefs who would make central coordination difficult.

### 1. The succession problem

After Aurangzeb's death, Shahu's release produced conflict with Tarabai's Kolhapur line. Maratha sardars shifted allegiance among contenders and Mughal authorities. Shahu prevailed with help from new sardars, Brahman banking families and Balaji Vishwanath, whom he made Peshwa in 1713.

The Peshwa began as chief minister but became the functional centre of power. This change was not merely personal ambition. The office coordinated patronage, negotiation, revenue claims and military expansion more effectively than a divided royal house.

### 2. Mughal sanction and Maratha expansion

Balaji Vishwanath worked with the Sayyid brothers and in 1719 secured recognition of Shahu's claims, including *chauth* and *sardeshmukhi* in the six Deccan provinces.

Plain-language distinction:

- **chauth:** a claim commonly expressed as one-fourth of assessed revenue, often paid to avoid Maratha exaction or obtain protection;
- **sardeshmukhi:** an additional claim, conventionally one-tenth, associated with the Maratha ruler's superior regional right.

They were not ordinary village land taxes identical to the state's basic assessment. They were political-fiscal claims layered upon existing revenue systems.

### 3. Baji Rao I and mobility

Baji Rao I, Peshwa from 1720 to 1740, used speed, cavalry and strategic campaigning to extend influence. The victory at Palkhed in 1728 forced the Nizam to recognise Shahu's Deccan claims. Maratha power spread into Malwa, Gujarat and Bundelkhand.

Mobility allowed armies to demand tribute across a wide area. But conquest did not immediately create civil administration. Negotiating with existing zamindars was faster than replacing them; revenue collection could precede territorial integration.

### 4. A change in the character of the state

Under the early Peshwas:

- the office became hereditary;
- Poona emerged as the effective political centre;
- Maratha chiefs received spheres for collection and expansion;
- Gaekwad, Holkar, Sindhia and Bhonsle houses rose;
- the state moved from a Maharashtra kingdom toward a subcontinental field of claims.

This was impressive capacity. Its institutional limit was built into the method: chiefs raised their own forces and retained much of the collection needed to sustain them.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If Maratha rulers sought Mughal *sanads*, they were not genuine rebels.

**Reply:** Rebellion and incorporation were not mutually exclusive in an imperial order with overlapping rights. Sanction could legitimise gains won by force. The Marathas challenged imperial command while using imperial legal-political language.

**Qualified verdict:** Maratha power was both a regional challenge to the Mughal state and a participant in its surviving framework of legitimacy.

### UPSC integration

- Explain Peshwa ascendancy as institutional concentration, not just palace intrigue.
- Use Palkhed as evidence of strategic mobility, not as a full military narrative.
- Distinguish expansion of claims from consolidation of administration.

## Lesson 8 — Maratha Power II: Confederacy, Fiscal Claims and the Limits of Integration

Progress: 8 / 17 | Stage: New formations | Subtopic: Structure, Panipat and recovery

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Concept focus:** Confederated structure and non-finality of Panipat checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Historical scope:** Later Company wars are relevant only when assessing the confederacy's structural limits.

### Visual first: strength and weakness came from the same design

```text
                     PESHWA AT POONA
                           |
          +----------------+----------------+
          |                |                |
       Sindhia           Holkar          Gaekwad
      Gwalior/Delhi      Indore          Baroda
          \                |                /
           \               |               /
                 Bhonsle of Nagpur

Each house: army + revenue base + territorial ambitions
Shared Maratha identity and interests, but no automatic unified command
```

The confederacy multiplied centres of initiative. It also multiplied vetoes, rivalries and separate calculations.

### 1. Vatan and saranjam

Two layers of rights mattered:

- **vatan:** hereditary local rights and offices rooted in regional society;
- **saranjam:** assignments granted for service, especially military maintenance, comparable in some respects to jagirs.

The Peshwas attempted to build vertical service ties through assignments, but older local rights did not disappear. Individuals might possess a bundle of fiscal, hereditary and service claims. Power therefore remained distributed among the centre, sardars, local officeholders and village elites.

### 2. Expansion under Nana Saheb

Balaji Baji Rao, or Nana Saheb, became Peshwa in 1740. Maratha influence reached its widest extent through campaigns, tribute demands and intervention in succession disputes. The Mughal emperor came under Maratha protection; Maratha forces moved into Punjab and repeatedly entered eastern and northern regions.

Yet influence had different depths:

| Zone | Typical form of Maratha power |
|---|---|
| Maharashtra heartland | Denser political and fiscal institutions |
| Malwa, Gujarat, Khandesh | More sustained administration and revenue arrangements |
| Distant northern or eastern zones | Campaigns, tribute, protection and political intervention, often without deep integration |

Map colour alone would conceal these differences.

### 3. Panipat without myth

At the Third Battle of Panipat on 14 January 1761, Ahmad Shah Abdali's coalition defeated the Maratha army. Losses and political shock were severe. The defeat damaged prestige and removed experienced leaders.

But three propositions must be separated:

1. Panipat was a grave military and political setback.
2. Abdali did not create a lasting north Indian empire from the victory.
3. Maratha power recovered under Madhav Rao and reasserted influence in north India.

Therefore, "Panipat ended Maratha power" is false. It exposed coalition failure, hostile regional memories, supply difficulties and command problems.

### 4. Why northern allies were scarce

Maratha exactions and interventions had alienated Rajput, Jat, Awadh and other powers. Heavy tribute demands made potential allies fear Maratha supremacy. The Sikh chiefs had their own interests. A wide sphere of military reach had not produced a widely accepted political compact.

This is a central distinction:

> Capacity to compel payment is not the same as capacity to build consent.

### 5. Administration and confederate autonomy

The leading houses developed regular administrations and armies. Their success should not be dismissed as mere plunder. Yet separate fiscal bases encouraged separate diplomacy. Poona politics, rivalries among chiefs and personal succession disputes reduced coordinated strategy.

The confederacy was not uniquely irrational. It was a workable solution to expansion across a vast, diverse space. Its limits became acute against an opponent that could combine central financial direction, naval access, disciplined infantry, diplomacy and sequential war.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Factionalism alone explains Maratha failure.

**Reply:** Factionalism mattered but is insufficient. The Company exploited fiscal resources, diplomatic sequencing and military organisation; Maratha states also recovered repeatedly and fielded formidable forces.

**Qualified verdict:** Confederated autonomy enabled rapid expansion but made sustained collective strategy costly. Failure emerged from the interaction of internal coordination problems with a strengthening external adversary.

### UPSC integration

- Never write that Panipat ended Maratha power.
- Use "depth of control" rather than treating every claimed region alike.
- Explain why extraction without legitimacy limited alliance-building.
- Reserve the detailed Anglo-Maratha war sequence for the later study of territorial expansion.

## Lesson 9 — Sikh Transformation: Khalsa, Banda Bahadur and the Misls

Progress: 9 / 17 | Stage: New formations | Subtopic: From community to regional power

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Khalsa formation, Banda's mobilisation, Dal Khalsa and misl organisation checked.
- **Concept focus:** Sikh political transformation and agrarian base checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Historical scope:** This lesson ends with eighteenth-century Sikh formation; later wars and annexation are not narrated.

### Visual first: identity becomes organisation

```text
Guru Nanak's devotional community
             |
institutional growth under successive Gurus
             |
Guru Hargobind: stronger armed dimension
             |
Guru Gobind Singh, 1699: Khalsa discipline and public identity
             |
Banda Bahadur: agrarian-military territorial challenge
             |
persecution, dispersal and regrouping
             |
Dal Khalsa / jathas / misls -> regional territorial power
```

Religious identity supplied solidarity, but institutions and changing Punjab politics converted it into durable armed capacity.

### 1. The Khalsa's political work

Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699. Initiation, visible symbols, discipline and the idea of authority vested in the Panth and Granth helped order a community under pressure. The Khalsa did not include every Sikh; *sahajdharis* and other groups remained part of the wider Panth.

The political significance lay in:

- a shared public identity;
- a disciplined brotherhood;
- reduced dependence on intermediary *masands*;
- a moral language of equality;
- readiness for collective defence.

It would be reductionist to describe the Khalsa as merely an army. It would be equally incomplete to ignore its military-political consequences.

### 2. Banda Bahadur and agrarian mobilisation

After Guru Gobind Singh's death in 1708, Banda Bahadur led a major challenge. Small Jat zamindars and peasants formed an important support base, though Punjab society was divided. Banda established territorial administration in captured areas, appointed officers and issued seals and coin.

Khatri commercial groups did not uniformly support him. Stability of routes and ties to revenue farming could align some with Mughal authority. Some Jat zamindars also opposed Banda. Community identity did not erase class, occupation or local interest.

His defeat and execution in 1716 were severe, but they did not end Sikh political development.

### 3. From dispersal to misls

Nadir Shah's and Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasions disrupted Punjab's administration. Sikh armed bands regrouped, acquired resources and filled spaces left by retreating armies. The *Dal Khalsa* coordinated forces; *jathas* developed into territorial *misls*.

The conventional classification speaks of twelve misls. Treat that number as a standard historical grouping, not as proof that organisation was eternally fixed. Chiefs and territorial relations changed.

Misls combined:

- kinship and personal following;
- armed mobility;
- shared Khalsa institutions;
- local revenue collection;
- cooperation against major threats;
- rivalry over territory and leadership.

### 4. Equality and hierarchy

Early misl practice retained participatory elements and a strong brotherhood ethos. Over time, powerful chiefs accumulated land and authority. Equality as a community ideal coexisted with social and political differentiation.

This pattern prevents two errors:

- romantic error: the polity permanently abolished hierarchy;
- cynical error: equality had no historical effect.

The ideal shaped mobilisation; material power reshaped the ideal.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Sikh state formation can be explained entirely by Mughal and Afghan repression.

**Reply:** Repression and invasion created pressures and opportunities, but they do not explain the community's internal institutions, agrarian support, leadership and capacity to regroup.

**Qualified verdict:** Sikh power emerged from the interaction of religious-political institution building, rural social change, armed resistance and imperial disruption.

### UPSC integration

- Separate Khalsa, Banda and misl stages.
- Avoid saying every Sikh was Khalsa.
- Qualify the conventional count of twelve misls.
- Use class and occupational differences to complicate a purely communal narrative.

## Lesson 10 — Jats, Rohillas and Rajputs: Three North Indian Paths

- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.

### Visual first: do not merge unlike states

| Formation | Social-political base | Route to authority | Central caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jat Bharatpur | Agriculturists, zamindar leadership, armed rural mobilisation | Revolt, fortification, territorial consolidation | Peasant origin did not prevent later extraction or elite rule |
| Rohilkhand/Farrukhabad | Afghan military migrants and commanders | Settlement, service, armed entrepreneurship and territorial control | Rohilkhand was not carved from land occupied by Ahmad Shah Durrani |
| Rajput states | Established dynasties, watan rights and regional courts | Withdrawal from central control, provincial office and local expansion | Rajputana remained politically divided |

The comparison prevents the vague label "rebel state" from hiding distinct genealogies.

### 1. Jat mobilisation and Bharatpur

Jat cultivators around Mathura, Agra and Delhi faced pressure from Mughal officials and mobilised under zamindar leaders. Earlier revolts were suppressed, but durable power developed under Churaman and Badan Singh. Suraj Mal, ruling in the mid-eighteenth century, expanded Bharatpur and gained a reputation for effective revenue and civil administration.

The sequence was:

```text
agrarian grievance + zamindar leadership
              -> armed mobilisation
              -> forts and territorial control
              -> ruling house and revenue administration
```

A movement with peasant participation could produce a new elite state. Social origin does not freeze later class character.

### 2. Rohillas and Afghan military settlement

Afghan military migrants and adventurers established principalities in north India. Muhammad Khan Bangash developed Farrukhabad. Ali Muhammad Khan consolidated Rohilkhand in the Himalayan foothill region between the Ganga and Kumaon hills.

The critical correction for Prelims is:

> Rohilkhand arose through Afghan settlement and military-political consolidation; it was not formed from territories occupied by Ahmad Shah Durrani.

The label "Afghan" does not make every Afghan-origin polity a territorial creation of the Durrani invasion.

### 3. Rajput autonomy and division

Rajput rulers used Mughal weakness to enlarge autonomy and sometimes held governorships of provinces such as Malwa or Gujarat. Yet Rajputana did not act as a unified bloc. Larger states competed with weaker neighbours; succession struggles, court intrigue and rivalry persisted.

Sawai Jai Singh of Amber stands out for statecraft, Jaipur's planned urban development and astronomical observatories at Jaipur, Delhi, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura. His work demonstrates serious scientific and mathematical patronage.

Its limit: one exceptional ruler and a network of observatories do not prove a general scientific revolution in eighteenth-century India.

### 4. Political identity and material interest

Jat, Afghan and Rajput identities mattered in mobilisation and legitimacy. None supplied automatic unity. Jat leaders fought other Jats; Afghan principalities competed; Rajput houses pursued separate dynastic interests. Political coalitions turned on tribute, territory, succession and security as well as identity.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** These smaller formations were marginal beside Bengal or the Marathas.

**Reply:** Scale is not the only measure of historical significance. They controlled routes, supplied allies and enemies, altered revenue flows, challenged larger powers and reveal how local social groups converted armed capacity into states.

**Qualified verdict:** Their importance lies both in regional power and in the variety of state-formation processes they expose.

- Avoid reading peasant origin as permanent peasant control.
- Use Jai Singh as evidence of selective scientific vitality, with an explicit limit.

## Lesson 11 — Mysore and Travancore: Southern Fiscal-Military Experiments

Progress: 11 / 17 | Stage: Independent kingdoms | Subtopic: Centralisation, commerce and military innovation

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Haidar Ali, Tipu Sultan, Martanda Varma and Travancore evidence checked.
- **Concept focus:** Independent-kingdom typology and modernisation limits checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Historical scope:** Mysore's institutional capacities are assessed here; the full Anglo-Mysore war sequence is treated separately.

### Visual first: two routes from commerce to force

```text
MYSORE
state revenue -> arsenal and disciplined army -> territorial control
      -> state-supported trade and manufacture -> renewed resources

TRAVANCORE
pepper/coastal commerce -> central revenue -> European-style army
      -> defeat/subordination of rivals -> roads, canals, irrigation
```

Both states connected economic resources to coercive and administrative capacity, but on different scales and through different institutions.

### 1. Mysore's Vijayanagara genealogy is not a Mughal-successor genealogy

The Wodeyar kingdom of Mysore emerged within the political field created by the decline of the Vijayanagara Empire and preserved a precarious regional existence thereafter. By the early eighteenth century, ministers Nanjaraj and Devraj dominated a weakened court. Haidar Ali rose through military service and established effective authority in 1761.

Thus the safe genealogy is:

- conventional political genealogy connects Mysore's emergence to the Vijayanagara imperial order;
- Mysore was not a Mughal successor province such as Bengal, Awadh or Hyderabad;
- Haidar Ali took control of an existing kingdom rather than founding it from imperial debris.

### 2. Haidar Ali's state-building

Haidar recognised the value of European drill and technical expertise. He established an arsenal at Dindigul in 1755 with French assistance, disciplined forces and subdued *poligars*. His administration combined military expansion with control of revenue.

Military borrowing was selective. Mysore did not become European; it appropriated useful methods within an Indian political economy.

### 3. Tipu Sultan's innovations

Tipu experimented with coinage, calendar, weights and measures, state trade, embassies and support for manufacture. He attempted to reduce jagir grants and strengthen state income. Mysore manufactured weapons and used disciplined infantry and artillery.

His land-revenue demand could be high, reportedly reaching around one-third of gross produce in some accounts. State-directed development therefore coexisted with substantial agrarian extraction.

Tipu's religious record must be handled without propaganda. He was an orthodox Muslim ruler who patronised Hindu institutions including the Sringeri temple after its devastation and employed Hindus in government. Coercive actions in particular political conflicts should not be converted into a timeless single-cause portrait; nor should patronage erase every episode of violence.

### 4. Travancore under Martanda Varma

Martanda Varma, ruling from 1729, subdued feudatories, defeated the Dutch at Colachel in 1741 and expanded Travancore. The state organised a stronger army with European assistance, developed an arsenal, encouraged trade and invested in roads, canals and irrigation.

Pepper commerce supplied an important revenue base. Control over trade helped finance centralisation; centralisation helped secure production and routes.

Travancore's scale was smaller than Mysore's, and Kerala's political geography differed. It should not be treated as a miniature copy.

### 5. Capacity and limits

Both polities challenge the claim that Indian rulers ignored all military and commercial innovation. Yet neither proves that the subcontinent experienced a general industrial or scientific transformation. Innovation remained selective, state-specific and constrained by fiscal pressure, geopolitical competition and limited institutional diffusion.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Mysore eventually lost to the Company, its reforms were superficial.

**Reply:** Outcome does not measure whether reforms were real. Mysore became the Company's most formidable southern opponent and developed significant military-industrial capacities. Defeat resulted from coalition warfare, resources and strategic circumstances, not the nonexistence of reform.

**Qualified verdict:** Mysore and Travancore were serious fiscal-military experiments, not completed modern nation-states or industrial economies.

### UPSC integration

- Treat Colachel as evidence of Travancore's capacity, not as a substitute for explaining its fiscal base.
- Balance Tipu's innovation, extraction and contested political-religious record.
- Do not narrate all Anglo-Mysore wars here.

## Lesson 12 — Agrarian Society: Villages, Peasants, Zamindars and the Cost of the State

Progress: 12 / 17 | Stage: Society and economy | Subtopic: Rural relations and differentiated burdens

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Peasant categories, zamindar levels, revenue pressure and village relations checked.
- **Concept focus:** Agrarian base and regional variation checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; present land categories are not mapped backward.
- **Prior dependency:** Lesson 2's extraction mechanism is assumed.

### Visual first: the agrarian ladder was not a simple two-class line

```text
RULER / TREASURY
       |
jagirdar, revenue farmer, provincial official
       |
large or intermediary zamindar / taluqdar / deshmukh
       |
primary zamindar, village headman, accountant, panchayat
       |
khudkasht cultivator | pahikasht/mobile cultivator | labouring poor
       |
LAND, CROP, LIVESTOCK, FAMILY LABOUR
```

Claims could overlap, and the same actor might hold more than one kind of right.

### 1. The village was connected, not isolated

The phrase "self-sufficient village" can mislead. Villages produced much of what they consumed, but they paid revenue, bought salt and metals, sold crops, supplied textiles or labour, borrowed from moneylenders and interacted with towns and armies.

Village institutions could organise irrigation, watch, customary service and dispute settlement. They were not egalitarian republics. Caste, landholding, credit and office produced unequal voice.

### 2. Differentiated cultivators

Book evidence distinguishes *khudkasht* peasants with more settled occupancy connections from *pahikasht* or mobile/non-resident cultivators. Larger peasants could possess cattle, seed and resilience; smallholders and labourers faced greater vulnerability.

Therefore "the peasant" is an abstraction. The effect of the same revenue demand varied with:

- size and quality of holding;
- occupancy or customary right;
- access to irrigation and credit;
- crop prices;
- family labour;
- proximity to war or raiding;
- power of the collecting intermediary.

### 3. Zamindar: landlord, collector, chief, or all three?

Zamindars ranged from major regional rulers to primary local landholders. They might possess forts and armed followers, collect revenue and claim community ties with cultivators. Their position allowed them to mediate between state and village.

They could protect peasants from distant officials, organise resistance or intensify extraction. No universal moral label captures the institution.

### 4. Revenue farming and short time horizons

Under *ijaradari*, collection rights were contracted to a revenue farmer for a fixed payment. If the farmer expected a short tenure, the incentive was to collect quickly and heavily. The state gained predictable contracted revenue; the cultivator bore the risk of over-assessment and coercion.

Yet not every farmer behaved identically, and direct collection was not automatically humane. The mechanism identifies an incentive, not an invariant outcome.

### 5. War, mobility and resilience

Campaigns could destroy standing crops, seize cattle, interrupt markets or increase extraordinary levies. Peasants also adapted:

- shifted cultivation or residence;
- sought protection under a different chief;
- joined armed bands;
- became soldiers;
- used community ties to bargain collectively;
- reclaimed land in expanding agrarian zones.

Rural people were constrained agents, not passive scenery.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Regional commercial growth proves that peasant conditions broadly improved.

**Reply:** More output and trade can coexist with unequal distribution. A cultivator may produce more while the state, landlord, creditor or merchant captures much of the gain.

**Qualified verdict:** The century contained agrarian expansion in some regions and acute pressure in others; productivity, state revenue and peasant welfare must be measured separately.

### UPSC integration

- Never equate state revenue with rural prosperity.
- Define the level of zamindar being discussed.
- Use ijaradari to explain incentive and risk, not as a universal single cause.
- Add peasant agency without romanticising resistance.

5. Zamindars ranged from chiefs to local collectors.

8. Short collection horizons could encourage over-extraction.
9. Warfare harmed crops, cattle, routes and market access.
10. Peasants could migrate, resist, bargain or enter military labour.
11. Output, revenue and welfare are separate variables.

## Lesson 13 — Artisans, Merchants, Bankers and Towns: The Commercial Web

Progress: 13 / 17 | Stage: Society and economy | Subtopic: Production, circulation and urban change

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Trade routes, manufactures, bullion, merchant communities and urban relocation checked.
- **Concept focus:** Commercial vitality and merchant-financier politics checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Prior dependency:** Bengal's finance and the state-formation engine are assumed.

### Visual first: from loom to treasury

```text
cultivator grows cotton / mulberry
             |
spinner, weaver, dyer, finisher
             |
merchant or dadani advance
             |
inland market / port / company factory
             |
Asian and European demand -> bullion or credit inflow
             |
banker, customs house and state treasury
```

Commercial expansion depended on a chain. Power at one link could shift risk to weaker participants.

### 1. India remained a major manufacturing economy

Cotton and silk textiles remained internationally desired. Important production centres included Dacca, Murshidabad, Patna, Surat, Ahmedabad, Varanasi, Lucknow, Lahore, Masulipatam and other regional towns. Shipbuilding flourished in Bengal, western India and parts of the southern coast. Metal goods, saltpetre, sugar, indigo, oils and other commodities entered extensive trade.

This evidence rejects the idea that manufacturing vanished with Mughal political contraction.

### 2. Political disruption was spatially selective

Delhi, Mathura, Surat and other centres experienced sack, changing patronage or insecure routes. New customs barriers could raise transaction costs. Yet oceanic trade and European demand stimulated production in other regions. Artisans migrated from declining courts to Hyderabad, Lucknow, Patna and regional centres.

The correct map contains:

- damaged corridors;
- resilient old centres;
- expanding export zones;
- newly patronised regional capitals.

### 3. Merchants were political actors

Merchants needed roads, contract enforcement, predictable customs and protection. States needed customs, supplies, intelligence and loans. This produced bargaining. Merchants could shift routes, withhold credit or support a claimant; rulers could grant privileges or impose levies.

Commercial communities were diverse: Hindu banias, Muslim merchants, Armenians and others operated across regions. Their identities mattered, but business partnerships and political calculations often crossed religious lines.

### 4. Advances and artisan dependence

The *dadani* system involved advances to producers. An advance could provide working capital and assured demand. It could also bind an artisan to deliver at a controlled price, especially where buyers possessed coercive or monopsonistic power.

Example:

```text
advance before production
        -> raw material and subsistence secured
        -> obligation to deliver specified cloth
        -> benefit if price and terms are fair
        -> dependence if buyer suppresses price or blocks alternatives
```

The example establishes ambiguity. It does not prove that every advance was exploitative or beneficial.

### 5. Bankers and information

Bankers did more than lend. Their correspondence and branches carried information about prices, harvests, political stability and creditworthiness. *Hundis* reduced the need to move large quantities of coin physically. A trusted network could transmit money across political borders.

This "commercial infrastructure" made fragmented sovereignty economically permeable. Political frontiers multiplied customs and risks, but did not seal regions into isolated economies.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since trade continued, political fragmentation had little economic effect.

**Reply:** Continued trade does not mean costless trade. Warfare, plunder, multiple customs posts and elite impoverishment damaged routes and luxury demand. The effect varied by commodity and location.

**Qualified verdict:** Eighteenth-century commerce was resilient and regionally dynamic, but its gains were uneven and its networks operated under rising political risk.

- Explain the two-sided effect of merchant advances.
- Link bankers to information and remittance as well as loans.
- Reject both "total collapse" and "unbroken prosperity."

## Lesson 14 — Caste, Gender and Education: Hierarchy with Regional Variation

Progress: 14 / 17 | Stage: Society and economy | Subtopic: Social structure and access to knowledge

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Caste divisions, women's work, marriage, widowhood and educational institutions checked.
- **Concept focus:** Social differentiation and regional variation checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; modern constitutional categories are not retrojected.
- **Method alert:** Normative rules are distinguished from varied social practice.

### Visual first: status was layered, not binary

```text
RELIGION
  |
  +-- Hindu: varna claims + many regional jatis
  |
  +-- Muslim: ashraf/ajlaf distinctions + lineage, sect and occupation

Across both:
class/wealth + gender + region + rural/urban location + political office
                              |
                    lived social position
```

No single identity axis explains the whole hierarchy.

### 1. Caste and mobility

Jati rules shaped marriage, dining, occupation and social rank. Panchayats and caste heads could impose fines, penance or exclusion. Yet practice varied by region, and political or economic success could raise a family's or group's standing. The Holkar family's ascent is a reminder that power could alter status, though such mobility did not dissolve the system.

Muslim society also contained hierarchy: lineage, status, sect and occupation separated *ashraf* elites from groups treated as *ajlaf*. Conversion did not necessarily erase inherited social distinctions.

### 2. Gender: patriarchy with different labour regimes

Most family systems were patriarchal, while parts of Kerala followed matrilineal inheritance. Upper-class women were more likely to experience seclusion in northern settings; peasant and poorer women often worked in fields, crafts or service because household survival required it.

This produces an important correction:

> Greater visibility in labour does not necessarily mean greater control over property, marriage or income.

Elite women such as Ahilyabai Holkar could exercise major political authority. Their significance is real, but exceptional rulers do not measure ordinary women's autonomy.

### 3. Marriage, widowhood and sati

Early marriage and arranged marriage were widespread. Dowry and expensive ceremonies placed pressure on some groups. Sati occurred especially among certain upper-status families in parts of Rajputana, Bengal and northern India; it was not a universal Hindu practice. Widow remarriage varied: it was prohibited among many upper castes but remained accepted among numerous non-Brahman, Jat and hill communities.

Regional and caste qualification is mandatory. A statement beginning "Indian women universally..." is almost always analytically unsafe.

### 4. Education

Elementary instruction existed in village and town schools and in *maktabs*. Reading, writing and arithmetic served practical needs. Higher learning centred on Sanskrit, Persian, law, religion, philosophy and logic, often supported by rulers, zamindars and wealthy patrons.

Access was unequal:

- higher castes and prosperous groups had advantages;
- Persian learning opened administrative opportunity to Hindus and Muslims;
- girls' education was limited, with elite exceptions;
- curricula gave limited space to rapidly changing natural science, technology and geography.

The older claim that education was simply absent is wrong. The stronger criticism concerns restricted access, traditional curriculum and limited institutional response to global scientific change.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Examples of mobility and women rulers show that hierarchy was flexible enough to be unimportant.

**Reply:** Exceptions reveal possible agency, not the disappearance of structural constraints. A woman ruler or upwardly mobile house operated within a society where most property, office and educational opportunity remained unequal.

**Qualified verdict:** Eighteenth-century society combined durable hierarchy with negotiated practice, regional diversity and limited channels of mobility.

- Separate labour participation from autonomy.
- Treat sati as socially and regionally concentrated, not universal.
- Describe education as present but unequal and intellectually constrained.

## Lesson 15 — Culture, Knowledge and Coexistence: Relocation, Not Silence


Cultural centres multiplied even as some older institutions lost resources.

### 1. Persianate continuity and regionalisation

Persian remained a major language of administration and elite culture. Regional courts adopted Mughal-derived etiquette, titles, literature and artistic forms. Urdu grew as a language of social interaction and poetry in northern India, with figures such as Mir and Sauda.

Persianate does not mean "foreign" or "Muslim only." Hindus wrote in Persian; Muslims wrote in Hindi, Bengali and other vernaculars. Shared courtly forms were produced locally by diverse participants.

### 2. Regional cultural vitality

Painters moved from the Mughal centre to provincial courts. Kangra and Rajput painting developed distinctive vitality. Lucknow supported music, poetry and crafts. Travancore patronised Malayalam and Sanskrit learning; Kunchan Nambiar's work and the development of Kathakali traditions indicate a lively regional sphere. Punjabi, Sindhi, Assamese, Tamil and Gujarati literary production continued.

This does not mean every genre flourished equally. Patron-dependent forms could decline when courts were impoverished, and some literature became highly conventional.

### 3. Science and technology

Jai Singh's observatories and translations of mathematical works demonstrate active knowledge exchange. Mysore's arsenals, weapon manufacture and military organisation show technical adaptation. Indian shipbuilding remained highly skilled.

The wider limitation was institutional:

- scientific inquiry did not diffuse broadly through education;
- rulers often sought military techniques more readily than a general scientific culture;
- experimental and industrial transformations occurring in western Europe were not matched at comparable scale.

Avoid civilisational fatalism. The issue is not an absence of intelligence; it is the breadth, organisation and cumulative institutional support for knowledge.

### 4. Religious coexistence

Political alliances rarely followed a stable Hindu-versus-Muslim line. Hindu and Muslim officials served rulers of different faiths; festivals, shrines, literary subjects and everyday practices crossed community boundaries. Bhakti and Sufi traditions supported shared devotional spaces.

Yet "composite culture" must not become a claim of perfect harmony. Sectarian disputes, status hierarchy and political violence existed. Coexistence means sustained interaction and overlapping practices, not the absence of boundaries.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling culture vibrant ignores traditionalism and scientific weakness.

**Reply:** Vitality and limitation concern different dimensions. A society can create powerful poetry, painting, music and regional literature while failing to institutionalise a broad scientific transformation.

**Qualified verdict:** Eighteenth-century culture was regionally creative and socially interconnected, but knowledge systems remained uneven and insufficiently transformed in science and technology.

## Lesson 16 — Decline Versus Transformation: How Historians Disagree

Progress: 16 / 17 | Stage: Interpretation | Subtopic: Conventional, materialist and revisionist readings

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Bipan Chandra's structural critique and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay's conventional-revisionist debate checked.
- **Concept focus:** Historiographical positions and qualified synthesis checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required.
- **Method alert:** Interpretations are evaluated against regional and social evidence, not chosen as slogans.

### Visual first: three lenses on the same century

| Lens | Main question | Strong evidence | Blind spot if used alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Imperial decline / "dark age" | What was lost when central power contracted? | invasions, warfare, fiscal disorder, weakened Delhi | underestimates regional states and commercial vitality |
| Materialist / agrarian crisis | What happened to surplus, jagirs and peasants? | jagirdari pressure, revenue competition, rural revolt | may overgeneralise crisis across prosperous regions |
| Regional / revisionist transformation | Which new groups captured resources and power? | zamindars, merchants, bankers, regional elites, expanding zones | can understate violence, extraction and imperial cohesion |

The lenses are strongest when made to correct one another.

### 1. The conventional decline thesis

Older narratives emphasised weak emperors, factional nobles, foreign invasions, military stagnation and political disorder. They captured the dramatic contraction of Mughal command and the suffering of Delhi and war-torn regions.

Their weakness was scale. The decline of one centre became the decline of "India." Bengal's surplus, regional courts and expanding powers appeared only as fragments of chaos.

### 2. Materialist explanations

Historians such as Satish Chandra and Irfan Habib highlighted the jagirdari system, agrarian surplus and conflict between state, nobles, zamindars and peasants. Too many claimants, unstable assignments and difficult collection weakened the ruling apparatus. Rural resistance and zamindar assertion became structurally intelligible.

This lens restores the material base beneath court intrigue. Its limit appears where revenue, cultivation and trade expanded. Crisis may concern distribution and imperial appropriation rather than simple absence of resources.

### 3. Regional and revisionist approaches

Regional historians shifted attention to the periphery. Agricultural expansion, monetisation and commerce strengthened local elites. Zamindars, revenue entrepreneurs, merchants and bankers possessed "portfolio" resources that could be converted into political power. The Mughal centre did not merely collapse; groups formerly contained within it gained autonomy.

Sekhar Bandyopadhyay consequently treats the century as transformation and decentralisation rather than a vacuum.

The risk is triumphal regionalism. Economic growth could enrich intermediaries while peasants remained vulnerable; local state formation could involve violence and coercion.

### 4. The synthesis

Build the answer in four clauses:

```text
1. crisis of central command and imperial fiscal-military coordination
2. redistribution of authority to regional elites and states
3. selective commercial, agrarian and cultural resurgence
4. unequal burdens, warfare and incomplete institutional transformation
```

This is not indecision. It is a scale-sensitive conclusion. The empire declined as a coordinating centre; regional political economies did not all decline with it.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A synthesis evades choosing the interpretation that is actually correct.

**Reply:** The positions answer different questions and operate at different scales. A Delhi-centred military question and a Bengal-centred commercial question can yield different true findings.

**Qualified verdict:** "Transformation amid crisis" is superior because it specifies what declined, what grew, where and for whom.

### UPSC integration

For "Was the eighteenth century a dark age?":

1. define the dark-age claim;
2. concede central crisis and war;
3. present regional-state evidence;
4. present commerce and culture;
5. add peasant and artisan qualifications;
6. conclude with scale and distribution.

### Revision notes

1. The conventional view foregrounds central political collapse.
2. It is strongest on invasion, disorder and imperial weakness.
3. It overgeneralises from the centre.
4. Materialist history links politics to surplus and jagirdari strain.
5. It explains rural conflict and elite competition.
6. Prosperous regions complicate a universal crisis thesis.
7. Revisionists foreground local elites, commerce and monetisation.
8. Regional growth could coexist with social exploitation.
9. Transformation is descriptive, not celebratory.
10. The best answer separates scale, region and social group.
11. "What declined?" is more useful than "Did India decline?"

## Lesson 17 — Vigorous Regions, Fragmented Strategy: Why the Company Found Openings

Progress: 17 / 17 | Stage: Synthesis | Subtopic: Opportunity without inevitability

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** State capacities, military limits, finance, diplomacy and regional fragmentation checked.
- **Concept focus:** Company-opportunity synthesis checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** Not required; no present analogy substitutes for historical causation.
- **Historical scope:** This lesson explains the openings; the detailed conquest chronology falls beyond its regional-state analysis.

### Visual first: Company advantage was relational

```text
INDIAN REGIONAL FIELD                         COMPANY CAPABILITIES
---------------------                         --------------------
multiple rival states       <diplomacy>       play rivals sequentially
separate fiscal bases       <finance>         Bengal revenue after conquest*
mobile but divided armies   <war>             drilled forces and supply
merchant-bankers            <credit>          corporate + metropolitan backing
land-based power            <sea>             naval communication and ports

*The detailed conquest-and-revenue sequence is not narrated in this regional comparison.
```

No column alone guaranteed victory. Advantage emerged through interaction over time.

### 1. Fragmentation was not simple weakness

Regionalisation produced capable states. Bengal generated revenue; Mysore innovated militarily; the Marathas projected power across India; the Sikhs consolidated Punjab; Travancore defeated a European power at Colachel. A fragmented map can contain strong units.

The strategic weakness lay elsewhere: no accepted institution coordinated these units against a common corporate adversary. Each ruler reasonably focused on immediate rivals. The Company entered as trader, creditor, ally and military partner before appearing as paramount power.

### 2. The alliance trap

A regional ruler could hire Company troops or seek Company support against a neighbour. In the short run this might solve a succession or territorial problem. In the long run it gave the Company:

- political intelligence;
- claims for payment or territory;
- influence over succession;
- a pretext for stationed forces;
- opportunities to isolate the next opponent.

This was not gullibility in every case. Actors faced immediate threats and incomplete information. The Company also changed character over time; earlier experience with ordinary merchant corporations could mislead rulers about its capacity and intentions.

### 3. Fiscal and military asymmetry

Indian states mobilised substantial resources, but payment and command often depended on personal coalitions. The Company increasingly combined:

- joint-stock organisation;
- credit in India and Britain;
- naval transport;
- fortified settlements;
- drilled infantry and artillery;
- salaried administration;
- access to revenue from conquered territories.

Indian military borrowing could be effective, as Mysore and Maratha armies showed. The asymmetry was not "European weapon versus Indian sword." It involved institutional finance, replenishment, command, diplomacy and the ability to shift resources between theatres.

### 4. Merchant-bankers and political risk

Financiers sometimes supported the Company or a rival claimant because they sought repayment, order or protection. Their choices were not expressions of national betrayal in a period before modern nationalism structured politics. They were risk calculations within a fragmented political market.

The Company became dangerous because it could turn a local transaction into cumulative territorial power. A banker or ruler bargaining over one crisis could not necessarily foresee the aggregate result of many such bargains.

### 5. Technology without technological determinism

European advances in science, navigation, military organisation and industry mattered. But battles were not decided by technology alone. Indian forces used muskets, artillery, rockets, European officers and disciplined infantry. Political defection, supply, alliance and finance frequently decided whether technology could be used effectively.

### 6. Counterfactual test

Ask: Was Company rule inevitable in 1707, 1740 or even 1750? No. Different coalitions, stronger naval policies, more durable regional coordination or Company setbacks could have changed the path. Historical explanation must identify mechanisms, not convert the eventual outcome into destiny.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since every major Indian power was eventually defeated, structural inferiority must have made conquest inevitable.

**Reply:** "Eventually" hides sequence. The Company rarely defeated all powers together. It acquired resources from one theatre, neutralised allies, intervened in succession disputes and returned stronger against the next opponent.

**Qualified verdict:** Company expansion was enabled by regional rivalry and institutional asymmetry, but achieved through contingent alliances, choices and cumulative victories.

### UPSC integration

- Never write "India was divided, therefore Britain won" as a complete explanation.
- Pair every Indian weakness with a Company capability.
- Distinguish local rationality from long-term aggregate consequences.
- State explicitly that conquest was contingent, not foreordained.
- Keep Plassey, Buxar, Carnatic and later war narratives in their own topics.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**20 marks | 250 words**

**Question:** Why did individually capable eighteenth-century Indian states fail to prevent the East India Company from accumulating political power? Analyse without treating conquest as inevitable.

#### Model answer

Eighteenth-century India contained substantial regional capacities: Bengal generated revenue and trade, the Marathas projected force across the subcontinent, Mysore adapted military technology, the Sikhs consolidated in Punjab, and Travancore defeated the Dutch at Colachel. Fragmentation therefore meant the absence of durable collective strategy, not the absence of power.

Each state prioritised immediate rivals, succession disputes and fiscal interests. Inviting Company support could provide troops, credit or diplomatic leverage in a local contest. Bankers similarly sought security and repayment within contemporary political conditions. Such choices were rational in the short term but gave the Company intelligence, claims, stationed forces and access to further intervention.

The Company's advantage was institutional and cumulative. Joint-stock and Indian credit, naval communication, fortified settlements, disciplined forces, supply systems and diplomacy allowed it to operate across theatres. Later territorial revenue could finance subsequent campaigns. Indian states also used muskets, artillery, rockets and European-trained units, so technology alone is insufficient.

Coordination problems interacted with this growing capacity. Separate fiscal bases and alliances allowed opponents to be isolated and confronted sequentially. Yet outcomes depended on contingent defections, coalitions, reforms and battlefield results; the Company also faced formidable resistance.

Thus conquest was neither preordained in 1707 nor explained by civilisational weakness. It emerged when a corporate power repeatedly converted local bargains and territorial gains into resources for the next contest. The detailed conquest sequence falls beyond this lesson's regional-state analysis.

#### Distinct scoring guidance

- 4 marks for evidence that regional states possessed genuine capacity.
- 4 marks for explaining local incentives and the alliance trap.
- 5 marks for the Company's financial, naval, military and diplomatic system.
- 4 marks for the sequential coordination mechanism.
- 3 marks for explicit contingency and rejection of technological or civilisational determinism.

## 10 marks | 150 words

### Question

**Distinguish the principal types of eighteenth-century Indian states and explain why the categories should not be treated as rigid compartments.**

### Model answer

Eighteenth-century regional polities may be classified by their route to autonomy. **Successor states** such as Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad arose when Mughal provincial governors converted delegated office into dynastic, practical independence while retaining imperial titles and legitimacy. **New or rebel formations** such as the Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas developed through resistance, military entrepreneurship, agrarian mobilisation or community organisation. **Independent kingdoms** such as Mysore, the Rajput states and Travancore possessed older regional bases and expanded their effective sovereignty as Mughal control weakened.

The categories are not rigid. Marathas challenged Mughal authority but sought imperial *sanads* and used inherited revenue forms. Hyderabad was autonomous in war and appointments while preserving the emperor's name in coin and *khutba*. Mysore retained Wodeyar kingship while Haidar Ali held effective power.

Thus the typology identifies different genealogies, but eighteenth-century sovereignty remained layered: imperial sanction, local custom, conquest, tribute and hereditary right overlapped.

### Scoring guidance

- 2 marks: accurate three-part typology.
- 4 marks: named examples and correct routes to power.
- 2 marks: explanation of overlap through at least two mechanisms.
- 2 marks: qualified conclusion on layered sovereignty.

## 15 marks | 250 words

### Question

**"The strength of eighteenth-century regional states rested on alliances with intermediaries who could also limit them." Analyse with reference to Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad.**

### Model answer

The successor states inherited Mughal offices but lacked the capacity or incentive to administer every locality directly. Their rulers therefore built coalitions with landed, commercial, fiscal and military intermediaries.

In **Bengal**, Murshid Quli Khan improved collection by disciplining zamindars, converting some jagir land into *khalisa* and relying on a smaller number of large landed magnates. Merchants and Jagat Seth bankers supplied credit, remittance and treasury services. This made Bengal fiscally powerful, yet financiers and zamindars became influential enough to affect succession politics.

In **Awadh**, Saadat Khan suppressed rebellious chiefs but often confirmed them after submission. Taluqdars and zamindars retained forts, followers and community influence. Their cooperation sustained revenue; their recurrent defiance exposed the negotiated limits of nawabi authority.

In **Hyderabad**, the Nizam incorporated semi-autonomous rulers through *peshkash* and patronage. Revenue farmers, hereditary jagirdars, traders and military nobles extended rule across a large Deccan territory. The result was reach without uniform control, and succession disputes could activate competing networks.

These intermediaries were not merely signs of weakness. They supplied local knowledge, collection, troops and credit at lower administrative cost. However, the same resources gave them bargaining power.

Therefore, successor-state capacity was **relational**: rulers were strongest when they coordinated intermediaries, and vulnerable when coalition partners defected, withheld revenue or pursued separate interests.

### Scoring guidance

- 3 marks: conceptual explanation of intermediaries.
- 6 marks: balanced, specific use of all three states.
- 3 marks: strength-limit duality.
- 2 marks: analytical comparison rather than serial description.
- 1 mark: concise relational conclusion.

## 20 marks | 250 words

### Question

**Was the eighteenth century in India an age of decline or a period of regional transformation? Discuss, and explain how its political economy created opportunities for Company expansion.**

### Model answer

The eighteenth century cannot be reduced either to a "dark age" or to an uncomplicated regional resurgence.

The **decline thesis** correctly identifies the contraction of Mughal command, jagirdari strain, court faction, invasion, warfare and disruption of major routes and cities. Peasants in many regions faced high demands, revenue farming and military depredation. Scientific and technological change lacked the breadth visible in western Europe.

Yet the decline of Delhi was not the collapse of all India. Bengal combined agrarian surplus, manufactures, bullion trade and sophisticated banking. Awadh and Hyderabad built durable successor regimes. Maratha and Sikh powers expanded through new military-fiscal and community institutions. Mysore and Travancore pursued selective military, commercial and administrative innovation. Lucknow, Hyderabad and regional courts attracted artists and literati.

The best interpretation is therefore **transformation amid crisis**: central coordination weakened while authority, commerce and patronage were redistributed, with unequal outcomes for regions and classes.

This order created Company opportunities but not inevitable conquest. Rival states lacked a durable coordinating mechanism; rulers used the Company against immediate opponents; bankers pursued security and repayment; separate armies and fiscal bases complicated collective strategy. The Company increasingly combined corporate credit, naval communication, disciplined forces, diplomacy and, after territorial gains, Indian revenue.

Thus individually vigorous polities were defeated sequentially, not because India lacked power, but because regional rivalry and uneven institutional capacity allowed a corporate state to turn local bargains into cumulative supremacy.

### Scoring guidance

- 5 marks: credible evidence for decline/crisis.
- 5 marks: credible evidence for regional transformation.
- 4 marks: social and distributional qualification.
- 4 marks: relational explanation of Company opportunity.
- 2 marks: non-teleological, balanced judgement.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core thesis

- The eighteenth century was not a political vacuum between Mughal and British rule.
- It combined imperial contraction, regional state formation, uneven commercial vitality, warfare and unequal social costs.
- Regional states reused Mughal forms while changing the location and social coalition of power.
- Company expansion became possible through the interaction of regional rivalry with corporate finance, diplomacy, naval access and military organisation.

## 2. Typology

| Type | Cases | Defining route |
|---|---|---|
| Successor | Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad | Mughal provincial authority became dynastic practical autonomy |
| New/rebel | Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Rohillas | resistance, community mobilisation or military entrepreneurship created states |
| Independent kingdom | Mysore, Rajput states, Travancore | older regional polities enlarged effective independence |

> **Memory line:** Province becomes dynasty; resistance becomes territory; old kingdom becomes assertive.

## 3. Common state-formation engine

- **Legitimacy:** imperial sanction, dynasty, community, conquest and customary right.
- **Revenue:** land demand, tribute, customs and assigned income.
- **Intermediaries:** zamindars, taluqdars, deshmukhs, revenue farmers and village officers.
- **Credit:** bankers, hundis, advances and treasury guarantees.
- **Force:** cavalry, infantry, artillery, forts and mobile military labour.
- **Coalition:** rulers governed through local elites rather than eliminating all of them.
- **Risk:** intermediaries who extended state reach could withhold resources or defect.

## 4. Hyderabad

- Founded as an autonomous regional power by Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I after 1724.
- Practical authority included war, treaty, appointment, rank and jagir distribution.
- Mughal emperor's name remained in coin and khutba.
- Semi-autonomous rulers paid peshkash and retained local control.
- Revenue farmers, hereditary jagirdars, traders and military-financial elites were important.
- Political recruitment crossed religious lines.
- Strength: broad incorporated network.
- Limit: widely diffused power and succession vulnerability.
- Arcot/Carnatic developed under Hyderabad's superior framework; detailed European rivalry belongs to Topic 03.

## 5. Bengal

- Murshid Quli Khan concentrated nazim and diwan authority.
- Formal tribute and allegiance initially continued.
- Revenue reform used surveys, zamindar discipline, khalisa conversion and farming.
- A few large zamindars gained prominence.
- Bengal produced textiles, silk, sugar, oil and other export goods.
- European purchases brought bullion.
- Hindu, Muslim and Armenian merchants operated at scale.
- Jagat Seth bankers linked credit, remittance, treasury and mint.
- Fiscal strength coexisted with pressure on cultivators and military vulnerability.
- Plassey, Buxar and Diwani belong to Topic 04.

## 6. Awadh

- Saadat Khan became governor in 1722.
- Armed zamindars were suppressed but often confirmed after submission.
- Revenue reform strengthened the state while effects on cultivators varied.
- Elite coalition included Indian Muslims, Afghans, Hindus and Khatri officials.
- Safdar Jung used the imperial wazirship while sustaining regional autonomy.
- Lucknow's rise shows movement of cultural patronage.
- Strength: rich agrarian-commercial base and strategic position.
- Limit: recurrent taluqdari bargaining and external entanglement.

## 7. Successor-state comparison

- Shared: provincial origin, dynastic succession, imperial symbolism, local revenue control, cross-religious elite recruitment.
- Bengal: strongest fiscal-commercial triangle.
- Awadh: strongest landed bargaining problem.
- Hyderabad: broadest incorporated and diffused network.
- Efficiency must be specified: extraction, order, commerce, justice or mobilisation.

## 8. Marathas

- Shahu-Tarabai conflict enabled the Peshwa's rise.
- Balaji Vishwanath became Peshwa in 1713.
- Mughal sanads of 1719 recognised claims.
- Chauth: commonly one-fourth claim.
- Sardeshmukhi: additional superior-right claim, conventionally one-tenth.
- Baji Rao I expanded into Malwa, Gujarat and Bundelkhand.
- Palkhed, 1728, demonstrated strategic mobility against Hyderabad.
- Major houses: Gaekwad, Holkar, Sindhia, Bhonsle.
- Vatan: hereditary local right; saranjam: service-linked assignment.
- Administrative depth was greatest in heartland and selected regions, thinner in distant tribute zones.
- Panipat, 14 January 1761, was severe but not terminal.
- Madhav Rao restored Maratha influence.
- Confederacy enabled initiative but impeded unified long-term strategy.
- Full Company-war sequence belongs to Topic 05.

## 9. Sikhs

- Khalsa founded by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699.
- It created public identity, discipline and armed solidarity.
- Not all Sikhs were Khalsa.
- Banda Bahadur mobilised important Jat peasant and small-zamindar support.
- Punjab society remained internally divided.
- Banda was executed in 1716; Sikh power later regrouped.
- Dal Khalsa, jathas and misls converted armed organisation into territorial power.
- Twelve misls is a conventional classification.
- Brotherhood and increasing chiefly hierarchy coexisted.

## 10. Jats, Rohillas and Rajputs

- Jat revolt joined agrarian grievance with zamindar leadership.
- Bharatpur consolidated under Churaman, Badan Singh and Suraj Mal.
- Peasant mobilisation could produce a hierarchical state.
- Farrukhabad was associated with Muhammad Khan Bangash.
- Rohilkhand arose through Afghan settlement and Ali Muhammad Khan's consolidation.
- Rohilkhand was not formed from Durrani-occupied territories.
- Rajput states expanded autonomy but remained divided.
- Sawai Jai Singh founded Jaipur and built observatories.
- His science was significant but not proof of a general scientific revolution.

## 11. Mysore and Travancore

- Wodeyar Mysore emerged from the post-Vijayanagara political field and existed before Haidar Ali.
- Haidar established effective authority in 1761.
- Dindigul arsenal was established in 1755 with French assistance.
- Tipu promoted military manufacture, state trade and administrative innovation.
- Agrarian extraction remained substantial.
- Tipu's religious record requires contextual balance.
- Martanda Varma ruled Travancore from 1729.
- Victory at Colachel in 1741 checked Dutch political power.
- Pepper revenue, army reform, roads, canals and irrigation supported centralisation.
- Both were selective fiscal-military experiments, not industrial nation-states.

## 12. Agrarian relations

- State finance rested primarily on agrarian surplus.
- Villages were linked to markets, credit and administration.
- Khudkasht and pahikasht indicate differentiated cultivator positions.
- Zamindars ranged from major chiefs to local revenue holders.
- Ijaradari could create short-term extraction incentives.
- War damaged crops, cattle and routes.
- Peasants migrated, resisted, bargained and joined military labour.
- Output, state receipts and peasant welfare must remain separate.

## 13. Artisans, merchants and bankers

- Textile, silk, shipbuilding and metal production remained important.
- Political disruption affected regions unevenly.
- Merchants required security and states required customs, supplies and credit.
- Dadani advances could enable production or deepen dependence.
- Hundis supported remittance and long-distance credit.
- Banking networks carried information as well as money.
- Commercial growth did not ensure artisan welfare.

## 14. Social structure

- Jati shaped marriage, dining, occupation and rank.
- Caste rules varied regionally; limited upward mobility occurred.
- Muslim society also contained status, lineage and occupational hierarchy.
- Patriarchy was widespread; matriliny existed in parts of Kerala.
- Poorer women often worked outside the home.
- Labour participation did not equal autonomy.
- Sati was concentrated among particular upper-status groups and regions.
- Widow remarriage varied across caste and region.
- Elementary education existed but access was unequal.
- Higher learning favoured Sanskrit, Persian, law, religion, philosophy and logic.
- Girls' education and scientific curriculum were limited.

## 15. Culture and knowledge

- Cultural patronage shifted from Delhi to regional courts.
- Persianate culture was locally produced by diverse communities.
- Urdu poetry and vernacular literatures expanded.
- Regional painting, music, architecture and dance displayed vitality.
- Jai Singh's astronomy and Mysore's military technology show selective achievement.
- Broad scientific and industrial diffusion remained limited.
- Hindu-Muslim interaction crossed service, language, shrine and festival.
- Composite culture did not eliminate hierarchy or conflict.

## 16. Historiography

- **Conventional:** central decline, invasion and disorder.
- **Materialist:** jagirdari strain, agrarian surplus and class conflict.
- **Revisionist/regional:** local elites, commercialisation and decentralisation.
- Best synthesis: crisis of central coordination + regional transformation + selective vitality + unequal burdens.

## 17. Company opportunity

- Regional states were vigorous but lacked durable collective coordination.
- Company alliances solved immediate local problems while expanding corporate access.
- Advantage rested on finance, naval reach, supply, command, intelligence and diplomacy, not weapons alone.
- Indian states selectively modernised and resisted strongly.
- Company gains accumulated sequentially.
- Conquest was contingent, not inevitable.
