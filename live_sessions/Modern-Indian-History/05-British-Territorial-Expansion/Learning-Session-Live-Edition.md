# British Territorial Expansion: Complete Learner-Facing Live Session

**Subject:** Modern Indian History
**Topic:** British Territorial Expansion
**Level:** Foundation to advanced UPSC analysis
**Historical span:** From the post-Bengal fiscal base to the annexation of Awadh in 1856

## How to use this session

This topic is not a catalogue of wars. It asks how a trading corporation acquired the capacity to defeat, subordinate or annex different Indian powers over nearly a century. Read it as a chain:

```text
Revenue base
    |
    v
Regularly paid army + artillery + supplies
    |
    v
Diplomatic isolation of one opponent at a time
    |
    v
War, indemnity, territorial cession or subsidiary treaty
    |
    v
Larger revenue and strategic base
    |
    v
Further war or indirect control
```

The chain was powerful, but never automatic. Mysore, the Marathas, Nepal and the Sikh kingdom repeatedly exposed Company limits. British victory depended on Indian soldiers, bankers, suppliers, allies and political divisions; it was neither proof of civilisational superiority nor the execution of an unchanged plan formulated in 1757.

## Lesson 1 — From a revenue base to a fiscal-military expansion system

Progress: 1 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Focus: the engine behind territorial growth

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Prerequisite recalled:** Bengal's Diwani supplied a major revenue base; the battles and settlement details belong to the preceding conquest stage.
- **Book context:** Bipan Chandra's conquest chapter and the permitted advanced source were checked.
- **Current-affairs link:** None is needed; this is a bounded historical mechanism.

### Visual first: the self-reinforcing engine

```text
              |
              v
 Artillery, magazines, transport and fort supply
              |
              v
 Ability to keep campaigning after a setback
              |

              |
              +-------------------------------+

```

**Caption:** Territorial growth became cumulative because each successful settlement could finance the next contest.

### Plain-language intuition

An Indian ruler could win a battle and still lose the war if his coalition broke, revenue collection faltered or troops went unpaid. The Company increasingly possessed something more durable than battlefield skill: a system that connected taxation, commercial credit, military salaries, sea-borne supply, arsenals, diplomacy and treaty enforcement.

This does not mean the Company was always richer or tactically superior. It means that, over time, it could convert dispersed resources into sustained campaigns more reliably than many opponents facing court rivalry, contested succession or confederate coordination problems.

### The technical mechanism

#### 1. Fiscal capacity

Revenue from controlled territories, especially Bengal, reduced dependence on remittances from Britain. Indian bankers and merchants also supplied credit, transport, grain and intelligence. Conquest was therefore financed substantially from Indian resources.

#### 2. A salaried sepoy army

The Company's army was mostly Indian in composition. Its advantage lay not in ethnicity but in organisation: regular cash pay when finances functioned, drill, artillery integration, standard command procedures and the ability to move formations between presidencies.

#### 3. Maritime and logistical reach

Ports, coastal shipping and naval protection allowed reinforcements and stores to move between Bengal, Madras and Bombay. This mattered in long wars against Mysore and the Marathas, although monsoon, disease, terrain and distance still imposed severe limits.

#### 4. Diplomatic sequencing

The Company usually tried to avoid fighting a united coalition. It alternated war, neutrality agreements, subsidies and separate peace. A defeated rival could become an ally against the next opponent.

#### 5. Conquest without immediate annexation

Control had several forms:

| Form | What changed | What formally survived |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial cession | Revenue and administration passed to the Company | The ruler might retain a reduced domain |
| Subsidiary alliance | Defence and external relations shifted toward Company control | Court, dynasty and nominal internal authority |
| Resident-mediated control | Political decisions were shaped through a British representative | Formal sovereignty |
| Direct annexation | Company administration replaced the court | Little or no dynastic rule |

These were not neat stages followed everywhere. Different instruments could coexist.

### Causal sequence: why expansion accelerated

1. Bengal supplied a comparatively secure fiscal base.
2. The Company used Indian revenue and credit to maintain larger forces.
3. It intervened in regional succession and alliance disputes.
4. War settlements produced money, territory or strategic access.
5. Acquired territory shortened supply lines and widened recruitment.
6. Subsidiary alliances shifted military costs to allied states.
7. By 1818, paramountcy described a hierarchy in which the Company claimed superior political authority.
8. After military predominance was established, annexation could be justified through lapse, alleged misgovernment, debt, strategic necessity or treaty breach.

### Example and limit

**Example:** The Nizam's cooperation helped isolate Mysore, while Maratha divisions enabled the Company to fight different chiefs at different moments.

**Limit:** Alliance politics could also work against the Company. In the early 1780s, the combination of Mysore, Maratha pressure, French rivalry and war in America placed British power under severe strain.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** If the Company often improvised in response to local crises, calling expansion a system imposes hindsight on contingent events.

**Reply:** Contingency shaped timing, but recurring capacities—revenue extraction, a standing army, inter-presidency movement, Residents, treaty finance and diplomatic isolation—made later interventions increasingly reproducible.

**Qualified assessment:** There was no unaltered master plan from 1757. Yet repeated decisions created an institutional path in which each acquisition lowered the cost or raised the feasibility of the next.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish annexation, subsidiary control, territorial cession and paramountcy.
- **Mains:** explain British success through a fiscal-military and diplomatic system, not merely "better weapons."
- **Essay use:** institutions can magnify incremental advantages into structural dominance.

### Revision register

1. Bengal revenue was a foundation, not a complete explanation.
2. Company armies were largely Indian.
3. Regular finance linked revenue to military continuity.
4. Logistics included ports, roads, magazines, animals and grain.
5. Diplomacy aimed at sequential rather than simultaneous warfare.
6. Cession, alliance, Residency and annexation were distinct control forms.
7. Indian bankers, soldiers, suppliers and allies were indispensable.
8. A battlefield reverse did not necessarily destroy the Company's system.
9. Expansion generated further revenue and strategic depth.
10. British growth combined contingency with cumulative institutional advantage.

### Lesson 1 practice

**MCQ 1.** Which formulation best explains why Bengal's revenues mattered to later Company expansion?

A. They supplied recurring cash for troops and logistics across several theatres.
B. They chiefly enlarged commercial dividends while distant armies remained financed by each presidency's customs.
C. They funded Bengal's forts, while military salaries outside the province continued to depend on parliamentary grants.
D. They replaced Indian credit networks with territorial collections but contributed little to inter-presidency campaigning.

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** Recurring territorial revenue helped pay sepoys, maintain magazines and support operations beyond Bengal; each clause describes the fiscal-military link.
- **B — Incorrect:** Dividends mattered, but Bengal resources also supported war; distant operations were not confined to customs collected by the campaigning presidency.
- **C — Incorrect:** Bengal financed more than local fortification, and Company armies did not normally rely on parliamentary grants as their principal Indian salary source.
- **D — Incorrect:** Territorial collections supplemented rather than displaced Indian bankers and contractors, and they materially strengthened inter-presidency campaigning.

**MCQ 2.** In this topic, "sequential conquest" most accurately refers to:

A. securing one rival's neutrality, then converting that neutral state into the next immediate annexation target.
B. rearranging alliances so major opponents were confronted in different combinations and at different times, while separate settlements reduced simultaneous military pressure.
C. rotating campaigns among presidencies while leaving relations among Indian powers outside Company strategy.
D. opening simultaneous offensives against several powers so that no separate settlement altered the coalition.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** A former neutral or ally could later be pressured, but sequential conquest did not require that state to become the next annexation target immediately.
- **B — Correct:** The method joined diplomacy and timing: allies, neutrals and separate peaces changed which major power the Company faced in each campaign.
- **C — Incorrect:** Inter-presidency rotation could occur, but the concept concerns political isolation as well as military deployment; Indian alignments were central.
- **D — Incorrect:** Broad mobilisation sometimes occurred, yet separate settlements and avoidance of a combined opposition were characteristic of the sequence.

**MCQ 3.** Which statement best captures the relation between contingency and structure in Company expansion?

A. Local crises determined the openings, while fiscal and military institutions became relevant only after each annexation was complete.
B. Accumulated institutions fixed the target and timing of expansion, leaving local succession and alliance crises with a minor role.
C. Uncertain openings were exploited through capacities that grew with revenue, armies, logistics and earlier settlements, each of which widened later choices without fixing them.
D. Diplomatic and battlefield contingencies shaped individual wars, but their outcomes did not enlarge reusable Company capacity.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Crises did create openings, but pre-existing revenue, armies and logistics shaped the Company's ability to exploit them before annexation.
- **B — Incorrect:** Institutional capacity widened possible action without fixing its exact target or timing; succession disputes, alliances and resistance remained consequential.
- **C — Correct:** The formulation gives contingency causal weight while explaining why each gain could increase the capacity used in later interventions.
- **D — Incorrect:** Settlements often supplied revenue, routes, troops or diplomatic leverage, so the consequences of contingent wars did become reusable resources.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how a fiscal-military system converted limited Company possessions into a platform for territorial expansion.

**Model answer**

The Company's expansion rested on connections between revenue, credit, military organisation and diplomacy. Bengal's fiscal resources helped maintain a paid sepoy army, artillery, magazines and long supply lines. Indian bankers, merchants, transporters and recruits were central to this capacity. Ports and presidency networks allowed forces and stores to be shifted between theatres.

Military power was then used selectively. The Company isolated one rival through alliances with another, intervened in succession disputes, and converted victory into indemnities, ceded districts or subsidiary treaties. These gains produced fresh revenue and strategic depth, while subsidiary states bore part of the cost of Company troops. Residents restricted external diplomacy before formal annexation became necessary.

However, expansion was not automatic. Mysore, the Marathas, Nepal and the Sikhs inflicted reverses, while London sometimes restrained expensive wars. Thus, contingent opportunities mattered, but an accumulating fiscal-military structure made the Company's recovery from setbacks and serial conquest increasingly effective.

**Scoring guidance:** Award for a clear revenue-army-logistics-treaty chain, Indian participation, one qualification and rejection of a weapons-only explanation.

## Lesson 2 — Haidar Ali and the First Anglo-Mysore War: when the Company could be checked

Progress: 2 / 14 | Stage: Core | Focus: Mysore's rise and the 1767-69 contest

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** The fiscal-military model is now tested against a strong southern state.
- **Book context:** The four-war sequence and alliance shifts were verified in both permitted books.
- **Boundary:** Carnatic rivalries appear only as the strategic setting for Mysore's confrontation with Madras.

### Visual first: a moving alliance triangle

```text
                 Marathas
                /        \
       temporary          changing
       bargains           calculations
              /            \
          Mysore -------- Hyderabad
             \              /
              \            /
           Madras Company power

No side occupied a permanent diplomatic corner.
```

**Caption:** The First War cannot be understood by placing "British" and "Mysore" in two fixed camps.

### Begin with the intuition

Haidar Ali's Mysore was dangerous to the Company because it joined mobile cavalry, disciplined infantry, artillery, revenue collection and access toward the Malabar coast. It was not an old state passively awaiting conquest. It was an expanding military power operating in the same competitive southern arena as Hyderabad, the Marathas and the Company.

### The bounded Carnatic bridge: why Madras could fight a territorial war

The Mysore wars began after the English and French companies had already competed through the presidency settlements of Madras and Pondicherry and through rival clients in the Carnatic and Hyderabad. The decisive European phase belongs to the earlier history of European settlements, but three consequences are indispensable here:

```text
Anglo-French presidency rivalry
          |
          +-- Madras develops a standing military establishment
          +-- companies learn to finance and drill Indian sepoys
          +-- succession politics become a route to military influence
          |
Wandiwash, 1760 + Treaty of Paris, 1763
          |
          v
French state-backed territorial competition declines
          |
          v
English Madras establishment remains an expanding regional army
```

Wandiwash in 1760 and the Treaty of Paris in 1763 curtailed the French Company's capacity to compete directly for territorial predominance. They did not remove French people, military expertise or diplomacy from India. French officers could still train or advise Indian armies, and Indian rulers could still seek French support. That was different from control by the French state or the French Company.

The Madras establishment inherited forts, recruitment networks, sepoy battalions, artillery practice and coastal supply links from the presidency rivalry. Mysore therefore confronted not a newly improvised trading-post guard but a regional military institution. At the same time, the First Anglo-Mysore War proves that the decline of direct French competition did not make Madras invulnerable.

### How Mysore acquired capacity

Haidar rose through military service and became the effective ruler of Mysore in the 1760s. He strengthened armed forces, employed European military techniques selectively and sought territorial resources. Western-coast connections mattered because they offered commerce, supplies and diplomatic contact.

Mysore's state was not simply a European copy. Its forces combined imported techniques with Indian recruitment, cavalry traditions, local revenue systems and political bargaining. The correct comparison is adaptive state-building, not imitation.

### First Anglo-Mysore War, step by step

1. In 1766 the Company allied with the Nizam of Hyderabad against Haidar in return for the Northern Circars arrangement.
2. The coalition proved unstable; the Nizam changed position.
3. Haidar survived pressure and manoeuvred rapidly.
4. In 1769 he threatened Madras.
5. The Treaty of Madras restored conquests and provided for mutual assistance.
6. When the Marathas attacked Haidar in 1771, the Company did not provide the expected assistance.
7. The breach deepened Haidar's distrust and helped prepare the next confrontation.

### What the war demonstrates

| Common assumption | Historical correction |
|---|---|
| Company victory was already inevitable | Haidar forced peace near Madras |
| Alliances were religious or permanent | Hyderabad and Maratha alignments changed |
| European drill alone decided wars | mobility, supply, diplomacy and timing mattered |
| Treaties permanently settled rivalry | the 1769 settlement left distrust unresolved |

### Example with limits

**Example:** Haidar's advance toward Madras showed that Company coastal centres could be threatened.

**Limit:** The Treaty of Madras did not establish durable Mysorean supremacy. Mysore still faced multiple neighbours and required dependable alliances that proved difficult to maintain.

### Objection and response

**Objection:** Haidar's success was merely a temporary result of British distraction.

**Response:** British difficulties mattered, but Mysore had to possess the command, mobility and resources capable of exploiting them. Opportunity without state capacity would not have produced the same result.

**Judgement:** The First War is evidence of genuine Mysorean strength and of the Company's vulnerability before its later expansion system matured.

### UPSC integration

- Use the First War to challenge inevitability narratives.
- Track the Treaty of Madras with the year 1769.
- Explain alliance volatility instead of assuming fixed anti-British unity.

### Revision register

1. Haidar Ali became Mysore's effective ruler in the 1760s.
2. Mysore combined Indian institutions with selectively adopted techniques.
3. Hyderabad and the Marathas pursued their own interests.
4. The 1766 alignment against Mysore did not remain stable.
5. Haidar threatened Madras in 1769.
6. The Treaty of Madras restored conquests.
7. It also promised mutual assistance.
8. Company non-assistance in 1771 damaged trust.
9. The First War disproves a simple inevitability thesis.
10. Capacity and opportunity must be analysed together.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why is the First Anglo-Mysore War important for understanding the contingent character of British expansion?

**Model answer**

The First Anglo-Mysore War shows that British expansion was neither linear nor predetermined. Haidar Ali commanded mobile forces and artillery and exploited the changing calculations of Hyderabad and the Marathas. The coalition formed in 1766 did not remain cohesive. Haidar recovered, advanced close to Madras and compelled the Treaty of Madras in 1769.

The settlement restored conquests and included mutual assistance, but it did not resolve the rivalry. When the Company failed to aid Haidar during a Maratha attack in 1771, mistrust intensified. Thus, treaties were temporary instruments inside a fluid balance of power rather than final settlements.

The war does not prove permanent Mysorean superiority; Mysore still faced several rivals and unstable alliances. It establishes that later Company success depended on fiscal growth, diplomacy and opponent isolation. In the 1760s, a capable Indian state could still threaten a presidency and dictate peace.

**Scoring guidance:** Credit chronology, alliance fluidity, Mysorean capacity, the 1769 treaty, the 1771 breach and a qualified conclusion.

## Lesson 3 — The Second Anglo-Mysore War: coalition, recovery and stalemate

Progress: 3 / 14 | Stage: Core | Focus: 1780-84 and the limits of battlefield-only explanation

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** The broken confidence after 1769 explains why later diplomacy mattered.
- **Book context:** Alliance reversal, Porto Novo, Haidar's death and the Treaty of Mangalore were cross-checked.
- **Exam caution:** Do not confuse the treaties of Madras, Mangalore and Seringapatam.

### Visual first: how the Company escaped strategic encirclement

```text
1780 danger
Mysore pressure + Maratha hostility + Hyderabad hostility + French rivalry
                              |
                              v
              Company diplomacy separates opponents
                   /                       \
          Hyderabad detached        Maratha peace, 1782
                   \                       /
                    v                     v
                    More force available for Mysore
                              |
                              v
                    no decisive conquest
                              |
                              v
                 Treaty of Mangalore, 1784
```

### The war as a systems contest

Haidar entered the Carnatic in 1780 and inflicted a severe defeat on Company forces at Pollilur. The Company's survival depended on more than one battlefield. Warren Hastings used diplomacy and concessions to loosen the hostile alignment, while troops and resources were redirected. Eyre Coote's victory at Porto Novo in 1781 checked Mysorean momentum without destroying Mysore.

Haidar died in 1782. Tipu Sultan continued the war. Neither side secured decisive mastery, and the Treaty of Mangalore in 1784 broadly restored conquests. It was one of the last settlements in which an Indian power negotiated with the Company without accepting subordination.

### Why numbers alone mislead

| Variable | Mysore's strength | Company's counter-capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Field operations | rapid advance and strong resistance | regrouping and reinforcement |
| Diplomacy | broad anti-Company possibilities | separate agreements and inducements |
| Leadership | Haidar, then Tipu | Hastings and experienced commanders |
| Strategic depth | strong southern territorial base | inter-presidency and maritime connections |
| Duration | capacity to sustain a long war | access to wider credit and revenue |

### Causation in sequence

1. Earlier grievances and wider imperial war produced a dangerous anti-Company moment.
2. Haidar invaded the Carnatic and achieved early successes.
3. The Company preserved its core positions.
4. Diplomacy reduced simultaneous pressure from Hyderabad and the Marathas.
5. Porto Novo checked Mysore but did not settle the war.
6. Haidar's death changed command without ending resistance.
7. Continued mutual strain produced the Treaty of Mangalore.

### Example and limit

**Example:** The Company obtained relief by separating adversaries, demonstrating that diplomacy could substitute for an immediate decisive victory.

**Limit:** This was not effortless divide-and-rule. Concessions, military endurance and external circumstances were required, and the settlement did not subordinate Tipu.

### Debate: did Indian disunity cause British victory?

**Claim:** Rivalries among Indian powers were decisive because the Company survived by separating Hyderabad and the Marathas from Mysore.

**Counter:** "Disunity" is too moralistic and vague. States everywhere pursued changing interests; the analytical issue is why the Company became increasingly capable of managing those shifts.

**Qualified conclusion:** Inter-state rivalry created openings, but the Company's fiscal, diplomatic and logistical machinery converted those openings into recovery.

### UPSC integration

- **Chronology:** Second War, 1780-84; Haidar died, 1782; Treaty of Mangalore, 1784.
- **Analysis:** distinguish tactical victory from strategic settlement.
- **Trap:** Porto Novo did not end the war or annex Mysore.

### Revision register

1. The Second War began in 1780.
2. Haidar achieved major early successes in the Carnatic, including Pollilur.
3. The Company faced pressures beyond Mysore alone.
4. Warren Hastings used diplomacy to reduce coalition pressure.
5. Porto Novo in 1781 checked Mysore.
6. Haidar died in 1782.
7. Tipu continued the conflict.
8. Neither side imposed complete defeat.
9. The Treaty of Mangalore ended the war in 1784.
10. "Disunity" must be converted into a precise alliance-and-capacity explanation.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** The Second Anglo-Mysore War was decided as much by coalition management as by combat. Discuss.

**Model answer**

The Second Anglo-Mysore War began in 1780 in an unusually dangerous setting for the East India Company. Haidar Ali's forces achieved major successes in the Carnatic, while Maratha and Hyderabad hostility and the wider Anglo-French struggle threatened to stretch Company resources. A battlefield-only account therefore misses the strategic problem.

Company recovery combined combat with coalition management. Its forces preserved key positions and Eyre Coote's victory at Porto Novo in 1781 checked Mysorean momentum. At the same time, Warren Hastings used concessions and negotiation to detach the Nizam and secure peace with the Marathas in 1782. This reduced the number of active fronts and released resources for the southern war. Haidar's death in 1782 altered leadership, but Tipu maintained resistance.

The Treaty of Mangalore in 1784 broadly restored conquests rather than imposing Company supervision. It therefore marked stalemate, not Mysore's subordination. Coalition diplomacy had saved the Company from strategic encirclement, but it had not yet produced decisive supremacy.

The war demonstrates that "Indian disunity" is an inadequate moral label. All states pursued changing interests. What became historically significant was the Company's growing ability to combine revenue, inter-presidency logistics, battlefield endurance and separate diplomacy. Coalition management was thus essential to recovery, while combat prevented that diplomacy from being conducted from a position of collapse.

**Scoring guidance:** Reward integration of the 1780 crisis, Porto Novo, diplomatic separation, Haidar's death, Mangalore and the distinction between recovery and conquest.

## Lesson 4 — Tipu Sultan, the Third and Fourth Wars: state-building, isolation and defeat

Progress: 4 / 14 | Stage: Core-advanced | Focus: 1790-92 and 1799

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** Mysore has survived two wars; the question is how the balance changes.
- **Book context:** Tipu's commercial controls, diplomatic initiatives, coalition isolation and the 1799 settlement were verified.
- **Interpretive caution:** Avoid both heroic mythology without limits and colonial caricature.

### Visual first: Tipu's strategic dilemma

```text
                 Search for external partners
            France / Ottoman lands / Afghanistan
                         |
                         v
Malabar trade <-- centralising Mysore --> army and forts
                         |
                         v
          British threat perception increases
                         |
          +--------------+--------------+
          |                             |
  Company diplomacy              Company resources
  wins allies                    sustain invasion
          \                             /
           v                           v
         Mysore isolated in 1790-92 and again in 1799
```

### Intuition before labels

Tipu tried to protect Mysore by making it harder to penetrate: stronger central direction, control over strategic commodities, military improvement and diplomatic searches for partners. The same measures made the Company see Mysore as an incompatible rival. Two expanding states competed for southern predominance; mutual suspicion was structural.

### Tipu's state: capability and controversy

Tipu maintained a disciplined army, strengthened fortifications, used rockets and sought overseas diplomatic contacts. State direction of trade in products such as pepper, sandalwood and cardamom aimed to increase revenue and strategic control.

This should not be simplified into either "modern nationalist" or "oriental despot." His reforms served a centralising monarchy pursuing war, revenue and territorial security. They could increase state capacity while also imposing burdens on contested regions. Assessment must separate administrative innovation from modern democratic nationalism.

### Third Anglo-Mysore War, 1790-92

The immediate crisis followed Tipu's attack on Travancore, a Company ally. Cornwallis built a coalition including the Nizam and the Marathas. Mysore was isolated and defeated. Under the Treaty of Seringapatam in 1792:

- Tipu ceded roughly half his territory to the victors.
- A large indemnity was imposed.
- Hostages were taken as security for payment.
- Mysore's strategic position was sharply reduced, though the state survived.

### Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799

Wellesley treated Tipu's contacts with the French as a security threat and rejected a durable balance among Indian states. Company and allied forces attacked. Tipu died defending Seringapatam on 4 May 1799.

The settlement did not annex all Mysore:

1. Significant territories were divided between the Company and the Nizam.
2. A reduced kingdom was restored to the Wodeyar dynasty.
3. The restored state entered subsidiary dependence.
4. The Company acquired decisive strategic control in southern India.

### Why Mysore lost

| Explanation | Contribution | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Diplomatic isolation | enabled multi-directional pressure | alliances were produced, not naturally fixed |
| Revenue and logistics | sustained Company campaigns | Mysore also possessed substantial capacity |

**Qualified assessment:** Tipu's defeat resulted from a narrowing strategic environment—territorial loss, failed external support, Company coalition-building and a larger fiscal-military base—not from technological backwardness alone.

## Lesson 5 — The Maratha confederacy and the First Anglo-Maratha War

Progress: 5 / 14 | Stage: Core | Focus: confederate strength, succession politics and the 1775-82 stalemate

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** Mysore showed how a centralising state resisted; the Maratha case introduces distributed authority.
- **Book context:** Wadgaon, Salbai and the roles of Nana Phadnis and Mahadji Sindhia were verified.
- **Concept warning:** A confederacy is not automatically a weak or chaotic state.

### Visual first: power without a single command centre

```text
                       Peshwa at Pune
                      nominal primacy
                   /        |         \
          Sindhia         Holkar       Bhonsle
          Gwalior         Indore       Nagpur
                   \        |         /
                       Gaekwad
                       Baroda

Shared Maratha political world + separate armies, territories and interests
```

### Intuition

The Maratha confederacy could mobilise enormous territorial and military resources, but no single authority could always compel all major houses to follow one strategy. This was a source of resilience as well as difficulty: defeat of one chief did not automatically destroy the whole system, but coordinated policy was hard to sustain.

### The succession opening

After the death of Peshwa Narayan Rao, Raghunath Rao sought Company support against the party governing for the infant Madhav Rao II. Bombay officials saw an opportunity to obtain political and territorial advantages.

This intervention began the First Anglo-Maratha War in 1775. It was not a simple national war between two unified blocs. Different Company presidencies disagreed, and Maratha leaders combined resistance with internal competition.

### Course of the war

1. Bombay supported Raghunath Rao.
2. The Treaty of Purandar in 1776 attempted a compromise, but authority disputes within the Company helped reopen conflict.
3. Maratha forces compelled a British detachment to accept the Convention of Wadgaon in 1779.
4. The Bengal government rejected that humiliation and sent forces across central India.
5. Neither side achieved complete victory.
6. Mahadji Sindhia helped mediate the Treaty of Salbai in 1782.
7. The settlement broadly restored the status quo and produced about two decades of relative Company-Maratha peace.

### First-war settlement chain

| Settlement | Date | Immediate political meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Treaty of Purandar | 1776 | Calcutta authorities attempted to limit Bombay's commitment to Raghunath Rao and recognise the Pune regency, but Company disagreement prevented a durable closure |
| Convention of Wadgaon | 1779 | a surrounded Bombay force accepted humiliating terms after Maratha operational success; the Bengal government refused to treat it as final |
| Treaty of Salbai | 1782 | Mahadji Sindhia mediated a wider peace that recognised Madhav Rao II and ended the inconclusive war without subordinating the confederacy |

The sequence matters because it shows three different forms of settlement: attempted compromise, field capitulation and negotiated inter-governmental peace. Treating them as interchangeable hides both Maratha leverage and divisions within Company authority.

### Why Wadgaon matters

Wadgaon is evidence that Maratha forces could outmanoeuvre and defeat Company troops. It prevents a retrospective assumption that British operational superiority was constant. Yet the Company could draw resources from another presidency and refuse to treat one field reverse as final.

### Confederacy: strength and coordination problem

| Feature | Strategic strength | Strategic risk |
|---|---|---|
| Several military houses | multiple armies and broad reach | divergent priorities |
| Flexible local command | rapid adaptation | no guaranteed common plan |
| Peshwa's prestige | symbolic centre | contested practical authority |
| Regional revenue bases | resilience after local loss | uneven contribution to joint war |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Blaming confederate structure merely disguises the ambitions of individual chiefs.

**Reply:** Individual agency matters, but institutional design shaped what those ambitions could do. A loose distribution of command made both plural resistance and coordinated strategy possible, depending on political leadership.

**Assessment:** The First War ended without British conquest because Maratha military power remained formidable. It also exposed recurring coordination and succession openings that later Company policy would exploit.

## Lesson 6 — Subsidiary Alliance: sovereignty removed in instalments

Progress: 6 / 14 | Stage: Core-advanced | Focus: Wellesley's instrument of indirect control

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** Military cases now reveal why the Company preferred control without administering every territory.
- **Book context:** Hyderabad, Awadh, Mysore and the treaty mechanism were checked in both permitted books.
- **PYQ alert:** The 2018 direct Prelims question tests the purposes, not merely the clauses, of this system.

### Visual first: what remained and what moved

```text
INDIAN RULER RETAINED                  COMPANY ACQUIRED
court and dynasty                     stationed troops
formal internal authority             subsidy or ceded territory
ritual legitimacy                     influence through Resident
some civil administration             control over external relations
                                      veto over foreign employment

                 Result: formal rule without full sovereignty
```

### Plain-language intuition

A subsidiary treaty resembled protection, but the protector decided the threat, stationed the force, influenced diplomacy and charged the protected state. The ruler survived, yet lost the practical means to act independently. This was conquest by redistribution of sovereign functions.

### Standard clauses and actual variation

Common provisions included:

- a permanent Company force stationed in or near the allied state;
- payment of subsidy, sometimes replaced by territorial cession;
- acceptance of a British Resident;
- restrictions on negotiations with other powers;
- restrictions on employing Europeans without Company approval;
- a Company promise of defence.

Not every treaty contained identical language or operated identically. Hyderabad used alliance to manage Maratha danger; Mysore received it after military defeat; Awadh lost territory when subsidy and intervention deepened. Treat form, timing and coercive context as variables.

### Wellesley's expansionary use

Wellesley arrived in 1798 amid global war with France and rejected a stable balance among Indian powers. Hyderabad became the first major state under his systematised version, dismissing French-trained forces. Mysore was placed under a special subsidiary arrangement after 1799. Awadh was compelled in 1801 to cede large territories and accept deeper intervention.

### Costs and advantages

| For the Company | For the allied ruler and society |
|---|---|
| maintained troops at another state's expense | fiscal pressure from subsidy |
| placed forces near strategic centres | reduced independent army |
| controlled diplomacy without immediate annexation | loss of external sovereignty |
| shifted war away from Company core territory | Resident intervention |
| could later allege disorder or payment failure | displaced soldiers and altered patronage |

### Mechanism of dependence

1. The ruler accepts protection against a rival or after defeat.
2. Company troops enter and indigenous forces are reduced.
3. Subsidy strains the treasury.
4. Payment disputes justify territorial cession or administrative advice.
5. The Resident acquires clients and influence at court.
6. The ruler becomes dependent on Company support against external and internal challengers.
7. Formal dynasty survives while effective sovereignty contracts.

### Objection, reply and qualification

**Objection:** Indian rulers were not dupes; some used alliance rationally against immediate rivals.

**Reply:** Correct. Hyderabad and other courts calculated short-term security benefits. But asymmetric enforcement meant the Company could expand the treaty's practical reach while the ally could not equally constrain Company action.

**Qualified assessment:** Subsidiary alliance was both negotiated and coercive. Its historical significance lies in the unequal long-term transfer of military and diplomatic capacity.

### UPSC integration

- Separate **provision** from **purpose** and **consequence**.
- Do not define it as automatic annexation.
- Use "graded sovereignty" to show why a dynasty could survive after political autonomy had narrowed.

2. Wellesley systematised and expanded the instrument from 1798.
3. Hyderabad was the first major acceptor under his aggressive phase.
4. Company troops were maintained through subsidy or cession.
5. A Resident institutionalised political influence.
6. External relations and foreign employment were restricted.
7. Internal non-interference was often promised but not reliably observed.
8. Indigenous military reduction increased dependence.

10. Indian rulers could accept it for rational short-term reasons.
11. Long-term enforcement remained structurally unequal.

## Lesson 7 — Bassein and the Second Anglo-Maratha War: intervention becomes restructuring

### Why Bassein was transformative

The Peshwa was the nominal head of the confederacy. His acceptance of subsidiary dependence therefore affected more than Pune. Baji Rao II gained restoration, but Company forces and a Resident entered the political centre of the confederacy. Sindhia and Bhonsle resisted; Holkar initially remained outside their campaign.

### The military and diplomatic sequence

1. Holkar defeated the combined forces of the Peshwa and Sindhia in October 1802.
2. Baji Rao II fled and signed the Treaty of Bassein at the end of 1802.
3. Company forces restored him at Pune.
4. Sindhia and Bhonsle fought the Company in 1803.
5. Company victories included Assaye and Argaon in the Deccan and Laswari in the north.
6. Raghoji II Bhonsle accepted the Treaty of Deogaon on 17 December 1803; the Company gained Cuttack and influence over Nagpur's external relations.
7. Daulat Rao Sindhia accepted the Treaty of Surji-Anjangaon on 30 December 1803; major northern and western territories were ceded and a Resident entered his court.
8. European employment and inter-house relations became subject to Company restrictions, although later revisions altered some terms.
9. Holkar later fought effectively, and Bharatpur resisted Lord Lake.
10. War costs, Company debt and renewed European danger produced criticism.
11. Wellesley was recalled, and the settlement associated with Rajghat in late 1805, implemented in early 1806, returned much of Holkar's territory and ended that phase of war.

### Who settled, and on what terms?

| Maratha house | Settlement | Strategic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Peshwa, Baji Rao II | Bassein, 1802 | subsidiary restoration placed Company power at Pune |
| Bhonsle of Nagpur | Deogaon, 1803 | ceded Cuttack and accepted restrictions backed by a Resident |
| Sindhia of Gwalior | Surji-Anjangaon, 1803 | surrendered extensive territory after defeats in north and Deccan |
| Holkar of Indore | Rajghat settlement, 1805-06 | peace followed effective resistance and retrenchment; much territory was restored |

This was not one Maratha surrender. Each house entered the war from a different position and received a distinct settlement. The contrast between the severe 1803 terms and the later accommodation with Holkar reveals the effect of resistance, debt and policy reversal.

### Why the Marathas did not fight as one

This was not simple cowardice or blindness. Each house faced different rivals and calculated the danger differently. Holkar had just defeated the Peshwa-Sindhia combination; Gaekwad had a separate relationship with the Company; Sindhia and Bhonsle bore the first resistance. The Company exploited these divisions, but it did not invent them from nothing.

### Limits of Company victory

The war enlarged Company authority, yet Holkar's mobile resistance and the failed storming of Bharatpur revealed operational limits. Expansion also produced debt. London and the Court of Directors could restrain a governor-general when the financial cost threatened Company interests.

### Competing interpretations

**Interpretation 1: British strategic brilliance.** Bassein placed the Company at the confederacy's centre and allowed separate campaigns.

**Interpretation 2: Maratha self-destruction.** Rival chiefs failed to coordinate against a common threat.

**Synthesis:** Company strategy succeeded because it entered an existing confederate conflict with superior fiscal continuity and alliance management. Neither British skill nor Maratha division alone is sufficient.

### The final Maratha contest

By the 1810s, Pindari raiding across central India supplied Lord Hastings with the setting for a vast military mobilisation. The Pindaris were not simply a Maratha national army; they were irregular bands linked in varying ways to the regional order. Hastings used the campaign to compel major chiefs into restrictive arrangements and surround potential resistance.

Before open war, the Treaty of Gwalior in November 1817 constrained Sindhia: he accepted Company direction against the Pindaris and relinquished important claims over Rajput states. In November 1817, Baji Rao II attacked the British Residency at Pune. Bhonsle forces acted at Nagpur, and Holkar forces also fought. Sindhia, already restricted by the Gwalior settlement, did not join a common front.

### Outcome

- Company forces defeated the Peshwa, Bhonsle and Holkar.
- Baji Rao II was deposed and pensioned at Bithur.
- The Peshwaship was abolished.
- Most Peshwa territory was annexed to the Bombay Presidency.
- A small Satara state was created under a descendant of Shivaji, but under Company predominance.
- Holkar accepted the Treaty of Mandsaur in January 1818 after defeat at Mahidpur; the settlement imposed subsidiary dependence and territorial concessions.
- Bhonsle accepted tighter subordinate relations at Nagpur.
- By 1818, Company supremacy extended across most of the subcontinent, though Punjab and Sindh remained outside direct control.

### The final restriction-and-settlement sequence

Gwalior and Mandsaur performed different work. The first reduced the chance that Sindhia would join a common front before the decisive campaign. The second settled the defeated Holkar house afterward. Neither should be confused with the earlier Rajghat accommodation that ended Holkar's part in the Second Anglo-Maratha War.

### Paramountcy as a doctrine of hierarchy

The Company increasingly claimed that its interests stood above those of Indian states. This did not mean that every state became a directly administered district. It meant the Company asserted the right to decide inter-state disputes, shape succession, control external relations, station troops and intervene when it judged imperial security at stake.

### Why indirect rule persisted

1. Direct administration was costly.
2. Dynasties supplied local legitimacy.
3. Princes could collect revenue and maintain order under supervision.
4. Buffer states separated Company territory from external powers.
5. Political variation allowed control to be tailored to local circumstances.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "Paramountcy" merely renamed conquest after victory and had no stable legal content.

**Reply:** Its boundaries were indeed flexible and self-defined, but that flexibility was politically useful. It expressed a real hierarchy in which the Company claimed final authority while preserving different internal forms.

**Qualified judgement:** Paramountcy was less a precise constitutional rule than an imperial doctrine that normalised unequal sovereignty.

## Lesson 9 — Nepal and Burma: frontier war, terrain and the limits of a single conquest model

Progress: 9 / 14 | Stage: Comparative | Focus: Nepal 1814-16 and Burma to 1852

### Nepal, 1814-16

The Gurkha kingdom had expanded from the Nepal valley toward the Sutlej and south into the Tarai. Company acquisition of Gorakhpur brought two expanding powers against an ill-defined border. A clash in 1814 opened war.

British officials expected a quick success but suffered repeated reverses. Mountain defence, local command and Gurkha discipline challenged numerical superiority. Greater Company resources eventually allowed occupation of Kumaon and pressure toward Kathmandu.

The settlement commonly associated with the Treaty of Sugauli required Nepal to cede Kumaon and Garhwal, abandon claims in parts of the Tarai and accept a British Resident. Nepal nevertheless remained a kingdom rather than being annexed. Subsequent relations were comparatively stable, and Gurkha recruitment became important to the Company's army.

### First Burmese War, 1824-26

Burma's expansion into Arakan, Manipur and Assam created a shared and contested frontier. Cross-border raids, refugees and disputes such as Shahpuri Island intensified tension. The Company also sought commercial access and feared rival influence.

The war was exceptionally expensive. Disease and climate killed many more soldiers than battle in some operations. The Treaty of Yandabo in 1826 imposed an indemnity, ceded Arakan and Tenasserim, ended Burmese claims to Assam, Cachar and Jaintia, recognised Manipur's position and opened formal relations.

### Second Burmese War, 1852

Commercial complaints at Rangoon and aggressive Company diplomacy supplied the immediate pretext. Dalhousie's government escalated demands. The Company captured Lower Burmese centres and annexed Pegu without a final negotiated treaty. Resistance continued after formal military victory.

### Comparative lesson

Neither frontier was an empty space awaiting a "natural boundary." The phrase disguised choices by an expanding empire confronting other states. Nepal survived because the settlement balanced strategic access with the cost of further conquest. Burma's coast and commerce encouraged progressive annexation.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Border clashes alone caused these wars.

**Reply:** Border incidents were real, but they operated within wider expansion, commercial ambitions and security fears. A spark is not a complete cause.

**Assessment:** Frontier expansion combined reciprocal state movement with asymmetric Company capacity and self-serving definitions of security.

### Punjab before the wars

Ranjit Singh consolidated a powerful Sikh kingdom and maintained a working balance among military, aristocratic and regional interests. The Treaty of Amritsar in 1809 helped stabilise the Sutlej frontier with the Company. During his lifetime, the Company did not conquer Punjab.

After his death in 1839, rapid succession struggles, assassinations and factional competition weakened central authority. ### First Anglo-Sikh War, 1845-46

Both sides prepared along the frontier. The Sikh army crossed the Sutlej, and war began in December 1845. Sikh soldiers fought strongly, but leadership failures and the conduct of senior officials damaged the campaign.

The Treaty of Lahore in March 1846:

- ceded the Jalandhar Doab to the Company;
- reduced the Lahore army;
- required a large indemnity;
- enabled a British force at Lahore;
- ceded hill territories, including Kashmir, to the Company when the indemnity could not be fully paid.

The Treaty of Amritsar of 16 March 1846 then transferred Kashmir to Gulab Singh in return for payment and recognition of Company supremacy. This was a distinct transaction after Lahore, not a clause that made Kashmir a directly administered Company province.

The Treaty of Bhairowal in December 1846 created a second stage of supervision during Duleep Singh's minority. A British Resident acquired authority over the Lahore administration, a council of regency operated under his direction, and Maharani Jindan was removed from the regency. Punjab retained the child ruler, but effective autonomy was sharply reduced.

### The two-stage Punjab settlement

| Settlement | Date | Function |
|---|---:|---|
| Treaty of Lahore | 9 March 1846 | ended the First War; imposed cession, indemnity, army reduction and a temporary British military presence |
| Treaty of Amritsar | 16 March 1846 | transferred Kashmir to Gulab Singh under a separate Company arrangement |
| Treaty of Bhairowal | December 1846 | deepened Resident supervision of Lahore during Duleep Singh's minority |

The distinction is constitutional as well as chronological: Lahore imposed the post-war settlement, Amritsar reorganised Kashmir, and Bhairowal converted temporary intervention into supervised minority government.

### Second Anglo-Sikh War, 1848-49

The Multan rising under Mulraj and the wider rebellion involving Chattar Singh and Sher Singh developed into renewed war. Early fighting at Ramnagar and Chillianwala was costly for the Company. After the decisive reversal at Gujarat in 1849, resistance collapsed. Dalhousie annexed Punjab on 29 March 1849, and the young Duleep Singh was displaced.

### Competing causal readings

| Reading | What it explains | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| Punjab collapsed from internal disorder | succession and court conflict | Company frontier preparation and intervention |
| British aggression alone caused war | imperial ambition and military concentration | real Lahore instability and autonomous army politics |
| Sikh treachery alone caused defeat | conduct of some leaders | fiscal, command and strategic conditions |
| Interactive explanation | internal crisis met an expansion-ready Company | requires careful, multi-causal narration |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Emphasising internal crisis risks blaming the conquered.

**Reply:** Internal conflict is historical evidence, not moral fault. It becomes determinist only when Company choices, provocations and readiness are omitted.

**Qualified assessment:** Punjab remained militarily formidable; British victory followed political destabilisation, leadership failures, costly combat and progressive supervision—not effortless superiority.

## Lesson 12 — Dalhousie's annexation toolkit: lapse, pension, debt and strategic cession

Progress: 12 / 14 | Stage: Advanced | Focus: why not every annexation used the same doctrine

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** Military predominance and paramountcy created room for more direct absorption.
- **Book context:** Satara, Nagpur, Jhansi, pension disputes and Berar were verified.
- **Exam trap:** The Doctrine of Lapse was one instrument, not a label for every Dalhousie annexation.

### Visual first: choose the actual instrument

```text
Succession without a recognised natural heir
        -> Doctrine of Lapse claim

Subsidy arrears or military-payment pressure
        -> territorial cession / assigned revenue

Title or pension treated as personal
        -> non-recognition after holder's death

Alleged maladministration
        -> annexation on misgovernment grounds

War and defeat
        -> conquest and direct annexation
```

### Doctrine of Lapse

Dalhousie's government refused to recognise an adopted heir as successor to the sovereignty of certain dependent states unless Company approval existed. Adoption could retain private and religious significance while the Company denied its political effect.

Major examples commonly associated with Lapse include:

| State | Year |
|---|---:|
| Satara | 1848 |
| Sambalpur | 1850 |
| Jhansi | 1854 |
| Nagpur | 1854 |

Dates and minor-state lists vary by administrative stage in summaries; the essential principle is the Company's claimed right to decide whether political sovereignty passed to an adopted heir.

### Other instruments

Dalhousie also refused to continue certain titles or pensions as hereditary rights. The treatment of Nana Sahib's claim to the ex-Peshwa's pension later became politically important.

Berar was assigned by Hyderabad in 1853 to meet the expense of the subsidiary force. It was not annexed through Lapse.

Punjab followed war in 1849. Lower Burma followed war in 1852. Awadh followed an allegation of misgovernment in 1856. Grouping all four under Lapse produces factual error.

### Why direct annexation increased

1. Company military supremacy reduced the external risk of absorption.
2. Officials claimed direct administration would be more efficient.
3. Annexed territory offered revenue, raw materials and markets.
4. Uniform administration appealed to centralising imperial ideology.
5. Protected allies appeared less necessary after major rivals had been defeated.

### Strongest defence and critique

**Defence:** The Company claimed that dependent rulers possessed limited sovereignty and that better administration protected subjects.

**Critique:** Paramountcy was self-defined, adoption had established legitimacy, and the alleged welfare standard was selectively invoked by the power that benefited from annexation.

**Qualified assessment:** Some states did suffer administrative problems. That fact does not establish that annexation was the only remedy or that Company judgement was disinterested.

## Lesson 13 — Awadh: from buffer ally to annexed kingdom

Progress: 13 / 14 | Stage: Advanced case study | Focus: 1765-1856

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** subsidy pressure, 1801 cession, Residency growth and the 1856 misgovernment claim were verified.

### Visual first: a ninety-year contraction

```text

        |

        |
rising subsidy + revenue pressure + court intervention
        |

        |

        |

        |

```

### Why Awadh first mattered as a buffer

Awadh separated Bengal from north-western powers and Maratha movement. The Company initially preferred an allied kingdom bearing military costs to the burden of direct frontier administration. A Resident and subsidised garrison gradually increased dependence.

### Fiscal pressure and the 1801 settlement

Rising subsidy demands strained the nawab's treasury and encouraged heavier extraction. The Company also sought secure commercial access and strategic territory. In 1801 Saadat Ali Khan ceded Rohilkhand, Gorakhpur and the Doab in lieu of military obligations. The remaining kingdom retained a court but lost much practical autonomy.

### The Residency effect

The Resident was not a passive ambassador. Company protection and patronage could reshape court alignments, shield clients and weaken the ruler's independent authority. At the same time, the Company criticised the resulting administration. This created a circular logic:

```text
intervention weakens autonomous capacity
              |
              v
administrative deterioration or paralysis
              |
              v
Company cites disorder as reason for more intervention
```

### Annexation in 1856

Wajid Ali Shah had heirs, so the Doctrine of Lapse did not apply. Dalhousie's government alleged chronic misgovernment and annexed the kingdom. Administrative problems were real, but Company responsibility for decades of fiscal and political constraint complicates its humanitarian justification.

### Example and limit

**Example:** Awadh reveals how indirect rule could prepare the conditions later invoked for direct annexation.

**Limit:** It would be equally wrong to deny all internal governance problems. The issue is causation and remedy: who contributed to dysfunction, who judged it, and who benefited from annexation?

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If subjects suffered under poor administration, annexation could be defended as reform.

**Reply:** A welfare claim requires consistent standards and a proportionate remedy. The Company tolerated or deepened the system while Awadh served strategic and fiscal purposes, then unilaterally made itself judge and beneficiary.

**Assessment:** Awadh was not a sudden application of reform. It was the culmination of a long transfer of military, fiscal and political autonomy.

## Lesson 14 — Why the Company won: agency, institutions, contingency and consequences

Progress: 14 / 14 | Stage: Synthesis | Focus: answering the core analytical question

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Dependency:** All regional cases are now available for comparison.
- **PYQ link:** The 2022 GS-I direct question asks why largely Indian Company armies defeated more numerous and better-equipped Indian forces.
- **Final caution:** No single-factor answer can explain nearly a century of uneven conflict.

### Visual first: an argument tree

```text
WHY DID THE COMPANY PREVAIL?
|
+-- Fiscal continuity
|   +-- Bengal revenue
|   +-- Indian credit and indemnities
|
+-- Military organisation
|   +-- salaried sepoys
|   +-- artillery, drill, officer system
|   +-- magazines, transport, naval links
|
+-- Political method
|   +-- one opponent at a time
|   +-- alliances, Residents, separate treaties
|   +-- use of succession and factional openings
|

|   +-- soldiers, bankers, merchants, suppliers
|   +-- allied rulers and local intelligence
|

    +-- Mysore's isolation
    +-- Maratha coordination problem
    +-- post-Ranjit succession crisis
    +-- difficult but bounded frontier resistance
```

### Start by rejecting weak answers

"The British had better weapons" is insufficient. Mysore and Punjab used modern artillery and disciplined formations. Some Indian armies were numerically smaller or better equipped than the premise suggests. "Indian disunity" is also incomplete: the Company itself was an arena of presidency disputes, financial crises and policy reversals.

The better explanation asks why the Company could coordinate resources, recover from defeat and keep opponents separated.

### Six interacting advantages

#### 1. Reliable fiscal mobilisation

Bengal revenue, territorial collections, war indemnities and Indian credit sustained standing forces. A state that could pay troops and suppliers regularly enjoyed a major advantage in long campaigns.

#### 2. Organised military labour

Most Company soldiers were Indian. Drill, unit discipline, artillery coordination and a professional command structure made their labour effective for Company objectives. This was an institutional arrangement, not a racial quality.

#### 3. Logistics and communications

Ports, river routes, magazines, bullock transport, grain contractors and inter-presidency reinforcement mattered as much as combat. Nepal and Burma demonstrate that terrain and disease could still disrupt these systems.

#### 4. Diplomacy and intelligence

The Company cultivated court factions, Residents, merchants and allied rulers. It often prevented simultaneous opposition: Marathas and the Nizam assisted against Mysore; Maratha houses fought separately; Sikh court divisions intersected with frontier preparation.

#### 5. Conversion of victory into capacity

Territorial cessions, subsidies and indemnities were not merely rewards. They funded troops, shortened frontiers and positioned forces for the next conflict.

#### 6. Political persistence

The Company could absorb reverses at Madras, Wadgaon, Bharatpur, Nepal or Chillianwala because its wider structure survived. Many opponents faced succession crises or confederate bargaining that made sustained central coordination harder.

### Indian agency: collaboration and resistance

Indian participation cannot be reduced to "betrayal." A sepoy seeking pay, a banker financing a campaign, a ruler seeking protection and a chief opposing a rival acted within particular constraints. Their choices helped make Company power, but they were not identical in motive or consequence.

Resistance was equally varied: Tipu defended a centralising kingdom; Maratha houses defended overlapping sovereignties; Gurkha forces protected a frontier state; Sikh soldiers acted through the Khalsa order. There was no single national command, but absence of later nationalism does not make resistance unreal.

### Historiographical debate

**Planned empire thesis:** aggressive officials repeatedly sought territorial and commercial advantage.

**Reluctant conquest thesis:** local instability and security threats pulled the Company into unwanted responsibilities.

**Fiscal-military synthesis:** officials did exploit openings, but choices were enabled and channelled by an accumulating system of revenue, troops, alliances and treaty control.

### Consequences by 1856

| Domain | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Political | Company became paramount over annexed and princely territories |
| Military | Indian states lost independent armed and diplomatic capacity |
| Fiscal | war and subsidiary costs were transferred to Indian revenues |
| Social | courts, soldiers and patronage groups were displaced |
| Administrative | direct provinces expanded, increasing governance and regulation needs |
| Strategic | frontiers reached Punjab, Sindh, Himalayas and Lower Burma |
| Resistance | annexation grievances accumulated before 1857 |

### Strongest objection and final judgement

**Objection:** A fiscal-military explanation makes conquest sound mechanical and underplays personalities, errors and chance.

**Reply:** The model is probabilistic, not mechanical. Haidar's manoeuvre, Tipu's choices, Maratha succession, Wellesley's ambition, Sikh leadership and frontier terrain changed outcomes. Institutions explain why the Company repeatedly recovered and capitalised on such events.

**Final judgement:** Company rule emerged from neither simple superiority nor accidental drift. It was a contingent conquest made cumulative by Indian-financed military organisation, strategic diplomacy and progressively unequal claims to sovereignty.

- End with a weighed conclusion, not "divide and rule" as a slogan.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate how major battlefield reverses changed Company strategy without ending its territorial ambitions.

**Model answer**

Company reverses rarely produced a single response. They encouraged changes in coalition policy, operational planning, treaty design and the acceptable cost of expansion.

These cases reveal institutional learning, but not a uniform march toward aggression. Sometimes the Company widened alliances; sometimes it accepted compromise; sometimes it resumed war after reinforcement. Reverses altered method because finance, terrain, political oversight and opponent strength varied.

Thus battlefield checks did not extinguish territorial ambition. They forced the Company to choose among diplomatic isolation, operational adaptation, fiscal retrenchment and renewed concentration of force.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish subsidiary alliance from direct annexation as instruments of Company expansion. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Model answer**

Subsidiary alliance subordinated a state without abolishing its dynasty. The ruler accepted Company troops, paid subsidy or ceded territory, received a Resident and lost freedom in external relations and foreign employment. Civil administration and ritual authority could survive, but independent military and diplomatic capacity contracted.

Direct annexation transferred sovereignty and administration to the Company. It could follow war, as in Punjab and Sindh; disputed succession under the Doctrine of Lapse, as in Jhansi; or alleged misgovernment, as in Awadh.

The instruments were connected. Subsidiary dependence could weaken finances, reduce indigenous armies and expand Residency influence, preparing later territorial cession or annexation. Yet annexation was not inevitable: several princely states remained under indirect rule because dynastic legitimacy, lower administrative cost and buffer functions benefited the Company.

Thus subsidiary alliance produced graded sovereignty, while annexation replaced the ruling authority. Both belonged to a flexible imperial system rather than a single uniform policy.

**Scoring guidance:** 2 marks for definitions, 3 for clauses and consequences, 3 for examples and linkage, 2 for a qualified comparison.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** Compare Mysore and the Maratha confederacy as military-political challenges to Company expansion. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer**

Mysore and the Maratha confederacy were the strongest southern and western challenges to Company growth, but their political structures differed.

Under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, Mysore was a centralising monarchy with a strong revenue base, disciplined infantry, cavalry, artillery, forts and state-directed commerce. It could concentrate resources and repeatedly threaten Madras. Its principal weakness became diplomatic isolation. The Company detached Hyderabad and the Marathas, imposed severe territorial losses in 1792 and destroyed Tipu's remaining strategic position in 1799.

The Maratha polity distributed power among the Peshwa and houses such as Sindhia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad. This confederate form created breadth and resilience: Maratha forces defeated the Company at Wadgaon, and Holkar later resisted effectively. However, succession conflict and divergent house interests complicated a sustained common command. The Company used Raghunath Rao's claim, the Peshwa's crisis at Bassein and separate wars against different chiefs. Final defeat in 1817-18 abolished the Peshwaship but retained subordinate Maratha states.

Both cases refute technological inevitability. Company success relied on Indian allies, revenue, logistics and diplomatic sequencing. Mysore's centralisation made it a concentrated rival vulnerable to isolation; Maratha pluralism prevented easy decapitation but created coordination openings. Their defeat arose from different political vulnerabilities exploited by the same expanding fiscal-military system.

**Scoring guidance:** 5 marks for Mysore, 5 for Marathas, 3 for direct comparison, 2 for qualification and judgement.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** "British paramountcy in India emerged through a continuum from protection to intervention and annexation." Analyse with regional examples. **(20 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer**

British paramountcy was not created by one proclamation. It emerged as the Company progressively redistributed sovereign functions through protection, treaties, war and selective annexation.

Subsidiary alliance was the central bridge. Hyderabad accepted Company troops against regional threats; Mysore was restored under dependence after 1799; and the Peshwa accepted Bassein in 1802. Rulers survived, but defence, diplomacy and foreign employment shifted toward the Company. Residents turned formal alliance into continuing court influence.

Intervention could then deepen through fiscal pressure. Awadh paid for Company troops, ceded extensive territory in 1801 and experienced growing Residency control before annexation for alleged misgovernment in 1856. Berar was assigned in 1853 for subsidiary-force costs. These cases show sovereignty eroded before direct rule.

War remained crucial. Maratha defeat in 1817-18 ended the Peshwaship; Sindh was annexed in 1843; Punjab passed from the restrictive Treaty of Lahore to direct annexation in 1849. Frontier cases varied: Nepal accepted territorial loss and a Resident but survived, while Lower Burma was progressively absorbed.

Dalhousie's Lapse policy added succession as an annexation ground in Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur, but it did not explain Punjab, Awadh or Berar.

Paramountcy therefore meant graded, unequal sovereignty: directly ruled provinces, subsidiary states, protected buffers and threatened dynasties. The continuum was flexible rather than automatic, shaped by local resistance, administrative cost and imperial calculations.

**Scoring guidance:** 4 marks for concept, 10 for regionally varied evidence, 4 for continuum analysis, 2 for qualification and conclusion.

- **D — Correct:** The combination of surviving heirs and the governance justification directly separates Awadh from a Doctrine of Lapse case.

## Misconception 9: "Frontier wars were merely defensive"

**Correction:** Reciprocal border movement interacted with Company commerce and self-defined security.

**MCQ 61.** Which explanation best treats frontier conflict without denying genuine border disputes?

A. Neighbouring expansion generated genuine disputes, while Company choices about commerce and security shaped how those disputes escalated.
B. Real border incidents explain escalation chiefly where the Company had no wider interest in routes, markets or strategic depth.
C. Frontier geography influenced military operations, but political definitions of security remained stable and mutually accepted before conflict.
D. Commercial access mattered in Burma and Sindh, whereas the Company's Nepal policy was unrelated to competing territorial expansion.

**MCQ 61: A**

- **A — Correct:** The formulation recognises reciprocal frontier movement while preserving Company agency in defining threats and choosing escalation.
- **B — Incorrect:** Border incidents interacted with wider strategic and commercial interests rather than becoming important chiefly in their absence.
- **C — Incorrect:** Geography mattered, yet the desired boundary and meaning of security were contested political choices rather than stable mutual agreements.
- **D — Incorrect:** Nepal involved overlapping territorial expansion and Company frontier security, even though commercial motives were more prominent in Burma and Sindh.

## Misconception 10: "Indian participants all collaborated for the same reason"

**Correction:** Soldiers, bankers, suppliers and rulers made different choices under different constraints.

**MCQ 62.** Which approach best incorporates Indian agency into the history of Company conquest?

A. Classify sepoys, bankers and allied rulers mainly by whether their choices objectively aided or resisted later nationalism.
B. Examine distinct Indian motives for pay, credit, security and patronage while tracing how Company institutions converted them into sovereignty.
C. Treat service, credit and alliance as equivalent political commitments because each contributed material resources to Company campaigns.
D. Emphasise Indian motives within each war while leaving the institutional destination of revenue, treaties and territorial gains analytically open.

**MCQ 62: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Later nationalism is a poor single measure for eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century choices made over pay, security, profit and rivalry.
- **B — Correct:** It takes individual motives seriously while tracing the unequal institutional outcome produced by their combined participation.
- **C — Incorrect:** Material contribution does not make a sepoy's employment, a banker's credit and a ruler's alliance the same political commitment.
- **D — Incorrect:** Motive alone is insufficient; analysis must identify that revenue, treaty power and territorial authority accumulated in Company institutions.

## Misconception 11: "Paramountcy meant identical administration everywhere"

**Correction:** It organised a hierarchy of direct and indirect control.

**MCQ 63.** Which political map is most consistent with Company paramountcy after 1818?

A. Direct provinces and princely courts retained separate foreign policies but shared Company military protection on negotiated terms.
B. Subsidiary states and protected rulers exercised equal treaty rights, while annexed provinces supplied the common imperial revenue.
C. Company provinces, allied states and protected dynasties occupied different positions beneath its paramount claim.
D. Resident-supervised courts retained internal variation, while each held a reciprocal veto over Company intervention in neighbouring states.

**MCQ 63: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Administrative forms differed, but princely foreign policy was normally constrained rather than separately sovereign.
- **B — Incorrect:** The order was hierarchical, so subsidiary and protected rulers did not possess treaty rights equal to the Company.
- **C — Correct:** Paramountcy combined direct and indirect rule by placing varied territories beneath the Company's asserted superior authority.
- **D — Incorrect:** Residents could influence courts, but subordinate states lacked a reciprocal veto over Company war or intervention.

## Misconception 12: "Expansion was either planned or accidental"

**Correction:** Deliberate choices worked through accumulated capacities amid genuine uncertainty.

**MCQ 64.** Which final judgement best accommodates policy change and institutional continuity?

A. Policy changed with governors-general, and retrenchment usually dissolved the military and fiscal leverage created by earlier acquisitions.
B. Local contingencies altered implementation, while a detailed territorial programme established before Bengal fixed the eventual sequence.
C. Institutional growth increased campaign capacity and therefore made resistance, personality and metropolitan restraint secondary across the period's outcomes.
D. Earlier settlements enlarged reusable capacity, although later interventions still depended on changing policy, resistance and circumstance.

**MCQ 64: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Retrenchment could return territory or slow war, but it did not erase the standing armies, revenues and treaty leverage already accumulated.
- **B — Incorrect:** Contingency mattered, yet there was no pre-Bengal schedule fixing the Mysore, Maratha, frontier and annexation sequence.
- **C — Incorrect:** Institutions improved the Company's options, but battlefield reverses, personalities, debt and metropolitan oversight still altered outcomes.
- **D — Correct:** The judgement links institutional continuity to genuine policy change and uncertainty without reducing either side of the explanation.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core thesis

- British territorial expansion was a cumulative fiscal-military process.
- Bengal revenue supplied a major base, but Indian credit, soldiers, suppliers and allies remained essential.
- Company advantage lay in coordination and recovery, not inherent European superiority.
- Victories generated new revenue, strategic routes and treaty leverage.
- Diplomacy usually sought to isolate opponents and avoid a united front.
- Expansion moved through alliance, subsidy, Residency, cession, paramountcy and annexation.
- Policy varied by governor-general and could be checked by cost, London oversight, terrain or resistance.

## 2. Mysore

- Wandiwash (1760) and the Treaty of Paris (1763) curtailed direct French territorial competition.
- The Madras establishment retained forts, sepoy forces, artillery practice and coastal supply networks from the presidency struggle.
- French officers serving Indian rulers did not amount to French state or Company control over those states.
- Haidar Ali built an adaptive military state and became effective ruler in the 1760s.
- First War, 1767-69: shifting alliances; Haidar threatened Madras; Treaty of Madras.
- Company non-assistance against the Marathas in 1771 deepened mistrust.
- Second War, 1780-84: early Mysorean success; Porto Novo, 1781; Haidar died, 1782; Treaty of Mangalore, 1784.
- Tipu strengthened central administration, forces, forts and state-regulated commerce.
- Third War, 1790-92: Travancore trigger; Company-Nizam-Maratha coalition; Treaty of Seringapatam.
- Mysore ceded about half its territory and paid indemnity.
- Fourth War, 1799: Wellesley invoked French danger; Tipu died at Seringapatam.
- Wodeyars restored in a reduced subsidiary kingdom.
- Best explanation: Mysorean capacity was overcome by isolation, territorial loss and a wider Company resource system.

## 3. Marathas

- Confederacy included Peshwa, Sindhia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad.
- Distributed authority created resilience and coordination difficulty.
- First War, 1775-82: Raghunath Rao intervention; Wadgaon, 1779; Treaty of Salbai, 1782.
- Purandar (1776) was an attempted compromise; Wadgaon (1779) a field capitulation; Salbai (1782) the wider negotiated peace.
- Salbai produced relative peace without destroying Maratha power.
- Treaty of Bassein, 1802: Baji Rao II accepted subsidiary restoration after Holkar's victory at Pune.
- Second War: Bhonsle accepted Deogaon and Sindhia Surji-Anjangaon in December 1803.
- Holkar later resisted; the Rajghat settlement of 1805-06 returned much of his territory.
- Bharatpur and rising debt exposed Company limits; Wellesley recalled.
- Third War: the November 1817 Treaty of Gwalior constrained Sindhia before the wider conflict.
- Holkar's defeat at Mahidpur was followed by the Treaty of Mandsaur in January 1818.
- Baji Rao II deposed and pensioned at Bithur; Peshwaship abolished.
- Dependent Satara preserved selected dynastic legitimacy.
- By 1818, Company predominance covered most of the subcontinent.

## 4. Subsidiary Alliance and paramountcy

- Wellesley systematised aggressive subsidiary policy from 1798.
- Hyderabad was the first major acceptor in this phase.
- Common terms: Company troops, subsidy or cession, Resident, restricted diplomacy and European employment.
- Short-term protection could be rational for Indian rulers.
- Long-term asymmetry weakened finances, armies and external sovereignty.
- Mysore, Awadh and the Peshwa entered through different coercive contexts.
- Paramountcy was a flexible claim of superior Company authority.
- It permitted graded sovereignty: provinces, subsidiary states, protected rulers and buffers.
- Indirect rule saved administrative cost and retained local legitimacy.

## 5. Frontier cases

- Nepal War, 1814-16: two expanding powers met at an uncertain border.
- Gurkha defence imposed reverses; Company resources eventually prevailed.
- Nepal ceded Kumaon and Garhwal, accepted a Resident and survived as a kingdom.
- First Burmese War, 1824-26: Assam-Manipur-Arakan frontier, commerce and rival influence.
- Disease and climate made the war exceptionally costly.
- Treaty of Yandabo imposed indemnity and territorial cessions.
- Second Burmese War, 1852: commercial complaints and escalating demands; Pegu annexed.
- Sindh: Indus trade opened in 1832; Afghan strategy and subsidiary pressure followed.
- Charles Napier annexed Sindh in 1843.
- "Natural frontier" was a political claim, not geographic inevitability.

## 6. Punjab

- Ranjit Singh maintained a powerful state and stable frontier arrangement.
- His death in 1839 was followed by succession conflict, court violence and fiscal strain.
- Khalsa army panchayats became political actors.
- First War, 1845-46: Sikh fighting capacity remained strong; leadership failures mattered.
- Treaty of Lahore ceded Jalandhar Doab, reduced army and expanded Company influence.
- Treaty of Amritsar, 16 March 1846, separately transferred Kashmir to Gulab Singh.
- Treaty of Bhairowal, December 1846, deepened Resident supervision during Duleep Singh's minority.
- Second War, 1848-49: Multan revolt; Ramnagar and Chillianwala; decisive Company victory at Gujarat.
- Punjab annexed on 29 March 1849.
- Internal instability and Company expansion must be analysed together.

## 7. Dalhousie and annexation grounds

- Lapse denied automatic political succession to an adopted heir without Company recognition.
- Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur are major examples.
- Adoption as a personal-religious institution was distinct from recognised sovereignty.
- Punjab followed war; Pegu followed war; Berar followed fiscal assignment.
- Pension and title discontinuance were separate methods.
- Awadh was annexed for alleged misgovernment, not Lapse.
- Direct rule promised revenue, markets and centralisation but created legitimacy grievances.

## 8. Awadh

- Began as a strategic buffer and subsidised ally.
- Resident and garrison expanded Company influence.
- Rising subsidy contributed to fiscal pressure.
- The 1801 cession transferred Rohilkhand, Gorakhpur and the Doab.
- Residency politics weakened autonomous authority.
- Company then criticised administrative failures in a system it had helped constrain.
- Wajid Ali Shah had heirs.
- Annexation in 1856 rested on alleged misgovernment.
- Court, military and landed displacement formed a bridge to 1857.

## 9. Why Company armies won

- Regular revenue and credit helped sustain pay.
- Organisation integrated Indian manpower with drill, artillery and command.
- Ports, magazines, transport and contractors supported campaign endurance.
- Residents, intelligence and alliances isolated opponents.
- Territorial gains financed future operations.
- Indian powers had specific, not generic, constraints.
- Mysore was isolated; Maratha command was distributed; Punjab destabilised after 1839.
- Company reverses prove that victory was not automatic.
- Indian participation involved varied motives and real agency.
- Best conclusion: contingent conquest became cumulative through institutional capacity.
