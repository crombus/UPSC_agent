# Integration of Princely States and Making of the Republic - Live Learning Session

> **Learner-first lock:** This session explains how independence left a territorial and constitutional problem that could not be solved by a flag change alone. It distinguishes accession from merger, administration from democratisation, and territorial consolidation from republican incorporation. Patel's political leadership is taught with V.P. Menon's institutional work, rulers' calculations, popular movements, negotiated guarantees and the coercive power of the new Union. No single-hero or single-method story is sufficient.

## Lesson 1 - Independence leaves a princely-state vacuum

### Visual: what lapsed, what did not automatically follow

```text
BEFORE 15 AUGUST 1947
British Crown
   |
   +-- directly governs British Indian provinces
   |
   +-- exercises paramountcy over princely rulers
          defence protection
          external control
          political supervision
          recognition and intervention
```

*Lapse removed the British relationship; it did not create hundreds of equally viable modern sovereign states.*

### Start with the intuition

Imagine a network of islands whose defence, railways, postal links and external relations have long been managed through a larger power. If that power disappears, each island may acquire a formal claim to decide its future. It does not thereby acquire an army, currency system, port, railway network or popular legitimacy.

✅ **Fact:** More than 560 princely states existed at independence; totals such as 562 or 565 vary with the classification of estates and states. The safe formulation is **more than 560**.

✅ **Fact:** These states occupied a large share of the subcontinent and differed enormously. Hyderabad was territorially and fiscally substantial; many others were tiny, fragmented or enclosed.

✅ **Fact:** British paramountcy lapsed on 15 August 1947. It was not automatically transferred to either successor dominion.

⚠️ **Inference:** The resulting problem had two levels:

1. **Legal level:** the old Crown-prince relationship ended.
2. **State-capacity level:** few rulers could provide defence, foreign representation, currency stability, communications and economic access independently.

### Paramountcy was not the same as annexation

Under indirect rule, a prince governed internally to varying degrees, but British power remained superior. It could regulate succession, external relations and strategic affairs, place Residents in states, and intervene politically. The arrangement preserved princely forms while limiting effective sovereignty.

| Term | What it meant | What it did not mean |
|---|---|---|
| British province | Territory directly administered under British Indian government | A princely state under treaty and paramountcy |
| Princely state | Internally ruled by a recognised prince under British supremacy | A fully equal sovereign in international affairs |
| Paramountcy | The Crown's superior political relationship with rulers | Ordinary provincial administration |
| Lapse | Termination of that Crown relationship | Automatic accession to India or Pakistan |

### Why formal independence looked possible

The 20 February 1947 British announcement stated that paramountcy obligations would not be handed to a government of British India. Some rulers read this as an opening to independent status. Jinnah publicly emphasised the states' freedom when paramountcy ended. Travancore, Bhopal and Hyderabad were among the prominent states that contemplated or announced independence.

But formal choice was constrained:

```text
No paramount power
      +
territorial enclosure or difficult access
      +
dependence on rail, post, ports, currency and markets
      +
popular demands for responsible government
      +
India/Pakistan security power
      =
independence legally arguable but often politically and materially fragile
```

### The people's claim

The nationalist answer rejected the idea that sovereignty belonged only to rulers. People in princely states had participated in wider nationalism and in Praja Mandal or States' Peoples' movements. Their demand joined two objectives:

- integration with the wider Indian nation; and
- responsible government within the state.

This is crucial. The integration problem was not simply **Delhi versus princes**. It was also **subjects versus autocratic rule**.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If paramountcy lapsed, every ruler became completely free to remain independent.

**Reply:** Lapse created a formal opening but not an unlimited practical right insulated from geography, popular will and successor-state security. The Indian position also denied that a ruler alone embodied the state. A mature answer must separate the strict legal discontinuity from the political contest over who could exercise sovereignty.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Paramountcy lapsed; it was not transferred.
- **Mains:** Begin with the gap between juridical choice and practical interdependence.
- **Trap:** Princely states were not automatically British provinces on 15 August.
- **Answer line:** "The end of empire dissolved the umbrella of paramountcy before it created a common constitutional roof."

### Revision notes

1. British India contained provinces and princely states under different legal arrangements.
2. Princely autonomy varied widely.
3. Paramountcy meant superior British political control, not direct provincial rule.
4. Paramountcy lapsed on 15 August 1947.
5. It did not pass automatically to either dominion.
6. More than 560 states existed; exact totals vary by classification.
7. Formal independence did not solve defence, external affairs or communications.
8. Geography and economic interdependence narrowed rulers' choices.
9. Praja Mandals located sovereignty in the people, not merely the prince.
10. Integration therefore combined international-style accession, internal democratisation and administrative consolidation.

- **A — Correct:** Paramountcy lapsed, while geography, security and economic links constrained the formally open choice.
- **B — Incorrect:** Standstill agreements were negotiated instruments, not an automatic conversion of Crown treaties or a universal electoral procedure.
- **C — Incorrect:** Lapse removed British supremacy but did not make every state materially capable of independent foreign and defence policy.
- **D — Incorrect:** Princely states required new relations, but they were not suspended outside all dominion interaction until a single later settlement.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Popular legitimacy was an additional problem, but this option omits the decisive material interdependence tested by the question.
- **B — Correct:** A formal claim could not by itself supply the networks and capacities on which most states depended.
- **C — Incorrect:** British protection ended with paramountcy; it did not remain available as the foundation of independence.
- **D — Incorrect:** Accession commonly preceded fuller merger, so the sequence assumed here is reversed.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** The Chamber represented princely interests and therefore did not most directly displace the ruler as the source of sovereignty.
- **B — Incorrect:** The Department dealt with rulers but did not itself embody the subjects' popular claim.

#### Local Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the lapse of paramountcy create a political crisis rather than a stable system of independent princely states?

**Model answer:**
The lapse of British paramountcy on 15 August 1947 ended the Crown's superior relationship with more than 560 princely states. It did not automatically transfer them to India or Pakistan, creating a formal space for rulers to choose.

Yet juridical freedom did not produce viable sovereignty. Many states were small, fragmented or enclosed within a dominion. Defence, external relations, railways, post, currency and markets linked them to the subcontinental system. Travancore, Bhopal and Hyderabad could contemplate independence, but even large states faced strategic and economic constraints.

Sovereignty was also contested internally. Praja Mandals and the States' Peoples' movement demanded responsible government and treated the inhabitants, not rulers alone, as members of the Indian nation.

Thus the crisis arose from a mismatch: empire withdrew its integrating authority before a republican successor framework had absorbed the states. Accession had to reconcile legal discontinuity, practical interdependence and popular legitimacy.

**Why this earns marks:** The model moves from legal lapse to capacity, popular legitimacy and a qualified state-building conclusion.

**Scoring note:** **Component rubric (10):** lapse and non-transfer 2 + material interdependence 2 + internal popular challenge 2 + crisis mechanism 2 + qualified conclusion 2 = 10.

*Patel supplied political authority and strategic firmness; Menon and the Department converted that authority into negotiable documents, visits and administrative follow-through.*

### The learner's problem

If Delhi demanded complete surrender immediately, rulers might unite in resistance. If it accepted indefinite independence, the Union could fragment. The strategy therefore had to ask for enough to secure India, but not so much that every ruler refused.

✅ **Fact:** The States Department was created on **27 June 1947**. Patel assumed charge and V.P. Menon became its Secretary.

### Why the offer was narrow

The initial Instrument of Accession focused on:

1. defence;
2. external affairs; and
3. communications.

These were the minimum functions needed to keep the subcontinent from becoming a patchwork of strategic enclaves. The ruler retained internal authority at this first stage.

⚠️ **Inference:** Narrowness was the instrument's political genius. It transformed accession from an immediate demand for abolition into a limited transfer that many rulers could sign without publicly admitting total defeat.

### Patel without a hero-only simplification

Patel's contribution included:

- recognising that delay could permit coordinated resistance;
- offering reassurance while preserving a firm deadline;
- using his reputation for decisiveness as bargaining leverage;
- accepting guarantees and staged integration rather than demanding instant uniformity;
- authorising differentiated responses when persuasion failed.

But Patel did not operate alone. Menon drafted, explained and carried instruments; Mountbatten used his standing with princes; state officials implemented arrangements; popular movements weakened autocratic claims; rulers and diwans made calculations; Nehru and the Cabinet shaped policy, especially in Kashmir and Hyderabad.

| Incomplete story | Better explanation |
|---|---|
| One leader personally persuaded every prince | Political authority worked through a department, officials and negotiated instruments |
| Princes joined only from patriotism | Patriotism, fear, realism, popular pressure and material dependence varied by state |
| Integration was entirely voluntary | Most accessions were negotiated, but pressure and later force were part of the repertoire |
| Force alone unified India | The overwhelming initial process used documents, guarantees and sequencing |

### The pressure behind persuasion

Patel's appeal carried an implied warning: terms after 15 August might be less favourable, and popular movements could become harder to restrain. Princes also feared more radical demands for abolition without compensation.

```text
Offer now:
limited accession + continuity + negotiated dignity
                     versus
Delay:
uncertainty + popular mobilisation + weaker bargaining position + possible coercion
```

This was not free consent in an empty room. Nor was every signature extracted at gunpoint. It was **bargaining under asymmetric power and a closing deadline**.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling the process negotiated disguises coercion.

**Reply:** Coercive capacity was real and must be acknowledged. Yet identical coercion cannot explain why most states signed limited instruments before military action, why guarantees mattered, or why different cases followed different routes. The accurate category is calibrated statecraft: persuasion, concession, time pressure, popular leverage and force in reserve.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** States Department - 27 June 1947; Patel as minister; V.P. Menon as Secretary.
- **Mains:** Evaluate leadership through institution, instrument, leverage and outcome.
- **Trap:** Do not erase Menon, Mountbatten, popular movements or the Cabinet.
- **Answer line:** "Patel's decisiveness became historically effective because Menon translated a political objective into a repeatable legal-administrative process."

### Revision notes

1. The States Department was established on 27 June 1947.
2. Patel held political charge; V.P. Menon served as Secretary.
3. The first demand was limited accession, not immediate uniform administration.
4. Defence, external affairs and communications formed the common core.
5. A narrow offer reduced the political cost of signing.
6. Deadline pressure weakened the attraction of delay.
7. Popular agitation strengthened Delhi's bargaining position.
8. Guarantees reassured rulers about dignity and material interests.
9. Mountbatten's access to princes supplemented Patel and Menon.
10. The process mixed consent, constraint and coercive capacity.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess the respective roles of Sardar Patel and V.P. Menon in the integration of princely states.

**Model answer:**
Patel and Menon formed a political-administrative partnership, but their achievement depended on a wider field of actors and pressures.

As Home and States Minister, Patel defined territorial fragmentation as an urgent security problem. He offered accession on defence, external affairs and communications, reassured rulers that internal authority would not vanish immediately, and warned that delay would weaken their terms. His credibility made the combination of conciliation and firmness persuasive.

V.P. Menon, Secretary of the States Department created on 27 June 1947, translated strategy into instruments and negotiations. He explained legal choices, travelled to courts, worked with diwans, prepared accession and merger arrangements, and later helped convert signatures into unions and administrations.

Their leverage was not personal alone. Mountbatten influenced princes; Praja Mandals challenged ruler-only sovereignty; geography and communications made isolation costly; the Cabinet and armed forces supplied state capacity. Hyderabad also shows that coercion entered when negotiation failed.

Therefore Patel provided political will and calibrated authority, while Menon supplied operational architecture. Their importance lies not in solitary heroism but in sequencing limited accession, negotiated guarantees and later consolidation before princely resistance could combine.

**Why this earns marks:** The model separates political authority from administrative execution and then locates both within a wider field of power.

### Visual: integration was a staircase, not one signature

```text
STEP 1                  STEP 2                 STEP 3                 STEP 4
ACCESSION               CONTINUITY             AUTHORITY              INCORPORATION
Instrument of           Standstill             Merger Agreement       Covenant / Constitution
Accession               Agreement              or transfer            of a Union
   |                        |                       |                       |
Dominion may legislate   existing practical     ruler cedes fuller     states combine under
on scheduled subjects    arrangements continue  governing power        common institutions
   |                        |                       |                       |
not full internal        not accession           not identical in       later Part B or other
absorption               by itself               every state            constitutional status
```

*The documents answered different questions: who controls common sovereign functions, what continues tomorrow, who governs internally, and what constitutional unit finally exists?*

### 1. Instrument of Accession

The Instrument enabled a ruler to accede to a dominion and authorise dominion institutions to act on listed subjects. In the standard limited arrangement, those subjects were defence, external affairs and communications, with related ancillary matters.

It did **not by itself**:

- abolish the ruler;
- merge the territory into a neighbouring province;
- create responsible government;
- standardise courts, revenue or civil services; or
- settle every later federal question.

### 2. Standstill Agreement

A Standstill Agreement aimed to preserve existing administrative arrangements while a long-term settlement was negotiated. It mattered because postal, rail, customs, trade and other connections could not simply stop at midnight.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **Accession allocates authority; standstill avoids interruption.**

Hyderabad signed a Standstill Agreement with India in November 1947 while continuing to negotiate. Kashmir offered standstill arrangements to both dominions; Pakistan accepted, but subsequent events made the arrangement insufficient to answer the security crisis.

### 3. Merger Agreement

Merger went beyond the narrow accession. A ruler transferred fuller governing authority to the Dominion government, allowing the state to be absorbed into a neighbouring province or administered in a new form.

### 4. Covenant

Where several states combined into a union, rulers entered a covenant establishing common institutions and often a **Rajpramukh**, usually selected from among prominent rulers. This route helped turn many small or medium principalities into larger administrable units such as PEPSU, Saurashtra, Madhya Bharat and Rajasthan.

### Same destination, differentiated routes

| Situation | Likely route | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Large state initially retaining internal government | Accession first | Rapidly secures common sovereign subjects |
| Transitional uncertainty in services | Standstill | Prevents administrative rupture |
| Small state unsuitable as a separate unit | Merger with province or centrally administered unit | Creates viable administration |
| Cluster of related states | Covenant forming a union | Pools authority while managing ruler status |
| Former state entering 1950 framework | Constitutional incorporation | Converts interim arrangements into Union structure |

### Privy purses and guarantees

Rulers surrendered authority in return for settlements that could include privy purses, titles, ceremonial privileges and recognition of succession. The Constitution originally protected aspects of this settlement, including privy-purse payments and regard for guarantees.

⚠️ **Inference:** These guarantees can be read in two ways:

- **Pragmatic settlement:** a limited fiscal and symbolic price for rapid, peaceful transfer.
- **Democratic compromise:** privileges for former autocrats sat uneasily beside republican equality.

The later abolition in 1971 belongs only as a bounded endpoint. It must not be projected backward as if the original bargain contained immediate abolition.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Once a state signed the Instrument of Accession, integration was complete.

**Reply:** Accession secured specified powers for the Dominion. It did not automatically create administrative uniformity, democracy or a stable federal unit. Merger, covenant, departmental integration, representation and constitutional classification were separate achievements.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Accession and standstill are not synonyms.
- **Mains:** Use the four-stage distinction to prevent narrative confusion.
- **Trap:** Privy purses were part of the original settlement and abolished later, not in 1947.
- **Answer spine:** accession -> continuity -> transfer/consolidation -> constitutional incorporation.

### Revision notes

1. The Instrument of Accession transferred authority on listed subjects.
2. The common limited subjects were defence, external affairs and communications.
3. It did not itself abolish internal princely government.
4. A Standstill Agreement preserved existing administrative arrangements.
5. Standstill did not equal final accession.
6. Merger agreements transferred fuller governing authority.
7. Covenants created unions of states and common institutions.
8. Rajpramukhs connected former rulers to transitional union government.
9. Privy purses and privileges formed part of the negotiated settlement.
10. Constitutional incorporation completed a stage that accession alone could not.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Accession was necessary but insufficient for the political integration of princely India. Explain.

**Model answer:**
The Instrument of Accession solved the immediate sovereign problem by authorising the Dominion to act on defence, external affairs and communications. Its limited scope made rapid signatures possible, but it left most internal administration with rulers.

Integration therefore required additional instruments. Standstill Agreements preserved rail, postal, trade and other arrangements during negotiation; they maintained continuity but did not settle sovereignty. Merger agreements transferred fuller governing authority and enabled small states to enter neighbouring provinces or central administration. Covenants combined clusters into unions such as PEPSU, Saurashtra, Madhya Bharat and Rajasthan, often under a Rajpramukh.

Administrative consolidation then had to unify revenue, courts, police and services. Praja Mandal pressure required responsible government, showing that territorial accession without democratisation would preserve princely subjecthood. The settlement also included privy purses and guarantees: a pragmatic price for peaceful transfer but an uneasy compromise with republican equality.

Finally, representation in the Constituent Assembly and the 1950 Part A, B and C framework gave the new units a constitutional place.

Thus accession closed the external sovereignty gap; merger, democratisation and constitutional incorporation converted a set of signatures into a functioning Union.

**Why this earns marks:** The model defines each instrument by function and shows why administration, democracy and constitutional status required later stages.

### Why these cases matter

The famous three crises can make integration look like a sequence of invasion, plebiscite and military action. That is misleading. Most states entered through negotiation. The quieter cases reveal how the strategy normally worked.

### Travancore

Travancore's rulers initially claimed the capacity for independence, drawing confidence from resources, coastal access and established administration. Yet independence required more than an announcement. Trade, defence, communications, popular politics and relations with the surrounding Indian Union narrowed the option. Negotiations ultimately secured accession.

### Bhopal

The Nawab of Bhopal had influence in princely circles and explored continued independence. But a collective princes' front did not consolidate. Once many rulers signed, resistance became increasingly isolated. Bhopal demonstrates a network effect:

```text
each additional accession
        |
        v
reduces possibility of a coordinated princely bloc
        |
        v
raises economic and political cost for remaining hold-outs
```

### Jodhpur

Jodhpur's ruler considered Pakistan's offer despite the state's social composition and location within the western Indian landmass. Negotiations addressed practical interests and induced accession to India.

⚠️ **Evidence caution:** Popular dramatic stories about a weapon being brandished during the Jodhpur negotiation are not necessary to explain the decision and should not carry an answer. The stronger evidence is geography, bargaining and the offer of safeguards.

### Negotiation as a changing game

Rulers did not decide simultaneously. The strategy benefited from cumulative momentum:

1. Secure cooperative states.
2. Demonstrate that the limited instrument preserves internal authority.
3. Prevent a common resistance front.
4. Isolate prominent hold-outs.
5. Shift from accession to fuller integration once external fragmentation is prevented.

### Consent and asymmetry

The signatures were legally meaningful. But the bargaining field was unequal. India controlled surrounding territory, transport, markets and growing coercive capacity. Popular organisations questioned princely legitimacy. Rulers also received guarantees.

The correct evaluation is neither "pure voluntary union" nor "mere annexation." It is a spectrum:

```text
persuasion ---- bargaining ---- deadline pressure ---- popular mobilisation ---- coercive action
   most accessions                                             exceptional crises
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If most rulers signed, consent settles the legitimacy debate.

**Reply:** Consent by a ruler answers one legal question but not whether subjects had representative government. Nor does it erase unequal bargaining power. Legitimacy grew through popular support, democratisation and constitutional citizenship, not through signatures alone.

- **Answer line:** "The success of accession became self-reinforcing: every signature weakened the feasibility of the next ruler's isolation."

### Chronology before judgement

✅ **Fact:** Junagadh was a coastal state in Saurashtra, surrounded on land by Indian territory and without land contiguity with Pakistan.

✅ **Fact:** Its Muslim Nawab chose accession to Pakistan despite an overwhelmingly Hindu population. Pakistan accepted the accession.

✅ **Fact:** A popular movement opposed the decision. A provisional government, commonly known as the **Arzi Hukumat**, was formed under Samaldas Gandhi.

✅ **Fact:** As administration deteriorated and the Nawab left, Dewan Shah Nawaz Bhutto invited the Government of India to assume administration in November 1947.

✅ **Fact:** A plebiscite in February 1948 overwhelmingly endorsed union with India.

### The legal-political problem

If the ruler alone possessed the legal capacity to sign, accession to Pakistan had a formal argument. India replied through geography and popular sovereignty. This created a principle problem because the same tests could not be invoked selectively without consequences for other disputed states.

| Claim | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Ruler's signature | Used the accession form available to states | Ignored population and practical geography |
| Contiguity | Made governance and communications intelligible | Was a political criterion, not a mechanical universal clause |
| Popular will | Connected sovereignty to inhabitants | Plebiscite occurred after Indian administrative takeover |
| Administrative necessity | Responded to collapse and invitation by the Dewan | India was an interested party, not a neutral external manager |

### The provisional government

The Arzi Hukumat mattered because it showed that opposition was not only a diplomatic claim by Delhi. Yet it should not be romanticised as a complete substitute for regular representative government. It operated during a rapidly escalating contest with Indian support and pressure around the state.

### Why Junagadh cannot simply be mapped onto Kashmir

Both disputes raised ruler, population and plebiscite arguments, but their geography and security sequences differed:

- Junagadh's ruler had already acceded to Pakistan despite lack of land contiguity.
- Kashmir's ruler had not acceded when tribal forces invaded; accession to India followed the security emergency.
- Junagadh's plebiscite occurred under Indian administration and settled control in practice.
- Kashmir moved into interstate war and the United Nations.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The plebiscite conclusively removes every legal-political controversy from the Junagadh process.

**Reply:** It supplied powerful evidence of popular preference, but historical analysis should still note the sequence: popular resistance, Indian pressure and takeover preceded the vote. Legitimacy was cumulative, not supplied by a timeless abstract referendum.

*Hyderabad was not one quarrel. It combined an external sovereignty dispute, a democratic struggle and an escalating security crisis.*

### Why Hyderabad was exceptional

Hyderabad was the largest princely state and lay within Indian territory. The Nizam sought independence rather than immediate accession. Unlike a small enclave, Hyderabad possessed substantial revenue, administration and armed forces, making delay more plausible. Yet its landlocked position inside India made durable external sovereignty strategically difficult.

### Standstill was a pause, not a settlement

India and Hyderabad concluded a Standstill Agreement in November 1947. India hoped that continued negotiation would lead to accession and representative government. The Nizam used the interval to preserve independence and improve his bargaining position.

The agreement therefore contained opposite expectations:

| Indian expectation | Nizam's expectation |
|---|---|
| Continuity while moving toward accession | Continuity while preserving sovereign independence |
| Representative government eases merger | Delay strengthens military and diplomatic leverage |
| Time exposes impracticality of isolation | Time may win improved status |

### Internal Hyderabad

Three political forces must be distinguished:

1. **Nizam's state:** defended dynastic authority and independence.
2. **Hyderabad State Congress:** demanded responsible government and launched satyagraha in August 1947.
3. **Ittehad-ul-Muslimeen and Razakars:** a militant political-paramilitary force led by Qasim Razvi, defending the Nizam's order and contributing to intimidation and violence.

A fourth force, the Communist-led Telangana peasant struggle, had agrarian objectives and its own chronology. It overlaps the crisis but is not identical to either the State Congress or Razakars.

### From patience to force

Negotiations continued for months. Patel increasingly insisted on accession and responsible government. Indian arguments combined:

- Hyderabad's enclosed geography;
- the danger of an independent enclave;
- Razakar violence and refugee flight;
- arms and security concerns;
- failure to democratise; and
- the breakdown of a negotiated path.

✅ **Fact:** Indian forces entered Hyderabad on **13 September 1948** in the action officially described as a "police action" and commonly known as **Operation Polo**.

✅ **Fact:** The operation ended with the Nizam's surrender on **17 September 1948**.

✅ **Fact:** Surrender and accession were not the same dated act. Bipan Chandra records the Nizam's **formal accession to the Indian Union in November 1948**. September marks military surrender and the end of effective resistance; November marks the later formal accession endpoint in that account.

✅ **Fact:** The Nizam was not immediately removed from all public status; he later served as Rajpramukh under the constitutional arrangement.

### Violence and controversy

Operation Polo ended the Nizam's sovereign claim and Razakar resistance, but integration cannot be narrated as a bloodless administrative correction. Military action and its aftermath included violence and civilian suffering, including communal violence. Exact casualty claims vary and should not be guessed.

⚠️ **Inference:** A complete evaluation must hold two propositions together:

- prolonged negotiation did not produce stable accession or responsible government;
- the coercive solution created its own moral and historical burden.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Operation Polo proves that the entire integration process was annexation by force.

**Reply:** Hyderabad proves that force was part of the Union's repertoire when negotiation failed; it does not erase the negotiated accession of most states. Conversely, the predominance of negotiation elsewhere cannot sanitise Hyderabad's coercion or aftermath.

```text
        |      autocratic rule vs responsible government
```

*One military event changed sovereignty, but the social conflict had different causes and a longer life.*

### Agrarian roots and political organisation

The Telangana movement developed against oppressive agrarian relations in parts of Hyderabad State. Political opposition had grown from the late 1930s through the Hyderabad State Congress and the **Andhra Mahasabha**. After the ban on the CPI was lifted in 1942, Communists expanded rapidly and gained control of the Andhra Mahasabha, converting a democratic-cultural platform into a major vehicle of peasant organisation.

Peasants faced jagirdars and deshmukhs who controlled very large estates. Two grievances gave mobilisation an immediate material focus:

- the government's hated **forced grain levy**; and
- **veth begar**, labour extracted without payment by landlords and officials.

From 1945 the movement spread through local sabhas. Communist organisers later created armed **dalams**, or squads, for village defence and agrarian action.

When Razakar attacks intensified, these squads also defended villages. This produced tactical overlap between anti-Nizam and agrarian resistance. The social programme, however, went beyond accession:

- restoration of land taken by landlords against old debt claims, especially during the Depression;
- occupation and distribution of government waste and forest land to the landless;
- attempts to raise agricultural wages;
- action on women's concerns, including wife-beating;
- first a 500-acre and later a 100-acre ceiling declared by the movement; and
- attack on forced levies, veth begar and landlord coercion.

The outcomes were not uniform. Peasants showed the strongest attachment to ancestral land that could be restored and to uncultivated land with no rival private claimant. They were less willing to claim a landlord's unrelated "surplus" land. After the movement declined, cultivators generally retained restored ancestral plots and occupied waste or forest land more successfully than ceiling-surplus land. This limit matters: revolutionary declarations did not automatically produce equally durable redistribution.

### Why conflation produces bad history

| Actor | Immediate objective | Social base or method |
|---|---|---|
| Hyderabad State Congress | Responsible government and integration | Satyagraha, later resistance |
| Communist-led Telangana movement | Agrarian transformation and anti-feudal struggle | Peasant mobilisation and armed dalams |
| Razakars | Defence of Nizam's order and militant political mobilisation | Paramilitary coercion |
| Indian Union | Accession, security and territorial authority | Negotiation followed by military action |

All could oppose or fight one another at different moments. A two-sided "India versus Nizam" frame erases peasants' autonomous demands.

### After Operation Polo

Many villagers initially saw the Indian Army as ending Razakar terror and princely rule. But the Communist leadership continued armed struggle against the new government. The Army then moved against guerrillas, causing further suffering. The movement was formally withdrawn in 1951 after a change in Communist strategy.

This creates an analytical sequence:

```text
anti-feudal struggle
      +
defence against Razakars
      ->
temporary convergence with anti-Nizam integration
      ->
sovereignty changes in September 1948
      ->
agrarian revolutionary objective remains
      ->
conflict with Indian state
```

### Reform as consequence and containment

The post-integration government responded quickly. The **Jagirdari Abolition Regulation, 1949** attacked the intermediary structure, while the **Hyderabad Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1950** created protected tenancy and a route to purchase. Bipan Chandra records that more than 600,000 tenants, cultivating over one-quarter of the cultivated area, received protected status; this figure is used here as the book's estimate rather than as an independently audited administrative total. Land ceilings followed in the mid-1950s.

Political mobilisation also affected implementation. Returning landlords could not fully restore old exactions; many accepted lower sale prices, faced pressure for higher wages, and recovered ceiling-surplus plots more readily than peasants' restored or newly cultivated waste land.

Reform can therefore be read in two ways:

1. **consequence:** organised peasants changed what the post-Nizam state had to legislate and enforce;
2. **containment:** statutory tenancy and land reform redirected an armed agrarian conflict into constitutional channels.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Telangana guerrillas fought Razakars, the peasant struggle was simply the popular wing of Indian accession.

**Reply:** Defence against Razakars created overlap, but agrarian redistribution and Communist revolutionary strategy were independent objectives. Continued armed conflict after Operation Polo proves that accession did not exhaust the movement's programme.

- **Trap:** Do not describe every anti-Nizam force as Congress-led.
- **Answer line:** "Territorial integration ended one sovereignty dispute; it did not dissolve the agrarian structures that had made princely rule socially explosive."

### The pre-invasion position

Jammu and Kashmir bordered both India and Pakistan. Maharaja Hari Singh delayed a choice and hoped to preserve independence. The population was majority Muslim, while the ruler was Hindu. The National Conference led by Sheikh Abdullah was a major popular force and opposed autocratic rule.

Kashmir therefore combined:

- ruler hesitation;
- demographic complexity;
- two-dominion geography;
- a mass political organisation; and
- strategic frontier significance.

### Invasion and accession

✅ **Fact:** Tribal forces entered Kashmir on **22 October 1947**, with support and participation connected to Pakistan.

✅ **Fact:** The Maharaja sought Indian military assistance as the attackers advanced.

✅ **Fact:** He signed the Instrument of Accession on **26 October 1947**.

✅ **Fact:** Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar on **27 October**.

The accession used the same legal form as other states, covering the standard subjects. Its context, however, made the act inseparable from military defence.

### Popular legitimacy and conditional language

Sheikh Abdullah and the National Conference supported resistance to the invasion and cooperation with India. India also stated that the people's will would be ascertained once order was restored.

This produced two simultaneous claims:

1. accession supplied the legal basis for Indian military action;
2. popular ratification remained part of India's public political position.

### War and the United Nations

The conflict expanded beyond raiders into an India-Pakistan war. India brought the matter to the United Nations. Security Council Resolution 47 of **21 April 1948** proposed a sequence involving withdrawal and force reduction before a plebiscite arrangement.

The sequence matters. A plebiscite was not an unconditional first step detached from demilitarisation and restored order. The conditions were not implemented. A ceasefire took effect from the beginning of 1949, leaving the former princely state divided by a ceasefire line.

### What this lesson does not claim

This session does not:

- resolve competing later sovereignty narratives;
- reteach the complete history of Article 370;
- treat a UN proposal as if its preconditions were fulfilled;
- equate Kashmir mechanically with Junagadh or Hyderabad; or
- carry the conflict beyond what is needed to understand integration by 1950.

### The second stage

Bipan Chandra describes fuller integration as beginning from December 1947 and proceeding rapidly through 1948-49. Small states could not all survive as separate federal units. The Union used several routes:

- merger into neighbouring provinces;
- combination into unions by covenant;
- direct central administration for selected territories; and
- retention of larger states in their existing territorial form.

### Major unions

Five prominent unions identified in the integration account were:

1. Saurashtra;
2. Madhya Bharat;
3. Rajasthan;
4. Patiala and East Punjab States Union, or **PEPSU**; and
5. Travancore-Cochin.

Mysore, Hyderabad and Jammu and Kashmir retained separate state forms in the 1950 framework.

The five unions should not be memorised as one undifferentiated merger:

| Union | Distinct route | State-building problem answered |
|---|---|---|
| Saurashtra | Covenant joined numerous Kathiawar states into one union | Dense fragmentation of small and medium jurisdictions |
| Madhya Bharat | Gwalior, Indore and neighbouring Central India states entered a covenanted union | Rival large courts had to share a common executive and legislature |
| Rajasthan | Built through successive stages as Rajput states entered expanding covenants | Integration occurred cumulatively rather than by one simultaneous signature |
| PEPSU | Eight Punjab states covenanted into a union | Territorial viability coexisted with unresolved tenancy and popular-legitimacy conflict |
| Travancore-Cochin | Two substantial southern states merged into one unit | A bilateral merger differed from both mass consolidation and phased Rajasthan |

These routes expose the design problem behind a covenant: who would be Rajpramukh, how rulers would be ranked, what ministers or legislatures would possess popular authority, and how liabilities, services and laws would be pooled. A ruler nominated under an interim bargain could help continuity, but nomination was not a substitute for elected legitimacy.

### PEPSU as a worked example

PEPSU combined eight Punjab princely states, including Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Faridkot, Kapurthala, Kalsia, Malerkotla and Nalagarh. The covenant was signed in May 1948 and the Union was inaugurated in July.

PEPSU shows why political consolidation and social democratisation could diverge. The Patiala Muzara movement originated in a late nineteenth-century transformation: **biswedars**, who had possessed revenue-collecting claims, secured proprietary status and reduced hereditary cultivators to occupancy tenants or tenants-at-will. Bipan Chandra describes the affected zone as roughly **800 villages comprising about one-sixth of Patiala State**; both figures are retained as source-qualified estimates.

Political opportunity developed in stages:

```text
late nineteenth-century dispossession
        |
Akali + Praja Mandal political awakening in the 1920s
        |
Communist leadership grows in the late 1930s
        |
1939 movement; open muzara-biswedar conflict from 1945
        |
independence without immediate responsible government
        |
Maharaja's repression -> appeals to the States Ministry
        |
PEPSU formed, July 1948 -> repression eases but conflict persists
        |
Lal Communist Party armed volunteers, 1948
        |
tenancy protection in 1952 -> proprietary-rights settlement in 1953
```

The Praja Mandal, backed by Congress and influenced by Brish Bhan's sympathy for the tenants, reinforced the movement. A Communist breakaway led by Teja Singh Swatantar formed the Lal Communist Party in 1948 and organised a small armed volunteer corps against landlord gangs. The force was defensive and limited rather than a mass army. The post-independence state also used repression; the conflict did not become democratic merely because Patiala had joined India.

The settlement emerged through legislation rather than union formation alone. The PEPSU Tenancy (Temporary Provision) Act, 1952 protected tenants from eviction. The PEPSU Occupancy Tenants (Vesting of Proprietary Rights) Act, 1953 then allowed occupancy tenants to become owners on compensation terms. This did not satisfy the Communist demand for confiscation without compensation, but it proved acceptable enough to end organised resistance.

### What administrative integration actually required

| Field | Integration task |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Reconcile land systems, taxes, treasuries and liabilities |
| Judiciary | Establish appellate hierarchy and common legal authority |
| Police | Replace palace-centred forces with public administration |
| Civil service | Absorb, retire or reorganise princely officials |
| Communications | Standardise rail, road, post and telegraph control |
| Representation | Move from nomination or personal rule toward elected government |
| Rights | Extend constitutional protections beyond former princely subjecthood |

### Praja Mandals as democratic infrastructure

Praja Mandals and the All India States' Peoples' Conference had prepared political cadres and popular demands before accession. They:

- disputed the ruler's claim to represent the state;
- demanded civil liberties and responsible ministries;
- connected local grievances to Indian nationalism; and
- pressured the Union not to preserve autocracy merely for territorial convenience.

Yet democratisation was uneven. Delhi sometimes prioritised order and rapid consolidation over immediate popular control. Former rulers could become Rajpramukhs. Local elites and officials often survived institutional transition.

### Guarantees: price or contradiction?

Privy purses and ceremonial privileges encouraged peaceful surrender of authority. Critics saw them as incompatible with republican equality. Defenders treated them as a small, predictable cost compared with prolonged conflict.

The better judgement asks:

```text
Did concessions speed peaceful transfer? Yes, often.
Did they preserve hierarchy symbolically and fiscally? Yes.
Did later abolition exist in the original bargain? No.
```

**Objection:** Administrative consolidation was merely technical and therefore secondary.

**Reply:** Without integrated revenue, courts, police, services and representation, accession would remain a diplomatic shell. Administrative choices determined whether former subjects experienced the Union as citizenship, continuity or a new remote authority.

### Princely representation in constitution-making

The Cabinet Mission framework reserved seats for princely states, but their entry required negotiation. The Constituent Assembly created a States Committee in December 1946. Negotiations with princely representatives addressed participation, powers and the voluntary character of entry.

Representatives from states began joining in April 1947, before the general accession drive was complete. Participation mattered for two reasons:

1. it weakened the claim that the Assembly represented only former British provinces;
2. it connected territorial integration to constitution-making rather than leaving states as external treaty partners.

Representation was not instantly democratic in every state; rulers retained influence over nomination. The process nevertheless opened the path toward a common constitutional legislature.

| Office | Holder | Function |
|---|---|---|
| President of the Constituent Assembly | **Dr Rajendra Prasad** | Presided over the Assembly's debates and institutional work |
| Chairman of the Drafting Committee | **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** | Led the committee responsible for preparing and revising the constitutional draft |

Rajendra Prasad did not chair the Drafting Committee, and Ambedkar did not preside over the Constituent Assembly. The distinction matters because constitution-making combined deliberation by the sovereign Assembly with specialised committee drafting.

### The 1950 territorial classification

The First Schedule of the Constitution organised the Union through categories:

| Category | Broad origin or character | Relevance here |
|---|---|---|
| Part A | Former Governors' Provinces | Directly administered British provincial inheritance |
| Part B | Former major princely states or unions | Hyderabad, Jammu and Kashmir, Madhya Bharat, Mysore, PEPSU, Rajasthan, Saurashtra, Travancore-Cochin |
| Part C | Former Chief Commissioners' provinces and selected centrally administered states | Direct central administration |
| Part D | Andaman and Nicobar Islands | Separate territorial category |

The categories did not create permanent equality of state form. They were transitional solutions to different inheritances. Detailed abolition of the A/B/C distinction in 1956 belongs to later state reorganisation.

### Rajpramukh and federal asymmetry

Part B states were often headed by a Rajpramukh rather than a governor in the Part A pattern. The role reflected the covenants and the negotiated transition from princely union to constitutional state.

⚠️ **Inference:** The Constitution did not erase history by pretending all units had arrived through the same route. It absorbed different routes through temporary asymmetry.

### Privy purses in the original Constitution

The settlement protected privy-purse payments and required regard for guarantees concerning rulers' personal rights, privileges and dignities. These provisions stabilised the original bargain. The Twenty-sixth Amendment abolished recognition and privy purses in 1971.

Keep the chronology exact:

```text
1947-49: guarantees help secure surrender and merger
1950: original Constitution protects the settlement
1971: later constitutional abolition
```

### Republic as a change in political identity

On 26 January 1950, India became a sovereign democratic republic under the Constitution. The historical significance for former princely states lay in:

- membership in one Union;
- constitutional legislatures and executives;
- common citizenship;
- fundamental rights;
- a national judiciary and constitutional remedies; and
- the replacement of the ruler's personal sovereignty by authority derived from the Constitution.

This did not mean instant social equality or perfect administration. It did change the normative source of rule.

### Achievements and unresolved costs

| Achievement | Qualification |
|---|---|
| Prevented territorial Balkanisation | Used asymmetric power and, in Hyderabad, force |
| Secured most accessions rapidly | Ruler consent was not the same as democratic consent |
| Created viable unions and administrations | Local institutions and inequalities persisted |
| Incorporated former states constitutionally | Part B asymmetry and Kashmir remained distinctive |
| Advanced common citizenship | Privy purses and ruler privileges survived initially |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Once the Constitution commenced, all historical distinctions disappeared.

**Reply:** The Constitution created common citizenship and Union authority but retained transitional categories, Rajpramukhs, guarantees and differentiated arrangements. Republican incorporation was decisive precisely because it constitutionalised, rather than denied, a complex transition.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the Instrument of Accession from the later processes of merger and constitutional incorporation.

**Model answer:**
The Instrument of Accession was the first and limited stage of integration. It authorised the Dominion to exercise power over scheduled subjects, principally defence, external affairs and communications. Its narrowness enabled rapid signatures without immediately abolishing internal princely government.

Merger went further. Through merger agreements or covenants, rulers transferred fuller authority. Small states entered neighbouring provinces, became centrally administered areas, or combined into unions such as PEPSU and Saurashtra. This required unification of revenue, courts, police and services.

Constitutional incorporation supplied a common political order. Princely representatives joined the Constituent Assembly; former major states and unions entered the 1950 First Schedule largely as Part B states, often under Rajpramukhs. Common citizenship and fundamental rights replaced personal princely sovereignty as the source of legitimacy.

Thus accession secured essential sovereign functions, merger created governable units, and the Constitution transformed territories and subjects into states and citizens of a Republic.

**Why this earns marks:** The final 10-marker defines and compares all three stages within the word ceiling, using unions and Part B as compact evidence.

**Scoring note:** **Component rubric (10):** accession definition 3 + merger/covenant distinction 3 + constitutional incorporation 3 + comparative conclusion 1 = 10.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** "The integration of princely states combined persuasion with pressure, concessions with coercion, and elite negotiation with popular politics." Discuss.

**Model answer:**
The integration strategy avoided both immediate annexation and acceptance of hundreds of independent sovereignties.

Persuasion began with a limited Instrument of Accession on defence, external affairs and communications. Patel reassured rulers, while V.P. Menon and the States Department converted policy into negotiable documents. Mountbatten's influence and guarantees of privy purses, dignities and succession lowered the cost of agreement.

Pressure was equally important. The 15 August deadline, dependence on Indian transport and markets, and the risk of less favourable later terms narrowed princely choice. As more rulers acceded, Travancore, Bhopal and Jodhpur became increasingly isolated. Praja Mandals and States' Peoples' movements challenged the claim that rulers alone embodied sovereignty.

Methods differed in the hard cases. Junagadh's accession to Pakistan was reversed through popular mobilisation, Indian takeover after the Dewan's invitation and a plebiscite. Hyderabad moved from a Standstill Agreement and prolonged negotiation to Operation Polo amid Razakar violence and democratic-agrarian conflict. Kashmir acceded during tribal invasion, linking the legal instrument to war and UN involvement.

Therefore integration was neither wholly voluntary nor merely coercive. Elite bargains were effective because popular politics, geography and state power altered rulers' calculations. Its achievement was rapid unity; its qualification lies in unequal bargaining and the human costs where force was used.

**Why this earns marks:** The model tests the paired thesis against normal negotiation, three exceptional cases and popular mobilisation before reaching a graded verdict.

**Scoring note:** **Component rubric (15):** persuasion and limited instrument 3 + pressure and concessions 3 + popular politics 2 + three hard cases 4 + balanced verdict 3 = 15.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate the integration of princely states as an achievement in territorial unity, democratic transformation and federal state-building.

**Model answer:**
Integration was a major territorial achievement because the lapse of paramountcy could have left more than 560 disconnected jurisdictions. The States Department under Patel and V.P. Menon secured rapid accession on defence, external affairs and communications, preventing strategic fragmentation.

Territorial unity, however, required differentiated action. Negotiation brought most states in; Junagadh used administrative takeover and plebiscite, Hyderabad ended in Operation Polo, and Kashmir acceded during invasion before war and UN involvement. These cases show legality, popular will, geography and security operating with different weight.

State-building formed the harder second stage. Merger agreements and covenants created viable units such as Rajasthan, Saurashtra and PEPSU. Revenue, courts, police, communications and civil services had to be integrated. Part B states and Rajpramukhs then constitutionalised transitional asymmetry.

Democratic transformation was real but incomplete. Praja Mandals demanded responsible government, while Telangana and Patiala exposed agrarian hierarchy. Common citizenship and fundamental rights in 1950 replaced princely subjecthood normatively. Yet rulers retained privy purses and privileges, local elites survived, and coercion produced suffering in Hyderabad.

The achievement should therefore be graded. India avoided Balkanisation and created a workable Union with remarkable speed. But territorial incorporation ran ahead of social equality, and consent operated within asymmetric power. Integration succeeded most fully when accession was followed by democratisation and constitutional citizenship.

**Why this earns marks:** The model evaluates territorial, administrative-federal and democratic achievement separately and qualifies each with evidence.

**Scoring note:** **Component rubric (20):** territorial unity 4 + differentiated hard cases 3 + union and administrative design 4 + democratic-social transformation 4 + constitutional incorporation 3 + graded verdict 2 = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. The constitutional opening

- More than 560 princely states existed; exact totals vary by classification.
- Provinces and princely states had different colonial relationships.
- Paramountcy meant superior Crown control over princes.
- It lapsed on 15 August 1947; it did not transfer automatically.
- Formal independence claims confronted geography, security, markets and communications.
- Nationalists and Praja Mandals argued that sovereignty belonged to peoples, not rulers alone.

## 2. States Department and accession strategy

- States Department created on 27 June 1947.
- Patel: political authority, urgency, calibrated firmness and settlement design.
- V.P. Menon: Secretary, negotiator, drafter and administrative organiser.
- Mountbatten influenced princely calculations; the Cabinet and officials also mattered.
- Initial accession covered defence, external affairs and communications.
- Limited scope made rapid signature easier.
- Deadline pressure and popular movements strengthened Union leverage.

## 3. Instruments

- **Instrument of Accession:** transfers listed authority to Dominion.
- **Standstill Agreement:** preserves existing relations during negotiation.
- **Merger Agreement:** transfers fuller internal governing power.
- **Covenant:** combines states into a union with common institutions.
- **Rajpramukh:** transitional head associated with Part B states or unions.
- Accession did not itself ensure democracy or administrative uniformity.

## 4. Negotiated accessions

- Most states joined without military action.
- Travancore initially claimed independence.
- Bhopal explored an independent princely position.
- Jodhpur considered Pakistan before acceding to India.
- Each additional accession weakened the feasibility of a collective princely bloc.
- Consent was meaningful but shaped by asymmetric power and interdependence.

## 5. Junagadh

- Coastal Saurashtra state; no land contiguity with Pakistan.
- Muslim Nawab, overwhelmingly Hindu population.
- Nawab acceded to Pakistan; Pakistan accepted.
- Popular movement and Arzi Hukumat opposed the decision.
- Dewan Shah Nawaz Bhutto invited Indian administration after collapse.
- India took over in November 1947.
- February 1948 plebiscite overwhelmingly favoured India.
- Analytical variables: ruler, people, contiguity and administrative viability.

## 6. Hyderabad

- Largest princely state; enclosed within India.
- Nizam sought independence.
- Standstill Agreement concluded in November 1947.
- India expected accession and representative government; Nizam used delay.
- Hyderabad State Congress demanded democracy.
- Ittehad-ul-Muslimeen and Razakars defended the Nizam's order.
- Operation Polo ran from 13 to 17 September 1948.
- Territorial integration was achieved by force after failed negotiation.
- Civilian suffering and communal violence qualify celebratory narratives.
- Nizam later served as Rajpramukh.

## 7. Telangana distinction

- Agrarian revolt against landlords, exactions and forced labour.
- Andhra Mahasabha became a major organisational channel under Communist influence.
- Forced grain levy and veth begar were immediate peasant grievances.
- Communist-led dalams organised peasants and fought Razakars.
- State Congress, Telangana guerrillas and Razakars were distinct.
- The movement restored debt-lost land, occupied waste and forest land, pressed wages and raised women's issues.
- Its ceiling programme moved from 500 to 100 acres, but ceiling-surplus occupation proved less durable than restored or newly cultivated land.
- Operation Polo changed sovereignty but did not fulfil agrarian revolution.
- Communist conflict continued until formal withdrawal in 1951.
- Jagirdari Abolition Regulation, 1949 and the Hyderabad Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1950 addressed movement-generated pressures.
- Bipan Chandra reports more than 600,000 protected tenants over more than one-quarter of cultivated land; treat this as the book's estimate.

## 8. Kashmir

- Muslim-majority population, Hindu ruler, two-dominion contiguity.
- Hari Singh initially delayed accession.
- National Conference under Sheikh Abdullah was the major popular force.
- Tribal invasion began on 22 October 1947.
- Maharaja sought aid; signed Instrument on 26 October.
- Indian troops airlifted on 27 October.
- Accession supplied legal basis for defence.
- India publicly linked popular approval to restored order.
- Conflict became India-Pakistan war and entered the UN.
- Resolution 47, 21 April 1948, placed withdrawal and force reduction before plebiscite arrangements.
- Conditions were not implemented; ceasefire from January 1949 left territorial division.

## 9. Merger, unions and administration

- Second stage accelerated from December 1947.
- Routes: provincial merger, covenant union, central administration, retention of large states.
- Major unions: Saurashtra, Madhya Bharat, Rajasthan, PEPSU and Travancore-Cochin.
- Saurashtra pooled numerous Kathiawar states; Madhya Bharat joined Gwalior, Indore and neighbours.
- Rajasthan developed through successive covenant stages; Travancore-Cochin was a bilateral merger.
- PEPSU combined eight Punjab states and was inaugurated in July 1948.
- Revenue, courts, police, civil services and communications required standardisation.
- Patiala Muzara origins lay in cultivators' late nineteenth-century reduction to tenancy by biswedars.
- Bipan Chandra estimates roughly 800 affected villages covering about one-sixth of Patiala.
- Akali, Praja Mandal and Communist currents successively shaped the movement; post-independence repression continued before the 1952-53 settlement.
- Praja Mandals drove responsible-government demands.
- A ruler nominated as Rajpramukh could provide continuity but not popular legitimacy.
- Privy purses and privileges eased transfer but contradicted egalitarian symbolism.

## 10. Republic and federal consequence

- States Committee negotiated princely participation in the Constituent Assembly.
- Representatives began joining from April 1947.
- Rajendra Prasad presided over the Constituent Assembly.
- B.R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee.
- The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949.
- It commenced on 26 January 1950.
- Part B contained major former princely states and unions.
- Part C covered centrally administered territories.
- Rajpramukhs embodied transitional asymmetry.
- Original constitutional guarantees protected privy-purse arrangements.
- Abolition came later in 1971.
- Common citizenship, rights and constitutional authority replaced princely subjecthood.
- Part A/B/C reclassification in 1956 belongs to later linguistic and state restructuring.
