# Foundation of the Indian National Congress and the Moderate Phase, 1885–1905

The Congress did not appear suddenly in December 1885. It emerged when regional associations, an educated intelligentsia, newspapers, new communications and a shared critique of colonial rule began to create an Indian political public. This session follows that development from its enabling conditions to the achievements and limits of Moderate nationalism, stopping at the threshold of the post-1905 phase.

The visual shows that nationalism required more than anger. People had to connect separate grievances and identify a common political cause.

### Begin with the intuition

A peasant facing revenue pressure, an artisan hurt by imported manufactures and an educated Indian excluded from higher office did not automatically see themselves as members of one political community. Nationalism grew when publicists and associations translated such experiences into a shared claim: foreign rule shaped the country’s economic and political condition.

✅ **Fact:** Bipan Chandra identifies modern education, the press, railways, telegraph and a unified postal system as important conditions that increased contact and political communication.

✅ **Fact:** The emerging intelligentsia used modern political ideas—rights, representation, liberty and accountable government—to interpret colonial policies.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Communications did not mechanically manufacture nationalism. They supplied infrastructure; common colonial subordination supplied a unifying political problem; organisers converted both into sustained action.

### The causal mechanism

1. **Comparable experience:** policies concerning revenue, expenditure, trade and public employment operated across provinces.
2. **Conceptual language:** educated Indians acquired a vocabulary with which to criticise discrimination and unrepresentative rule.
3. **Circulation:** newspapers and correspondence allowed an argument made in one presidency to travel.
4. **Association:** organised bodies turned opinion into petitions, meetings and campaigns.
5. **Nationalisation:** repeated cooperation made “India” a practical arena of politics, not merely a geographical expression.

### Example—and its limit

The railway enabled leaders to attend conferences and circulate newspapers more widely. Yet a railway carriage did not by itself create patriotic commitment. The political meaning arose because travellers and readers connected regional experience to an all-India diagnosis.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Early nationalism was only an elite imitation of British liberal thought.

**Reply:** Its language was partly liberal, but its content was increasingly rooted in Indian evidence—poverty, racial barriers, fiscal burdens and economic drain. Borrowed concepts became instruments for criticising the colonial order that claimed to embody them.

### UPSC use

Use a three-part explanation: **material integration + intellectual critique + political organisation**. Avoid single-cause answers such as “railways created nationalism” or “English education alone created the Congress.”

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** Explain how communications and political ideas helped transform local grievances into early Indian nationalism. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer:** Late nineteenth-century grievances were dispersed across occupations and provinces. Modern education enabled an emerging intelligentsia to interpret racial exclusion, poverty, trade policy and unrepresentative administration through ideas of rights and accountability. Newspapers converted isolated complaints into public arguments, while railways, telegraph and post improved contact among leaders and associations. Provincial campaigns could therefore be compared, publicised and repeated elsewhere. Foreign rule also provided a common object of criticism. These conditions did not automatically create nationalism: communications could carry loyalism as easily as protest. Political workers supplied the missing agency by building associations, drafting petitions and framing colonial problems as Indian problems. Thus, infrastructure widened the field of interaction, ideas supplied a critical vocabulary and organisation converted both into an emerging national movement.

**Scoring guidance:** Award most marks for a causal chain linking material networks, political interpretation and organised agency; reserve credit for the qualification against technological determinism.

The sequence shows a change in political capacity. Early bodies established the right to petition; later bodies widened issues, constituencies and coordination until a recurring national forum became both possible and necessary.

### Part A — The first associational layer: representation without broad mobilisation

✅ **Fact:** The **Landholders' Society (1837)** represented the class interests of landlords in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

✅ **Fact:** The **Bengal British India Society (1843)** used a wider language of general public interest. It merged with the Landholders' Society in **1851** to form the **British Indian Association**.

✅ **Fact:** The **Madras Native Association** and **Bombay Association** were established in **1852**. Like the early Bengal bodies, they were dominated by wealthy or prominent persons, worked mainly through petitions and remained provincial.

These organisations mattered because they made collective representation normal. Their limit was built into their social form: a body speaking mainly for landlords or urban notables could criticise administration, yet it could not easily create sustained popular agitation or an all-India political constituency.

### Part B — The widening layer: new audiences, issues and techniques

✅ **Fact:** Dadabhai Naoroji founded the **East India Association in London in 1866** and later opened branches in prominent Indian cities. It carried Indian questions to British public opinion.

✅ **Fact:** The **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha**, organised in the 1870s under leaders including M.G. Ranade, claimed to represent a wider public and developed serious economic criticism through its journal.

By the 1870s, younger political workers judged several older associations too narrow in programme, activity and social base. The causal transition was therefore not simply “more associations.” It was a search for organisations able to sustain campaigns on questions extending beyond the immediate interests of one class or city.

### Part C — Why the Indian Association marked a qualitative advance

✅ **Fact:** Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose founded the **Indian Association in July 1876**.

Its aims were to create strong public opinion on political questions and unite Indians around a common programme. Its organisational choices tried to widen participation:

| Device | What changed | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Lower membership fee | Made entry less dependent on wealth than in older elite bodies | A low fee did not by itself remove literacy, distance or class barriers |
| Branches in Bengal and towns outside Bengal | Carried organisation beyond one metropolitan committee | Coverage remained uneven and did not make it a fully representative all-India body |
| Banerjea's 1877–78 tours | Built inter-provincial opinion around civil-service reform | Touring created contact, not permanent machinery everywhere |
| Tenant demonstrations, 1883–85 | Mobilised thousands of peasants for changes to the Rent Bill in tenants' favour | Issue-based participation did not equal continuous peasant control of the association |
| Plantation-labour agitation | Challenged oppressive conditions under the Inland Emigration regime | Advocacy for labourers was not the same as a durable worker-led organisation |

The Association also campaigned against the Arms Act and Vernacular Press Act. These campaigns joined civil liberties, administrative access, agrarian protection and labour conditions within a wider political programme. That reach differentiated it from older bodies without turning it into a modern mass party.

### Part D — Conference experience becomes an institutional bridge

✅ **Fact:** The Indian Association sponsored an **All-India National Conference at Calcutta in December 1883**, attended by leaders from outside Bengal. It called for another conference in December 1885 and later merged with the Congress.

The conference proved that inter-provincial deliberation could be organised. Yet it did not become the representative body of political workers across India. Simultaneously, younger leaders formed the **Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884)** and the **Bombay Presidency Association (1885)** because older provincial organisations had become ineffective or conservative.

The mechanism was cumulative:

```text
elite petitions establish a public claim
          ↓
new associations widen issues and political audiences
          ↓
campaigns reveal both the value and limits of provincial action
          ↓
the 1883 conference demonstrates inter-provincial coordination
          ↓
a recurring all-India organisation becomes the next institutional requirement
```

### Example—and its limit

The Indian Association's tenant demonstrations show that pre-Congress politics could reach beyond educated professionals. But temporary mobilisation around a Rent Bill does not prove that peasants became equal, continuous decision-makers in early nationalism. Use the example to qualify the “purely elite” label, not to invent a mass-party structure.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If most associations remained elite and provincial, they cannot explain a national organisation.

**Reply:** They did not individually contain the Congress in miniature. Together they produced leaders, campaign repertoires, cross-regional contacts and evidence that isolated pressure was inadequate. Their limitations were themselves causal: repeated provincial campaigns generated the demand for a recurring national centre.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Because these associations petitioned authorities, they were merely loyalist clubs.

**Reply:** Their constitutional language often remained loyal, yet their campaigns normalised public criticism, collective claims and the idea that government should answer Indian opinion. Methodological caution did not erase their nation-building function.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** Assess the contribution and limitations of pre-Congress political associations. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer:** Pre-Congress associations moved Indian politics through distinct stages. The Landholders' Society and Bengal British India Society established collective petitioning, merging as the British Indian Association in 1851; the Madras Native Association and Bombay Association extended this elite provincial form. The Indian Association widened the repertoire through a low fee, branches, tours, tenant demonstrations and plantation-labour advocacy, and sponsored the 1883 All-India National Conference. New bodies in Madras and Bombay then strengthened inter-provincial capacity. Limits remained: leadership was urban and educated, wider mobilisation was issue-bound, and no body achieved durable all-India representation. Precisely because campaigns outgrew provincial machinery, a recurring national forum became necessary. These associations prepared Congress through personnel, methods and demonstrated institutional need, not by already constituting a mass national party.

**Scoring guidance:** Credit named organisations and functions separately; a high answer must distinguish preparation of nationalism from possession of a mass base.

### Visual: how an issue became a political lesson

```text
racial inequality in law
        ↓
Ilbert Bill controversy, 1883
        ↓
European mobilisation defeats or dilutes reform
        ↓
educated Indians observe organised pressure at work
        ↓
provincial protest seems insufficient
        ↓
need for durable all-India coordination becomes clearer
```

### Plain-language intuition

The Ilbert Bill controversy mattered not because it founded the Congress directly, but because it exposed the power of organised racial solidarity. A proposal associated with allowing qualified Indian judges to try European British subjects met intense European opposition. Educated Indians saw that abstract professions of equality could retreat before organised colonial privilege.

✅ **Fact:** The 1883 agitation in favour of the Bill became a major countrywide political campaign.

✅ **Fact:** Earlier campaigns had already addressed civil-service access, expenditure, the Vernacular Press Act and plantation labour.

### The press as an organiser

Newspapers did three jobs:

1. **Information:** reporting events beyond one locality.
2. **Interpretation:** explaining why an issue represented racial or colonial inequality.
3. **Coordination:** announcing meetings, resolutions and shared demands.

The press was not socially universal; literacy and circulation imposed limits. Yet its contents moved through public readings, meetings and oral discussion. Its influence therefore exceeded the number of individual subscribers.

### Comparison

| Isolated grievance | Politicised grievance |
|---|---|
| “This decision hurts my locality.” | “The same structure affects several provinces.” |
| Seeks a private exception | Demands a public principle |
| Ends when the case ends | Produces organisation and memory |
| Addresses an official | Addresses officials and a wider public |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The Ilbert Bill agitation proves only racial conflict among elites.

**Reply:** Its immediate arena was narrow, but its political lesson was larger: collective organisation could shape colonial policy, and Indian interests required coordination across regions. It was an accelerator, not a complete cause.

### UPSC use

Treat the controversy as **context**, not as the entire origin story. Link it to prior association-building and subsequent national coordination.

Its significance must be qualified: the agitation was socially limited and did not independently cause the Congress. Rather, it added urgency to earlier campaigns and reinforced the need for a durable all-India forum able to coordinate opinion and action.

✅ **Fact:** The first session of the Indian National Congress met in Bombay in December 1885.

✅ **Fact:** W.C. Bonnerjee presided, A.O. Hume played an important organisational role and the session was attended by **72 delegates**.

### Declared aims

Bipan Chandra’s account identifies four connected aims:

1. friendly relations among nationalist political workers from different regions;
2. development and consolidation of national unity beyond caste, religion and province;
3. formulation of popular demands and their presentation before government;
4. training and organisation of public opinion.

The last aim is easy to underestimate. Congress was not only a pressure group asking rulers for concessions. It also addressed Indians, teaching them to think, speak and organise in national terms.

### Institution rather than event

The first meeting did not create a mass party. Its innovation was a **repeatable national forum**. Annual sessions could aggregate grievances, adopt resolutions, rotate leadership and give regional leaders a common stage.

### Comparison: organiser, president and cause

| Element | Proper historical role |
|---|---|
| A.O. Hume | Important organiser and facilitator |
| W.C. Bonnerjee | President of the first session |
| Seventy-two delegates | Verified first-session attendance |
| Earlier associations and national forces | Broader historical foundations |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** A meeting of 72 educated people was too small to be nationally important.

**Reply:** Numerical smallness limits representativeness, but not institutional significance. The meeting created a durable arena in which national claims, leadership and public opinion could be organised.

```text

                ≠
```

### The claim

The safety-valve theory holds that British-linked founders encouraged a constitutional organisation to release dangerous popular discontent and prevent revolution. Some accounts connect this claim to Hume, Lord Dufferin and alleged secret reports.

### The evidence problem

✅ **Fact:** Hume, a retired civil servant, played a major organising role.

✅ **Fact:** Bipan Chandra’s detailed historiographical argument rejects the crude safety-valve story as having little basis in historical fact.

✅ **Fact:** The often-repeated story of “seven volumes” of secret reports entered later writing through accounts associated with Hume’s papers and William Wedderburn’s biography.

⚠️ **Qualification:** The existence of anxiety about unrest, or Hume’s desire to channel political action constitutionally, would illuminate one organiser’s motive. It would not prove that the colonial state created Indian nationalism or controlled Congress causation.

### A stronger causal explanation

```text
economic and racial grievances
 + modern education and press
 + pre-1885 associations and campaigns
 + Indian leaders seeking wider coordination
 + Hume's facilitation
 = foundation of Congress
```

This model neither erases Hume nor makes him the maker of Indian nationalism.

### Historiographical comparison

| Reading | Useful insight | Major weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Crude safety-valve thesis | Notices Hume’s official background and possible caution | Mistakes motive for total causation and relies on weakly established secret-report claims |
| Purely spontaneous nationalist story | Emphasises Indian agency and prior associations | Can understate organisation, negotiation and contingency |
| National-forces plus facilitation | Joins structural conditions, Indian initiative and Hume’s role | Requires careful distinction among cause, occasion and motive |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If Hume consciously wanted a safe constitutional outlet, the Congress was necessarily a colonial creation.

**Reply:** An institution can serve an organiser’s expected purpose yet escape that purpose because participants, grievances and subsequent action have independent sources. Facilitation helps explain timing and form; it does not exhaust historical causation.

### UPSC verdict

Write: **“Hume mattered as organiser; the safety-valve thesis is inadequate as a total explanation.”** Never invent a secret archive or claim that an alleged document conclusively proves official sponsorship.

1. Hume’s organisational role is established.
2. The crude safety-valve thesis treats Congress as a managed outlet.
3. Bipan Chandra rejects that thesis as a sufficient historical explanation.
4. The “seven volumes” story has a contested evidentiary history.
5. Possible motive is not equivalent to social causation.
6. Earlier associations prove political activity predated the Congress.

**Question:** Distinguish between organiser motive and historical causation in the foundation of the Indian National Congress. (15 marks, 250 words)

The early Congress was socially narrow but geographically ambitious. Its members could speak across provinces more easily than they could mobilise the whole society.

✅ **Fact:** In its early decades Congress functioned largely as an annual conference rather than a continuously mobilised mass party.

✅ **Fact:** Its leadership prominently included lawyers, professionals, journalists and public figures from major urban centres.

### What annuality accomplished

- regular national contact;
- rotation of venue and presidency;
- resolutions that standardised demands;

Sessions did not automatically create local cadres, sustained village organisation or equal representation. Activity between sessions depended heavily on provincial associations, newspapers and individual leaders.

### Visual: the Moderate theory of change

```text
collect facts and grievances
          ↓
frame constitutional demands
          ↓
petitions · resolutions · press · legislatures
          ↓
educate Indian opinion + influence British opinion
          ↓
incremental reform and political capacity
```

### Why they chose these methods

Moderates generally believed that British public opinion and liberal principles could be invoked against unjust colonial policy. They also knew that a new political nation required education, organisation and common language before riskier confrontation could be sustained.

Their repertoire included:

- petitions and memorials;
- resolutions at annual sessions;
- public meetings and speeches;
- newspaper campaigns;
- legislative criticism;
- deputations or delegations to Britain;
- work among sympathetic British politicians and publicists.

The familiar phrase “petition, prayer and protest” is a later shorthand. It can identify the style, but should not substitute for analysis.

### Two audiences

| Audience | Intended effect |
|---|---|
| Colonial authorities and British public | Reform policy and expose inconsistency |
| Indian public | Build common knowledge, confidence and political organisation |

This double audience explains why a petition that failed administratively could still succeed educationally.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Petitioning presumed that the colonial state could be morally persuaded despite benefiting from exploitation.

**Reply:** That faith imposed a real strategic limit. Yet open constitutional agitation also protected a young organisation, developed national claims and revealed the repeated gap between liberal promises and colonial practice.

### Balanced assessment

Moderate method was not simply cowardice or wisdom. It was a historically intelligible strategy whose organisational gains exceeded its direct transfer of power.

From 1885 to 1892 Congress pressed for expansion and reform of legislative councils, more representative membership and greater capacity to discuss administration. ### Civil services

Moderates demanded Indianisation of higher services and simultaneous civil-service examinations in India and Britain. The demand combined:

- **economic reasoning:** highly paid European officials remitted salaries and pensions;
- **political reasoning:** administration should include Indians;
- **moral reasoning:** racial exclusion contradicted equality.

### Civil liberties

Press freedom, speech and association were not ornamental rights. Without them, political education and lawful opposition could not operate. Moderate defence of these liberties sometimes extended even to leaders with whom they disagreed.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** These were employment demands of a small educated class.

**Reply:** Access to office was certainly important to that class. But council accountability, civil liberties and reduced racial monopoly also raised broader questions about who governed, who paid and whose opinion counted.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the drain mechanism

```text
Indian revenues and export surplus
          ↓
payments made abroad without an equivalent return to India
          ↓
salaries · pensions · home charges · interest · imperial costs
          ↓
loss of investible surplus
          ↓
weak capital formation + persistent poverty
          ↓
economic criticism becomes political criticism
```

### The conceptual leap

Earlier complaints could blame bad harvests, individual officials or temporary policy. Moderate economic nationalism argued that poverty was connected to the **structure of colonial rule**.

✅ **Fact:** Dadabhai Naoroji made poverty and the drain of wealth central themes of nationalist analysis.

✅ **Fact:** R.C. Dutt developed a systematic economic history of colonial rule; M.G. Ranade also contributed to economic criticism and industrial thinking.

### What “drain” means

The drain referred to resources transferred from India abroad without a corresponding economic return within India. It included official remittances and other external payments associated with alien administration and imperial connection.

Do not reduce the argument to “all exports are loss.” Trade becomes drain in the nationalist analysis when a portion of India’s surplus finances unrequited external obligations rather than domestic accumulation.

### A second mechanism: deindustrialisation

```text
British mechanised production lowers unit costs
                    +
colonial tariff and free-trade asymmetry opens the Indian market
                    +
conquest weakens courts and older centres of artisanal demand
                    +
railways carry imported manufactures farther inland
                    ↓
handicrafts lose export and domestic markets unevenly
                    ↓
artisans shift toward agriculture or lower-return work
                    ↓
industrial employment and indigenous accumulation remain weak
```

**Deindustrialisation** means the decline of established handicraft and artisanal production, especially textiles; it does not mean that every craft vanished or that India simply “failed to invent factories.”

✅ **Fact:** Nationalist economists linked handicraft decline to competition from British machine-made goods, discriminatory tariff conditions and the loss of older patronage.

✅ **Fact:** Colonial trade increasingly positioned India as a supplier of raw materials and a market for British manufactures. One-way free trade meant that Indian producers faced metropolitan industrial competition without an equivalent protective industrial policy.

✅ **Fact:** Railways intensified market integration. They could lower transport costs and connect regions, but under colonial trade conditions they also widened the inland reach of imported manufactures and facilitated outward movement of raw materials.

⚠️ **Qualification:** The decline varied across crafts and regions; some producers adapted or survived. English industrialisation supplied a cost advantage, while colonial policy and infrastructure shaped how forcefully that advantage operated in India.

### Drain and deindustrialisation are connected, not interchangeable

| Mechanism | Central question | Principal effect |
|---|---|---|
| Drain | Where did Indian revenues and surplus go? | Unrequited transfer weakened domestic saving and capital formation |
| Deindustrialisation | What happened to Indian production and employment under colonial trade conditions? | Handicraft decline, artisan displacement and increased pressure on agriculture |

Drain concerns the destination and return of resources. Deindustrialisation concerns the restructuring of production, markets and employment. They reinforced one another: weak accumulation restricted industrial renewal, while craft decline narrowed the productive base from which accumulation could occur.

### Why it was politically radical

The critique undermined the claim that British rule was economically benevolent. Railways, trade and administrative order could no longer be counted only as benefits; nationalists asked who controlled them, whose interests they served and whether they promoted independent Indian development.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Moderate economists used imperfect data and could not isolate every cause of poverty.

**Reply:** Numerical precision was limited, but the structural insight remained powerful: colonial decision-making directed Indian resources toward imperial priorities and constrained domestic development.

### Revision notes

1. Economic nationalism was a central Moderate achievement.
2. Naoroji made poverty and drain major political questions.
3. R.C. Dutt historicised colonial economic policy.
4. Ranade connected reform with economic development.
5. Drain means unrequited external transfer, not every export.
6. External salaries, pensions and charges featured in the critique.
7. Drain weakened domestic capital formation.
8. Poverty was presented as policy-linked, not natural.
9. Railways and trade were assessed by control and developmental effect.
10. Economic analysis eroded the moral legitimacy of empire.
11. Exact drain estimates should not be guessed.
12. Deindustrialisation concerns production and employment; drain concerns unrequited transfer.
13. Tariff asymmetry and one-way free trade exposed handicrafts to unequal competition.
14. Railways intensified market penetration but did not originate craft decline by themselves.
15. Craft decline was substantial but regionally and sectorally uneven.

**Question:** Distinguish drain from deindustrialisation and explain how the two mechanisms reinforced colonial underdevelopment. (15 marks, 250 words)

Its immediate consequence was weaker saving and capital formation; it did not mean that every export was a loss.

Deindustrialisation concerned production and employment. British mechanised goods entered an Indian market shaped by free-trade and tariff asymmetry, while conquest weakened courtly patronage. Railways later widened the inland market for imports and facilitated raw-material exports. Handicrafts, especially textiles, consequently lost markets and artisans were pushed toward agriculture or lower-return work.

The processes reinforced each other. External transfer reduced resources available for industrial renewal, while craft decline narrowed the productive base and intensified agrarian pressure. Yet they must not be collapsed: one tracks unrequited transfer, the other structural change in industry. Nor was craft decline uniform; regions and occupations adapted unevenly. Moderate economic nationalism turned both mechanisms into a political argument that an unaccountable colonial state organised Indian resources and markets around imperial rather than national development.

### From criticism to an alternative

Moderate economics was not only the drain theory. It proposed a different ordering of public priorities: development and welfare rather than imperial warfare; Indian employment rather than costly racial monopoly; industrial protection rather than automatic free-trade orthodoxy.

✅ **Fact:** Nationalists condemned high military expenditure, including costs linked to imperial wars and frontier policy.

✅ **Fact:** They criticised tariff arrangements that favoured British manufacturers and sought support for Indian industry.

✅ **Fact:** They connected land revenue, taxation and inadequate welfare expenditure to poverty.

### Why “free trade” was not neutral in a colonial economy

In theory, free trade exposes all producers to competition. In colonial India, the starting positions and policy power were unequal. British manufacturers had mechanised production and metropolitan political support; Indian handicrafts had already faced Company coercion, loss of patronage and weak access to capital. Colonial authorities controlled tariffs and transport priorities.

The result was a patterned trade relationship:

```text
India supplies raw materials ──> British industry
India buys manufactures <────── British factories
       │
       └─ Indian producers face import competition without sovereign tariff choice
```

Railways integrated markets but did not decide whose industry would benefit. In the absence of a national tariff and industrial policy, cheaper transport could strengthen the reach of Lancashire cloth as well as connect Indian producers. This is why Moderates demanded not isolation from trade, but conditions for **Indian industrialisation**: tariff protection where needed, encouragement of indigenous enterprise, technical education, irrigation and productive public expenditure.

### Example—and its limit

Cotton textiles show the mechanism clearly: mechanised British cloth, tariff policy and expanding transport networks placed pressure on Indian hand production. But textiles cannot stand for every craft or every region. A sound answer states the broad restructuring and then preserves uneven survival and adaptation.

### Swadeshi precursor—without crossing the 1905 boundary

Economic nationalism encouraged the use and development of Indian goods before the mass boycott politics associated with the anti-partition movement. Here, treat **indigenous industry and consumption as an economic precursor**. The organised Swadeshi movement after the Partition of Bengal belongs to the next topic.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Tariff protection and service reform benefited Indian capitalists and educated job-seekers more than peasants.

**Reply:** Benefits were uneven, but the programme also included land-revenue relief, salt-tax criticism and welfare expenditure. Its larger significance was the claim that a national economy required priorities different from those of an empire.

```text
LEVEL 1 — immediate concessions
Indian Councils Act 1892 · official inquiries and debate
                ↓

leadership · organisation · press · legislative argument
                ↓
LEVEL 3 — ideological transformation
poverty explained through colonialism · nation imagined politically
```

If only Level 1 is counted, Moderate achievement appears meagre. If only Level 3 is counted, their political limitations disappear. A balanced answer uses all three.

### Immediate gains

The Indian Councils Act 1892 was connected to sustained demands for council reform. It modestly enlarged councils and allowed limited budget discussion and questions. The Welby Commission provided an official forum in which Indian expenditure criticism could be presented. Neither transferred effective power.

### Political capacities

Moderates:

- created a continuing all-India organisation;
- trained a generation in public argument;
- made budget and administration politically intelligible;
- defended civil liberties and press space;
- established networks between provinces and Britain.

### Ideological achievement

Their deepest contribution was the critique of colonialism. By linking poverty, drain, trade, expenditure and racial exclusion to foreign rule, they weakened the idea that empire was a disinterested modernising agency.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Political education is an excuse used when a movement fails to obtain power.

**Reply:** It would be an excuse if no measurable capacity followed. Here a durable organisation, shared demands, trained leadership and an anti-colonial economic framework survived and were used by later phases. The limited concessions remain evidence of failure at the level of power.

### Non-teleological qualification

Later mass politics was not guaranteed. Moderate work created resources and arguments; later actors transformed them under new circumstances.

2. The 1892 Act was a concession but not responsible government.
3. The Welby Commission carried fiscal criticism into an official arena.

6. Civil-liberty defence preserved political space.

**Question:** Propose a three-level framework for evaluating the achievements of the Moderates. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Visual: concession without transfer of power

```text
nationalist demand
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓
```

### British responses were not uniform

The colonial state could ignore, ridicule, restrict or selectively concede. Some officials initially tolerated a limited Congress; hostility increased as criticism sharpened. Procedural reform could absorb pressure while protecting executive authority.

The 1892 settlement illustrates the pattern: discussion and a restricted representational principle expanded, but no elected majority, responsible executive or control over the purse followed.

### Strategic limits

1. **Excessive faith in British justice:** Moderates often expected liberal opinion to correct imperial policy.
2. **Narrow pressure:** petitions carried limited cost for rulers when not backed by wider mobilisation.
3. **Social distance:** educated leadership struggled to translate economic critique into popular organisation.
4. **Incremental goal:** reform within empire did not directly challenge sovereignty.

### “Political mendicancy”

This later criticism captures frustration with repeated appeals, but becomes misleading if used to deny economic nationalism, civil-liberty defence or institutional construction.

### Strongest defence and residual

**Defence:** A cautious method protected a new organisation, accumulated evidence and avoided premature destruction.

**Residual problem:** Caution could become dependency on the ruler’s moral conversion. When the state’s interests blocked reform, education alone could not compel transfer of power.

### Revision notes

1. Colonial response ranged from tolerance to hostility.
2. Selective concession could legitimise consultation without surrendering power.
3. The 1892 Act illustrates this ceiling.
4. Moderate pressure imposed limited direct cost on government.
5. Faith in British liberalism was genuine and strategically limiting.
6. Narrow social reach weakened leverage.
7. Reform within empire differed from a sovereignty claim.
8. “Political mendicancy” is a critical label, not a complete history.
9. Caution helped organisational survival.
10. Survival alone could not overcome colonial interests.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** How did selective colonial concessions both assist and contain early nationalist politics? (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** Selective concessions assisted early nationalism by opening limited arenas of discussion. Enlarged councils, questions on administration and official inquiries allowed nationalist leaders to publicise budgets, expenditure and racial discrimination. Such spaces trained representatives and made government claims contestable.

The same concessions contained politics. Nomination, restricted representation and executive supremacy ensured that consultation did not become control. The Indian Councils Act 1892 widened procedure but denied an elected majority, responsible government and command over the purse. Government could thus present reform while preserving colonial authority.

This dual effect tested the Moderate strategy. Access to institutions validated constitutional work and generated political education; repeated refusal of substantive power exposed the limits of faith in British liberalism. Because Congress still had a narrow social base, petitioning imposed little direct cost on rulers. Selective reform therefore neither simply deceived nor genuinely democratised. It created platforms that nationalists used, while designing those platforms to absorb criticism without surrendering sovereignty.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** “The early Congress was nationally ambitious but socially and regionally uneven.” Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** The Congress was nationally ambitious because it assembled leaders from different regions, rotated annual sessions, formulated common demands and explicitly sought unity beyond caste, religion and province. Peasants, workers, most women, many subordinated castes, interior districts and princely-state populations had limited direct access. The early Congress should therefore be understood as a significant but incomplete national arena: it made all-India politics recurrent while exposing the unresolved task of democratising nationalism.

The annual session created recurrence and visibility; it did not automatically create continuous organisation in every district or equal participation by every social group.

### What the annual-session form accomplished

Before Congress, inter-provincial cooperation was episodic. An annual meeting gave political workers a predictable national rendezvous. Changing venues, common resolutions, presidential addresses and press reporting helped leaders compare regional experience and present demands as Indian rather than merely provincial.

The form performed three institutional tasks:

1. **Aggregation:** provincial claims were debated and converted into common demands.
2. **Reproduction:** recurring meetings renewed leadership ties and organisational memory.
3. **Public projection:** the Congress could speak as an all-India forum even without an all-India electorate.

This was a real innovation. A national institution need not begin with universal representation. But its claim must still be tested against who could enter, speak, organise between sessions and shape priorities.

### Why incorporation remained uneven through 1905

| Dimension | Incorporating movement | Continuing limit |
|---|---|---|
| Region | Rotating sessions and provincial leaders connected presidency networks | Interior districts, several provinces and princely India had thinner organisational presence |
| Class | Indian Association campaigns and economic criticism addressed tenants, labourers and consumers | Congress leadership and deliberation remained dominated by educated urban professionals |
| Gender | National language could in principle include all Indians | Most women had little direct access to Congress leadership or sessions |
| Caste and community | Unity across caste, religion and province was a declared goal; minority safeguards recognised pluralism | Social hierarchy and unequal institutional access did not disappear through declaration |
| Organisational time | Annual recurrence prevented one-off coordination | A conference meeting yearly was not continuous local mobilisation |

The Indian Association's lower fee, branches, tenant demonstrations and plantation-labour agitation prove that wider outreach existed before 1885. They also show its limit: episodic advocacy or mobilisation did not transfer control of nationalist institutions to tenants or workers. Congress inherited both the aspiration to widen and the practical difficulty of doing so.

### The mechanism of uneven nationalisation

```text
annual national deliberation + provincial networks + press circulation
                              ↓
                    a shared national agenda

weak local machinery + unequal resources + social barriers
                              ↓
               uneven participation and limited leverage
```

The same institutional form therefore produced achievement and constraint. Annual sessions were feasible for a young national organisation and protected inter-regional continuity. Yet dependence on delegates, urban networks and periodic meetings made it difficult to convert national resolutions into sustained social incorporation.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling Congress national despite restricted participation merely confuses aspiration with representation.

**Reply:** The distinction should be preserved, not used to erase either side. Congress was national in arena, programme and inter-regional claim; it was not socially representative in a democratic sense. Its importance lies in institutionalising the first while leaving the second unresolved.

### Final balanced verdict

Through 1905, Congress made national deliberation recurring without making participation uniform. Its annual-session structure linked regions and standardised demands, but thin continuous machinery and unequal social access restricted incorporation. This is an institutional explanation of both Moderate achievement and limited political leverage, not a claim that the phase merely “prepared” a later movement.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** How did the annual-session form of Congress nationalise political deliberation while producing uneven regional and social incorporation through 1905? (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** The annual Congress session solved a specific institutional problem. Provincial associations had accumulated leaders and campaign experience, but cooperation remained intermittent. A predictable yearly meeting assembled delegates across regions, renewed personal networks, standardised resolutions and projected local grievances as Indian questions. Rotating venues, presidential addresses and press circulation gave the organisation continuity and public visibility without requiring a pre-existing national electorate.

Its incorporating capacity was nevertheless uneven. Presidency towns supplied disproportionate educational, legal, financial and newspaper resources. Provincial associations remained necessary between sessions, while interior districts and princely India possessed thinner links. Educated urban professionals dominated deliberation; peasants, workers, most women and many subordinated castes had little direct institutional voice.

The Indian Association demonstrates both movement and limit. Its lower fee, branches, tours, tenant demonstrations and plantation-labour agitation widened issues and audiences, but issue-based mobilisation did not create continuous worker or peasant control. Similarly, Congress could articulate national economic interests without representing affected groups equally.

The annual form therefore nationalised the arena, agenda and rhythm of politics, yet not its social depth. It was an effective bridge from provincial association to all-India deliberation, but weak continuous local machinery and unequal access limited mobilisation and leverage through 1905.

**Scoring guidance:** Reward explanation of annual recurrence as a mechanism, differentiated regional and social evidence, the Indian Association example with its limit, and a verdict separating national scale from representative depth.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question:** Why did the early Congress treat the organisation of public opinion as an objective in its own right? (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer:** In 1885 an all-India electorate or mass party did not exist. Public opinion therefore had to be politically constructed. Congress sessions linked regional leaders, resolutions translated varied grievances into common demands, and newspapers circulated those demands beyond the meeting hall. Petitions and budget criticism addressed government, but also taught Indians how colonial policy affected services, expenditure, trade and rights. Organisation of opinion created shared concepts, evidence and confidence for sustained action. Its reach remained unequal because literacy, urban concentration and a narrow social base limited participation. Yet without this educative work, occasional discontent would not readily become a continuous national movement. Public opinion was thus not merely a means of obtaining reform; it was part of making the political nation that could demand reform.

**Scoring guidance:** Look for the absence of a pre-existing mass public, at least three opinion-forming mechanisms, and one social-reach qualification.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question:** “Moderate economic nationalism made representation an economic necessity.” Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** Moderate economists argued that Indian poverty was connected to decisions made by an alien and unaccountable government. Naoroji’s drain analysis highlighted unrequited transfers through official remittances and external charges. R.C. Dutt connected land revenue and policy to historical impoverishment; nationalists criticised high military expenditure, inadequate welfare spending and tariff arrangements favourable to British manufacturers.

These claims made representation economically necessary. If councils could not control expenditure, imperial priorities would continue to dominate the budget. If higher services remained racially monopolised, administrative costs and remittances would persist while Indian needs received insufficient attention. If Indian opinion lacked institutional force, trade and industrial policy would serve metropolitan rather than national development.

The argument had limits. Moderate council demands remained narrow, and educated and capitalist interests shaped parts of the programme. Representation alone would not automatically redistribute resources to peasants or workers. Nevertheless, economic critique transformed constitutional reform from an abstract liberal preference into a question of material control: who taxes, who spends and whose development the state serves. Political accountability and economic nationalism therefore became mutually reinforcing.

**Scoring guidance:** Award for a chain from economic diagnosis to institutional demand, named evidence, and a qualification concerning narrow representation and distribution.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question:** The Moderate phase combined nation-making, colonial critique and strategic misjudgement. Discuss with reference to the period 1885–1905. (20 marks, 300 words)

**Model answer:** Moderate nationalism performed three historically connected functions. First, it advanced **nation-making**. The Congress converted networks created by pre-1885 associations into a recurring all-India forum. Annual sessions, resolutions, the press and deputations linked regions and trained public opinion in a common political vocabulary.

Second, it developed a **colonial critique**. Naoroji, Ranade, R.C. Dutt and others connected poverty to drain, high military expenditure, land-revenue pressure, racial exclusion and trade policy. Council and budget work exposed the gap between administrative procedure and accountability. Civil-liberty campaigns defended the public sphere required for politics.

Third, it contained a **strategic misjudgement**. Moderate leaders placed substantial faith in British liberal opinion and expected gradual constitutional advance. Petitions and persuasion imposed limited cost on a state whose imperial interests often contradicted the requested reforms. The Indian Councils Act 1892 enlarged discussion but retained executive control, illustrating both achievement and ceiling.

The social base deepened the contradiction. Educated professionals supplied analytical and organisational skills, but peasants, workers, most women, subordinated castes and many regions remained weakly represented. Yet “elite” does not mean politically empty: Congress created a national institution and delegitimised colonial economic claims.

By 1905 these resources remained valuable while the repertoire faced crisis. Later politics inherited the organisation and critique but transformed mobilisation and goals. Thus the phase joined durable nation-making with penetrating analysis and a limited theory of how colonial power could be compelled.

**Scoring guidance:** Use three balanced dimensions, named leaders and institutions, 1892 as evidence, social-base analysis and a bounded 1905 conclusion; do not reward a simple success/failure list.

| Early Congress spoke nationally, so it represented everyone | Separate national aspiration from social reach |

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Core thesis

The Indian National Congress emerged in 1885 from accumulated Indian political development: shared colonial grievances, modern education, communications, newspapers and pre-existing regional associations. A.O. Hume was an important organiser, but his motive cannot substitute for historical causation. Moderate nationalism created an all-India political platform and a structural economic critique, while its narrow base and excessive faith in British liberalism restricted its capacity to compel colonial power.

## Foundation facts

- First Congress session: **Bombay, December 1885**.
- President: **W.C. Bonnerjee**.
- Organisational role: **A.O. Hume**.
- Attendance: **72 delegates**.
- Aims: inter-regional contact, national unity, formulation of demands, training and organisation of public opinion.

## Pre-Congress spine

- Landholders' Society: 1837, a Bengal-Bihar-Orissa landlord-interest association.
- Bengal British India Society: 1843, framed a wider public-interest claim.
- British Indian Association: 1851 merger of the two Bengal bodies; elite petitioning.
- Madras Native Association and Bombay Association: 1852; provincial organisations of prominent persons.
- East India Association: 1866, Dadabhai Naoroji, London, with later branches in Indian cities.
- Poona Sarvajanik Sabha: organised in the 1870s; public representation and economic criticism.
- Indian Association: July 1876, Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose.
- Indian Association reach: low fee, branches, tours, tenant demonstrations and plantation-labour advocacy.
- All-India National Conference: Calcutta, December 1883; an inter-provincial bridge that did not become fully representative.
- Madras Mahajan Sabha: 1884.
- Bombay Presidency Association: 1885.
- Contribution: petitions, memorials, meetings, press work, campaigns, trained personnel.
- Causal transition: wider campaigns exposed the insufficiency of intermittent provincial coordination.
- Limitation: regional, urban, educated and often propertied bases; wider outreach remained issue-bound.

## Enabling conditions

- Common colonial policies created comparable grievances.
- Modern education supplied concepts of rights and representation.
- Press converted events into common political arguments.
- Railways, telegraph and post increased inter-regional contact.
- Economic criticism connected poverty with foreign rule.
- Ilbert Bill controversy revealed organised racial privilege and the need for coordination.

## Safety-valve debate

- Hume’s organisational importance is established.
- He may have preferred constitutional channeling of discontent.
- The crude thesis of an officially manufactured Congress is inadequate.
- The alleged “seven volumes” narrative has contested evidentiary status.
- Separate four categories: organiser motive, founding occasion, social causation and later outcome.
- Exam verdict: Congress was Indian in historical causation and mixed in its immediate organisation.

## Moderate leaders

- Dadabhai Naoroji: drain, poverty, East India Association, senior Congress leadership.
- M.G. Ranade: public association, economic development and reform.
- R.C. Dutt: economic history of colonial rule.
- Gopal Krishna Gokhale: legislative and constitutional work.
- Pherozeshah Mehta: Bombay public and constitutional politics.
- Surendranath Banerjea: Indian Association and public agitation.
- Badruddin Tyabji: Congress leadership and inter-communal national politics.
- W.C. Bonnerjee: first Congress president.

## Methods

- Petitions and memorials.
- Congress resolutions.
- Public meetings and speeches.
- Newspaper campaigns.
- Legislative questions and budget criticism.
- Deputations to Britain.
- Influence on British public and parliamentary opinion.
- Political education of the Indian public.

## Demands

- Expansion and reform of legislative councils.
- More elected representation and greater council powers.
- Indianisation of higher services.
- Simultaneous civil-service examination in India and Britain.
- Reduced military and imperial expenditure.
- Lower land-revenue pressure and salt-tax relief.
- Tariff support and encouragement of Indian industry.
- More expenditure on education, irrigation, sanitation and health.
- Freedom of speech, press and association.

## Economic critique

- Drain: unrequited external transfer, not all exports.
- Expatriate salaries, pensions and external official charges formed part of the argument.
- Drain weakened savings and domestic capital formation.
- High military expenditure served imperial priorities.
- Trade and tariff policy favoured British manufactures.
- Land-revenue pressure and welfare neglect contributed to poverty.
- Foreign capital was criticised when it displaced Indian enterprise and reinforced control.
- Poverty became a political indictment of colonial structure.
- Deindustrialisation: decline of handicraft production and employment, especially textiles; not disappearance of every craft.
- British mechanisation supplied a cost advantage; tariff asymmetry and one-way free trade shaped its Indian impact.
- Loss of courtly patronage weakened demand for specialised artisanal production.
- Railways widened inland import penetration and facilitated raw-material exports under colonial priorities.
- Craft decline pushed many artisans toward agriculture, though outcomes varied by craft and region.
- Drain tracks unrequited transfer; deindustrialisation tracks productive and employment restructuring.
- Constructive demands included industrial protection, indigenous enterprise, technical capacity and productive public expenditure.

## Achievements

- Created a durable all-India institution.
- Connected provincial leaders and political publics.
- Trained leadership and habits of public argument.
- Standardised demands and a vocabulary of rights.
- Made budgets and administration politically contestable.
- Defended civil liberties.
- Developed economic nationalism.
- Secured limited procedural reform in 1892.
- Used official forums such as the Welby Commission debate.
- Undermined the ideological legitimacy of empire.

## Limitations

- Narrow educated, urban and professional social base.
- Weak direct participation by peasants, workers, most women and subordinated groups.
- Uneven regional reach, especially beyond presidency networks.
- Annual-conference character and limited local machinery.
- Excessive faith in British justice and public opinion.
- Low-pressure methods imposed little cost on rulers.
- Reform within empire did not yet amount to a sovereignty demand.
- The 1892 Act expanded discussion without accountable power.

## British response

- Initial tolerance could coexist with official suspicion.
- Criticism was ignored, ridiculed, restricted or selectively accommodated.
- Procedural concessions helped absorb pressure.
- Executive control, nomination and fiscal authority remained colonial.
- Repression of press and political activity exposed the limits of liberal claims.

## Institutional form and incorporation to 1905

- Annual sessions converted intermittent provincial cooperation into recurring national deliberation.
- Rotating venues, resolutions, addresses and press circulation projected an all-India arena.
- Provincial associations remained necessary for political work between sessions.
- Presidency towns retained advantages in education, law, finance, print and association.
- Interior districts, princely India, peasants, workers, most women and many subordinated castes had weaker direct access.
- National scale of claim did not equal democratic depth of representation.
- Indian Association outreach demonstrates widening without proving continuous mass control.

## Fifteen-second recall chain

```text
associations → communications → common grievances → 1885 Congress
→ constitutional agitation → economic critique → limited 1892 concession
→ political education + social limits → strategic crisis by 1905
```
