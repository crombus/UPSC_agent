# British Conquest of Bengal: Complete Learner-Facing Live Session

> **Subject:** Modern Indian History | **UPSC use:** Prelims and GS-I
> **Core period:** 1717-1772 | **Level:** Foundation to advanced analysis
> **Central argument:** Bengal was not taken in one battle. Commercial privilege became political leverage at Plassey, military supremacy at Buxar, legal-fiscal authority through the Diwani, and unaccountable rule under the Dual Government.

## How to use this session

Read the lessons in order. Each solves a dependency needed by the next:

```text
Bengal's wealth and state capacity
        ↓
Company privilege collides with fiscal sovereignty
        ↓
Fortification and jurisdiction produce the 1756 crisis
        ↓
Elite coalition and Company force produce Plassey
        ↓
Client rule turns influence into extraction
        ↓
Mir Qasim tries to restore fiscal equality and armed autonomy
        ↓
Buxar breaks the wider Bengal-Awadh-Mughal coalition
        ↓
Allahabad and Diwani convert victory into legal revenue power
        ↓
Dual Government separates power from responsibility
        ↓
Bengal's revenue, credit and army create a Company-state
```

The session deliberately does **not** narrate the full Carnatic, Mysore, Maratha or Sikh war sequences. It explains only the Bengal mechanism that later made wider expansion financially possible.

## Lesson 1 — Bengal before conquest: rich province, functioning state

Progress: 1 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Political-fiscal-commercial setting

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Canonical Basic and Advanced files checked; Bipan Chandra Chapter IV and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay's eighteenth-century transition chapter checked.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Boundary check:** Earlier European-company history is background; this lesson begins with Bengal as an eighteenth-century regional polity.

### Visual first: why Bengal attracted a company

```text
Fertile delta and agrarian surplus
            +
Textile, silk, saltpetre and opium production
            +
River routes, ports and dense markets
            +
Revenue administration and merchant credit
            ↓
High-value trade + collectible revenue + financeable armies
            ↓
Bengal is valuable not because it is "weak",
but because it has resources worth controlling.
```

**Caption:** The same institutions that made Bengal prosperous also made control of Bengal unusually rewarding.

### Intuition before terminology

Imagine a prosperous marketplace protected by a government that taxes trade, polices routes and settles disputes. A foreign company first wants permission to buy and sell there. It then seeks exemption from taxes, protection for its agents, fortified premises and influence over officials. The prize is not merely merchandise. It is the authority that decides **who pays, who is protected and who commands force**.

Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan, Shuja-ud-Din and Alivardi Khan was autonomous in practice while retaining Mughal forms of legitimacy. This was not an empty political shell. Its rulers collected revenue, managed powerful zamindars, supervised trade and tried to keep European companies subordinate. Therefore, the later Company victory cannot be explained by saying that Bengal had simply collapsed.

### Mechanism: prosperity becomes strategic vulnerability

1. Bengal's agriculture and manufacturing generated marketable surplus.
2. Merchants and bankers moved advances, bullion and credit through regional networks.
3. The Nawab required customs and land revenue to sustain administration and defence.
4. The Company needed Bengal goods for Asian and European trade.
5. A privilege that reduced the Company's costs reduced the Nawab's revenue at the same time.
6. Once the Company acquired coercive leverage, Bengal's organised revenue system could be redirected to finance Company purchases and troops.

This is the first major analytical correction: **conquest did not replace economic emptiness with order; it captured and redirected an existing productive system.**

### Example, with its limit

Bengal goods formed a major part of English imports from Asia. That fact helps explain Company interest. It does not prove that conquest was planned from the beginning, nor that all Bengal regions were equally prosperous. The delta, Bihar and Orissa had different agrarian and commercial arrangements.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** If Bengal possessed wealth, administrative experience and a large army, prosperity should have protected it.

**Reply:** Resources protect a state only when fiscal authority, military command and elite cooperation remain connected. Bengal's crisis separated them: some commanders, bankers and court factions chose regime change, while the Company combined a fortified base, naval access, credit and disciplined troops.

**Qualified assessment:** Prosperity did not cause defeat by itself. It raised the stakes and made elite defection and fiscal capture exceptionally consequential.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish Bengal's commercial products from later plantation geographies.
- **Mains:** begin a conquest answer with state capacity and political economy, not with the battlefield.
- **High-value phrase:** "The Company captured a functioning fiscal-commercial system and then altered its incentives."

### Revision notes

1. Bengal was a prosperous Mughal successor province, not a political vacuum.
2. Autonomy in practice coexisted with Mughal forms of legitimacy.
3. Agrarian surplus, manufactures, riverine trade and credit made Bengal valuable.
4. Customs and land revenue were instruments of sovereignty.
5. European companies originally operated within rules set by Indian authorities.
6. Merchant-bankers were political actors because credit could sustain armies and regimes.
7. Prosperity increased the reward from controlling Bengal's revenue machinery.
8. Regional variation prevents treating Bengal, Bihar and Orissa as identical.
9. The core story is redirection of existing resources, not creation of wealth by conquest.
10. State strength can fail when command, finance and legitimacy become disconnected.

### Lesson-local MCQs

B. A fortified coastal base let Company merchants bypass some regional controls.
C. Weakened Mughal supervision left regional legitimacy with limited fiscal force.
D. Nawabi customs income mattered more than the province's agrarian and manufacturing surplus.

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** Bengal's collectible surplus, manufactures and commercial networks could support both Company investment and military expenditure.
- **B — Incorrect:** Coastal access aided trade and reinforcement, but European settlements remained subject to disputes over Nawabi jurisdiction.
- **C — Incorrect:** Mughal political forms retained symbolic value even as Bengal became autonomous in practice.
- **D — Incorrect:** Customs were important, but Bengal's transformative value rested on the wider revenue and production base that could sustain commerce and force.

**Q2. What is the most defensible description of early eighteenth-century Bengal?**

A. A province administered directly from Delhi without meaningful regional autonomy.
B. A regional state exercising practical autonomy while retaining Mughal institutional legitimacy.
C. A regional coalition whose fiscal capacity depended heavily on autonomous zamindars.
D. A port-centred polity whose inland authority depended primarily on European companies.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The decline of effective imperial supervision allowed Bengal's governors to consolidate regional authority.
- **B — Correct:** Regional autonomy and continued use of Mughal forms existed together rather than excluding each other.
- **C — Incorrect:** Bengal had functioning fiscal and administrative institutions despite factional and local constraints.
- **D — Incorrect:** European trade was significant, but Nawabi authority and the agrarian revenue system extended well beyond the port settlements.

**Q3. Which statement best relates Bengal's prosperity to its capacity for political resistance?**

A. Revenue abundance could compensate for fragmented command during an external challenge.
B. Commercial expansion shifted decisive military authority from the Nawab to merchant houses.
C. Prosperity supplied resources, but defence required cohesive command and cooperation.
D. Export growth reduced the political importance of customs enforcement and jurisdiction.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Revenue supported resistance only when command and political cooperation converted it into usable capacity.
- **B — Incorrect:** Merchant houses influenced credit and coalition-making but did not replace the Nawab's formal military authority.
- **C — Correct:** Bengal's wealth created substantial means, while divided command and elite realignment determined whether those means could be defended.
- **D — Incorrect:** Expanding trade increased rather than reduced the fiscal and political stakes attached to customs jurisdiction.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Explain why Bengal's prosperity was both a source of regional strength and an attraction for Company intervention.

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

Bengal's fertile agriculture, textile and silk production, riverine communications and active merchant-credit networks made it one of the most resource-rich successor states. These assets sustained Nawabi administration, customs collection and military expenditure, showing that Bengal was not an empty political shell.

The same concentration of taxable production and tradable goods attracted the English East India Company. Control over customs privileges lowered commercial costs; influence over the Nawab yielded payments and political protection; control over land revenue could finance export purchases and troops without equivalent bullion imports. Thus, existing regional capacity became a potential fiscal base for a corporate state.

However, prosperity did not mechanically cause conquest. Bengal fell when resource control became separated from political cohesion: court factions, commanders and financiers realigned while the Company used fortified bases, naval supply and disciplined forces. Bengal's wealth magnified both its strength and the rewards of capturing it.

**Scoring guidance:** Reward answers that connect production, revenue and credit to both Nawabi capacity and Company incentives. Cap responses that merely list commodities or equate wealth with inevitable defeat.

## Lesson 2 — The 1717 farman and the dastak problem

Progress: 2 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Privilege, private trade and fiscal sovereignty

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** The distinction between Company trade and servants' private trade is verified in canonical and book evidence.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Dependency:** Lesson 1 established why customs revenue mattered to a functioning regional state.

### Visual first: one privilege, two interpretations

| Issue | Company interpretation | Nawabi interpretation | Conflict produced |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1717 farman | Broad commercial immunity for Company goods | Defined privilege subject to regional enforcement | Scope of exemption |
| Dastak | Pass protecting privileged consignments | Instrument liable to misuse | Revenue leakage |
| Private trade | Servants sought Company cover | Private goods remained taxable | Unequal competition |
| Customs | Cost to be minimised | Revenue and sovereign right | Fiscal confrontation |

**Caption:** The dispute was not simply whether trade should occur, but who could define and police the limits of privilege.

### Plain-language intuition

A tax exemption is manageable if the exempt trader and exempt goods are clearly identified. It becomes destructive when officials use the exemption for their own commerce or sell access to others. The Company's servants used passes meant for Company goods to shield private trade. Indian merchants who paid customs then faced competitors using political privilege rather than commercial efficiency.

### Technical mechanism

The 1717 farman of Farrukhsiyar confirmed important Company privileges in Bengal. A *dastak* was a pass used for the movement of privileged goods. Company servants, however, engaged in private trade and extended or sold the protective use of these passes beyond the intended corporate trade.

The effects formed a chain:

```text
Private goods move under privileged passes
        ↓
Customs officials cannot collect normal duty
        ↓
Nawabi treasury loses revenue
        ↓
Duty-paying Indian merchants face unequal costs
        ↓
Company servants gain private fortunes
        ↓
Enforcement becomes a test of sovereign authority
```

Murshid Quli Khan and his successors objected to expansive Company claims. The problem was therefore older than Siraj-ud-Daula. His accession intensified an accumulated institutional conflict.

### Example, with its limit

Mir Qasim later abolished inland duties for all traders. This shows that the central issue was competitive inequality. Yet abolition also reduced an important revenue source and was adopted in a moment of severe confrontation; it was not a costless free-trade reform.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Dastak misuse was corruption by individual servants, not a reason to describe the conflict as political.

**Reply:** Private abuse became political when Company institutions protected the abusers, challenged Nawabi customs officers and demanded immunity from regional law. The question was who possessed final jurisdiction.

**Qualified assessment:** Personal enrichment sharpened the dispute, but the deeper issue was institutional: corporate privilege was expanding beyond the Nawab's capacity to regulate it.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims trap:** the farman did not grant unrestricted exemption to every private transaction of every Company servant.
- **Mains link:** use "fiscal sovereignty" to connect customs loss, merchant disadvantage and political authority.
- **Comparison:** commercial immunity before 1757 prepared the ground; Diwani after 1765 transferred revenue authority itself.

### Revision notes

1. Farrukhsiyar's farman dates to 1717.
2. Company goods and servants' private goods were not identical legal categories.
3. Dastaks protected privileged consignments from normal customs collection.
4. Misuse shifted revenue from the Nawab toward private Company fortunes.
5. Indian traders faced unequal competition when they continued to pay duties.
6. Bengal Nawabs had challenged expansive Company claims before Siraj's accession.
7. Customs enforcement was part of sovereign government, not a minor trade formality.
8. Private interest and corporate protection reinforced each other.
9. The conflict joined commercial, fiscal and jurisdictional questions.
10. Mir Qasim's later equalisation policy directly addressed this inherited problem.

### Lesson-local MCQs

**Q4. Why did misuse of dastaks become a sovereignty issue rather than remain a commercial irregularity?**

A. It expanded Company brokerage among Asian merchants without resolving Nawabi customs claims.
B. It encouraged imperial mediation between Calcutta's claims and Murshidabad's revenue officers.
C. It prompted proposals for a negotiated customs rate covering the European companies.
D. It obstructed Nawabi taxation and enforcement while Company institutions shielded private trade.

**MCQ 4: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Wider commercial influence was consequential, but the sovereignty issue arose when the Company obstructed the Nawab's power to tax and enforce.
- **B — Incorrect:** Imperial language framed the privilege, yet no effective mediation restored Nawabi control over Company private trade.
- **C — Incorrect:** A negotiated rate could have contained a trade dispute; the actual escalation followed protected evasion and resistance to enforcement.
- **D — Correct:** Fiscal loss became a constitutional conflict because the Company resisted the Nawab's authority to regulate privileged commerce.

**Q5. Which legal distinction identifies the specific abuse behind Nawabi objections to the 1717 farman?**

A. Corporate trade covered by privilege differed from servants' private trade that remained liable to duty.
B. Company exports were exempt, whereas its Asian country trade was administered by the Nawab.
C. Imperial port concessions differed from transit dues collected through Bengal's inland authorities.
D. Company military stores received passes, whereas commercial merchandise required Nawabi licences.

**MCQ 5: A**

- **A — Correct:** Extending corporate exemption to employees' private business was the central abuse contested by Bengal's rulers.
- **B — Incorrect:** The disputed line ran between corporate goods and servants' private commerce, not between export and Asian country-trade jurisdictions.
- **C — Incorrect:** Port and transit levies formed part of the fiscal setting, but this distinction does not identify the misuse of corporate passes for private goods.
- **D — Incorrect:** The farman was not a military-stores concession; its abuse concerned ordinary private commerce moving under Company protection.

**Q6. What was the most direct economic injury to duty-paying Indian merchants from dastak misuse?**

A. Their access to Company shipping became conditional on accepting lower sale prices.
B. Their goods paid tax while privileged private rivals could avoid it.
C. Their purchases increasingly required advances from Company-linked commercial agents.
D. Their borrowing costs rose as bankers redirected funds toward Nawabi revenue contracts.

**MCQ 6: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Shipping and sale terms could matter, but dastak misuse injured merchants most directly through unequal customs liability on comparable goods.
- **B — Correct:** Unequal customs liability raised Indian merchants' relative costs and distorted competition.
- **C — Incorrect:** Advance systems shaped procurement, but they were not the immediate customs-cost difference created by privileged passes.
- **D — Incorrect:** Credit allocation affected commercial capacity, yet the specific injury tested here was the duty differential at points of trade.

**Q7. Which sequence best captures the institutional escalation created by Company privilege?**

A. The Nawab narrowed exemptions, Company servants accepted assessed duties, and the dispute remained commercial.
B. Company servants widened private exemptions, after which the Nawab regularised them through a lower tariff.
C. Exemption expanded into private commerce, enforcement weakened, and a revenue dispute became a jurisdictional contest.
D. Imperial confirmation limited the privilege, and a joint customs arrangement restored enforceable boundaries.

**MCQ 7: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Acceptance of assessed duties would have contained the problem; Company protection of private privilege prevented that settlement.
- **B — Incorrect:** The Nawab did not regularise the expanded exemption, because doing so would preserve the fiscal inequality at issue.
- **C — Correct:** The sequence joins commercial abuse to fiscal loss and ultimately to competing claims of authority.
- **D — Incorrect:** Imperial authority did not produce a jointly enforced settlement capable of restraining Company servants.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, answer in 250 words):** Examine how the misuse of commercial privileges converted an Anglo-Bengal trade dispute into a conflict over sovereignty.

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

The 1717 farman granted the English East India Company important customs privileges for its corporate trade. The conflict arose because Company servants extended the protection of *dastaks* to private transactions and sometimes made privileged access available to Indian associates. This produced three linked injuries.

First, the Nawabi treasury lost customs revenue required for administration and defence. Second, duty-paying Indian merchants faced unequal competition from private traders operating behind Company immunity. Third, local customs officers could not enforce Bengal's rules without confronting an armed corporation that claimed its own interpretation of imperial authority.

The issue therefore moved beyond corruption. Murshid Quli Khan and later Nawabs had already resisted expansive claims. Under Siraj-ud-Daula, unauthorised fortification, asylum to a court fugitive and refusal to accept regulation added territorial and jurisdictional challenges to the fiscal dispute. A ruler unable to tax private trade, regulate forts or enforce orders within his territory would possess sovereignty only nominally.

However, commercial privilege alone did not make war inevitable. Court faction, Anglo-French tension and Company confidence after southern warfare shaped the timing. The best conclusion is that trade supplied the material stakes, while contested enforcement transformed those stakes into a struggle over political authority.

**Scoring guidance:** Full credit requires the three-part chain of revenue loss, unequal competition and failed enforcement. Reward qualification on timing; do not reward answers that treat the farman as unlimited private immunity.

## Lesson 3 — Fortification, asylum and the Calcutta crisis of 1756

Progress: 3 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: From fiscal dispute to open confrontation

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** The 1756 sovereignty triggers are cross-checked in both permitted books.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Caution:** The contested "Black Hole" narrative is not used as the causal centre.

### Visual first: the sovereignty test

```text
Who may fortify? ───────┐
Who shelters fugitives? ├──► Who rules inside Bengal?
Who regulates trade? ───┤
Who may wage local war? ┘
```

**Caption:** Siraj's confrontation with the Company involved several powers normally claimed by a territorial ruler.

### Plain-language intuition

A warehouse becomes politically different when it is fortified, supplied by ships and used to resist the ruler's orders. By 1756, the Company's Calcutta establishment was no longer merely a place where merchants stored cloth. Fortification in anticipation of European conflict, protection given to people wanted by the Nawab, and resistance to customs regulation signalled an enclave claiming exceptional authority.

### Step-by-step causation

1. Siraj-ud-Daula succeeded Alivardi Khan in 1756 amid court hostility and succession tensions.
2. The Company was strengthening Calcutta's defences without Nawabi permission.
3. It offered protection to Krishna Ballabh, connected to an official accused by the Nawab's government.
4. Siraj treated fortification and asylum as challenges to his jurisdiction.
5. After warnings failed, he seized the Company's factory at Kasimbazar and captured Calcutta on 20 June 1756.
6. English officials regrouped at Fulta under naval protection and awaited forces from Madras.
7. The crisis shifted from enforcement to armed recovery and political intrigue.

The French response also matters: they were more willing to comply with the order regarding fortification. This made the English refusal look less like unavoidable self-defence and more like a choice to challenge authority.

### The "Black Hole" problem

Accounts of deaths among English prisoners in Calcutta became a powerful imperial story. Details, numbers and later uses of the episode remain contested. Even if one discusses the imprisonment, it should not replace the verified structural causes: privilege misuse, fortification, asylum and jurisdiction. A dramatic episode is not automatically the best causal explanation.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Siraj's haste and political insecurity show that the crisis was primarily a personal failure.

**Reply:** His temperament and preparation affected execution, but the demands he made concerned recognisable powers of government. Any ruler would confront an armed corporation that fortified without permission and resisted taxation.

**Qualified assessment:** Siraj defended legitimate sovereignty through uneven strategy. The Company exploited both his errors and the structural conflict its own privileges had created.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** 1756 capture of Calcutta precedes Plassey in 1757.
- **Mains:** separate causes from later justificatory narratives.
- **Analytical frame:** "The immediate crisis concerned Calcutta; the underlying contest concerned jurisdiction."

### Revision notes

1. Siraj-ud-Daula became Nawab in 1756.
2. Company fortification of Calcutta lacked Nawabi permission.
3. Protection of a court fugitive challenged the Nawab's jurisdiction.
4. Siraj seized Kasimbazar before moving against Calcutta.
5. Fort William fell on 20 June 1756.
6. Company personnel regrouped at Fulta with naval protection.
7. Reinforcements came from Madras under Watson and Clive.
8. Company recovery combined force with political intrigue.
9. Siraj's personal weaknesses do not invalidate the sovereignty basis of his objections.
10. "Black Hole" details are contested and should not organise the causal narrative.

### Lesson-local MCQs

**Q8. Which issue most clearly turned Calcutta's fortification into a constitutional challenge?**

A. It widened the customs perimeter within which Company officers claimed protection for goods.
B. It altered the balance between English and French warehouses along the Hooghly.
C. It strengthened defence against a possible French attack while leaving permission unresolved.
D. It asserted an armed enclave's capacity to disregard the territorial ruler's permission.

**MCQ 8: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Commercial jurisdiction around Calcutta mattered, but the fort itself challenged who possessed the authority to authorise armed works.
- **B — Incorrect:** European rivalry formed context, but the decisive issue was the Nawab's authority within Bengal.
- **C — Incorrect:** Fear of French attack explains the Company's strategic motive, not its right to fortify against the Nawab's order.
- **D — Correct:** Building defensive works against the Nawab's direction implied an independent claim to security and jurisdiction.

**Q9. Which treatment of the "Black Hole" episode meets the strongest historical-evidence standard?**

A. Separate disputed prison details from the independently verified fiscal and jurisdictional causes.
B. Use later Company narratives as sufficient evidence for both the scale and political meaning of the event.
C. Treat the imprisonment as one immediate episode and derive the conquest chiefly from its reported casualties.
D. Prefer the Company's retrospective account because it was produced by participants in the conflict.

**MCQ 9: A**

- **A — Correct:** This distinguishes what remains disputed from the separately evidenced conflicts over privilege, fortification, asylum and jurisdiction.
- **B — Incorrect:** Later Company testimony is evidence to evaluate, not a self-validating measure of casualty scale or causal importance.
- **C — Incorrect:** Even if treated as an immediate episode, casualty claims cannot displace the prior institutional causes of confrontation.
- **D — Incorrect:** Participation gives an account proximity, but also interests and retrospective framing that require corroboration.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, answer in 150 words):** Was Siraj-ud-Daula's conflict with the Company in 1756 merely the reaction of an insecure ruler?

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

Siraj's insecurity and hurried military decisions influenced the form of the 1756 crisis, but they do not adequately explain its substance. The Company was extending private trade under disputed customs privileges, fortifying Calcutta without permission and protecting a person wanted by the Nawab's government. Each issue concerned a normal attribute of territorial sovereignty: taxation, defence and jurisdiction.

Siraj first moved against Kasimbazar and then captured Calcutta on 20 June 1756. His failure to destroy the Company's naval refuge and his inability to stabilise court support aided its recovery. Yet the Company's refusal to accept regulation, unlike the more compliant French response to fortification orders, made confrontation likely.

The contested "Black Hole" story should not become the causal centre. Thus, personal weakness affected Siraj's strategy, while the conflict itself arose from a structural contest between Nawabi authority and an increasingly armed, privileged corporation.

**Scoring guidance:** Award most marks for distinguishing the legitimacy of Siraj's claims from weaknesses in implementation. Deduct for making the contested prison narrative the sole cause.

## Lesson 4 — The coalition behind Plassey

Progress: 4 / 14 | Stage: Political entry | Subtopic: Court faction, bankers and Company strategy

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Named actors and the collaboration debate are checked against canonical and permitted book evidence.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Dependency:** The Company had recovered Calcutta; the next question is how force joined an internal coalition.

### Visual first: a coalition of unlike interests

```text
Company leadership ── wants privilege, security and leverage
        │
Mir Jafar and commanders ── seek altered court power
        │
Jagat Seth banking house ── seeks a predictable political settlement
        │
Merchants/intermediaries ── protect commercial positions
        │
Disaffected court figures ── resist Siraj's reordering of influence
        └──────────────► Regime-change coalition
```

**Caption:** Cooperation did not mean identical motives; it meant temporary agreement on replacing Siraj.

### Intuition before terminology

Conspiracies succeed when several groups who distrust one another still prefer the same immediate outcome. The Company could supply armed force and external protection. Mir Jafar could provide a claimant and military non-cooperation. Bankers could provide credit and legitimacy within elite networks. Court figures could connect the plan to the Nawab's administration.

### Full mechanism

After recovering Calcutta, the Company did not merely prepare for a conventional battle. It joined a plan to replace Siraj with Mir Jafar, the *mir bakhshi* or senior military office-holder. Rai Durlabh, the Jagat Seth banking house, Amichand and other figures appear in the politics surrounding the conspiracy, though their roles and motives should not be flattened into one story.

Merchant-bankers mattered because eighteenth-century warfare depended on cash, remittance and confidence. The Jagat Seths were not modern national bankers choosing between "India" and "Britain." They were political-financial actors evaluating court stability, obligations and risk. Their support made regime change more feasible; it did not give them complete control over its consequences.

### Competing interpretations

| Interpretation | What it explains | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| "Betrayal caused Plassey" | Inactivity and elite defection | Company's independent force, naval supply and fortification |
| "Superior arms caused victory" | Discipline and coercive capacity | Why much of the Nawab's army did not fight |
| "Merchant collaboration caused conquest" | Credit and political brokerage | Divergent motives and later loss of Indian control |
| Integrated view | Coalition + coercion + fiscal stakes | Must still allow contingency and individual choice |

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Emphasising Indian collaborators excuses colonial aggression.

**Reply:** Explanation is not exoneration. Company officials consciously used intrigue and promises to secure exceptional privileges and political control. Indian agency explains the route by which aggression succeeded; it does not erase unequal outcomes.

**Qualified assessment:** Plassey required both an internal fracture and an external corporate power able to convert that fracture into durable leverage.

### UPSC integration

- Use actors by function: **Mir Jafar—claimant/commander; Rai Durlabh—court-military defection; Jagat Seths—finance; Company—coercion and guarantees.**
- Avoid nationalist anachronism: eighteenth-century actors operated through dynastic, corporate, regional and commercial interests.
- A high-scoring answer distinguishes motive, means and consequence.

### Revision notes

1. Plassey was prepared through a regime-change conspiracy.
2. Mir Jafar offered an alternative Nawab and commanded a major contingent.
3. Rai Durlabh's non-cooperation weakened Siraj's battlefield position.
4. Jagat Seths represented the political importance of credit and banking.
5. Amichand was involved in negotiations but should not dominate the explanation.
6. Coalition members had overlapping, not identical, interests.
7. "Betrayal" identifies conduct but does not fully explain structural success.
8. Company naval and military capacity made its promises credible.
9. Indian agency and colonial responsibility can be analysed together.
10. The coalition sought regime change; the Company captured the greatest long-term gain.

Progress: 5 / 14 | Stage: Political entry | Subtopic: 23 June 1757 and its meaning

- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.

### Visual first: three layers of Plassey

```text
BEFORE THE FIELD       ON THE FIELD              AFTER THE FIELD

Promises to Mir Jafar  Major contingents inactive Mir Jafar installed
Credit and intrigue    Company force credible    Payments and privileges
        └─────────────── Political coup with military enforcement ───────────────┘
```

**Caption:** Plassey was militarily limited but politically immense because it enforced a prepared regime change.

### Plain-language intuition

Calling Plassey "a battle only in name" does not mean weapons were irrelevant. Some troops fought and died, including forces associated with Mir Madan and Mohan Lal. It means the outcome was shaped less by a full contest between two committed armies than by prior arrangements ensuring that large sections of the Nawab's force would not act decisively.

### What happened and what it established

On 23 June 1757, Company forces under Robert Clive met Siraj-ud-Daula's army at Plassey near Murshidabad. Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh did not commit their major contingents to Siraj's cause. Following defeat, Siraj fled, was captured and killed; Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab.

Plassey's importance lay in four gains:

1. **Regime leverage:** the Company demonstrated that it could make and unmake a Nawab.
2. **Financial transfers:** compensation, gifts and payments enriched the Company and its servants.
3. **Commercial privilege:** Company and private interests gained greater protection.
4. **Strategic base:** access to Bengal's resources strengthened the Company's position against competitors.

Plassey did **not** yet provide the final legal and fiscal settlement of 1765. It opened the door; Buxar and the Diwani later made the position harder to reverse.

### Plassey and military causation

The Company had a disciplined force, artillery practice, naval supply and a defensible base. Those capacities mattered because conspirators could trust that the Company might survive if the plan was exposed. Yet the low-intensity battle cannot be used as evidence that a small European force simply destroyed the full military power of Bengal through technology.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** If Plassey was decided by defection, it was not a genuine conquest.

**Reply:** A conquest can begin through political capture rather than territorial occupation. Installing a dependent ruler and extracting resources altered who exercised effective power even without immediate annexation.

**Qualified assessment:** Plassey was the decisive point of political entry, but not the final military or legal completion of Bengal's subordination.

### UPSC integration

> **Answer spine:** Plassey gave a client → Buxar gave military supremacy → Allahabad gave legal-fiscal title.

- Do not write that Plassey alone made the Company sovereign over Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
- Do not reduce it to either "treachery" or "superior weapons."
- Evaluate turning points by criteria: regime influence, battlefield supremacy, revenue rights and administrative control.

## Lesson 6 — Mir Jafar and the post-Plassey extraction regime

Progress: 6 / 14 | Stage: Political entry | Subtopic: Client rule, payments and private fortunes

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Post-Plassey transfers and trade-finance change are checked; unsupported totals are avoided.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Dependency:** Plassey created leverage; this lesson shows how leverage was monetised.

### Visual first: the client-regime trap

```text
Company installs dependent Nawab
            ↓
Nawab promises compensation, gifts and concessions
            ↓
Treasury and offices face repeated demands
            ↓
Nawab needs revenue and autonomy to govern
            ↓
Company distrusts any attempt at independence
            ↓
Client becomes "incapable" and replaceable
```

**Caption:** The arrangement weakened the ruler through the very obligations used to justify his installation.

### Plain-language intuition

A puppet ruler is useful only while he can deliver resources. If extraction drains his treasury and damages his authority, he becomes less able to satisfy the patron. The patron then treats the resulting weakness as a reason to replace him. This circular logic defined Mir Jafar's position.

### Forms of extraction

After Plassey, Company officials and military personnel received compensation and gifts, while the Company obtained major commercial and territorial advantages. Private fortunes and corporate gains overlapped but were not identical:

| Channel | Beneficiary | Political effect |
|---|---|---|
| Compensation and stipulated payments | Company and affected interests | Burden on Nawabi treasury |
| Gifts and private rewards | Senior servants and officers | Personal incentive for intervention |
| Expanded private trade | Company employees and associates | Customs loss and coercive commerce |
| Territorial/revenue concessions | Company institution | More durable fiscal base |
| Bengal-financed investment | Company trade | Reduced dependence on imported bullion |

The post-Plassey shift in trade finance was structurally important. Instead of relying to the same degree on bullion imported from Britain to buy Indian goods, the Company increasingly used resources obtained in Bengal. The province's surplus could thus finance the export of its own products.

### Mir Jafar's limits

Mir Jafar had not ceased to be a political actor. He tried to navigate Company pressure and other connections, but his dependence and depleted resources narrowed his room for manoeuvre. When he failed to meet expanding expectations, the Company replaced him in 1760 with his son-in-law Mir Qasim, receiving further concessions.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Gifts and indemnities were normal eighteenth-century political practices, so "plunder" is an anachronistic label.

**Reply:** Gifts were not unusual, but their scale, repetition and connection to an armed corporation's power to replace rulers altered their meaning. Private enrichment also distorted official decision-making.

**Qualified assessment:** The phrase "Plassey plunder" captures coercive transfer, but analysis should distinguish one-time payments, private fortunes, commercial privilege and the later systematic use of revenue.

### UPSC integration

- Separate **Company** interests from **Company servants' private** interests.
- Link personal fortunes to the "men on the spot" debate: local incentives could push expansion.
- Avoid unverified numerical totals; explain channels and consequences.

### Revision notes

3. Corporate gain and servants' private enrichment overlapped.
4. Private trade continued to damage customs revenue and Indian competitors.
5. Bengal resources began financing Company purchases.
6. Reduced bullion dependence changed the structure of Company trade.
7. Extraction weakened Mir Jafar's capacity to govern and pay.
8. Dependency produced a cycle of demand, weakness and replacement.
9. Mir Qasim replaced Mir Jafar in 1760.
10. "Plunder" should be disaggregated into distinct fiscal and commercial channels.

### Visual first: the reform triangle

```text
                  /              \

               \                  /
             cleaner administration
```

**Caption:** Mir Qasim understood that autonomy required finance, administration and force to reinforce one another.

### Plain-language intuition

Mir Qasim was not simply "anti-British." He initially owed his position to the Company and granted valuable concessions. Conflict emerged when he tried to make Nawabi government capable of acting independently. A ruler cannot preserve autonomy if officials are corrupt, the treasury is empty and the army depends on the power that installed him.

### Reform programme

Mir Qasim shifted his capital from Murshidabad to Munger, away from the immediate centre of Company influence. He sought to:

- improve revenue administration and reduce leakage;
- discipline officials and restore order;
- build a modernised army trained along European lines;
- create a more secure political-military base;
- limit the misuse of Company privilege.

These steps should be read as a fiscal-military programme. Revenue funded troops; troops protected revenue collection; administrative discipline made both more reliable.

### Limits and resistance

Reform was not socially neutral. Stronger collection and new office-holders could alienate established interests. A modernised army still required experienced command, supplies and sustained finance. Mir Qasim also confronted an opponent with coastal access, reinforcements, a fortified base and influence among Bengal's elites.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Since Mir Qasim gained power through Company support, his later claim to independence was opportunistic.

**Reply:** Political origin does not eliminate subsequent sovereignty. He had paid heavily for accession and expected to govern; the Company expected continuing subordination. Their incompatible expectations produced conflict.

**Qualified assessment:** Mir Qasim mounted the most serious Nawabi recovery attempt, but reform could not quickly repair fractured elite relations or neutralise the Company's external resources.

## Lesson 8 — The inland-duty conflict: equality that threatened privilege

Progress: 8 / 14 | Stage: Resistance | Subtopic: Fiscal equalisation and war

### Visual first: two possible solutions

```text
Problem: Company private traders avoid duties; Indian traders pay

Solution 1: Make Company private trade pay normal duty
        └─ Company servants resist enforcement

Solution 2: Abolish inland duties for all traders
        └─ Formal equality removes the privileged advantage

Result: The dispute reveals that the Company sought privilege,
not merely low taxation as a universal principle.
```

**Caption:** Mir Qasim answered unequal exemption by equalising conditions, but equality threatened interests built on exceptional status.

### Plain-language intuition

If one runner is allowed to start halfway down the track, the organiser can restore fairness either by moving that runner back or by moving everyone forward. Mir Qasim first struggled to regulate Company servants and then abolished inland duties so Indian merchants would no longer be disadvantaged. The Company opposed the loss of its relative advantage.

### Technical mechanism

Company servants used dastaks and political pressure to protect private trade. Local officers who tried to collect duties faced humiliation or coercion. Mir Qasim's abolition of internal customs duties attacked the problem at its root:

- Indian and Company-linked traders would face the same formal rate;
- the Nawab sacrificed revenue to remove discriminatory advantage;
- the Company's private merchants lost a privilege valuable precisely because others paid;
- dispute over taxation became open war in 1763.

The policy can be described as **fiscal equalisation**, but not as modern laissez-faire ideology. It was a defensive state measure in a sovereignty struggle.

### Example, with its limit

Equal treatment could improve competition for Indian merchants. Yet abolishing duties deprived the Nawab of income at a moment when he was financing military reform. It was therefore an economically costly strategic response, not an uncomplicated liberal reform.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Mir Qasim abolished duties only to provoke the Company and therefore bears equal responsibility for war.

**Reply:** The policy followed repeated failure to enforce ordinary customs against protected private traders. It removed discrimination rather than imposing a special penalty on the Company.

**Qualified assessment:** The measure was rational as an assertion of equality but risky as fiscal policy. Its rejection exposed how deeply private privilege had become tied to Company power.

### UPSC integration

- **Core line:** "When regulation failed, Mir Qasim equalised by abolition."
- Do not say he abolished only Company duties; he abolished inland duties generally.

6. It also sacrificed Nawabi revenue.

The inland-duty conflict arose because Company servants extended corporate customs privileges to private trade. Nawabi officers lost revenue, while Indian merchants paid duties that privileged competitors avoided. Attempts to enforce normal taxation met Company resistance.

Mir Qasim responded by abolishing inland duties for all traders. The policy was logically equalising: if privileged private trade could not be taxed, Indian traders would no longer be placed at a formal disadvantage. Yet this very equality threatened Company servants, whose profits depended on a relative exemption rather than on a universal low-tax principle.

The measure also had a fiscal cost. Mir Qasim sacrificed customs income while trying to finance administrative and military reform. It therefore represented a desperate sovereignty strategy rather than a developed laissez-faire programme. Company opposition showed that commercial privilege had become backed by political and coercive power; a tax settlement could not succeed without agreement over jurisdiction.

Thus, abolition failed to produce peace because the dispute was not merely about the rate of duty. It concerned who could define privilege, discipline private traders and command obedience within Bengal. War was the institutional consequence of incompatible sovereignties.

After winning, the Company could have tried to annex everything. ## Lesson 11 — Diwani and Dual Government: power without responsibility

Progress: 11 / 14 | Stage: Fiscal state formation | Subtopic: 1765-1772 institutional design

### Visual first: the broken accountability circuit

```text

        │

```

**Caption:** Those with the resources could deny responsibility; those bearing responsibility lacked effective resources.

### Plain-language intuition

If one institution controls the budget and another is blamed for services, each has an incentive to pass responsibility. The Company could maximise revenue while presenting administration as the Nawab's duty. The Nawab's establishment could neither command sufficient resources nor control the power that shaped collection.

### How the system worked

From 1765 to 1772, the Company held the Diwani while the Nawab nominally retained Nizamat responsibilities. Collection and administration continued through Indian officials and deputies, which gave the system an appearance of continuity. Yet the Company controlled the decisive fiscal and military levers.

The formula "power without responsibility and responsibility without power" is useful because it identifies an incentive failure:

1. Revenue maximisation benefited the Company.
2. Administrative costs and public blame could be displaced onto the Nawab.
3. Intermediaries faced pressure to remit assessments upward.
4. No accountable sovereign integrated collection, policing, justice and relief.
5. Company servants' private interests further complicated supervision.

### Was it truly "dual"?

Formally, functions were divided. Practically, Company influence penetrated Nizamat through its right to shape appointments, military dominance and control of finance. Therefore, "Dual Government" should not imply equal partners. It was indirect Company supremacy behind a retained Nawabi facade.

### End of the arrangement

The arrangement became associated with corruption, fiscal disorder and administrative failure. Warren Hastings ended Dual Government in 1772 and brought revenue administration more directly under Company management. The constitutional regulation that followed belongs to the next stage of the wider course.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Because Indian officials carried out collection, exploitation cannot be attributed to Company rule.

**Reply:** Agency in implementation does not settle who set remittance pressures, controlled appointments, commanded force and benefited from revenue. The system deliberately used Indian intermediaries under Company supremacy.

**Qualified assessment:** Indian officials possessed room for action and could exploit local power, but the institutional incentive originated in the Company's separation of fiscal command from public accountability.

```text

                    ↓

                    ↓

```

**Caption:** Political economy affects disaster by shaping reserves, obligations and response capacity, not by creating rainfall failure.

### Plain-language intuition

A drought or crop failure becomes a famine when people cannot obtain food and institutions fail to protect access. A household with savings, flexible demands and relief can survive a bad harvest better than one already facing heavy claims and coercive markets. Therefore, explaining the Bengal famine requires both environmental shocks and the fiscal-administrative conditions that shaped vulnerability.

### Social channels

**Cultivators:** Revenue pressure could reduce reserves and increase dependence on intermediaries or credit. Effects varied by locality, crop and bargaining power; peasants were not one uniform class.

**Artisans:** Company servants and agents could use advances, purchasing power and political coercion to obtain goods on disadvantageous terms. Yet production did not vanish immediately, and artisans could sometimes negotiate, evade or shift markets.

**Merchants:** Duty-paying traders suffered from privilege before Mir Qasim's equalisation attempt. Some merchants collaborated with the Company and profited, while others lost competitive space. "Indian merchant" is not a single political position.

**Intermediaries:** Zamindars, revenue contractors, officials and agents transmitted demands but also pursued their own interests. Their participation does not remove the Company's structural control.

### The famine of 1770

The famine occurred during Dual Government. Crop failure and disease were important. The defensible colonial link is that an extractive, poorly accountable order aggravated vulnerability and failed to coordinate effective relief. Avoid two errors:

- **monocausality:** "taxation caused the drought";
- **exoneration:** "because rainfall failed, institutions were irrelevant."

No mortality figure is necessary for the analytical point, and contested totals should not be guessed.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** Since environmental failure initiated scarcity, colonial institutions should not be treated as causal.

**Reply:** Causes operate at different stages. Weather can reduce supply; revenue pressure, market access and relief capacity determine exposure, entitlement and mortality.

**Qualified assessment:** The Company did not manufacture the climatic shock, but its unaccountable fiscal system worsened social resilience and response.

Thus, conquest reshaped resilience and bargaining power. Its social significance lay not only in what was collected, but in how authority, accountability and market coercion were reorganised.

## Lesson 13 — From merchant corporation to military-fiscal state

Progress: 13 / 14 | Stage: Consequences | Subtopic: Revenue, credit, army and further power

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** The bullion-to-revenue transition and military-fiscal interpretation are checked.
- **CA search:** Not run; no genuine recent linkage is required for this historical subtopic.
- **CA found:** None used.
- **Boundary:** Later conquest campaigns are not narrated; only their Bengal fiscal precondition is explained.

### Visual first: the self-reinforcing Company-state

```text
Bengal land revenue
       ├────────► Company "investment" in export goods
       │                    └──► commercial returns
       │
       └────────► salaries, supplies and sepoy army
                            └──► greater coercive reach
                                      └──► more revenue security
```

**Caption:** Revenue reduced dependence on external bullion and linked commerce to a standing territorial army.

### Plain-language intuition

A merchant buys goods with capital brought to market. A state taxes a territory and uses recurring revenue to maintain force. After the Diwani, the Company increasingly did both. This hybrid character explains why it could pursue profit while exercising sovereignty.

### The fiscal transformation

Before political control, the Company commonly imported bullion to finance Indian purchases. Post-Plassey transfers began altering this pattern; Diwani made the change systematic. Revenue could be used to buy Bengal goods for export—the Company's "investment"—and to maintain troops.

The cycle mattered:

1. territorial revenue paid for disciplined forces;
2. force protected revenue collection and political influence;
3. political influence secured commercial privilege and procurement;
4. commercial returns and revenue expanded creditworthiness;
5. stronger credit and force increased capacity for future war.

This is a **military-fiscal state** logic housed inside a joint-stock corporation. It does not mean the Company ceased to be commercial. Its unusual power came from joining commercial accounting, private incentives, public charters, Indian revenue and organised violence.

### The role of Indian soldiers and financiers

Company armies were largely composed of Indian soldiers. Their effectiveness depended on regular payment, drill, command, supplies and institutional continuity—not racial ability. Indian bankers, merchants, revenue officials and suppliers also sustained the system. Colonial power was foreign-directed but locally assembled.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** If Indians provided soldiers, credit and administration, the resulting state cannot be described as British conquest.

**Reply:** Composition does not determine command or distribution of gains. The Company's governing institutions decided strategy, protected corporate interests and directed revenue toward its military-commercial objectives.

**Qualified assessment:** The Company-state was neither simply imported nor purely indigenous. It was a British-directed corporate sovereignty constructed through Indian resources, labour and collaboration.

- Reject racial explanations of military success.
- Distinguish finance of later wars from narration of those wars.
- Use Bengal's fiscal mechanism as one component of the wider question on Company-army success.

### Visual first: an argument wheel

| Lens | Central claim | Evidence it highlights | Risk if used alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial | Privilege and private trade drove conflict | Farman, dastaks, procurement | Understates military and court politics |
| Conspiracy | Elite defection produced Plassey | Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, bankers | Cannot explain durable post-1757 control |

**Caption:** Each lens reveals a mechanism; none alone explains the whole sequence.

### Plain-language intuition

Historiography is not a contest to choose one slogan. It asks what each explanation can account for. "Betrayal" explains why Plassey involved limited fighting but not why Company rule endured. "Military superiority" helps with Buxar but not the 1717 dispute. "Economic greed" explains stakes but not the exact sequence. A robust answer combines mechanisms and identifies where each becomes decisive.

### The three-stage synthesis

```text
PLASSEY (1757)
Political entry through coalition and coercion
        ↓
BUXAR (1764)
Military supremacy over a wider regional-imperial coalition
        ↓
DIWANI (1765)
Legal-fiscal authority and recurring territorial revenue
        ↓
DUAL GOVERNMENT (1765-72)
Indirect rule exposes the accountability deficit
```

### Was conquest planned?

One interpretation stresses local initiative by Company servants—the "men on the spot" who pursued private fortunes and responded to regional opportunities. Another notes that the Company had long practised armed trade, depended on British charters and naval power, and operated within an expanding imperial-commercial world. The evidence supports interaction rather than a simple choice:

- local officials made contingent decisions and conspiracies;
- metropolitan institutions supplied chartered authority, finance and maritime capacity;
- Indian political fractures and credit networks shaped opportunity;
- conquest developed through escalation rather than a single settled blueprint.

### Decline, transformation and contingency

Bengal's fall should not be turned into proof that eighteenth-century Indian states were uniformly decayed. Bengal possessed substantial wealth and administration; Mir Qasim attempted serious reform; Awadh and imperial authority joined resistance. Defeat arose from particular combinations of faction, privilege, command and finance.

Nor should collaboration be treated as collective consent. Different Indian actors made choices under limited information, seeking dynastic security, profit or survival. The Company was uniquely positioned to convert temporary bargains into cumulative institutional power.

### Strongest objection, reply and qualified assessment

**Objection:** A multi-causal explanation avoids deciding what mattered most.

**Reply:** Causal ranking can be stage-specific. Commercial-jurisdictional conflict explains the opening; elite collaboration explains Plassey's form; organisation explains Buxar's verdict; Diwani explains durability.

**Qualified assessment:** The conquest was contingent in route but structural in consequence: no single event was inevitable, yet each Company gain enlarged the resources available for the next.

- Write a criterion-based verdict: entry, supremacy, title, administration.
- Include one historiographical tension and resolve it through synthesis.
- Transition carefully: Bengal revenue enabled later expansion, but the detailed campaigns require separate analysis.

**Question (20 marks, answer in 300 words):** "The British conquest of Bengal was contingent in its route but cumulative in its consequences." Evaluate.

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

Bengal's resources financed investment, soldiers and supplies, allowing each gain to strengthen the means of preserving the next. Dual Government then concealed Company supremacy behind Nawabi responsibility until its failures forced more direct administration in 1772.

Commercial, conspiratorial, military and fiscal interpretations are thus stage-specific rather than mutually exclusive. Indian agency shaped the route, while Company command determined the asymmetrical long-term distribution of power. Bengal did not mechanically cause all later conquests, but it created the fiscal-military platform from which wider expansion became more feasible.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish the historical significance of Plassey, Buxar and the Diwani. (Answer in 150 words)

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

Plassey, Buxar and the Diwani represent three different stages of Bengal's conquest.

**Plassey (1757)** gave the Company political entry. Through an alliance with Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, bankers and court factions, it removed Siraj and installed a dependent Nawab. Its battlefield scale was limited, but its regime impact and financial rewards were large.

**Buxar (1764)** supplied the wider military verdict. Hector Munro defeated Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II, demonstrating superiority over a Bengal-Awadh-imperial coalition rather than merely exploiting a palace conspiracy.

**Diwani (1765)** institutionalised victory. The imperial grant gave the Company revenue rights in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Recurring revenue financed trade and armed force, while the Nawab retained nominal administrative responsibility.

Thus, Plassey created leverage, Buxar made removal difficult, and Diwani gave durable fiscal authority. No single event alone completed the process.

**Scoring guidance:** Six marks for accurate three-stage distinction, two for institutional consequence and two for a qualified comparative verdict.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question:** Examine the proposition that the Anglo-Bengal conflict was fundamentally a struggle over fiscal sovereignty. (Answer in 250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Fiscal sovereignty provides the most continuous thread from the 1717 farman to the Diwani. Company servants extended duty-free passes to private trade, depriving the Nawabi treasury of customs and disadvantaging Indian merchants. Bengal's attempts to enforce normal duties therefore raised the constitutional question of who could define privilege within the province.

Under Siraj, the issue widened. Unauthorised fortification and asylum to a court fugitive joined taxation to defence and jurisdiction. Plassey then allowed the Company to install Mir Jafar, extract payments and protect private commerce. Mir Qasim's reforms demonstrate the fiscal core most clearly: he strengthened revenue administration and, when selective enforcement failed, abolished inland duties for all traders. The Company opposed the loss of its relative advantage, and conflict became war.

After Buxar, the Company sought not immediate blanket annexation but Diwani—the recurring revenue of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Dual Government then separated this fiscal power from Nawabi administrative responsibility.

Nevertheless, fiscal conflict was not sufficient by itself. Court faction determined Plassey's route, military organisation decided Buxar, and imperial legitimacy shaped the settlement. Fiscal sovereignty was therefore the structural axis connecting the stages, while coalition, force and law determined how control changed hands.

**Scoring guidance:** Award for tracing the fiscal thread across all stages and then limiting it with political-military variables. A narrow dastak-only answer cannot receive top marks.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question:** Evaluate the role of Indian elites, soldiers and institutions in the making of Company rule in Bengal. (Answer in 300 words)

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

Company rule in Bengal was imposed through an asymmetrical partnership with Indian actors rather than by a self-contained European force. Court elites such as Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh weakened Siraj's command at Plassey. The Jagat Seth banking house and commercial intermediaries supplied credit, connections and confidence for regime change. Their motives were dynastic, financial and factional, not a shared project of colonial sovereignty.

Indian participation continued after 1757. Revenue officials, zamindars, contractors and deputies operated the inherited fiscal machinery. Merchants supplied goods and credit. Company armies relied heavily on Indian sepoys, whose effectiveness came from regular pay, drill, logistics and a comparatively continuous command structure. Mir Qasim also used Indian and adapted military institutions to mount serious resistance, showing that Indian agency was not synonymous with collaboration.

These facts do not dissolve colonial responsibility. The Company controlled the fortified base, external reinforcement, strategic decisions and the distribution of institutional gains. It could use temporary alliances to replace rulers, protect private interests and, after Diwani, redirect Bengal revenue toward export investment and military maintenance. Collaborators did not share equally in the resulting sovereignty and often lost leverage once the Company no longer needed their bargaining power.

The most accurate description is therefore a British-directed, locally assembled Company-state. Indian resources and choices made conquest operational; Company command made the outcome cumulative and colonial. This framework avoids both the myth of effortless European superiority and the opposite error of treating indigenous participation as consent to permanent foreign rule.

**Scoring guidance:** Reserve top marks for differentiated agency—collaboration, resistance and implementation—combined with a clear account of command asymmetry. Deduct for racial explanations or collective blame.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## One-page thesis

The British conquest of Bengal was a process, not an event. Bengal's prosperous agrarian-commercial economy and organised revenue system attracted an armed Company whose 1717 privilege was stretched through servants' private trade. Customs loss, unequal competition, unauthorised fortification and resistance to Nawabi jurisdiction produced the crisis of 1756. Plassey in 1757 was a conspiracy-backed regime change that installed Mir Jafar and opened extraction. Mir Qasim's fiscal-military reform and customs equalisation showed that Company power remained contestable. Buxar in 1764 defeated a wider Bengal-Awadh-Mughal coalition. The 1765 settlement then used imperial legitimacy to grant Diwani, preserved Awadh as a buffer and separated Company revenue power from Nawabi responsibility. Dual Government institutionalised extraction without accountability until 1772. Bengal revenue financed Company investment and Indian sepoy armies, creating the fiscal platform for wider—but not inevitable—expansion.

## Essential terminology

| Term | Meaning | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Farman | Imperial order confirming privileges | Not the same as Diwani |
| Dastak | Pass for privileged Company goods | Not blanket private-trade immunity |
| Diwani | Revenue-collection rights/civil fiscal authority | Did not make Company the Nawab |
| Nizamat | Nominal policing, criminal justice and executive responsibility | Not equal effective power after 1765 |
| Dual Government | 1765-72 split between Company fiscal power and Nawabi responsibility | Not an equal partnership |
| Company investment | Purchase of Indian export goods using Company funds | Not modern capital investment in factories |
| Military-fiscal state | Political order where recurring revenue sustains organised armed force | Does not imply commerce disappeared |

## Event capsules

### 1717 farman

- Confirmed important Company trade privileges.
- Company servants' private trade exceeded the legitimate corporate exemption.
- Misuse reduced customs revenue and hurt duty-paying Indian traders.
- Conflict existed before Siraj and became a question of enforcement authority.

### Crisis of 1756

- Siraj inherited court opposition and accumulated Company-Nawab tension.
- Company fortified Calcutta without permission.
- Protection of Krishna Ballabh challenged jurisdiction.
- Siraj seized Kasimbazar and captured Calcutta on 20 June 1756.
- English officials regrouped at Fulta and received Madras reinforcements.
- Contested "Black Hole" accounts must not replace structural causes.

### Plassey, 23 June 1757

- Company coalition included Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, bankers and court interests.
- Major Nawabi contingents remained inactive; some troops fought.
- Siraj fled, was captured and killed.
- Mir Jafar became Nawab.
- Company received leverage, payments, privilege and strategic advantage.
- Best judgement: politically decisive, militarily limited.

### Mir Jafar and extraction

- Compensation, gifts, concessions and private trade depleted capacity.
- Company and servant interests overlapped but were not identical.
- Bengal resources increasingly financed Company purchases.
- Dependence made Mir Jafar both useful and incapable.
- Mir Qasim replaced him in 1760.

### Mir Qasim

- Shifted capital to Munger.
- Reformed revenue administration and army.
- Tried to rebuild fiscal-military autonomy.
- Abolished inland duties to equalise trading conditions.
- Policy sacrificed revenue but removed Company servants' relative advantage.
- Defeat in 1763 led him to seek a wider coalition.

### Buxar, 22 October 1764

- Company commander: Hector Munro.
- Opponents: Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II.
- Wider and more active military contest than Plassey.
- Company organisation, command, payment and supply mattered.
- Best judgement: military supremacy, not yet complete institutional settlement.

### Allahabad and Diwani, 1765

- Shah Alam II granted Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
- Imperial legitimacy gave legal form to coercively acquired power.
- Shuja-ud-Daula was restored; Awadh remained a buffer.
- Company took revenue without immediate blanket annexation.
- Diwani financed trade and armed force.

### Dual Government, 1765-72

- Company: decisive fiscal and military power.
- Nawab: nominal administrative responsibility.
- Indian deputies: operational continuity and intermediary agency.
- Incentive: maximise revenue while shifting blame and welfare costs.
- 1770 famine exposed vulnerability and response failure.
- Warren Hastings ended the arrangement in 1772.

## Analytical doctrines

### Fiscal sovereignty doctrine

A ruler who cannot define exemptions, tax private commerce, regulate forts or discipline offenders has lost effective sovereignty even if royal titles remain.

### Coalition doctrine

Plassey was possible because Company force and Indian elite fracture were complementary. Defection without coercive credibility might fail; force without internal access would face a larger contest.

### Three-stage conquest doctrine

> **Plassey gave a client; Buxar gave supremacy; Diwani gave revenue.**

This formulation should be qualified by adding that direct Company administration followed the failures of Dual Government.

### Power-responsibility doctrine

Institutions fail when the authority receiving income is insulated from responsibility for order and welfare. Dual Government converted this mismatch into a governing principle.

### Famine qualification doctrine

Use:

```text
harvest/disease shock
  + reduced reserves and unequal access
  + extractive demands
  + fragmented relief responsibility
  = aggravated famine vulnerability
```

Never claim revenue policy created the weather; never treat weather as sufficient explanation.

### Agency-with-asymmetry doctrine

Indian bankers, soldiers, merchants, zamindars and officials were active makers of events. Their agency does not imply equal control, common motives or equal gains. Company command turned local participation into cumulative colonial capacity.
