# Linguistic Reorganisation and Regionalism, 1947-1967 - Live Learning Session

> **Learner-first lock:** This session explains why a strong post-Partition Union chose to recognise regional languages, how popular pressure became commission-based and parliamentary change, and why accommodation generally strengthened rather than dissolved India. *The demand arose not from a belief that every language was a separate nation, but from the problem of making mass democracy work through languages people understood.*

### 1. Begin with the map people inherited

✅ **Fact:** British provincial boundaries accumulated through conquest, annexation and administrative convenience. They did not consistently follow language or culture. Madras, Bombay and the Central Provinces, among others, contained large and differently situated language groups; princely territories added another layer of discontinuity.

This matters because a province is not merely a line. It controls schools, subordinate courts, public employment, political debate and everyday contact with government. When the dominant administrative language is distant from much of the population, formal institutions remain harder to enter.

### 2. The caution supplied by Bengal

✅ **Fact:** The 1905 partition of Bengal was officially defended on administrative grounds but became inseparable from divide-and-rule and communal-political calculations; the partition was annulled in 1911.

The lesson was not that all boundary revision was illegitimate. It was that the **purpose and consent behind redrawing** mattered. A boundary imposed to divide a political public was unlike a boundary demanded to make administration more representative.

### 3. Early precedents were real but uneven

✅ **Fact:** Orissa became a separate province in 1936 after a long movement to unite important Odia-speaking areas. It is the clearest pre-independence linguistic-province precedent.

✅ **Fact with qualification:** Sind was separated from Bombay Presidency in 1936. This demonstrated that colonial provincial boundaries could be altered, but Sind is not a clean language-only template: regional, administrative, communal and political considerations overlapped.

The examples established possibility, not a settled all-India rule.

### 4. Why nationalism moved into mother tongues

After 1919, the national movement sought participation beyond English-educated elites. Political communication therefore shifted toward regional languages:

- meetings and pamphlets reached wider publics;
- local grievances could be connected to national claims;
- volunteers could organise through familiar speech;
- education and public discussion became less dependent on English.

✅ **Fact:** Congress reorganised its provincial organisation on a linguistic basis in the Nagpur reorganisation conventionally associated with 1920, with implementation and constitutional consolidation commonly dated to 1921. The source book uses 1921; the safe formulation is **the 1920-21 Congress reorganisation**.

### 5. The administrative-democratic logic

```text
mother tongue in public life
        |
        +--> easier primary education and literacy
        +--> wider political participation
        +--> more intelligible administration and courts
        +--> regional culture gains legitimate space
        |
        v
language community gains a state-level arena
without acquiring separate sovereignty
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Linguistic organisation converted cultural recognition into democratic access. Its strongest justification was not romantic attachment to language but the practical ability of ordinary citizens to understand and influence government.

### Example and its limit

The Odia movement illustrates how a dispersed language community could seek administrative coherence. But it does **not** prove that every language can be enclosed within a perfectly homogeneous territory. Migration, bilingual borderlands and mixed cities make that impossible.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Organising politics by language trains citizens to prefer province over nation.

**Reply:** Congress used linguistic provincial units while remaining an all-India movement. Regional languages became channels through which national politics reached the masses. The relevant question is whether identity is given a constitutional stake within the Union or converted into hostility toward other citizens.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus:** Post-independence consolidation and reorganisation; regionalism.
- **Prelims trap:** Congress's linguistic organisation preceded state reorganisation by decades.
- **Mains use:** Begin with conquest-made provinces, then connect mother-tongue politics to democratic legitimacy.
- **Answer line:** "Linguistic provinces were first imagined as instruments of mass democracy, not as miniature nation-states."

### Revision notes

1. Colonial provincial boundaries followed conquest and convenience more than linguistic cohesion.
2. Multilingual provinces and divided language regions created administrative distance.
3. Bengal 1905 warns that the purpose and consent behind boundary-making matter.
4. Orissa in 1936 is the clearest pre-independence linguistic-province precedent.
5. Sind's 1936 separation was a mixed regional-political precedent, not a pure linguistic model.
6. Mass mobilisation after 1919 required mother-tongue communication.
7. Congress's linguistic reorganisation is safely dated to the 1920-21 Nagpur process.
8. The source book foregrounds 1921 implementation.
9. Language aided education, political participation and accessible administration.
10. Linguistic organisation did not imply separate sovereignty.
11. No boundary could create complete linguistic homogeneity.

#### Local Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did the linguistic-province demand become part of the nationalist democratic programme before 1947?

**Model answer:**
British provinces were products of piecemeal conquest and administrative convenience. They often combined several language communities or divided one community across provinces and princely territories. Such units could be governed, but they were poorly suited to mass democracy.

After 1919, nationalism had to communicate beyond an English-educated minority. Congress therefore mobilised in mother tongues and reorganised its provincial organisation through the 1920-21 Nagpur process. Language connected political debate with popular speech and made education, subordinate administration and public participation more accessible. The creation of Orissa in 1936 also showed that a language-based provincial claim could receive institutional form.

The commitment did not imply that each language constituted a sovereign nation. Congress remained an all-India movement. Nor could linguistic boundaries eliminate all minorities. The demand was instead for administratively coherent units inside a common nation.

Thus linguistic provinces entered nationalism as a method of democratising the state while preserving national unity.

**Why this earns marks:** It turns a binary legacy narrative into a causal account of why inherited boundaries generated a democratic language principle, while keeping party organisation distinct from sovereign statehood.

**Scoring note:** Rubric (10 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2): conquest-made provincial problem (2); post-1919 mother-tongue mobilisation (2); 1920-21 Congress reorganisation (2); Orissa or another qualified precedent (2); states-without-sovereignty conclusion (2).

**Causal handoff to Lesson 2:** Once language had become a democratic organising principle, independence forced leaders to decide whether that inherited promise could survive Partition-era risks.

### 1. The same principle entered a different risk environment

Topic 29 established the new Republic's immediate problem: it required enough central capacity to survive Partition, integrate territory and begin democratic government. Redrawing all internal boundaries at once could disrupt officials, revenues, police jurisdictions, transport, assets and development plans.

✅ **Fact:** Leadership concerns included Partition dislocation, refugees, economic and law-and-order problems, Kashmir and a war-like situation with Pakistan.

✅ **Dated evidence:** Speaking on the linguistic question on **27 November 1947**, Nehru stated: **"First things must come first and the first thing is the security and stability of India."** The sentence captures his ordering of priorities: linguistic reorganisation remained a recognised principle, but immediate consolidation came first.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This was a sequencing dispute. A reform considered democratically legitimate could still be judged administratively dangerous at a particular moment.

### 2. The Linguistic Provinces Commission

✅ **Fact:** The Constituent Assembly appointed the Linguistic Provinces Commission in 1948 under Justice S.K. Dhar, usually called the Dhar Commission.

It advised against immediate linguistic reorganisation, stressing national unity and administrative convenience. The Constituent Assembly therefore did not embed a complete linguistic map in the Constitution.

**Important distinction:** The Commission did not prove language irrelevant. It judged that language alone should not control an immediate nationwide settlement under crisis conditions.

### 3. The JVP Committee

✅ **Fact:** Congress appointed the JVP Committee in December 1948. Its members were:

| Initial | Member | Position relevant to the name |
|---|---|---|
| J | Jawaharlal Nehru | Prime Minister and Congress leader |
| V | Vallabhbhai Patel | Deputy Prime Minister |
| P | Pattabhi Sitaramayya | Congress President |

The committee again advised delay, placing unity, security and economic development first.

Yet its position was **qualified**. Where a demand became insistent and overwhelming and other affected language groups agreed, creation of a state remained possible. That was a political safety valve, not a permanent prohibition.

### 4. Why caution could be reasonable without being final

```text
If redrawing begins too early:
administrative disruption + competitive agitation + border conflict

If every demand is suppressed indefinitely:
broken nationalist promise + alienation + escalation

JVP balance:
delay the general operation,
but retain a route for compelling and broadly agreed cases
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Dhar and JVP simply betrayed the nationalist commitment.

**Reply:** Their caution arose after Partition had shown the destructive potential of territorialised identity and while state capacity was strained. The fair criticism is not that delay was irrational; it is that indefinite delay became politically untenable when a broad, persistent demand could no longer be treated as a temporary disturbance.

### UPSC application

- Separate **principle** from **timing**.
- Distinguish Dhar, a Constituent Assembly commission, from JVP, a Congress committee.
- Avoid saying JVP permanently rejected linguistic states.
- Use Topic 29's strong-Union foundation: security created caution; democratic legitimacy later required accommodation.

### Revision notes

1. Post-1947 hesitation did not erase the pre-1947 commitment.
2. Partition created administrative, political and economic dislocation.
3. Kashmir and security pressures shaped the timing debate.
4. Immediate reorganisation threatened disruption of scarce state capacity.
5. The Linguistic Provinces Commission was appointed in 1948.
6. Justice S.K. Dhar chaired it.
7. Dhar stressed unity and administrative convenience.
8. JVP was a Congress committee, not the SRC.
9. JVP meant Nehru, Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya.
10. JVP advised delay, not an unconditional permanent ban.
11. Overwhelming demand and agreement of affected groups kept concession possible.

#### Local Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Was the early national leadership's hesitation over linguistic states a reversal of principle? Explain.

**Model answer:**
The hesitation changed timing, not the underlying principle. Congress had organised provincially on linguistic lines in 1920-21 and repeatedly accepted the democratic value of mother-tongue administration.

Independence, however, arrived with Partition, refugee rehabilitation, division of services and assets, communal violence, Kashmir and severe economic constraints. The leadership feared that simultaneous boundary campaigns would disrupt administration and intensify rival claims. The 1948 Dhar Commission therefore opposed immediate reorganisation, and the Congress's JVP Committee placed unity, security and development first.

Yet JVP did not close the question permanently. It allowed that an insistent, overwhelming demand, acceptable to other affected groups, could justify a new state. This qualification preserved the earlier commitment while postponing its general application.

The leadership can therefore be criticised for underestimating popular urgency, but not accurately described as abandoning linguistic accommodation. Andhra would expose the limits of postponement and force a transition from caution to institutional settlement.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains postponement as a conflict between an accepted democratic norm and founding-state capacity, rather than misreading caution as repudiation of the linguistic principle.

### 1. A movement older than the immediate crisis

✅ **Fact:** The demand for a Telugu-speaking Andhra unit had developed over decades and had broad party support. It was not created by one fast.

The central difficulty was Madras city. Andhra leaders claimed it, while linguistic and geographic arguments placed it in the Tamil region. Thus even a broadly accepted linguistic principle could not mechanically determine a mixed and symbolically valuable capital.

### 2. Potti Sriramulu's fast and death

✅ **Fact:** Potti Sriramulu, a Gandhian freedom fighter, began his fast on **19 October 1952**.

✅ **Fact:** He died after **58 days**, on **15 December 1952**.

His death triggered widespread disorder, demonstrations, hartals and violence in Andhra areas. The government conceded a separate state.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Martyrdom did not invent the claim; it transformed the political cost of refusing a long-standing claim. Popular pressure from below altered the Union's calculation.

### 3. Keep the two creations separate

| Date | Unit | Territorial meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 October 1953 | Andhra State | Telugu-speaking areas separated from Madras State |
| 1 November 1956 | Andhra Pradesh | Andhra State united with the Telangana region of former Hyderabad State |

✅ **Fact:** The Andhra State Act, 1953 gave legal form to the first creation.

⚠️ **UPSC trap:** Do not call the 1953 unit Andhra Pradesh. Andhra State was the **first post-independence state created on a linguistic basis**; the 1956 SRC settlement generalised and extended the principle.

### 4. Why Andhra changed national policy

Before Andhra, leaders could treat linguistic reorganisation as a deferred general question. After Andhra, selective concession generated equivalent claims elsewhere. The Union now needed:

- criteria applicable beyond one language;
- an inquiry capable of hearing competing regions;
- a process for assets, services, legislatures and borders;
- a national rather than case-by-case settlement.

This is why Andhra leads causally to the SRC.

### Example and limit

Andhra supports the 2026 formulation that reorganisation was driven by popular pressure from below. But "driven" does not mean "designed entirely from below." Parliament, the Union executive and later the SRC converted pressure into territorial and institutional rules.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Conceding after violence rewarded coercion and made future agitation inevitable.

**Reply:** That risk was real. The answer was not indefinite refusal but a general commission-based process. Andhra revealed both the democratic force of sustained consent and the danger of deciding every boundary through street escalation.

### UPSC application

- Use the exact sequence: long demand -> Madras dispute -> fast -> death -> concession -> 1953 state -> 1956 merger.
- For "pressure from below," explain both mobilisation and institutional conversion.
- Mention violence neutrally; do not romanticise it as the only source of legitimacy.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain how the Andhra movement altered both the timing and the method of linguistic reorganisation.

**Model answer:**
The Andhra demand was not a sudden product of Potti Sriramulu's fast. Telugu organisations and political parties had sought a separate unit for decades, and the JVP Committee accepted that a strong case existed. Immediate settlement failed largely because Andhra and Tamil leaders disagreed over Madras city.

Sriramulu began a fast on 19 October 1952 and died after fifty-eight days on 15 December. Demonstrations, hartals and violence followed. The Union concluded that continued refusal threatened order and democratic legitimacy more than concession did. The Andhra State Act created Andhra State on 1 October 1953.

This changed timing: a question postponed after Partition became urgent. It also changed method. Conceding one state encouraged movements elsewhere, but case-by-case responses risked competitive escalation. The government therefore appointed the States Reorganisation Commission in August 1953 to examine the Union as a whole.

Andhra also supplies an important distinction. The 1953 state comprised Telugu areas separated from Madras; Andhra Pradesh emerged only on 1 November 1956 through the Telangana merger.

### 1. Appointment and composition

✅ **Fact:** The Union appointed the States Reorganisation Commission in **August 1953**.

| Member | Role |
|---|---|
| Justice Fazl Ali | Chair |
| K.M. Panikkar | Member |
| H.N. Kunzru | Member |

The Commission reported in **1955** after receiving extensive representations and encountering meetings, demonstrations, hunger strikes and rival boundary campaigns.

### 2. The question it had to answer

The SRC did not ask simply, "Which language is spoken here?" It had to decide whether a proposed unit could function securely, administratively and economically while treating minorities fairly.

Its working criteria can be organised as:

1. preservation and strengthening of Indian unity and security;
2. linguistic and cultural homogeneity;
3. administrative convenience and manageability;
4. financial and economic viability;
5. balanced development;
6. interests of linguistic and other minorities.

✅ **Fact:** It broadly accepted language as an important organising principle.

⚠️ **Qualification:** Language was a strong presumption, not an automatic algorithm. Mixed cities, border districts, economic networks and minority populations required judgement.

### 3. Why public representations mattered

Representations supplied information about:

- language use and cultural affiliation;
- district-level contiguity;
- access to capitals and institutions;
- revenue and developmental capacity;
- fears of minority domination;
- rival claims to the same territory.

But mass mobilisation could also exaggerate extinction, migration or communal danger. The Commission criticised parochial frenzy and "border warfare" among people recently united in the freedom struggle.

### 4. Report and political decision

✅ **Fact:** The SRC submitted its report in 1955. The Union broadly accepted the linguistic principle but modified recommendations.

✅ **Fact:** The Commission opposed immediate division of Bombay and Punjab. Those exceptions exposed questions language alone could not settle: a contested metropolis in Bombay and a language-script-religion overlap in Punjab.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Once language was accepted, adding viability, administration and minority criteria allowed elites to dilute popular sovereignty.

**Reply:** A state boundary distributes institutions, revenue, water, jobs and minority status. Ignoring those effects would also be undemocratic. The proper criticism concerns how criteria were weighted, not whether non-linguistic criteria were legitimate.

### UPSC application

- Never reduce the SRC to "one language, one state."
- Use Bombay and Punjab to prove the multi-criterion character.
- For the 2026 PYQ, the SRC is the hinge between bottom-up pressure and top-down institutional settlement.
- A commission recommendation is not self-executing; Parliament and the Constitution supplied implementation.

### 1. The States Reorganisation Act, 1956

✅ **Fact:** The States Reorganisation Act was **Act 37 of 1956**, received assent on **31 August 1956**, and used **1 November 1956** as the appointed day.

The Act redrew territories and dealt with consequences that a map change necessarily produces, including, as applicable:

- state formation and territorial transfer;
- representation in Parliament and state legislatures;
- adaptation of legislative arrangements;
- High Court jurisdiction and related judicial provisions;
- allocation or continuation of services;
- division or adjustment of assets, liabilities and institutions;
- creation of zonal councils for consultation and coordination.

### 2. The Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956

✅ **Fact:** The Seventh Amendment supplied the constitutional restructuring required by the new map.

It:

- revised Article 1 and the First Schedule;
- abolished the old constitutional classification of Part A, Part B and Part C states, with former Part D territory also absorbed into the new states/Union-territory framework;
- established the simpler distinction between **states** and **Union territories**;
- adjusted representation and provisions concerning High Courts and public services;
- enabled arrangements suited to the reorganised Union.

> **Precision rule:** Do not say the States Reorganisation Act alone abolished the old categories. Territorial legislation and constitutional amendment worked together.

### 3. The new count

✅ **Fact:** On **1 November 1956**, India had **14 states and 6 Union Territories** under the new arrangement.

This was not merely cartography. Legislators, judges, civil servants, records, property and liabilities had to be assigned so that government continued on the next day.

### 4. The bounded Article 3 process

Article 3 explains how later territorial revision remains possible without making a commission report or a movement demand self-executing:

```text
Union proposal for forming or altering a state
                 |
                 v
Bill requires the President's recommendation
                 |
                 v
President refers it to the affected state legislature
for views within the specified or extended period
                 |
                 v
Parliament may enact the change by law
```

✅ **Fact:** The affected legislature is entitled to express its views, but its consent is not a constitutional veto. Parliament remains the law-making authority. The point here is only the bounded historical mechanism; detailed doctrine belongs to Polity.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The distinction between the Act and Amendment is legalistic detail with little historical value.

**Reply:** The distinction reveals how democratic accommodation remained constitutionally ordered. Popular mobilisation changed the map, but Parliament had to preserve representation, courts, services and continuity. That is the mechanism by which pressure became stable government rather than administrative rupture.

### UPSC application

- Prelims may test Act versus Amendment.
- Mains should describe 1956 as a combined territorial-constitutional settlement.
- Remember Act 37, assent 31 August, appointed day 1 November.
- Connect zonal councils with inter-state consultation, not with a new level of sovereignty.
- For later changes, distinguish Article 3 consultation from state consent: presidential recommendation, reference for views, then parliamentary law.

- assets and institutions.

### 1. Why the contrast matters

Nagaland is relevant only because it proves that post-independence reorganisation used more than one logic. Andhra, Maharashtra and Gujarat principally involved language and administration. Nagaland involved:

- a distinct tribal-ethnic history;
- limited integration into pre-independence provincial politics;
- an armed sovereignty challenge;
- negotiations over special protections.

### 2. From agreement to statehood

✅ **Fact:** The 1960 **16-Point Agreement** between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention supplied the immediate negotiated context.

✅ **Fact:** Parliament enacted the **State of Nagaland Act, 1962**.

✅ **Fact:** Nagaland became a state on **1 December 1963**.

Statehood therefore followed negotiation and legislation, but it did not erase every insurgent disagreement or secure universal Naga consent.

### 3. Article 371A

✅ **Fact:** Article 371A provides special safeguards concerning, among other matters:

- Naga religious or social practices;
- Naga customary law and procedure;
- administration of civil and criminal justice involving customary law;
- ownership and transfer of land and its resources.

Parliamentary laws on these matters do not automatically apply to Nagaland unless the state Legislative Assembly so decides.

⚠️ **Boundary:** Detailed constitutional doctrine and the wider history of Naga insurgency belong to Polity and Topic 31 respectively.

### 4. The analytical contrast

Language-state accommodation says: give a predominant language community an administratively intelligible state.

Asymmetric accommodation says: because a region's history and institutions differ sharply, give it special protections inside a shared constitutional order.

Both reject secession, but the second goes beyond ordinary symmetry.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Special safeguards create unequal citizenship and encourage further exceptional demands.

**Reply:** Uniform rules can produce substantive inequality where customary institutions, land relations and political histories differ. Asymmetry can make common citizenship credible by protecting what accession to the common order might otherwise endanger. The remaining risk is weak accountability or unresolved conflict, which requires continuing democratic negotiation.

```text
        +--> regional elites and voters gain a federal arena
```

### 1. Why language could strengthen democracy

✅ **Fact:** The source book links linguistic states with mass education, administration and politics through languages widely understood by citizens.

This can deepen:

- participation in elections and parties;
- comprehension of government;
- regional literature and public debate;
- recruitment of local political leadership;
- acceptance that the Union belongs to many cultures.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** A citizen may possess layered loyalties. Tamil, Punjabi, Marathi or Gujarati identity need not subtract from Indian identity when constitutional institutions make both usable.

### 2. A federal stake, not a licence to exit

Statehood offered:

- a legislature;
- a responsible government;
- administrative recognition;
- bargaining power in national institutions.

It did **not** offer:

- separate sovereignty;
- exclusive ownership by the state-language majority;
- immunity from parliamentary reorganisation;
- freedom to deny equal citizenship to migrants or minorities.

### 3. The strongest fragmentation objection

Critics feared a chain:

```text
language state -> hardened identity -> rival nationalism
-> weak Centre -> secession -> Balkanisation
```

The fear was not absurd after Partition. Language movements sometimes used harsh rhetoric and violence. Bombay and Punjab showed how boundaries could intensify competition.

### 4. Evidence-based reply

The historical record by the late 1960s suggested a different chain:

```text
recognition -> reduced cultural grievance
-> administration through intelligible language
-> electoral competition for state power
-> bargaining with the Union
-> deeper investment in the federal order
```

✅ **Fact:** Reorganisation did not paralyse the Centre. States continued to cooperate in planning and development.

✅ **Fact:** Linguistic politics did not permanently define all politics within states; class, caste, development, party competition and leadership also mattered.

### 5. The qualification: language does not homogenise

Every language-majority state contained:

- linguistic minorities;
- dialect and script differences;
- sub-regional inequalities;
- tribal and religious communities;
- migrants and mixed cities.

Therefore linguistic federalism works only when **majority language** means an administrative medium, not proprietary ownership.

### 6. Example and limit

Andhra demonstrates successful recognition; Bombay demonstrates difficult bargaining; Punjab demonstrates communalisation risk. No single case proves the entire thesis. Taken together, they show that accommodation usually reduced the cost of belonging to India but generated new internal minority and distribution questions.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The survival of border and water disputes disproves the integration thesis.

**Reply:** Integration does not mean absence of conflict. It means conflict is processed through elections, courts, commissions, Parliament and intergovernmental bargaining rather than through general withdrawal from the Union. Persistent disputes are limits, but their institutionalisation is also evidence of federal consolidation.

- Frame the answer as mechanism plus evidence plus residual cost.
- Avoid the categorical claim that language "solved" regionalism.
- Distinguish loyalty to a region from hostility to another region or the Union.
- Use the phrase "states, not sovereignty" as an analytical shorthand.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** "Linguistic federalism gave regional identities a stake in India rather than a route out of it." Examine.

**Model answer:**
The case for linguistic federalism rested on democratic access. Politics, education and administration conducted through a predominant regional language were more intelligible to ordinary citizens. Statehood also gave regional leaders a legislature, government and recognised arena for bargaining with the Union.

This accommodation separated cultural recognition from sovereignty. Andhra, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Haryana were created as states within India, not as language nations entitled to exit. The Centre retained defence, macro-level coordination and constitutional authority, while states continued to cooperate in planning and administration. By removing the fear of cultural domination, reorganisation reduced a grievance that could have become separatist.

The argument must be qualified. Bombay involved violence over a shared metropolis. Punjab showed how language, script and religion could be communalised. Every linguistic state contained minorities, migrants and borderlands; Belagavi, Chandigarh and river-water disputes survived. Language did not erase sub-regional inequality or create homogeneous units.

These limits do not reverse the verdict. Integration means that conflict becomes electoral, legislative, judicial and intergovernmental, not that conflict disappears. Linguistic federalism largely transformed claims for recognition into competition and negotiation within a common constitutional order.

### 1. Regional identity is not automatically regionalism

✅ **Fact:** The source book distinguishes pride in one's state, language or culture from hostile regionalism. A person can be Tamil and Indian without contradiction.

Demanding development, defending federal features, seeking a state within India or asking for devolution is not inherently anti-national. The threshold is crossed when one region's interest is advanced through hostility toward another region, migrants or the nation as a whole.

### 2. Regional inequality

Colonial rule concentrated ports, rail links, commerce, modern industry and administrative investment in a limited set of enclaves. Regions outside those circuits often entered independence with weaker infrastructure, narrower markets, lower revenue and thinner administrative capacity.

The Union attempted correction through:

- Finance Commission tax devolution and grants;
- plan transfers and special assistance during the planning era;
- location of public-sector plants and major infrastructure;
- freight, credit and industrial policies affecting private location;
- incentives intended to draw private investment toward backward regions.

### 3. Sub-regionalism: the state can contain its own centre-periphery divide

Sub-regionalism appears when a region argues that the dominant political or economic centre inside its own state captures public investment, irrigation, employment or cultural recognition. Its later expressions reveal several distinct grammars:

|---|---|---|

| Bodoland | Bodo ethnic identity, land and political autonomy | not an ordinary language-state replication |

These examples are not collapsed into the 1947-67 chronology. They show the longer analytical afterlife of a problem already visible by the late 1960s: a linguistic state can still contain unequal sub-regions.

### 4. DMK: genealogy, communication and electoral conversion

The DMK inherited two pre-independence currents: the **Justice Party-led non-Brahmin movement** and Periyar E.V. Ramaswamy's radical, anti-caste and anti-religious **Self-Respect Movement**. Periyar and C.N. Annadurai established the **Dravidar Kazhagam (DK) in 1944**. In **1949**, the organisation split and Annadurai founded the **Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK)**.

Annadurai, M. Karunanidhi, M.G. Ramachandran and other cultural figures used **drama, cinema, journals, pamphlets and public oratory** to build a mass base, especially among younger and rural-background voters. The party initially carried:

- anti-Brahmin and anti-North rhetoric;
- Tamil cultural assertion;
- opposition to Hindi;
- a demand for Dravidanadu.

Its political and electoral trajectory changed:

1. it broadened Tamil rather than racial appeal;
2. although it did not contest the 1952 election directly, it helped nearly thirty Assembly candidates win;
3. it contested the 1957 and 1962 elections, winning **50 Assembly seats and seven Lok Sabha seats in 1962**;
4. during the India-China war it supported the national cause and suspended secessionist propaganda;
5. after the Sixteenth Amendment it formally gave up secession and shifted toward state autonomy;
6. the 1965 anti-Hindi agitation and a broad electoral alliance expanded its support;
7. in 1967 it won **138 of 234 Assembly seats**, defeated Congress and formed government under C.N. Annadurai.

✅ **Fact:** The 1967 victory shows how an oppositional identity movement could become a governing regional party inside India.

### 5. Akali politics

Akali mobilisation combined Sikh institutional interests, Punjabi language, Gurmukhi and demands for a Punjabi-speaking state. It belonged to constitutional politics but carried a communalised dimension. The correct analysis avoids both extremes: neither every Akali demand nor every Punjabi Suba supporter can be dismissed as communal, yet religious coding cannot be ignored.

### 6. Sons of the soil: when development competition becomes nativist

✅ **Fact:** Shiv Sena was founded in **1966** under Bal Thackeray.

Its "sons of the soil" politics claimed preferential access for Marathi-speakers and treated some migrants as outsiders. The social mechanism is more precise than a simple dislike of migration:

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Migration does not automatically produce nativism. The politics becomes more likely when local educated groups experience blocked mobility, migrants are visible in desired occupations, and organisations can convert economic competition into a cultural claim. Within this topic's cutoff, only Shiv Sena's 1966 foundation and this mechanism are required; later campaigns lie beyond 1967.

### 7. How the system responded


**Reply:** Unprincipled concession can create that incentive. Yet refusal can radicalise legitimate identity and development claims. The successful pattern combined non-negotiable Union integrity and equal citizenship with negotiable boundaries, language use, autonomy and resource allocation.

### 1. The unavoidable minority

No map could put every speaker of one language inside one state or remove every other language from it. Linguistic reorganisation therefore changed who formed the state majority; it did not abolish minority status.

The democratic problem has two sides:

- minorities need assurance against cultural, educational and employment discrimination;
- the state needs common institutions and a workable public language.

### 2. Constitutional safeguards: know their functions

| Provision | Safeguarding function |
|---|---|
| Article 29 | Protects the right of sections of citizens to conserve a distinct language, script or culture |
| Article 30 | Protects religious and linguistic minorities' right to establish and administer educational institutions |
| Article 347 | Allows presidential recognition of a language spoken by a substantial section of a state's population for specified purposes |
| Article 350A | Directs efforts to provide mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage for children of linguistic-minority groups |
| Article 350B | Provides a Special Officer for linguistic minorities |

✅ **Fact:** The Special Officer's institution is commonly known through the **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities**, who investigates safeguards and reports.

⚠️ **Boundary:** These provisions are introduced here only to distinguish safeguarding from boundary-making; doctrinal detail belongs to Polity.

### 3. Residual territorial and distributive disputes

Examples include:

- **Belagavi/Belgaum:** a Karnataka-Maharashtra boundary dispute;
- **Chandigarh:** Union Territory and joint capital claimed within Punjab-Haryana politics;
- river-water disputes;
- division of assets and institutions;
- uneven investment between regions inside a state.

These conflicts often combine identity with tangible resources. A boundary dispute may concern language, but water, irrigation, jobs, revenue and administrative convenience sustain it.

### 4. Internal dominance and sub-regionalism

A state-language majority can dominate:

- smaller language communities;
- tribal regions;
- dialect groups;
- economically backward sub-regions;
- migrants in cities.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Reorganisation moves the minority problem down one level. It solves centre-versus-region recognition only if the new state practises plural citizenship internally.

### 5. Urdu: a linguistic minority without a territorial-majority state

Urdu exposes a limit of the territorial solution. Unlike Telugu, Marathi or Gujarati, post-Partition Urdu speakers did not possess a compact territorial majority from which a corresponding state could be formed. They were dispersed across several states and were often bilingual in everyday life.

The resulting problems concerned implementation rather than a new boundary:

- recognition for specified official purposes where a substantial population used Urdu;
- availability of mother-tongue primary instruction and qualified teachers;
- permission, aid and practical space for Urdu-medium or Urdu-teaching institutions;
- access to forms, offices and lower-level public services;
- reliable identification of need where speakers might report another regional language.

✅ **Fact:** Bipan Chandra records that Urdu speakers formed substantial minorities in several states, including Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, but Urdu lacked the support that a territorial-majority language received from its own state government. In both **Uttar Pradesh and Bihar**, the dispute therefore centred on second-language recognition, schooling and administrative support rather than a claim to an Urdu-majority state.

✅ **Fact with attribution:** The Uttar Pradesh government defended resistance to second-official-language status by invoking an **SRC-associated recommendation that a language should be spoken by at least 30 per cent of a state's population** before becoming a second official or regional language. The threshold was politically consequential because a dispersed minority could be substantial yet remain below it at the state level.

✅ **Fact:** The education dispute was practical. Urdu declined in many primary schools, its use as a medium of instruction narrowed, and the supply of teachers and textbooks remained uneven. These were state-level implementation questions even when constitutional safeguards and Union monitoring existed.

✅ **Fact:** Nehru rejected the communal treatment of Urdu, described it as an Indian language embodying cultural integration, and urged the Uttar Pradesh chief minister to recognise it as a second official language in districts where it was widely used while providing full minority-language facilities elsewhere. Limited concessions were weakened by lax implementation, and the state government declined to give the arrangement statutory force.

✅ **Fact:** Articles 29, 30, 347, 350A and 350B provide different routes for cultural conservation, educational institutions, recognition, primary instruction and monitoring. None automatically creates a territorial state or guarantees uniform state-level implementation.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Urdu was also vulnerable to communalisation after Partition: a language with a broad Indian literary and regional history could be treated politically as the property of one religious community. That framing weakened ordinary linguistic claims by converting recognition into a loyalty test.

The institutional division of labour is crucial:

```text
constitutional guarantee and Union monitoring
                  |
                  v
Special Officer / Commissioner reports on safeguards
                  |
                  v
state and local authorities implement schooling,
recognition, staffing and administrative access
                  |
                  v
rights may remain uneven when implementation is weak
```

The Union can monitor, report and use constitutional channels; routine education and administration still depend heavily on state implementation. Urdu therefore shows why minority protection cannot be replaced by territorial reorganisation.

> **Later evidence beyond the 1967 cutoff:** Bipan Chandra notes Urdu's recognition in the Telangana region of Andhra Pradesh from **1968** and cites **1979-80** Uttar Pradesh schooling data to demonstrate the persistence of the implementation gap. These later examples confirm the mechanism; they are not part of the topic's core 1947-67 chronology.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Minority safeguards obstruct integration by preserving separate identities.

**Reply:** Forced uniformity creates insecurity and resistance. Safeguards make integration credible by assuring citizens that participation in a state does not require cultural disappearance. They must, however, be paired with access to the state's common public institutions.

### 1. Article 343 and the transition problem

✅ **Fact:** Article 343 declares Hindi in Devanagari script the official language of the Union and provided a fifteen-year transition during which English would continue for Union official purposes.

As 26 January 1965 approached, non-Hindi regions feared that ending English would:

- disadvantage candidates in all-India services;
- reduce access to Union institutions;
- create cultural and political domination;
- shift opportunity toward Hindi-speaking citizens.

### 2. The Official Languages Act, 1963

✅ **Fact:** The Official Languages Act, 1963 provided for continued use of English for specified Union official purposes beyond the constitutional transition.

Ambiguity and distrust remained. Political assurances were not yet experienced as sufficiently durable by many non-Hindi speakers.

### 3. The 1965 anti-Hindi mobilisation

✅ **Fact:** In Madras State, the DMK organised opposition and students became central participants in the 1965 agitation. Protest escalated into serious violence and deaths.

The issue was not whether Hindi could develop. It was whether the Union would make Hindi the exclusive gateway to national administration and opportunity.

### 4. The 1967 statutory and political settlement

✅ **Fact:** The 1967 amendment and accompanying political settlement gave durable assurance that English would continue alongside Hindi for Union official purposes and communication with non-Hindi states.

> **Precision rule:** Do not call English an official constitutional "associate language." That phrase is common political shorthand. The constitutional and statutory position is continued use of English alongside Hindi for specified Union purposes.

The settlement reduced the existential character of the controversy. Hindi could expand without a fixed coercive deadline, while non-Hindi regions retained access through English.

### 5. The three-language formula: school policy, not the Union's legal language rule

The three-language formula sought to distribute language learning rather than determine state borders. In broad historical form, it combined:

- the regional language or mother tongue;
- Hindi in non-Hindi areas, with another modern Indian language encouraged in Hindi-speaking areas;
- English.

⚠️ **Qualification:** Adoption and implementation varied across states. The formula was an educational-political compromise, not one uniform constitutional command, and it did not settle the separate question of which language the Union would use officially.

### 6. Recruitment and access: why language policy affected opportunity

The controversy reached beyond symbolism because Union language could shape:

- civil-services and other all-India recruitment examinations;
- training, departmental examinations and advancement;
- correspondence between the Union and non-Hindi states;
- access to laws, forms, public information and central institutions;
- the ability of regional-language educated citizens to compete without first crossing an abrupt Hindi barrier.

✅ **Exact 1967 recruitment settlement:** A **parliamentary policy resolution**, distinct from the Official Languages Act, provided that public-service examinations would be conducted in **Hindi, English and the regional languages**. Candidates were additionally expected to know **Hindi or English**. This widened the examination gateway while retaining a common language requirement for Union service.

The distinction matters:

```text
Official Languages Act / 1967 amendment
        -> Union official work and Centre-state communication

separate parliamentary policy resolution
        -> public-service examination media
        -> Hindi + English + regional languages
        -> candidate also knows Hindi or English
```

The compromise therefore had four linked but distinct institutional mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Function | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Continued English for Union purposes | preserved inter-state and all-India access during an open-ended transition | retained advantages for the English-educated |
| Multilingual public-service examinations under the separate resolution | opened examination media to Hindi, English and regional languages while requiring additional knowledge of Hindi or English | examination access did not erase unequal educational preparation |
| Hindi development and translation machinery | expanded an Indian official language without an exclusive deadline | expansion could still be experienced as pressure |
| Regional-language education and the three-language formula | widened schooling and attempted reciprocal language learning | uneven state implementation and differing political acceptance |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Equality required avoiding both an abrupt Hindi monopoly and an English monopoly. The historical settlement managed the first danger more successfully than it removed the second.

### 7. Link to regionalism

The controversy helped the DMK's 1967 victory, but the party also helped cool the issue after entering government. This is the accommodation cycle:

```text
fear of cultural domination
        -> mobilisation
        -> electoral change
        -> statutory assurance
        -> regional party accepts responsibility within Union
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Retaining English perpetuated colonial privilege and delayed an Indian link language.

**Reply:** That cost was real, especially for citizens without English education. But immediate Hindi exclusivity would have redistributed disadvantage toward non-Hindi regions and threatened federal trust. The settlement was a political compromise, not a declaration that language inequality had ended.

### UPSC application

- Separate state boundaries from Union working language.
- Use Article 343, 1963, 1965 and 1967 as the sequence.
- Never use "associate language" as formal constitutional terminology.
- Route detailed provisions, language lists and case law to Polity.
- Keep the three-language formula within education policy and the historical compromise; do not confuse it with Article 343 or state-border reorganisation.
- Connect civil-services recruitment and regional-language education to material access, not merely symbolic identity.

#### Local Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Why must the official-language controversy be distinguished from linguistic reorganisation of states?

**Model answer:**
Linguistic reorganisation answered a territorial question: how should India's internal states be drawn so that administration and politics reflect predominant regional languages? The Union official-language controversy answered a different question: which language or languages should be used in central government and communication across states?

Article 343 made Hindi in Devanagari the Union's official language while providing a fifteen-year period for continued English. The Official Languages Act, 1963 authorised continued English use beyond that transition. Nevertheless, many non-Hindi speakers feared that 1965 would make Hindi the exclusive gateway to Union employment and institutions. In Madras State, students and the DMK drove a powerful anti-Hindi mobilisation, accompanied by violence and deaths.

The 1967 settlement used distinct instruments. The statutory amendment secured continued English for Union work and communication. A separate parliamentary resolution provided public-service examinations in Hindi, English and regional languages, while requiring candidates also to know Hindi or English. Translation and gradual Hindi development formed another part of the mechanism.

The three-language formula addressed education rather than Union legal status: broadly, the regional language, Hindi or another modern Indian language, and English. State implementation varied. English is often called an "associate language," but that is not its formal constitutional label.

Both questions affected regional trust, yet they used different institutions. State reorganisation gave languages territorial arenas; the official-language compromise protected federal access.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates Union-language law, school policy and recruitment access, then explains how distinct instruments jointly reduced fears without erasing regional-language claims.

**Causal handoff to Lesson 14:** The Union-language compromise completes the evidence needed to judge the larger model: diversity remained negotiable through institutions while common citizenship and Union integrity set the boundary.

### 1. Back-link: what Topic 29 made possible

Topic 29 explained why the Republic combined:

- a Union strong enough to survive Partition and integration;
- universal franchise and elected governments;
- federal and cultural accommodation;
- courts, services and Parliament capable of processing conflict.

Linguistic reorganisation tested whether strength meant uniformity. The answer that emerged was different: the Union remained strong partly because it was able to revise its own internal map.

### 2. The complete causal sequence

1. Colonial boundaries lacked linguistic-democratic coherence.
2. Nationalism mobilised through mother tongues and made a pre-1947 commitment.
3. Partition and security crises produced temporary postponement.
4. Dhar and JVP institutionalised caution, with JVP preserving a qualified opening.
5. Andhra's mass movement made indefinite delay impossible.
6. The SRC generalised the issue through multiple criteria.
7. The 1956 Act and Seventh Amendment converted recommendations into a working map.
8. Bombay and Punjab required later negotiated settlements.
9. Minority safeguards acknowledged that language states remained plural.
10. The official-language compromise protected access to the Union.
11. Elections transformed movements such as the DMK into governing participants.
12. Nativism and communalisation remained warnings against proprietary regional identity.

### 3. Was reorganisation only pressure from below?

**Yes, in an important sense:**

- longstanding associations sustained demands;
- Sriramulu's death forced Andhra;
- agitations kept Bombay and Punjab open;
- elections imposed costs on resistant governments.

**No, if "only" is implied:**

- Congress had made an earlier ideological commitment;
- state elites negotiated and sometimes opposed claims;
- commissions balanced rival evidence;
- Parliament enacted boundaries;
- Article 3 required presidential recommendation and consultation with the affected legislature before parliamentary law;
- constitutional amendment preserved institutional continuity;
- courts, minority safeguards and intergovernmental arrangements shaped outcomes.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The best formula is **pressure from below, processed and bounded from above**.

### 4. Achievements

- cultural recognition without language sovereignty;
- wider participation and intelligible administration;
- stronger legitimacy for state and Union institutions;
- electoral absorption of major regional movements;
- a revisable rather than sacred internal map;
- scope for asymmetric accommodation where ordinary linguistic logic did not fit.

### 5. Limits

- linguistic minorities and internal dominance;
- disputed borders and capitals;
- river-water and resource conflicts;
- regional and sub-regional inequality;
- sons-of-soil exclusion;
- communalisation of language;
- violence as a pressure mechanism;
- incomplete settlements in security-sensitive regions.

### 6. Forward routes

- **Topic 31:** tribal integration, Naga and Mizo insurgencies, autonomy, Fifth and Sixth Schedule approaches.
- **Topic 33 and later political topics:** regional parties, the post-1967 party system, coalition politics and later centre-state crises.
- **Polity:** Article 3, official-language doctrine, special provisions and detailed minority rights.
- **Boundary reminder:** Article 3 concerns territorial law-making; the official-language compromise concerns Union work, recruitment, communication and educational access.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Repeated concessions prove that the Centre lacked a coherent principle and responded only to pressure.

**Reply:** Policy did evolve under pressure, and uneven responsiveness is a legitimate criticism. Yet a coherent framework emerged: preserve Union sovereignty and equal citizenship; negotiate state boundaries, language use and autonomy; test viability and minority effects; enact change through constitutional institutions. Adaptability was a feature of consolidation, not merely a symptom of weakness.

### UPSC application

- For "comment," state a thesis, validate the claim, then limit it.
- Use chronology as causation, not as a date list.
- Pair every achievement with a residual problem.
- End with a federal mechanism: recognition converted exit pressures into voice.

#### Local Mains practice - 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** "India's internal map became durable because it remained revisable." Evaluate with reference to linguistic reorganisation and regionalism up to 1967.

**Model answer:**
India inherited provinces shaped by conquest rather than linguistic-democratic coherence. Congress had accepted linguistic organisation in 1920-21, but Partition, Kashmir, administrative disruption and scarcity made the leadership postpone a general settlement after 1947. Dhar and JVP expressed this caution, though JVP left room for overwhelming demands.

Andhra showed why revisability mattered. Potti Sriramulu's death in December 1952 forced Andhra State in 1953 and led to the SRC. The Commission accepted language within a wider test of unity, administration, viability, development and minority interests. The States Reorganisation Act and Seventh Amendment then produced fourteen states and six Union Territories on 1 November 1956.

Article 3 kept later revision constitutionally bounded: a Bill required presidential recommendation and reference to the affected legislature for views, while Parliament retained final law-making authority.

That map was not final. Bombay's rival movements and electoral pressure produced Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960. Punjab's language-script-religion conflict produced Punjab, Haryana and Chandigarh in 1966. Nagaland followed a distinct asymmetric route.

Revisability broadened participation and converted recognition demands into federal bargaining. DMK's movement from secession toward autonomy and its 1967 electoral victory illustrates absorption through democracy.

Limits persisted: minorities, Belagavi, Chandigarh, water disputes, inequality, communalisation and sons-of-soil nativism. Durability therefore came not from perfect boundaries but from institutions able to renegotiate them without surrendering Union integrity or equal citizenship.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates the complete causal arc while preserving the crucial asymmetry between revisable internal arrangements and the non-negotiable framework of Union integrity and equal citizenship.

## Original 10-mark question - 150 words

**Question:** Why did the creation of Andhra State in 1953 make an all-India reorganisation inquiry unavoidable?

**Model answer:**
Andhra State transformed linguistic reorganisation from a postponed principle into an immediate federal problem. The SRC, appointed in August 1953, answered that need. Andhra thus supplied the political trigger; the Commission converted its precedent into a general, multi-criterion process.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains why Andhra altered the national decision environment: one concession generated comparison pressures that case-by-case postponement could no longer contain.

## Original 15-mark question - 250 words

**Question:** The States Reorganisation Act and the Seventh Amendment converted identity politics into institutional federalism. Discuss.

**Model answer:**
Popular linguistic movements created pressure for boundaries that reflected languages used in politics, education and administration. Yet a new map also required continuity of legislatures, courts, services, representation, assets and inter-state coordination.

The States Reorganisation Act, Act 37 of 1956, received assent on 31 August and operated from 1 November. It altered territories and managed consequential arrangements. Kerala and enlarged Mysore emerged; former Hyderabad was divided among Andhra Pradesh, Bombay and Mysore; Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Punjab were reconstituted; and fourteen states and six Union Territories formed the new structure.

The Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act performed a distinct task. It revised Article 1 and the First Schedule, replaced the old Part A, B and C classification and related territorial scheme with states and Union territories, and adapted constitutional provisions concerning representation, High Courts and services.

Together, the measures transformed claims expressed through movements into general rules and functioning governments. They also preserved Union authority: language communities received states, not sovereignty.

The conversion was incomplete. Bombay and Punjab required later divisions; minorities, borders, capitals and river waters remained contested. Nevertheless, the combination of territorial statute and constitutional amendment made recognition compatible with administrative continuity and a strong federal Union.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats the Act and Amendment as complementary parts of one institutional conversion and evaluates success through both governmental continuity and unresolved territorial limits.

**Scoring note:** Rubric (15 = 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 3): distinct Act and Amendment functions (4); exact dates and 14/6 structure (3); four accurate map examples (3); continuity of courts/services/representation (2); states-not-sovereignty thesis with two residual limits (3).

## Original 20-mark question - 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate linguistic reorganisation as a model of democratic conflict management in India up to 1967.

**Model answer:**
Linguistic reorganisation began with a democratic contradiction: Congress had mobilised in mother tongues and accepted linguistic organisation in 1920-21, but the post-Partition state postponed the promise for security, administrative and economic reasons. The model succeeded not by ending conflict but by making borders and language policy negotiable while keeping Union integrity and equal citizenship non-negotiable.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core thesis

Linguistic reorganisation was India's method of giving regional cultures **states without giving them sovereignty**. It arose from popular pressure but became durable because commissions, Parliament, constitutional amendment, elections and safeguards converted demands into federal institutions.

## B. Antecedents

- British conquest produced multilingual and culturally incoherent provinces.
- The 1905 Bengal partition warns that imposed boundary-making can serve division.
- Orissa's 1936 creation is the clearest pre-independence linguistic precedent.
- Sind's 1936 separation was a broader regional-political precedent, not a pure language model.
- Congress used mother tongues for mass mobilisation after 1919.
- Its linguistic provincial reorganisation belongs to the 1920-21 Nagpur process.
- Administrative logic: education, courts, politics and government become more intelligible.
- Democratic logic: regional identity gains voice within an all-India movement.

## C. Post-1947 caution

- Partition, refugees, Kashmir, law and order, economic scarcity and divided administration shaped policy.
- On 27 November 1947, Nehru said: "First things must come first and the first thing is the security and stability of India."
- Dhar Commission, 1948, under Justice S.K. Dhar advised against immediate reorganisation.
- JVP: Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya.
- JVP advised delay but allowed a route for insistent, overwhelming and broadly acceptable demands.
- The conflict was principle versus timing, not language versus nation.

## D. Andhra

- Telugu demand was long-standing and broadly supported.
- Madras city blocked agreement.
- Potti Sriramulu began his fast on 19 October 1952.
- He died after 58 days on 15 December 1952.
- Disorder and mass pressure followed.
- Andhra State Act, 1953 created Andhra State from 1 October 1953.
- It was the **first post-independence state created on a linguistic basis**.
- Andhra Pradesh arose only on 1 November 1956 after Telangana's merger.
- Andhra came before the SRC report and triggered a nationwide process.

## E. SRC and criteria

- Appointed August 1953.
- Justice Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru.
- Worked amid representations, demonstrations and rival claims.
- Reported in 1955.
- Criteria: unity/security, linguistic-cultural homogeneity, administration, financial/economic viability, balanced development and minority interests.
- Broadly accepted language but rejected automatic one-language-one-state logic.
- Opposed immediate division of Bombay and Punjab.
- Government accepted recommendations with modifications.

## F. The 1956 legal architecture

- States Reorganisation Act: Act 37 of 1956.
- Assent: 31 August 1956.
- Appointed day: 1 November 1956.
- Territorial and consequential work: legislatures, representation, High Courts, services, assets/liabilities and zonal councils.
- Seventh Amendment: constitutional restructuring.
- It revised Article 1 and the First Schedule.
- It replaced the old Part A/B/C and related territorial categories with states and Union territories.
- Never say the reorganisation statute alone abolished the categories.
- Outcome: 14 states and 6 Union Territories.
- Article 3 process: presidential recommendation, reference to the affected legislature for views, then parliamentary law; the state legislature has no veto.

## G. Main 1956 outcomes

| Region | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Kerala | Travancore-Cochin, minus southern Tamil taluks, plus Malabar and Kasaragod |
| Mysore | Kannada areas from Bombay, Hyderabad and Madras plus Coorg; renamed Karnataka in 1973 |
| Madhya Pradesh | old MP minus Vidarbha plus Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh and Bhopal |
| Rajasthan | absorbed Ajmer with related adjustments |
| Punjab | absorbed PEPSU |
| Hyderabad | Telangana to Andhra Pradesh; Marathwada to Bombay; Kannada districts to Mysore |
| Bombay | enlarged bilingual state including Saurashtra, Kutch, Marathwada and Vidarbha |
| Madras | lost Malabar and other non-Tamil areas; received southern Tamil taluks from Travancore-Cochin |

The map was a negotiated approximation, not a homogeneous linguistic mosaic.

## H. Bombay, 1956-60

- SRC resisted immediate division.
- Bombay city was multilingual, economically vital and symbolically contested.
- A separate centrally administered city was proposed, then bilingual Bombay continued.
- Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti demanded Maharashtra with Bombay.
- Maha Gujarat Janata Parishad demanded Gujarat.
- C.D. Deshmukh resigned.
- 1957 elections exposed Congress weakness.
- Bombay Reorganisation Act divided the state from 1 May 1960.
- Bombay city went to Maharashtra; Ahmedabad was Gujarat's initial capital.

## I. Punjab, 1956-66

- PEPSU merged into Punjab in 1956.
- Punjabi, Hindi and Pahari claims coexisted.
- Gurmukhi/Devanagari and Sikh/Hindu mobilisation complicated language reporting.
- Akali Dal led Punjabi Suba; Jan Sangh opposed it.
- Not every supporter had a communal motive.
- Nehru refused a demand he believed had become religiously territorial.
- Punjab Reorganisation Act operated from 1 November 1966.
- Result: Punjabi-speaking Punjab, Haryana, Chandigarh UT and joint capital.
- Hill areas went to then-UT Himachal Pradesh; statehood came in 1971.
- Chandigarh, boundary, waters, assets and institutional disputes persisted.

## J. Nagaland contrast

- Not ordinary linguistic state-making.
- 16-Point Agreement provided the negotiated context.
- State of Nagaland Act, 1962.
- Statehood: 1 December 1963.
- Article 371A protects specified Naga practices, customary law/justice and land-resource arrangements.
- It represents asymmetric ethnic-security accommodation within the Union.
- Full tribal and insurgency history belongs to Topic 31.

## K. Regionalism typology

- **Regional identity:** pride compatible with Indian identity.
- **Constitutional regional demand:** statehood, autonomy or devolution within India.
- **Sub-regionalism:** a region's claim against internal state dominance.
- **Regional inequality politics:** demand for fair development and resources.
- Colonial concentration can produce a low-revenue, weak-capacity and low-investment cycle.
- Finance Commission and plan transfers, public-sector location and incentives to private investment are distinct corrective channels.
- Telangana, Vidarbha, Saurashtra, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand-Bundelkhand, Gorkhaland, Bodoland and Jharkhand express different sub-regional logics.
- **Nativism:** sons-of-soil exclusion of migrants.
- Nativism is conditional: scarce desired jobs, visible migrant competition and political organisation can activate it.
- **Communalised regionalism:** territory and language coded through religion.
- **Secessionism:** demand to leave the Union.
- Judge by objective, method, target and treatment of other citizens.

## L. Parties and politics to 1967

- DMK inherited the Justice Party-led non-Brahmin and Periyar's Self-Respect currents.
- Periyar and Annadurai established DK in 1944; Annadurai founded DMK after the 1949 split.
- Drama, cinema, journals, pamphlets and oratory helped build its mass base.
- It initially supported Dravidanadu and anti-North/anti-Hindi claims.
- It broadened toward Tamil cultural and social-development politics.
- It helped nearly thirty Assembly candidates in 1952 and won 50 Assembly and seven Lok Sabha seats in 1962.
- It suspended secessionist propaganda during the 1962 war.
- It abandoned secession and shifted to autonomy.
- Anti-Hindi mobilisation strengthened it.
- In 1967 it won 138 of 234 Assembly seats; Annadurai formed government.
- Akali politics mixed language, Sikh institutions and federal claims.
- Shiv Sena was founded in 1966 with sons-of-soil nativism.
- Detailed later regional-party politics belongs to Topic 33 and later topics.

## M. Minorities and limits

- A predominant language never creates exclusive state ownership.
- Article 29: conserve language, script and culture.
- Article 30: minority educational institutions.
- Article 347: specified recognition on a substantial-section demand.
- Article 350A: mother-tongue primary instruction.
- Article 350B: Special Officer; Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities.
- Urdu illustrates a dispersed linguistic minority without a territorial-majority state.
- In Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, its central problems concerned recognition, education, teachers and administrative access.
- The Uttar Pradesh dispute invoked an SRC-associated 30 per cent threshold for second-official or regional-language status.
- Nehru pressed for district-level recognition where Urdu was widely used and minority-language facilities elsewhere.
- Union monitoring and reporting depend on state and local implementation.
- Communalisation can distort a linguistic-rights claim into a loyalty test.
- Andhra's 1968 recognition and Uttar Pradesh's 1979-80 schooling data are later evidence outside the 1947-67 core chronology.
- Belagavi/Belgaum: boundary dispute.
- Chandigarh: capital and territorial dispute.
- Rivers, assets and public investment create distributive conflict.
- Regional inequality and internal dominance survive boundary change.

## N. Official language

- Boundary reorganisation differs from Union official-language policy.
- Article 343 names Hindi in Devanagari and provided a fifteen-year transition with English.
- Official Languages Act, 1963 continued English for specified Union purposes.
- 1965 anti-Hindi mobilisation reflected fears of opportunity and cultural domination.
- 1967 settlement retained English alongside Hindi for Union purposes.
- A separate parliamentary resolution provided public-service examinations in Hindi, English and regional languages.
- Candidates were additionally expected to know Hindi or English.
- The recruitment settlement was related to, but not enacted by, the Official Languages Act.
- Civil-services recruitment and Union communication made language a question of material access.
- The three-language formula was an educational compromise, implemented unevenly across states.
- Its broad elements were the regional language, Hindi or another modern Indian language, and English.
- "Associate official language" is political shorthand, not formal constitutional terminology.
- Detailed doctrine belongs to Polity.

## O. Final evaluative balance

### Achievements

1. administrative and political accessibility;
2. cultural recognition;
3. wider mass participation;
4. regional stakes in the federation;
5. electoral absorption of movements;
6. preservation of a strong Union through flexible internal boundaries.

### Limits

1. minorities within states;
2. disputed borders and capitals;
3. resource and water conflicts;
4. regional inequality;
5. nativist exclusion;
6. communalisation;
7. violence as leverage;
8. incomplete asymmetric settlements.

> **Final answer line:** Linguistic reorganisation succeeded because India treated its internal map as constitutionally revisable, regional identity as legitimate, secession as unacceptable and equal citizenship as the limit on majority-language power.
