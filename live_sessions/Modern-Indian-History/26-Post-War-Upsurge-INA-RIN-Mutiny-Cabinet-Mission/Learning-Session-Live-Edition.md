### Visual: the strategic fork

```text
Conflict within Congress after Tripuri, 1939
                  │
          Forward Bloc formed
                  │
      house arrest and surveillance
                  │
escape from Calcutta, 16–17 January 1941
                  │
        Kabul → Berlin → Axis diplomacy
                  │
        Indian Legion in Europe
                  │
 transfer by German and Japanese submarines
                  │
       Singapore, 2 July 1943
```

*The sequence shows a change of theatre and method: Bose moved from internal party mobilisation to seeking an armed force abroad.*

### Start with the intuition

Bose’s premise was simple but dangerous: a world war had weakened Britain, so Indian nationalists should use the enemy’s crisis rather than wait for it to end. This differed from Gandhi’s insistence that freedom could not safely be built through dependence on another imperial power.

✅ **Fact:** After the Congress presidency conflict associated with Tripuri in 1939, Bose founded the **Forward Bloc**. He escaped from Calcutta in disguise on the night of **16–17 January 1941**, travelled through Kabul, reached Germany and pursued diplomatic and military support.

✅ **Fact:** In Europe, Bose was associated with the **Indian Legion**, recruited substantially from Indian prisoners of war held by Germany.

⚠️ **Inference:** The Indian Legion and later INA were linked by Bose’s strategic idea—turn Indian military manpower captured by Britain’s enemies into an anti-colonial force—but they were not the same organisation or theatre.

### Mechanism: why external aid looked attractive

1. Britain’s war dispersed its resources.
2. Indian troops were fighting across imperial theatres.
3. Captured troops created a possible recruitment pool.
4. Radio and diplomatic recognition could project an alternative centre of Indian authority.
5. Military pressure from outside might coincide with revolt inside India.

The weakness lay in step five. It required Axis interests to align with Indian sovereignty and required military entry into India to generate, rather than repel, popular support.

### Example—and its limit

The Indian Legion gave Bose an organised armed presence and propaganda platform in Europe. Yet geography kept it far from an Indian campaign. It demonstrated intent and organisation, not a realistic European route to Delhi.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Cooperation with Axis powers discredited the freedom claim because Germany and Japan were themselves authoritarian or imperial.

**Reply:** The objection is morally and historically serious. Bose treated the alliance instrumentally: Britain’s enemy could supply the military opening Britain would never voluntarily grant. But instrumental intention did not control the ally’s conduct. Japanese aims and treatment of Indian personnel remained independent constraints.

**Balanced verdict:** Explain Bose’s strategic logic without converting explanation into endorsement. His choice was anti-British and nationalist in purpose, externally dependent in means, and exposed to the ambitions of states whose goals were not identical to India’s freedom.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Forward Bloc, escape, Germany, Indian Legion and Singapore must remain in sequence.
- **Mains:** Contrast an internal mass strategy with an external military strategy.
- **Trap:** Do not call the Indian Legion the first INA.
- **Boundary:** Detailed Quit India strategy belongs to Topic 25.

### Revision notes

1. Bose’s external strategy followed his conflict with the Congress high command.
2. Forward Bloc belongs to 1939.
3. He escaped from Calcutta on 16–17 January 1941.
4. His route included Kabul and Berlin.
5. The Indian Legion was organised in the European theatre.
6. External military pressure was intended to converge with Indian anti-colonial action.
7. Axis aid created capacity but also dependence.
8. Japanese or German support did not make their interests identical to Indian nationalism.
9. Strategic explanation must not erase the moral problem of alliance.
10. Bose reached Singapore only in July 1943, after the first INA crisis.

### Lesson-local practice

**A.** Both redirected overseas Indian manpower against British rule.
**B.** Both were raised by the Indian Independence League after the fall of Singapore.
**C.** Both operated under one continuous command headquartered at Rangoon.
**D.** Both relied chiefly on Congress ministries for recruitment and supplies.

Which assessment best balances the opportunity and danger in Bose’s Axis diplomacy?

**A.** It subordinated the freedom struggle to the political programme accepted by Congress.
**B.** Britain’s wartime weakness created opportunity, but reliance on imperial allies endangered Indian autonomy.
**C.** It separated military recruitment from all diplomatic dependence on Germany and Japan.
**D.** It ensured equal control of strategy, supplies and occupied territory between Bose and his allies.

#### Answers and option explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** The shared strategy was to redirect Indian personnel located in enemy-controlled theatres against the Raj.
- **B — Incorrect:** The Indian Legion was organised in Europe before the IIL-sponsored first INA emerged in Southeast Asia.
- **C — Incorrect:** The formations belonged to different theatres and did not share an uninterrupted Rangoon command.
- **D — Incorrect:** Neither overseas force derived its principal manpower or logistics from Congress provincial governments.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Congress did not adopt Bose’s external alliance as its authorised wartime programme.
- **B — Correct:** Bose gained resources from Britain’s enemies, yet could not make their war aims identical to Indian sovereignty.
- **C — Incorrect:** Recruitment, transport and propaganda were possible precisely because foreign-state support was available.
- **D — Incorrect:** Later command and provisioning disputes show that formal cooperation did not create strategic equality.

#### Local Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain the strategic logic and the principal limitation of Subhas Chandra Bose’s external military route to freedom.

**Model answer:**
Bose believed that Britain’s existential war created an exceptional opening for Indian independence. After forming the Forward Bloc and escaping from Calcutta in January 1941, he sought diplomatic and military assistance abroad, first in Germany and later in Southeast Asia. The Indian Legion and the reorganised INA expressed one strategic idea: Indian prisoners and expatriates could be converted into an armed force that would enter India while anti-colonial sentiment weakened British authority from within.

The strategy gave Bose an army, radio reach and an alternative governmental claim. Its principal limitation was dependence on Axis powers. Germany and Japan pursued their own war aims; Indian sovereignty could not command their strategy, supplies or treatment of troops. Geography restricted the European effort, while the Southeast Asian campaign remained tied to Japanese operations.

Thus Bose identified Britain’s vulnerability with clarity, but the means chosen created a gap between nationalist purpose and allied power.

**Scoring note (10 marks):** strategic sequence 3 + external-route mechanism 3 + dependence/moral limit 3 + qualified verdict 1 = 10/10.

## Lesson 2 — The first INA: organisation, autonomy and breakdown

Progress: 2/13 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Rash Behari Bose, Indian Independence League and Mohan Singh

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — permitted OCR book, especially the first-INA account
🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; evidence is controlled by the wartime record
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: three actors, three functions

```text
Japanese victories in Southeast Asia
                 │
      Indian prisoners of war
                 │
       Mohan Singh: military core
                 │
Indian Independence League: political umbrella
                 │
 Rash Behari Bose: senior nationalist coordination
                 │
first INA division formed, 1 September 1942
                 │
dispute over size, command and autonomy
                 │
Mohan Singh and Niranjan Singh Gill arrested, December 1942
```

*The first INA was not simply “created by Bose”: it preceded Subhas Bose and depended on a tense relationship among Indian nationalists, prisoners and Japanese command.*

### Plain-language model

Imagine an army whose weapons, transport and theatre are controlled by an ally. Is it an allied national army or an auxiliary unit? That question broke the first INA.

✅ **Fact:** The **Indian Independence League (IIL)** supplied a political framework among Indians in Southeast Asia, with **Rash Behari Bose** playing the senior coordinating role.

✅ **Fact:** **Mohan Singh**, drawing upon Indian prisoners captured after Japanese victories, led the military formation. The first INA division was formed on **1 September 1942** and grew into a substantial force.

✅ **Fact:** By December 1942 serious differences had emerged. Japanese authorities preferred a limited force subordinate to their operational purposes, while Mohan Singh wanted a much larger army with an autonomous national role. **Mohan Singh and Niranjan Singh Gill** were arrested.

### Why organisation was not enough

| Requirement of a national army | First-INA difficulty |
|---|---|
| Political authority | IIL supplied a claim, but its leverage over Japan was limited |
| Independent command | Japanese command controlled the wider theatre |
| Voluntary legitimacy | Recruitment from POWs raised questions of consent and conditions |
| Strategic equality | Japan viewed the force more narrowly than Mohan Singh did |
| Sustainable supplies | Arms, food and transport depended on Japan |

⚠️ **Inference:** The collapse was not evidence that Indian troops lacked nationalist sentiment. It exposed a structural contradiction: an army claiming national sovereignty could not easily remain subordinate to another power’s operational priorities.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Japan enabled the force, disagreement over command was merely personal.

**Reply:** The disagreement over scale reveals a constitutional issue in military form. A token auxiliary could advertise cooperation; a substantial army could claim an independent political mission. Mohan Singh’s resistance concerned the character of the force, not only his position.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Rash Behari Bose/IIL, Mohan Singh/first INA, Subhas Bose/reorganisation.
- **Mains:** Use “autonomy versus auxiliary status” as the causal key.
- **Trap:** The first INA did not begin under Subhas Bose.
- **Evidence discipline:** Use qualitative scale because published force totals depend on counting conventions.

### Revision notes

1. Japanese victories created a pool of Indian POWs.
2. The IIL supplied a political umbrella.
3. Rash Behari Bose was the senior coordinating nationalist.
4. Mohan Singh led the military formation.
5. First INA division: 1 September 1942.
6. The first division developed into a substantial force, but no precise strength is used here.
7. Japanese and Indian expectations diverged over role and scale.
8. Mohan Singh sought an autonomous national army, not a token formation.
9. Mohan Singh and Niranjan Singh Gill were arrested by December 1942.
10. The first phase had broken down before Subhas Bose arrived.

**D.** Mohan Singh’s demand for a substantial autonomous national army conflicted with Japan’s preference for a limited subordinate force.

What distinguishes the first INA from the reorganised phase?

**A.** Bose added reorganised military and claimant-government leadership.
**B.** Mohan Singh replaced the IIL with a constituent assembly elected inside British India.
**C.** Rash Behari Bose transferred the force from Japanese support to British logistical control.
**D.** The later organisation confined recruitment to POWs and discontinued expatriate civilian participation.

#### Answers and option explanations

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Rash Behari Bose coordinated Indian nationalist organisation; he did not command Japan’s Burma forces.
- **B — Incorrect:** Mohan Singh led the INA’s military organisation rather than serving as the League’s overarching political president.
- **C — Correct:** The IIL supplied the political umbrella within which POW recruitment and the first INA took shape.
- **D — Incorrect:** Gill was an INA officer involved in the 1942 crisis, not head of Bose’s later claimant government.

**MCQ 4: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** The conflict concerned dependence on Japan, not a proposal to re-enter British military command.
- **B — Incorrect:** The expatriate organisation was already mobilising and did not suspend its effort for elections.
- **C — Incorrect:** The disagreement involved scale, command and purpose, not settled autonomy followed by a dress dispute.
- **D — Correct:** Competing views of who would control and use the army produced the decisive institutional rupture.

**MCQ 5: A**

- **A — Correct:** Bose added a claimant governmental authority to a reorganised army and wider expatriate mobilisation.
- **B — Incorrect:** No elected constituent body in British India replaced the IIL during the wartime reorganisation.
- **C — Incorrect:** Japanese dependence continued; the INA was not supplied or directed by Britain.
- **D — Incorrect:** The second phase recruited civilians and raised expatriate funds as well as using former POWs.

#### Local Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Why did the first INA break down despite a favourable anti-British environment in Southeast Asia?

**Model answer:**
The first INA emerged after Japanese victories produced large numbers of Indian prisoners of war. Mohan Singh supplied its military leadership, while the Indian Independence League and Rash Behari Bose offered political coordination. Its first division was formed on 1 September 1942 and developed into a substantial force. Quit India also intensified the hope that external military action might connect with resistance inside India.

Yet the enabling environment contained the cause of failure. Arms, logistics and theatre command remained Japanese. Mohan Singh envisaged a substantial Indian national army; Japanese authorities preferred a limited subordinate force. The disagreement therefore concerned autonomy, scale and purpose. Was the INA an allied force fighting for Indian sovereignty, or a subsidiary unit serving Japanese operations?

By December 1942 the dispute had become irreconcilable, and Mohan Singh and Niranjan Singh Gill were arrested. The breakdown did not prove the absence of nationalist motivation. It proved that external sponsorship could create an army but could not guarantee equality of command.

The first INA thus established personnel and political possibility, while its collapse demonstrated the central danger Bose would inherit: dependence on Japan could never be fully reconciled with the claim to sovereign Indian action.

**Scoring note (15 marks):** differentiated actors 3 + formation/breakdown chronology 3 + autonomy-versus-auxiliary mechanism 5 + Japanese dependence 2 + qualified verdict 2 = 15/15.

## Lesson 3 — Bose in Southeast Asia: army, government and mobilisation

Progress: 3/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Reorganisation, Provisional Government and symbols of sovereignty

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — permitted OCR account of Bose’s arrival and reorganisation
🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; commemoration is not needed to establish the historical mechanism
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from force to claimant state

```text
Bose reaches Singapore, 2 July 1943
                  │
reorganises INA + recruits civilians + raises funds
                  │
Rangoon and Singapore headquarters
                  │
Rani of Jhansi Regiment
                  │
Provisional Government of Free India, 21 October 1943
                  │
war declaration + foreign recognition + “Dilli Chalo”
```

*Bose added political architecture to military manpower: the INA would act in the name of a government, not merely as an expatriate unit.*

### The key transformation

✅ **Fact:** Bose reached **Singapore on 2 July 1943** through a submarine journey involving German and Japanese vessels.

✅ **Fact:** He reorganised the INA, established headquarters at **Singapore and Rangoon**, recruited civilians and raised funds.

✅ **Fact:** The **Rani of Jhansi Regiment**, associated with **Lakshmi Sahgal**, gave women a highly visible military place within the movement.

✅ **Fact:** On **21 October 1943**, Bose established the **Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind)** at Singapore. It declared war on Britain and the United States and received recognition from Axis powers and their associates.

### Why create a government before possessing India?

The government performed four political tasks:

1. **Authority:** It claimed that the army fought for an Indian sovereign, not Japan.
2. **Representation:** It offered expatriates an institution to fund and serve.
3. **Diplomacy:** Recognition converted the struggle into an international claim.
4. **Discipline:** A cabinet and command structure linked mobilisation to a stated political end.

⚠️ **Inference:** Recognition gave external visibility, not democratic domestic authorization. Its significance lay in symbolic sovereignty and mobilisation, not control over India.

### Symbols and their work

- **“Dilli Chalo”** turned a distant army’s objective into a geographical command.
- **“Give me blood and I will give you freedom”** framed sacrifice as exchange for sovereignty.
- **“Jai Hind”** operated as an inclusive national salutation.
- Renaming the Andaman and Nicobar Islands **Shaheed** and **Swaraj** asserted symbolic possession, although effective control remained constrained by Japan.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A government dependent on Axis recognition and without effective control over India was merely theatrical.

**Reply:** It lacked territorial and democratic foundations, so it was not a state equivalent to a government governing India. Yet “theatrical” understates its political work. It supplied an authority in whose name soldiers served, broadened fundraising and recruitment, and later made British prosecution appear to many Indians as a trial of a rival national allegiance.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** 2 July 1943 and 21 October 1943 must not be merged.
- **Mains:** Distinguish symbolic sovereignty from effective territorial sovereignty.
- **Trap:** Foreign recognition did not establish full operational independence from Japan.
- **Gender angle:** Use the Rani of Jhansi Regiment as evidence of mobilisation, without treating one regiment as equality across the movement.

### Revision notes

1. Bose reached Singapore on 2 July 1943.
2. This began the second INA phase.
3. Headquarters operated at Singapore and Rangoon.
4. Civilians as well as former POWs were mobilised.
5. Funds were raised among expatriate Indians.
6. Lakshmi Sahgal is associated with the Rani of Jhansi Regiment.
7. Provisional Government date: 21 October 1943.
8. Its functions were authority, diplomacy, recruitment and morale.
9. Axis recognition did not equal control over Indian territory.
10. Symbols later magnified the political meaning of the trials.

#### Local Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why was the Provisional Government of Azad Hind politically significant despite its limited territorial control?

**Model answer:**
The Provisional Government of Azad Hind, proclaimed by Subhas Bose at Singapore on 21 October 1943, made the INA the military arm of a claimant Indian authority. It declared war, sought recognition, mobilised expatriate funds and recruits, and presented INA service as allegiance to India rather than desertion from Britain.

Its symbols—“Dilli Chalo,” “Jai Hind,” the Rani of Jhansi Regiment and the renaming of the Andamans and Nicobars—gave distant military action a national vocabulary. This later mattered during the Red Fort trials, when prosecution for treason collided with the public belief that the accused had served an Indian cause.

The limit is essential. Axis recognition supplied neither democratic authorisation inside India nor independent control over Japanese strategy and occupied territory. Its significance was mobilisational and symbolic, not equivalent to effective sovereignty.

## Lesson 4 — Imphal–Kohima: military campaign, retreat and measured significance

### Visual: the campaign’s causal chain

```text
Japanese advance from Burma
          │
INA battalion accompanies Japanese army
          │
frontier crossed toward Imphal–Kohima, 1944
          │
long supply lines + monsoon + Allied resistance
          │
discriminatory provisioning and weak operational autonomy
          │
campaign fails → Japanese retreat
          │
INA retreat, mid-1944 to mid-1945
```

*The INA’s political ambition was larger than the military capacity available to it.*

### What happened?

✅ **Fact:** An INA battalion commanded by **Shah Nawaz Khan** accompanied Japanese forces on the Indo-Burma front and participated in the Imphal campaign.

✅ **Fact:** The combined offensive failed around **Imphal and Kohima**. The consulted book records demoralising treatment of INA personnel, including inferior rations, inadequate arms and menial assignments.

✅ **Fact:** The retreat began in mid-1944, continued into mid-1945 and ended with surrender in Southeast Asia.

### Why did the campaign fail?

| Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Dependence on Japanese planning | INA could not determine the larger campaign |
| Extended supply lines | Food, ammunition and medical support deteriorated |
| Difficult terrain and monsoon | Movement and resupply became punishing |
| Allied recovery and resistance | Initial Japanese momentum was reversed |
| Unequal treatment | Morale and the claim of allied equality weakened |

⚠️ **Inference:** Military failure followed from the combined limits of the Japanese offensive and the INA’s subordinate material position. It should not be explained as either pure cowardice or inevitable nationalist triumph.

### Evidence-qualified significance

The campaign mattered in three different registers:

1. **Military:** it failed to liberate Indian territory or trigger a sustained internal uprising.
2. **Symbolic:** crossing toward India and suffering retreat supplied a narrative of sacrifice.
3. **Political afterlife:** captured personnel became the subjects of trials that generated far more domestic mobilisation than the campaign itself.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the campaign failed, the INA was historically insignificant.

**Reply:** This confuses battlefield outcome with political afterlife. The military verdict was defeat. The later political verdict arose because Indians debated who possessed the right to judge those who had fought under the Azad Hind claim.

**Reverse caution:** Political impact must not be projected backward into a claim that the INA won the campaign or alone secured independence.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Place Imphal–Kohima after the October 1943 government.
- **Mains:** Use the three-register method—military, symbolic, political.
- **Trap:** Avoid unsupported casualty or army-strength figures.
- **Evidence:** State only that Bose was officially reported to have died in an August 1945 air crash; do not overclaim disputed circumstances.

### Revision notes

1. INA action depended on the Japanese Burma campaign.
2. Shah Nawaz Khan commanded an INA battalion on the front.
3. Imphal and Kohima belong to 1944.
4. Supply, terrain, monsoon and Allied resistance constrained the offensive.
5. The book records discriminatory Japanese treatment.
6. Retreat continued from mid-1944 into mid-1945.
7. Military liberation did not occur.
8. Symbolic sacrifice survived battlefield defeat.
9. Captivity created the later trial issue.
10. Bose’s death should be described only as officially reported in August 1945.

### Visual: the prosecution paradox


*The state tried individuals for disloyalty; the campaign put colonial authority itself on trial.*

### The accused and the defence

✅ **Fact:** The first famous Red Fort trial joined **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon**.

✅ **Fact:** The defence included **Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, K.N. Katju, Jawaharlal Nehru and Asaf Ali**.

✅ **Fact:** Congress organised an **INA Relief and Enquiry Committee**, authorised fundraising channels and assisted released personnel, though employment efforts had limited success.

### Why the combination mattered

The accused were publicly read as a Muslim, Hindu and Sikh serving one army. This did not abolish communal politics; it produced a powerful, issue-specific national symbol at a moment of increasing communal polarisation.

The campaign spread through:

- newspapers and pamphlets such as the widely circulated “Patriots Not Traitors”;
- INA Day and INA Week observances;
- large meetings and election platforms;
- student boycotts and rallies;
- market closures and donations;
- municipal, gurdwara, women’s and kisan bodies;
- support in varying degrees from Congress, League, Communists, Akalis, Hindu Mahasabha and other organisations.

✅ **Fact:** The consulted account stresses the reach from major cities to distant areas and “remotest villages.”

### Groundswell versus confrontation

The broad INA **groundswell** consisted of meetings, money, publicity, sympathy and electoral mobilisation. It should be separated from the brief urban **confrontations** that followed in Calcutta and Bombay. Conflating them exaggerates both violence and revolutionary potential.

### Effect on the state apparatus

✅ **Fact:** Some government employees and service personnel attended meetings or contributed funds. The Commander-in-Chief reported a growing feeling of sympathy and concluded that general Army opinion favoured leniency.

⚠️ **Inference:** The key effect was not that the armed forces had universally rebelled, but that exemplary punishment could no longer be assumed to command the loyalty or acceptance on which discipline rested.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Congress merely adopted a popular issue for electoral advantage.

**Reply:** Elections amplified the issue, but the permitted book records early advocacy, an AICC resolution, legal defence, relief work and fundraising. Electoral use and substantive mobilisation can coexist.

**Limit:** Cross-party support for INA prisoners did not mean agreement on Pakistan, constitutional design or future power.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Learn the three accused and the defence team without mixing organisation offices.
- **Mains:** Explain the prosecution paradox and social transmission mechanism.
- **Trap:** Do not call all INA mobilisation violent.
- **Analytical line:** “The INA lost its campaign but colonial prosecution lost control of its meaning.”

### Visual: one climate, several streams

```text
war inflation + scarcity + famine memory + demobilisation
                         │
release of leaders + expectation of rapid freedom
                         │
      ┌──────────────────┼───────────────────┐
      ▼                  ▼                   ▼

      └──────────────────┴───────────────────┘

             but not one central command
```

*The post-war upsurge was a shared political climate expressed through different grievances and organisations.*

### Why did peace produce unrest?

War’s end removed one explanation for postponing political change, but it did not remove inflation, scarcity, famine memories or workplace hardship. Released leaders encountered crowds expecting rapid advance. Elections encouraged mobilisation rather than quieting it.

✅ **Fact:** The consulted book identifies a country-wide strike wave and major peasant or popular struggles including **Tebhaga**, the **Warli revolt**, **Punjab kisan morchas**, **Punnapra–Vayalar** in Travancore and **Telangana**.

✅ **Fact:** These struggles possessed an anti-imperialist edge because landlords, employers or princely authorities often formed part of the Raj’s social support, but many did not confront the colonial government as directly as the INA demonstrations and RIN revolt.

### Three analytical layers

| Layer | Typical demand | Relation to colonial rule |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Wages, food, tenancy, crop shares | War economy and protected interests |
| Political | Release, civil liberties, responsible government | Direct challenge to legitimacy |
| Social | Dignity, racial equality, end of abuse | Colonial hierarchy experienced in daily life |

⚠️ **Inference:** The value of grouping these movements is not to pretend they shared one programme. It is to see how a broad legitimacy crisis allowed economic and social grievances to acquire an anti-imperial language.

### Different actors, demands and mechanisms

| Stream | Principal actors | Immediate demand | Mechanism of action | Relationship to political authority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Post-war strike wave | Industrial workers and trade-union organisers across factories, railways, ports and other workplaces | Better wages, food, conditions and dignity after wartime inflation and discipline | Strikes, stoppages, picketing and city-wide solidarity | Employers were the immediate bargaining target, but wartime controls, policing and colonial labour power gave many disputes an anti-Raj edge |
| Tebhaga, Bengal | **Bargadars** or sharecroppers, with important participation by peasant women and kisan organisers | Cultivator to retain **two-thirds** of produce; harvested crop to be kept in the bargadar’s own **khamar** before division | Harvesting and storing grain under cultivator control, collective resistance to jotedar claims | A struggle inside directly ruled British India against agrarian intermediaries protected by the colonial legal and police order |
| Warli movement, western India | Warli Adivasi cultivators and labourers, aided by left organisers | Relief from forced labour, debt, low wages and landlord-creditor domination | Collective refusal, bargaining, mobilisation and resistance in village labour relations | The immediate oppressors were landlords and creditors within British India; colonial administration and policing sustained the wider hierarchy |
| Punjab kisan morchas | Kisan organisations drawing on Kirti Kisan, Congress, Akali and other rural networks | Tax reduction, debt relief and restraint of landlord power | Morchas, meetings, petitions and organised pressure | Directed against revenue and agrarian policy under a British Indian provincial ministry criticised for large-landlord dominance |
| Punnapra–Vayalar, Travancore | Coir and other workers, peasants and communist organisers | Labour protection and responsible government against autocratic rule | Strikes, mass mobilisation and armed resistance met by severe repression | A **princely-state** confrontation: the immediate authority was Travancore’s ruler and Dewan, sustained within the British paramountcy system rather than ordinary provincial government |
| Telangana, Hyderabad State | Peasants, agricultural labourers and communist-led village organisers | End illegal exactions, landlord coercion and **vetti** or forced labour; defend cultivation and village rights | Village organisation, refusal of levies, redistribution or protection of land, and an armed dimension | A **princely-state** struggle against local landed power and the coercive order of the Nizam’s Hyderabad; it continued beyond 1947, which lies outside this topic |

These are not six interchangeable examples. Tebhaga changed the crop-share and control of storage; Warli joined tribal labour, debt and social domination; Punjab morchas pressed policy and debt claims; Punnapra–Vayalar confronted a princely autocracy through a labour-popular rising; Telangana combined agrarian revolt with an armed challenge to Hyderabad’s landlord-state order. The strike wave was broader and industrial rather than a single peasant campaign.

**Example and limit:** Moving grain to a bargadar’s khamar in Tebhaga changed bargaining power because possession affected measurement and division. It did not by itself abolish zamindari or make Tebhaga identical to Telangana’s armed village struggle.

### The three direct urban upsurges

The permitted account distinguishes three confrontations:

1. **21 November 1945, Calcutta:** student procession connected to the INA trials.
2. **11 February 1946, Calcutta:** protest around the seven-year sentence of INA officer **Rashid Ali**.
3. **18 February 1946, Bombay:** RIN ratings began their strike.

Their recurrent pattern was:

```text
specific group defies authority
        → repression or arrest
        → city crowds and workers join
        → solidarity elsewhere
        → coercive suppression
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling all post-war unrest a national upsurge erases class-specific demands.

**Reply:** That risk is real. Tebhaga cannot be reduced to an INA solidarity protest, nor a wage strike to a constitutional demand. “Upsurge” is useful only as a description of synchronised political energy, not as proof of identical causes or leadership.

- **Trap:** Do not describe every peasant struggle as a direct attack on the colonial state.

✅ **Fact:** Resentment also surrounded the arrest of rating **B.C. Dutt** for writing “Quit India” on HMIS Talwar.

The strike therefore fused service conditions with national dignity. Bad food was not politically trivial when distributed through a racial hierarchy and accompanied by abuse.

### Spread on 19 February

✅ **Fact:** Ratings at **Castle Barracks and Fort Barracks** joined on 19 February.

✅ **Fact:** They acted partly upon an incorrect rumour that Talwar ratings had been fired upon. The factual correction matters: mobilisation can be historically significant even when one immediate report was false.

✅ **Fact:** The revolt spread widely across ships and shore establishments and involved many ratings. No precise fleet-wide total is used because published counting conventions differ.

Congress, Muslim League and Communist flags were flown together. National slogans and INA memory entered the protest.

⚠️ **Inference:** The display expressed an organisational and tactical unity against British authority. It did not prove that communal political conflict had disappeared. Muslim ratings later looked to the League, while others approached Congress and Socialist leaders.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — permitted book discussion of leadership responses and limits
🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; retrospective political claims are tested against contemporary evidence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: four responses to one crisis

```text
Ratings ask: continue or surrender?
          │
 ┌────────┼───────────┬──────────────┐
 ▼        ▼           ▼              ▼
Patel   Jinnah      Gandhi       Communists/workers
surrender  Muslim ratings  criticises       general strike,
to avoid   advised to      unprepared       street solidarity,
destruction surrender      mutiny            peace efforts too
```

*No major political response can be reduced to “support” or “betrayal”; each reflected a view of force, organisation and the coming transfer.*

### Patel’s advice

✅ **Fact:** Vallabhbhai Patel advised surrender after assessing the overwhelming naval and military force assembled in Bombay. He feared the ratings could be destroyed.

⚠️ **Inference:** Patel prioritised preservation of life, disciplined political strategy and the possibility of negotiation. That can be criticised as conservative, but it was not based on an imaginary absence of British coercive capacity.

### Jinnah’s intervention

✅ **Fact:** Jinnah addressed his advice to **Muslim ratings**, who heeded the call to surrender.

⚠️ **Inference:** This response demonstrates the limit of the joint flags. Organisational identities remained politically operative even during common action.

### Gandhi’s criticism

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi criticised the mutiny as unprepared action lacking a call from a prepared revolutionary organisation. He argued that “unity at the barricade” without constitutional unity was unstable.

His position followed a theory of political force:

1. Courage is not enough.
2. Means require disciplined organisation.
3. Armed action must answer the question of legitimate command.
4. A brief barricade cannot substitute for a durable political settlement.

### Workers and political activists

Communist mobilisation helped produce a general strike in Bombay. Workers, students, shopkeepers and transport employees paralysed much of the city. Individual Congressmen, Socialists and Communists participated, while organisations also tried at points to restore peace.

### Related service unrest—with bounds

✅ **Fact:** The book records sympathetic **RIAF strikes** in Bombay-area and other units, a **sepoy strike at Jabalpur**, and restlessness at Colaba.

⚠️ **Limit:** These episodes show wider disaffection; they do not establish that the army or air force as institutions had joined a national insurrection.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** National leaders surrendered a revolutionary opportunity to secure office.

**Reply:** The revolt was brave and politically electrifying, but short, urban and operationally exposed. The British deployed troops, naval ships and aircraft; Indian troops also participated in restoring control. No prepared national command linked sailors, army units, villages and political organisations. Negotiation and mass action were not mutually exclusive in Congress strategy.

**Residual criticism:** Leadership caution still disappointed militants and left unresolved the ratings’ fear of victimisation. A balanced answer can recognise both strategic realism and the moral cost of asking insurgents to surrender.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Patel, Jinnah and Gandhi took distinguishable positions.
- **Mains:** Present each response through its strategic logic, not caricature.
- **Trap:** Joint flags did not mean permanent communal unity.
- **Comparison:** Broad INA sympathy included conservative groups; the RIN confrontation narrowed participation to more militant urban sections.

### Revision notes

1. Patel advised surrender under overwhelming coercive pressure.
2. Jinnah’s appeal was directed to Muslim ratings.
3. Gandhi criticised action without prepared political command.
4. Workers gave major street support in Bombay.
5. Communists called a general strike.
6. Individual activists crossed organisational boundaries.
7. Political organisations also participated in peace efforts.
8. RIAF sympathy strikes indicate wider service disaffection.
9. Jabalpur sepoys struck, but this was not an army-wide rebellion.
10. British coercive capacity remained intact.
11. Strategic realism and abandonment are rival interpretations requiring evidence.
12. The strongest verdict recognises courage, political impact and operational isolation together.

#### Local Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Were the national leaders’ responses to the RIN revolt strategic realism or a retreat from popular militancy? Discuss.

**Model answer:**

Moreover, Congress strategy treated negotiation and a possible later mass movement as sequential tools, not mutually exclusive choices.

However, realism had a moral cost: ratings bore the risk of victimisation after surrender, and leadership caution narrowed their bargaining power. The best judgement is therefore dual. Leaders correctly recognised that an isolated revolt could be crushed, but their response also exposed the distance between spontaneous service militancy and party-controlled transfer politics.

## Lesson 9 — Significance without mythology: why Britain was leaving

Progress: 9/13 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Coercion, legitimacy, timing and multi-causal withdrawal

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — permitted OCR analysis of colonial capacity and transfer logic
🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; causal weighting rests on contemporary sequence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: capacity and willingness are different

```text
Could Britain repress one short revolt in February 1946?  YES
                         │
Could Britain govern India indefinitely through repeated repression,
with depleted officials and uncertain Indian collaboration?  NO
                         │
        ┌────────────────┼─────────────────┐
        ▼                ▼                 ▼
war exhaustion     legitimacy erosion   collaboration risk
        │                │                 │
        └────────────────┴─────────────────┘
                         ▼
negotiated withdrawal becomes safer than renewed mass confrontation
```

*The end of empire did not require the state to lose every battle; it required the expected cost and reliability of continued rule to become unacceptable.*

### The timing test

✅ **Fact:** The British Cabinet decided to send the Cabinet Mission on **22 January 1946**.

✅ **Fact:** The public announcement on **19 February 1946** had been scheduled before the RIN revolt began on 18 February.

Therefore the proposition “the RIN revolt caused the Cabinet Mission” fails elementary chronology.

### What INA and RIN did show

- INA sympathy penetrated civilian society, official circles and sections of the armed forces.
- RIN action demonstrated that racial discipline could turn service grievances into nationalist defiance.
- Related unrest raised doubts about future reliability if a larger mass movement occurred.
- Punishment and repression themselves risked deepening anti-British feeling.

⚠️ **Inference:** INA/RIN sharpened evidence of a pre-existing legitimacy crisis. They did not create British war exhaustion, decades of mass nationalism or the constitutional commitment to transfer.

### Why the coercive-capacity claim must be qualified

✅ **Fact:** Troops suppressed Calcutta confrontations and forced surrender in Bombay and Karachi. Wavell judged the Indian Army broadly steady.

Yet long-term rule required more than a temporary firing order:

| Resource of rule | Post-war problem |
|---|---|
| British officials | Depleted, war-weary and difficult to replace |
| Indian officials | Increasingly conscious of future Indian political masters |
| Armed forces | Discipline survived, but sympathy and unrest raised future risk |
| Loyalist prestige | INA prosecution and 1942 repression weakened faith in British justice |
| Political settlement | Congress demanded transfer; League demanded decisive safeguards |
| British economy | War had reduced the resources available for prolonged empire |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If British troops could suppress the revolt, INA/RIN had no bearing on withdrawal.

**Reply:** Immediate suppression and sustainable rule are different questions. A state may win a street confrontation yet judge that repeated confrontations cannot be managed with declining legitimacy, personnel and collaboration.

**Opposite objection:** Armed-force unrest alone frightened Britain into immediate exit.

**Reply:** The January decision, earlier constitutional planning and the later eighteen-month transfer process disprove a single-trigger account.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Cabinet decision—22 January; announcement—19 February.
- **Mains:** Rank causes rather than list them.
- **Trap:** Never write that RIN caused the Mission.
- **Best thesis:** “INA and RIN were indicators and accelerants within a cumulative crisis, not self-sufficient causes.”

### Revision notes

1. Cabinet decided on the Mission before the RIN revolt.
2. Announcement timing cannot prove causation.
3. British coercive power remained effective in February 1946.
4. Sustainable rule required reliable Indian collaboration.
5. INA sympathy widened the loyalty problem.
6. RIN action dramatized service disaffection.
7. War exhaustion reduced Britain’s administrative and financial capacity.
8. Decades of mass nationalism had eroded consent.
9. Elections and constitutional deadlock shaped the mode of withdrawal.
10. INA/RIN should receive significant but not exclusive causal weight.
11. Repression capacity and political viability must be distinguished.

**Model answer:**

## Lesson 10 — From the C.R. Formula to Simla: territory, timing and representation

Progress: 10/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: C.R. Formula, Gandhi–Jinnah talks, Wavell Plan and nomination deadlock

🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; this is a closed constitutional negotiation
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: two failed bridges before the electoral test

```text
April 1944: C.R. Formula
contiguous Muslim-majority districts → adult plebiscite after war
       │                    │
       │                    └→ partition effective only after full transfer
       └→ defence, communications and essential services by agreement
                              │

timing, sovereignty and who could bind whom
                              ▼

                              │

                              │

             League monopoly ↔ Congress national claim
                              ▼
Simla fails → elections must test representative strength
```

*The 1944 talks could not agree how Pakistan would be defined or implemented; Simla then failed over who possessed authority to represent Muslims inside a common executive.*

### Part A — C.R. Formula and Gandhi–Jinnah talks, 1944

Start with the problem. The Lahore Resolution had made a separate Muslim political future central, but “Pakistan” still lacked agreed boundaries, a binding decision procedure and a settled relationship with the rest of India. **C. Rajagopalachari** tried to turn the slogan into a sequence of decisions.

✅ **Fact:** In **April 1944**, the C.R. Formula proposed:

1. Muslim League support for the demand for Indian independence and cooperation in forming a provisional government.
2. After the war, a commission would demarcate **contiguous districts in the north-west and north-east where Muslims were in an absolute majority**.
3. The **adult population** of those demarcated areas would vote in a plebiscite on separation.
4. If separation was chosen, the resulting sovereign entities would make agreements on **defence, communications, commerce and other essential services**.
5. The arrangement would become operative **only after the full transfer of power by Britain**.

```text
British transfer first
        ↓
commission identifies contiguous absolute-majority districts
        ↓
adult plebiscite within those districts
        ↓
separation only if approved
        ↓
joint arrangements for essential services negotiated
```

The sequence mattered. It did not concede an immediately sovereign Pakistan in the League’s claimed six-province form. It offered a post-transfer vote in demarcated districts and retained negotiated interdependence.

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi used the Rajaji formula as the basis for talks with Jinnah in **September 1944**. Gandhi described the possible outcome as separation within an Indian family after freedom; Jinnah demanded prior acceptance of the principle of Pakistan as the League understood it.

| Point | Formula/Gandhi position | Jinnah’s objection |
|---|---|---|
| Territory | Contiguous districts with an absolute Muslim majority | District demarcation could truncate the claimed provinces and produce a “moth-eaten” Pakistan |
| Electorate | Adult population of the demarcated area | Non-Muslim votes would help decide a Muslim nation’s claim |
| Timing | Decision and implementation after the war and full transfer | Pakistan should be accepted in principle before a common transfer |
| Sovereignty | Separation accompanied by essential-services agreements | Joint arrangements could restrict the completeness of sovereignty |
| Authority | Gandhi negotiated personally from a Congress-national position | Jinnah insisted that the League alone represented Muslim nationhood |

**Strongest objection:** The formula was simply Congress acceptance of Pakistan.

**Reply:** It crossed an important conceptual threshold by accepting a possible separation. But its district-based plebiscite, post-transfer timing and mandatory interdependence were precisely the limits that prevented it from being Jinnah’s Pakistan. Rajagopalachari’s initiative also did not bind the imprisoned Congress Working Committee; Gandhi negotiated in a personal capacity.

**Balanced significance:** The talks failed, but they clarified the unresolved variables—territory, electorate, timing, sovereignty and representative authority—that reappeared at Simla and in the Cabinet Mission grouping controversy.

### Part B — Wavell Plan and Simla Conference, 1945

### Wavell’s purpose

✅ **Fact:** Wavell had succeeded Linlithgow as Viceroy in 1943. He believed post-war India could become ungovernable by force and sought a Congress–League coalition before unrest deepened.

✅ **Fact:** The proposed Executive Council was to be entirely Indian except for the **Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief**.

✅ **Fact:** It proposed equal representation for **caste Hindus and Muslims**, along with separate representation for the Scheduled Castes.

✅ **Fact:** The Simla Conference ran from **25 June to 14 July 1945**.

### The representational conflict

Jinnah insisted that the League should nominate all Muslim members. Congress could not accept this without conceding that it represented only caste Hindus. The contradiction was visible because **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, a Muslim, was Congress president.

This was more than a quarrel over names:

| League claim | Congress objection |
|---|---|
| Electoral/political community requires one authoritative spokesman | National organisation cannot be reduced to a Hindu communal body |
| Muslim nominees must owe their position to the League | Congress must remain able to include Muslims in its national claim |
| Parity protects against majoritarian control | Parity may overrule broader electoral and territorial principles |

⚠️ **Inference:** Simla showed that constitutional office could not be distributed until political representation was settled. The coming elections would test the rival claims, but not reconcile them.

### Distinctions and limits

| 1944 negotiation | 1945 negotiation |
|---|---|
| Asked whether and where separation might occur | Asked who could enter a common interim executive |
| Used a future district plebiscite | Used parity and communal nomination |
| Failed over Pakistan’s definition and sequencing | Failed over the League’s monopoly claim to Muslim representation |
| Gandhi negotiated personally; formula did not bind Congress organisation | Congress and League met the Viceroy as organised parties |

The common thread was representation, but the institutional questions were different. Do not collapse the C.R. Formula into the Wavell Plan or treat Simla as a plebiscite proposal.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Wavell could have formed a council without the League.

**Reply:** Formally possible, politically fragile. Wavell believed a coalition without the principal Muslim claimant could not stabilise the transition. His decision strengthened the League’s veto, but it reflected the problem the scheme was meant to solve.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** C.R. Formula—April 1944; Gandhi–Jinnah talks—September 1944; Simla—June–July 1945.
- **Mains:** Trace the shift from territorial decision rules to representative nomination.
- **Trap:** Congress did not reject the idea of an Indian Executive Council.
- **Transition:** Elections became necessary because Simla lacked a fresh test of representation.

### Revision notes

1. C.R. Formula belongs to April 1944.
2. It proposed a post-war commission for contiguous absolute Muslim-majority districts.
3. The adult population of those districts would vote on separation.
4. Separation would operate only after full transfer of power.
5. Defence, communications and other essential services required agreement.
6. Gandhi–Jinnah talks failed in September 1944.
7. Jinnah objected to truncated territory, a mixed electorate, deferred implementation and limited sovereignty.
8. Rajagopalachari’s initiative was not an official binding Congress offer.
9. Wavell became Viceroy in 1943 and anticipated a post-war governing crisis.
10. His plan sought a Congress–League coalition.
11. Only Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief would remain British in the council.
12. Parity was proposed between caste Hindus and Muslims, with Scheduled Caste representation.
13. Conference dates: 25 June–14 July 1945.
14. League claimed exclusive Muslim nomination.
15. Congress rejected being constitutionally reduced to a caste-Hindu body.

## Lesson 11 — Elections of 1945–46: two mandates, not one referendum

Progress: 11/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Congress and League performance, franchise limits and bargaining power

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical election evidence and permitted book account
🔍 CA Search: "Historical session — no current-affairs search required"
📰 CA Found: None; electoral interpretation is controlled by the historical franchise
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the split verdict

```text
restricted franchise + separate electorates
                  │
      ┌───────────┴───────────┐
      ▼                       ▼
general constituencies     Muslim constituencies
Congress dominance         League dominance
      │                       │
claim to Indian nation     claim to Muslim nation
      └───────────┬───────────┘
                  ▼
Cabinet Mission must reconcile two powerful mandates
without treating the election as a territorial plebiscite
```

*The election clarified who could negotiate; it did not tell negotiators exactly what constitutional territory voters had chosen.*

### Evidence

✅ **Fact:** Congress won **over 90 per cent of general seats** in the provincial elections, including **23 of 36 labour seats**.

✅ **Fact:** Across India, Congress received **80.9 per cent of votes in general constituencies**.

✅ **Fact:** The Muslim League received **74.7 per cent of votes in Muslim constituencies** and swept most Muslim seats.

✅ **Fact:** The electorate was heavily restricted—about **one-tenth of the population**—and voting operated through separate electorates.

### What changed?

1. Congress’s national claim gained strong electoral confirmation in general constituencies.
2. The League could no longer be dismissed as merely an elite organisation without Muslim electoral support.
3. Third forces lost bargaining space.
4. Any constitutional scheme now had to accommodate both Congress and League.

### What did not change?

- The poll was not universal adult suffrage.
- It was not a referendum on partition.
- No agreed boundary or constitutional design for Pakistan appeared on the ballot.
- Muslim-majority provinces remained internally complex.

Punjab is the best warning. The League won **75 of 175 seats**, while Congress had 51, Akalis 22 and Unionists 18; Khizr Hyat Khan Tiwana formed a coalition with Congress and Akalis, keeping the League from provincial office. ### Strongest objection and reply

## Lesson 12 — Attlee and the Cabinet Mission: designing unity with a weak centre

Progress: 12/13 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Announcement, arrival, Union subjects, groups and constitution-making

📚 Book context: Queried — canonical plan provisions and permitted OCR evidence

📰 CA Found: None; exact constitutional wording controls the lesson
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the three-tier constitution

```text
                     UNION
       defence + foreign affairs + communications
                         │
       ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
       ▼                 ▼                 ▼
   SECTION A          SECTION B          SECTION C
 Madras, Bombay,      Punjab, NWFP,      Bengal,
 U.P., Bihar,         Sind               Assam
 C.P., Orissa
       │                 │                 │
       └──────── groups frame group subjects ────────┘
                         │
                     PROVINCES
             retain all remaining subjects
```

*The plan tried to give Congress one India and the League powerful Muslim-majority blocs without creating sovereign Pakistan.*

### Political opening

✅ **Fact:** On **15 March 1946**, Prime Minister Clement Attlee stated that a minority would not be allowed to veto the progress of the majority. This is distinct from his **20 February 1947** deadline announcement, which belongs to Topic 27.

✅ **Fact:** The Cabinet Mission—**Pethick-Lawrence, Stafford Cripps and A.V. Alexander**—arrived in India on **24 March 1946**. Its constitutional statement was issued on **16 May 1946**.

### Why the Mission rejected Pakistan

The Mission judged a sovereign Pakistan administratively, economically and strategically unsatisfactory. Partition would not solve minority problems because substantial non-Muslim populations would remain in Pakistan and Muslims would remain in India. Punjab and Bengal themselves contained mixed regions.

### Exact constitutional architecture

1. **Union:** a Union of **British India and the Indian States**, with an **executive and legislature** composed of representatives of both. It would control defence, foreign affairs and communications, plus finances required for these subjects.
2. **Communal safeguard:** a major communal question in the Union legislature required both an overall majority of those present and voting and a majority of the representatives present and voting from **each of the two major communities**.
3. **Provinces and States:** provinces retained all subjects other than Union subjects and all residuary powers; States retained every subject and power they did not cede to the Union.
4. **Groups/sections:** provinces would meet in sections and could create group constitutions and common subjects.
5. **Constituent Assembly scale:** provincial seats were allotted roughly at **one representative per million people**. Provincial legislators voted in **General, Muslim and Sikh** electoral colleges by **proportional representation with the single transferable vote**.
6. **Membership:** British India was allotted **296 representatives**; the States could receive **up to 93**, with their method of selection to be negotiated.
7. **Procedure:** after a preliminary meeting, British Indian representatives divided into Sections A, B and C. Each section first settled provincial constitutions and decided whether to establish a group constitution and which provincial subjects to administer jointly. The sections and State representatives then reassembled to settle the Union constitution.
8. **Advisory Committee:** the Assembly would establish a committee on **fundamental rights, minority protections, and tribal and excluded areas**.
9. **Interim Government:** all portfolios, **including the War Member’s portfolio**, were to be held by Indian leaders enjoying popular confidence while constitution-making proceeded.

The sections were:

- **A:** Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Central Provinces and Orissa.
- **B:** Punjab, North-West Frontier Province and Sind.
- **C:** Bengal and Assam.

✅ **Fact:** After the **first general elections** under the new constitution, a province could leave its group.

✅ **Fact:** After **ten years**, a province could call for reconsideration of the group or Union constitution.

### The central contradiction

The Mission described grouping as optional while requiring provinces to enter compulsory sections for the initial constitution-making process.

```text
Congress reading: province may choose not to be bound
                         │
text says sections initially meet compulsorily
                         │
League reading: compulsory grouping creates durable blocs
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Ambiguity was not a drafting footnote. It enabled initial acceptance because each party could see its objective in the plan, but it also postponed the conflict into the Assembly.

### How constitution-making was meant to move

```text
Provincial assemblies elect community representatives by PR-STV
                              │
British India 296 + States up to 93
                              │
preliminary meeting + officers + Advisory Committee
                              │
provincial representatives divide into A / B / C
                              │
sections frame provincial constitutions and decide group machinery
                              │
sections + States reassemble for Union constitution
                              │
Indian-held Interim Government runs administration meanwhile
```

The sequence explains why “grouping optional, sections compulsory” became explosive. The sections were not decorative labels: they performed constitution-making work before the full Union Assembly settled the centre.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The plan was merely partition under another name.

**Reply:** It preserved one Union, one constitution-making framework and common sovereign subjects. Yet the weak centre and grouped provinces offered the League state-like leverage. It was neither a unitary India nor two sovereign states.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Memorise Union subjects and section composition exactly.
- **Mains:** Explain the bargain before explaining failure.
- **Trap:** A province could leave a group after the first general election, not necessarily before initial sectioning.
- **Boundary:** Do not carry the story into Mountbatten or the 1947 plan here.

2. Mission members: Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, Alexander.
3. Arrival: 24 March 1946; statement: 16 May 1946.
4. Mission rejected sovereign Pakistan.
5. Union embraced British India and States, with its own executive and legislature.
6. Union subjects: defence, foreign affairs, communications and their finance.

8. Provinces retained residuary authority; States retained all unceded powers.

11. C: Bengal, Assam.

### Initial acceptance

✅ **Fact:** The League accepted the plan on **6 June 1946**, reading compulsory grouping as the basis of Pakistan within the proposed structure.

Congress accepted entry into the Constituent Assembly but insisted that provinces and the Assembly retained freedom. Patel emphasised that Pakistan had been rejected and one Assembly envisaged.

✅ **Fact:** In his AICC speech on **7 July 1946**, Nehru stated that Congress was not bound by anything except its decision to enter the Constituent Assembly.

✅ **Fact:** The League withdrew acceptance on **29 July 1946**.

### Why Nehru’s statement mattered—and why it is not the whole explanation

To the League, the statement meant a Congress majority in a sovereign Assembly could alter grouping. To Congress, accepting permanent compulsory groups would trap Assam and NWFP and hollow out national unity.

**Strong objection:** Nehru carelessly destroyed an agreed settlement.

**Reply:** His formulation supplied the immediate occasion for withdrawal and deserves causal weight. But the agreement already rested on opposed meanings. Silence might have postponed rather than removed the conflict over whether the Assembly could change the plan.

### Interim Government

✅ **Fact:** Britain proceeded with a Congress-led Interim Government on **2 September 1946**, with Nehru as de facto head.

✅ **Fact:** The League joined on **26 October 1946**, but participation did not signify acceptance of a common constitutional objective. Departments and executive decisions became another arena of conflict.

### End-of-1946 limit

✅ **Fact:** On **6 December 1946**, the British government supported the League’s compulsory-grouping interpretation.

✅ **Fact:** The Constituent Assembly first met on **9 December 1946**; the League boycotted it.

⚠️ **Inference:** Ambiguity explains the July rupture but not the entire failure. Even after Britain clarified grouping in the League’s favour, the League did not join the Assembly. The deeper conflict concerned whether one sovereign Indian constitution-making process could embody two national claims.

### Boundary consequence

The League’s Direct Action decision and the ensuing communal violence marked the grave consequence of constitutional rupture. Their detailed history, Mountbatten’s arrival, Partition and transfer mechanics are routed forward to Topic 27.

- **Prelims:** 6 June → 7 July → 29 July → 2 September → 26 October → 6/9 December.
- **Mains:** Distinguish proximate trigger from structural cause.
- **Trap:** League entry into Interim Government did not equal acceptance of the Constituent Assembly.
- **Boundary:** Stop at end-1946; do not narrate the 1947 plan.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Why did the INA trials have greater political impact than the INA’s military campaign?

**Model answer:**
The INA campaign remained tied to Japanese strategy and failed at Imphal–Kohima. Supply problems, Allied resistance, retreat and surrender prevented it from liberating India or creating sustained internal revolt.

The trials operated in a transformed political setting. At the Red Fort, Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon became a Hindu–Muslim–Sikh symbol. A defence team including Bhulabhai Desai, Sapru, Katju, Nehru and Asaf Ali gave the case legal-national visibility. Congress relief work, newspapers, students, markets, municipalities and several political organisations spread sympathy far beyond the battlefield.

The prosecution also raised a legitimacy question: did Britain possess the moral right to punish Indians who claimed allegiance to an Indian government? Sympathy among officials and service personnel made severe punishment risky.

Thus battlefield defeat limited military achievement, while prosecution nationalised the question of patriotism. The impact remained political, not proof that the INA alone forced withdrawal.

**Scoring note (10 marks):** military limits 3 + trial mechanisms 4 + legitimacy impact 2 + qualification 1 = 10/10.

## 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Examine the significance and limitations of the RIN revolt of February 1946.

**Model answer:**
The RIN revolt began on 18 February 1946 among a large body of HMIS Talwar ratings protesting racial discrimination, poor food and abuse; resentment over B.C. Dutt’s arrest added nationalist meaning. Castle and Fort Barracks joined on 19 February, and action spread widely across ships and shore establishments, with Karachi the second major centre.

Its significance was psychological and political. Congress, League and Communist flags were displayed together; workers and students paralysed Bombay; related RIAF sympathy strikes and a Jabalpur sepoy strike suggested wider service disaffection. The revolt showed that colonial racial hierarchy could endanger discipline within a coercive arm.

Its limits were equally important. The action was brief, urban and not planned by a national political organisation. Joint flags represented temporary organisational unity, not resolution of communal politics. British troops, naval power and aircraft remained available; Indian units helped restore control. Patel advised surrender, Jinnah appealed separately to Muslim ratings, and Gandhi criticised unprepared mutiny.

Nor did the revolt cause the Cabinet Mission: the British Cabinet had decided on 22 January to send it. The RIN episode should therefore be treated as a dramatic indicator and accelerant of imperial crisis, not a nationwide revolution or single cause of transfer.

**Scoring note (15 marks):** chronology and grievances 4 + political significance 4 + operational/political limitations 5 + causal judgement 2 = 15/15.

## 20 marks — 250 words

**Question:** “The Cabinet Mission Plan was the most ingenious and the most fragile attempt to preserve Indian unity.” Assess.

**Model answer:**
The Cabinet Mission confronted two strong electoral mandates: Congress dominance in general constituencies and League dominance in Muslim constituencies. It rejected sovereign Pakistan but recognised that a strong unitary centre would not satisfy the League.

Its ingenuity lay in three tiers. A Union would control defence, foreign affairs and communications; provincial groups would frame common group subjects; provinces retained the rest. Sections A, B and C provided Muslim-majority blocs without creating separate states. A Constituent Assembly, Interim Government, post-election provincial exit from groups and ten-year constitutional reconsideration added flexibility.

Its fragility lay in the same grouping device. The plan called grouping optional while requiring initial section meetings. The League accepted on 6 June because compulsory groups implied Pakistan’s basis. Congress entered the Assembly while asserting provincial freedom and Assembly sovereignty. Nehru’s 7 July speech exposed the incompatibility; the League withdrew on 29 July.

Ambiguity was a proximate cause, not the whole failure. Congress feared a hollow centre and trapped provinces; the League feared that an Assembly majority would dismantle its blocs. Even after Britain supported compulsory grouping on 6 December, the League boycotted the Assembly on 9 December.

The plan was therefore ingenious because it placed one Union over two political blocs. It was fragile because it could secure consent only while each side believed it could later alter the settlement.

**Scoring note (20 marks):** electoral/constitutional context 4 + institutional design 7 + fragility and breakdown 6 + balanced assessment 3 = 20/20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Bose and the external route

- Forward Bloc followed the 1939 Congress conflict.
- Bose escaped Calcutta on 16–17 January 1941.
- Route: Kabul, Germany and later Southeast Asia.
- Indian Legion belonged to the European theatre.
- Strategic idea: use captured Indian manpower and Britain’s enemies.
- Core risk: anti-colonial purpose depended on Axis resources and strategy.
- Explain the logic without endorsing Axis politics.

## 2. First INA

- IIL supplied political coordination; Rash Behari Bose was the senior nationalist figure.
- Mohan Singh organised Indian POWs militarily.
- First INA division formed 1 September 1942.
- The first division developed into a substantial force; precise strength is omitted.
- Japanese preferred a token force; Mohan Singh wanted a larger autonomous army.
- Mohan Singh and Niranjan Singh Gill were arrested by December 1942.
- Breakdown arose from autonomy versus auxiliary status.

## 3. Bose’s reorganisation

- Bose reached Singapore on 2 July 1943.
- Reorganised INA; headquarters at Singapore and Rangoon.
- Recruited civilians and raised expatriate funds.
- Rani of Jhansi Regiment associated with Lakshmi Sahgal.
- Provisional Government formed 21 October 1943.
- Symbols: Dilli Chalo, Jai Hind, sacrifice and claimant sovereignty.
- Foreign recognition did not equal democratic or territorial control.

## 4. Campaign and retreat

- INA battalion under Shah Nawaz joined the Indo-Burma campaign.
- Imphal–Kohima offensive failed in 1944.
- Supply, terrain, monsoon, Allied resistance and Japanese decline mattered.
- Book records discriminatory Japanese treatment of INA personnel.
- Retreat continued from mid-1944 to mid-1945.
- Military defeat must be separated from later political afterlife.
- Bose was officially reported dead in an August 1945 air crash; do not claim more.

## 5. Trials and mobilisation

- Red Fort accused: Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon.
- Defence: Bhulabhai Desai, Sapru, Katju, Nehru, Asaf Ali.
- Congress formed INA Relief and Enquiry Committee.
- Campaign reached press, students, markets, funds, municipalities, villages and several parties.
- Hindu–Muslim–Sikh symbolism was powerful but issue-specific.
- Armed-force and official sympathy made exemplary punishment dangerous.
- Distinguish broad groundswell from short violent confrontations.

## 6. Post-war upsurge

- War left inflation, scarcity, famine memory and workplace distress.
- Leader release and elections raised expectations.
- Labour strikes, Tebhaga, Warli, Punjab kisan action, Punnapra–Vayalar and Telangana had distinct programmes.
- Shared anti-imperial climate did not create one organisation.
- Three direct confrontations: 21 November 1945 Calcutta; 11 February 1946 Calcutta; 18 February 1946 Bombay.
- Common pattern: initial defiance → repression → city solidarity → wider sympathy → suppression.

## 7. RIN exact chronology

- 18 February: a large body of Talwar ratings strike.
- Grievances: racial discrimination, bad food and abuse.
- B.C. Dutt arrest resentment added nationalist content.
- 19 February: Castle and Fort Barracks join.
- Rumour that Talwar ratings had been fired upon was incorrect.
- Karachi became second major centre.
- The revolt spread widely across ships and shore establishments; disputed totals are omitted.
- Congress, League and Communist flags jointly flown.
- British troops, naval ships and aircraft imposed coercive pressure.
- Surrender occurred on 23 February.

## 8. Responses and limits

- Patel advised surrender because he feared destruction.
- Jinnah addressed Muslim ratings separately.
- Gandhi criticised mutiny without prepared command.
- Communist call brought major worker action in Bombay.
- RIAF sympathy strikes and Jabalpur sepoy strike indicate wider unrest.
- They do not prove institutional armed-force revolution.
- Unity at the barricade was brief and organisational.
- Colonial coercive capacity remained intact.

## 9. Why Britain moved toward transfer

- Cabinet decided on Mission on 22 January 1946.
- 19 February announcement was scheduled before the revolt.
- RIN did not cause Mission dispatch.
- INA/RIN sharpened the loyalty and legitimacy problem.
- Long causes: mass nationalism, 1942, declining loyalism.
- Structural causes: British war exhaustion and depleted administration.
- Political causes: Congress–League mandates and need for negotiated settlement.
- Best verdict: INA/RIN were significant indicators and accelerants.

## 10. C.R. Formula, Gandhi–Jinnah talks, Wavell Plan and Simla

- C.R. Formula: April 1944; contiguous absolute Muslim-majority districts.
- Adult plebiscite after the war; operation only after full British transfer.
- Defence, communications and other essential services required agreement.
- Gandhi–Jinnah talks failed in September 1944.
- Jinnah rejected district truncation, mixed voting, deferred implementation and constrained sovereignty.
- Rajagopalachari’s proposal did not bind the Congress organisation.
- Wavell sought post-war coalition and executive Indianisation.
- Council all Indian except Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief.
- Parity between caste Hindus and Muslims; separate Scheduled Caste representation.
- Simla dates: 25 June–14 July 1945.
- Jinnah claimed League monopoly over Muslim nomination.
- Congress refused reduction to a caste-Hindu party; Azad was president.
- Conference failed over representation, not rejection of Indian executive responsibility.

## 11. Elections

- Congress: over 90% of provincial general seats; 23/36 labour seats.
- Congress: 80.9% of general-constituency votes.
- League: 74.7% of Muslim-constituency votes.
- Franchise: roughly 10% of population; separate electorates.
- Punjab: League 75/175, Congress 51, Akalis 22, Unionists 18.
- Election certified representative standing, not a defined territorial referendum.
- Two mandates made grouping politically intelligible.

## 12. Cabinet Mission

- Attlee statement: 15 March 1946; no minority veto over majority progress.
- Members: Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, A.V. Alexander.
- Arrival: 24 March 1946; constitutional statement: 16 May 1946.
- Rejected Pakistan; proposed united but highly decentralised India.
- Union of British India and States, with an executive and legislature.
- Union: defence, foreign affairs, communications and necessary finance.
- Major communal questions required overall and concurrent community majorities.
- Provinces retained residuary powers; States retained all unceded powers.
- Sections A, B and C as listed in the recall table.
- Constituent Assembly seats were roughly one per million.
- General, Muslim and Sikh electoral colleges used PR-STV.
- British India: 296 seats; States: up to 93.
- Sections framed provincial constitutions and decided group machinery before reassembling with States for the Union constitution.
- Advisory Committee: fundamental rights, minorities, tribal and excluded areas.
- Interim Government: all portfolios, including War Member, held by Indians.
- Province could leave group after first general elections.
- Ten-year reconsideration of group or Union constitution.
- Contradiction: optional grouping, compulsory sections.

## 13. Breakdown through 1946

- League acceptance: 6 June.
- Nehru AICC statement: 7 July.
- League withdrawal: 29 July.
- Direct Action is only the boundary consequence here.
- Congress-led Interim Government: 2 September.
- League joined: 26 October.
- Britain supported compulsory grouping: 6 December.
- Assembly met with League boycott: 9 December.
- Ambiguity explains rupture; rival sovereign claims explain failure to repair.
- Route communal violence, Mountbatten, Partition and transfer mechanics to Topic 27.
