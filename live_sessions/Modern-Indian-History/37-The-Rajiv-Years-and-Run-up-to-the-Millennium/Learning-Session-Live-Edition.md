# The Rajiv Years and the Run-up to the Millennium - Live Learning Session

> **What this session teaches:** It opens with Rajiv Gandhi's succession on 31 October 1984 and closes with the 1999 election, Pokhran-II, Lahore and Kargil at the edge of the millennium. It explains how a record majority could coexist with weak party institutions; how technology, education, local-government and peace initiatives opened real possibilities; how identity concessions, Sri Lanka and Bofors drained authority; how Chandra Shekhar's short ministry bridged the National Front collapse and the 1991 election; and how Mandal, Mandir, Market, post-Cold War adaptation and regional parties rebuilt the political system. The mechanics of the 1991 reform are studied in the post-independence economic synthesis and in Economy; present-day anti-defection doctrine is studied in Polity. Both limits are marked where they arise.

## How to read this session

Work through the lessons in order; each answer depends on the one before. Inside a lesson, take the visual first, then the plain-language explanation, then the exact evidence, then criticism and reply, and only then the examination use.

Two labels run throughout:

| Label | Meaning | Use in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Fact** | Directly supported by a verified source - the canonical topic files, the standard survey history of post-1947 India, an official electoral or governmental record, or a verified question paper | Assert it. Dates, seat totals and statute names can be stated flatly |
| ⚠️ **Analytical inference** | Interpretation, evaluation, comparison or synthesis built on those facts | Argue it, signal it, and expect a counter-position |

Knowing which sentences need evidence and which need reasoning is what separates a confident answer from an over-confident one.

### Visual: three events that must never be collapsed into one

```text
31 October 1984
Indira Gandhi assassinated
        |
same day: Rajiv Gandhi sworn in as Prime Minister
        |
31 October - 3 November: Sikhs targeted in mass violence
        |
24-27 December: general election held in most constituencies
        |
Congress wins 404 of 514 seats polled in that round
        |
1985: deferred polling held in Punjab and Assam
        |
final Eighth Lok Sabha tally: Congress 415 of 543
```

*Constitutional succession, the state's failure to protect citizens, and electoral authorisation are three events with three different standards of judgement. They are linked; they are not the same thing.*

### 1. The immediate problem, in ordinary language

A government whose head is killed at eight in the morning must still sign orders by the afternoon. There is no procedure that produces a leader by consensus overnight and no time to hold an election first, so the party in office nominates a successor and the President appoints.

✅ **Fact:** Rajiv Gandhi took office on 31 October 1984, the day of the assassination. Senior Congress leaders and President Giani Zail Singh treated immediate succession as necessary for continuity of government.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** that solved an executive-continuity problem and nothing else. It conferred legal authority to govern, not popular authorisation, and settled none of the disputes - Punjab, Assam, the state of the party - that the new Prime Minister inherited. The December election supplied authorisation; the two must be kept apart.

At the same time Sikh citizens were attacked in Delhi and elsewhere.

✅ **Fact:** The assassination was carried out by two of Indira Gandhi's own security guards; criminal liability for that act attaches to identified individuals through judicial process.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** nothing in the act of two individuals transfers to a religious community, and the state's duty to protect every citizen is not suspended by mourning or administrative confusion. The honest formulation is twofold and the halves are independent: individual criminal liability on one side, institutional failure of protection on the other. Accepting one does not require softening the other. That habit - keeping the act, the actor, the community and the state's obligation in separate columns - governs the whole session.

### 2. Two numbers, two denominators

| Figure | What it measures | Why the denominator is what it is |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Congress 404 of 514 | The immediate result of the December 1984 round | Polling was deferred in Punjab and Assam, so only 514 constituencies had been decided |
| ✅ Congress 415 of 543 | The final strength of the Eighth Lok Sabha | The deferred seats voted in 1985, restoring the full House and raising the Congress total |
| ❌ 404 of 543 | Nothing | It mixes the December numerator with the full-House denominator |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the two correct figures answer different questions, so state the pair with one clause on the deferral. That shows you know why the denominator moved. A second confusion is equally costly: Rajiv Gandhi did not win an election and then become Prime Minister. October precedes December.

### 3. What the election decided

✅ **Fact:** Polling in most constituencies took place on 24-27 December 1984, on a campaign fought largely on national unity and integrity.

| Force | Contribution | Limit the historian must add |
|---|---|---|
| Sympathy | An exceptionally powerful emotional wave | Explains a vote, not later governing quality |
| Fear for national unity | Voters had watched Punjab, Assam and the assassination in sequence | A stability mandate is not a policy mandate |
| Opposition fragmentation | Anti-Congress forces were divided and leaderless | Regrouping remained possible, as 1988-89 proved |
| Rajiv's image | Youth, technological optimism, a reputation for cleanliness | Expectations rose so high that ordinary disappointment looked like betrayal |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** because the coalition of support was emotional, defensive and personal rather than programmatic, the majority was at once the largest and the least anchored of the period.

### 4. Crisis-born majority versus organisational depth

```text
CRISIS-BORN MAJORITY                 ORGANISATIONAL DEPTH
national wave, one leader            state units that select and discipline
emotional, defensive appeal          their own leaders; membership rolls,
transferable in one election         internal elections, local cadre
                                     accumulated over years
        both deliver seats; only the second survives a bad
        year, a scandal or an unpopular decision
```

✅ **Fact:** The Congress that fought the 1984 election had not held credible internal elections; candidate selection was substantially controlled from the centre, and local intermediaries retained the power to enrol members and shape organisational outcomes.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the mandate therefore bought governing room, not political security. A party resting on a national wave has no shock-absorber when the wave recedes - which is why everything attempted after 1984 was carried on a structure that could not by itself hold the electorate.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Calling this mandate fragile is perverse; it was the largest Congress victory ever recorded.

**Reply:** Fragility refers to foundations, not size. Sympathy, a security appeal and one attractive leader supplied breadth across constituencies; none of the three repaired the organisation, resolved Punjab or Assam, or created an accepted internal procedure for choosing leaders. A structure can be wide and shallow at once.

**Residual:** A landslide can be democratically impeccable and institutionally hollow. The useful question is what a government builds with it - which governs Lessons 2 and 3.

### UPSC application

Open with **constitutional succession -> obligation of citizen protection -> electoral authorisation**, one step per sentence. Use 404 of 514 for the December verdict, add 415 of 543 for the House, and explain the deferral in a clause. Where a question asks about the "nature" of the mandate, the marks lie in the gap between scale and depth. Never write that the election excused the violence or that the violence invalidated the votes.

### Examiner traps in this lesson

- Writing that Rajiv Gandhi became Prime Minister after winning the election; the oath preceded the poll by two months.
- Writing "404 of 543" instead of 404 of 514 or 415 of 543.
- Treating the anti-Sikh violence as a by-product of transition rather than a failure of the state's protective obligation.
- Assigning collective responsibility for the assassination to a community, or treating mixed prosecution outcomes as evidence that the violence is unproven; both are category errors.
- Calling the verdict a "policy mandate"; it was a unity-and-stability mandate.

### Revision notes

1. Rajiv Gandhi became Prime Minister on 31 October 1984, the day of the assassination.
2. Succession was executive and immediate; authorisation came only in December.
3. Anti-Sikh violence followed between 31 October and early November 1984.
4. Criminal guilt is individual; collective targeting is indefensible.
5. Most polling took place on 24-27 December 1984.
6. Congress won 404 of the 514 seats polled in that round.
7. Punjab and Assam polling was deferred and completed in 1985.
8. The final Eighth Lok Sabha tally was Congress 415 of 543.
9. The mandate combined sympathy, fear for unity, opposition division and a modernising image.
10. A crisis-born majority is not organisational depth.
11. The correct question about 1984 is what the majority was used to build.

### Concept check

**Question:** An answer states: "Congress won 404 of 543 seats in December 1984, and this policy mandate allowed Rajiv Gandhi to become Prime Minister." Identify every error and rewrite it.

**Model answer:** Three errors. The denominator: 404 belongs with 514, because Punjab and Assam polling was deferred; 543 belongs with 415, the total after those seats voted in 1985. The character of the verdict: the campaign turned on unity, stability and personal appeal after an assassination, so it authorised a government rather than endorsing a programme. The sequence: he was sworn in on 31 October 1984 and sought the mandate afterwards. Corrected: "Sworn in on 31 October 1984 after his mother's assassination, Rajiv Gandhi sought authorisation in December, when Congress won 404 of the 514 seats then polled - 415 of 543 once deferred Punjab and Assam constituencies voted in 1985 - on an appeal to unity and stability rather than a detailed programme."

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating seat scale as proof of political strength. The largest majority of the period rested on the weakest organisational base, which is why the disappointment of 1987-89 was so sharp.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain why Rajiv Gandhi's 1984 mandate was electorally overwhelming yet institutionally demanding. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

The mandate was overwhelming because succession followed a national trauma and the Congress campaign fused Rajiv Gandhi's leadership with unity and integrity. In the December poll Congress won 404 of the 514 seats then contested, rising to 415 of 543 once deferred Punjab and Assam polling was completed in 1985. Youth, technological optimism and a "clean" image added positive expectation to the sympathy wave.

It was institutionally demanding because none of those forces repaired what was broken. The organisation remained centralised and dependent on intermediaries, Punjab and Assam were unsettled, and the state's failure to protect Sikh citizens demanded accountability rather than electoral vindication. The winning coalition rested on one leader and one extraordinary moment.

The mandate therefore supplied exceptional governing space, not durable authority. Rajiv Gandhi's test was whether personal legitimacy could be converted into capable institutions, negotiated settlements and accountable government.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = sequence and result stated exactly (succession before poll, 404/514 with 415/543) 3 + causes of scale 2 + institutional deficits named 3 + qualified verdict tying scale to fragility 2.**

**Bridge to Lesson 2:** A landslide creates room to experiment. The next question is what Rajiv Gandhi intended to do with it - and his answer was a particular idea of modernisation.

### Visual: modernisation was three streams, not one gadget

```text
                     MODERN STATE CAPACITY
                              |
      +-----------------------+-----------------------+
      |                       |                       |
technology missions       human capability       democratic reach
water, literacy,          education, rural       telecom, information,
```

*The claim was that science, communication and a particular style of administration could be turned on mass problems. Buying machines was the smallest part of it.*

### 1. What "mission mode" means

An ordinary department works to an annual budget, a fixed hierarchy and a financial year. That is adequate for routine administration and poor at any task that crosses several departments, needs expertise the department does not employ, and must reach a stated target by a stated date. Mission mode is the answer:

```text
define one bounded social problem
        ->
fix a time-bound, measurable target
        ->
assemble scientific and administrative expertise across departments
        ->
monitor delivery against the target rather than against expenditure
        ->
solve implementation problems at mission level instead of escalating them
```

✅ **Fact:** Six technology missions were framed around drinking water, adult literacy, immunisation, milk production, edible oils and village telephones.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** five of the six are mass-capability problems of the poorest households; only one is a communications technology. Any description of the agenda as urban-elite fascination must explain away the other five. The method was itself the innovation: converting a diffuse aspiration into a bounded target with named technical responsibility transferred to civilian development the project discipline India had reserved for atomic energy, space and defence. That form outlived its author - population-scale registration, real-time monitoring and cross-departmental teams remain the standard design of Indian delivery programmes, as the 2026 digital-infrastructure record shows.

### 2. Telecommunications and computers

✅ **Fact:** Sam Pitroda and the Centre for Development of Telematics (C-DOT) became the public face of an effort to design and build digital switching equipment in India rather than import it. Computer policy lowered barriers to components, encouraged domestic production and widened institutional use.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the argument was historical, not technical. India had missed the first industrial revolution because colonial rule structured the economy around raw-material export; the government's case was that missing the information revolution would be irreversible in the same way. On that reading computerisation was strategic preparation, and the test is not how many machines were bought in the 1980s but whether skills, firms and institutions existed when demand arrived. The cost must be recorded alongside: the immediate beneficiaries were concentrated in cities, government and large firms, and the village telephone mission was the thin end of a distribution that stayed sharply unequal for another decade. Preparation is defensible; it is not the same as inclusion.

### 3. Human capability: education, employment, women, environment

| Instrument | ✅ What it did | ⚠️ What it could not do |
|---|---|---|
| National Policy on Education, 1986 | Made literacy a national campaign and framed school and distance-education expansion | Policy framing does not equalise school quality across states |
| Operation Blackboard | Supplied basic facilities and teaching material to primary schools | Facilities are a precondition of learning, not learning |
| Navodaya Vidyalayas | Free, merit-selected residential schools for children of poor rural families, one per district | A selective school reaches the talented few, not the average child |
| Jawahar Rozgar Yojana | Rural employment for at least one member of every rural poor family for 50-100 days a year, the Union bearing the larger share; launched for Jawaharlal Nehru's birth centenary | Guarantees on paper depend on local administrative capacity |
| National Perspective Plan for Women, 1988 | Proposed reserving 30 per cent of elected panchayat seats for women and that half of grassroots functionaries be women; addressed women's health and education | A proposal is not a law; the reservation entered the statute book only through later constitutional amendment |
| Environmental policy | A dedicated Ministry for Environment, mandatory clearance for large projects, a large Ganga cleaning programme | Clearance regimes and river programmes proved far harder to enforce than to announce |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** "modernisation" in this government's vocabulary meant raising citizens' capability - literacy, health, employment, representation - as much as raising the technological level of the state. The elitism charge survives only if Navodaya schools and cultural festivals abroad are treated as the whole programme.

### 4. Achievement and limitation

| Claim | Evidence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Technology became a development instrument | Mission-mode water, literacy, immunisation, milk, oilseeds, telephony | Implementation was uneven and depended on existing state capacity |
| Computers prepared future capability | Domestic production and institutional use expanded | Immediate benefits were concentrated; diffusion took a decade |
| Rural concerns were genuinely present | Employment, schools, literacy, women's representation, local government | Ambition exceeded delivery capacity |
| Liberalising signals appeared | Selected relaxation of controls and import barriers | The systemic break came in 1991, under a different government and compulsion |

**Objection:** The agenda was an elite fascination with gadgets dressed up as development.

**Reply:** Telecom and computers were the visible symbols, but the programme's own targets were literacy, drinking water, immunisation, oilseeds, milk, rural schooling and rural employment. A government that wanted only gadgets would not build its administrative innovation around those six problems.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** technological capacity improves delivery only when administration, access and accountability move with it. Mission design outran administrative capacity in the 1980s, and the same design delivered at population scale only when identity, payment and connectivity infrastructure caught up a generation later.

### UPSC application

Write **modernisation of aspirations and of administrative method**, then prove it with both halves: the mission-mode design, and the capability instruments - NPE 1986, Operation Blackboard, Navodaya, Jawahar Rozgar Yojana, the women's plan, the environment ministry. Reserve one sentence for uneven delivery and one for the boundary: selected deregulation anticipated but did not constitute the 1991 reform. If invited to compare with present-day delivery platforms, argue continuity of *method* and discontinuity of *scale*, and mark the second half as inference.

### Examiner traps in this lesson

- Reducing the programme to "computers and telecom"; name three missions and one social-policy instrument.
- Writing that Rajiv Gandhi launched the 1991 reforms.
- Treating the 1988 proposal of 30 per cent panchayat seats for women as an enacted law.
- Using Navodaya Vidyalayas as proof of elitism without noting their design - free, merit-selected, residential, rural.
- Claiming the missions succeeded uniformly; coverage varied sharply with state capacity.

### Revision notes

1. Six technology missions: drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils, village telephones.
2. Mission mode = bounded problem, time-bound target, cross-departmental expertise, monitored delivery.
3. C-DOT and Sam Pitroda symbolised indigenous telecom design capacity.
4. Computer policy eased components, encouraged production and widened institutional use.
5. The strategic argument was that India could not miss a second technological transition.
6. NPE 1986 carried literacy, Operation Blackboard and distance education.
7. Navodaya Vidyalayas: free, merit-selected residential schools for rural children, one per district.
8. Jawahar Rozgar Yojana: 50-100 days a year for one member of every rural poor family.
9. National Perspective Plan for Women, 1988 proposed 30 per cent of panchayat seats for women.
10. Environment: dedicated ministry, mandatory clearance for large projects, the Ganga programme.
11. Rajiv-era measures anticipated liberalisation; they were not the 1991 package.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes that Rajiv Gandhi's modernisation "was about computers for the urban middle class". Refute it from the design of the programme, then state the criticism that survives.

**Model answer:** On content, five of the six technology missions addressed mass capability - drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk and edible oils - and only village telephony was a communications technology; alongside them ran NPE 1986, Operation Blackboard, Navodaya Vidyalayas, the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana's 50-100 days of rural work and the 1988 proposal of 30 per cent of panchayat seats for women. On method, mission mode attached a bounded social problem to a dated, measurable target with cross-departmental technical responsibility, which is an instrument for reaching the unreached. The surviving criticism is distributional and immediate: the benefits of computerisation and telecom access were concentrated in cities, government and large firms for another decade, and several missions delivered unevenly because they depended on the administrative capacity each state already possessed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating preparation and inclusion as one claim. Arguing that computerisation was strategically necessary is compatible with conceding that its early benefits were unequally distributed.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Assess whether Rajiv Gandhi's modernisation programme was socially inclusive. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

The programme was technologically visible but not socially narrow. Six missions applied coordinated science and administration to drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils and village telephones. C-DOT and computer policy built communications capability, while the National Policy on Education, 1986, Operation Blackboard, Navodaya Vidyalayas and the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana addressed rural schooling and employment, and the 1988 National Perspective Plan for Women proposed reserving 30 per cent of panchayat seats for women.

Its inclusiveness was programmatic rather than achieved. Delivery varied with state administrative capacity, early technology access stayed concentrated in cities and large institutions, and the women's panchayat reservation remained a proposal until later constitutional amendment. Selected deregulation also anticipated rather than constituted the 1991 break.

Rajiv Gandhi therefore widened what development was taken to mean by tying technology to basic capability. The durable contribution was to modernise state ambition; the limitation was the distance between mission design and universal implementation.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = named mission and social-policy evidence 3 + explanation of mission-mode method 2 + distributional and capacity limits 3 + verdict separating ambition from delivery 2.**

**Bridge to Lesson 3:** Mission design could bypass departmental inertia because it created a new channel outside the old one. Democratic institutions cannot be bypassed that way, and that is where the next difficulty begins.

### Visual: reform succeeded where rules could be rewritten and stalled where power had to be redistributed

```text
PROBLEM                    RESPONSE                     RESULT
party switching       ->  52nd Amendment, 1985    ->  Tenth Schedule created
weak village bodies   ->  constitutional bills     ->  debate advanced; bills failed
consumer and access   ->  Lok Adalats, Consumer    ->  new low-cost remedies

```

*The pattern is the lesson. Where a defect could be corrected by writing a new rule, this government acted fast. Where correction meant taking power from people whose support it needed, nothing moved.*

### 1. The anti-defection reform

Through the 1960s and 1970s legislators had repeatedly crossed the floor for office or money, toppling ministries between elections and making the voter's choice reversible by purchase.

✅ **Fact:** The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 inserted the Tenth Schedule after discussion with opposition parties. Under the original scheme a split was recognised where one-third of the members of a legislature party broke away; defection outside that protection attracted disqualification from the House. The question whether a member had incurred disqualification was decided by the presiding officer of the House concerned.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** two points of exactness are tested constantly. The one-third split protection belongs to the original 1985 design and was removed much later, so the present two-thirds merger rule must never be read backwards. And the adjudicator was the Speaker or Chairman from the beginning - not the Election Commission, not a court.

| Democratic gain | Democratic cost |
|---|---|
| Governments became harder to topple by purchase between elections | Party leadership acquired leverage over the individual legislator |
| Individual floor-crossing for office was restrained | Genuine dissent could be treated as, or feared as, defection |
| A formal procedure replaced pure political bargaining | The presiding officer, who owes the office to a party, decides cases affecting that party |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the difficulty is structural, not personal. The office combines impartial presiding with continuing party membership, and the schedule loaded the first with consequences for the second. Since no time limit was built in, delay has the same practical effect as decision - which is why contemporary litigation keeps returning to timelines and to the scope of the presiding officer's discretion. Modern doctrine is Polity's subject; this lesson supplies the disorder, the 1985 remedy and the trade-off designed into it.

### 2. The local-government initiative

✅ **Fact:** The government prepared constitutional amendment bills to give elected local bodies a constitutional basis, preceded by an unusually wide consultation. Between December 1987 and June 1988 the Prime Minister met some four hundred district collectors and officers in charge of districts; state chief secretaries followed in July 1988; a Panchayati Raj gathering of about eight thousand delegates was held in January 1989 and a conference on Panchayati Raj for Women in May; the All India Congress Committee supported the proposals in May 1989, and a meeting of chief ministers followed.

✅ **Fact:** The bills failed in the Rajya Sabha, where the government lacked a majority. The rural and urban frameworks were enacted later, as the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments, under the Narasimha Rao government.

```text
Rajiv initiative + national consultation
        ->
constitutional bills fail in the Rajya Sabha
        ->
73rd and 74th Amendments enacted later under Narasimha Rao
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the honest formulation credits agenda-setting to one government and enactment to another. Writing that Rajiv Gandhi "passed" the amendments is wrong; writing that he had nothing to do with them erases a consultation that shaped their content, including the reservation of seats for women that the 1988 plan had proposed.

### 3. The failed party reform

✅ **Fact:** At the Congress centenary in December 1985 Rajiv Gandhi attacked the "power-brokers" he blamed for reducing the party to a shell and promised renewal. No durable internal democratisation followed: local bosses could enrol members and shape organisational elections, thereby acquiring the further legitimacy of being elected. As close advisers were estranged, older intermediaries returned; by early 1989 an adviser closely identified with the previous political style was back at the centre of government.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the paradox is structural. A legislative reform needs a parliamentary majority, which he had; an organisational reform needs the consent or defeat of the intermediaries who deliver candidates, members and votes, and a leader carried in on a national wave has no independent base with which to defeat them. This is also where the continuity thesis bites: over-centralisation, reliance on a small circle of advisers and weak internal procedure reproduced the de-institutionalisation of the preceding decade, but without the mass authority that had sustained it - so the same method offered far less protection when the weather turned.

### 4. Judging an institutional reform properly

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** test a reform by asking whether incentives changed, and for whom.

| Measure | Incentive it changed | Incentive it left or created |
|---|---|---|
| Tenth Schedule | Legislators lost the option of profitable floor-crossing | Party leaderships gained a disciplinary instrument; presiding officers acquired a partisan-looking function |
| Local-government bills | None in this term - they failed | The agenda and consultation record survived for a later government |
| Centenary critique | None; a diagnosis is not a redistribution | Central nomination and intermediary bargaining continued unchanged |

**Objection:** The Tenth Schedule proves that institutional reform here was serious and comprehensive.

**Reply:** It shows that one pathology was addressed by statute. It also created new tensions between stability, legislative judgement and impartial adjudication; local-government reform failed within the term; and the party was not reformed at all. Serious, yes; comprehensive, no.

**Residual:** Reform that changes a rule is cheap in political capital. Reform that changes who holds power is expensive - and the second kind is what the Congress organisation needed.

### Concept check

**Question:** Rajiv Gandhi held an unprecedented parliamentary majority. Why could he create the Tenth Schedule but neither democratise the Congress nor constitutionalise panchayats? Give the mechanism in each case.

**Model answer:** The three tasks needed different kinds of power. The Tenth Schedule needed only a constitutional amendment in a House he controlled, and it imposed its costs on individual legislators of all parties, so cross-party discussion was possible; it passed in 1985. Constitutionalising local government needed the same process in both Houses, and the government lacked a Rajya Sabha majority, so the bills failed despite a consultation running from four hundred district officers in 1987-88 to an eight-thousand-delegate gathering in January 1989 - which is why the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments belong to Rao's government. Democratising the Congress needed neither votes nor drafting but the removal of intermediaries who controlled membership rolls, organisational elections and candidate selection; a leader whose authority came from a national wave rather than an organisational base had no instrument with which to defeat them.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming a large majority is a general-purpose solvent. It converts fully in one chamber, is checked in the other, and buys almost nothing inside a party organisation.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Why did Rajiv Gandhi find it easier to legislate against defection than to democratise the Congress organisation? (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

The defection problem was visible, externally located and legislatively bounded. Cross-party discussion produced the Fifty-second Amendment, 1985, which inserted the Tenth Schedule, recognised a split only at one-third of a legislature party and left disqualification to the presiding officer. The measure promised stability and public action against horse-trading, even though it later raised concerns about dissent and about the neutrality of the office deciding the cases.

Party reform was harder because it threatened internal beneficiaries. The centenary speech of December 1985 attacked "power-brokers", but local bosses could still enrol members and shape organisational elections, while centralised candidate selection and dependence on a small circle of advisers reproduced the structure being criticised; as those advisers were estranged, older intermediaries returned.

A single constitutional act could therefore change an external parliamentary rule. Democratisation required a sustained redistribution of power inside the ruling organisation, for which parliamentary numbers were useless.

**Bridge to Lesson 4:** Disciplining legislators was the narrower achievement. The government's strongest political work came where it negotiated with regional movements instead of legislating at them.

## Lesson 4 - The 1985-86 accords and federal accommodation

### Visual: one method, three different conflicts

| Settlement | The conflict it addressed | Core method | Durable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Punjab Accord, July 1985 | Territorial, river-water, autonomy and representation disputes, overlaid with militancy | A negotiated package with the recognised Akali leadership | An agreement needs implementation machinery, or it stalls |

*Three agreements in twelve months, one method, three genuinely different problems. The comparison instructs only if the differences survive it.*

### 1. Why compare without homogenising?

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Punjab combined federal disputes over territory and river waters with an escalating armed conflict, so the negotiation had to satisfy a constitutional claim and reduce violence at once. Assam was a dispute about who lawfully belonged to the electorate, so the settlement had to produce a legal test applicable to individuals. Mizoram was an insurgency over political status, so the settlement had to offer the insurgent leadership a credible constitutional destination. Different problems required different instruments, and the instruments are what an answer should compare.

### 2. The mechanism of accommodation

```text
recognise that a political grievance exists
        ->
identify interlocutors who can lawfully speak for the movement
        ->
separate negotiable demands from coercion
        ->
record reciprocal commitments in a dated public document
        ->
bring movement actors into elections and into office
        ->
implement credibly, and be seen to implement, over years
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the first step is the most political - a state that refuses to recognise a grievance has only coercion left, and one that recognises it too late negotiates with actors already displaced by harder rivals. The last step is the most administrative, and it is where two of these three accords ran into difficulty.

### 3. Case file: Assam and the citizenship categories

✅ **Fact:** The Assam Accord was signed on 15 August 1985. Its settlement of the foreigners question used entry-date categories anchored to 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971, with safeguards for the cultural, social and linguistic identity and heritage of the Assamese people.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the categories are legal, not ethnographic. A date-based test can only be applied to a named individual through evidence and adjudication. Language, religion, appearance or district of residence are not proofs of status; they may prompt an inquiry and can never conclude one, and aggregate demographic change cannot determine any individual's citizenship. This is the single most important control in the lesson, because the alternative - inferring status from identity - is both unlawful and historically catastrophic.

✅ **Fact:** Implementation of the remaining commitments remained contested long afterwards; the Clause 6 high-level committee's recommendations were still being negotiated between the state government, the Union government and the students' body in 2026.

### 4. Case file: Punjab and the implementation gap

✅ **Fact:** The Punjab Accord of July 1985 was concluded with Akali leader Sant Harchand Singh Longowal, who was assassinated shortly after signing.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Punjab shows what happens when the sequence breaks at the last step. Commitments on territory and river waters were contested, implementation was delayed, and the leader who had staked his authority on the agreement was killed. Once delivery stalls, the moderate signatory loses the argument inside his own movement to those who said negotiation would yield nothing - so non-implementation does not merely postpone peace, it strengthens the harder faction.

### 5. Case file: Mizoram and why it worked

✅ **Fact:** The Mizoram Accord of June 1986 involved renunciation of violence by the Mizo National Front and its incorporation into constitutional politics; the insurgent leadership entered elected government and the settlement held.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Mizoram succeeded because four conditions coincided - a bounded dispute about political status, an interlocutor who could deliver the armed cadre, a constitutional destination worth disarming for, and reasonably prompt implementation. Remove any one and the outcome changes: Punjab lost the fourth, and Assam's fourth was still being negotiated four decades later.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Accords of this kind reward movements that pressure the state, teaching every future movement that coercion pays.

**Reply:** Negotiation validates a claim, not a method. What was conceded in each case was a political demand defensible on the merits - a territorial settlement, a lawful determination of citizenship, an autonomous constitutional status. What was not conceded was immunity for violence; Mizoram was explicitly conditional on renouncing it. Refusing to negotiate legitimate claims because illegitimate methods accompanied them guarantees permanent conflict.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** durable peace needs four things after signature - implementation machinery, continuing consent on both sides, competitive elections that give former combatants a stake, and equal rights for everyone affected. An accord is a framework, not a peace.

### UPSC application

Use these settlements to argue **asymmetric but constitutional nation-building**: India accommodated regional claims by varying the constitutional arrangement rather than insisting on uniformity. Structure the comparison as grievance -> interlocutor -> instrument -> implementation -> outcome, filling each cell differently. Reserve the strongest line for the Assam categories: a citizenship cut-off is a legal test requiring individual determination. Never describe every regional movement as separatist or any of these settlements as complete.

### Examiner traps in this lesson

- Treating the three accords as interchangeable; grievances, instruments and outcomes all differ.
- Writing that signature ended the conflict; Punjab's commitments were contested and Assam's Clause 6 was still being negotiated decades later.
- Using the Assam cut-off dates as though they classify communities rather than individuals.
- Crediting Mizoram's success to generosity rather than design.
- Forgetting that all three were concluded within about a year of the 1984 mandate - the strongest evidence that the mandate was used for something.

### Concept check

**Question:** All three accords were negotiated by the same government within about a year, yet only one produced durable peace. Using the accommodation sequence, locate where Punjab and Assam diverged from Mizoram.

**Model answer:** Mizoram satisfied every step: a bounded dispute over political status, a Mizo National Front leadership able to deliver its armed cadre, a constitutional destination worth disarming for, and prompt implementation, so former insurgents entered elected government and the peace held. Punjab broke at implementation - commitments on territory and river waters were disputed and delayed, and Sant Longowal was assassinated soon after signing, so the moderate signatory could show his movement no return for negotiating and the harder faction was strengthened. Assam broke at the instrument: entry-date categories tied to 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971 converted a mass grievance into an administrative task of individual determination requiring decades of adjudication, and its Clause 6 safeguards were still under negotiation with the Centre in 2026. The divergence is therefore not in the negotiation but in what had to happen after the signature.

**Misconception to avoid:** Concluding that Punjab and Assam show negotiation to be ineffective. Both show that negotiation without delivery fails - an argument for implementation machinery, not against accommodation.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Compare the Punjab, Assam and Mizoram accords as instruments of national integration. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The accords of 1985-86 marked a shift from delayed coercive management to negotiated incorporation, but each addressed a different conflict and tested a different stage of settlement.

The Punjab Accord of July 1985 sought to reconcile territorial, river-water and autonomy disputes after militancy and the events of 1984 had destroyed trust. Its instrument was a package agreed with the recognised Akali leadership. Its failure point was implementation: commitments were contested and delayed, and Sant Longowal's assassination soon after signing removed the leader on whose authority the agreement depended, strengthening those who had argued that negotiation would yield nothing.

The Mizoram Accord of June 1986 succeeded most completely. The Mizo National Front renounced violence, insurgent leadership entered elected government, and an accommodated constitutional status acquired legitimacy that has since held.

Their common achievement was to treat regional identity as negotiable within the Union rather than as a threat to it - asymmetric but constitutional nation-building. Their variation shows that signature only begins the work: durable integration also requires lawful determination, faithful implementation and continuing consent.

### Part A - The global agenda

#### A1. Disarmament: three instruments, in order

✅ **Fact:** The **Six-Nation Five-Continent Initiative** was formed by Indira Gandhi shortly before her death, bringing together the heads of government of **Argentina, Greece, India, Mexico, Sweden and Tanzania** to press the superpowers to reduce and eliminate nuclear weapons. Within a month of winning the December 1984 election, Rajiv Gandhi convened the first summit of the six.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this was before Gorbachev came to power and before disarmament had entered the superpower agenda - a coalition of six middle and small powers across five continents creating pressure from outside the bloc system, the classic non-aligned instrument applied to the nuclear question.

✅ **Fact:** In November 1986, during Gorbachev's visit, the two leaders issued a call for a non-violent world; the programme became known as the **Delhi Declaration** and set out a plan for disarmament. The Initiative then matured into an **Action Plan for Nuclear Disarmament**, which Rajiv Gandhi presented to the **United Nations General Assembly's third special session on disarmament in June 1988**, calling for the **elimination of all nuclear weapons by 2010**.

| Instrument | Year | What it was | What it added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Six-Nation Five-Continent Initiative | from 1984-85 | A standing coalition of six heads of government | Pressure from outside the superpower blocs |
| Delhi Declaration | November 1986 | A bilateral Indo-Soviet statement of principles | Superpower endorsement of the principle |
| Action Plan for Nuclear Disarmament | June 1988 | A staged programme at a UN special session, targeting elimination by 2010 | A timetable and a universal forum |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the progression runs coalition -> principle -> programme, which is why the 1988 Action Plan, and not the 1986 Declaration, is the clearest *programmatic* form of the claim. It also commits India for the rest of the session to a demand for universal, non-discriminatory disarmament - the position that shapes the CTBT dispute and the 1998 tests in Lesson 10.

#### A2. Anti-apartheid and Namibia

✅ **Fact:** India pursued the struggle against apartheid vigorously, securing a Commonwealth majority for sanctions although the United Kingdom under Margaret Thatcher did not move. The **AFRICA Fund** - **Action for Resisting Invasion, Colonialism and Apartheid** - was established at the Non-Aligned summit at **Harare in 1986** to help frontline African states bear the cost of sanctions; by the Belgrade Non-Aligned summit three years later, contributions from developing and developed countries had reached about half a billion dollars. On Namibia, India recognised SWAPO, and Rajiv Gandhi visited the frontline states of Zambia, Zimbabwe, Angola and Tanzania in May 1986; Namibia became independent in 1990.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the AFRICA Fund converted a moral position into a transfer of money to states bearing the material cost of a shared policy - the difference between declaratory and operational solidarity, and the strongest evidence that Indian Third World diplomacy in this period was more than rhetorical.

#### A3. Great powers, China and multilateral extras

✅ **Fact:** Relations with the superpowers improved without realignment. A 1985 visit to the United States went well and secured access to a supercomputer India had sought for weather processing, though American commitments in Pakistan limited movement; with Gorbachev a close relationship developed, the two leaders meeting eight times in five years. Rajiv Gandhi's 1988 visit to China was the first by an Indian Prime Minister since Nehru's in 1954, following a dip after border incidents in 1986, and was succeeded by efforts on long-standing problems, improved trade and extended consular contacts. India also brokered modalities for the Vietnamese withdrawal from Kampuchea from January 1987, including repeated meetings with the deposed Kampuchean ruler in Paris, before a twenty-one-nation conference produced withdrawal, elections under United Nations auspices and a coalition government. The Non-Aligned Movement was given a defining purpose in nuclear disarmament; a compact G-15 was proposed as a counterpart to the G-7; and a Planet Protection Fund was proposed at the ninth NAM summit.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** these are the marks of **strategic autonomy** in practice. The test of autonomy is not distance from everybody but the ability to improve several relationships at once without accepting any as a constraint on the others.

### Part B - The neighbourhood

#### B1. The record, bluntly

✅ **Fact:** Relations with immediate neighbours were the weakest part of the record. Water disputes with Bangladesh continued. Nepal imposed heavy duties on Indian goods while discounting Chinese goods, took delivery of Chinese assault rifles and anti-aircraft guns in 1988 and required work permits of Indian residents; India responded with what amounted to an **economic blockade in March 1989**, and negotiations began only in September. With Pakistan, hopes raised by Benazir Bhutto's premiership and by **Rajiv Gandhi's visit - the first by an Indian Prime Minister after Nehru** - were not realised, as support for insurgency in Kashmir and Punjab continued.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Nepal is the neighbourhood policy in miniature. The leverage worked, in that talks began; it failed, in that it converted a trade-and-transit dispute into a lasting grievance about Indian coercion. Power that is available is not power that is usable without cost, and the dispute was finally settled only by the successor government.

#### B2. Maldives: the controlled case

✅ **Fact:** When the Maldives faced a coup attempt its government asked for Indian help; India provided it and the attempt was defeated.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** four features made this bounded - a request from the recognised government, a small and clearly identified threat, an achievable military objective, and an obvious termination point. Hold them in mind; Sri Lanka failed on three.

#### B3. Sri Lanka: from relief mission to counter-insurgency

✅ **Fact:** The problem began in 1983, when thousands of Sri Lankan Tamils fled to Tamil Nadu after heavy repression in Jaffna. When Sri Lanka blockaded Jaffna, India sent supplies in fishing boats; the Sri Lankan Navy stopped them; India then air-dropped supplies using transport aircraft carrying Indian and foreign journalists, after which Colombo permitted supplies by boat.

✅ **Fact:** President J.R. Jayewardene then approached Rajiv Gandhi, and negotiations produced the Agreement of **July 1987**: the northern and eastern provinces, where Tamils were the majority, to be merged into a single province; substantial devolution of power; dissolution of the LTTE and surrender of its arms within a very short period; and provision for the Indian Army to assist if requested.

✅ **Fact:** The accord failed because the LTTE had given only reluctant consent, was not a signatory, distrusted the Sri Lankan state and refused to surrender its weapons. Jayewardene asked for Indian military assistance to implement the agreement, and the Indian Peace Keeping Force became involved in fighting guerrillas who knew the terrain and had local support. When Ranasinghe Premadasa succeeded Jayewardene he asked India to withdraw; Rajiv Gandhi agreed to a phased withdrawal, troops began returning in mid-1989, and withdrawal was completed only after the 1989 Indian elections.

```text
humanitarian crisis + Tamil Nadu pressure + strategic concern
        ->
bilateral accord signed with Colombo
        ->
disarmament timetable assumes LTTE compliance
        ->
LTTE refuses: it never signed, and it distrusts Colombo
        ->
Colombo requests enforcement; peacekeeping becomes counter-insurgency
        ->
Tamil militants see coercion; Sinhala opinion sees a foreign army
        ->
new president demands withdrawal; phased exit
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is the textbook case of **mission creep** - a bounded political purpose expanding because an assumption about local consent failed. Name the defect precisely: **an invitation from a government is not consent from the parties who must perform the agreement.** Colombo could sign; only the LTTE could disarm.

| Test | Maldives | Sri Lanka |
|---|---|---|
| Who invited India? | The recognised government | The recognised government |
| Whose compliance was needed? | The same government | A non-signatory armed movement |
| Was the objective achievable? | Yes, quickly | Only by defeating an embedded insurgency |
| Clear termination point? | Yes | No agreed enforcement or exit framework |
| Outcome | Rapid, bounded success | Expanding commitment, contested legitimacy, phased withdrawal |

#### B4. Defence modernisation - and the seed of the next lesson

✅ **Fact:** Rajiv Gandhi authorised a modernisation that roughly doubled defence expenditure; in the last two years of the term defence took about one-fifth of total government spending. The guided-missile programme begun in 1983 produced successful tests of the short-range Trishul and Prithvi and the intermediate-range Agni. The Navy expanded with a nuclear-powered submarine leased from the USSR and a second aircraft carrier purchased from Britain; the Army obtained howitzers from Sweden and sanction for an indigenous main battle tank.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the same purchasing programme that gave India credible missiles and artillery produced the arms contracts on which the government's credibility was destroyed. Lesson 6 begins there.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The Sri Lanka accord failed; therefore the intervention was irrational from the outset.

**Reply:** Reconstruct the 1987 decision problem - refugee flows into Tamil Nadu, pressure from a state sharing the refugees' language, a humanitarian blockade next door, and an interest in not letting outside powers manage a conflict on India's southern maritime flank. Mediation had a defensible logic, and the accord's substantive content, devolution inside a united Sri Lanka, has outlasted the operation.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** a defensible objective can rest on an indefensible theory of implementation. What was missing was mechanism - secured consent from every party whose performance the agreement required, an agreed enforcement structure, and a stated exit condition.

### UPSC application

Sri Lanka is the standing Indian example for questions on intervention, peacekeeping, the influence of state politics on foreign policy, and unintended consequences. Write it as a decision problem - motive, instrument, assumption, failure of the assumption, consequence - and put Maldives in the very next sentence as the controlled comparison. For disarmament questions give the three-step sequence with its dates, and use the 2010 elimination target to show that the later CTBT refusal continued a position rather than inventing one.

### Concept check

**Question:** Both operations began with an invitation from a recognised government, yet one ended in days and the other in a phased withdrawal after years of fighting. Which single distinction explains it, and what does it imply for designing an intervention?

**Model answer:** The distinction is between the party that issues the invitation and the party whose compliance the mission requires. In the Maldives they were the same: the government invited India, the threat was a small coup attempt, the objective was to defeat it, and the mission then had an obvious end. In Sri Lanka they differed: Colombo signed the July 1987 Agreement, but the undertaking to be performed - dissolution of the LTTE and surrender of its arms in a very short period - could only be performed by an organisation that had not signed, had consented reluctantly and distrusted the Sri Lankan state. When it refused, the Indian Peace Keeping Force was asked to enforce the agreement, and peacekeeping between consenting parties became counter-insurgency. The design implication is that an intervention needs consent from every actor whose performance the agreement depends on, an agreed enforcement mechanism if that consent fails, and a stated exit condition.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Why did the India-Sri Lanka Agreement of 1987 lead from mediation to military entanglement? (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

India entered the Sri Lankan conflict for intelligible reasons. Tamil refugees had been arriving in Tamil Nadu since 1983, the blockade of Jaffna created a humanitarian emergency in which India resorted first to supply by fishing boat and then to air-dropping relief, state-level political pressure was intense, and Delhi had a strategic interest in the conflict not being managed by outside powers. The July 1987 Agreement accordingly tried to preserve Sri Lankan unity while providing for a merged northern and eastern province, substantial devolution, cessation of hostilities and the surrender of LTTE arms.

The implementation design was defective. The LTTE had given only reluctant consent, was not a signatory and distrusted Colombo, yet the schedule assumed its rapid disarmament and no enforcement structure had been agreed with it. When it refused, Jayewardene requested Indian assistance, and the Indian Peace Keeping Force moved from separating consenting parties to fighting a locally embedded organisation with superior knowledge of the terrain. India then occupied the worst available position: Tamil militants treated it as coercive, sections of Sinhala opinion treated it as a foreign occupying army, and Premadasa demanded withdrawal on succeeding to the presidency.

The objective was therefore not irrational, but the consent model and exit strategy were inadequate. By contrast the Maldives operation, with a matching invitation, an achievable objective and an obvious termination point, succeeded in days. The episode's durable lesson is that regional influence cannot substitute for local political ownership.

*Three losses of a different kind - of reformist credibility, of secular credibility and of personal credibility - plus a fourth element without which they would not have been fatal: somebody able to organise them into a single electoral choice.*

### 1. Shah Bano: what was lost

✅ **Fact:** In 1985 the Supreme Court granted maintenance to a divorced Muslim woman. The judgment became controversial because orthodox Muslim organisations argued that it interfered with Muslim personal law. Arif Mohammad Khan, a young Muslim minister encouraged by the Prime Minister, defended it in Parliament. Under pressure from a powerful agitation and from close advisers, Rajiv Gandhi agreed to legislation negating the judgment; Arif Mohammad Khan resigned. The resulting statute was the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the historically decisive event is the *signal* sent by an executive reversal of a judgment its own minister had defended in the House - the personal-law question itself belongs to Polity and Social Justice. The government lost on both flanks at once. Liberal and secular opinion saw a retreat from gender justice under organised religious pressure; Hindu mobilisation saw proof that a determined minority agitation could obtain a legislative reversal. A concession meant to contain conflict demonstrated that conflict worked. The personnel consequence mattered too: the resignation of the minister who had carried the government's argument gave the emerging opposition a credible secular Muslim voice, and he joined V.P. Singh's front two years later.

### 2. Ayodhya: pairing without conflation

✅ **Fact:** The locks at the disputed shrine at Ayodhya were opened in 1986.

| The two decisions are NOT | The two decisions ARE |
|---|---|

| Evidence of a single policy on religion | Evidence of a *strategy of balancing*, and of its failure |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** balancing assumes a concession to one side cancels a concession to the other. In a competitive identity market it does the opposite: each concession is read by the rival mobilisation as proof that pressure works, so both escalate and the party holding the middle is squeezed from both directions. The mechanism is general, and it returns in Lesson 8 with the squeeze running the other way. Follow the political sequence and its electoral consequences only; the religious merits and the later litigation are not our subject here.

### 3. Bofors: the machinery of credibility loss

✅ **Fact:** The Fairfax controversy concerned the engagement by V.P. Singh, as Finance Minister, of an American detective agency to investigate illegal foreign-exchange holdings abroad; a forged letter suggesting the investigation touched a close friend of the Prime Minister surfaced, and leading industrialists were reported to be involved on opposite sides. V.P. Singh was moved from Finance to Defence, which the Opposition presented as protection rather than as the explained need for a capable Defence Minister.

✅ **Fact:** The HDW submarine controversy followed. When India sought a further order from the West German shipyard from which it had bought four submarines in 1981 and asked for a discount, the yard declined, citing a seven per cent commission on the sale. V.P. Singh, then Defence Minister, ordered an enquiry without consulting the Prime Minister; because the 1981 contract had been awarded under a Congress government this was treated in Cabinet as an unfriendly act, and he soon resigned. The press and the Opposition read the resignation as proof of his honesty, and the "Mr Clean" label moved to him.

✅ **Fact:** On **16 April 1987**, days after that resignation, the Bofors allegations broke. First reported on **Swedish Radio**, they alleged that the equivalent of sixty crore rupees had been paid as bribes to Indian officials and Congress party members to secure the contract for **410 howitzer guns** from the Swedish firm Bofors against strong French competition. Pursued vigorously by the Indian press, they escalated into a charge against the Prime Minister and his family, which he publicly denied. A Joint Parliamentary Committee report gave what amounted to a clean chit; the Comptroller and Auditor General's report cast doubt on the procedure for selecting the guns. The Opposition treated the latter as proof of guilt, which it did not state, demanded resignation and resigned en masse from the Lok Sabha shortly before the 1989 election. In mid-1987 President Giani Zail Singh became the centre of an effort to dismiss the Prime Minister, encouraged by opposition leaders and some Congress dissidents; it did not proceed, in part because V.P. Singh declined to participate.

### 4. From Jan Morcha to National Front: the missing ingredient

✅ **Fact:** V.P. Singh, Arif Mohammad Khan, Arun Nehru and other Congress dissidents formed the **Jan Morcha** on **2 October 1987**. His decisive victory in the **Allahabad by-election of June 1988**, in which the Bofors gun served as an unofficial campaign symbol, persuaded the Left and the BJP that he could defeat Congress.

✅ **Fact:** Opposition unity was pursued as a three-stage strategy: unity among centrist non-Congress secular national parties; a National Front of non-Left secular parties, regional and national; and seat adjustments with the Left and the BJP. The second stage was completed first, with the **National Front of seven parties formed on 6 August 1988**. The **Janata Dal was formed on 11 October 1988**, Jayaprakash Narayan's birthday, by the merger of the Jan Morcha, Congress(S), Janata and Lok Dal. The third stage produced an agreement under which the Janata Dal-led National Front and the BJP did not contest against each other in about **85 per cent** of seats where both would otherwise have nominated, with a narrower arrangement with the Communist parties.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** each stage supplies something distinct - the Jan Morcha a moral claim, the by-election proof of electability, the Front a vehicle, the Janata Dal a single symbol, the seat adjustments the arithmetic. Remove any one and Congress probably survives 1989 with a reduced majority. That is why "Bofors caused the 1989 defeat" is weak: the scandal was the fuel, the three-stage strategy was the engine.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Rajiv Gandhi lost simply because corruption allegations overwhelmed a good policy record.

**Reply:** The allegations were powerful because trust had already been eroded from two other directions. Shah Bano cost him the reformist claim, the Ayodhya pairing the secular claim, and an unreformed organisation could absorb neither shock. Bofors then removed the personal claim compensating for all of it - and none of the three losses would have become seats without the seat-adjustment arithmetic.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** a scandal becomes electorally decisive when three conditions coincide - it attacks the governing party's specific source of legitimacy, it supplies a simple moral narrative for diffuse failures, and an opposition exists that can convert diffuse anger into a single vote.

Under first-past-the-post a party's seat return depends on how its opponents are distributed, not only on its own support - which is why the seat adjustment of 1988-89 mattered more than any swing, converting an unchanged pattern of anti-Congress sentiment into a majority of seats against Congress.

### 2. Four words that are not synonyms

| Term | Meaning | In 1989 |
|---|---|---|
| **Seat adjustment** | Parties agree not to nominate against each other in specified constituencies; a pre-election device creating no post-election obligation | About 85 per cent of seats where both the Front and the BJP would otherwise have contested; a narrower arrangement with the Left |
| **Outside support** | A party votes to sustain a ministry without joining it; its members hold no office and are not bound by collective responsibility | The form adopted separately by the BJP and the Left |
| **Coalition cabinet** | Parties share ministerial office and collective responsibility | Not used; both supporters stayed outside |
| **Common minimum programme** | An agreed policy floor binding government and partners | Effectively absent; it became a normal instrument only later in the decade |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the distinction between outside support and cabinet membership is the whole explanation of the ministry's behaviour. An outside supporter bears none of the cost of a decision and can withdraw at any moment without resigning from anything, so it enjoys maximum leverage at minimum risk - which is why a minority government supported from outside by two mutually hostile poles cannot take any decision that forces those poles to choose. It is also why "the BJP and the Left formed a bloc in 1989" is wrong in substance: they never shared an organisation, a programme or a cabinet, and the deeper force was the inherent anti-Congressism of both rather than any agreement between them.

### 3. What the government did, and what consumed it

✅ **Fact:** V.P. Singh was sworn in on **2 December 1989**, accompanied only by Devi Lal as Deputy Prime Minister. Formation had been difficult: Chandra Shekhar opposed his leadership, and Devi Lal insisted on the deputy prime ministership.

✅ **Fact:** In eleven months the ministry completed the withdrawal of Indian troops from Sri Lanka and settled the trade and transit dispute with Nepal. In Jammu and Kashmir it appointed Jagmohan as Governor without wide consultation; Chief Minister Farooq Abdullah resigned in protest, the assembly was dissolved, and the Governor was later recalled. Punjab remained as difficult as before and Kashmir deteriorated. A very large share of its energy went on internal rivalry - between Chandra Shekhar and the Prime Minister, between Devi Lal and Ajit Singh, and over Devi Lal's promotion of his son to the chief ministership of Haryana, whose contested election at Meham was countermanded by the Election Commission after findings of rigging and intimidation. Arif Mohammad Khan and Arun Nehru resigned; Devi Lal was dismissed on **1 August 1990**.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the pattern repeats 1977-79 and deserves a general statement. Anti-Congressism was repeatedly capable of *transfer* - moving votes away from Congress across very different states and social groups - and repeatedly incapable of *organisation*, that is, of producing an agreed programme, an accepted leader and a procedure for settling disputes. A negative coalition can win an election and cannot run a government, because governing needs precisely the affirmative agreement the coalition was designed to avoid.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Support for one ministry by the BJP and the Left at the same time was simply incoherent.

**Reply:** It was coherent *as an electoral strategy* with a single negative bounded objective - remove Congress from office - and judged against that objective it succeeded completely. **Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** a strategy can succeed at government *formation* and fail at government *maintenance*, because the two need opposite things: agreement about an adversary, and agreement about a programme. Every arrangement in the following decade attempts to solve the second problem, and the common minimum programme is the instrument eventually invented for it.

Always give the arithmetic - 197 and 39.5 per cent, 146, 85, 52 - and then do the majority sum out loud, because that calculation alone shows why survival depended on two parties the ministry could not simultaneously satisfy.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Distinguish the political logic of the Janata experiment of 1977 from the National Front arrangement of 1989. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Both arose from anti-Congress convergence, but their organisational forms and failure modes differed.

In 1977 Congress (O), the Jana Sangh, the Bharatiya Lok Dal and the Socialists attempted an actual merger into the Janata Party, assuming that a single party could be manufactured from components retaining separate loyalties, programmes and leaders. It failed at integration: dual membership and the leadership contest broke it from inside.

In 1989 the Janata Dal anchored a National Front of 146 seats with regional allies, while the BJP's 85 and the Left's 52 sustained it from outside after seat adjustments covering about 85 per cent of jointly contested constituencies. This looser design made no claim to ideological unity, which is why it could be assembled at all - and why it collapsed the moment Mandir and Mandal forced its supporters to choose.

Thus 1977 was a failed merger and 1989 a fragile support architecture; together they taught that coalition management, not a substitute single party, was the skill national politics now required.

✅ **Fact:** On **7 August 1990** V.P. Singh announced in Parliament that the report of the Mandal Commission - appointed by the Janata government of 1977-79 and unimplemented since - would be implemented. The recommendation implemented was **27 per cent reservation** of posts in government services and public undertakings for the backward classes; with **22.5 per cent** already reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, the reserved total reached **49.5 per cent**. A second stage, covering reservation in educational institutions and promotions, was to follow.

| # | Question | What it asks | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Normative** | Did historically disadvantaged groups have a claim to representation in state employment? | ✅ Groups long excluded from state power had an argument of justice, formally accepted in principle for the Scheduled Castes since the freedom struggle. ⚠️ Extending it needed a fresh public case, which was not built |
| 2 | **Design** | Were the categories, criteria and institutional scope adequate? | ✅ Critics argued the identification method was outdated and ignored post-independence change; some listed groups had been major beneficiaries of land reform and the Green Revolution, while others were close to the Scheduled Castes in condition. ✅ The CPI(M) and others urged economic criteria. ⚠️ Lumping unlike groups together risks the most disadvantaged being unable to compete for the benefit |
| 3 | **Political** | Why was it announced when and how it was? | ✅ It came six days after Devi Lal's dismissal and two days before a rally he had called to show his strength; close associates, allies and the Left and BJP were not consulted. ⚠️ Timing does not settle the merits, but it explains the reception |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** keeping the four apart is what permits a balanced answer. You may hold that the justice claim was sound, the identification method flawed, the announcement damaging and the systemic effect democratising, without contradiction. Collapsing them produces the two standard weak answers - "pure social justice", which ignores 2 and 3, and "pure electoral manipulation", which ignores 1 and 4.

### 2. The protest, and what it reveals

✅ **Fact:** The reaction in north India was strong and violent. Students were at the forefront, often supported by teachers and office workers; protest took the form of rallies, attacks on public property and burning of buses, and police firing occurred in Delhi, Gorakhpur, Varanasi and Kanpur among other places. From mid-September a few students attempted self-immolation. The protest subsided when the Supreme Court granted a stay on implementation on **1 October 1990**.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the intensity is explicable without endorsement. Government employment was one of the few career routes still reachable by competitive examination without influence or money, so blocking close to half of it at a stroke was experienced as a change in the rules of an already narrow competition. A darker consequence must be recorded: much of the protest argued against caste as an organising principle, yet its later stages saw upper-caste students form previously unthinkable "forward caste" associations - a mobilisation conducted in the name of caste-blindness ended by manufacturing new caste blocs. The deeper structural point is that a distributive conflict this bitter is a symptom of scarcity; where secure public jobs are tiny relative to aspirants, every reservation question becomes zero-sum.

### 3. Geography: why Mandal detonated in the Hindi belt

✅ **Fact:** Mandal transformed politics in the Hindi belt and had far less purchase in the south, where backward-caste mobilisation had preceded it by decades.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the reason is sequence. In much of southern India the non-Brahmin and Dravidian movements, and the reservation arrangements growing out of them, had already carried intermediate and backward castes into political office, administration and education across the first half of the twentieth century and the decades after independence. By 1990 that transition had largely happened, so a central employment quota changed the share of Union posts without changing who held power in the state. In the Hindi belt no comparable transition had occurred, because the Congress umbrella had accommodated backward-caste voters without transferring leadership to them. Mandal therefore arrived in the north as a *founding* event of backward-caste assertion and in the south as an *incremental* one - which is also why the violent anti-Mandal protest was overwhelmingly northern, and why the parties built on backward-caste assertion after 1990 are concentrated in the same belt.

### 4. Mandir and the withdrawal of support

✅ **Fact:** On **25 September 1990** L.K. Advani began a rath yatra of some six thousand miles from Somnath in Gujarat towards Ayodhya. It ended on **23 October** at Samastipur in Bihar with his arrest and the BJP's withdrawal of support. On **30 October** there was firing on a crowd attempting to reach the site at Ayodhya; the yatra, the arrest and the firing raised communal tension sharply and were followed by rioting with many deaths in north India. On **5 November** the Janata Dal split, fifty-eight legislators electing Chandra Shekhar as their leader; on **7 November** the government ended, after eleven months.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** trace the mechanism exactly. The BJP's support was *outside* support - it held no office, so withdrawal cost it nothing and required no resignation. V.P. Singh could not satisfy the BJP without losing his Left allies and his own party's secular wing, so no position available to him retained both supporters. Withdrawal removed the votes needed to retain the confidence of the House, and the Janata Dal split then removed the possibility of reconstruction. The ministry fell because a minority government sustained from outside by two incompatible poles had finally been forced to choose.

### 5. Crossing, not replacing

```text
IF religion had absorbed caste:        WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED:
caste-based parties would decline      OBC-centred parties grew AND
as Hindu nationalist parties grew      Hindu nationalist strength grew,
                                       often in the same states and
                                       among overlapping electorates
       |                                        |
       v                                        v
one cleavage, one possible              two crossing cleavages, no
national majority                       automatic national majority
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the test of the crossing claim is concurrent growth. Had religious consolidation genuinely absorbed caste differentiation, backward-caste parties would have withered as Hindu nationalist strength rose. Instead both expanded in the same decade, frequently competing for the same voters in the same states, and caste continued to shape candidate selection, alliance formation and welfare claims even among voters who responded to religious appeals. That is the direct evidence for crossing rather than substitution, and why neither mobilisation delivered a stable national majority in the 1990s.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Identity politics of both kinds simply fragmented Indian democracy and made governance impossible.

**Reply:** It also brought previously under-represented social groups and regional leaderships into real political power, and through elections rather than agitation alone. The number of governments rose; so did the social range of those forming them.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** democratic deepening and polarisation occurred together and neither cancels the other. Wider representation is a genuine gain; it does not by itself supply constitutional restraint, protection of minorities, or the universal public goods whose absence made the distributive conflict so bitter.

### Concept check

**Question:** What observation would genuinely test - rather than merely restate - the claim that Mandal and Mandir were crossing rather than successive mobilisations? Why does the obvious alternative test fail?

**Model answer:** The genuine test is concurrent growth among overlapping electorates: whether OBC-centred parties expanded in the same states, at the same time and often among the same voters as Hindu nationalist organisations, and whether caste continued to structure candidate selection, alliance bargaining and welfare claims among voters who also responded to religious appeals. If religious aggregation had absorbed caste differentiation, backward-caste parties should have contracted as Hindu nationalist strength grew; the 1990s show both expanding, which is decisive. The obvious alternative test - looking for states where Hindu nationalist gains coincided with backward-caste decline - fails because it assumes what is in dispute. Substitution can always be found somewhere in a large federal system for reasons of local leadership, alliance arithmetic or incumbency, so a substitution-only test cannot distinguish absorption from ordinary electoral fluctuation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading the BJP's later national success backwards as proof that religion defeated caste. The same decade produced the OBC-centred parties that made coalition arithmetic unavoidable.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

"Mandal and Mandir were rival answers to the same crisis of political representation." Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

By 1990 the Congress cross-caste umbrella had weakened and no successor had assembled a stable national majority. Mandal and Mandir were competing solutions to that vacancy, and they ran in opposite directions.

Mandal answered it by differentiation. Implementing 27 per cent reservation for the backward classes on 7 August 1990, taking the reserved total to 49.5 per cent alongside the existing 22.5 per cent, recognised groups long excluded from state employment and created the organised backward-caste politics that produced the coalition era's regional parties. Its costs were real: abrupt timing two days before a rival's show of strength, criteria that critics judged outdated, and violent north Indian protest that ended only with the Supreme Court's stay of 1 October 1990.

Mandir answered it by aggregation. Advani's rath yatra from Somnath on 25 September 1990 sought to unite Hindu voters across caste around Ayodhya. His arrest at Samastipur on 23 October produced the BJP's withdrawal of outside support, and the ministry fell on 7 November.

The two therefore crossed rather than succeeded one another, and concurrent growth proves it: OBC-centred parties and Hindu nationalist organisations expanded together, often among the same voters, while caste continued to shape nominations and alliances. The regional qualification matters too, since Mandal was a founding event in the Hindi belt and an incremental one in a south already mobilised for decades.

Both deepened participation and both intensified polarisation. Because neither cleavage could reliably aggregate a national majority, their interaction made coalition government the system rather than a phase.

### Part A - The bridge that must not be skipped

#### A1. A splinter ministry sustained by its opponent

✅ **Fact:** The Janata Dal split on **5 November 1990**, fifty-eight legislators electing Chandra Shekhar as their leader. He became Prime Minister on **10 November 1990** with the support of Congress. Congress withdrew that support on **5 March 1991**, and a general election followed.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is the form taught in Lesson 7 in an extreme version. It was not a Congress coalition cabinet: a very small breakaway group held office because the largest party in the House chose to sustain it from outside without taking responsibility for it. The supporter therefore controlled the ministry's life while bearing none of its costs. Skipping this ministry is the single most damaging chronological error in the topic, because without it the reader cannot see why an election was being held in May 1991 - and without that, the assassination appears to cause the election rather than interrupt it.

#### A2. The election that began before the assassination

✅ **Fact:** The **first polling round was held on 20 May 1991** and **had been completed** when Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated at Sriperumbudur, near Madras, on **21 May 1991**, while rounding off a phase of campaigning; the killing was attributed to LTTE militants. The remaining phases were postponed and completed in June, and Congress emerged as the single largest party with **232 seats**.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "The assassination caused the 1991 election" | False. Congress's withdrawal of support on 5 March caused it; polling had already begun |
| "A sympathy wave affected the 1991 result" | Defensible. The postponed phases were fought in changed conditions, and contemporary accounts attribute part of the Congress recovery to it |
| "The whole 1991 Congress result is a post-assassination sympathy verdict" | Unsafe. The first round was completed before 21 May, so any such claim must be confined to the later phases |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the assassination also closes a circle opened in Lesson 5 - an intervention undertaken for intelligible strategic and humanitarian reasons ultimately cost the Prime Minister who ordered it his life. State that connection without extension: an organisation's act cannot be transferred to Tamil populations, exactly as the 1984 assassination could not be transferred to Sikh citizens. The same rule of individual responsibility governs both ends of this session.

### Part B - The Rao government, 1991-96

#### B1. Minority to full term

✅ **Fact:** P.V. Narasimha Rao formed what was initially a minority Congress government on **21 June 1991**. It gradually achieved a working majority and lasted a full five-year term.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is a political achievement routinely omitted from answers treating Rao as an economic administrator. Between 1989 and 1991 India had four Prime Ministers and two elections, and the arithmetic of the House had defeated three consecutive attempts at stable government. Rao's ministry showed that a government without an initial majority could govern for a full term and take decisions of the largest consequence - which changed what politicians believed possible and made the coalition decade thinkable.

#### B2. The economic break - and where History stops

✅ **Fact:** A balance-of-payments crisis provided the setting for a reform programme under Rao with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister; it was the most radical economic reform the country had undertaken.

```text
acute external crisis narrows the range of available alternatives
        ->
a government without a prior electoral mandate for reform acts,
because the alternative is default
        ->
the direction of policy changes fundamentally
        ->
later governments of every complexion retain the broad shift
        ->
a crisis-born decision becomes a settled consensus
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the important claim is counter-intuitive and worth stating outright - the reform succeeded politically *because* no consensus existed for it. A consensus would have required the negotiation that thirty years of argument had failed to produce; a crisis suspended the veto instead. That is also why it was neither reversed nor repudiated: by the time the emergency passed, the change was a fact rather than a proposal. The measures themselves - devaluation, industrial licensing, trade, tariff and financial-sector reform, disinvestment - belong to the post-independence economic synthesis and to Economy. A History answer states the crisis, the actors, the political conditions and the durability, and stops. The boundary with Lesson 2 must also hold: selected deregulation in the 1980s changed aspirations, while 1991 reset the relationship between state and market.

#### B3. The wider political record

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the evidentiary rule established for Bofors applies here in the reverse direction. The hawala episode inflicted real, measurable, cross-party political damage; the charges did not survive. A historian records both and lets neither erase the other - which is what protects the accused from the historian and the historian from the propagandist.

#### B4. Babri: the failure the term cannot be assessed without

✅ **Fact:** The Babri Masjid was demolished on **6 December 1992**. The government failed to prevent the demolition and failed to prevent the widespread rioting that followed.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** in a History answer the weight of the event is constitutional, not theological. A structure at the centre of a dispute pending before the state's own institutions was destroyed, and the Union government's protective obligation - the same obligation examined in Lesson 1 - was not discharged. The consequence was an acceleration of communal polarisation that shaped the rest of the decade. Record the date, the failure and the political consequence neutrally; do not adjudicate religious claims or the later litigation.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** 1991 is simply the year of economic reform, and Rao simply its administrator.

**Reply:** The eight months from November 1990 contain a party split, a splinter ministry sustained by its own opponent, that supporter's withdrawal, an election interrupted after its first round, an assassination closing the Nehru-Gandhi line in active politics, a minority government that became a full-term government, the most radical economic reform in the country's history, the substantial normalisation of Punjab, and within eighteen months the worst failure of constitutional protection since 1984. A one-dimensional reading discards most of the evidence.

**Residual:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** turning points are multi-dimensional. Policy innovation, political durability, institutional failure and unproved scandal can occupy the same five years without cancelling one another, and the graded verdict is the one an examiner rewards.

Assess the Chandra Shekhar-to-Rao transition as a multi-dimensional turning point in post-independence India. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Part A - Coalition normalisation, 1996-99

#### A1. The 1996 sequence, and two dates that are both correct

✅ **Fact:** In 1996 Congress fell to 140 seats while the BJP became the largest party with 161, up from 120 in 1991. A short-lived BJP government ran from **16 May to 1 June 1996** but could not obtain majority support; Vajpayee announced his resignation on **28 May**, thirteen days after taking office.

"Thirteen-day government" counts from the oath on 16 May to the resignation announcement on 28 May; the official tenure listing runs to 1 June because an outgoing ministry remains in office until the successor arrangement is completed. Announcement and formal terminal date are different administrative events, and explaining the difference demonstrates control of constitutional practice.

✅ **Fact:** A United Front government followed under H.D. Deve Gowda, supported by Congress and the CPI(M), with the CPI joining as a partner - giving India its first Communist Home Minister in Indrajit Gupta. Congress withdrew support on **30 March 1997**, failed to form a government itself, then supported a second United Front ministry under I.K. Gujral. That support too was withdrawn, and fresh elections were held in **February 1998**.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the constitutional lesson first met in Lesson 7 is now settled practice - being the largest party creates an opportunity to be invited and no entitlement to survive. What sustains a ministry is the continuing confidence of the House, assembled in a fragmented Lok Sabha from parties that may prefer to supply it from outside.

#### A2. 1998 and 1999

✅ **Fact:** The BJP won 182 seats in February 1998 and formed a government under Vajpayee with the support of parties including the TDP, the AIADMK and the Trinamool Congress. The large number of allies with competing demands prevented stability; the AIADMK's withdrawal led to the government losing a confidence motion in **April 1999**, by a single vote on the recorded division. Efforts to form an alternative failed and the ministry continued as a caretaker until elections in **September and October 1999**.

| Source | What it records | What it does not record |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Election Commission statistical report, 1999 | Party-wise results, including the BJP's 182 seats | Alliance totals; "NDA" is not a category in a party-wise report |
| ✅ Survey history of the period | That the tally of the BJP and its allies improved from 253 to **296**, that the BJP's own tally was unchanged, and that the BJP and allies polled **41.3 per cent** against **34.7 per cent** for Congress and its allies | The rule by which parties were counted as "allies"; the inclusion criterion is not stated |
| ✅ President's press communique, 11 October 1999 | That the Chief Election Commissioner delivered the results notification on 10 October; that the BJP emerged as the single largest party and MPs adhering to the NDA formed the largest political group; that the NDA's resolution of 10 October unanimously elected Vajpayee its leader; that the Telugu Desam Party and the All India Trinamul Congress had separately communicated support; that Article 75(3) requires the appointee to enjoy the confidence of the majority; and that the election results together with those support documents **satisfied the President that Vajpayee would command a majority** | Any numerical alliance aggregate. It records a constitutional satisfaction reached on stated materials, with no alliance seat total and no arithmetic |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** state the scale *with its provenance attached*, and state the appointment on the communique's own terms. Two cautions follow. The 296 figure is a reported aggregate whose inclusion rule is unstated, so it must not be passed off as an official electoral statistic - alliance totals are politically constructed, since whether a party counts as an ally depends on whether one uses pre-poll agreements, post-poll letters or subsequent voting. And the President's satisfaction is a constitutional determination on named materials under Article 75(3), exactly as strong as the communique says, so it cannot be converted into an independent demonstration that the alliance held a specified number of seats.

#### A3. Why coalitions became normal

| Structural cause | Political effect |
|---|---|
| The Congress cross-class umbrella weakened | No party could assume a national majority |
| OBC assertion and regional mobilisation grew | State-based parties acquired bargaining power at the Union level |
| The BJP expanded geographically | A second national pole emerged; competition became bipolar above and plural below |
| Social cleavages crossed rather than aligned | No single identity reliably aggregated a majority |
| Economic reform required continuity across governments | Alliances learned common programmes, coordination committees and negotiated restraint |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the deepest consequence is federal rather than arithmetical. Coalition government **federalised the Union executive**: parties whose entire base lay in one state now sat in the Union Cabinet and shaped national policy, so territorial and social diversity entered executive power instead of merely petitioning it. The costs are equally real - opaque bargaining, disproportionate leverage for a small ally holding the confidence margin, and short-term survival displacing policy - and by 1999 the party system had begun to manage them through pre-election alliances and agreed programmes rather than post-election improvisation.

### Part B - Post-Cold War external adaptation

✅ **Fact:** The demise of the Soviet Union, the end of the Cold War and India's shift to liberalisation coincided in 1991. India needed capital, technology and export markets, and there was no Soviet Union to fall back upon.

| Direction | ✅ Historical movement | ⚠️ Analytical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Russia and Central Asia | Post-Soviet ties preserved and old links with the Central Asian republics actively rebuilt | Continuity without dependence on a bloc that no longer existed |
| United States | Greater engagement despite continuing disagreements | Pragmatic adjustment to a more unipolar order, not alignment |
| ASEAN and Look East | India became an ASEAN **sectoral dialogue partner in 1992** and a **full dialogue partner in 1996**; contemporary assessment was that India had made little effort to join at the right time | Correction of a missed opportunity and the start of an eastward reorientation |
| SAARC | Continued engagement without economic maturity | A neighbourhood institution below its potential |
| Indian Ocean and Africa | Active participation in bringing Indian Ocean countries together, including South Africa | Widening the map beyond traditional partners |
| West Asia | Support for the Palestinian cause maintained while ties with Israel were opened; friendly relations with Iran sustained; India declined to join the ostracism of Iraq and Iran | Multi-vector diplomacy rather than a change of camp, which also limited attempts to use Islamic multilateral forums against India |
| Multipolarity | Continued advocacy of a plural order, with support for NAM expressed at its tenth summit at Jakarta | Preventing unipolar consolidation treated as an Indian interest |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the pattern is recalibration, not abandonment. India kept its instruments - non-alignment, SAARC, the Palestinian position - while adding new ones and reweighting old ones. The claim behind it was explicitly economic: in the emerging order economic weight rather than ideological position determines influence, which is why external adaptation and the 1991 reform are two faces of one adjustment.

### Part C - The nuclear choice, Lahore and Kargil

#### C1. The dual position

✅ **Fact:** From Nehru onwards India argued for nuclear disarmament and a nuclear-weapons-free world - pursued most actively through the Action Plan of 1988 - while maintaining scientific and technological readiness. The first test was conducted in 1974 under Indira Gandhi. The governments of Rajiv Gandhi, Narasimha Rao, Deve Gowda and I.K. Gujral all maintained readiness to exercise the option; Rao is said to have been close to authorising tests in 1995 before American pressure intervened.

✅ **Fact:** The reasons were stated plainly. The Non-Proliferation Treaty had been framed to confine nuclear-weapon status to a small group that China subsequently forced its way into; the CTBT was pressed on non-nuclear states without any corresponding commitment by the nuclear powers to disarm within any time frame, and India's attempts to have such a commitment written in were brushed aside - which is why India refused it, as it had refused the NPT. Security reinforced the position: a nuclear China with a large arsenal and delivery systems, nuclear ships in the surrounding seas, and a Pakistan with nuclear and long-range missile capability, testing the 1,500-kilometre Ghauri before the Indian tests of May 1998.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the two halves are not hypocrisy. India's objection was to a *discriminatory* order, not to restraint; demanding universal disarmament while refusing a treaty that entrenches an existing hierarchy is one consistent position, and understanding it is what connects 1988 to 1998 rather than treating them as a reversal.

#### C2. Pokhran-II

✅ **Fact:** The Vajpayee government authorised tests within weeks of taking office. On **11 May 1998** three underground tests were conducted at Pokhran, one thermonuclear with a claimed yield of about 45 kilotons; on **13 May** two further lower-yield tests generated data for computer simulation and future sub-critical experiments. Vajpayee declared India a nuclear-weapon state; unlike 1974 there was no description of the tests as peaceful. The Prithvi and Agni missiles could now carry nuclear warheads, and the scientific effort was led by A.P.J. Abdul Kalam and R. Chidambaram.

✅ **Fact:** The tests were widely welcomed domestically, and the leader of the Opposition praised the scientists while restating a commitment to a nuclear-weapons-free world. The handling afterwards was widely criticised: the Defence Minister had named China as a principal threat a week before the tests, the Prime Minister's letter to the American President naming China and Pakistan was published in the New York Times, and organisations within the governing family engaged in triumphalism that a leading national daily condemned editorially; China, whose relations with India had been improving, reacted sharply. The United States imposed sanctions immediately and several countries suspended aid, but collective G-8 action was not obtained, France, Russia and Germany continued normal economic links, and Britain, then presiding over the European Union, did not secure a strong common anti-India position. The Secretary-General of the United Nations observed publicly that an exclusive club retaining nuclear weapons could not credibly tell others not to acquire them.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** distinguish the *decision* from its *management*. One can hold that exercising the option was defensible on grounds of an inequitable global order and a deteriorating regional balance, and simultaneously hold that naming adversaries and permitting triumphalism converted a security decision into a diplomatic problem.

#### C3. Lahore and Kargil

✅ **Fact:** In early 1999 Vajpayee initiated bus diplomacy, travelling on the first bus service between India and Pakistan to Lahore. As later events showed, Pakistani army personnel and associated militants had been infiltrating across the Line of Control well before the journey. By May it emerged that Pakistani forces had intruded deep across the Line of Control and occupied key strategic heights in the Kargil sector; India mounted a very difficult and costly counter-offensive from a disadvantageous position to evict them.

✅ **Fact:** International reaction favoured India almost unanimously; the United States, Britain and China pressed Pakistan to withdraw, and Pakistan's claim that it had no regular soldiers on the Indian side was not believed. Domestically the episode raised questions about why infiltration reported from as early as August 1998 had not been acted on; a brigade commander in the sector alleged, with documentary material, that warnings had reached the highest levels, and the government was compelled to institute a high-level inquiry committee. Concern was also expressed about the appearance of senior service officers in party political settings, which sat badly with the tradition of keeping the armed forces out of politics.

```text
a genuine diplomatic opening at Lahore
            +
covert intrusion and serious questions about intelligence and preparedness
            +
a costly military response conducted under nuclear conditions
            =
dialogue remains necessary, and verification and preparedness are
indispensable; neither substitutes for the other
```

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the wrong conclusion - "Lahore proves dialogue is useless" - fails on its own terms. Kargil demonstrated a failure of *verification*: the intrusion was under way before the bus journey, so it was not caused by it, and the alternative to talking is not safety but the same intrusion without any channel to manage it. Some contemporary commentary speculated that the situation had been allowed to develop for political advantage; that allegation was not established and is not reproduced here as fact. What is established, and examinable, is the intelligence and preparedness controversy and the inquiry that followed.

### Part D - Synthesis: Mandal, Mandir, Market - and its limits

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the standard synthesis reads the decade as the dissolution of the Nehruvian consensus under three converging pressures - **Mandal**, representation and backward-caste assertion; **Mandir**, religious mobilisation and the BJP's expansion; **Market**, liberalisation and a new relationship between state and economy. Two qualifications travel with it. It is incomplete: it understates gender, regional diversity, institutional reform, post-Cold War foreign policy, security choices and welfare, and says nothing about the federalisation of the executive that was arguably the decade's most durable institutional change. And "dissolution" carries a contested judgement - critics of the decline narrative argue that the same decade *deepened* democracy by bringing subordinated castes, regional forces and market energies into the mainstream, replacing an elite consensus with genuine competition. The strongest answer holds the tension: the period was both a crisis of the old order and a democratic deepening.

### Strongest objection, reply and verdict

**Objection:** The 1990s were simply a decade of unstable governments and reactive foreign policy - four Prime Ministers between 1996 and 1999, a government defeated by one vote, a nuclear test followed within a year by a war.

**Reply:** Instability was real, and so was learning. The decade normalised alternation, brought regional parties into Union office, showed that a minority government could complete a full term, and preserved economic direction across governments of opposite complexions. Externally it produced deliberate adaptation: Look East institutionalised in 1992 and 1996, West Asia handled on multiple vectors, the nuclear option exercised on a position held since 1988, and diplomacy attempted again after nuclearisation.

**Verdict:** ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the dominant-party system dissolved into a competitive federal order while strategic policy adjusted to a post-Soviet world. Participation and external options widened; communal polarisation, opportunistic bargaining and a serious security failure were the costs, and an honest conclusion prices them in.

### UPSC application

For coalition questions, give the sequence with exact dates and always explain the 28 May / 1 June distinction. For foreign policy, use the table of directions, because breadth with named milestones beats a general statement about diversification. For nuclear questions run CTBT refusal -> Pokhran-II (11 and 13 May 1998) -> Lahore (February 1999) -> Kargil (May-July 1999) and insist on that order. For "transformation of Indian politics since the late 1980s", use Mandal-Mandir-Market as the spine, add the two qualifications, and close on the crisis-and-deepening tension rather than a one-word verdict.

### Examiner traps in this lesson

- Placing Pokhran-II after Kargil; the tests were May 1998, Lahore February 1999, Kargil May-July 1999.
- Concluding that Kargil proved dialogue useless; infiltration preceded the bus journey.
- Writing that Vajpayee first became Prime Minister in 1998; he led a short government in 1996.
- Treating "thirteen-day government" and "1 June" as contradictory.
- Presenting the 296 figure as an official electoral statistic; the Election Commission publishes party-wise results only.
- Converting the President's recorded satisfaction into an arithmetical proof; the communique supplies no alliance seat total.
- Saying India "broke" its disarmament position in 1998; the objection had always been to a discriminatory order.
- Treating Mandal-Mandir-Market as a complete explanation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two statements are offered about 1999: (a) "the NDA officially won 296 seats" and (b) "the President's communique proves the alliance held a majority of seats". Assess each, and write the formulation you would use.

**Model answer:** Statement (a) is a reported alliance aggregate, not an official statistic: the Election Commission's 1999 report publishes party-wise results - the BJP's own 182 - and recognises no alliance category, while the figure of 296, up from 253, comes from the standard survey account, which does not state the rule by which a party counted as an ally. Statement (b) misdescribes the document. The communique of 11 October 1999 records that the results notification was delivered, that the BJP was single largest party and NDA MPs the largest political group, that the NDA had unanimously elected Vajpayee its leader on 10 October, and that the Telugu Desam Party and the All India Trinamul Congress had separately communicated support; on those materials, citing Article 75(3), the President recorded satisfaction that Vajpayee would command a majority. That is a constitutional satisfaction on named documents, not a proof of a seat total, and no alliance figure appears in it. The formulation: "In 1999 the BJP's own strength was unchanged at 182, while the alliance around it grew substantially - reported at 296 seats and 41.3 per cent against 253 previously - and the President, on the election results together with the NDA leadership resolution and separate TDP and Trinamul communications, recorded his satisfaction that Vajpayee would command a majority."

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing that caution about a number means refusing to teach it. The scale of the 1999 alliance is historically important; what changes is that the figure travels with its source and its known limitation.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Evaluate how domestic coalition politics and post-Cold War foreign-policy adaptation jointly redefined India during 1996-99. (20 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Coalition politics in these years looked like instability and functioned as federalisation. Vajpayee's first ministry, sworn on 16 May 1996, announced resignation on 28 May with its formal tenure listed to 1 June; Congress then sustained and successively removed two United Front governments under Deve Gowda and Gujral, withdrawing support on 30 March 1997; the BJP-led coalition of February 1998 fell in April 1999 by a single vote when the AIADMK withdrew.

Yet the fragmentation had structural democratic roots. The Congress umbrella had weakened, Mandal had organised backward-caste politics, and regional formations now converted state mandates into Union influence. Cabinets therefore incorporated territorial and social diversity directly, and Rao's completed term had already shown that numerical weakness need not prevent governing or reverse policy.

External strategy adapted in parallel. After the Soviet collapse India rebuilt Russian and Central Asian ties, engaged the United States more closely, institutionalised Look East as an ASEAN sectoral dialogue partner in 1992 and full dialogue partner in 1996, sustained SAARC without integration, and opened relations with Israel while maintaining the Palestinian position and ties with Iran. Refusing the CTBT on the long-held objection to a discriminatory order, it tested at Pokhran on 11 and 13 May 1998, attempted confidence-building at Lahore in February 1999, and then evicted the Kargil intrusion.

By 1999 the BJP's 182 seats, a reported alliance strength of 296 and the President's Article 75(3) satisfaction produced more coherent alliance management. The decade widened federal participation and strategic options while retaining the risks of opportunistic bargaining, polarisation and security failure.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These run across lesson blocks. Attempt each from memory before reading the model answer; the misconception line names the error that most often costs marks in that block.

## Block A - Mandate, modernisation and institutions, Lessons 1-3

### Cumulative check A1 - what a landslide did and did not buy

**Question:** Track the 1984 majority through Lessons 1 to 3, stating at each stage what it was sufficient for and what it was not.

**Model answer:** At the verdict it sufficed to authorise a government - 404 of the 514 seats then polled, 415 of 543 once the deferred Punjab and Assam constituencies voted in 1985 - and did not suffice to constitute a programme, because the campaign was fought on unity and stability. In the domestic programme it sufficed to create administrative channels outside the departmental system, as mission mode did for drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils and village telephony, and did not suffice to guarantee delivery, which depended on each state's existing capacity. In legislative reform it sufficed to amend the Constitution in the Lok Sabha, producing the Fifty-second Amendment and the Tenth Schedule, and did not suffice in the Rajya Sabha, where the local-government bills failed despite consultation running from four hundred district officers in 1987-88 to eight thousand delegates in January 1989. Inside the Congress it sufficed for nothing, because organisational power lay with intermediaries controlling membership and candidate selection.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating parliamentary numbers as fungible power. They convert fully in one chamber, partially in the other and not at all inside a party organisation - and the whole trajectory of 1984-89 follows from that asymmetry.

### Cumulative check A2 - the method that outlived its author, and the Speaker's problem that began in 1985

**Question:** State what was genuinely new about mission mode, and then explain how the 1985 design created the modern vulnerability of the Speaker's office - without importing present-day doctrine.

**Model answer:** Mission mode was new in administrative form, not technology: a bounded social problem attached to a dated, measurable target with cross-departmental technical responsibility, performance judged against the target rather than expenditure - the project discipline previously reserved for atomic energy, space and defence, turned on drinking water, literacy and immunisation. Its survival is visible in the population-scale registration and real-time monitoring of present-day delivery programmes. The Speaker's vulnerability begins with the same government's other reform. The Fifty-second Amendment inserted the Tenth Schedule, recognised a split only at one-third of a legislature party, and placed the disqualification decision with the presiding officer - an office requiring impartiality while its holder remains a party member, now loaded with decisions affecting party strength in the House. No time limit was built in, so delay produces the same practical result as a decision. The historical contribution stops there; how the tension should now be resolved belongs to the Polity route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Back-projecting current rules. The two-thirds merger provision and the removal of the split exception came much later, and the one-third exception is exactly what made the original design a compromise.

## Block B - Accommodation, external activism and the erosion of authority, Lessons 4-6

### Cumulative check B1 - the consent test, at home and abroad

**Question:** Apply one test - "whose consent does this arrangement actually require?" - to the Mizoram Accord, the Assam Accord and the India-Sri Lanka Agreement.

**Model answer:** In Mizoram the required consent was the Mizo National Front leadership's, and that leadership both gave it and could deliver its armed cadre, so renunciation of violence, constitutional incorporation and entry into elected government produced durable peace. In Assam the consent was of a different kind: the agitation's leadership could accept entry-date categories anchored to 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971, but performance fell to an administrative process of determining individual status on evidence over decades, so consent had to be renewed continuously - which is why Clause 6 was still being negotiated with the Centre in 2026. In Sri Lanka the consent that mattered was never obtained: Colombo signed the July 1987 Agreement, but dissolving the LTTE and surrendering its arms could only be done by an organisation that had not signed and distrusted the signatory state. The comparison establishes that viability depends not on the authority of the party that signs but on the position of the party that must perform.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a lawful invitation or an authoritative signature as proof of feasibility. Legality settles whether a state may act; it says nothing about whether the act can succeed.

### Cumulative check B2 - four instruments, and why three good years ended in defeat

**Question:** Rank the Six-Nation Initiative, the Delhi Declaration, the 1988 Action Plan and the AFRICA Fund by the kind of commitment each represented; then explain why three peace accords and rising international standing did not prevent the 1989 defeat.

**Model answer:** The Six-Nation Five-Continent Initiative - Argentina, Greece, India, Mexico, Sweden and Tanzania - was a standing political coalition built before disarmament reached the superpower agenda. The Delhi Declaration of November 1986 was a bilateral statement of principle with Gorbachev. The Action Plan presented to the UN General Assembly's third special session in June 1988, targeting elimination of all nuclear weapons by 2010, was a staged programme in a universal forum and is the strongest evidence that the position was programmatic. The AFRICA Fund - Action for Resisting Invasion, Colonialism and Apartheid - set up at Harare in 1986 and holding about half a billion dollars by the Belgrade summit, was an actual transfer of resources. The ranking is coalition, principle, programme, transfer. None of it offset the domestic losses, because the accords addressed regional grievance rather than national standing: the Shah Bano reversal cost the reformist claim, the Ayodhya pairing the secular claim, and Bofors from 16 April 1987 the personal credibility substituting for a weak organisation. Discontent became seats only through organisation - Jan Morcha on 2 October 1987, Allahabad in June 1988, the National Front on 6 August, the Janata Dal on 11 October, and seat adjustments covering about 85 per cent of jointly contested constituencies.

**Misconception to avoid:** Merging the 1986 Declaration and the 1988 Action Plan, and assuming achievements and failures net off electorally. They do not: achievement is credited to a record, while credibility loss attacks the basis on which any record is believed.

## Block C - Fragmentation and crossing identities, Lessons 7-8

### Cumulative check C1 - the arithmetic that explains a decade

**Question:** Do the 1989 majority calculation explicitly, then state the two general propositions about Indian elections it illustrates.

**Model answer:** In a House of 543 the working majority mark is about 272. Congress held 197 seats on 39.5 per cent of the vote and was nowhere near it; the National Front held 146, the BJP 85 and the Left 52, and only their sum - 283 - clears the mark. The first proposition concerns first-past-the-post: a party's seat return depends on how its opponents are distributed as much as on its own support, which is why a seat adjustment covering roughly 85 per cent of jointly contested constituencies mattered more than any change of opinion, and why 39.5 per cent could produce a landslide in one election and defeat in another. The second concerns leverage: when survival depends on the sum of several separately negotiated relationships, a supporter's bargaining power reflects the margin it controls rather than the seats it holds, and an outside supporter enjoys that leverage without bearing the cost of any decision.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading 1989 as a swing of opinion towards the opposition. Congress's vote share remained substantial; what changed is that its opponents stopped competing against each other.

### Cumulative check C2 - two mobilisations, one electorate

**Question:** State the geometry of Mandal and Mandir in one sentence, give the evidence distinguishing crossing from substitution, and add the regional qualification a national answer needs.

**Model answer:** Mandal divided the Hindu electorate into competing status groups along a caste axis while Mandir sought to unite that same electorate across those groups along a religious axis, so the two ran at right angles and neither could reliably aggregate a national majority. The distinguishing evidence is concurrent growth: through the 1990s OBC-centred parties and Hindu nationalist organisations both expanded, frequently in the same states and among overlapping electorates, and caste continued to shape candidate selection, alliance bargaining and welfare claims even among voters responsive to religious appeals - whereas substitution would have required backward-caste parties to contract as religious mobilisation grew. The regional qualification is that Mandal was not uniform: it transformed the Hindi belt, where no prior transfer of political leadership to backward castes had occurred, and had far less purchase in the south, where non-Brahmin and Dravidian mobilisation had already carried those groups into office, administration and education decades earlier. That is also why the violent anti-Mandal protest was overwhelmingly northern.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using the BJP's later national success as retrospective proof that religion defeated caste. The same decade produced the caste-based parties that made coalition arithmetic unavoidable.

## Block D - Rupture and coalition normalisation, Lessons 9-10

### Cumulative check D1 - the calendar as an argument

**Question:** Three chronological errors recur: that the assassination caused the 1991 election, that Pokhran-II followed Kargil, and that V.P. Singh was succeeded by Rao. Give the dated correction for each and the analytical claim each error destroys.

**Model answer:** The election was caused by Congress withdrawing support from the Chandra Shekhar ministry on 5 March 1991; the first polling round was held on 20 May and was complete when the assassination occurred on 21 May, after which the remaining phases were postponed to June. That error destroys the ability to discuss a sympathy effect honestly, since a claim confined to the later phases is defensible while a claim about the whole result is refuted by the calendar. Pokhran-II was conducted on 11 and 13 May 1998, Lahore bus diplomacy followed in February 1999, and the Kargil intrusion was evicted between May and July 1999; that error destroys the point that nuclearisation was followed by an attempt at dialogue and then by a failure of verification. Between V.P. Singh and Rao stands Chandra Shekhar, Prime Minister from 10 November 1990 after the Janata Dal split of 5 November, on external Congress support; that error destroys the causal chain entirely, because without that ministry there is no explanation of why an election was being held in May 1991.

**Misconception to avoid:** Thinking chronology is presentational. Each date carries a causal claim, and inverting any one licenses a specific analytical mistake.

### Cumulative check D2 - two evidentiary tests, and what the coalition decade institutionalised

**Question:** Bofors and hawala produced opposite outcomes; show how one discipline handles both. Then name the institutional changes the 1996-99 period made permanent, including the one a "decline" narrative usually misses.

**Model answer:** The discipline is a four-rung ladder - allegation, institutional controversy, political consequence, criminal liability - each requiring different evidence and none implying the next. Bofors: allegations reported on Swedish Radio on 16 April 1987 about the contract for 410 howitzer guns; a Joint Parliamentary Committee clean chit alongside a Comptroller and Auditor General report questioning the selection procedure; severe political damage used to great effect in 1989; and no criminal finding asserted here. Hawala: mid-1990s charges against many Congress and opposition leaders; real cross-party political damage; charges later found almost entirely unsustainable. Four changes then became permanent after 1996: that being the largest party creates an opportunity to be invited and no entitlement to survive, as the thirteen days from 16 May to the resignation announcement of 28 May showed; that outside support and its withdrawal are routine instruments, used by Congress against two United Front ministries and by the AIADMK in April 1999; that coalition craft - pre-election alliances, common programmes, coordination machinery - replaced post-election improvisation; and, most often missed, that coalition government federalised the Union executive, seating state-based parties in the Union Cabinet.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing evidentiary restraint is scepticism about events, or equating the number of governments with the quality of governance.

## Block E - Final synthesis

### Cumulative check E1 - the graded verdict

**Question:** Write, in six sentences, the qualified verdict on 1984-99 that an examiner should be able to extract from this session.

**Model answer:** A record majority in 1984 showed that democratic authority can be conferred overwhelmingly and still rest on institutionally shallow foundations. The government used that space well where a rule could be written or a settlement negotiated - mission-mode development, the Tenth Schedule, the Punjab, Assam and Mizoram accords, activist disarmament and anti-apartheid diplomacy - and badly where authority had to be redistributed or consent secured, as the failed Congress renewal and the Sri Lankan intervention show. Between 1985 and 1989 two identity decisions and one arms contract destroyed the credibility on which the project rested, and an organised opposition converted that loss into seats. From 1989 the system reorganised around two crossing cleavages, caste differentiation and religious aggregation, neither of which could reliably produce a national majority, so coalition government became the norm. The 1991 rupture showed that a crisis could carry a policy revolution three decades of argument had not, and that a minority government could complete a full term, while the Babri demolition of 6 December 1992 showed that constitutional protection could fail in the same years. By 1999 India had a competitive federal order, an alliance-management repertoire, a declared nuclear capability, a diversified external strategy and unresolved costs in polarisation, opaque bargaining and security failure.

**Misconception to avoid:** An undifferentiated verdict such as "the period weakened Indian democracy" or "deepened it". Both are supported on some dimensions and refuted on others, and naming the dimensions is what converts a competent answer into a strong one.

### Cumulative check E2 - the synthesis and its limits

**Question:** "Mandal, Mandir and Market" is the standard summary of the period. State what it captures, what it omits, and why "dissolution" is contested.

**Model answer:** It captures three simultaneous transformative pressures - the organisation of backward-caste representation after 7 August 1990, the religious mobilisation running from the rath yatra of 25 September 1990 to the demolition of 6 December 1992, and the liberalisation begun under a minority government in 1991 - and explains why they interacted rather than succeeded one another. It omits a great deal: gender, which enters through the 1988 women's plan and the local-government reservations; regional diversity, since the triad is largely a north Indian story; institutional change, including the Tenth Schedule and the constitutionalisation of local government; the whole of post-Cold War foreign policy from Look East to the CTBT refusal and Pokhran-II; welfare provision; and the federalisation of the Union executive. "Dissolution" is contested because it embeds a judgement: read as decline, the period marks the unravelling of the Nehruvian consensus, but critics argue the same events deepened democracy by bringing subordinated castes, regional forces and market energies into the mainstream. The strongest answer holds both.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using the triad as a complete explanation because it is memorable. It is an organising device, and an examiner rewards the candidate who uses it and then names what it leaves out.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Question 1 - 10 marks, 150 words

**Why did Rajiv Gandhi's modernising achievements fail to secure a second majority in 1989?**

### Model answer

The government did change India's developmental imagination. Six technology missions tied coordinated science and administration to drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils and village telephony; C-DOT and computer policy built communications capability; the 1986 education policy, Operation Blackboard, Navodaya Vidyalayas and the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana widened the social agenda.

None of this repaired the foundations of rule. The Congress organisation remained centralised and dependent on intermediaries whom the 1985 centenary speech criticised without displacing. The Shah Bano reversal of 1986 and the opening of the Ayodhya locks rewarded rival identity mobilisations, and the Bofors allegations of April 1987 destroyed the personal credibility compensating for organisational weakness.

Organisation then converted discontent into seats: Jan Morcha in October 1987, Allahabad in June 1988, the National Front and Janata Dal later that year, and seat adjustments covering about 85 per cent of jointly contested constituencies.

Policy modernisation therefore survived Rajiv Gandhi; political trust did not.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = named achievements 3 + the three credibility losses 3 + opposition construction with dates 2 + verdict distinguishing record from trust 2.**

## Question 2 - 10 marks, 150 words

**Examine the claim that the accords of 1985-86 represented a distinctive approach to Indian federalism.**

### Model answer

They were distinctive in method rather than generosity. Within roughly a year the Union concluded the Punjab Accord of July 1985 with Sant Longowal, the Assam Accord of 15 August 1985 and the Mizoram Accord of June 1986, treating three regional claims as negotiable within the constitutional order instead of as threats to it.

The method was consistent: recognise the grievance, identify lawful interlocutors, separate negotiable demands from coercion, record reciprocal commitments, and bring movement actors into elections. The instruments differed because the conflicts did - a territorial and resource package in Punjab, entry-date categories anchored to 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971 in Assam, and renunciation of violence for constitutional status in Mizoram.

Outcomes diverged at implementation. Mizoram endured; Punjab's commitments were contested and Longowal was assassinated; Assam's Clause 6 safeguards remained under negotiation decades later.

The approach was therefore asymmetric but constitutional federalism - genuinely distinctive, and only as durable as its delivery.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = the three accords dated 2 + the shared method stated as a sequence 3 + differentiated instruments 3 + implementation-dependent verdict 2.**

## Question 3 - 15 marks, 250 words

**Examine the proposition that the Rajiv years combined a modernising state with a weakening political centre.**

### Model answer

The modernising half is well evidenced. Six technology missions applied dated targets and cross-departmental expertise to drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils and village telephony; C-DOT and computer policy built communications capability on the argument that India could not afford to miss a second technological transition; the National Policy on Education, 1986, Operation Blackboard, Navodaya Vidyalayas, the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana and the National Perspective Plan for Women of 1988 extended the agenda to schooling, rural employment and women's representation. The Fifty-second Amendment of 1985 addressed legislative instability, the Punjab, Assam and Mizoram accords demonstrated a capacity for negotiated federal settlement, and the Action Plan presented to the United Nations in June 1988 gave India's disarmament claim a programmatic form.

The political centre nevertheless weakened, largely by the same government's hand. The Congress remained dependent on personal leadership: the centenary attack on "power-brokers" in December 1985 altered no rule and no appointment, and over-centralisation with reliance on a small circle of advisers reproduced the previous decade's de-institutionalisation without the mass authority that had sustained it. Panchayati Raj constitutionalisation failed in the Rajya Sabha. Shah Bano and the Ayodhya locks were handled as balancing concessions and energised both mobilisations. The 1987 Agreement drew India into combat in Sri Lanka on an untested assumption about LTTE consent. Bofors then removed the credibility advantage on which everything rested.

The paradox is therefore real and causally connected: state ambition became more technological while the authority to sustain it was spent faster than it was rebuilt.

**Unique scoring note:** **15 marks = modernisation evidence across missions, social policy and institutions 5 + weakening evidence across party, identity, foreign policy and credibility 5 + the continuity-with-the-previous-decade argument 2 + causal integration rather than two lists 2 + verdict 1.**

## Question 4 - 15 marks, 250 words

**"Negotiated accommodation strengthened Indian unity more than coercion ever did." Critically examine with reference to 1985-86.**

### Model answer

The proposition is substantially supportable. Three conflicts that had defeated a decade of coercive management were addressed by negotiation within about a year. The Mizoram Accord of June 1986 is the strongest evidence: an insurgency ended because renunciation of violence was exchanged for credible constitutional status and entry into elected government, and the peace has held. The Assam Accord of 15 August 1985 converted a mass agitation into legal categories anchored to 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971 with safeguards for Assamese identity, and restored an electoral route. The Punjab Accord of July 1985 attempted the same conversion for territorial, river-water and autonomy disputes.

Three qualifications must be recorded. First, accommodation is not self-executing: Punjab's commitments were contested and delayed, and Longowal's assassination shortly after signing removed the leader on whose authority the agreement depended, strengthening those who had argued that negotiation yields nothing. Second, an instrument can create work rather than finish it - Assam's date categories required decades of evidence-based individual determination, and its Clause 6 safeguards were still being negotiated with the Centre in 2026. Third, negotiation has preconditions, requiring an interlocutor who can deliver, which is why Mizoram succeeded and why the Sri Lankan agreement of 1987, concluded with a party that could not perform it, failed.

The defensible verdict is conditional: accommodation strengthened unity wherever it was implemented, and where implementation lapsed it did not merely fail but actively rewarded the harder faction.

**Unique scoring note:** **15 marks = the three settlements with exact dates and instruments 4 + Mizoram as the strongest case 3 + the three qualifications 4 + the precondition argument using Sri Lanka as contrast 2 + conditional verdict 2.**

## Question 5 - 20 marks, 250 words

**"Between 1989 and 1999, India moved from a dominant-party system to a competitive federal order." Critically evaluate.**

### Model answer

The 1989 verdict was more than an alternation. Congress remained largest with 197 seats and 39.5 per cent of the vote, yet declined to attempt formation, and government became possible only as a sum - the National Front's 146 with the BJP's 85 and the Left's 52 supporting separately from outside. Mandal, announced on 7 August 1990, organised backward-caste politics and produced the state-level parties of the coalition era, while the rath yatra from 25 September 1990 expanded the BJP across northern India. Because those cleavages crossed, neither reliably aggregated a majority.

The 1991 Congress government began in a minority, completed five years and initiated liberalisation, showing that policy continuity no longer required single-party dominance. In 1996 the largest party could not prove a majority within thirteen days; United Front ministries then depended on regional formations and outside Congress support, withdrawn on 30 March 1997. The 1998 coalition fell by one vote in April 1999, after which a pre-election alliance and agreed coordination produced a more durable arrangement.

The critical evaluation runs three ways. This was democratic federalisation, because coalition cabinets brought state-based parties into Union executive power. It was costly, through unstable support, opaque bargaining and severe communal polarisation, of which the demolition of 6 December 1992 was the worst instance. And national parties did not disappear: the BJP became a second pole and Congress remained substantial.

The decade therefore replaced one-party predominance not with permanent fragmentation but with competitive alliance politics.

**Unique scoring note:** **20 marks = 1989 arithmetic and the outside-support form 4 + Mandal and Mandir as crossing cleavages 4 + the 1991-99 chronology with exact dates 5 + three-way critical evaluation including costs and surviving national parties 4 + qualified verdict 3.**

## Question 6 - 20 marks, 250 words

**Assess how far India's foreign and security policy between 1984 and 1999 represented continuity rather than change.**

### Model answer

Continuity is the stronger claim on the nuclear question. From Nehru onwards India argued for universal disarmament while preserving technological readiness, tested in 1974, and pressed the case hardest through the Six-Nation Five-Continent Initiative of Argentina, Greece, India, Mexico, Sweden and Tanzania, the Delhi Declaration of November 1986 and the Action Plan presented to the United Nations in June 1988 with a target of elimination by 2010. When the CTBT was pressed on non-nuclear states without any reciprocal disarmament commitment, India refused it as it had refused the NPT. Pokhran-II on 11 and 13 May 1998 therefore exercised an option every intervening government had preserved, on an objection to a discriminatory order held consistently for decades.

Continuity also marks the wider posture. Strategic autonomy survived the Soviet collapse: Russian and Central Asian ties were rebuilt, engagement with the United States deepened without alignment, ties with Israel were opened while the Palestinian position and relations with Iran were maintained, and support for a plural order continued through NAM.

Change was nonetheless real. Look East institutionalised eastward engagement through ASEAN sectoral dialogue partnership in 1992 and full dialogue partnership in 1996, correcting a recognised late start. Economic capability replaced ideological position as the declared basis of influence after 1991. And the neighbourhood record shifted sharply - from the Sri Lankan intervention of 1987 to the Lahore initiative of February 1999 and the Kargil conflict that followed.

The period is therefore best read as constant objectives pursued through substantially reordered instruments.

**Unique scoring note:** **20 marks = the disarmament chain from the Six-Nation Initiative to 1998 with dates 5 + the discriminatory-order argument that makes 1988 and 1998 consistent 4 + strategic-autonomy continuity across four directions 4 + genuine changes named with milestones 4 + continuity-through-reordered-instruments verdict 3.**

## Corrective drills

Each drill gives the flawed prompt, the formulation that should replace it, and why the error fails. Rehearse the middle column aloud; it is the sentence you want under time pressure.

### Drill 1 - the 1984 denominators

**Prompt as commonly written:** "Congress won 404 seats in a 543-seat poll in December 1984."

**Correct formulation:** "Congress won 404 of the 514 seats polled in December 1984, because polling in Punjab and Assam was deferred; once those constituencies voted in 1985 the Congress total in the Eighth Lok Sabha stood at 415 of 543."

**Why the error fails:** it pairs a numerator bounded to one round with the denominator of a completed House. The two figures answer different questions, and stating both with the deferral explained is what demonstrates command of the fact.

### Drill 2 - local government

**Prompt as commonly written:** "Rajiv Gandhi's majority gave India the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments."

**Correct formulation:** "Rajiv Gandhi initiated constitutional local-government reform through an exceptionally wide consultation from 1987 to 1989, but the bills failed in the Rajya Sabha, where the government lacked a majority; the frameworks were enacted later, as the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments, under Narasimha Rao."

**Why the error fails:** it treats a majority in one House as sufficient for constitutional amendment and credits enactment to a government that did not achieve it - erasing the more interesting point, that the agenda survived the government that created it.

### Drill 3 - the evidence ladder

**Prompt as commonly written:** "Bofors proved the government's corruption, as the 1989 result confirmed."

**Correct formulation:** "Allegations reported from 16 April 1987 concerning the howitzer contract, together with a CAG report questioning the selection procedure, inflicted severe political damage that the Opposition used effectively in 1989 - while a Joint Parliamentary Committee report gave a clean chit and no criminal finding against a named individual is asserted here."

**Why the error fails:** it climbs two rungs of the ladder without evidence. An audit observation is not an adjudication and an electoral verdict measures trust rather than liability, so the sentence can be demolished by one counter-example while the corrected version cannot.

### Drill 4 - the 1989 support architecture

**Prompt as commonly written:** "The BJP and the Left joined the National Front coalition in 1989."

**Correct formulation:** "The Janata Dal-led National Front held 146 seats and formed the ministry; the BJP's 85 and the Left's 52 sustained it through separately negotiated outside support, neither entering the cabinet nor sharing a programme with the other."

**Why the error fails:** cabinet membership entails collective responsibility and resignation on withdrawal; outside support entails neither. The error therefore makes November 1990 inexplicable, because it cannot account for the BJP withdrawing support without resigning from anything.

### Drill 5 - crossing axes

**Prompt as commonly written:** "After 1990 religious mobilisation replaced caste politics in north India."

**Correct formulation:** "Through the 1990s OBC-centred parties and Hindu nationalist organisations expanded concurrently, often in the same states and among overlapping electorates, and caste continued to shape nominations and alliances even among voters responsive to religious appeals - so the two mobilisations crossed rather than one replacing the other."

**Why the error fails:** it asserts substitution while the evidence shows simultaneous growth, and it cannot then explain why no single cleavage delivered a national majority - the fact that made the coalition decade necessary.

### Drill 6 - the 1991 sequence

**Prompt as commonly written:** "Rajiv Gandhi's assassination led to the 1991 election, which Congress won."

**Correct formulation:** "Congress withdrew support from the Chandra Shekhar ministry on 5 March 1991, the first polling round was held on 20 May and had been completed when Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated on 21 May; the remaining phases were postponed to June, Congress emerged largest with 232 seats, and Rao formed an initially minority government on 21 June."

**Why the error fails:** it reverses cause and interruption and silently deletes a Prime Minister. Restoring the Chandra Shekhar ministry and the withdrawal of 5 March gives the election a cause and confines the sympathy claim honestly to the later phases.

### Drill 7 - the 1999 alliance

**Prompt as commonly written:** "The NDA officially won 296 seats in 1999, and the President's communique proves the alliance held that majority."

**Correct formulation:** "In 1999 the BJP's own strength was unchanged at 182 in the Election Commission's party-wise report, while the alliance around it grew substantially - reported at 296 seats and 41.3 per cent of the vote, against 253 previously - and the President, on the results notification received from the Election Commission together with the NDA resolution electing Vajpayee its leader and separate support communications from the Telugu Desam Party and the All India Trinamul Congress, recorded under Article 75(3) that he was satisfied Vajpayee would command a majority."

**Why the error fails:** the Election Commission publishes party-wise results and recognises no alliance category, so no alliance figure can be "official"; and the communique states a constitutional satisfaction reached on named materials, not a seat arithmetic. The corrected version keeps the scale and the appointment reasoning, and attaches the provenance of each.

### Drill 8 - the security chain

**Prompt as commonly written:** "Kargil showed that talking to Pakistan is pointless, which is why India tested at Pokhran."

**Correct formulation:** "India refused the CTBT because its disarmament commitment had been excluded, tested at Pokhran on 11 and 13 May 1998, attempted confidence-building at Lahore in February 1999, and then evicted the Kargil intrusion between May and July 1999 - an intrusion already under way before the bus journey, which makes it a failure of verification and preparedness rather than of dialogue."

**Why the error fails:** it inverts the chronology and converts a verification failure into an argument against negotiation. Since the infiltration preceded the Lahore initiative, the initiative cannot have caused it, and abandoning dialogue would have removed the channel without removing the intrusion.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Period thesis

- 1984-99 converted a record single-party majority into a competitive federal order.
- Rajiv Gandhi modernised state ambition and method faster than the institutions of political trust.
- Negotiated accommodation and technological capability were the durable gains; identity balancing, Sri Lanka and Bofors drained authority.
- Mandal, Mandir, Market and regional assertion reshaped competition; federalisation of the Union executive was the least-noticed consequence.

## 2. Succession and the 1984 verdict

- Prime Minister from 31 October 1984; succession preceded authorisation.
- Anti-Sikh violence followed; individual criminal liability and the state's protective obligation are separate propositions.
- December result: Congress 404 of 514 polled; Punjab and Assam deferred; final tally 415 of 543 after the 1985 polls.
- Mandate sources: sympathy, fear for unity, opposition division, a modernising image.
- Crisis-born majority is not organisational depth.

## 3. Modernisation: missions, capability, method

- Six missions: drinking water, literacy, immunisation, milk, edible oils, village telephones.
- Mission mode: bounded problem, dated measurable target, cross-departmental expertise, monitored delivery.
- C-DOT and Sam Pitroda; computer policy eased components, production and institutional use; the strategic argument was against missing a second technological transition.
- NPE 1986; Operation Blackboard; Navodaya Vidyalayas - free, merit-selected, residential, one per district.
- Jawahar Rozgar Yojana: 50-100 days for one member of every rural poor family; Union bore the larger share; Nehru birth centenary.
- National Perspective Plan for Women, 1988: proposed 30 per cent of panchayat seats for women, half of grassroots functionaries.
- Environment: dedicated ministry, mandatory clearance, the Ganga programme; Planet Protection Fund proposed at NAM.
- Rajiv-era measures anticipated liberalisation; 1991 was the rupture.

## 4. Institutional record and the organisation problem

- Fifty-second Amendment, 1985 inserted the Tenth Schedule; drafted after opposition consultation.
- Original split exception one-third; original adjudicator the presiding officer; no time limit.
- Trade-off: stability gained; dissent and adjudicator-neutrality costs created.
- Lok Adalats and consumer protection belong to the same accountability stream.
- Panchayati Raj consultation: 400 district officers 1987-88; chief secretaries July 1988; 8,000 delegates January 1989; women's conference and AICC May 1989.
- Bills failed in the Rajya Sabha; 73rd and 74th Amendments came later under Rao.
- Centenary speech December 1985; no internal democratisation; older intermediaries returned by early 1989.
- Continuity thesis: over-centralisation and adviser dependence reproduced earlier de-institutionalisation without its mass authority.

## 5. The accords

- Punjab Accord July 1985 with Sant Longowal, assassinated soon after.
- Assam Accord 15 August 1985; cut-offs 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971; identity safeguards; Clause 6 still under negotiation in 2026.
- Mizoram Accord June 1986; renunciation of violence for constitutional incorporation; the durable success.
- Sequence: recognise, identify interlocutors, separate demands from coercion, record commitments, enter elections, implement.
- Legal status, not identity, determines citizenship; non-implementation strengthens the harder faction.

## 6. Foreign policy and security to 1989

- Six-Nation Five-Continent Initiative: Argentina, Greece, India, Mexico, Sweden, Tanzania; formed by Indira Gandhi; first summit within a month of the 1984 election.
- Delhi Declaration with Gorbachev, November 1986. Action Plan at the UN third special session on disarmament, June 1988; elimination target 2010.
- AFRICA Fund - Action for Resisting Invasion, Colonialism and Apartheid - Harare 1986; about half a billion dollars by Belgrade. SWAPO recognised; frontline states visited May 1986; Namibia independent 1990.
- United States 1985 visit and the supercomputer; Gorbachev met eight times in five years; China 1988, first Indian Prime Minister since 1954; India did not condemn Tiananmen.
- Kampuchea: India brokered modalities for the Vietnamese withdrawal from January 1987; NAM given a disarmament purpose; G-15 and Planet Protection Fund proposed.
- Nepal: economic blockade March 1989, talks from September, settled by the successor government. Pakistan: first prime-ministerial visit after Nehru; no breakthrough. Maldives: invited, bounded, rapid.
- India-Sri Lanka Agreement July 1987: merged northern and eastern provinces, devolution, LTTE dissolution and arms surrender, Indian assistance on request. The LTTE was not a signatory, consented reluctantly, refused to disarm; IPKF slid into counter-insurgency; withdrawal began mid-1989, completed after the 1989 elections.
- Defence spending roughly doubled; Trishul, Prithvi and Agni tested; submarine lease, second carrier, Swedish howitzers, indigenous tank sanctioned.

## 7. Decline, 1985-89

- Shah Bano judgment 1985; Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986; Arif Mohammad Khan resigned. Ayodhya locks opened 1986; politically paired, legally distinct; balancing rewarded the rival mobilisation.
- Fairfax and HDW preceded Bofors; V.P. Singh moved from Finance to Defence and then resigned.
- Bofors: 16 April 1987, Swedish Radio, about sixty crore rupees, 410 howitzer guns; JPC clean chit; CAG doubts on procedure; no criminal finding asserted. A mid-1987 attempt to dismiss the Prime Minister through the President did not proceed.
- 1987 drought handled at scale; growth about 5.5 per cent a year over the term.
- Jan Morcha 2 October 1987; Allahabad by-election June 1988; National Front 6 August 1988; Janata Dal 11 October 1988; seat adjustment about 85 per cent.

## 8. The National Front and the 1990 crisis

- 1989: Congress 197 and 39.5 per cent; National Front 146 (Janata Dal itself 143); BJP 85; Left 52; 283 against a mark of about 272. Outside support, not cabinet membership; no effective common minimum programme.
- V.P. Singh sworn 2 December 1989 with Devi Lal as Deputy Prime Minister; Sri Lanka withdrawal completed; Nepal dispute settled; Jagmohan's appointment prompted Farooq Abdullah's resignation; Devi Lal dismissed 1 August 1990.
- Mandal announced 7 August 1990: 27 per cent for the backward classes; 22.5 per cent existing; 49.5 per cent total; second stage on education and promotions envisaged.
- Criticism: timing, absence of consensus-building, divisiveness, outdated identification criteria; economic criteria urged by the CPI(M) and others.
- Protest concentrated in north India; firing in Delhi, Gorakhpur, Varanasi, Kanpur; self-immolation attempts from mid-September; Supreme Court stay 1 October 1990.
- Regional variation: founding event in the Hindi belt; incremental in a south mobilised for decades.
- Rath yatra from Somnath 25 September 1990, about six thousand miles; arrest at Samastipur 23 October; BJP withdrawal; firing near Ayodhya 30 October; Janata Dal split 5 November; government ended 7 November.
- Mandal divided by caste; Mandir sought to aggregate by religion; concurrent growth proves crossing, not substitution.

## 9. Rupture and the Rao term

- Chandra Shekhar Prime Minister 10 November 1990 on external Congress support; not a coalition; Congress withdrew 5 March 1991.
- First polling round held and completed on 20 May; Rajiv Gandhi assassinated at Sriperumbudur 21 May; LTTE militants held responsible; later phases postponed to June.
- Congress 232; Rao formed an initially minority government 21 June 1991; acquired working strength; completed five years.
- Balance-of-payments crisis; reform with Manmohan Singh; measures belong to Economy; mechanism - crisis suspends a veto consensus never overcame.
- First year lowered caste and communal temperature; Punjab substantially normalised; Kashmir and Assam improved without settlement; momentum slowed after 1994.
- Corruption and hawala charges damaged credibility but were later found almost entirely unsustainable. Babri Masjid demolished 6 December 1992; the government failed to prevent it or the rioting.

## 10. Coalition decade and external adaptation

- 1996: Congress 140; BJP largest at 161, up from 120 in 1991; Vajpayee sworn 16 May, resignation announced 28 May, tenure listed to 1 June.
- United Front under Deve Gowda with Congress and CPI(M) support; CPI joined; Indrajit Gupta first Communist Home Minister; Congress withdrew 30 March 1997; Gujral followed; elections February 1998.
- 1998: BJP 182 with TDP, AIADMK, Trinamool and others; AIADMK withdrawal; confidence motion lost by one vote April 1999; caretaker until September-October polls.
- 1999: BJP unchanged at 182; alliance reported at 296 seats and 41.3 per cent against 253 previously; the Election Commission publishes party-wise results only; the President's communique of 11 October records the largest-group position, the NDA resolution, separate TDP and Trinamul support and his Article 75(3) satisfaction, with no alliance seat total.
- Coalition craft: pre-election alliances, common programmes, coordination; coalition government federalised the Union executive.
- Soviet collapse and liberalisation coincided in 1991; Russia and Central Asia rebuilt; United States engaged without alignment.
- ASEAN sectoral dialogue partnership 1992; full dialogue partnership 1996; SAARC continued without integration; Indian Ocean grouping including South Africa.
- West Asia: Israel ties opened while the Palestinian position and relations with Iran were maintained; India declined to join the ostracism of Iraq and Iran; NAM supported at the Jakarta summit.
- NPT rejected and CTBT refused because a disarmament commitment was excluded; first test 1974; readiness preserved under Rajiv Gandhi, Rao, Deve Gowda and Gujral; Rao close to testing in 1995.
- Pokhran-II: three tests 11 May 1998, one thermonuclear with a claimed 45-kiloton yield; two more on 13 May; India declared a nuclear-weapon state; Kalam and Chidambaram led the effort.
- Criticism attached to the handling: naming adversaries, the letter published abroad, public triumphalism; United States sanctions; several countries suspended aid; G-8 collective action failed.
- Lahore bus diplomacy February 1999; Kargil intrusion discovered and evicted May-July 1999; international opinion favoured India; a high-level inquiry followed; unproved allegations of deliberate political timing are not reproduced.
- Mandal, Mandir and Market is useful but incomplete; the decline reading is contested by a democratic-deepening reading.

## 11. Debate grid

| Debate | Strong claim | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Rajiv as moderniser | Altered the technology and development aspirations of the state | Implementation and institution-building lagged |
| Identity politics | Elite balancing intensified both mobilisations | Wider social and legal disputes had deeper roots |
| Mandal | Democratised representation in employment and politics | Classification criteria and scarce-opportunity conflict were real problems |
| Mandir | Created a second national political pole | Produced deep and lasting communal polarisation |
| Market | Produced a durable change no government reversed | Crisis-driven, without prior consent or a protection framework |
| Coalitions | Federalised national executive power | Opaque bargaining, small-ally leverage, survival over policy |
| Nuclear choice | Consistent with a long-held objection to a discriminatory order | Post-test handling converted security into a diplomatic problem |

## 12. Rapid answer conclusions

- **Rajiv:** "He modernised the state's ambitions faster than he renewed the institutions of political trust."
- **Accords:** "Accommodation strengthens unity only where signature is followed by credible implementation."
- **Sri Lanka:** "An invitation settles the legality of entry and nothing about the feasibility of the task."
- **Mandal-Mandir:** "Crossing caste and religious mobilisations widened participation while preventing easy national aggregation."
- **1991:** "A crisis carried an economic rupture that three decades of argument had not."
- **Coalitions:** "The 1990s converted regional diversity from parliamentary arithmetic into executive power."
- **1999:** "India ended the century with a competitive federal order, a declared nuclear capability and an unfinished argument about the costs of both."
