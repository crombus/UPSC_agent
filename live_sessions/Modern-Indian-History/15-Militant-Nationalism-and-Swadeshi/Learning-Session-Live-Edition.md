# Militant Nationalism and the Swadeshi Movement, 1903–1911

> This complete live edition follows one connected question: how did an agitation against an administrative decision become a laboratory of mass anti-colonial politics?

## Roadmap

1. Why militant nationalism grew out of, yet moved beyond, Moderate politics
2. Curzon, the Partition of Bengal and the anti-partition turn
3. Boycott, Swadeshi, indigenous enterprise and national education
4. Samitis, passive resistance, constructive work and uneven social participation
5. Regional spread and the distinct political roles of Lal-Bal-Pal and Aurobindo
6. Moderate–Extremist tensions and the Surat split
7. Colonial repression, constitutional manoeuvre, annulment and the capital shift
8. Achievements, limits, communal questions, historiography and legacy

The sequence is dependency-led. First understand why the old method appeared insufficient; then follow the trigger, programme, organisation, leadership, conflict, state response and long-term significance. Open militant agitation must be distinguished from clandestine revolutionary organisations and armed operations. Later Gandhian mass movements are used only for careful comparison.

The visual shows that militant nationalism was not a sudden emotional outburst. It was a new answer to a problem created by the earlier phase: if colonial rule caused poverty and repeatedly rejected reform, why should appeals to colonial goodwill remain the principal strategy?

### Begin with the intuition

Imagine a group that has spent years proving an injustice, submitting petitions and requesting limited reform. Its own arguments succeed intellectually: more people now understand the injustice. Yet the authority being petitioned restricts rights instead of transferring power. The group's success in political education then exposes the weakness of its method.

That is the bridge from Moderate to militant nationalism. The militants inherited the Moderates' critique of drain, poverty and racial exclusion. They disputed the **theory of change**—the belief that British liberal conscience could gradually reform colonial rule.

### The causal foundations

1. **Recognition of colonial purpose.** Nationalists increasingly connected Indian poverty and industrial weakness with imperial extraction. If exploitation was structural, reform from within appeared inadequate.
2. **Disappointing constitutional experience.** The Indian Councils Act of 1892 offered discussion and representation without responsible power.
3. **Attack on existing space.** Curzon's period included reduced Indian influence in the Calcutta Corporation, the Indian Universities Act of 1904 and a wider official hostility to nationalist opinion. The issue was no longer simply that reforms were slow; even limited public space seemed insecure.
4. **Material distress.** Famines, plague administration, unemployment among educated Indians and low-paid employment sharpened discontent. These conditions did not mechanically create nationalism, but they widened the audience for a critique of colonial rule.
5. **International confidence.** Ethiopia's defeat of Italy at Adwa in 1896 and Japan's victory over Russia in 1905 weakened the myth of automatic European superiority. Japan showed that an Asian society could modernise without Western political control.
6. **Cultural and moral self-assertion.** Bankim Chandra's patriotic symbolism, Vivekananda's language of strength and Tilak's political use of public festivals helped convert grievance into confidence.
7. **A trained political generation.** The earlier Congress had created newspapers, associations, public speakers and an all-India vocabulary. Militancy could therefore expand an existing movement rather than begin from zero.

### What “militant” meant

**Militant nationalism** meant assertive, self-reliant and sacrifice-oriented politics. Its central propositions were:

- political freedom or **swaraj** must become the governing goal;
- Indians must rely primarily on their own organised strength;
- politics must continue beyond annual Congress sessions;
- a broader public must be mobilised;
- boycott, passive resistance and constructive work could impose costs on colonial rule.

It did **not** mean that every militant nationalist advocated secret assassination. Open mass agitation and underground revolutionary action overlapped historically, but they were different strategies: one sought public mobilisation and organised withdrawal; the other relied on clandestine cells and selective armed action.

### Example—and its limit

Tilak's Ganapati and Shivaji festivals created public spaces for nationalist speech where ordinary political meetings faced restrictions. They show how inherited cultural forms could acquire a modern political function.

**Limit:** a festival does not automatically produce inclusive mass politics. Its symbols may communicate powerfully to one audience and weakly—or ambiguously—to another. Cultural mobilisation must therefore be judged by both energy and reach.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Extremism” was merely youthful impatience and rhetoric; Moderate constitutional politics remained the only practical course.

**Reply:** impatience alone cannot explain a coherent programme of boycott, national education, self-reliance and passive resistance. Yet the objection identifies a real weakness: militants generated political energy more successfully than a durable all-India organisation capable of directing it.

### UPSC use

- **Causation answer:** classify roots into ideological, political, economic, international and cultural factors.
- **Comparison answer:** show continuity in colonial critique but rupture in goal, agency and method.
- **Trap:** do not write “Moderates failed, therefore Extremists appeared.” Explain the intermediate mechanism: Moderate political education made colonial obstruction more visible.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** Explain the roots of militant nationalism in India at the beginning of the twentieth century. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

Militant nationalism emerged from continuity in anti-colonial analysis and rupture in political method. Moderate leaders had exposed drain, poverty and racial discrimination, but the limited Councils Act of 1892 and official attacks on civic space weakened faith in reform through British goodwill. Curzon's municipal, university and anti-Congress policies intensified this disillusionment. Famines, plague conditions and educated unemployment widened social frustration, while the spread of modern education produced politically articulate young Indians. International events supplied confidence: Ethiopia's victory over Italy and Japan's victory over Russia challenged the myth of European invincibility. Bankim, Vivekananda and Tilak encouraged cultural self-respect, strength and sacrifice. Militants therefore advocated swaraj, self-reliance, continuous agitation, boycott and wider mobilisation. They did not reject the Moderates' economic critique; they argued that a colonial state would yield only to organised Indian pressure. Their weakness was the absence of a disciplined organisation capable of sustaining that pressure nationally.

The dispute was not whether Bengal posed administrative difficulties. The crucial question was why the chosen boundaries divided Bengali-speaking people and politically weakened Calcutta when other administrative rearrangements were possible.

### The precise regional scheme

The Government of India **announced the decision on 19 July 1905**; the formal order is commonly dated **20 July**. Keeping these adjacent dates distinct avoids treating a one-day documentary difference as a different political event. The new province of **Eastern Bengal and Assam**, with **Dacca** as its capital, had roughly **31 million** people in the contemporary account; the remaining Bengal had roughly **54 million**, including about **18 million Bengalis** and **36 million Biharis and Oriyas**. Another contemporary demographic formulation described the eastern province as having about **18 million Muslims and 12 million Hindus**.

These numbers explain the politics:

- Bengali speakers were placed under two administrations.
- Bengalis became a minority in the western unit containing large Hindi- and Oriya-speaking populations.
- Eastern Bengal and Assam became Muslim-majority.
- Calcutta's political and cultural centrality was deliberately reduced.

**Fact–analysis distinction:** Bengal's size was a genuine administrative problem. Nationalists argued that separating Bihar and Orissa from Bengali-speaking areas could have addressed administration without dividing the Bengali linguistic community. Official writings by H.H. Risley and Curzon openly treated the weakening of a united political opposition as an advantage. Thus administrative convenience and political calculation coexisted; the first does not erase the second.

### From petition to boycott

The proposals became public in **December 1903**. The initial phase relied on meetings, press campaigns, memoranda and petitions. Surendranath Banerjea, Krishna Kumar Mitra and others mobilised opinion through newspapers and large meetings. Zamindars joined educated professionals and political workers.

The decision was announced despite protest. **Surendranath Banerjea called for boycott of British goods and institutions at a Calcutta meeting on 17 July 1905**. That initiative must be distinguished from the later formal resolution. The government's 19 July announcement and 20 July order then made the failure of petitioning unmistakable:

```text
December 1903–mid-1905
petitions · memoranda · press · meetings
                 ↓ official refusal
17 July 1905 Banerjea boycott call
                 ↓
19 July announcement · 20 July formal order
                 ↓
7 August 1905 Calcutta Town Hall
Boycott Resolution and formal Swadeshi proclamation
                 ↓
16 October 1905 partition takes effect
fasting · hartal · processions · rakhi · Bande Mataram
```

On 16 October, people fasted, walked barefoot, bathed in the Ganga and tied rakhis as a symbol of Bengal's unity. Rabindranath Tagore's songs and **Bande Mataram** gave collective emotion a shared language. Anandamohan Bose laid the foundation of Federation Hall.

### Example—and its limit

The rakhi ceremony converted a domestic-cultural object into a public symbol of unity across the two halves of Bengal.

**Limit:** symbolic unity cannot be assumed to equal equal political participation. The movement's class, regional and religious reach must be examined separately.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Nationalists ignored a real administrative difficulty and converted routine reorganisation into agitation.

**Reply:** administrative difficulty was real, but the selected design divided the linguistic population, disregarded sustained public objection and was defended privately in political terms. A balanced answer should acknowledge the problem of scale before demonstrating why the remedy appeared partisan.

- Separate **announcement**, **formal movement launch** and **implementation**.
- For Curzon questions, connect university and municipal policy to partition without reducing all his policies to a single motive.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** “The Partition of Bengal combined an administrative problem with a political solution.” Examine. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

The Bengal Presidency, with a population of about 78 million in contemporary accounts, was difficult to administer. Curzon therefore presented partition as an efficiency measure. The question, however, is not whether reorganisation was needed but why this form was selected.

The 1905 scheme created Eastern Bengal and Assam, with Dacca as capital and a Muslim majority, while the western unit included Bengali-, Hindi- and Oriya-speaking populations. It divided Bengali speakers between administrations and reduced their relative weight in the remaining Bengal. Nationalists argued that Bihar and Orissa could instead have been separated without fragmenting the Bengali linguistic region.

Political evidence strengthened their suspicion. Risley's official note described a united Bengal as a power and division as a means of weakening opponents. Curzon also sought to reduce Calcutta's role as a centre of Congress politics. The government proceeded despite petitions, meetings and press criticism from December 1903 onward.

Thus a genuine administrative difficulty was addressed through boundaries carrying clear political and communal consequences. The July 1905 decision converted a constitutional protest into boycott and Swadeshi; the 7 August Town Hall resolution and 16 October mourning demonstrated this shift.

Partition should therefore be understood neither as pure administrative reform nor as motive without context. Administrative necessity supplied the public rationale; divide-and-rule calculations shaped the chosen design and its nationalist reception.

Boycott asked, “What should Indians stop supporting?” Swadeshi asked, “What must Indians create so that withdrawal is sustainable?” Confusing the two turns a political-economic programme into a shopping slogan.

### Boycott as political pressure

The boycott of Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt sought to make Indian protest economically visible. Foreign cloth was publicly burned; shops were picketed; domestic and ceremonial consumption became politicised. Priests, guests, washermen, students and householders could participate through daily decisions.

Boycott worked best when:

- a foreign product had an available substitute;
- local networks could enforce social approval or disapproval;
- public meetings explained the political meaning of sacrifice;
- traders and consumers accepted short-term inconvenience.

It weakened when Indian substitutes were costly, scarce or uneven in quality. Therefore, boycott could signal solidarity more easily than transform the whole colonial economy.

The early effect was nevertheless measurable within a bounded field. Bipan Chandra records protest meetings in Bengal, including one at Barisal attended by **ten to twelve thousand**, and reports that the value of British cloth sold in **some mofussil districts** fell **five- to fifteen-fold between September 1904 and September 1905**. These are striking local and time-specific indicators, not an all-Bengal or all-India estimate.

### Swadeshi as production

Swadeshi meant preference for indigenous goods and the creation of indigenous capacity. The period saw textile mills, handloom concerns, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks, insurance companies and stores. P.C. Ray's Bengal Chemical enterprise became a durable example, while many ventures failed because patriotic energy could not replace capital, technology or business skill.

**Analytical inference:** this is economic nationalism in institutional form. The earlier critique explained why colonial trade harmed India; Swadeshi tried to alter production and consumption.

### National education

The colonial education system was criticised as officially controlled, inadequate and denationalising. The response was not simply to leave school. It was to create an alternative:

- Bengal National College, with Aurobindo as principal;
- the **National Council of Education**, established in **August 1906**;
- national schools in several regions;
- literary, scientific, technical and physical education;
- vernacular instruction for wider access;
- Bengal Technical Institute and efforts to obtain technical training abroad.

National education joined intellectual autonomy to industrial self-reliance. Yet its reach remained limited, and students risked scholarships, employment and recognised credentials.

### Culture as political infrastructure

Songs by Rabindranath Tagore, Rajanikanta Sen, **Dwijendralal Ray, Mukunda Das and Syed Abu Mohammed** carried political meaning beyond formal speeches and across different political traditions. Tagore's **Amar Sonar Bangla**, written in this setting, later became Bangladesh's national anthem. **Vande Mataram** became a movement song during the anti-partition agitation.

Visual art also became a field of self-assertion. **Abanindranath Tagore** turned from Victorian naturalism towards Mughal, Rajput and Ajanta traditions; **Nandalal Bose** emerged within this renewal. In science, the original research of **Jagdish Chandra Bose** and **Prafulla Chandra Ray** demonstrated that cultural confidence could include modern scientific creation rather than a retreat from modernity. These examples are representative: the point is the breadth of self-assertion, not a catalogue of personalities.

Sakharam Ganesh Deuskar's *Desher Katha* criticised colonial economic domination and helped shape Swadeshi political consciousness. In the direct 2020 PYQ, the official key is unavailable locally; its displayed statements must therefore remain neutral in the PYQ index.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Swadeshi was protectionist sentiment without economic viability.

**Reply:** many enterprises were fragile, but viability is only one criterion. The movement created demand, entrepreneurship, technical education and a political understanding of consumption. The sound judgement is “institutionally creative but economically uneven,” not either total success or mere symbolism.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** Analyse Swadeshi as a constructive programme rather than merely a boycott campaign. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Boycott was Swadeshi's visible weapon, but construction supplied its deeper political content. Refusing Manchester cloth or Liverpool salt imposed symbolic and commercial costs on colonial trade. Such withdrawal, however, could not endure without Indian substitutes.

Swadeshi therefore promoted handloom production, textile mills, soap and match factories, tanneries, stores, banks and insurance companies. P.C. Ray's Bengal Chemical enterprise illustrated durable indigenous initiative, although many ventures lacked capital, technology and commercial expertise. The movement converted consumption into a choice about national power.

Its educational programme was equally constructive. Bengal National College, associated with Aurobindo, and the National Council of Education established in 1906 sought literary, scientific and technical education under national control. Vernacular instruction aimed at wider reach, while technical institutions linked education with productive capacity.

Constructive work also entered culture and society through songs, folk theatre, arbitration, craft training and local service. These activities built **atma-shakti**—the confidence and institutional capacity to resist dependence.

The programme nevertheless remained uneven. Indigenous substitutes were not always affordable; national institutions had limited reach; several enterprises failed. Thus Swadeshi did not overturn the colonial economy. Its achievement was to join protest with institution-building and make economic choice, education and cultural creativity parts of anti-colonial politics.

## Lesson 4 — Samitis, Passive Resistance and Social Participation

Progress: 4/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Organisation, constructive work and the uneven making of mass politics

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: how a volunteer body could connect politics and daily life

```text
SAMITI
  ├─ political communication → songs · lectures · village meetings
  ├─ discipline             → physical and moral training
  ├─ constructive work      → schools · crafts · famine/epidemic relief
  ├─ parallel service       → arbitration of local disputes
  └─ mobilisation           → boycott · processions · public commitment

Result: nationalism becomes an organised social practice
Limit: reach remains locally uneven and vulnerable to repression
```

### Plain-language intuition

A movement cannot survive on speeches alone. People need organisations that explain its goals, coordinate action and demonstrate that collective capacity can solve everyday problems. The **samitis**, or volunteer associations, attempted this work.

### Samitis and constructive power

The best-documented example was **Ashwini Kumar Dutt's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti** in Barisal. Its branches carried messages to villages through songs and magic-lantern lectures, trained volunteers, organised schools and craft instruction, assisted during famine and epidemics and established arbitration committees. Constructive work also included campaigns against **caste oppression, early marriage, dowry and alcohol consumption**: self-rule was presented as social regeneration as well as refusal of colonial authority.

Bipan Chandra records **159 branches** reaching across Barisal district and reports that, by August 1906, **523 disputes** had been settled through **89 arbitration committees**. These figures establish exceptional organisational density in one district; they must not be projected onto Bengal as a whole.

This mattered because an arbitration committee did more than settle a dispute. It suggested that Indians could reduce dependence on colonial institutions by organising credible local alternatives.

**Limit:** samitis were not a parallel state across Bengal. Barisal was an unusually deep pocket of organisation; its experience cannot be generalised mechanically to every district.

### Passive resistance

Militant leaders proposed extending boycott beyond goods to government schools, courts, titles, employment and administrative cooperation. **Passive resistance** meant organised refusal to assist colonial commerce and officialdom.

Its logic was:

```text
colonial rule depends on routine Indian cooperation
                  ↓
withdraw selected cooperation
                  ↓
raise administrative and economic costs
                  ↓
combine sacrifice with alternative institutions
                  ↓
force political concession and build self-rule capacity
```

This was more ambitious than consumer boycott. Yet it remained incompletely implemented. Militants did not create a central organisation, common discipline or nationwide chain of command.

### Participation: evidence and limits

| Group | Named or concrete evidence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Students | picketing, propaganda, meetings; fines, expulsion, scholarship and service penalties | highly visible, but institutional repression could disrupt study and careers |
| Women | urban middle-class women joined processions and picketing; women also rejected foreign bangles and utensils | a major breach of domestic confinement, but not yet socially representative of all women |
| Workers | efforts to organise strikes in foreign-managed concerns, including Eastern Indian Railway and Clive Jute Mills | selective and episodic; no sustained all-India labour organisation |
| Peasants | meetings, jatras and constructive work; unusually strong reach in Barisal | generally limited, rarely organised around agrarian demands |
| Muslims | Abdul Rasul, Liaquat Husain and businessman Guznavi joined; Barisal reached many Muslim peasants | broad Muslim peasant support was not secured across Bengal |
| Zamindars and urban groups | some zamindars, lawyers, merchants, city poor and lower-middle groups participated | motives and intensity varied; unity against partition did not erase class differences |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling Swadeshi a mass movement exaggerates an urban bhadralok agitation.

**Reply:** its reach was undeniably uneven, and peasants and workers were not organised on their own demands. Yet students participated on a massive scale, women entered public politics, selected strikes occurred, and Barisal demonstrated rural penetration. The accurate category is an **early and incomplete mass movement**, not either a fully national uprising or a purely elite petition.

### UPSC use

- Use named evidence, then state the limit in the same sentence.
- Distinguish exposure to nationalist politics from autonomous class mobilisation.
- Passive resistance here was an evolving proposal; do not retroactively equate it with Gandhi's later disciplined satyagraha.

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** Assess the extent to which the Swadeshi Movement inaugurated mass politics in India. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Swadeshi inaugurated mass politics by moving nationalism beyond petitions into repeated public action, but it did not yet create a socially comprehensive national movement.

Its techniques widened participation. Students organised meetings, propagated Swadeshi and picketed shops despite expulsion and employment penalties. Urban middle-class women entered processions and picketing, while household choices such as rejecting foreign bangles politicised domestic life. Selected strikes in foreign-managed concerns, including Eastern Indian Railway and Clive Jute Mills, attempted to connect workers' grievances with nationalism.

Volunteer samitis carried politics into local society. Ashwini Kumar Dutt's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti in Barisal used songs, lectures, schools, relief, craft training and arbitration. Meetings, jatras and constructive work exposed sections of the peasantry to nationalist ideas; Barisal achieved notable reach among predominantly Muslim peasants.

The limits were equally important. Rural participation remained concentrated in pockets, peasants were not generally organised around agrarian demands, worker action was episodic and women's participation was socially uneven. Many Muslims joined, including Abdul Rasul and Liaquat Husain, but the movement failed to secure the mass of Muslim peasants. It also lacked a central organisation capable of sustaining passive resistance.

Therefore Swadeshi was not mature all-India mass politics. It was its experimental beginning: it discovered public ritual, boycott, volunteer organisation and constructive work, while revealing the need for wider social demands, inclusive idioms and disciplined coordination.

### Visual: a shared current, not a single identical ideology

| Leader | Main arena | Distinct contribution | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bal Gangadhar Tilak | Maharashtra and all-India propagation | festivals, press, self-reliance, popular agitation, extension beyond Bengal | his earlier agrarian intervention did not make Swadeshi an all-India peasant movement |
| Lala Lajpat Rai | Punjab and national public life | assertive nationalism, education, social work and Punjab mobilisation | not an underground revolutionary organiser |
| Bipin Chandra Pal | Bengal and lecture circuits | boycott, national regeneration, expansive Swadeshi message | later withdrew from active politics amid repression and division |
| Aurobindo Ghose | Bengal | political independence, passive resistance, national education and radical journalism | his links with revolutionary circles do not erase the distinct character of his open political programme |

### Plain-language intuition

“Lal-Bal-Pal” is a memory aid, not an explanation. The leaders shared distrust of colonial benevolence and belief in self-reliance, but they worked through different regional institutions and gave different weight to culture, education, public agitation and resistance.

### Spread beyond Bengal

- **Maharashtra:** Tilak carried the message to Poona and Bombay. His Ganapati and Shivaji festivals, *Kesari* and *Mahratta*, earlier no-tax advocacy and public reputation linked Swadeshi to an existing militant tradition.
- **Punjab and northern India:** Lala Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh spread assertive politics in Punjab; **Syed Haidar Raza** led the movement in Delhi. Rawalpindi, Kangra, Jammu, Multan and Haridwar saw activity.
- **Madras Presidency:** **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai** carried Swadeshi into southern economic and political action. Bipin Chandra Pal's lecture tour helped energise the Presidency.
- **Andhra:** **Harisarvottam Rao** became associated with the regional spread and suffered repression.

Spread did not mean uniform replication. Bengal possessed the immediate grievance, dense student activity and samiti networks. Elsewhere leaders translated Swadeshi into local political languages.

### Ideological distinctions

**Tilak** treated political rights as claims to be won through organised pressure, not gifts. His use of festivals made public culture a political arena.

**Lajpat Rai** combined nationalism with social and educational work. The exact 2018 Prelims question asks which leader wrote biographies of Mazzini, Garibaldi, Shivaji and Shrikrishna, stayed in America and entered the Central Assembly. The local official key is unavailable; the reproduced PYQ remains neutral. Biographical evidence identifies Lajpat Rai, which may be taught as a historical fact outside that unkeyed block.

**Bipin Chandra Pal** argued for national regeneration and carried the movement through extensive public speaking. His emphasis joined self-reliance with a larger political transformation.

**Aurobindo** articulated independence as the “life-breath” of a nation, supported extended boycott and passive resistance, and served as principal of Bengal National College. He was more willing than some colleagues to leave open a violent response to intensified repression. That does not make all his public politics identical to underground operations.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The “Lal-Bal-Pal” label proves a coherent all-India Extremist party already existed.

**Reply:** the label captures a recognisable leadership current and regional spread, but no disciplined national party integrated their work. This gap explains why repression, retirement and imprisonment could decapitate the movement.

- Compare leaders by **arena, method, institution and limit**, not slogans alone.
- Pair Chidambaram Pillai with Madras, Syed Haidar Raza with Delhi and Harisarvottam Rao with Andhra.
- Avoid placing Lajpat Rai inside a secret revolutionary society merely because he was an assertive nationalist.

## Lesson 6 — Congress Conflict and the Surat Split

Progress: 6/8 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Programme, pace, organisational control and the breakdown of 1907

### Visual: disagreement moved from method to control

```text

Congress supports Swadeshi and boycott for Bengal
            ↓

Swaraj + Swadeshi + Boycott + National Education
            ↓

mutual distrust + procedural conflict + charged delegates
            ↓

            ↓
both wings weakened, state gains room to isolate militants
```

### Why unity initially held

The anti-partition struggle was not born as an exclusively Extremist movement. Bengal Moderates such as Surendranath Banerjea and Krishna Kumar Mitra led its initial constitutional phase. The 1905 Banaras Congress under Gokhale supported Swadeshi and boycott in Bengal.

At the 1906 Calcutta session, **Dadabhai Naoroji** served as a compromise president. The Congress accepted four important ideas: swaraj, Swadeshi, boycott and national education. But agreement on words concealed disagreement on scope.

### The real points of conflict

| Question | Moderate tendency | Militant tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate objective | annul partition and secure reform | transform agitation into struggle for swaraj |
| Geography | concentrate boycott in Bengal | extend movement across India |
| Boycott | primarily foreign goods | extend to schools, courts, titles and services |
| Pace | controlled pressure within Congress discipline | sustain and escalate popular action |
| Political risk | avoid provoking destructive repression | accept sacrifice as necessary |

The conflict was therefore strategic, not a simple struggle between patriots and loyalists. Moderates feared that an unprepared country and uncontrolled confrontation would destroy the organisation. Militants feared that organisational caution would drain the movement of its historic opportunity.

### Surat, 26 December 1907

The session opened on **26 December 1907** at Surat. The venue had already been shifted from Nagpur, and rumours that the Moderates would dilute the four Calcutta resolutions intensified mistrust. The Extremists demanded a guarantee that the resolutions would stand and decided to oppose the elected president, **Rash Behari Ghose**, as procedural leverage.

The sequence matters. Delegates arrived after three days of hostile public meetings; the presidential dispute came before substantive business; shouting, blows and chairs followed; a shoe struck the dais; and the police cleared the hall. The session collapsed before a political programme could be debated. Moderates subsequently retained the Congress machinery and excluded militant leaders.

Neither breakdown was inevitable nor wholly desired by the senior leaders. **Gokhale** had warned that a split would let the bureaucracy suppress both sections. **Tilak** made last-minute efforts to preserve unity and, according to Lajpat Rai's later account, twice agreed to waive opposition to Ghose and leave the Calcutta resolutions to the Subjects Committee, but could not carry his followers. This does not erase responsibility on either side; it shows how factional pressure defeated attempted compromise.

### Strongest argument for each side

**Moderate case:** durable organisation matters more than an emotional high tide. A movement without preparation, resources or discipline may invite repression and collapse.

**Militant case:** institutions survive only by responding to historical openings. Restricting boycott when popular energy was available would preserve machinery by sacrificing purpose.

**Balanced reply:** each diagnosed the other's real weakness. The Moderates underestimated the need for mass pressure; the militants underestimated organisational preparation. The split converted correct partial insights into mutual defeat.

### Consequences

- Congress lost militant energy and younger cadres.
- militants lost the only established all-India platform.
- repression became easier because leaders could be isolated.
- the government exploited the rupture by combining repression, conciliation of Moderate opinion and suppression of militant organisation.
- Swadeshi's decline accelerated, though its methods and political memory survived.

### UPSC use

- Do not date the split to 1906; Calcutta deferred conflict, Surat formalised it in 1907.
- Avoid moral binaries. Compare theories of preparedness, pressure and organisation.
- Explain why the split weakened both, rather than treating Moderate control as victory.

The Surat split was both ideological and organisational because disagreements over political method became a struggle for control of the Congress.

Thus ideology supplied the dispute's content, while organisational control determined its explosive form and damaging outcome.

The colonial response was not simply “crush the movement.” It combined coercion, political division, limited constitutional concession and territorial-symbolic rearrangement.

### Repressive instruments

Public meetings and processions were restricted; newspapers were prosecuted; students faced fines, expulsion and disqualification from scholarships or government service. The public shouting of Bande Mataram was restricted in East Bengal. The **Barisal Conference of April 1906** was forcibly dispersed and delegates were assaulted.

Leadership was then removed:

- Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh were deported in 1907 after Punjab unrest;
- Tilak received six years' imprisonment in 1908;
- Ashwini Kumar Dutt and Krishna Kumar Mitra were among Bengal leaders deported in 1908;
- Chidambaram Pillai and Harisarvottam Rao were imprisoned;
- Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo withdrew from active politics.

The effect was disproportionate because the movement lacked a central structure able to replace arrested or retired leaders.

### Concession and division

The **Indian Councils Act of 1909**, or Morley-Minto Reforms, enlarged councils but did not create responsible government. It also introduced separate electorates for Muslims. In this episode, the relevant point is bounded: the state sought to isolate militant nationalism, attract Moderate opinion and organise representation through separate political channels.

### The 1911 settlement

In 1911 the government announced that it would:

1. annul the 1905 partition and reunite eastern and western Bengal;
2. reorganise Bihar and Orissa separately;
3. shift the imperial capital from Calcutta to Delhi.

The **Bihar and Orissa province was formally constituted in 1912**. Thus 1911 is the year of the Delhi Durbar announcement and political settlement; 1912 is the implementation date for the new province.

Annulment was a real nationalist achievement because sustained resistance forced reversal of a major decision. Yet it was qualified:

- it arrived after the open movement had declined;
- repression and the Congress split had weakened nationalist organisation;
- the capital shift reduced Calcutta's strategic centrality;
- administrative reorganisation still separated Bihar and Orissa.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Annulment proves a complete Swadeshi victory.

**Reply:** the original division was reversed, which should not be minimised. But victory did not mean transfer of power, recovery of organisational unity or resolution of communal representation. The state conceded the disputed arrangement while relocating the capital and preserving imperial control.

### UPSC use

- Write “repression + co-option + constitutional rearrangement,” not repression alone.
- Keep 1907 Surat, 1909 reforms and 1911 annulment in sequence.
- Do not claim the capital was shifted solely because of Swadeshi; present political distancing from Calcutta as part of a broader imperial decision.

### Revision notes

1. Student penalties attacked the movement's most active cadre base.
2. Press prosecution restricted political communication.
3. Barisal 1906 became a major symbol of official coercion.
4. Deportation and imprisonment removed regional leaders.
5. Organisational weakness magnified the effect of repression.
6. The 1909 reforms enlarged councils without responsible government.
7. Separate electorates form part of this state-response context but require a wider history of communal representation.
8. Partition was annulled in 1911.

### Visual: the same features generated both achievement and weakness

| Movement feature | Achievement produced | Limitation produced |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural idiom | emotional reach, songs, festivals and shared symbols | could be read as insufficiently inclusive |
| Boycott | visible sacrifice and commercial pressure | difficult where substitutes were scarce or costly |
| Constructive work | schools, enterprises, arbitration and confidence | institutions remained uneven and often fragile |
| Student leadership | energy, communication and disciplined picketing | easy for the state to target through educational sanctions |
| Regional networks | spread beyond Bengal through local leaders | no uniform all-India organisation |
| Militant leadership | swaraj, self-reliance and active resistance | dependence on a few leaders increased vulnerability |

### Four achievements

**1. Political transformation.** Nationalism moved from occasional petition to continuous public action. Boycott, processions, picketing, volunteer bodies and passive resistance widened the repertoire.

**2. Economic and educational construction.** Indigenous enterprise and national education showed that resistance could create institutions rather than merely denounce rule.

**3. Social entry into politics.** Students and women participated conspicuously; workers and peasants entered in selected contexts. The movement's mass character was incomplete but historically new.

**4. Psychological decolonisation.** Atma-shakti, sacrifice and confidence challenged colonial claims of Indian dependence. The goal increasingly moved from annulment toward swaraj.

### Why the movement declined

- severe repression removed leaders and constrained communication;
- the Surat split severed militant energy from Congress machinery;
- no central organisation coordinated passive resistance;
- mobilisation outside Bengal was uneven;
- indigenous goods and institutions faced material constraints;
- peasants and workers were not generally mobilised around their own economic demands;
- mass movements cannot remain indefinitely at peak intensity.

### The communal question—neither denial nor caricature

Named Muslim leaders such as **Abdul Rasul**, **Liaquat Husain** and **Guznavi** participated. The Barisal Samiti reached many Muslim peasants. Therefore, “Muslims opposed Swadeshi” is false.

Yet the movement failed to win the mass of Muslim peasantry. Several forces interacted:

- official encouragement of Muslim elite support for partition;
- the Nawab of Dacca's pro-partition position;
- class relations in parts of Bengal, where many landlords were Hindu and many peasants Muslim;
- Hindu-inflected symbols that could inspire some and alienate or be misrepresented to others;
- the growth of separate political organisation after the agitation.

**Qualified judgement:** cultural forms were not inherently communal, and many reflected shared popular traditions. But a nationalist movement is judged also by how symbols are received within unequal social relations. State manipulation mattered; so did the movement's incomplete inclusive strategy.

### Historiographical readings

| Reading | Insight | Risk if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Nationalist | heroic sacrifice, self-reliance and defeat of partition | may understate social exclusion and organisational failure |
| Structural anti-colonial | new repertoire eroded colonial ideological control | may treat local participation as more uniform than evidence permits |
| Class-social | asks whether peasants and workers acted on their own grievances | may underplay political learning short of class organisation |
| Regional | stresses Bengal's depth and differentiated spread elsewhere | may fragment an emerging all-India political current |
| Communal-politics | studies official patronage, elite interests and symbolic reception | may falsely reduce the movement to Hindu communal mobilisation |

**Bipan Chandra's assessment** calls Swadeshi the **“first round” in the national popular struggle against colonialism**. He also borrows Antonio Gramsci's imagery to describe it as an important battle in a longer **“war of position.”** Used carefully, this means that boycott, constructive institutions and political education weakened colonial legitimacy before power could be displaced. It does not mean that Gramsci analysed Swadeshi directly or that local mobilisation was already nationwide.

**Analytical synthesis:** Swadeshi was innovative, regionally concentrated, socially widening and organisationally incomplete. Its importance lies in both the immediate struggle and the political capacities accumulated for later movements.

### Legacy—and boundary control

Later nationalism reused boycott, constructive work, indigenous production, national education and withdrawal of cooperation. Gandhi later supplied broader organisation, a more explicit discipline of non-violence and greater capacity to enter villages.

Do not claim identity:

- Swadeshi passive resistance was not yet Gandhian satyagraha.
- Swadeshi samitis were not the later Congress mass organisation.
- the later underground revolutionary turn relied on clandestine armed organisation, unlike the open boycott and constructive programme taught here.

A high-quality verdict weighs outcome at three levels:

```text

Institutional: enterprises, schools, samitis and new political techniques
Long-term: mass-action repertoire and psychological shift toward swaraj
```

### Original Mains practice

**Question:** “Swadeshi lost organisational momentum but permanently changed the grammar of Indian nationalism.” Critically evaluate. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Swadeshi declined as an open movement by 1908, yet it changed nationalism's goals, methods and social imagination.

Its immediate trigger was Bengal's partition, but militants widened the objective toward swaraj. Processions, picketing, songs, volunteer samitis, arbitration and passive resistance shifted politics beyond annual petitions. Students mobilised extensively, women entered public agitation, selected workers struck and pockets such as Barisal saw rural participation.

The movement's organisation could not sustain this innovation. Repression attacked press, meetings, students and leaders. Surat divided Congress machinery from militant energy. Passive resistance lacked central discipline; indigenous ventures faced capital and technology constraints; peasants and workers were seldom organised around their own demands. Muslim participation was real but limited, shaped by official patronage, class relations and an incompletely inclusive idiom.

Nevertheless, annulment in 1911 demonstrated that sustained agitation could reverse a major decision. More importantly, boycott, constructive work, self-reliance, national education and withdrawal of cooperation became a durable repertoire. Gandhi later enlarged and disciplined these techniques rather than inventing them without precedent.

Thus Swadeshi was neither a complete mass revolution nor a failed Bengali episode. Its organisation receded, but its innovations altered the accepted meaning of nationalist action.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish boycott, Swadeshi and passive resistance in the anti-partition movement. (150 words)

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

The three concepts formed an escalating but connected programme. **Boycott** was withdrawal: initially the refusal of foreign goods, especially cloth and salt, enforced through pledges, picketing and public burning. In its extended version it could include government schools, courts, titles and services.

**Swadeshi** was construction: the use and production of Indian goods, creation of banks, insurance companies, factories and stores, and development of national scientific and technical capacity. It made boycott materially and morally sustainable.

**Passive resistance** supplied the political theory linking these acts. Since colonial commerce and administration depended on Indian cooperation, organised refusal could raise the costs of rule and compel change. It therefore moved beyond consumption toward non-cooperation.

They were not identical. Boycott could occur without adequate alternatives; Swadeshi institutions could grow without complete administrative withdrawal; passive resistance remained incompletely organised. Together they joined pressure, self-reliance and preparation for self-rule.

**Rubric (10):** three precise definitions 4 + relationship 2 + examples 2 + limitations 1 + conclusion 1 = 10.

**Question:** Evaluate the role of national education and samitis in transforming Swadeshi from protest into constructive politics. (250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

National education and samitis addressed the central weakness of boycott: withdrawal from colonial goods and institutions was unsustainable without Indian alternatives.

The National Council of Education, established in 1906, sought literary, scientific and technical education under national control. Bengal National College, with Aurobindo as principal, and technical institutions challenged an official system criticised as controlled and denationalising. Vernacular instruction aimed at wider access, while technical learning linked education to productive self-reliance.

Samitis converted this institutional idea into local practice. Ashwini Kumar Dutt's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti in Barisal used songs, lectures and volunteer training to communicate nationalism. It organised schools, craft instruction, famine and epidemic relief and arbitration committees. Such work demonstrated **atma-shakti**: Indians could govern parts of social life through collective capacity.

Their political value lay in connecting moral discipline, welfare and resistance. They created cadres and took nationalist ideas beyond formal Congress meetings. Yet the transformation remained partial. National schools lacked recognised pathways and wide resources; many samitis were locally concentrated; repression could close institutions and remove leaders. Arbitration did not amount to a parallel national government.

Therefore education and samitis did not merely support agitation. They supplied a constructive theory of freedom as capacity-building, while also revealing the need for scale, finance and durable coordination.

**Rubric (15):** thesis 2 + education institutions 4 + samiti functions 4 + political mechanism 2 + limits 2 + verdict 1 = 15.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question:** The strength and weakness of the Swadeshi Movement arose from the same attempt to turn culture, consumption and local society into instruments of politics. Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Swadeshi's originality lay in politicising spheres previously treated as private or social. This generated exceptional energy but also exposed uneven resources and identities.

Consumption became action through boycott of foreign cloth and salt, picketing and household refusal. It enabled participation without formal office, but poorer consumers could not always afford Indian substitutes. Indigenous factories, banks, insurance and stores connected sacrifice to production, though many enterprises lacked capital and technical capacity.

Culture carried nationalism through Bande Mataram, Tagore's songs, rakhi, festivals, jatras and public processions. These forms made politics emotionally intelligible. Yet their reception was unequal. Hindu-inflected symbols could be misrepresented by officials and communal elites or fail to reassure Muslim peasants within a class-divided countryside.

Local society became political through students, women and samitis. Barisal's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti joined lectures, relief, schools, crafts and arbitration, and reached many Muslim peasants. Elsewhere rural penetration was shallower; workers' strikes were selective and peasants were seldom organised around their own demands.

Thus the movement's greatest strength—embedding nationalism in everyday life—was also its weakness: everyday life was structured by price, class, gender, region and religious identity. Swadeshi created the repertoire of popular nationalism but lacked the inclusive demands and central discipline needed to sustain it nationwide.

**Rubric (20):** interpretive thesis 2 + consumption 4 + culture 4 + local organisation/participation 4 + limits and counter-evidence 4 + integrated verdict 2 = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Core thesis

The anti-partition agitation transformed Indian nationalism because it linked a concrete grievance to a new theory of action: colonial rule would be challenged through self-reliance, boycott, constructive institutions, sacrifice and wider mobilisation. Its methods survived because its organisation did not.

## Roots of militant nationalism

- Moderate economic criticism exposed the exploitative structure of colonialism.
- Limited concessions and repeated refusal weakened faith in reform from within.
- Curzon's municipal, university and political measures signalled official hostility.
- Famines, plague conditions and educated unemployment widened frustration.
- Adwa (1896) and Japan's victory over Russia (1905) challenged European invincibility.
- Bankim, Vivekananda and Tilak contributed cultural confidence and self-respect.
- Militants retained earlier economic nationalism but changed goal, agency and method.
- “Militant” means assertive mass pressure; it is not synonymous with underground violence.

## Partition of Bengal

- Proposals became public in December 1903.
- Banerjea called for boycott on 17 July; the government announced its decision on 19 July, issued the formal order on 20 July, and implemented partition on 16 October 1905.
- Eastern Bengal and Assam, capital Dacca, had a Muslim majority.
- Bengali speakers were divided, and Bengalis became a minority in the western unit.
- Bengal was genuinely administratively unwieldy.
- Nationalists argued that Bihar and Orissa could be separated without dividing Bengali speakers.
- Risley and Curzon treated weakening united nationalist influence as a political advantage.
- Therefore administrative rationale and political design must be evaluated together.

## Anti-partition chronology

- 1903–mid-1905: petitions, memoranda, press campaigns and meetings.
- July 1905: official decision despite protest.
- 7 August 1905: Calcutta Town Hall meeting and Boycott Resolution.
- 16 October 1905: mourning, fasting, hartal, Ganga bathing, rakhi and Bande Mataram.
- Early leadership included Bengal Moderates; militants became dominant as constitutional protest failed.

## Boycott and Swadeshi

- Boycott = withdrawal from foreign goods and, in extended form, colonial institutions.
- Swadeshi = creation and use of indigenous goods, firms and capabilities.
- Boycott made political cost visible; Swadeshi sought sustainable alternatives.
- Industries included textiles, handloom, soap, matches, chemicals, banks and insurance.
- Many ventures failed because patriotic demand could not replace capital, skill and technology.
- Economic achievement was substantial but regionally and commercially uneven.

## National education and culture

- National Council of Education established in August 1906.
- Bengal National College was associated with Aurobindo.
- Programme included literary, scientific, technical and physical education.
- Vernacular instruction sought wider reach.
- Songs, folk theatre, art, rakhi and festivals formed political communication networks.
- Vande Mataram became a major song of the anti-partition movement.
- *Desher Katha* belongs to the intellectual-cultural environment of Swadeshi; direct PYQ treatment remains unkeyed.

## Samitis, constructive work and passive resistance

- Samitis were volunteer associations, not automatically secret revolutionary societies.
- Ashwini Kumar Dutt's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti was strongest in Barisal.
- Functions included lectures, songs, discipline, schools, crafts, relief and arbitration.
- Atma-shakti meant moral confidence plus practical social capacity.
- Passive resistance proposed organised withdrawal of cooperation from commerce and administration.
- It anticipated later non-cooperation but lacked central discipline and nationwide execution.

## Social participation

- Students: mass organisers, propagandists and picketers; faced severe educational penalties.
- Women: entered processions, picketing and politicised domestic consumption.
- Workers: selected strikes in foreign-managed concerns; no sustained nationwide labour front.
- Peasants: meaningful exposure and participation in pockets, especially Barisal; few independent agrarian demands.
- Muslims: Abdul Rasul, Liaquat Husain, Guznavi and sections of Barisal peasants participated.
- Limitation: most Muslim peasants were not mobilised; class relations, official policy and symbolic reception interacted.

## Regional spread and leaders

- Tilak: Maharashtra, press, festivals, assertive public politics and wider extension.
- Lajpat Rai: Punjab, education, social work and militant nationalism; not a secret-society leader.
- Bipin Chandra Pal: Bengal ideologue and all-India lecture activity.
- Aurobindo: independence, passive resistance, radical journalism and national education.
- Syed Haidar Raza: Delhi.
- V.O. Chidambaram Pillai: Madras Presidency.
- Harisarvottam Rao: Andhra.
- Regional spread was real but less intense and differently organised than Bengal.

## Congress conflict and Surat

- Banaras 1905 supported Swadeshi and boycott for Bengal.
- Calcutta 1906 under Naoroji adopted swaraj, Swadeshi, boycott and national education.
- Moderates feared unprepared confrontation and preferred controlled scope.
- militants sought all-India extension and broader non-cooperation.
- Surat, 26 December 1907, joined strategic distrust to a presidency dispute over Rash Behari Ghose and the fate of the Calcutta resolutions.
- Gokhale warned against division; Tilak made unsuccessful last-minute efforts to compromise before the session collapsed.
- Moderates retained machinery; militants lost the all-India platform.
- Both wings were weakened, assisting colonial isolation and repression.

## Repression and 1911

- Restrictions targeted meetings, press, processions and Bande Mataram.
- Students were fined, expelled and barred from benefits or service.
- Barisal Conference, April 1906, symbolised coercion.
- Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Ajit Singh, Dutt, Mitra, Pillai and Rao faced imprisonment or deportation.
- The 1909 reforms enlarged councils without responsible government and introduced separate electorates.
- In 1911 partition was annulled, reorganisation of Bihar and Orissa was announced, and the capital shifted to Delhi; the new Bihar and Orissa province was constituted in 1912.
- Annulment was a real but qualified victory.

## Achievements and limits

| Achievement | Limitation |
|---|---|
| changed nationalism from petition to popular action | no durable central organisation |
| introduced boycott and passive resistance | incomplete nationwide implementation |
| built educational and economic institutions | limited resources and survival |
| brought new social groups into politics | participation remained uneven |
| challenged colonial cultural hegemony | some symbols had exclusion risks |
| secured eventual annulment | no transfer of political power |
| created a repertoire for later movements | later Gandhi had to widen and discipline it |
