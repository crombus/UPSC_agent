# Revolutionary Nationalism, Phase I (1907-1917) — Live Session Edition

This session asks a difficult historical question: why did a small but influential section of Indian nationalists move from open agitation to secret armed action, and what did that choice achieve or foreclose? It evaluates violence historically without either colonial criminalisation or patriotic romanticisation.

### Visual: the causal fork after Swadeshi

```text
Swadeshi mobilisation
        |
        +--> boycott, national education, passive resistance
        |
        +--> repression + Congress split + declining open mobilisation
                         |
                         v
             impatient nationalist youth
                         |
          "Can a brave minority break public fear?"

       secret cells + exemplary armed action + sacrifice
```

The point is not that Swadeshi mechanically "caused" bombs. It created expectations of active resistance; repression and organisational decline then made clandestine action appear to some young activists to be the remaining path.

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine a political movement that has taught young people that freedom requires sacrifice, but whose open meetings are dispersed, newspapers prosecuted and leaders arrested or divided. Some participants may conclude: a whole population is not yet ready, so a disciplined minority must act first. A dramatic deed, they hope, will expose the ruler's vulnerability and cure society's fear.

That is the intuition behind **exemplary action**: the deed is meant not only to remove a particular official but to communicate courage. Its political subject is a **vanguard**, a small advance group claiming to awaken a much larger nation.

### Terminology without moral flattening

Older colonial records and much older historiography commonly used **"terrorism"** or **"revolutionary terrorism."** The label highlights violence intended to produce political fear, but it also carries the colonial state's judgement and can collapse anti-colonial purpose into ordinary criminality. In analytical writing, prefer:

- **revolutionary nationalism** for the ideological current;
- **armed revolutionary activity** for its repertoire;
- **targeted assassination**, **bomb attack**, **political dacoity** or **planned military rising** for specific methods.

This terminology does not sanitise violence. It permits two truths at once: participants pursued national liberation and sacrifice; their methods could kill unintended people, substitute a small conspiracy for consent, and invite severe repression.

### Why the current emerged

1. **Closure of open space:** repression after the Swadeshi high tide weakened public organisation.
2. **Youth impatience:** constitutional petition seemed slow, while even mass boycott had not delivered freedom.
3. **Revenge and justice:** officials identified with repression became targets.
4. **Psychological strategy:** exemplary sacrifice was expected to destroy the myth of British invincibility.
5. **Vanguard expectation:** a few trained activists would act; the public would be inspired; soldiers or masses would eventually rise.
6. **International models:** memories of Russian, Irish and other revolutionary struggles made small-group action appear historically plausible.

### A bounded antecedent: the Chapekars

In 1897, the Chapekar brothers killed plague commissioner W.C. Rand and Lieutenant Ayerst in Poona amid anger at intrusive plague administration. This was a precursor, not proof that a coordinated all-India revolutionary movement already existed. The denser secret-society phase belongs mainly after 1905.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling these actors nationalists romanticises violence and erases victims.

**Reply:** Historical explanation is not moral absolution. A sound account names the anti-colonial objective, the coercive method, unintended victims and strategic consequences together. Conversely, merely repeating the colonial label prevents analysis of why educated youth accepted sacrifice and why their actions gained popular sympathy.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish movement, organisation, publication, person and event.
- **Mains:** explain the causal chain from Swadeshi's constraints to a different theory of political agency.
- **Trap:** militant nationalism was broader than armed revolution; not every Extremist was a secret-society member.
- **Answer use:** define the current before assessing it: "a clandestine, vanguardist anti-colonial strategy centred on exemplary armed action."

### Revision notes

1. Revolutionary nationalism was an ideological current; armed revolutionary activity was its method.
2. The colonial label "terrorism" should be attributed and handled critically.
3. Swadeshi supplied militant expectations, not an automatic cause.
4. Repression and organisational decline narrowed open political space.
5. Exemplary action aimed to communicate courage beyond the immediate target.
6. Vanguard theory assigned initiative to a disciplined minority.
7. Heroism and strategic effectiveness are separate questions.
8. The Chapekars belong as an 1897 precursor.
9. Militant nationalism and secret revolutionary organisation are not synonyms.
10. Ethical evaluation must include intended purpose, means, victims and consequences.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why revolutionary nationalism emerged after the high tide of the Swadeshi movement.

**Word-count rule for every model in this session:** count each Unicode alphanumeric sequence matched by `\b\w+\b` as one word. Hyphenated, apostrophised and dotted forms therefore split at punctuation; numerals count.

**Model answer (145 words):** Revolutionary nationalism emerged from Swadeshi's energy but adopted a different theory of change. Open boycott, national education and passive resistance had widened political expectation; yet repression, the 1907 Congress split and declining mobilisation convinced some impatient youth that public agitation had reached a ceiling. Secret groups therefore treated a self-sacrificing minority as a vanguard. Targeted attacks and spectacular sacrifice were expected to punish repressive officials, expose colonial vulnerability and dissolve popular fear. The 1897 Chapekar action offered a precursor, while the denser Bengal and Maharashtra networks developed after 1905. This shift was not inevitable: open nationalist work continued, and militant nationalism was broader than armed conspiracy. Nor did exemplary action create a mass organisation. Thus revolutionary nationalism was produced by, but not reducible to, Swadeshi's decline: it transformed frustration into clandestine action while inheriting Swadeshi's stress on courage and self-reliance.

### Visual: protection that becomes a prison

```text
physical training -> recruitment -> oath -> compartmentalised cell
                                             |
                     +-----------------------+----------------------+
                     |                       |                      |
                 propaganda            arms/bombs          targeted action
                     |                       |                      |
```

Secrecy reduced exposure but also restricted trust, recruitment, debate and mass accountability. The same organisational feature was both shield and ceiling.

### Repertoire and purpose

| Method | Immediate purpose | Political expectation | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical training | discipline and courage | create committed cadres | fitness did not create a mass base |
| Oath-bound cells | loyalty and secrecy | survive surveillance | compartmentalisation weakened coordination |
| Newspapers/pamphlets | political education | turn spectators into sympathisers | censorship and prosecution |
| Arms and bomb preparation | operational capacity | demonstrate vulnerability of Raj | accidents, scarcity and forensic trails |
| Targeted assassination | punish a named official | exemplary action | misidentification and civilian harm |
| Political dacoity | raise funds | finance arms and fugitives | coercion could alienate society |
| Soldier outreach | trigger wider revolt | convert vanguard action into insurrection | required trust and coordination largely absent |

**Political dacoity** meant robbery undertaken to finance political activity. Its claimed purpose distinguished it from personal enrichment, but its coercive effect on victims remained real.

### Social base

The early Bengal current drew heavily from educated youth and lower-middle-class or bhadralok milieus; Maharashtra networks similarly depended on small circles. Ghadar later broadened the profile abroad through migrant labourers and ex-soldiers. Yet the movement did not build durable organisations among India's peasants and industrial workers.

Women often supported households, communication and concealment, but Phase I's formal cadres and public memory remained overwhelmingly male. Do not import the more visible armed participation of women in the later Chittagong phase into 1907-17.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A small cell cannot be called political because it lacks a popular mandate.

**Reply:** It was political in aim, symbolism and effect, but that does not make it representative. The distinction allows a balanced verdict: the cells challenged colonial legitimacy and generated martyr traditions, yet their self-appointed vanguard could not substitute for organised popular participation.

### UPSC integration

Use a three-column answer: **method → intended political effect → structural limit**. Avoid treating "bomb" as a complete explanation. A method becomes historically meaningful only when connected to its theory of change.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse how the organisational form of early revolutionary nationalism shaped both its strengths and weaknesses.

**Model answer (168 words):** Early revolutionary organisations relied on selective recruitment, physical training, oaths, compartmentalised cells and clandestine communication. This form matched their theory of exemplary action. A small, committed vanguard could prepare arms, conduct targeted attacks, raise funds through political dacoities and use newspapers or trials to demonstrate that colonial power was vulnerable. Secrecy also enabled activity when public meetings and publications faced repression.

Yet the organisational shield became a strategic prison. Restricted recruitment prevented a durable peasant or worker base; compartmentalisation impeded coordination; scarce weapons encouraged isolated actions; and dependence on personal trust made informers exceptionally destructive. Political dacoity could estrange the public whose awakening the movement sought. The social base was mainly educated male youth, although Ghadar later drew migrant labourers and ex-soldiers abroad. Women's supportive roles should not be confused with the larger female armed participation of the later Chittagong phase.

Thus the cell was well suited to symbolic defiance and survival in the short term, but poorly suited to converting courage into sustained mass power.

**Rubric (15):** organisational features 4 + strengths 3 + weaknesses 4 + social-base evidence 2 + balanced judgement 2 = 15.

The labels overlap historically, but they are not interchangeable institutions.

### Three distinctions

**Calcutta Anushilan milieu:** a broad associational and physical-culture environment from which several revolutionary circles developed. "Anushilan" conveyed disciplined cultivation or training.

**Dacca Anushilan:** a more tightly organised eastern Bengal network led by **Pulin Behari Das**. It must not be reduced to a mere alternate name for every Calcutta group.

**Jugantar:** first, a revolutionary newspaper that spread militant ideas; second, a name commonly associated with the Barindra Kumar Ghosh-led revolutionary group or network. **Bhupendranath Datta served as an editor of the newspaper and was imprisoned for sedition in 1907; Barindra's distinct importance lay in the associated operational circle.** Newspaper personnel and operational leadership overlapped within the same milieu but were not interchangeable roles.

### Network functions

Recruitment often moved through gymnasia, student connections and personal recommendation. Cells combined physical culture, ideological reading, secrecy, arms acquisition and bomb preparation. Newspapers widened the imagined audience; clandestine groups narrowed operational knowledge.

This helps explain why a "society" may look larger in police records than the number capable of executing an action. Sympathy, reading, training, fundraising and operational membership were different degrees of involvement.

### Social and gender qualification

Bengal's network was strongest among educated Hindu youth. That pattern reflected access to schools, urban networks and the cultural idiom of the time; it should not be mistaken for a representative cross-section of Bengal. Women's assistance existed, but this phase did not attain the later scale of women's direct action associated with Chittagong.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Distinguishing these labels is pedantic because colonial intelligence treated them as one conspiracy.

**Reply:** Police aggregation is itself evidence of surveillance, not proof of organisational identity. Precision matters because leadership, geography, publication and operational responsibility differ. UPSC frequently tests exactly such association errors.

### UPSC integration

- *Jugantar* → newspaper as well as shorthand for an associated current.
- Do not make Aurobindo the commander of every Bengal cell.

**Model answer (139 words):** Bengal revolutionary activity was a connected field, not a single organisation. The best description is therefore a networked revolutionary milieu with identifiable nodes, not three unrelated bodies and not one seamless organisation.


Muzaffarpur was the attack. Alipore was the ensuing wider conspiracy prosecution. The causal connection does not make them the same event.

### Muzaffarpur, April 1908

The intended target was **Douglas Kingsford**, regarded by revolutionaries as a repressive judge. **Khudiram Bose** and **Prafulla Chaki** attacked a carriage they believed carried him. Kingsford was not inside; **two women were killed**. Chaki died by suicide while evading capture. Khudiram was arrested, tried and executed.

The episode generated sympathy for youthful sacrifice, but it also exposes the ethical and strategic danger of secret violence: imperfect information converted a targeted plan into civilian deaths. Popular mourning does not remove that fact.

### Alipore Bomb Case, 1908-09

The investigation led police to the **Maniktala/Muraripukur garden** and the Barindra-led circle's bomb-making network. The resulting Alipore prosecution tried a wider alleged conspiracy. **Barindra Kumar Ghosh** was convicted; **Aurobindo Ghosh was tried and acquitted**.

During the proceedings, approver **Narendranath Goswami** was killed inside Alipore Jail by **Kanailal Dutt and Satyendranath Bose**. This detail belongs only as evidence of the organisation's fierce internal code and the state's evidentiary struggle; sensational narration obscures the larger case.

### Significance

- The prosecution exposed infrastructure, not merely two bomb-throwers.
- Trials and executions created martyrs and public attention.
- Police seizure and testimony damaged the network.
- The unintended deaths revealed the gap between claimed selectivity and actual control.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since Kingsford was the intended target, the deaths of the two women were merely accidental and analytically secondary.

**Reply:** Intention matters for classifying the action, but foreseeable risk matters for evaluating it. The mistake is central evidence about clandestine violence: weak intelligence and irreversible force can defeat the proclaimed selectivity of the method.

### UPSC integration

Remember a four-step sequence: **Kingsford target → two women killed → Chaki suicide/Bose execution → wider Alipore conspiracy case**. Do not say Aurobindo was convicted, and do not describe Muzaffarpur as the Alipore bombing.

### Punjab's distinct setting

**Reply:** Biography cannot erase chronology. Organisations and coalitions change. The 1912 conspiracy preceded Ghadar's formation; Rash Behari's later wartime role connected networks that had distinct origins.

The ship was forced to leave Canadian waters. Wartime British orders restricted disembarkation on its return. At **Budge Budge** near Calcutta, official attempts to control the passengers produced a violent clash, deaths, arrests and escapes.

### Why war appeared decisive

War committed British military and administrative attention elsewhere. Ghadar leaders issued **Ailan-e-Jung**, a proclamation of war, and urged migrants to return to India, spread rebellion and win Indian soldiers.

The call outpaced preparation. Activists had mobilisation energy abroad but incomplete knowledge of political mood inside Punjab. Many villagers and influential bodies did not respond as expected.

### State screening and revolutionary coordination

The **Ingress into India Ordinance, 1914** enabled scrutiny, detention and restriction of returning emigrants. Some reached Punjab, but surveillance reduced freedom of movement and communication.

When early efforts lacked central direction, **Rash Behari Bose** came to Punjab through contacts involving **Sachindranath Sanyal** and **Vishnu Ganesh Pingle**. Emissaries contacted army units. A rising was planned first for **21 February 1915**, then advanced to **19 February** after fears of betrayal.

The Criminal Investigation Department had penetrated the network. Authorities made pre-emptive arrests and disrupted units. Call this an **attempted rising** or **planned mutiny**, not a completed general mutiny.

### Singapore Mutiny: bounded comparison

The Singapore Mutiny of February 1915 formed part of the wider wartime crisis of loyalty among Indian troops and existed in an environment touched by revolutionary propaganda. Evidence does not justify reducing it to a centrally commanded Ghadar operation. Similar timing and anti-British effect do not establish organisational identity.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The plan failed only because an informer betrayed the date.

**Reply:** Penetration was decisive, but it succeeded because deeper weaknesses existed: open propaganda, inadequate central organisation, uneven soldier commitment, little mass preparation and unrealistic assessment of British capacity. Betrayal triggered failure; it did not create every vulnerability.

## Lesson 14 — Achievement, failure, historiography and legacy

Progress: 14/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: How to judge Phase I without romance or dismissal

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: a four-level verdict

```text
DECLARED OBJECTIVE: overthrow British rule
        -> not achieved

STRATEGIC CAPACITY: mass rising and durable coordination
        -> weak; February 1915 pre-empted

POLITICAL EFFECT: fearlessness, martyrs, international visibility
        -> substantial but uneven

IDEOLOGICAL LEGACY: secular, democratic, egalitarian resistance
        -> important; later peasant/left paths, but not yet Phase II socialism
```

A movement can fail at its maximal objective and still alter political imagination. Conversely, symbolic success cannot be used to conceal operational failure.

### Achievements

1. **Psychological defiance:** dramatic sacrifice challenged assumptions of colonial invincibility and Indian passivity.
2. **Internationalisation:** London, Paris, North America, Berlin and Kabul made Indian anti-colonialism a transnational problem.
3. **Political education:** *Ghadar* carried the established critique of colonial exploitation to migrant workers.
4. **Secular and egalitarian inheritance:** Ghadar's rhetoric attacked sectarian division, loyalism and hierarchy.
5. **Tradition of resistance:** trials and martyrdom entered popular memory.

### Failures and limits

1. **No mass organisation:** most domestic cells inspired rather than organised the population.
2. **Substitutionism:** vanguard action assumed that a deed could substitute for sustained political work.
3. **Weak preparation:** arms, finance, communications and central command remained inadequate.
4. **Surveillance vulnerability:** informers and special laws disrupted cells.
5. **Social narrowness:** educated male youth dominated many domestic groups; Ghadar's labour base abroad did not translate into rural India.
6. **Ethical-strategic cost:** Muzaffarpur's unintended victims exposed the danger of irreversible violence based on mistaken intelligence.
7. **External dependence:** Indo-German plans relied on powers with different objectives.

### Historiographical positions

| Lens | What it illuminates | What it risks missing |
|---|---|---|
| Colonial "terrorism" frame | coercive acts and security concerns | anti-colonial purpose and unequal state violence |
| Heroic nationalist frame | courage, sacrifice and martyrdom | victims, strategic weakness and social narrowness |
| Mass-strategy critique | failure to organise peasants/workers | psychological and symbolic consequences |
| Transnational/social-history lens | migration, race, labour and global networks | domestic ideological diversity if overextended |

The strongest assessment combines these lenses. Method must be judged without moral flattening, and effect must be measured at several levels.

### Bounded legacy

Some surviving Ghadarites later participated in peasant and left politics, suggesting that secular and egalitarian commitments could move toward mass organisation. This is a legacy, not permission to import the HRA, HSRA, Kakori, Bhagat Singh or Chittagong into Phase I. Those later formations changed ideology, social analysis and tactics and belong to Topic 21.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since the revolutionaries failed to overthrow the Raj and lost many cadres, the movement was futile.

**Reply:** Judging only by the declared maximum objective would also flatten other unsuccessful phases of nationalism. Ghadar deepened consciousness, tested strategy and created secular resistance traditions. Yet the reply must not become evasion: the failed rising wasted scarce leadership, and symbolic capital did not compensate those killed, imprisoned or harmed.

### UPSC integration

Use a graded verdict:

```text
military/insurrectionary result: failure
organisational learning: costly but significant
psychological and symbolic result: durable
ideological/transnational legacy: substantial
```

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Question 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Directive:** Explain.

**Question:** Explain the idea of "exemplary action" in early revolutionary nationalism.

**Model answer (147 words):** Exemplary action treated a dramatic deed as political communication. After repression and the decline of Swadeshi mobilisation, some young nationalists believed that a disciplined minority should act before the majority was organised. Targeted assassination, bomb attacks and self-sacrifice would punish officials, expose colonial vulnerability and dissolve popular fear. The Muzaffarpur attack on Kingsford illustrates both the theory and its limit: Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki acted against a named official, but the wrong carriage was attacked and two women died. Trials and executions created martyr memory, yet did not produce sustained mass organisation. The theory therefore rested on a vanguard assumption—that courage could spread from deed to nation. Its achievement was psychological defiance; its weakness was substitutionism, because inspiration was expected to perform the work of recruitment, organisation and political education. Exemplary action was thus neither ordinary crime nor an adequate strategy of national liberation.

**Rubric (10):** definition 2 + causal context 2 + named example 2 + mechanism 2 + limitation and verdict 2 = 10.

## Question 2 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Directive:** Discuss.

**Question:** Discuss the transnational geography of revolutionary nationalism from India House to Ghadar.

**Model answer (203 words):** Revolutionary nationalism became international before it became mass-based. In London, Shyamji Krishna Varma founded India House and promoted the Indian Home Rule Society and *The Indian Sociologist*. V.D. Savarkar later became its central revolutionary organiser, while Madan Lal Dhingra's assassination of Curzon-Wyllie in 1909 brought imperial surveillance directly onto the network. In continental Europe, Madame Bhikaji Cama and S.R. Rana sustained propaganda; Cama's Stuttgart flag episode in 1907 made Indian nationalism visible internationally.

North America added a different social base. Punjabi migrant labourers and ex-soldiers encountered racial exclusion and linked their humiliation to India's subordination. Pre-Ghadar papers and associations prepared the ground. The Hindi Association, formed at Portland in May 1913 under Sohan Singh Bhakna with Har Dayal's ideological leadership, used Yugantar Ashram, the *Ghadar* paper and *Ghadar di Goonj* to connect Pacific and Asian port communities.

World War I shifted overseas politics toward strategic collaboration. The Berlin Committee sought German assistance; Ram Chandra provided American links; Mahendra Pratap and Barkatullah pursued the Kabul mission. These networks internationalised the cause and circulated a secular republican message. However, distance, surveillance, uncertain arms routes and dependence on foreign interests prevented them from replacing domestic organisation.

**Rubric (15):** London 3 + Europe 2 + Ghadar origins/network 4 + wartime extension 2 + comparison 2 + limits/verdict 2 = 15.

## Question 3 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Directive:** Critically examine.

**Question:** Critically examine the proposition that Ghadar's greatest achievement lay in ideology rather than insurrection.

**Model answer (229 words):** Ghadar's insurrectionary objective failed. Its *Ailan-e-Jung* urged emigrants to return during World War I and win Indian soldiers. The colonial state used the Ingress into India Ordinance to screen returnees; Punjab's population proved less ready than activists expected. Rash Behari Bose, Sachindranath Sanyal and Vishnu Ganesh Pingle helped coordinate a rising first planned for 21 and then 19 February 1915, but CID penetration enabled pre-emption. Weak command, arms, secrecy and mass preparation made betrayal devastating.

Its ideological achievement was more durable. The *Ghadar* paper translated the nationalist critique of drain, taxation, poverty and imperial war into direct language for migrant workers. *Ghadar di Goonj* spread it through poetry and recitation. Although the base was mainly Punjabi and Sikh, the movement accepted leaders and heroes across religions and regions, rejected colonial loyalism and imagined an independent republic. Har Dayal's anarchist, syndicalist and socialist influences contributed egalitarian and internationalist elements. Some surviving Ghadarites later entered peasant and left politics.

This achievement should not be romanticised. The ideology retained ambiguities, lacked a developed institutional programme and encouraged premature belief that emotional militancy abroad could be reproduced in India. Severe repression also removed a generation of secular activists. Nevertheless, measured by political education and the creation of democratic, secular and egalitarian resistance rather than immediate seizure of power, Ghadar's ideological contribution exceeded its failed military result.

**Rubric (20):** insurrectionary evidence 5 + failure analysis 4 + ideological evidence 5 + ambiguities 3 + later legacy 2 + verdict 1 = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Definition and emergence

- Revolutionary nationalism was a clandestine anti-colonial current using exemplary armed action.
- Prefer "revolutionary nationalism" or precise method names; attribute the older colonial label "terrorism."
- Its emergence followed Swadeshi's mobilisation, repression, Congress division and youth impatience.
- Vanguard theory expected a brave minority to break fear and stimulate wider revolt.
- The Chapekar action of 1897 was a bounded precursor.

## Organisation and methods

- Recruitment used personal trust, gymnasia, physical training, ideological reading and oaths.
- Cells were compartmentalised; secrecy protected and restricted them.
- Methods included propaganda, arms and bomb preparation, targeted assassination, political dacoity and soldier outreach.
- Political dacoity claimed a political fundraising purpose but retained coercive costs.
- Domestic social bases were narrow and predominantly educated and male.

## Bengal

- Calcutta Anushilan was a broad milieu of related circles.
- Dacca Anushilan, led by Pulin Behari Das, was a distinct eastern Bengal network.
- *Jugantar* was a newspaper edited by Bhupendranath Datta and also a name associated with Barindra's operational circle.
- Datta's 1907 sedition imprisonment belongs to the newspaper's history; it does not make him the leader of Barindra's bomb-making circle.
- Muzaffarpur, April 1908: target Kingsford; Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki; two women killed.
- Chaki died by suicide; Khudiram was tried and executed.
- Alipore, 1908-09: wider conspiracy case after Maniktala/Muraripukur discovery.
- Barindra was convicted; Aurobindo was tried and acquitted.
- Kanailal Dutt and Satyendranath Bose killed approver Narendranath Goswami in jail.

## Maharashtra, Punjab and Delhi

- Chapekars killed Rand and Ayerst at Poona in 1897.
- Mitra Mela preceded Savarkar's Abhinav Bharat, organised in 1904.
- Anant Kanhere killed A.M.T. Jackson at Nasik in December 1909.
- Savarkar linked Maharashtra networks with later London activism.
- Punjab's 1907 agrarian agitation made Ajit Singh a political bridge.
- Lajpat Rai was a public nationalist, not a secret-society member.
- Hardinge was wounded by a bomb in Delhi on 23 December 1912.
- Rash Behari Bose and Sachindranath Sanyal were linked to the wider conspiracy.

## London and Europe

- Shyamji Krishna Varma founded India House.
- He was associated with the Indian Home Rule Society and *The Indian Sociologist*.
- Savarkar later became central at India House.
- Madan Lal Dhingra killed Curzon-Wyllie on 1 July 1909.
- Madame Cama and S.R. Rana belonged to continental propaganda networks.
- Cama displayed an Indian nationalist flag at Stuttgart in 1907.
- Berlin/Indian Independence Committee belongs only to the 1914-17 wartime phase.

## Ghadar

- Pacific-coast migrants included Punjabi peasants, labourers and ex-soldiers.
- Racial exclusion was linked to India's colonial subordination.
- Pre-Ghadar work included Ramnath Puri, Tarak Nath Das, G.D. Kumar, *Free Hindustan*, *Swadesh Sevak* and United India House.
- Hindi Association formed at Portland in May 1913.
- Sohan Singh Bhakna was president; Lala Har Dayal was leading ideologue.
- Yugantar Ashram in San Francisco became headquarters.
- *Ghadar*: Urdu, 1 November 1913; Gurmukhi, 9 December 1913.
- *Ghadar di Goonj* spread poems through oral circulation.
- Kartar Singh Sarabha, Barkatullah and Ram Chandra were important.
- Network geography included North America, the Philippines, Hong Kong, China, the Malay States, Singapore and India.
- Ideology was anti-colonial, republican, secular, egalitarian and internationalist, with transitional ambiguities.

## Komagata Maru and wartime plan

- Canada's continuous journey rule operated as an exclusionary device.
- Gurdit Singh chartered Komagata Maru with 376 passengers.
- Vancouver refused entry; a Shore Committee organised support.
- Ghadar activists amplified the episode but did not originate it as a party operation.
- Forced return ended in a clash at Budge Budge.
- World War I generated "Britain's difficulty, India's opportunity."
- *Ailan-e-Jung* called emigrants home for revolt.
- Ingress into India Ordinance, 1914 controlled returnees.
- Rash Behari Bose, Sachindranath Sanyal and Vishnu Ganesh Pingle coordinated soldier contacts.
- Rising date moved from 21 to 19 February 1915.
- CID penetration enabled pre-emption; it was not a completed general mutiny.
- Singapore belongs to the wider wartime context, not a simple Ghadar command label.

## Indo-German effort and repression

- Berlin Committee sought German help and maintained links with Ram Chandra.
- Raja Mahendra Pratap and Barkatullah established a provisional government at Kabul in 1915.
- It sought Afghan support but exercised no sovereignty in India.
- State response used CID, informers, interception, deportation, internment and trials.
- Explosive Substances Act 1908; Indian Press Act 1910; Ingress Ordinance 1914; Defence of India Act 1915.
- Lahore conspiracy trials and Mandalay prosecutions removed cadres.

- Insurrectionary objective: failed.
- Psychological effect: challenged colonial invincibility.
- Organisational effect: tested networks but exposed the cost of secrecy.
- Ideological effect: especially strong in Ghadar's secular-democratic-egalitarian current.
- Ethical limit: intended selectivity did not prevent unintended victims.
- Strategic limit: exemplary action could not substitute for mass organisation.
- Later peasant and left trajectories are a bounded legacy.
