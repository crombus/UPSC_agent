# Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1919-1922) - Learning Session Live Edition

The central puzzle is simple: how did Indian nationalism move from condemning colonial injustice to asking millions of people to withdraw the everyday cooperation on which colonial rule depended? This session builds the answer in dependency order: legitimacy crisis, Khilafat grievance, alliance, Congress authorisation, programme, organisation, social translations, violence, withdrawal and legacy.

## Roadmap

|---|---|---|

## Lesson 1 - From protest to withdrawal of consent

### Visual: the legitimacy rupture

```text
Wartime loyalty and reform expectations
                  |
     Rowlatt coercion + Punjab wrongs
                  |
     inquiries without adequate redress
                  |
  faith in constitutional cooperation falls
                  |
 non-cooperation becomes thinkable:
 "Why assist a government that will not repair injustice?"
```

The bridge from 1919 to 1920 is not simply that Indians became angrier. The deeper change was a crisis of political legitimacy. Many nationalists had assumed that loyal constitutional pressure could gradually reform the Raj. The Rowlatt legislation, Jallianwala Bagh and wider Punjab repression weakened that assumption. The official response did not provide the redress nationalists considered morally adequate.

This lesson does not retell the massacre; Topic 19 owns that story. Topic 20 needs its consequence: **Punjab wrongs** became one of the three grounds on which Gandhi justified Non-Cooperation, beside the **Khilafat wrong** and **Swaraj**.

### 1. Cooperation is a political resource

Colonial government did not operate by force alone. It also needed Indian students in its institutions, lawyers in its courts, voters and representatives in its councils, title-holders who displayed prestige, merchants who circulated imported goods, and officials who implemented orders.

```text
Colonial authority = coercion + routine Indian participation + belief in legitimacy
Non-Cooperation attacks the second and third terms without beginning with violence.
```

Plainly put, Gandhi asked people to stop making an unjust system look normal. Returning a title or leaving a government college did not physically overthrow the state. It publicly declared that obedience was no longer morally automatic.

### 2. Why the constitutional offer did not settle the crisis

The Montagu-Chelmsford reforms created limited openings, but executive power remained protected and the system did not answer the Punjab grievance. Constitutional advance and coercion arrived together.

**Fact:** the government did not annul the Rowlatt Act, make the redress demanded for Punjab, or satisfy the nationalist claim to self-government.

**Analytical inference:** Non-Cooperation was both a method and a verdict: the colonial state had forfeited its claim to voluntary assistance.

### 3. Gandhi's ethical logic

For Gandhi, means and ends formed one chain. Swaraj gained through uncontrolled violence would reproduce domination rather than self-rule. Non-cooperation with injustice was paired with self-discipline, constructive work and willingness to bear punishment.

**Strongest objection:** Boycotting schools, courts and councils could hurt Indians before it hurt the Raj.

**Reply:** Continued participation also carried a cost: it normalised an authority that refused justice. The objection remained practically important because alternatives were initially uneven.

### UPSC application

Explain causation without claiming Jallianwala alone caused Non-Cooperation. A strong answer combines Punjab wrongs, Khilafat, constitutional disappointment, post-war distress and Gandhi's strategy.

### Revision notes

1. Topic 19 owns the detailed Rowlatt-Jallianwala narrative.
2. Topic 20 begins from its political result: a legitimacy rupture.
3. Punjab wrongs became one stated ground for Non-Cooperation.
4. Failed redress mattered as much as the original outrage.
5. Colonial rule relied on participation as well as coercion.
6. Non-cooperation aimed to withdraw consent without armed rebellion.
7. Gandhi linked political action to the moral quality of its means.
8. The 1919 reforms did not satisfy responsible-government demands.
9. Jallianwala was a bridge, not the movement's sole cause.
10. Alternative institutions were needed because boycott imposed costs.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain how the Punjab wrongs made Non-Cooperation a plausible strategy rather than merely another protest resolution.

**Model answer:**

The Punjab wrongs transformed a grievance against particular officials into a broader crisis of colonial legitimacy. Wartime expectations and promises of constitutional advance had encouraged belief that British rule could be reformed through loyal pressure. Rowlatt repression, Jallianwala Bagh and martial-law humiliations exposed the coercive limits of that belief, while subsequent official action failed to provide adequate redress.

Gandhi therefore shifted the question from what concessions Indians should request to whether they should continue assisting an unjust authority. Titles, schools, courts, councils and imported cloth became sites where consent could be withdrawn. This converted an abstract dispute into daily choices. Punjab was not the sole cause: Khilafat anxiety, post-war distress and disappointment with reforms also converged. Punjab supplied the moral rupture; Non-Cooperation supplied an organised method for expressing it.

## Lesson 2 - The Khilafat question: an international symbol becomes Indian politics

Progress: 2/14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Origins, demands, leaders, trends and Ottoman chronology

Book context: queried - Bipan Chandra and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay OCR evidence
CA search: "official India Turkey historical Khilafat commemoration recent"
CA found: none sufficiently relevant; this lesson is historical
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: two meanings of one symbol

```text
Ottoman Sultan-Caliph after World War I
                 |
      fear of punitive peace terms
                 |
      KHILAFAT as religious symbol
                 |
  Indian Muslim political mobilisation
                 |
 anti-British protest and pan-Indian alliance
```

The Caliph was not an Indian ruler. Why did his fate mobilise Indians? Because a symbol can carry more than its literal institutional meaning. For many Muslims, the Ottoman Sultan's position as Caliph and his relation to Islamic holy places represented religious dignity. In India, British treatment of Turkey also became evidence of imperial bad faith.

### 1. Three demands

The movement demanded that:

1. the Caliph retain control over Muslim holy places;
2. he retain sufficient pre-war territories to sustain his position;
3. the *jazirat-ul-Arab* - Arabia, Syria, Iraq and Palestine in the contemporary formulation - not pass under non-Muslim sovereignty.

The demands appeared pan-Islamic because they concerned the wider Islamic world. Their Indian political use was anti-imperial: they could unite a Muslim public divided by region, language, class and sect, and connect it to struggle against British rule.

### 2. Organisation, leaders and trends

Sekhar Bandyopadhyay dates a Khilafat Committee in Bombay to March 1919. The safe cross-source formulation is that a committee existed before the All-India Khilafat Conference at Delhi in November 1919. That conference threatened withdrawal of cooperation if demands were rejected.

| Trend | Important figures | Preferred course |
|---|---|---|
| Younger radical nationalists | Mohammad Ali, Shaukat Ali, Maulana Azad, Hasrat Mohani | Mass agitation and Hindu-Muslim joint action |
| Religious-public leadership | Ulama, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Abdul Bari and others | Religious authority joined to politics, with internal differences |
| Moderate merchant tendency | Bombay merchants and constitutional leaders | Delegations, representation and negotiation |

The moderate route lost credibility after appeals to the Viceroy, Lloyd George and Paris produced no satisfactory result. Gandhi first mediated between tendencies and then became a leading advocate of non-cooperation.

### 3. Treaty and Turkish chronology

| Date | Development | Precision rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1918-19 | Ottoman defeat, armistice and Paris peace context | Do not treat the final settlement as already implemented |
| May 1920 | Sevres terms became public/known in the evidence used here | Publication is not formal signature |
| August 1920 | Treaty of Sevres formally signed | It was resisted and never fully implemented as designed |
| 1922 | Turkish nationalists ended the Sultanate | Ending monarchy was not yet abolition of Caliphate |
| 1923 | Lausanne superseded Sevres | It recognised the nationalist Turkish settlement |
| 1924 | Caliphate abolished | The Indian agitation lost its institutional symbol |

> Key distinction: **Sultanate = political monarchy; Caliphate = claimed religious office.**

### 4. Power and limit of the symbol

The symbol mobilised urban Muslims, gave anti-imperialism emotional force and enabled inter-communal cooperation. It could not give Indian leaders control over Turkish nationalism.

**Strongest objection:** Bringing a religious symbol into nationalism encouraged communal consciousness.

**Reply:** Taking up a demand chiefly affecting Muslims was not inherently communal. National politics often grows by recognising a group's particular injury. The limitation was that leadership did not always convert religious mobilisation into durable secular consciousness, while escalating rhetoric produced strains.

### UPSC application

Avoid both reductions: “only religious” and “simply secular nationalism.” Khilafat was a religious-symbolic grievance translated into anti-imperial mass politics.

### Revision notes

1. Many Muslims regarded the Ottoman Sultan as Caliph.
2. The movement made three territorial and sacred-place demands.
3. Its appearance was pan-Islamic; its Indian force was anti-British.
4. Bombay, March 1919 is supported for committee formation.
5. November 1919 securely dates the Delhi conference.
6. Mohammad Ali and Shaukat Ali were the Ali brothers.
7. Azad, Ajmal Khan and Hasrat Mohani were important leaders.
8. Moderate merchants preferred delegation; radicals preferred agitation.
9. Sevres terms were known in May 1920; signing followed in August.
10. Sevres was not fully implemented and Lausanne replaced it in 1923.
11. Sultanate ended in 1922; Caliphate ended in 1924.
12. Turkish developments created an external limit on Indian strategy.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Analyse the Khilafat movement as both a pan-Islamic mobilisation and an Indian anti-imperial movement.

**Model answer:**

The Khilafat movement arose from anxiety over the defeated Ottoman Empire and the future of the Sultan-Caliph. Its demands concerned Muslim holy places, sufficient Turkish territories and protection of Arab lands from non-Muslim sovereignty. Its language and symbol were therefore pan-Islamic.

Yet its operation in India was distinctly anti-imperial. The Caliphate supplied a common symbol to Muslims divided by region, language, class and sect. Failed delegations weakened the moderate merchant tendency and strengthened Mohammad Ali, Shaukat Ali, Maulana Azad and ulama who favoured mass agitation. Gandhi treated the issue as an opportunity to bring Muslim masses into joint struggle and cement Hindu-Muslim unity.

The alliance achieved deep practical cooperation, but its foundation was vulnerable. Indians could not control Turkish nationalism. The Sultanate ended in 1922, Lausanne replaced Sevres in 1923 and the Caliphate was abolished in 1924. Religious rhetoric could also outrun Gandhian discipline.

Thus, Khilafat was neither merely foreign religious sentiment nor simply secular nationalism. It was a religious-symbolic grievance converted into Indian anti-imperial politics, with extraordinary mobilising power and structural fragility.

**Scoring note:** High-band answers preserve the dual character and accurate Ottoman chronology; one-sided labels lose analytical marks.

## Lesson 3 - Building the Gandhi-Congress-Khilafat alliance

Progress: 3/14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Rationale, resistance and launch chronology

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - canonical files and book chronology
CA search: "recent official India nonviolent mass movement commemoration"
CA found: none needed for this alliance-design lesson
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: coalition without identical motives

```text
Punjab wrongs ---------\
Khilafat wrong ----------> shared method: NON-COOPERATION -> Swaraj
Constitutional failure --/

Different grievances + shared opponent + agreed discipline
```

A coalition does not require identical motives. It requires a shared diagnosis, workable action and trust. Gandhi saw Khilafat as an opening: Congress could recognise a grievance felt intensely by Muslims, while Khilafat leaders could connect their cause to anti-colonial struggle.

Gandhi's rationale had three layers: ethical solidarity, strategic expansion of nationalism, and political education through joint struggle. Tilak also recognised the opportunity for common action. His death on 1 August 1920 removed a towering leader just as the campaign accelerated; it is context, not the cause of Gandhi's rise.

Established Congress politicians feared an unfamiliar mass campaign, possible violence and abandonment of council politics. Opposition or hesitation came from C.R. Das at stages of debate, Bepin Chandra Pal, Mohammad Ali Jinnah, Annie Besant and G.S. Khaparde. Positions changed: Das joined the Nagpur compromise.

### Launch chronology

```text
1-2 June 1920: Central Khilafat Committee at Allahabad approves escalation
        |
1 August 1920: hartal begins the campaign atmosphere
        |
31 August 1920: canonical account dates the Khilafat launch/formulation
        |
September 1920: Calcutta Special Congress authorises Congress action
```

Do not collapse these into one date. Committee decisions, hartal, formal Khilafat action and Congress authorisation were connected but institutionally distinct.

Gandhi promised Swaraj within one year, though its exact institutional meaning remained underdefined. The practical clarity lay in peaceful withdrawal, constructive self-reliance and staged escalation. When the Khilafat Committee launched non-cooperation on 31 August 1920, Gandhi marked his own entry by surrendering the **Kaiser-i-Hind Medal** awarded for his wartime services. The act made renunciation of colonial honour concrete rather than merely programmatic.

**Strongest objection:** The alliance subordinated Indian nationalism to a religious issue outside India.

**Reply:** Gandhi joined, rather than substituted, Khilafat, Punjab and Swaraj. Yet dependence on an externally determined symbol remained a strategic vulnerability.

### UPSC application

Distinguish coalition-building from cynical opportunism. The alliance was strategic and grounded in a public ethical claim of inter-communal solidarity.

### Revision notes

1. Different grievances could share one anti-imperial method.
2. Gandhi framed Khilafat support as solidarity and strategy.
3. Congress resistance reflected fear of violence and lost council openings.
4. Das's position evolved toward compromise.
5. Jinnah, Besant, Khaparde and Pal resisted the new course.
6. Tilak's death was context, not a programme stage.
7. Allahabad, 1 August, 31 August and Calcutta were distinct moments.
8. Swaraj within one year was inspiring but underdefined.
9. Non-violence was ethical principle and organisational discipline.
10. The alliance was genuine without being permanent.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Was the Gandhi-Congress-Khilafat alliance merely tactical? Discuss.

**Model answer:** The alliance was tactical because actors with different goals adopted a common method against colonial rule. Gandhi saw that Khilafat could bring Muslim masses into national politics; Khilafat leaders gained a nationwide organisational partner. Punjab wrongs, Khilafat wrongs and Swaraj were joined through Non-Cooperation.

Yet “merely tactical” understates its ethical content. Gandhi argued that Hindus should defend a Muslim grievance as justice. Joint meetings, volunteer work and boycotts created practical fraternity. The alliance embodied his belief that groups enter politics through particular injuries and discover a common adversary.

Its limits were structural. Congress leaders feared violence and council boycott. Khilafat leaders differed over constitutional methods, religious rhetoric and whether non-violence was principle or convenience. The symbol's fate also depended on Turkey. Thus the alliance was simultaneously a strategic coalition, an ethical experiment and genuine shared mobilisation. Its contingency explains its decline but does not make the unity unreal.

**Scoring note:** Reward distinction between strategic coalition and opportunism, supported by both cooperation and internal disagreement.

## Lesson 4 - Calcutta and Nagpur: authorising mass struggle

Progress: 4/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Democratic debate, ratification and staged commitment

### Visual

```text
Calcutta Special Session, September 1920
 debate -> amendment -> approval in principle
                 |
Nagpur Annual Session, December 1920
 ratification -> staged implementation -> machinery
```

Calcutta supplied the contested political decision; Nagpur ratified it and remade the organisation that would carry it. At Calcutta, Congress supported non-cooperation until Punjab and Khilafat wrongs were removed and Swaraj established. Old-guard concerns included violence, council elections and uncertainty about mass politics.

Nagpur confirmed the programme. A compromise brought C.R. Das to Gandhi's side. The objective was altered toward Swaraj through peaceful and legitimate means. “Legitimate” did not confine Congress to routine constitutional cooperation, because disciplined non-cooperation was now accepted.

| Category | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Adopted | Formally authorised | titles, schools, courts, councils |
| Implemented unevenly | Attempted with varied results | educational and legal boycott |
| Conditional later stage | Reserved for escalation | civil disobedience, tax refusal |
| Announced but never launched | Stopped before activation | Bardoli mass civil disobedience |

**Strongest objection:** Gandhi's prestige made debate a formality.

**Reply:** Amendments, resistance, compromise and departures show genuine strategic conflict, even though Gandhi's influence was decisive.

### UPSC application

Use different verbs: **Calcutta authorised; Nagpur ratified, staged and reorganised.**

7. Boycotts and constructive work were adopted.
8. Tax refusal was a conditional later step.

Nagpur in December converted that decision into durable strategy. It ratified the programme, accepted staged implementation and reorganised Congress for continuous work through linguistic committees, a stronger AICC, a Working Committee and low-fee membership.

Calcutta therefore marks the contested choice of non-cooperation; Nagpur marks consolidation, sequencing and organisational embodiment. Conflating them hides the difference between declaring a strategy and building the party able to execute it.

## Lesson 5 - Nagpur turns Congress into a mass organisation

Progress: 5/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Linguistic PCCs, AICC, Working Committee, membership and limits

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Nagpur organisation in canonical and Bipan Chandra
CA search: "Gujarat Vidyapith 72nd Padvidaan 2026 official"
CA found: official Gujarat Vidyapith page announces the ceremony for 27 August 2026, a bounded institutional-memory link
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from annual platform to continuous machine

```text
OLD WEAKNESS                    NAGPUR RESPONSE
annual deliberation      ->     Working Committee of 15
elite access             ->     four-anna subscription
administrative provinces ->     linguistic Provincial Congress Committees
episodic resolutions     ->     AICC + continuous implementation
```

The most durable achievement of Non-Cooperation may have been organisational. A movement cannot depend on one annual meeting. It needs local units, cheap entry, intermediate coordination and an executive able to act between sessions.

Nagpur reorganised Provincial Congress Committees on linguistic lines. This aligned organisation more closely with languages through which political communication actually occurred. A Working Committee of fifteen, including president and secretaries, provided an executive centre; the AICC strengthened national coordination.

Membership was opened to men and women aged twenty-one or above on an annual subscription of four annas. Bipan Chandra records reduction of the age to eighteen in 1921. Keep the sequence clear: the Nagpur rule and later reduction are not the same decision.

Organisation required money, members and material symbols. The **Tilak Swaraj Fund** set a target of **one crore rupees** and was oversubscribed. At the AICC session at **Vijayawada in March 1921**, Congress directed workers to spend the next three months concentrating on fund collection, membership enrolment and charkha distribution. The membership target of one crore was not reached - the contemporary estimate was roughly fifty lakhs - but the drive greatly widened Congress's organisational field. The different outcomes matter: the fund target was exceeded; the membership target was not.

### Significance and limits

Cheap membership lowered a barrier, but a rule does not automatically produce equal participation. Literacy, caste power, gender norms, money, local notables and repression still shaped access. Linguistic organisation improved communication but did not erase uneven regional strength.

**Objection:** Since elite leaders still dominated committees, “mass party” is exaggerated.

**Reply:** Leadership hierarchy persisted, but the institutional capacity, membership principle and local reach changed qualitatively. Mass organisation means widened mechanisms, not perfect social democracy.

### UPSC application

Use Nagpur as evidence that the movement changed the Congress, not just public mood.

Calling Non-Cooperation “all-India” describes its political horizon, not uniform intensity. It reached groups and regions outside older Congress politics, but participation depended on pre-existing organisation and local grievance.

Plantation labour illustrates the danger of assuming a single script. Assam tea-garden workers could read freedom as freedom of movement from oppressive labour regimes. Such action could exceed Congress plans and meet coercion. The Chandpur episode revealed both autonomous aspiration and leadership's difficulty in controlling consequences.

Where peasants had already organised, the movement was often stronger. Where no prehistory of mobilisation existed, response could be muted.

**Strongest objection:** Emphasising autonomy dissolves the national movement into unrelated local protests.

**Reply:** Local movements shared symbols, timing and an anti-colonial horizon, but their distinct grievances explain variation. National connection and local agency are compatible.

### Visual: from grievance to autonomous politics

```text
high rents + illegal levies + ejectment + forced labour
                    |
         Kisan Sabha mobilisation
                    |
       Congress-Khilafat contact in 1921
                    |
       Eka oath, panchayat and local leaders
                    |
 divergence over violence and control -> repression by March 1922
```

Awadh tenants faced high rents, renewal fees, illegal levies and arbitrary ejectment under taluqdars. Baba Ramchandra's grassroots work and Kisan Sabha organisation supplied a mobilisation base that Non-Cooperation could connect to but did not create from nothing.

Eka, meaning “unity,” expressed a sharper autonomous phase. Its meetings used a sacred-water ritual and oath. Participants promised to pay recorded rent on time, resist ejectment, refuse forced labour, withhold help from criminals and follow panchayat decisions. Grievances included rent **generally fifty per cent above the recorded rent**, oppression by thekedars and share-rent practices. Madari Pasi and other lower-caste leaders acquired prominence. Many small zamindars also joined because of heavy revenue demand.

Congress and Khilafat leaders urged non-violence, but Eka increasingly followed its own leadership and contact diminished. Severe repression ended it by March 1922.

### Eka and Bardoli - only the required comparison

| Dimension | Eka, 1921-22 | Bardoli Satyagraha, 1928 |

Volunteer corps turned resolutions into street-level organisation. Joint drilling brought Congress and Khilafat workers together below the level of famous leaders. Volunteers pledged non-violence in word and deed, inter-communal unity, swadeshi and khadi; Hindu volunteers were also asked to oppose untouchability.

The government understood their importance and declared Congress and Khilafat volunteer bodies illegal. A sharper confrontation had already developed over military service. At Karachi on **8 July 1921**, the All-India Khilafat Committee resolved that Muslim service in the British Indian Army was religiously unlawful and called for the message to reach Muslim soldiers. The **Ali brothers were arrested in September 1921** on the army-service issue. Congress leaders then publicly repeated and broadened the challenge, turning repression of one resolution into a test of political speech and loyalty. By late 1921 many leading nationalists were jailed while Gandhi remained outside.

The annual Congress at **Ahmedabad in December 1921** resolved to continue non-violent non-cooperation with greater vigour. It called on people, especially students, to enrol in volunteer organisations and offer themselves for arrest. Volunteers pledged non-violence in word and deed, inter-communal unity, swadeshi and khadi; Hindu volunteers also undertook active opposition to untouchability. Ahmedabad therefore joined mobilisation, volunteer discipline and deliberate exposure to repression immediately before the Bardoli escalation debate.

The Prince of Wales arrived on **17 November 1921**. A nationwide strike and boycott displayed the movement's strength. In Bombay, however, violence against Europeans, Anglo-Indians and Parsis, followed by repression, demonstrated the danger of uncontrolled crowds. Gandhi treated it as a strategic warning and postponed full civil disobedience.

The date matters: 17 November, not September. One retrospective OCR phrase wrongly associates the Bombay riot with September; the event chronology controls.

**Objection:** Declaring volunteers illegal proves they were a disguised army.

**Reply:** Drilling and discipline alarmed the state, but the formal pledges required non-violence. The more difficult point is that pledges did not always control mass behaviour.

At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district, a Congress-Khilafat procession confronted police on 4 February 1922. Police firing intensified the clash. The crowd attacked and burned the police station; twenty-two policemen were killed.

Local volunteers were protesting police oppression and market grievances. Rumours that Gandhi's power had made bullets harmless, and the idiom of impending “Gandhi raj,” reveal how national authority was locally reimagined. Shahid Amin's insight is useful here: villagers were political actors who gave nationalism meanings shaped by local experience.

Gandhi had announced mass civil disobedience for Bardoli. Chauri Chaura made him conclude that the required non-violent environment did not exist. The Working Committee met at Bardoli and on **12 February 1922** stopped activities leading to law-breaking and redirected workers toward construction.

Sekhar's narrative says withdrawal on 11 February followed by the Bardoli resolution; the canonical and Bipin control securely date the formal CWC resolution to 12 February. Preserve the distinction instead of inventing false harmony.

Gandhi was arrested on **10 March 1922** and sentenced to six years. The official 2025 question asks who defended those arrested after Chauri Chaura; its official Series-A key exists, but the displayed PYQ section later remains neutral as required.

**Strongest objection:** One local incident should not have determined a national campaign.

**Reply:** For Gandhi it was evidence, not merely a trigger: launching tax refusal amid weak discipline risked wider violence and crushing repression. Whether that inference justified total withdrawal remains the next lesson's debate.

The event occurred just after Gandhi announced mass civil disobedience at Bardoli. The movement was about to move from boycott toward direct legal and fiscal confrontation. Gandhi read the violence as evidence that cadres and crowds lacked the non-violent discipline required for such escalation. The CWC's Bardoli resolution of 12 February stopped law-breaking activity and redirected effort to construction.

Critics could reasonably argue that one locality should not cancel nationwide momentum. Yet Gandhi's concern was systemic: Bombay violence had already shown the control gap, and broader disorder could invite severe repression and corrupt the means of Swaraj. Chauri Chaura became national because it tested the movement's foundational claim that mass power could remain non-violent.

### Visual: judge the decision by explicit criteria

```text
ETHICS: could violent means produce Gandhian Swaraj?
CONTROL: could Congress discipline a widening mass movement?
MOMENTUM: was a rare nationwide opportunity discarded?
REPRESSION: would escalation survive colonial force?
CLASS: did restraint protect property or protect peasants?
LEGACY: what remained after immediate retreat?
```

Gandhi's case combined ethics and strategy. Non-violence was constitutive of Swaraj, not a convenient technique. A movement unable to restrain violence was not ready for mass civil disobedience; premature escalation could invite repression that the organisation could not withstand.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Lala Lajpat Rai resented withdrawal. Subhas Chandra Bose called retreat at the boiling point a national calamity. Jawaharlal Nehru also reacted critically, though leaders avoided open revolt against Gandhi. Their strongest argument was momentum: a nationwide mobilisation is difficult to recreate, and withdrawal demoralised activists.

R. Palme Dutt's class-protection thesis argues that Congress restraint shielded landlords and capitalists from radicalising peasants. Bipan Chandra replies that Chauri Chaura showed no anti-property programme, agrarian unrest was already ebbing in important areas, and withdrawal sought to pre-empt repression rather than save property. The reply does not erase Congress caution toward rent refusal; it challenges a single-cause class verdict.

### Achievements and limits

| Achievement | Corresponding limit |
|---|---|
| first sustained all-India mass movement | important regions and groups remained unevenly involved |
| Congress transformed organisationally | local hierarchy continued |
| fear of colonial power weakened | Swaraj did not arrive within one year |
| national institutions and cadre survived | many boycotts proved reversible |
| Muslim participation gave mass depth | unity rested partly on an external religious symbol |
| local political education expanded | local aims could escape non-violent discipline |
| fifth stage created pressure | it was never tested because Bardoli did not launch |

Hindu-Muslim unity was real: shared meetings, volunteer work, sacrifice and large Muslim participation cannot be dismissed. It was also strained by divergent commitments to non-violence, religious idiom, Malabar violence, later communal deterioration and abolition of the Caliphate. None of this made Partition inevitable.

The immediate strategic vacuum produced debate between council entry and continuation of constructive work. Naming that bridge is necessary; developing Swarajists, No-changers, HRA or HSRA belongs to Topic 21.

### Balanced verdict

Judged by Gandhi's means-end doctrine, withdrawal was obligatory. Judged by short-term momentum, it was costly. Judged organisationally, it prevented an uncontrolled escalation and preserved a Congress capable of future struggle. The fairest conclusion is conditional, not absolute.

### UPSC application

Build evaluation around explicit criteria rather than declaring withdrawal simply right or wrong. Pair each achievement with a limit, and end with the bounded council-entry versus constructive-work bridge without developing Topic 21.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Explain why Non-Cooperation can be called a programme of both delegitimation and reconstruction.

**Model answer:** Non-Cooperation delegitimised colonial rule by withdrawing the everyday support on which it depended. Surrender of titles denied prestige; boycott of schools, courts, councils and official functions challenged institutional authority; foreign-cloth boycott made consumption an anti-imperial act.

The programme was also reconstructive. National institutions such as Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith and Bihar Vidyapith offered educational alternatives. Arbitration and panchayats sought non-colonial dispute settlement, while khadi and charkha promoted self-reliance. Temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity and anti-untouchability linked political freedom to social discipline.

The two sides were interdependent. Refusal without alternatives was difficult to sustain; construction without confrontation risked political quietism. Although implementation was uneven and reversible, their combination transformed Congress from a petitioning body into an organisation practising withdrawal of consent and preparing citizens for Swaraj.

**Scoring note:** Credit the interaction between negative and positive programmes, not two disconnected lists.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Analyse the role of local agency in expanding and destabilising the Non-Cooperation Movement.

**Model answer:** Local agency expanded Non-Cooperation by translating an all-India programme into concrete grievances. Awadh tenants connected Swaraj with high rents, ejectment and forced labour. Eka developed oath-taking, panchayat discipline and leadership under Madari Pasi. Tribal communities linked Gandhi's message to forest access; workers interpreted freedom through mobility, wages and authority; Akalis applied satyagraha to distinct gurdwara reform.

Such translations made the movement genuinely mass. Congress did not awaken an inert countryside; it often amplified organisations and grievances already present. The same autonomy, however, destabilised central strategy. Peasants could move toward rent refusal, workers toward confrontational strikes, and crowds toward violence. Malabar passed from tenant-Khilafat overlap into anti-state rebellion and communal violence. Chauri Chaura showed national symbols being read through local police and market conflict.

The relationship was therefore neither command nor separation. Congress supplied symbols, timing, networks and an anti-colonial horizon; local actors supplied social content and initiative. Where these aligned, mobilisation deepened. Where expectations exceeded Gandhian non-violence or the cross-class programme, control weakened. Local agency was thus the source of both the movement's unprecedented breadth and its strategic fragility.

**Scoring note:** High-band responses use contrasting regions and explain the expansion-destabilisation paradox.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Non-Cooperation failed to deliver Swaraj in one year but permanently changed the grammar of Indian nationalism. Critically examine.

**Model answer:** In immediate terms, Non-Cooperation failed. Swaraj did not arrive within one year; institutional boycotts were uneven; students and lawyers often returned; khadi imposed costs; council boycott varied; and the movement never entered its announced Bardoli tax stage. Violence at Bombay, Malabar and Chauri Chaura exposed a discipline gap. The Khilafat alliance also depended on Turkish events and weakened after 1922.

Yet the movement changed nationalist politics structurally. It made withdrawal of consent - from honours, schools, courts, councils and imported cloth - a mass method. Nagpur's linguistic PCCs, low-fee membership, AICC and Working Committee converted Congress into a continuous organisation. Peasants, workers, women, traders, students, lawyers, Muslims, Sikhs and lower-caste groups entered a shared political rhythm, though on unequal terms. National education, khadi networks and cadres survived retreat.

Most importantly, colonial power lost some of its aura of legitimacy and invincibility. Millions learned that ordinary choices could become political acts. Local agency also expanded the meaning of Swaraj beyond elite constitutional reform.

Thus the promise failed as a timetable but succeeded as political education and organisational transformation. Its grammar - disciplined mass action, boycott, construction, sacrifice and staged civil disobedience - shaped later struggles.

**Scoring note:** The best answer balances measurable failure against organisational, social and psychological transformation.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Why the movement became possible

- The detailed Rowlatt-Jallianwala history belongs to Topic 19. Its necessary bridge is the destruction of confidence in the Raj's justice.
- Punjab wrongs and failed redress transformed protest against officials into a crisis of legitimacy.
- The 1919 constitutional reforms appeared inadequate because coercion and limited reform arrived together.
- Colonial government depended on Indian participation as well as force. Non-Cooperation targeted that dependence.
- Gandhi's means-end doctrine required peaceful withdrawal, self-suffering and constructive self-rule.

## 2. Khilafat origins and meanings

- Indian Muslims feared punitive treatment of the defeated Ottoman Empire and weakening of the Sultan-Caliph.
- Three demands concerned holy places, sufficient Turkish territories and non-Muslim sovereignty over the Arab lands.
- Sekhar dates the Bombay Khilafat Committee to March 1919; all sources safely establish that it existed before the Delhi conference of November 1919.
- Key leaders: Mohammad Ali, Shaukat Ali, Maulana Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan and Hasrat Mohani; ulama including Abdul Bari mattered.
- A moderate Bombay merchant current preferred delegations; younger leaders and ulama increasingly favoured mass agitation.
- The symbol was pan-Islamic in form and anti-imperial in Indian political use.
- It united a Muslim public divided by region, language, class and sect, but did not automatically create secular or permanent unity.

## 3. Ottoman chronology

- Wartime pledges and post-war negotiations created expectations about Turkish territorial integrity.
- Sevres terms became public in May 1920; the treaty was formally signed in August 1920.
- Sevres faced Turkish nationalist resistance and was not fully implemented in its designed form.
- The Sultanate, the political monarchy, ended in 1922.
- Lausanne in 1923 superseded Sevres.
- The Caliphate, the religious-political office, was abolished in 1924.
- Never collapse Sultanate and Caliphate into one event.

## 4. Alliance and authorisation

- Gandhi saw Khilafat as an ethical duty of solidarity and an opportunity for Hindu-Muslim unity.
- Congress could widen its mass base; Khilafat leaders could connect their grievance to a national anti-British campaign.
- Established leaders feared disorder, council boycott and uncertain mass politics.
- The Allahabad Khilafat conference on 1-2 June 1920 approved staged non-cooperation.
- The hartal began on 1 August; Tilak died that day.
- Canonical material dates a formal Khilafat launch/formulation to 31 August.
- Gandhi joined the 31 August launch by surrendering his Kaiser-i-Hind Medal.
- Calcutta in September authorised Congress Non-Cooperation after debate.
- Nagpur in December ratified, staged and organisationally equipped the strategy.

## 5. Nagpur reorganisation

- PCCs were reorganised linguistically, aligning politics with languages of mobilisation.
- A Working Committee of fifteen created continuous executive leadership.
- The AICC strengthened national-provincial coordination.
- Four-anna annual membership widened formal access.
- Men and women aged twenty-one could join; the age was reduced to eighteen in 1921.
- The objective became attainment of Swaraj through peaceful and legitimate means.
- The Tilak Swaraj Fund exceeded its target of one crore rupees.
- Vijayawada in March 1921 ordered a three-month drive for funds, members and charkhas.
- The membership target of one crore was not met; the contemporary estimate was roughly fifty lakhs.
- “Mass party” describes expanded machinery and access, not complete equality inside Congress.

## 6. Programme and stages

- Titles and honours were to be surrendered, not merely hidden from public use.
- Government schools and colleges were boycotted; national education was promoted.
- Lawyers withdrew from courts; panchayat and arbitration alternatives were encouraged.
- Legislative councils and official functions were boycotted.
- Foreign cloth boycott, swadeshi, khadi and charkha made daily economic conduct political.
- Temperance weakened excise and formed part of moral reform.
- Hindu-Muslim unity and anti-untouchability were constructive aims, though the latter often received weaker implementation.
- Calls concerning civil services, police or army represented sensitive escalation, not a completed universal first stage.
- Selective civil disobedience and non-payment of taxes were conditional later stages.
- Gandhi announced Bardoli for mass civil disobedience, but it never launched.

## 7. Institutions and participants

- Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith and Bihar Vidyapith embodied national education.
- Jamia began at Aligarh and later moved to Delhi.
- C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Rajendra Prasad were prominent lawyers who gave up practice.
- Students participated substantially but sometimes returned where alternatives failed.
- Women picketed, spun, raised funds, donated jewellery and entered public protest; leadership equality did not follow automatically.
- Merchants supplied networks, funds and import pledges, while profitability and exchange rates also shaped choices.
- Khadi became a freedom symbol but could be more expensive than mill cloth.

## 8. Regional and social spread

- Participation was nationwide in aspiration but uneven in geography and social depth.
- Pre-existing mobilisation often explains strength better than simple top-down Congress creation.
- Awadh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Sind, Assam and Maharashtra saw important peasant participation.
- Andhra and Bengal included forest satyagraha or tribal mobilisation.
- Madras, Bengal and Assam saw labour or plantation participation with distinct local grievances.
- Lower-caste and non-Brahman responses varied; Justice Party opposition weakened Congress in parts of Tamil country.
- Towns often began with middle-class boycott; rural areas translated Swaraj through agrarian and local authority.

## 9. Awadh, Kisan Sabha and Eka

- Awadh tenants faced excessive rents, renewal fees, illegal levies, forced labour and ejectment.
- Eka rents were described as generally fifty per cent above the recorded rent.
- Baba Ramchandra and Kisan Sabha work preceded and fed Non-Cooperation.
- Eka meant unity and used oath-taking before sacred water.
- Its programme included recorded rent paid on time, resistance to ejectment, refusal of forced labour, no aid to criminals and obedience to panchayats.
- Madari Pasi and other lower-caste leaders represented grassroots autonomy.
- Tenants and some small zamindars participated.
- Congress contact weakened when Eka diverged over control and violence.
- Severe repression ended the movement by March 1922.
- For the bounded 2026 comparison: Bardoli 1928 opposed enhanced land revenue under tightly disciplined Congress leadership; it is not developed further here.

## 10. Malabar

- Mappila tenants faced insecure tenure, high rents, renewal fees and landlord exactions.
- The Manjeri conference of April 1920 encouraged tenant organisation.
- Tenant and Khilafat meetings shared audiences and leaders.
- Government prohibitions and arrests displaced prominent Congress-Khilafat intermediaries.
- The Tirurangadi raid on 20 August 1921, rumours and police firing supplied the proximate trigger.
- Government offices, records and landlord interests were attacked as rebellion spread.
- Later phases included violence against Hindus, coercion and communal targeting.
- Colonial repression was severe and national leadership lost control.
- Correct verdict: neither “pure peasant rebellion” nor “only communal riot”; distinguish origin, trigger and transformation.

## 11. Akali, labour, merchants, women and lower-caste politics

- Akali mobilisation originated in Sikh gurdwara reform and control.
- SGPC and Akali Dal organisation applied satyagraha and encountered colonial repression.
- Congress support and non-cooperation idiom created overlap, but Akali goals remained distinct.
- Labour mobilisation was constrained by Congress's cross-class coalition and Gandhi's capital-labour harmony.
- Assam plantation experience and industrial unrest showed worker initiative and leadership limits.
- Merchants supported boycott selectively and could resist total rupture.
- Women widened public nationalism without automatically securing equal organisational authority.
- Lower-caste and non-Brahman responses were regionally divided, not a single bloc.

## 12. Repression and the high tide

- Congress and Khilafat volunteers drilled together and connected workers at grassroots level.
- Pledges required non-violence, unity, swadeshi and khadi; Hindu volunteers were asked to oppose untouchability.
- The government declared volunteer corps illegal.
- The All-India Khilafat Committee at Karachi on 8 July 1921 declared Muslim army service religiously unlawful.
- The Ali brothers were arrested in September on the army-service issue.
- Congress leaders repeated and broadened the declaration, making it a civil-liberties confrontation.
- The Prince of Wales arrived on 17 November 1921.
- Nationwide boycott showed reach; Bombay violence exposed the discipline gap and postponed escalation.
- Ahmedabad Congress in December renewed the movement and called for volunteer enrolment and offers of arrest.
- Its pledge required non-violence, inter-communal unity, swadeshi and khadi, plus anti-untouchability work by Hindu volunteers.

## 13. Chauri Chaura and withdrawal

- On 1 February 1922 Gandhi announced an ultimatum before Bardoli mass civil disobedience.
- Chauri Chaura occurred on 4 February 1922 in Gorakhpur district.
- Police fired on a Congress-Khilafat procession; the crowd burned the station.
- Twenty-two policemen were killed.
- Police oppression, market grievances, rumour and Gandhi-raj expectations shaped the local event.
- Shahid Amin's supplementary insight is that villagers produced their own political meanings rather than merely executing national orders.
- The formal CWC Bardoli resolution stopping law-breaking activity dates to 12 February.
- Sekhar's 11 February phrasing may mark decision before formal resolution; do not collapse the distinction.
- Gandhi was arrested on 10 March 1922 and sentenced to six years.

## 14. Withdrawal debate

- Gandhi argued that non-violence defined Swaraj and that the movement lacked readiness for fiscal escalation.
- C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Lala Lajpat Rai resented retreat.
- Subhas Bose described withdrawal at peak enthusiasm as a national calamity.
- Jawaharlal Nehru also reacted critically but did not lead an open break.
- R. Palme Dutt argued that restraint protected propertied interests from radical peasants.
- Bipan Chandra answered that Chauri Chaura showed no anti-property programme, agrarian unrest was not uniformly rising, and repression avoidance was a credible motive.
- Congress caution toward rent refusal and labour conflict remains a genuine qualification.
- Verdict must use criteria: ethical consistency, organisational control, momentum, repression risk and long-term capacity.

## 15. Achievements, limits, unity and legacy

- Non-Cooperation was the first sustained all-India mass movement under Congress leadership.
- It brought new groups into a common political rhythm and weakened fear of the Raj.
- Nagpur permanently increased Congress's organisational capacity.
- National institutions, khadi networks, cadres and constructive habits survived.
- It did not win Swaraj within one year.
- Boycotts were uneven, costly and reversible.
- Class and regional interests diverged; non-violent control remained incomplete.
- The fifth stage was never tested.
- Hindu-Muslim unity was deep in meetings, volunteerism and Muslim participation.
- The unity was strained by religious rhetoric, divergent commitments, Malabar and later communal deterioration.
- Turkish abolition of the Caliphate removed the alliance's external symbol.
- These strains did not make Partition inevitable.
- Withdrawal opened a strategic debate between council entry and constructive continuity.
- Full Swarajist, No-changer and revolutionary development belongs to Topic 21.
