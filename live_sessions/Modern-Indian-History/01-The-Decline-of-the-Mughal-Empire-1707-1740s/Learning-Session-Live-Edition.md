# The Decline of the Mughal Empire, 1707-1740s - Live Session Edition

The Mughal Empire did not disappear when Aurangzeb died in 1707. What weakened was more precise: Delhi's ability to make its appointments obeyed, collect provincial revenue, discipline nobles, coordinate armies and defend the frontier. The dynasty, its titles, its administrative vocabulary and its political legitimacy survived much longer.

This session therefore asks a sharper question: **how did enforceable imperial command contract while Mughal institutions and legitimacy continued to organise regional politics?**

Ahmad Shah Abdali and Panipat remain later consequences rather than causes of the 1707-1740s contraction.

The visual separates the central state from the dynasty, the regions and society. If these are merged into one word, "decline" becomes misleading.

### Diagnostic 1: separate four meanings of decline

In ordinary language, an empire is effective when its commands travel. A governor is transferred and actually leaves. A jagirdar receives an assignment that yields enough revenue to maintain troops. Provincial officials remit money. Noble contingents assemble under one command. A frontier warning produces a timely response.

**Mughal decline** in this topic means the progressive loss of that enforceable, empire-wide fiscal-military command.

It does **not** mean:

- the dynasty ended in 1707;
- every province ceased to be governed;
- trade and agriculture collapsed everywhere;
- Persian administration vanished;
- regional rulers immediately declared modern national independence; or
- British conquest followed automatically.

### Diagnostic 2: reconstruct the imperial machine

The Mughal Empire was not a modern unitary bureaucracy. It was a layered political arrangement.

| Element | Plain-language function | Technical expression |
|---|---|---|
| Emperor | supreme allocator of status, office and favour | mansab, title, farman, jagir transfer |
| Nobility | supplied administration and military service | mansabdars and their contingents |
| Revenue assignment | paid many officials through a claim on land revenue | jagir |
| Crown revenue | supported the central treasury more directly | khalisa |
| Provincial order | carried executive and fiscal authority outward | subahdar and provincial diwan |
| Local intermediaries | connected assessment with cultivation and coercion | zamindars, qanungos, chaudhuris |
| Political legitimacy | made commands and succession claims publicly intelligible | Timurid dynasty, coin, khutba, seal |

The system's strength was flexibility. The emperor could transfer nobles, prevent permanent local entrenchment and combine diverse elites within one hierarchy.

Its vulnerability was dependence on credible circulation:

```text
productive assignment
      -> noble can maintain service
      -> centre can enforce collection
      -> provincial revenue continues
      -> emperor can reward and transfer again
```

If assignments yielded less than their paper value, if transfers were resisted, or if governors retained revenue, the same loop reversed.

### Threshold test: what changed in 1707?

Aurangzeb died at Ahmadnagar in 1707 after the long Deccan phase of his reign. The empire remained formidable. Bipan Chandra emphasises that administration was still capable, the army remained substantial and the dynasty commanded respect. Recovery was possible.

Yet the inheritance was difficult:

- prolonged southern war had consumed men, money and attention;
- Maratha resistance remained unresolved;
- new Deccani and Maratha claimants enlarged the competition for imperial rewards;
- some Rajput relationships had been damaged;
- northern supervision and north-western defence had suffered; and
- agrarian and jagirdari pressures already existed.

The correct opening judgment is therefore: **1707 exposed and transmitted accumulated strain; it did not create every weakness overnight.**

### Stress-test the definition: Bengal

Think of the empire as a large credit network. The emperor's promise of a rank or jagir works only if the assignment can be delivered and the revenue realised. Once participants doubt the promise, they seek safer provincial bases.

**What the analogy establishes:** political authority depended on confidence that imperial rewards and sanctions would be implemented.

**Where it stops:** an empire is not merely a financial firm. Dynasty, military force, local rights, identity and sacred-political legitimacy also shaped obedience.

### Boundary challenge: did regional growth disprove decline?

**Objection:** If the dynasty survived and regional states continued Mughal forms, perhaps there was no real decline - only decentralisation.

**Reply:** The loss of collective defence, dependable remittance and central transfer power was real. Karnal in 1739 cannot be explained as a harmless administrative rearrangement. Regional vitality qualifies the geographical scale of decline; it does not erase the collapse of imperial coordination.

### Diagnostic verdict

The safest formula is:

> **Decline at the centre, redistribution in the regions, continuity in political form and uneven change in economy and culture.**

### Translate the diagnosis into an UPSC argument

- **Syllabus use:** foundation for modern Indian history "from about the middle of the eighteenth century".
- **Prelims trap:** dynasty, sovereignty and effective command are not identical.
- **Mains use:** define the level of decline before listing causes.
- **PYQ status:** no direct routed 2018-2026 question tests this exact bounded problem.
- **Probable framing:** "The Mughal Empire declined as a fiscal-military system before it disappeared as a political idea. Examine."

### Four-level recall sheet

1. Mughal decline means contraction of enforceable central fiscal-military command.
2. The dynasty did not end in 1707.
3. The emperor allocated mansabs, offices, titles and jagirs.
4. Jagirs were revenue assignments, not ordinary private ownership of the soil.
5. Khalisa revenue was more directly available to the crown.
6. Provincial government depended on both executive and revenue institutions.
7. Zamindars were locally rooted intermediaries with rights and armed influence.
8. The system required credible transfer, audit, remittance and arbitration.
9. Aurangzeb left a formidable but heavily strained empire.
10. Central decline could coexist with regional state-building.
11. Symbolic legitimacy could survive coercive weakness.
12. The best initial frame is centre-versus-region, not collapse-versus-prosperity.

- **C — Correct:** Surviving institutions made recovery conceivable, while inherited strains made the contested succession unusually damaging.

### Apply the four-level definition

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish the decline of Mughal imperial command from the disappearance of the Mughal political order.

**Model answer**

Mughal imperial decline after 1707 meant the weakening of Delhi's ability to enforce appointments, collect provincial revenue, maintain noble contingents and defend the frontier. It did not mean that the dynasty or its political language disappeared.

Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad increasingly controlled revenue, military force and succession, yet their rulers retained Mughal offices, titles, coins or khutba. Maratha leaders also sought imperial sanads for fiscal claims. The emperor's authority therefore lost coercive depth before it lost legitimating value.

This distinction explains why Nadir Shah could defeat the imperial host at Karnal in 1739 while Muhammad Shah remained emperor afterward. It also prevents the error of treating regional state formation as political emptiness. The period was a contraction of central command inside a continuing Mughal institutional and symbolic order.

**Scoring guidance:** reward a precise definition, one provincial example, one legitimacy example and a concluding distinction between force and form.

### Visual: why succession consumed the state

```text
NO FIXED PRIMOGENITURE
        |
each prince needs money + provincial base + nobles + troops
        |
field battle decides the throne
        |
victor must reward supporters
        |
more mansabs, offices and jagir claims

```

The succession system converted a dynastic contest into an empire-wide redistribution of soldiers, offices and revenue.

### Timeline opening: the throne is decided in battle

Aurangzeb's sons included Muazzam, Azam and Kam Bakhsh. Muazzam defeated Azam at the Battle of Jajau in June 1707 and became **Bahadur Shah I**, also known as Shah Alam I. Kam Bakhsh was defeated near Hyderabad in January 1709.

The new emperor was about sixty-five years old. Bipan Chandra describes him as learned, dignified and able. This matters because it prevents a lazy explanation: decline did not begin because the first successor was simply incompetent.

### Policy ledger: five fronts, one short reign

Bahadur Shah faced several fronts at once:

| Arena | His approach | Why settlement remained incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Rajputs | moved from coercion toward restoration and accommodation | high mansabs and major governorships remained disputed |
| Marathas | granted sardeshmukhi but not a complete chauth settlement | Shahu-Tarabai rivalry and competing claims continued |
| Bundelas | conciliated Chhatrasal | accommodation did not solve wider regional autonomy |
| Jats | conciliated Churaman tactically | local armed power remained outside dependable control |
| Sikhs | initially sought accommodation, then campaigned against Banda Bahadur | military action captured positions but did not erase mobilisation |

The lesson is not that conciliation was false. It is that a short reign could not settle every relationship while also rewarding the coalition that had won the throne.

### Decision point: when compromise remains incomplete

Consider the Maratha case. Recognising one fiscal claim but not another could reduce immediate hostility without fixing who had the lawful right to collect, from which territory and under whose enforcement.

```text
partial recognition
      -> rival interpretations remain
      -> collectors and commanders test the boundary
      -> local revenue faces overlapping claims
      -> centre must bargain again
```

**Limit of the example:** not every dispute came from legal ambiguity; military opportunity and local power also mattered.

### The hidden bill after victory

Bahadur Shah's promotions and jagir grants were politically understandable. A victor in a succession war needed to reward supporters and reconcile opponents. Yet each grant increased demand on the assignment system. Bipan Chandra records serious deterioration in state finances during the reign.

This produces an important causal distinction:

- **Immediate decision:** grant rank and assignment to stabilise support.
- **Structural consequence:** more claims compete for productive revenue.

### Counterfactual test: would more time have solved the crisis?

**Objection:** Bahadur Shah's short reign means he should be treated only as a missed opportunity, not as part of the decline.

**Reply:** His reign demonstrates the mechanism of decline. Even an able, conciliatory emperor could not simultaneously settle the Rajputs, Marathas and Sikhs, restore finances and satisfy noble supporters. The constraint was institutional, though choices still mattered.

### Historical verdict

Bahadur Shah neither saved nor destroyed the empire. He slowed some conflicts but passed on unresolved settlements and worsened reward pressure. His reign is evidence against both extreme personal blame and total structural determinism.

### Exam use from the chronology

- **Prelims:** Bahadur Shah I, Jajau, Shahu, Banda Bahadur.
- **Mains:** use him to show that "weak successors" is an incomplete thesis.
- **Probable framing:** "Bahadur Shah's reign reveals both the recoverability and the structural constraints of the post-Aurangzeb empire. Discuss."
- **Boundary:** detailed Sikh state formation belongs beyond this topic.

### Chronology and causation recall

1. Muazzam became Bahadur Shah I after Jajau in 1707.
2. Kam Bakhsh was defeated near Hyderabad in 1709.
3. Bahadur Shah was capable and experienced.
4. He pursued conciliation with Rajputs, Marathas, Jats and Bundelas.
5. His Maratha settlement remained partial.
6. Shahu and Tarabai represented rival Maratha claims.
7. Banda Bahadur kept Punjab militarily demanding.
8. Succession victory required rewards to supporters.
9. Promotions and jagir grants increased fiscal pressure.
10. A short reign limited the consolidation of compromise.
11. Able rulership could mitigate but not erase structural constraints.

#### Answers and option explanations

**MCQ 4: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Bahadur Shah combined accommodation and force across arenas; the Maratha settlement was partial rather than primarily coercive.
- **B — Incorrect:** He used promotions and grants to build support and actively negotiated several regional conflicts.

### Chronological Mains drill

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why does Bahadur Shah I weaken a purely personality-based explanation of Mughal decline?

**Model answer**

Bahadur Shah I was experienced, conciliatory and administratively capable. He restored accommodation with Rajput rulers, worked with Churaman Jat and Chhatrasal Bundela, and attempted a settlement with Maratha interests. His reign therefore shows that the empire did not immediately fall into the hands of uniformly incompetent rulers.

Yet he faced simultaneous constraints. The 1707 succession had consumed resources; supporters expected mansabs and jagirs; Shahu and Tarabai divided Maratha politics; Banda Bahadur sustained pressure in Punjab; and Rajput concessions remained contested. Partial settlements reduced immediate conflict but did not rebuild reliable revenue or obedience.

His short reign ended in 1712 before compromise could become stable administration. Bahadur Shah thus demonstrates that individual capacity mattered, but an able emperor operated inside a strained fiscal-military and political structure that no brief personal effort could easily repair.

**Scoring guidance:** reward the direct challenge to personality theory, named policy evidence and a final balance between agency and structure.

### Visual: the strong-wazir dilemma

```text
WEAK OR DISTRUSTFUL EMPEROR
          |
needs an able wazir to coordinate finance and nobles
          |
ABLE WAZIR CENTRALISES DECISIONS
          |
emperor fears replacement + nobles fear exclusion
          |
intrigue blocks reform
```

The same ministerial strength that could restore administration could also make the emperor and rival nobles feel unsafe.

### Coalition starting point: Jahandar Shah's accession

After Bahadur Shah died in 1712, another succession conflict followed. Jahandar Shah prevailed with the support of **Zulfiqar Khan**, one of the most powerful nobles of the time. The reign lasted only until January 1713.

Jahandar Shah is often remembered through moralising descriptions of court pleasure. That is weak explanation. The more instructive issue is the relation between ruler and minister.

### Reform dossier: Zulfiqar Khan's programme

Zulfiqar Khan believed imperial recovery required a broad political coalition and tighter administration.

He:

- cultivated Rajput rulers;
- confirmed an arrangement concerning Maratha chauth and sardeshmukhi;
- conciliated Churaman Jat and Chhatrasal Bundela;
- checked reckless growth of jagirs and offices;
- tried to compel mansabdars to maintain their required troops; and
- abolished jizya during the reign.

These measures addressed real problems: narrow coalitions, paper military strength and excessive patronage.

### Fiscal experiment: revenue farming

The important technical term is **ijara**, or revenue farming.

```text
government contracts a fixed payment
          |
revenue farmer obtains collection right
          |
farmer keeps collection above the contracted sum
          |
cash reaches government quickly
          |
incentive for aggressive extraction rises
```

Why use it? A cash-strapped government receives a predictable amount without maintaining the whole collection apparatus.

Why is it dangerous? The contractor's profit grows when collection exceeds the promised payment. Short horizons and coercive power can increase pressure on cultivators.

**Limit:** revenue farming was not automatically identical everywhere, and contractors could also possess useful capital and local knowledge. The institutional incentive, not a universal moral label, is the exam point.

### Political-feasibility test

Zulfiqar Khan's power alarmed rival nobles. Jahandar Shah himself feared that the wazir might become too strong. Reform therefore lacked the trust needed for implementation.

This is a recurring pattern:

| Reform need | Political fear |
|---|---|
| check promotions | supporters lose expected rewards |
| inspect troop quotas | nobles expose under-maintained contingents |
| restrain jagir grants | factions lose patronage |
| empower wazir | emperor fears becoming ceremonial |
| conciliate regional chiefs | rivals call compromise surrender |

The imperial centre did not lack all policy ideas. It lacked a stable coalition willing to bear the costs of reform.

### Failure sequence

Farrukhsiyar, supported by the Sayyid Brothers, defeated Jahandar Shah near Agra in January 1713. Zulfiqar Khan was killed. The episode taught nobles two lessons: a powerful minister could determine succession, and losing a succession struggle could destroy both office and life.

### Defence of the experiment, and its limit

**Objection:** If Zulfiqar Khan spread ijara, he should be treated as an agent of decline rather than a reformer.

**Reply:** His record was contradictory. He attempted troop and jagir discipline while using a cash-raising device that intensified agrarian risks. Historical evaluation should preserve both functions instead of forcing him into a hero-or-villain category.

### Institutional verdict

The Zulfiqar experiment shows that administrative capacity survived. Its failure came from contradictory fiscal choices and, more fundamentally, the inability to combine ministerial authority with imperial trust and noble consent.

### Compare reform intent with governing capacity

- **Prelims distinction:** wazir was not emperor; ijara was not jagir.
- **Mains use:** evidence that remedies existed but coalition incentives blocked them.
- **Probable framing:** "The later Mughal crisis was not a want of reform proposals but a want of political capacity to enforce them. Explain with reference to Zulfiqar Khan."

### Reform ledger

1. Jahandar Shah succeeded Bahadur Shah in 1712.
2. Zulfiqar Khan was the decisive supporting noble and became wazir.
3. He sought a broad coalition with Rajputs, Marathas, Jats and Bundelas.
4. He attempted to check excessive jagir and office growth.
5. He pressed mansabdars to maintain sanctioned contingents.
6. Ijara meant contracting revenue collection for a fixed government payment.
7. Revenue farming offered cash predictability but encouraged over-extraction.
8. A strong wazir threatened emperor and rival nobles.
9. Court mistrust paralysed implementation.
10. Farrukhsiyar and the Sayyids ended the experiment in 1713.
11. The episode demonstrates policy capacity without coalition durability.

### Write from policy to outcome

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The failure of Zulfiqar Khan was a failure of political coalition as much as of fiscal policy." Discuss.

**Model answer**

Zulfiqar Khan entered the wizarat under Jahandar Shah with a plausible recovery programme. He favoured accommodation with Rajputs, Marathas, Jats and Bundelas, tried to restrain excessive jagir and office grants, and sought enforcement of mansabdars' troop obligations. These measures recognised that military weakness and a narrow ruling coalition were connected.

His fiscal approach, however, was contradictory. The spread of ijara offered immediate and predictable cash to government but allowed revenue farmers to profit from collection above the contracted sum. It could intensify pressure on cultivators and weaken direct administrative control.

The deeper obstacle was political. Effective reform required an empowered wazir, but an empowered wazir frightened Jahandar Shah and excluded rival noble clients. The emperor intrigued against his own minister, while other factions supported Farrukhsiyar and the Sayyid Brothers. In January 1713, the reform coalition collapsed with military defeat.

Zulfiqar Khan therefore cannot be described simply as either reformer or destroyer. His programme contained both discipline and extraction, but its failure chiefly revealed that the court lacked a durable bargain among emperor, minister and nobility.

**Scoring guidance:** reward evidence from coalition policy, jagir discipline, ijara and the emperor-wazir trust problem; require a balanced verdict.

### Visual: the levers of sovereignty

```text
                         IMPERIAL POWER
                               |
       +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
       |           |           |           |           |
     seal       appointments  jagirs      army      provinces
       |           |           |           |           |
 FARRUKHSIYAR wants personal control over these levers
                               X
 SAYYID ABDULLAH and HUSAIN ALI claim authority needed to govern
```

The conflict was not merely about pride. It was about who controlled the instruments that made an imperial order effective.

### Entry condition: a dependent emperor

Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali Khan Baraha helped Farrukhsiyar defeat Jahandar Shah in 1713. Abdullah became wazir; Husain Ali became mir bakhshi and later held major provincial command.

Because they had supplied the military coalition that won the throne, they expected to control policy. Farrukhsiyar wanted to rule personally and repeatedly tried to weaken them through favourites and rival nobles.

### Evidence caution: faction labels

Mughal nobles are often grouped as Turani, Irani, Afghan, Hindustani or Baraha, and Deccani. These terms indicate origin, kinship, recruitment and service networks.

They were **not** fixed modern political parties.

| Label | What it may explain | What it cannot safely prove |
|---|---|---|
| Turani | Central Asian service and affinity networks | permanent unity on every issue |
| Irani | Persian-linked recruitment and patronage | automatic Shia political solidarity |
| Hindustani/Baraha | Indian-born and Sayyid networks | a coherent national faction |
| Afghan | military and kinship connections | one stable bloc across provinces |
| Deccani/Maratha entrants | incorporation from southern politics | common loyalty to Delhi |

Office, jagir, kinship, provincial base and immediate survival could cross these labels.

### Governing record: the Sayyids' policies

The Sayyid Brothers pursued accommodation:

- jizya was abolished;
- pilgrim taxes were removed from a number of places;
- Ajit Singh and Jai Singh received influence;
- Churaman Jat was cultivated; and
- an agreement with Shahu recognised swarajya and fiscal claims connected with chauth and sardeshmukhi in return for support.

These policies aimed to enlarge the ruling coalition. They also created opponents among nobles who feared the Sayyids' patronage monopoly or disliked their alliances.

### Sovereignty test: ministry becomes king-making

The repeated struggle ended in 1719. Farrukhsiyar was deposed and killed. Two short-lived emperors, Rafi-ud-Darajat and Shah Jahan II, followed before Muhammad Shah was installed.

This mattered beyond one death.

```text
nobles make emperor
      -> emperor resists
      -> nobles depose emperor
      -> throne appears dependent on armed patrons
      -> every faction sees succession as capturable
```

The Sayyids did not abolish the dynasty. They needed Timurid legitimacy. But by demonstrating that an emperor could be made and unmade, they weakened the aura that made the dynasty an arbiter above faction.

### Causal verdict: symptom, cause or both?

The Sayyids were a **symptom** because noble military coalitions already determined accession.

They were also a **cause** because:

- deposition deepened insecurity;
- promises of offices and jagirs intensified competition;
- provincial and Maratha alliances entered court struggles; and
- rivals mobilised dynasty, religion and "old nobility" against them.

Husain Ali was assassinated in 1720; Abdullah Khan was defeated. Their fall ended their dominance, not the structural contest.

### Document boundary: the 1717 farman

Farrukhsiyar's 1717 farman gave the English East India Company important commercial privileges in Bengal. It was not a grant of territorial sovereignty. The later disputes over those privileges and the Company's conflict with Bengal arose in a subsequent phase of eighteenth-century expansion.

Here it serves one limited purpose: a politically dependent emperor could still issue an imperial document with long consequences.

### Best defence of the king-makers

**Objection:** The Sayyids were reforming ministers unfairly demonised by rivals; their fall, not their dominance, caused decline.

**Reply:** Their conciliatory policies deserve recognition, but the coercive deposition and multiplication of dependent emperors imposed a real legitimacy cost. A balanced answer must treat them as reformers, faction leaders and accelerants at the same time.

### Build a sovereignty answer

- **Prelims:** Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali; king-makers; Farrukhsiyar; 1719.
- **Mains:** distinguish the substance of sovereignty from the title of emperor.
- **Trap:** faction labels are fluid networks, not rigid ethnic parties.
- **Probable framing:** "The Sayyid Brothers did not create the Mughal crisis, but they changed its political form. Examine."

2. Abdullah Khan became wazir.

Yet their struggle with Farrukhsiyar over appointments, seal, jagirs and military resources paralysed government.

### Visual: an exit from the centre


The visual shows decline as the movement of capable service away from Delhi, not the disappearance of capable people.

### Recovery window: why the long reign mattered

Muhammad Shah ruled from 1719 to 1748. The end of rapid enthronements created time for recovery. Imperial prestige still mattered, artillery remained formidable and northern administration had not completely disintegrated.

This makes the subsequent contraction historically important: the empire did not fail for lack of any opportunity.

### Reform memorandum: Nizam-ul-Mulk's diagnosis

Nizam-ul-Mulk became wazir in 1722. He sought to:

- scrutinise promotions and jagirs;
- reduce revenue farming and corruption;
- protect crown resources;
- restore discipline among nobles; and
- strengthen administrative hierarchy.

But reform created immediate losers. Court favourites and clients benefited from the practices he wished to restrict. Muhammad Shah did not sustain full trust in his wazir.

### Exit decision: the 1724 turn

Nizam-ul-Mulk left the court for the Deccan. He defeated Mubariz Khan at Shakar Kheda in 1724 and consolidated what became Hyderabad.

This was not a modern declaration of national independence. It was:

```text
imperial rank + Deccan viceroyalty + personal military following
          + regional revenue networks
          = autonomy in substance, Mughal continuity in form
```

Coins, khutba, titles and mansab vocabulary continued to connect Hyderabad to the emperor. War, appointment, revenue and diplomacy increasingly operated without Delhi's direction.

### Competing explanation: court failure or institutional exit?

The sobriquet "Rangila" and stories about Muhammad Shah's pleasures cannot explain why Nizam-ul-Mulk found regional power more secure than central reform.

The central analytical question is incentive:

- At Delhi, reform exposed Nizam to faction and imperial suspicion.
- In the Deccan, the same administrative and military capacity could build a durable base.

The empire's problem was not absence of talent. It was inability to align talented nobles' survival with service to the centre.

### Hyderabad as evidence, with limits

Nizam's departure resembles an experienced manager leaving a failing headquarters to control a profitable regional division.

**What it establishes:** decentralisation can result from rational elite choice under institutional uncertainty.

**Where it stops:** Hyderabad was a political society with military, landed and cultural relationships, not a commercial branch office.

### Counterfactual: could Delhi still have recovered?

**Objection:** Since Nizam retained Mughal symbols, Hyderabad should not count as evidence of imperial decline.

**Reply:** The distinction is between legitimacy and command. Continued symbols made regional rule easier to legitimise, but Delhi's inability to control Hyderabad's war, appointments and revenue is precisely evidence of contraction.

### Convert an elite exit into a causal argument

- **Prelims:** Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah; 1724; Hyderabad.
- **Mains:** use "flight of capacity from centre to region".
- **Trap:** symbolic allegiance does not prove effective subordination.
- **Boundary:** comparative Hyderabad institutions require separate treatment.

### Recovery-versus-exit ledger

1. Muhammad Shah reigned from 1719 to 1748.
2. The end of rapid enthronements created a recovery opportunity.
3. Nizam-ul-Mulk became wazir in 1722.
4. His reform programme threatened court clients.
5. Imperial mistrust weakened implementation.
6. Nizam returned to the Deccan.
7. Shakar Kheda in 1724 enabled Hyderabad's consolidation.
8. Hyderabad retained Mughal titles and symbols.
9. It controlled major practical functions regionally.
10. Capable nobles did not disappear; they redirected capacity.
11. Political contraction and cultural vitality could coexist under Muhammad Shah.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The departure of Nizam-ul-Mulk symbolised not the disappearance of Mughal institutions but their regional appropriation." Discuss.

**Model answer**

Nizam-ul-Mulk's career began within the Mughal imperial order. He possessed high mansab, provincial experience and the wizarat. His attempted reforms at Delhi sought stricter jagir control, reduced revenue abuse and restoration of noble discipline.

The programme failed because it threatened court clients and lacked sustained imperial support. Nizam therefore returned to the Deccan, defeated Mubariz Khan at Shakar Kheda in 1724 and consolidated Hyderabad. He used an imperial viceroyalty, a personal military following and regional revenue networks to exercise war, appointment and diplomacy with little reference to Delhi.

Yet Hyderabad retained Mughal political forms. Titles, mansab vocabulary, coin and khutba continued to acknowledge the emperor. These were not meaningless remnants: they reduced the legitimacy cost of regional autonomy.

The case thus reveals two simultaneous processes. The centre lost control over resources and office-holders, while the administrative and symbolic language of empire remained useful. Mughal institutions survived by changing location and political content. Nizam's departure was therefore a decisive sign of central contraction, not a clean institutional rupture.

## Lesson 6 - The jagirdari crisis: how paper rank lost military substance

CA found: none authoritative; an exam-preparation discussion in September 2026 repeated established doctrine but added no new historical evidence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the fiscal-military feedback loop

```text
mansab promises rank and service obligation
          |
jagir should finance salary and troops
          |
paper jama exceeds real hasil / suitable paibaqi is scarce
          |
be-jagiri, arrears or low-yield assignment
          |
short-term extraction or under-maintained contingent
          |
weaker collection and coercion
          |
still lower hasil + fiercer competition for good jagirs
```

This is the core structural mechanism. The crisis was not simply "corruption"; it was a widening gap between promised status, assigned revenue and actual military service.

### 1. Build the vocabulary from the problem

Suppose the emperor promotes a noble and expects him to maintain cavalry. The noble requires revenue.

| Term | Precise meaning | Common confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Mansab | numerical rank and service status | not a piece of land |
| Jagir | assigned claim on revenue to meet salary and service | not necessarily hereditary ownership |
| Khalisa | revenue reserved more directly for the crown | not all imperial territory |
| Paibaqi | revenue area available or awaiting assignment | not a guaranteed high-yield reserve |
| Be-jagiri | absence, delay or shortage of a suitable assignment | not a synonym for all corruption |
| Jama | assessed revenue on paper | not cash actually collected |
| Hasil | revenue actually realised | may fall below jama |

### 2. Why expansion had previously helped

Expansion could add territories and revenue assignments. It also created new claimants, but the emperor could often reward them from a growing pool.

When expansion slowed while the number and expectations of mansabdars increased, competition intensified. The Deccan made the problem sharper: war created new service claims, but insecure and contested areas did not necessarily yield their assessed revenue.

### 3. Satish Chandra's structural thesis

The jagirdari-crisis interpretation argues:

1. the number and claims of mansabdars increased;
2. suitable revenue-yielding assignments did not keep pace;
3. nobles competed for better jagirs and offices;
4. frequent transfer encouraged immediate extraction;
5. inadequate hasil weakened troop maintenance;
6. factional control of appointment and transfer became more valuable; and
7. the emperor lost the capacity to satisfy and discipline the service elite.

The thesis connects court faction to countryside and battlefield.

### 4. Paibaqi nuance

It is unsafe to write, "There was no land left." Paibaqi existed, but availability on paper did not guarantee secure and productive collection. An assignment could be:

- devastated by war;
- subject to Maratha claims;
- resisted by zamindars;
- over-assessed;
- already promised;
- difficult to reach; or
- politically costly to transfer.

The real issue was **credible assignability**, not simple geographical absence.

### 5. Jama-hasil example

Imagine a jagir assessed at 10 lakh rupees. The noble plans troops and household expenditure around that jama. War, arrears and resistance allow only 6 lakh to be realised.

The four-lakh gap can produce several responses:

- reduce the troop contingent;
- borrow from bankers;
- press cultivators and zamindars;
- seek transfer to a better jagir;
- join a faction controlling appointments; or
- convert provincial office into a more secure hereditary base.

**Limit:** the numbers are illustrative, not a historical statistic.

### Diagnostic challenge: was the problem only physical scarcity?

An analytical qualification asks whether allocation choices, especially the treatment of Deccan revenue and khalisa, made part of the scarcity political or policy-produced rather than simply geographical.

This objection improves the thesis. It reminds us to separate:

- total assessed resources;
- secure realised revenue;
- crown cash;
- assignable revenue; and
- political distribution.

It does not eliminate the crisis. It changes "not enough land existed" into "the imperial system could not turn available resources into credible assignments and service."

### Diagnostic verdict

The jagirdari thesis is strongest as a mechanism, not a universal measurement. It explains why fiscal shortfall generated faction, extraction and military weakness. It must be combined with agrarian resistance, regional variation, succession politics and Deccan war.

### How UPSC can test the mechanism

- **Prelims:** distinguish mansab, jagir, khalisa, paibaqi, be-jagiri, jama and hasil.
- **Mains:** use the loop, not a list.
- **Trap:** jagirdari crisis was not merely moral corruption or total absence of assignments.
- **Probable framing:** "How did the jama-hasil gap transform an administrative strain into a political and military crisis?"

### Institutional diagnostic checklist

1. Mansab defined rank and service obligation.
2. Jagir supplied a revenue claim for salary and service.
3. Khalisa supported the crown more directly.
4. Paibaqi meant available or unassigned revenue area.
5. Be-jagiri meant lack or delay of a suitable assignment.
6. Jama was assessment; hasil was realisation.
7. A paper rank did not guarantee a paid contingent.
8. Frequent transfer could encourage short-term extraction.
9. Deccan conflict widened the gap between assessment and collection.
10. Jagir competition fed noble faction.
11. Allocation choices could make the crisis partly policy-produced.
12. The thesis is a causal mechanism, not a claim of identical conditions everywhere.
13. Structural explanation must connect centre, noble, village and army.

### Write the structural answer

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine the jagirdari crisis as an explanation of Mughal decline.

**Model answer**

The jagirdari thesis explains decline by connecting imperial patronage with rural revenue and military service. Mansabdars expected assignments whose hasil could finance salary and contingents. As claims increased and secure productive paibaqi failed to keep pace, be-jagiri and competition for better jagirs intensified.

Frequent transfer encouraged short-term extraction. Where jama exceeded hasil, nobles cut troop expenditure, borrowed, pressured zamindars or joined factions controlling appointment and transfer. Fiscal weakness thus reduced coercion; weaker coercion further reduced collection. The crisis helps explain both court rivalry and military under-performance.

However, the thesis should not be written as literal disappearance of land. Assignments varied by security, assessment and political accessibility. Allocation choices and the treatment of khalisa could make scarcity partly policy-produced. Regional evidence also shows that Bengal and other areas could retain strong revenue and commerce even while Delhi lost control.

The jagirdari crisis is therefore strongest as a variable mechanism linking elite competition, agrarian pressure and military weakness. It is central but not sufficient without succession, Deccan war, local resistance and provincial autonomy.

The visual prevents a common error: hardship alone does not explain why some protests become lasting political powers.

### Actor map: who was the zamindar?

A zamindar was not merely a salaried tax collector and not automatically a European-style landlord. The category varied widely.

Zamindars could possess:

- hereditary local rights;
- a share in revenue;
- knowledge of cultivation and households;
- clan, caste or community ties;
- forts and armed retainers; and
- influence over village society.

The Mughal state needed their cooperation. When the centre weakened, the same local depth enabled withholding, bargaining or rebellion.

### Transmission chain: how pressure travelled downward

```text
centre needs revenue
      -> jagirdar or khalisa official demands collection
      -> revenue farmer seeks contracted sum plus profit
      -> zamindar transmits, negotiates or resists
      -> cultivator bears crop, price, war and tax risk
```

The state could respond to declining remittance by raising pressure. This often worsened the cause of decline:

- peasants fled or concealed produce;
- zamindars withheld payment;
- collection required greater force;
- armies became more expensive; and
- realised revenue fell further.

### Interpretation I: agrarian crisis

Irfan Habib and the Aligarh materialist tradition emphasise high surplus extraction and agrarian resistance. This reading restores peasants and zamindars to a story otherwise dominated by emperors and nobles.

It helps explain why Jat, Sikh and other movements had rural social bases.

But it requires qualification:

- resistance could reflect opportunity as well as misery;
- local leaders had their own claims;
- some regions expanded cultivation;
- commercialisation could empower new intermediaries; and
- rural society was not one homogeneous class.

### Case evidence I: Jat assertion near the core

Churaman and Jat power around Agra-Mathura drew on fortified countryside, zamindari authority and peasant support. The Jats could negotiate with the court and also disrupt imperial communication.

The immediate lesson is that Delhi struggled to discipline powerful local intermediaries near its own core. The later development of Bharatpur is a separate stage of regional state formation.

### Case evidence II: Sikh assertion as an enforcement test

After Guru Gobind Singh, Banda Bahadur led a major challenge in Punjab. Bahadur Shah campaigned against the movement, and Mughal forces captured strongholds, but mobilisation persisted beyond one campaign.

The immediate lesson is that frontier and provincial enforcement absorbed imperial resources, while religious-community organisation could combine with agrarian grievance and armed leadership. The later misls and Sikh kingdom belong to a subsequent historical phase.

### Scale warning: region is not empire

A heavily taxed village is like dry fuel, but it does not ignite without a spark and organisation.

**What it establishes:** grievance requires leadership and networks to become sustained political action.

**Where it stops:** rural actors possessed agency and objectives; they were not passive material waiting for elite ignition.

### Revisionist challenge: was this rural vitality?

**Objection:** If cultivation and commerce grew in some regions, "agrarian crisis" is false.

**Reply:** Growth in total output or monetisation can coexist with unequal extraction, peasant insecurity and conflict over who controls surplus. The crisis need not mean universal production collapse; it can mean a destabilising struggle over revenue and rights.

### Transmission verdict

Agrarian stress weakened the empire through collection failure and local militarisation. It became politically decisive where zamindars, chiefs or organised communities converted social grievance into durable control.

The Deccan was not simply another territory. It changed the cost, distance and social composition of imperial rule.

### Opening balance sheet: conquest without settlement

An empire can become weaker by expanding if new territory costs more to secure than it reliably yields. Aurangzeb's long Deccan commitment is the classic example.

Annexing Bijapur and Golconda removed buffer states but created direct responsibility for their administration and defence. Maratha resistance survived through mobility, forts, local knowledge and dispersed leadership.

The post-Aurangzeb court inherited:

- garrisons and communications across great distance;
- Maratha power not eliminated;
- new Deccani and Maratha entrants seeking rank and assignment;

### Bargaining field: Shahu and divided Maratha legitimacy

Shahu was released in 1707. His claim competed with Tarabai's political base. Mughal policy could use this division, but it could not dictate the outcome.

Bahadur Shah recognised sardeshmukhi but did not complete a settlement of chauth or Maratha succession. Zulfiqar Khan moved toward agreement. The Sayyid-Maratha settlement of 1718-19 recognised Shahu's swarajya and fiscal claims in exchange for service and support.

```text
          |

          |
```

A document made a claim more legitimate; it did not make implementation automatic.

Under Baji Rao I from 1720, Maratha strategy reached toward Malwa, Gujarat and northern politics. For the present causal chain, this shows:

- reduced imperial revenue;
- increased bargaining by regional actors; and
- the centre's inability to monopolise fiscal claims.

The confederacy's internal development, regional administration and the road to Panipat form a later comparative sequence.

**Reply:** Seeking sanction shows the survival of Mughal legitimacy. Collecting revenue through independent military pressure shows the loss of Mughal monopoly over coercion. The same evidence demonstrates continuity of form and contraction of force.

### Deccan inheritance verdict

Aurangzeb's Deccan policy was neither the sole cause nor irrelevant background. It amplified distance, cost, jagir competition and Maratha leverage. Post-1707 courts then failed to convert negotiated recognition into stable enforcement.

**Model answer**

- the governor disciplined local power through his own forces;

Delhi's retreat did not leave empty space. Authority was exercised by provincial regimes, zamindars, chiefs, bankers and military groups.

- no centre commanded all regions;
- several actors claimed overlapping rights;
- imperial defence lost shared finance; and

### Visual: an invasion as an institutional stress test

```text

          |
no coherent frontier response
          |
Nadir: Qandahar -> Kabul -> Peshawar -> Lahore
          |
Mughal nobles assemble but dispute plan and command
          |

          |

          |

```

Karnal was not a battle between guns and swords. It was a test of whether the Mughal state could convert resources into coordinated action.

### Source question I: motive, pretext and opportunity

Nadir had rebuilt Persian power through prolonged warfare. His state and army required resources. India's accumulated wealth and the visible weakness of the Mughal frontier made invasion attractive.

Diplomatic complaints concerning Afghan fugitives and frontier control supplied a pretext. They do not by themselves explain the invasion.

**Source caution:** claims that Nizam-ul-Mulk or Saadat Khan invited Nadir lack secure documentary proof and must not be stated as fact.

### Source question II: what the warnings prove

The north-west defence system depended on:

- timely intelligence;
- diplomacy;
- maintained garrisons;
- provincial reinforcement;
- subsidies and relations with frontier groups; and
- decisive central command.

Delhi received warnings but did not translate them into an integrated response. By the time Lahore fell, preparation was hurried.

### Battlefield reconstruction: Karnal

The conventional modern date of the Battle of Karnal is **24 February 1739**. Some older printed narratives use a different February date, which is why date control matters.

The Mughal camp included Muhammad Shah, Nizam-ul-Mulk, Saadat Khan and Khan-i-Dauran. The army had cavalry, artillery and large resources. Its weakness lay in coordination.

| Mughal problem | Nadir's advantage |
|---|---|
| nobles disputed command and precedence | unified strategic direction |
| separate contingents acted without one plan | disciplined tactical integration |
| heavy camp reduced mobility | mobile firepower and manoeuvre |
| paper strength exceeded reliable deployable force | tighter control over the fighting army |
| intelligence did not become action | initiative remained with invader |

Saadat Khan engaged without a unified plan. Khan-i-Dauran moved to support him and was mortally wounded. The imperial host was defeated in detail.

### After the battle: what is certain about Delhi

Muhammad Shah submitted and accompanied Nadir to Delhi. Violence against Persian soldiers, amid a rumour that Nadir had been killed, was followed by an ordered massacre.

Nadir seized treasury wealth and celebrated objects including the Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor. He also acquired territories west of the Indus. He then withdrew.

**Numerical caution:** estimates of casualties and plunder vary. Do not present one precise total as settled fact.

### Causal test: prior weakness or new destruction?

Nadir did not originate:

- jagir competition;
- court faction;
- provincial autonomy;
- rural resistance; or
- the Maratha fiscal challenge.

All predated 1739.

But the invasion caused new damage:

- imperial prestige collapsed;
- treasury and elite wealth were removed;

**Objection:** Calling Nadir a revealer understates his devastating material impact.

**Reply:** "Revealer" identifies causal order, not insignificance. The invasion was decisive in timing, prestige, treasury and frontier security. It was secondary only as the origin of the deeper institutional crisis.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Nadir Shah's invasion was both an exposure and an acceleration of Mughal decline. Examine.

The invasion nevertheless accelerated decline. Delhi suffered massacre and plunder; the Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor were taken; territories west of the Indus were ceded; and imperial prestige collapsed. Provincial rulers and foreign companies could now see that the centre could not defend itself.

Yet Nadir withdrew, Muhammad Shah remained emperor and residual Mughal resistance survived into 1748. The invasion was therefore not the origin of the institutional crisis. It was a devastating material shock that revealed prior weakness and sharply worsened its fiscal, military and psychological consequences.

### Continuity puzzle: why legitimacy outlived command

For generations, Mughal offices and documents had structured elite expectations. A title signalled rank. A sanad stated a lawful claim. Coin and khutba made sovereignty public. Persian records connected administrators across regions.

Abandoning all of this would create uncertainty. Regional rulers therefore found it useful to preserve imperial forms while changing who controlled resources.

### Institutional ledger: what survived?

| Continuity | Political use |
|---|---|
| Imperial confirmation | strengthened a disputed provincial succession |
| Mansab and title | located a regional ruler inside a known hierarchy |
| Coin and khutba | acknowledged a shared sovereign language |
| Farman and sanad | framed commercial, fiscal and territorial claims |
| Persian administration | preserved trained personnel and record practice |
| Mughal court culture | supplied prestigious artistic and ceremonial forms |

The Maratha search for documentary recognition and the Company's reliance on the 1717 farman show that even non-provincial actors treated imperial documents as valuable.

### Interpretive test: symbol does not mean fiction

It is tempting to call coin and khutba a "legal fiction". That phrase can mislead.

The emperor might lack direct control, but a symbol could:

- influence rival claimants;
- reduce the cost of obedience among officials;
- justify taxation;
- support diplomatic claims; and
- connect a new regime with inherited political memory.

Symbolic power was weaker than military command but still politically real.

### Cultural evidence under Muhammad Shah

Muhammad Shah's reign retained important patronage of music and painting. Late-Mughal Delhi remained an urban and cultural centre. Persianate forms also moved toward Awadh, Hyderabad, Bengal and other courts.

The correct inference is not "culture flourished, therefore the state was strong."

It is:

```text
political command can contract
while cultural production continues
and patronage changes location
```

### Comparative verdict: decline is not a dark age

The older dark-age image captures real violence:

- agrarian extraction;
- factional conflict;
- route insecurity;
- Nadir's catastrophe; and
- weakening collective defence.

The regional-transformation view captures:

- successor-state administration;
- merchant and banker networks;
- continuing trade;
- cultural decentralisation; and
- persistence of Mughal institutions.

Neither should erase the other.

### Objection: did continuity make decline superficial?

**Objection:** Stressing continuity romanticises fragmentation and understates peasant suffering.

**Reply:** Continuity is an institutional claim, not a welfare claim. A functioning regional revenue order could be coercive. Cultural production could flourish amid inequality. The balanced interpretation distinguishes governance and vitality from social justice.

### Layered-sovereignty verdict

The eighteenth-century transition was not the replacement of one complete order by another. It was an uneven recombination: Mughal legitimacy and administrative practice survived while coercive and fiscal power moved to provinces and regional networks.

### Write the layered-sovereignty answer

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Why did Mughal legitimacy survive the contraction of Mughal power?

**Model answer**

Mughal legitimacy survived because it remained a shared institutional language among nobles, provincial rulers, zamindars, merchants and external claimants. Titles and mansabs located elites within a familiar hierarchy; farmans and sanads framed lawful privileges; coin and khutba made sovereignty publicly recognisable.

Regional rulers used these forms while controlling resources independently. Hyderabad retained imperial symbols even as Nizam-ul-Mulk exercised war, appointment and revenue authority. Awadh and Bengal sought or accepted confirmation without surrendering practical autonomy. Maratha leaders pursued sanads for fiscal claims, and the English Company later relied on Farrukhsiyar's 1717 farman.

This legitimacy was not equivalent to direct command. Delhi increasingly failed to transfer governors, collect remittance or coordinate armies. Yet inherited sanction reduced the political cost of regionalisation and helped competing regimes present innovation as continuity.

Mughal power therefore declined before Mughal political memory. The gap between symbolic sovereignty and coercive capacity explains both the dynasty's longevity and the layered character of eighteenth-century state formation.

### Opening argument: ruler-centred decline

Older narratives, especially those associated with Jadunath Sarkar, emphasised:

- Aurangzeb's choices;
- weak successors;
- declining courtly character;
- military incompetence; and
- moral decay.

**Contribution:** they preserve chronology, political agency and the consequences of decisions.

**Limit:** moral labels can substitute for mechanism, turn faction into personal vice and overstate communal causation.

### Structural counter-argument: jagirdari crisis

This interpretation links:

- growing service claims;
- shortage or poor quality of assignable revenue;
- be-jagiri;
- jama-hasil divergence;
- short-term extraction;
- faction; and
- under-maintained contingents.

**Contribution:** it explains how fiscal structure became political and military weakness.

**Limit:** it must be regionally tested and qualified by allocation choices.

### Agrarian counter-argument: extraction and resistance

The agrarian interpretation focuses on surplus extraction, peasant stress, flight and zamindar-led resistance.

**Contribution:** it explains the rural base beneath court politics.

**Limit:** resistance can coexist with cultivation growth, monetisation and political opportunity.

### Revisionist challenge: regional transformation

Muzaffar Alam and C.A. Bayly are associated with attention to:

- regional political orders;
- bankers and merchants;
- commercial and agrarian networks;
- local portfolio resources; and
- continuing Mughal idioms.

**Contribution:** it corrects the picture of a uniform dark age.

**Limit:** regional vitality can understate violence, unequal extraction and the real loss of collective defence.

### Evidence round: what can each source prove?

| Source | Strongest use | Hostile question |
|---|---|---|
| Court chronicle | succession, office, elite conflict | whose patron or enemy shapes the narrative? |
| Farman or sanad | legal claim and intended hierarchy | was the order implemented? |
| Revenue assessment | formal fiscal demand | how much was actually realised? |
| Coin or khutba | acknowledged sovereignty | did the issuer control appointments and armies? |
| Company record | commercial dispute and corporate interest | what inland society lies outside its view? |
| Invasion chronicle | route, diplomacy and victory narrative | are numbers triumphalist or rhetorical? |
| Regional tradition | local memory and political identity | is the account retrospective or heroic? |

The method is **claim -> evidence -> significance -> limit**.

### Chronological boundary: Ahmad Shah Abdali

Nadir's death in 1747 enabled Ahmad Shah Durrani, or Abdali, to build Afghan power. His first invasion in 1748 was checked near Sirhind. Repeated later invasions and Panipat in 1761 belong to the successor-state and eighteenth-century political field.

For the 1707-1740s argument, Abdali proves only:

- the frontier opening outlived Nadir;
- residual Mughal resistance still existed in 1748; and
- external pressure continued to exploit central contraction.

Do not turn Abdali into a cause of the 1707-1739 crisis.

### Teleology challenge: was British ascendancy inevitable?

No. Mughal decline created a competitive political field, not a predetermined British victory.

Company expansion required later developments:

- fortified commercial settlements;
- maritime power;
- disciplined and financed armies;
- alliances with Indian elites;
- Plassey and Buxar;
- Bengal revenue after the Diwani; and
- later commercial, military, fiscal and territorial policies.

Opportunity is not outcome.

The most convincing ranking is:

1. **Fiscal-military structural strain** made imperial service less credible.
2. **Succession and faction** prevented central arbitration and reform.
3. **Agrarian and regional assertion** reduced remittance and created alternative powers.
4. **Deccan inheritance and Maratha leverage** amplified cost and overlapping claims.
5. **Military-command weakness** converted fiscal and political strain into defeat.
6. **Nadir Shah** determined dramatic timing, visibility and further damage.

No cause operates alone.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Model 1 - 10 marks

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why did wars of succession weaken the Mughal Empire beyond the immediate battlefield?

**Model answer**

Mughal succession lacked a fixed primogeniture rule. Each prince therefore mobilised provincial treasuries, noble coalitions and military contingents to win the throne. Battles such as Jajau in 1707 consumed soldiers and commanders, but their wider effect came after victory.

The new emperor had to reward supporters with mansabs, offices and jagirs while reconciling defeated factions. This increased pressure on productive assignments and made patronage central to political survival. Nobles learned that control over a prince could yield the wizarat, governorships and revenue.

Repeated contests also encouraged provincial officials and zamindars to strengthen local positions while the centre was distracted. After Bahadur Shah's death, Zulfiqar Khan and later the Sayyid Brothers showed that nobles could make emperors.

Succession wars therefore weakened finance, administration and dynastic arbitration. They were not isolated family battles but recurring redistributions of the state's coercive and fiscal resources.

**Scoring guidance:** reward primogeniture, coalition finance, post-war rewards and the transition from princely to noble king-making.

## Model 2 - 10 marks

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the distinction between jama and hasil and its political significance.

**Model answer**

**Jama** was the assessed revenue demand recorded for a territory, whereas **hasil** was the amount actually realised. The distinction mattered because mansabs and jagirs were often planned through paper assessment, while troops and administration required real cash or produce.

Where war, zamindar resistance, over-assessment or multiple fiscal claims reduced hasil, a jagirdar could not easily maintain the sanctioned contingent. He might press cultivators, borrow, seek transfer to a better assignment or join a faction controlling appointments.

The gap therefore connected village conditions with court politics and military weakness. It also explains why paibaqi on paper did not guarantee a usable jagir.

The jama-hasil distinction turns "financial decline" into a mechanism: the empire promised rank and service through assessed revenue but increasingly failed to convert assessment into dependable collection.

**Scoring guidance:** require both definitions and a complete rural-to-court or fiscal-to-military connection.

## Model 3 - 15 marks

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** The Sayyid Brothers were symptoms of Mughal weakness as well as agents of further decline. Discuss.

**Model answer**

The Sayyid Brothers became powerful because succession already depended on armed noble coalitions. Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali raised Farrukhsiyar in 1713 and received the wizarat and military-pay office. Their dominance was therefore a symptom of the emperor's dependence on elite patrons.

They were not merely destructive. They abolished jizya, cultivated Rajput and Jat leaders and negotiated with Shahu's Maratha interest. Such policies sought a broader coalition capable of stabilising the empire.

However, their conflict with Farrukhsiyar concerned the substance of sovereignty: seal, appointments, jagirs, army and provincial resources. His deposition and killing in 1719 demonstrated publicly that nobles could unmake an emperor. The rapid installation of dependent princes weakened Timurid arbitration and encouraged rival factions to mobilise around office, dynasty and religion.

The Sayyids also used patronage promises and provincial alliances to survive, intensifying the competition they were trying to manage. Their fall in 1720 removed them but did not restore the centre.

They were thus products of fiscal and political strain, partial reformers and accelerants of monarchical dependence.

**Scoring guidance:** reward the symptom-agent structure, two reform examples, the 1719 rupture and a balanced conclusion.

## Model 4 - 15 marks

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The Mughal military crisis was a fiscal and command crisis expressed on the battlefield." Examine.

**Model answer**

The Mughal army remained large and possessed cavalry, infantry and artillery. Its weakness lay less in the absence of weapons than in the institutions that financed and coordinated them.

Mansabdars were expected to maintain contingents through jagir income. Be-jagiri, weak assignments and jama-hasil gaps encouraged them to reduce effective troop strength. Arrears and uncertain payment weakened discipline, while regional retention of revenue reduced crown cash for logistics and frontier defence.

Command was equally fragmented. Noble contingents retained separate loyalties and disputed precedence. At Karnal in 1739, Saadat Khan engaged without an integrated plan; Khan-i-Dauran moved to assist him and was mortally wounded. Nadir Shah's unified direction, mobility and disciplined fire defeated an imperial host with substantial resources.

Technology still mattered, but it had to be organised through pay, intelligence, transport and one command structure. The Mughal defeat was therefore the battlefield result of a deeper failure to convert assessed revenue, nominal ranks and large contingents into a coherent fighting force.

**Scoring guidance:** require fiscal mechanism, command mechanism, Karnal evidence and a qualification on technology.

## Model 5 - 20 marks

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Why did Mughal central authority decline between 1707 and the 1740s? Give a weighted multi-causal explanation.

**Model answer**

Mughal authority declined because the fiscal-military contract joining emperor, nobility, province and countryside ceased to reproduce dependable revenue, service and obedience.

First, Aurangzeb's Deccan wars left high military costs, unresolved Maratha power and more claimants on uncertain assignments. Second, succession without primogeniture consumed resources and made noble coalitions decisive. Farrukhsiyar's deposition in 1719 showed that king-makers could capture the throne.

Third, the jagirdari crisis supplied the structural mechanism. Be-jagiri, poor paibaqi and jama-hasil gaps intensified competition, short-term extraction and under-maintained contingents. Ijara and revenue pressure contributed to peasant flight, arrears and zamindar resistance.

Fourth, resources moved regionally. Murshid Quli Khan in Bengal, Saadat Khan in Awadh and Nizam-ul-Mulk in Hyderabad converted office into autonomy while retaining Mughal legitimacy. Maratha fiscal claims further reduced the centre's monopoly over revenue.

Finally, military and frontier coordination failed. Nadir Shah's victory at Karnal in 1739 devastated prestige, treasury and defence, but it struck a system already weakened.

The causes must therefore be ranked: fiscal-military strain formed the core; faction and regionalisation transmitted it; Nadir accelerated and exposed it. Mughal form survived after enforceable central command contracted.

**Scoring guidance:** reward causal ranking, at least six named evidence units, one regional qualification and the origin-versus-accelerant distinction.

## Model 6 - 20 marks

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Was the early eighteenth century a period of decline or regional transformation? Critically discuss.

**Model answer**

The early eighteenth century was both a decline of Mughal central command and an uneven regional transformation. A single all-India label obscures the different levels.

Evidence for decline is substantial. Succession wars and the Sayyid deposition of Farrukhsiyar weakened dynastic arbitration. Jagir competition, jama-hasil gaps and revenue farming increased fiscal pressure. Provincial remittance and noble contingents became unreliable. Karnal and the 1739 sack of Delhi exposed the failure of collective defence.

Yet regional administration did not disappear. Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad retained Mughal offices while localising revenue, force and succession. Maratha, Jat and Sikh networks increased their bargaining power. Merchant-bankers, trade and cultural patronage continued in several centres, and Mughal titles, coin, khutba and Persian administration remained useful.

The revisionist emphasis on regional vitality corrects a total dark-age narrative but must not become a regional golden age. Strong collection could burden cultivators; warfare and multiple claims produced insecurity.

The best verdict is two-level: Delhi suffered real fiscal-military decline, while political capacity and culture were redistributed through regions with unequal social costs. Transformation is the wider frame; decline remains indispensable for the imperial centre.

**Scoring guidance:** reward evidence for both sides, social qualification and an explicit centre-region conclusion rather than an evasive middle position.

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 - "The empire ended in 1707"

**Why it occurs:** learners confuse a ruler's death with institutional extinction.

**Repair:** say, "Aurangzeb's death opened a succession and transmission crisis; the dynasty and imperial forms survived."

**Retrieval test:** name one post-1707 emperor, one surviving institution and one lost central capacity.

## Error 2 - "Weak kings caused everything"

**Why it occurs:** narrative history makes personalities memorable.

**Repair:** place each ruler inside the reward, jagir, faction and provincial system. Bahadur Shah was capable; Nizam-ul-Mulk and Murshid Quli Khan prove talent survived.

**Retrieval test:** explain why capable nobles used their ability regionally.

## Error 3 - "Jagirdari crisis means corruption"

**Why it occurs:** extraction and bribery are visible symptoms.

**Repair:** define the structural mismatch among mansab claim, suitable assignment, jama, hasil and service obligation.

**Retrieval test:** draw the low-hasil-to-weak-contingent loop.

## Error 4 - "Jagirdar equals zamindar"

**Why it occurs:** both appear in land-revenue history.

**Repair:** jagir is an assigned revenue claim; zamindari rights are locally rooted and variable.

**Retrieval test:** ask which role depends mainly on imperial transfer and which on local social depth.

## Error 5 - "The Sayyids were simply villains"

**Why it occurs:** "king-makers" and Farrukhsiyar's death dominate memory.

**Repair:** pair their coalition reforms with the legitimacy cost of deposition.

**Retrieval test:** state one conciliatory policy and one destabilising consequence.

## Error 6 - "Autonomy means formal independence"

**Why it occurs:** modern sovereignty categories are projected backward.

**Repair:** use "autonomy in substance, Mughal continuity in form."

**Retrieval test:** identify one practical power and one retained imperial symbol in Hyderabad.

## Error 7 - "Agrarian crisis means farming stopped everywhere"

**Why it occurs:** crisis is confused with uniform production collapse.

**Repair:** distinguish output, extraction, distribution, security and political control of surplus.

**Retrieval test:** explain how Bengal could be commercially strong while cultivators faced pressure.

## Error 8 - "Nadir caused the decline"

**Why it occurs:** 1739 is dramatic and visually memorable.

**Repair:** list three pre-1739 symptoms, then state Nadir's new effects.

**Retrieval test:** complete the line: "origin in..., acceleration through..."

## Error 9 - "Mughals lost because they had no guns"

**Why it occurs:** later European conquest is projected backward.

**Repair:** focus on pay, contingent strength, intelligence, mobility and unified command.

**Retrieval test:** explain Karnal without using the phrase "technological backwardness."

## Error 10 - "Regional vitality disproves decline"

**Why it occurs:** revisionism is treated as simple reversal.

**Repair:** separate the level of Delhi from the level of Bengal or Hyderabad.

**Retrieval test:** produce one sentence containing both "central decline" and "regional capacity."

- **A — Incorrect:** Jagirdari strain is central, but treating agrarian and regional processes mainly as local expressions understates their independent organisation and feedback.
- **B — Incorrect:** Agrarian conflict shaped extraction and resistance, but assignment politics and regional state-building cannot be reduced to its timing and form.
- **C — Incorrect:** Regional reorganisation explains the relocation of capacity, yet fiscal-service and agrarian tensions also operated within the imperial system before that transfer matured.
- **D — Correct:** The synthesis connects fiscal service, rural conflict and regional reorganisation while allowing their relative force to vary by reign, province and arena.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 - Governing thesis

- Core period: 1707 through the 1740s.
- Decline means loss of enforceable central fiscal-military command.
- The dynasty and Mughal legitimacy survived much longer.
- Regional government did not vanish; authority shifted.
- Economic and cultural outcomes varied by region.
- Final line: **form survived, force shifted**.

## Register 2 - Imperial machine

- Emperor: mansab, title, office, jagir, farman and arbitration.
- Mansab: rank plus service expectation.
- Jagir: assigned revenue claim, normally transferable.
- Khalisa: revenue more directly reserved for the crown.
- Subahdar: provincial executive-military authority.
- Provincial diwan: fiscal check in principle.
- Zamindars: locally rooted, variable rights and armed influence.
- System depended on credible transfer, audit, remittance and force.

## Register 3 - Inherited strain in 1707

- Aurangzeb died at Ahmadnagar.
- Empire still formidable; recovery was possible.
- Long Deccan war consumed men, money and attention.
- Bijapur and Golconda annexation enlarged obligations.
- Maratha resistance survived.
- Deccani and Maratha entrants enlarged reward claims.
- Rajput relationships and north-west attention had suffered.
- Do not make religious policy the sole cause.

## Register 4 - Bahadur Shah I

- Muazzam defeated Azam at Jajau in 1707.
- Kam Bakhsh defeated near Hyderabad in 1709.
- Bahadur Shah: learned, experienced and conciliatory.
- Restored accommodation with Rajputs after initial coercion.
- Granted sardeshmukhi but no complete chauth settlement.
- Did not settle Shahu-Tarabai rivalry.
- Conciliated Churaman and Chhatrasal.
- Campaigned against Banda Bahadur.
- Promotions and grants worsened fiscal pressure.
- Died in 1712 before compromise consolidated.

## Register 5 - Jahandar Shah and Zulfiqar Khan

- Jahandar Shah won the 1712 succession with Zulfiqar's support.
- Zulfiqar became powerful wazir.
- Coalition policy: Rajputs, Marathas, Jats, Bundelas.
- Attempted jagir restraint and troop enforcement.
- Ijara spread as a cash-raising method.
- Revenue farming created over-extraction incentives.
- Emperor feared his wazir; nobles feared exclusion.
- Farrukhsiyar defeated the regime in January 1713.
- Lesson: reform failed through both policy contradiction and coalition mistrust.

## Register 6 - Farrukhsiyar and Sayyids

- Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali raised Farrukhsiyar in 1713.
- Abdullah: wazir; Husain Ali: military-pay and major command.
- Conflict concerned seal, appointment, jagir, army and provinces.
- Faction labels: Turani, Irani, Afghan, Hindustani/Baraha, Deccani.
- Labels were fluid affinity networks, not fixed parties.
- Sayyids abolished jizya and used broad accommodation.
- Negotiated Shahu's swarajya, chauth and sardeshmukhi claims.
- Farrukhsiyar deposed and killed in 1719.
- Rapid puppet succession damaged dynastic prestige.
- Sayyid dominance ended in 1720; structural crisis remained.

## Register 7 - Muhammad Shah and Nizam

- Muhammad Shah: 1719-1748.
- Long reign created a possible recovery window.
- Mughal army, artillery and prestige still mattered.
- Nizam-ul-Mulk became wazir in 1722.
- Reform threatened favourites and clients.
- Nizam left Delhi for the Deccan.
- Defeated Mubariz Khan at Shakar Kheda in 1724.
- Hyderabad controlled practical functions regionally.
- Mughal title, coin, khutba and office language continued.
- Capable service moved from centre to region.

## Register 8 - Jagirdari mechanism

- Paibaqi: revenue area available or awaiting assignment.
- Be-jagiri: no suitable assignment or delay.
- Jama: assessed revenue.
- Hasil: actually realised revenue.
- Paper rank could exceed real fiscal support.
- Frequent transfer encouraged short-term extraction.
- Poor assignments produced arrears or reduced contingents.
- Nobles sought patrons controlling better jagirs.
- Faction and fiscal scarcity reinforced one another.
- Allocation policy and regional variation qualify simple shortage.

## Register 9 - Agrarian feedback

- Ijara contracted collection for a fixed state payment.
- Revenue farmer's profit encouraged collection above contract.
- Peasants could resist through arrears, flight or concealment.
- Zamindars could withhold, fortify and mobilise.
- Agrarian grievance required leadership and organisation.
- Jat evidence: Churaman, Agra-Mathura, fortified rural power.
- Sikh evidence: Banda Bahadur, Punjab mobilisation.
- Identity, land, revenue and political opportunity interacted.
- Crisis did not mean identical production collapse everywhere.

## Register 10 - Deccan and Maratha bridge

- Deccan war enlarged cost and distance.
- Maratha mobility and fort networks survived.
- Shahu released in 1707; Tarabai remained rival.
- Chauth: conventionally one-fourth fiscal claim.
- Sardeshmukhi: conventionally additional ten per cent claim.
- 1718-19 recognition exchanged claims for service/support.
- Sanad did not guarantee implementation.
- Baji Rao widened pressure toward Malwa and Gujarat.
- Full Maratha state formation requires separate comparative study.

## Register 11 - Provincial autonomy

- Formula: provincial office + regional resources + local coalition.
- Bengal: Murshid Quli Khan; revenue provincialised; 1717 confirmation.
- Awadh: Saadat Khan appointed 1722; military-fiscal consolidation.
- Hyderabad: Nizam-ul-Mulk; 1724 consolidation.
- All retained Mughal forms while localising command.
- Bengal base: agrarian-commerce, zamindars, bankers.
- Awadh base: middle Ganga revenue and local chiefs.
- Hyderabad base: Deccan military and revenue networks.
- Strong regions are evidence against universal decline and for central decline.
- Full comparison requires separate treatment.

## Register 12 - Nadir Shah

- Persian war finance and Indian wealth shaped motive.
- Frontier complaints were a pretext, not complete cause.
- Invitation allegation lacks secure proof.
- Route: Qandahar, Kabul, Peshawar, Lahore, Karnal, Delhi.
- Karnal conventionally dated 24 February 1739.
- Mughal problem: divided command, arrears, poor integration.
- Nadir advantage: unified direction, mobility, disciplined fire.
- Delhi suffered massacre and plunder.
- Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor taken.
- Territories west of Indus ceded.
- Nadir withdrew; no permanent Persian rule over Delhi.
- Casualty and booty totals remain contested.
- Invasion exposed, accelerated and deepened decline.

## Register 13 - Continuity

- Timurid dynasty remained politically useful.
- Coin and khutba expressed acknowledged sovereignty.
- Mansab, jagir, diwan and subah vocabulary continued.
- Farman and sanad remained usable claims.
- Persian administration supplied trained continuity.
- Marathas sought imperial recognition.
- Company later invoked the 1717 farman.
- Muhammad Shah's court remained culturally productive.
- Patronage moved to regional courts.
- Institutional continuity did not guarantee peasant welfare.

## Register 14 - Historiography

- Older/Sarkar emphasis: Aurangzeb, ruler and moral-political decline.
- Contribution: agency and chronology.
- Risk: moral and communal reduction.
- Satish Chandra: jagirdari and fiscal-military mechanism.
- Irfan Habib: agrarian extraction and resistance.
- Analytical qualification: allocation choices could make scarcity partly policy-produced.
- Alam/Bayly: regional politics, commerce, credit and continuity.
- Revisionist risk: understate violence and collective-defence collapse.
- Best synthesis: central decline plus uneven regional transformation.
