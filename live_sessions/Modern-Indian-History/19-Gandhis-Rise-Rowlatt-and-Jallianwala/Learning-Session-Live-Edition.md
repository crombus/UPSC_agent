# Gandhi's Rise, the Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala Bagh — Live Session Edition

## Learner-first roadmap

The central puzzle is not simply, “What did Gandhi do between 1915 and 1919?” It is:

> How did a recently returned leader, working first through narrow local disputes, acquire the credibility and organisation needed for an all-India protest—and why did the first national experiment end in both mobilisation and tragedy?

```text
Wartime pressure and political expectation
                    ↓
South African training in disciplined resistance
                    ↓
Return, observation, ashram and cadre formation
                    ↓
Champaran → Ahmedabad → Kheda
 evidence       labour       revenue
 inquiry        discipline   solidarity
                    ↓
Satyagraha as ethics + pressure + organisation
                    ↓
Rowlatt legislation attacks civil liberty
                    ↓
First all-India satyagraha, rapid spread, weak control
                    ↓
Punjab crisis → Amritsar violence → Jallianwala Bagh
                    ↓
Inquiry without full accountability
                    ↓
Gandhi's rise: credibility + idiom + networks + conjuncture
```

### Learning sequence

1. The India Gandhi entered: wartime pressure, expectations and political networks.
2. South Africa: from petitions to satyagraha and mass action.
3. Return to India: Gokhale's advice, observation, ashram and political method.
4. Champaran: inquiry, civil disobedience, local agency and negotiated redress.
5. Ahmedabad: industrial conflict, arbitration, fasting and its moral hazards.
6. Kheda: revenue refusal, peasant solidarity, qualified withdrawal and limits.
7. Satyagraha and ahimsa: doctrine, political mechanism, objections and replies.
8. Rowlatt legislation: committee, bills, enacted law and the civil-liberties breach.
9. Rowlatt Satyagraha: organisation, hartal, violence and withdrawal.
10. Punjab and Jallianwala Bagh: context, sequence, responsibility and martial law.
11. Inquiry, moral rupture and Gandhi's rise: accountability, local agency and transition.

This roadmap keeps Topic 18 as context and Topic 20 as a bridge. It does not reteach Home Rule, the Lucknow Pact, Khilafat, the Non-Cooperation programme, Nagpur reorganisation or later movement chronology.

The visual shows that Gandhi did not create political discontent from nothing. He entered a country in which material suffering, repression, political awakening and disappointed expectation had already accumulated.

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine a government asking a population for extraordinary wartime sacrifice while suggesting that political trust will later be rewarded. When the war ends, that population expects relaxation and reform. If the government instead preserves exceptional coercive powers, the issue becomes larger than one statute. It becomes a test of whether the government's promise of lawful progress can be believed.

That is the setting in which the Rowlatt controversy acquired national force. The same legal provision would not necessarily produce the same political explosion in a different conjuncture.

### 1. The inherited situation

✅ **Fact:** Wartime repression continued against nationalists, while Punjab experienced coercive recruitment and the wider population suffered from high prices, disease and economic distress.

✅ **Fact:** Political India expected substantial constitutional concessions after the war.

✅ **Fact:** Home Rule activity had already created politically alert audiences and usable contacts. When the Rowlatt agitation began, younger Home Rule workers joined the Satyagraha Sabha, and old address lists were used to re-establish connections.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Gandhi's national breakthrough was therefore conjunctural. Personal leadership mattered, but it worked because a prepared political field already existed.

### 2. Expectation is politically powerful

Repression alone does not explain the anger. The sharper mechanism was:

```text
sacrifice during war
      ↓
expectation of reciprocal reform
      ↓
continuation of emergency-style coercion
      ↓
perception of betrayal
      ↓
broader legitimacy crisis
```

The word **legitimacy** means the belief that authority is entitled to obedience. A government may possess force yet lose legitimacy if its actions contradict its own claim to rule through law and reform.

### 3. What Gandhi added—and what he did not

Gandhi added:

- a disciplined method of resistance;
- a language of truth, suffering and moral responsibility;
- willingness to court punishment personally;
- techniques for converting local grievance into public investigation;
- a recognisable programme of pledge, hartal, fasting, prayer and selective disobedience.

He did not create:

- Punjab's wartime grievances;
- Champaran's indigo oppression;
- Ahmedabad's wage dispute;
- Kheda's crop failure;
- the pre-existing Home Rule political public;
- the local leaders who invited, interpreted and organised each struggle.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If wartime hardship and Home Rule networks already existed, Gandhi's role can be reduced to arriving at the right moment.

**Reply:** Conditions explain opportunity, not successful political translation. Many leaders confronted the same conjuncture. Gandhi's distinctive contribution was to connect material grievances, an accessible moral vocabulary, personal risk and a repeatable repertoire of action. Yet the reply must remain qualified: even this repertoire depended on local organisers and could fail when discipline did not match scale.

### 5. UPSC use

- **Prelims:** distinguish background conditions from the specific provisions of the Rowlatt law.
- **Mains:** avoid “great man” causation. Use a four-part explanation: local credibility + mass idiom + organisation + postwar conjuncture.
- **Trap:** do not teach Home Rule in detail here. Its relevance is that it supplied audiences and contacts later activated in 1919.

### Revision notes

1. Wartime hardship included inflation, disease, repression and recruitment pressures.
2. Punjab's coercive recruitment and wartime repression form essential background to 1919.
3. Postwar expectations made renewed repression appear as betrayal.
4. Home Rule networks predated Gandhi's all-India Rowlatt campaign.
5. Old Home Rule contact lists assisted Satyagraha Sabha mobilisation.
6. Gandhi entered an already politicised field; he did not create all grievances.
7. His contribution was translation: grievance into disciplined public action.
8. Legitimacy is belief in the rightfulness of authority, not mere possession of force.
9. A conjunctural explanation combines structure, organisation and leadership.
10. Topic 18 supplies context only; its detailed programme belongs outside this lesson.

#### Answers and explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** The postwar clash between expected political relaxation and renewed emergency-style coercion gave the legislation symbolic force beyond its clauses.
- **B — Incorrect:** Dyarchy concerned the distribution of provincial subjects under constitutional reform, whereas the Rowlatt dispute centred on civil-liberty safeguards.
- **C — Incorrect:** Kheda's threshold-based remission claim explains a local revenue campaign, not the nationwide reaction to preventive powers.
- **D — Incorrect:** Home Rule constitutional demands prepared political audiences, but they do not by themselves explain the immediate anger directed at the Rowlatt measure.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** A uniform village machinery of this kind emerged neither from the Home Rule Leagues nor from the 1919 Satyagraha Sabha.
- **B — Correct:** Younger activists and inherited contact lists gave the new campaign a ready communication base without making the organisations identical.
- **C — Incorrect:** Labour organisation mattered in particular industrial centres, but it was not the principal nationwide inheritance from Home Rule.
- **D — Incorrect:** Legislative resignations expressed opposition, while the Sabha's mobilisation depended on public networks rather than delegated statutory bargaining.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** This account underestimates local leaders, earlier associations and the grievances that existed before Gandhi's intervention.
- **B — Incorrect:** Material distress created receptivity, but a common idiom and organised repertoire were still required to turn discontent into coordinated action.
- **C — Correct:** The formulation joins context, local agency and Gandhi's political method while retaining the organisational weakness revealed by violence.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reform proposals did not remove wartime discontent, and opposition to Rowlatt preceded the later culmination of the Punjab crisis.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why the postwar conjuncture was indispensable to Gandhi's emergence as an all-India leader in 1919.

**Model answer**

Gandhi's emergence in 1919 cannot be explained by personal charisma alone. The First World War had produced high prices, disease, repression and, particularly in Punjab, coercive recruitment. Indian wartime contribution also generated expectations of constitutional advance. The proposed continuation of emergency-style powers through the Rowlatt measures therefore appeared as a breach of political faith, not merely another statute.

At the same time, Home Rule activity had already enlarged political audiences and created networks of workers and contacts. The Satyagraha Sabha could draw upon younger Home Rule activists and old address lists. Gandhi supplied the missing political translation: a simple idiom of truth and non-violence, personal willingness to suffer, and a programme of pledge, hartal and civil disobedience.

However, the ensuing violence showed that opportunity exceeded organisational capacity. Thus Gandhi rose through the interaction of postwar grievance, inherited networks, local credibility and a distinctive method—not through single-man causation.

**Scoring guidance (10):** 2 marks for wartime distress; 2 for expectation/betrayal; 2 for Home Rule networks; 3 for Gandhi's added method and credibility; 1 for qualification on organisational limits.

**Transition:** The conjuncture explains why India was ready. The next lesson asks what political equipment Gandhi carried into that situation.

### Visual: the method was learned in stages

```text

                    ↓

petitions · memorials · publicity · Natal Indian Congress · Indian Opinion
                    ↓
limits of appeal to imperial justice
                    ↓

```

This timeline shows that satyagraha did not emerge fully formed. Gandhi moved from constitutional petitioning to civil resistance, then learned how mass participation changed both the power and the risks of the method.

### Plain-language intuition

A petition asks authority to correct a wrong. Satyagraha goes further: it refuses cooperation with the wrong, accepts the legal penalty and tries to make repression morally and politically costly. It is neither quiet submission nor an attempt to physically destroy the opponent.

### 1. Racial setting and the moderate phase

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi went to South Africa in 1893. Indians there included merchants, indentured or ex-indentured labourers and their descendants, and they faced systematic racial disabilities.

✅ **Fact:** In the phase from 1894 to 1906, Gandhi used petitions, memorials and publicity. He helped establish the **Natal Indian Congress** and published **Indian Opinion**.

This phase trained him in:

- drafting and legal argument;
- fundraising;
- journalism and propaganda;
- uniting religious, regional and class segments;
- negotiating with authorities.

Its limit was equally important. Appeals to an imperial sense of justice did not reliably remove racial legislation.

### 2. The 1906 turn

✅ **Fact:** In 1906 the Transvaal registration measure required Indians to carry registration certificates containing fingerprints.

✅ **Fact:** At a mass meeting on 11 September 1906, Indians resolved to refuse submission and accept the consequences.

The sequence was:

```text
unjust registration requirement
      ↓
public pledge
      ↓
non-registration
      ↓
prosecution and imprisonment
      ↓
negotiation with General Smuts
      ↓
breach of understanding
      ↓
public burning of certificates
```

The pledge mattered because satyagraha required participants to bind themselves before repression arrived.

### 3. Phoenix Settlement and Tolstoy Farm

✅ **Fact:** Phoenix Settlement and Tolstoy Farm supported communal living, publication, families and the discipline of resisters. Tolstoy Farm housed families when imprisonment and loss of income made prolonged struggle difficult.

These were political institutions, not retreats:

| Need created by resistance | Organisational response |
|---|---|
| Families lose earners | Common support |
| Campaign needs communication | *Indian Opinion* |
| Volunteers require discipline | Shared work and rules |
| Long struggle exhausts funds | Collective economy and fundraising |
| Diverse participants need cohesion | Community life |

### 4. The 1913 mass expansion

✅ **Fact:** Resistance widened in 1913 around the £3 poll tax imposed on ex-indentured Indians and a judgment invalidating marriages not conducted according to Christian rites and official registration.

✅ **Fact:** Women entered the campaign; mine workers, many of them Tamil, struck; a march of more than two thousand men, women and children crossed the border.

This was a qualitative change:

```text
merchant-led rights campaign
          ↓
women + workers + ex-indentured labourers
          ↓
mass suffering becomes visible
          ↓
wider Indian and imperial pressure
          ↓
negotiated concessions
```

✅ **Fact:** Negotiations involving Gandhi, General Smuts, C.F. Andrews and Viceroy Hardinge helped secure concessions concerning the poll tax, registration certificates and recognition of Indian marriages.

### 5. From South African indenture to the Indian abolition campaign

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi's South African work made the conditions of indentured and ex-indentured Indians a continuing part of his public politics.

✅ **Fact:** After returning to India, he joined the campaign against further indentured emigration. In 1917 he toured for an all-India agitation with a definite deadline; before that deadline the Government announced that indentured emigration from India had stopped. The outcome followed sustained pressure by several Indian nationalists and reformers rather than one individual acting alone.

This is a bounded chronology point:

```text
South African experience of indenture
              ↓
Indian public campaign against further recruitment
              ↓
government decision ending indentured emigration in 1917
```

The safe conclusion is that Gandhi was an important campaigner against indenture, not that he single-handedly created or administered abolition.

### 6. Lessons—and limits

**Transferable lessons**

1. Begin with a concrete injustice.
2. Investigate and explain the wrong.
3. Secure a pledge before action.
4. Accept punishment to expose coercion.
5. Build institutions that can sustain families and morale.
6. Keep negotiation open.
7. Convert repression into wider public pressure.

**Limits**

- South Africa was a settler-colonial racial setting, not India in scale or social composition.
- The struggle concerned specific disabilities rather than control of an entire colonial state.
- Gandhi's early politics did not initially represent all oppressed Africans; avoid turning the experience into a universal, completed anti-racist programme.
- Negotiated concession did not abolish the whole racial order.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Satyagraha was only pressure politics dressed in moral language.

**Reply:** It certainly exerted pressure; otherwise it would have had no political effect. Its distinctiveness lay in restricting the means of pressure: no physical injury, readiness to bear punishment, public truth claims and continued negotiation. The unresolved tension is real—self-suffering can morally appeal, but it can also impose indirect pressure on allies or opponents.

### UPSC use

- Explain South Africa as **training**, not as a perfect rehearsal.
- Distinguish moderate petitioning (1894–1906), satyagraha from 1906 and mass participation in 1913.
- Use Phoenix and Tolstoy to demonstrate that Gandhian politics required organisational infrastructure.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “South Africa gave Gandhi a repertoire, not a ready-made Indian programme.” Discuss.

**Model answer**

South Africa supplied Gandhi with a tested repertoire of political action, but its transfer to India required adaptation.

From 1894 to 1906 Gandhi practised a moderate politics of petitions, memorials and publicity through the Natal Indian Congress and *Indian Opinion*. Their limited results encouraged the 1906 turn to satyagraha against compulsory registration. Public pledge, refusal, imprisonment, certificate burning and negotiation established a new sequence of resistance. Tolstoy Farm and Phoenix Settlement showed that moral protest needed material institutions to sustain families, communication and cadre discipline.

The 1913 phase widened the movement. The £3 tax and marriage judgment drew ex-indentured labourers, women and mine workers into struggle. A mass march, suffering under repression and wider public pressure helped secure negotiated concessions on tax, registration and marriages. Gandhi thus learned that disciplined non-cooperation could combine moral claim, mass participation and bargaining.

Yet this was not a ready-made programme for India. South Africa involved a smaller Indian minority confronting specific racial disabilities in a settler colony. India contained far greater regional, class and political diversity, while the target was an entire colonial state. The South African settlement also left the wider racial order intact.

Therefore, South Africa gave Gandhi techniques, confidence and organisational habits. Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda and finally 1919 tested whether they could work under Indian conditions.

**Scoring guidance (15):** 3 marks for moderate phase; 4 for satyagraha mechanics; 3 for 1913 mass expansion; 3 for institutions; 2 for limits and qualified verdict.

**Transition:** Gandhi now possessed a method. He still had to learn the country to which he intended to apply it.

### Visual: Gandhi's Indian entry method

```text
Return in January 1915
          ↓
Gokhale's advice: first understand India
          ↓
travel, observe, listen, avoid immediate national command
          ↓
Ahmedabad/Sabarmati ashram
shared work · simplicity · truth · non-violence · discipline
          ↓
select concrete local struggles
          ↓
investigate → mobilise → negotiate → accept limited settlement
```

The sequence is important: Gandhi did not return and instantly announce a national campaign. Observation and organisation preceded intervention.

### 1. Gokhale and restraint

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi returned to India in January 1915 and regarded Gopal Krishna Gokhale as a political mentor.

✅ **Fact:** On Gokhale's advice, Gandhi spent roughly his first year travelling and studying Indian conditions rather than taking an immediate public position on major political questions.

This restraint performed three functions:

- it reduced the danger of importing South African assumptions mechanically;
- it allowed Gandhi to encounter Indian social diversity;
- it signalled that leadership would be earned through work rather than claimed by reputation.

### 2. Distance from existing currents

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi did not immediately merge himself into the Home Rule agitation. His earlier faith in purely moderate methods had eroded, while he also differed over the timing and method of wartime agitation.

This does not mean political isolation. It means he was searching for a form of politics consistent with non-violent satyagraha.

### 3. The 1918 War Conference and recruitment

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi attended Lord Chelmsford's War Conference in Delhi in 1918 and supported the resolution seeking Indian assistance to the British war effort.

✅ **Fact:** He subsequently promoted voluntary military recruitment, particularly in Kheda. This position coexisted uneasily with his non-violent creed and reflected his then belief that willing wartime service could strengthen India's claim to political rights.

This episode should not be confused with coercive colonial recruitment in Punjab. Gandhi advocated voluntary enlistment; the Punjab administration's coercive wartime practices form part of the later background to 1919.

### 4. Ashram as political technology

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi organised an ashram at Ahmedabad; Sabarmati became associated with training in truth, non-violence, simplicity, labour and community discipline.

An ashram was not a substitute for politics. It attempted to answer practical questions:

| Political problem | Ashram response |
|---|---|
| How can volunteers endure hardship? | Simple living and shared labour |
| How can means match ends? | Rules governing conduct |
| How can diverse people cooperate? | Common discipline |
| How can a movement outlast a speech? | Cadre formation |
| How can moral claims be made credible? | Leaders practise demanded sacrifice |

### 5. No hagiography

The discipline Gandhi demanded could inspire trust, but it also concentrated moral authority in the leader. His capacity to declare a fast, certify non-violence or suspend action could protect a movement from escalation; it could also reduce space for autonomous worker or peasant decision-making.

That tension must remain visible:

```text
discipline protects non-violence
             but
central moral authority can narrow participant autonomy
```

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Ashram discipline moralised politics and diverted attention from institutions, class interests and material power.

**Reply:** Gandhi did not abandon material grievances; his first three Indian struggles concerned indigo dues, wages and land revenue. Discipline was intended to make collective action sustainable and limit destructive escalation. Still, the objection identifies a genuine cost: the leader's ethical judgment could override the preferred tempo of participants.

### 7. UPSC use

- Use the phrase **“observation before mobilisation.”**
- Treat the ashram as cadre and conduct infrastructure, not merely a spiritual residence.
- Balance moral credibility with concerns about paternalism and centralised authority.

### Revision notes

1. Gandhi returned to India in January 1915.
2. Gokhale advised him to study Indian conditions.
3. Gandhi travelled before taking a major public stand.
4. He did not immediately join the Home Rule movement.
5. The Ahmedabad/Sabarmati ashram trained a disciplined community.
6. Simplicity and shared labour linked personal conduct to political credibility.
7. Ashram discipline was designed to support non-violent action.
8. Local struggles became tests of an adapted Indian method.
9. Gandhi's authority was earned through intervention, not merely imported from South Africa.
10. Discipline could protect movements but also centralise moral decision-making.
11. At the 1918 War Conference Gandhi supported wartime assistance and later encouraged voluntary recruitment.
12. His recruitment support must be distinguished from coercive wartime recruitment in Punjab.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine the political significance of Gandhi's ashram and his initial period of observation in India.

**Model answer**

After returning in January 1915, he followed Gokhale's advice to travel and understand Indian conditions before taking a major public position. This reduced the danger of treating South Africa as a ready-made model for a much larger and more diverse society.

The Ahmedabad/Sabarmati ashram functioned as political infrastructure. Shared labour, simple living, truth and non-violence trained volunteers to endure hardship and linked the leader's conduct to the sacrifices demanded from followers. However, ethical discipline had an ambivalent side. Gandhi's authority to judge whether a struggle remained non-violent could prevent destructive escalation, but could also restrict worker or peasant autonomy.

Thus observation gave Gandhi contextual knowledge, while the ashram supplied a durable organisational ethic. Together they prepared—not automatically guaranteed—his effective entry into Indian mass politics.

Champaran was not simply “Gandhi saved peasants.” Local grievance, local initiative, documentary investigation, controlled defiance and negotiation formed one chain.

### 1. The grievance

✅ **Fact:** Under **tinkathia**, cultivators were compelled to grow indigo on three-twentieths of their holdings.

✅ **Fact:** As German synthetic dyes damaged the natural indigo market, planters attempted to secure enhanced rents and illegal dues as the price of releasing cultivators from the obligation.

The grievance therefore evolved:

```text
forced commercial cultivation
          ↓
market decline for indigo
          ↓
planters monetise release from the old burden
          ↓
rent enhancement and illegal exactions
```

### 2. Local agency

✅ **Fact:** **Raj Kumar Shukla**, a local cultivator, repeatedly pursued Gandhi and persuaded him to come to Champaran.

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi's inquiry was sustained by **Brij Kishore**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **J.B. Kripalani**, **Mahadev Desai**, **Narhari Parikh** and others.

This matters analytically. Gandhi brought visibility and method; local actors supplied invitation, knowledge, translation, access and continuity.

### 3. Defiance with legal responsibility

✅ **Fact:** District authority ordered Gandhi to leave. He refused and expressed willingness to face trial and punishment.

This is the core satyagraha move:

```text
lawful command in form
      but unjust in purpose
             ↓
open non-compliance
             +
acceptance of legal consequence
             ↓
authority must either punish inquiry
or retreat from its order
```

The Government allowed the inquiry to proceed. The victory was not that law vanished; it was that an administrative command lost moral and political defensibility.

### 4. Investigation as mobilisation

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi and colleagues toured villages and carefully recorded peasant statements. The permitted book account describes evidence from about 8,000 peasants.

Investigation served several purposes:

- established factual credibility;
- converted dispersed complaints into a common case;
- gave peasants confidence to speak;
- enabled negotiation from evidence rather than rhetoric;
- restrained exaggeration by checking testimony.

### 5. Commission and settlement

✅ **Fact:** The Government appointed a Commission of Inquiry and included Gandhi.

✅ **Fact:** The inquiry accepted abolition of tinkathia and compensation for illegal enhancement. Gandhi agreed to a compromise refund of 25 per cent of illegally taken money.

Why accept less than full refund?

Gandhi argued that compelling planters to refund anything broke their prestige and acknowledged peasant rights. The point was not the arithmetic alone; it was the changed relation of power.

### 6. Limits and strongest objection

**Objection:** A 25 per cent refund and official commission were modest reforms that left colonial property and authority intact.

**Reply:** Correct as a structural limit. Champaran was not an anti-colonial transfer of power. Its significance lay in demonstrating that peasants could speak collectively, an unjust order could be disobeyed openly, and official machinery could be forced to recognise a grievance. A good answer distinguishes **limited settlement** from **large methodological significance**.

```text
want protection against wartime cost-of-living loss

```

The dispute was triangular, not a simple confrontation between Gandhi and “the British.” The immediate adversaries were Indian millowners, including people socially close to Gandhi.

### 1. Grievance and actors

✅ **Fact:** Ahmedabad mill workers disputed withdrawal of a **plague bonus** after the epidemic, arguing that wartime increases in the cost of living continued.

✅ **Fact:** Millowners offered a 20 per cent increase; Gandhi's study of costs, profits and living expenses supported a 35 per cent claim.

✅ **Fact:** **Anasuya Sarabhai**, sister of millowner Ambalal Sarabhai, was an important organiser on the workers' side.

Her role breaks a simplistic class map. Family location did not mechanically determine political alignment.

### 2. Method

Gandhi first sought arbitration. When millowners withdrew from the arrangement, he advised a strike.

The strike's discipline included:

- daily meetings on the Sabarmati riverbank;
- a daily news bulletin;
- no violence against employers;
- no coercion against strike-breakers;
- a defined wage demand rather than unlimited confrontation.

### 3. The fast

✅ **Fact:** As worker morale weakened and hostility towards strike-breakers hardened, Gandhi fasted. The fast also fulfilled his promise that he would be the first to starve if the strike produced starvation.

✅ **Fact:** The millowners accepted arbitration, and the tribunal later awarded the 35 per cent increase.

The fast had two political effects:

```text
self-suffering
   ├── reassures workers that leader shares risk
   └── places moral pressure on millowners
```

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A leader's fast can become moral coercion. Even without physical force, it may burden opponents and followers with responsibility for his health.

**Reply:** Gandhi treated the fast as self-discipline and fulfilment of a promise, not punishment inflicted on others. It helped restrain worker violence and revived morale. Yet the objection cannot be dismissed: a fast by a revered leader alters bargaining power and may constrain free consent. Ahmedabad therefore reveals the **conversion/pressure tension** inside satyagraha.

### 5. Comparison with Champaran

| Dimension | Champaran | Ahmedabad |
|---|---|---|
| Constituency | Peasant cultivators | Industrial workers |
| Immediate opponent | European planters and district authority | Indian millowners |
| Principal method | Inquiry plus refusal of exclusion order | Strike, arbitration and fast |
| Evidence | Recorded testimonies | Cost, profit and wage analysis |
| Outcome | Tinkathia ended; partial refund | Tribunal; 35% award |
| Main limit | Narrow agrarian settlement | Paternalism and moral pressure |

- Never call Champaran Gandhi's first hunger strike in India; Ahmedabad is the relevant episode.
- Show that Gandhi could confront Indian capital as well as colonial officials.
- Use the fast as an analytical issue, not devotional detail.

### Visual: Kheda's solidarity mechanism

```text
crop yield below one-fourth
          ↓
claim to remission under revenue rules
          ↓
petitions and inquiries fail
          ↓
collective pledge to withhold revenue
          ↓
wealthier peasants also refuse
to prevent pressure isolating poorer peasants
          ↓
attachment of crops, cattle and household goods
          ↓
secret direction: recover only from those able to pay
          ↓
movement withdrawn as exhaustion grows
```

Kheda's logic was not “nobody will ever pay tax.” It was a conditional refusal intended to enforce remission under existing revenue rules.

### 1. Grievance

✅ **Fact:** Crop failure left Kheda peasants in distress. Their case was that yield had fallen below one-fourth of normal, entitling them under the revenue code to total remission.

✅ **Fact:** Inquiries by the Servants of India Society, Vithalbhai Patel and Gandhi supported the peasants' claim.

### 2. Organisation and actors

✅ **Fact:** The **Gujarat Sabha**, with Gandhi as president, played a leading role.

✅ **Fact:** **Vallabhbhai Patel** and **Indulal Yagnik** toured villages and urged firmness.

The pledge contained an important distributive logic:

```text
if only the poorest refuse
      → state can isolate and crush them

if those able to pay also refuse
      → collective bargaining protects the weakest
```

Those able to pay could do so if government openly suspended collection from the genuinely distressed. The aim was rule-based relief, not permanent tax abolition.

### 3. State pressure

✅ **Fact:** Repression included seizure of cattle and household goods and attachment of standing crops.

This tested whether moral resolve could survive material loss. It also revealed unequal staying power: poorer households could not endure a prolonged revenue confrontation as easily as prosperous ones.

### 4. Withdrawal and outcome

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi learned that secret instructions limited collection to peasants able to pay—the substance of the demand, though not publicly conceded.

✅ **Fact:** With peasants exhausted, the movement was withdrawn.

This was neither a total victory nor simple defeat:

| Measure | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Public state capitulation | No |
| Practical restriction on collection | Yes |
| Peasant exhaustion | Significant |
| Collective confidence and cadre growth | Yes |
| Full agrarian transformation | No |

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling Kheda a victory disguises exhaustion and a face-saving administrative concession.

**Reply:** A precise account should not call it a complete victory. The campaign secured the practical core of selective suspension and developed organisers such as Patel, but it ended before an unambiguous public surrender. Its importance lies in conditional tax refusal, solidarity across unequal capacity and experience with state repression.

```text
NEGOTIATION AND OPPORTUNITY TO CORRECT
          ↓
OPEN REFUSAL TO COOPERATE WITH THE WRONG
          ↓
NO PHYSICAL INJURY + ACCEPTANCE OF PENALTY
          ↓
SELF-SUFFERING MAKES REPRESSION VISIBLE
          ↓
conversion / public pressure / bargaining
          ↓
settlement, withdrawal or disciplined escalation
```

Satyagraha is best understood as a political sequence. Remove fact-finding, restraint, readiness to suffer or negotiation, and the method changes character.

### 1. Truth

**Satyagraha** may be rendered as holding firmly to truth or truth-force. Truth performs two roles:

1. The protester must identify a concrete wrong rather than merely hate an opponent.
2. Because one's grasp of truth may be incomplete, negotiation and correction must remain possible.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This second point is why a compromise can be consistent with satyagraha. If neither side is assumed infallible, settlement need not mean moral surrender.

### 2. Ahimsa

**Ahimsa** means non-injury or non-violence. In Gandhi's political method it demanded more than absence of weapons:

- no physical attack;
- no hatred of the wrongdoer;
- no coercion of strike-breakers by crowds;
- restraint in speech and conduct;
- discipline even under provocation.

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi treated non-violence as requiring strength and fearlessness, not passive cowardice.

### 3. Self-suffering

The satyagrahi accepts arrest, loss, hardship or injury rather than inflicting it.

Political mechanism:

```text
state claims lawful, civilised rule
              +
peaceful resister accepts punishment publicly
              ↓
disproportionate repression becomes visible
              ↓
support shifts; governing legitimacy becomes costlier
```

This works best when:

- there is a public capable of judging the conflict;
- communication carries evidence of repression;
- the opponent values legitimacy or faces wider pressure;
- resisters remain disciplined.

It works poorly when repression is hidden, exterminatory or indifferent to public legitimacy.

### 4. Refusal to cooperate

Satyagraha is active. It withdraws participation from a wrong:

- refusal to register;
- refusal to leave an inquiry area;
- strike;
- withholding revenue under specified conditions;
- disobedience of selected laws.

The aim is to make injustice difficult to administer without treating the opponent as an enemy to be destroyed.

### 5. Means and ends

Gandhi argued that unjust means corrupt the end produced by them. The method therefore joins the desired future to present conduct:

| Desired end | Required means |
|---|---|
| Society without domination | No physical domination in struggle |
| Truthful public order | No deliberate falsehood |
| Responsible citizenship | Open acceptance of consequences |
| Reconciliation | Preserve possibility of negotiation |

### 6. Constructive and organisational innovations

The method required:

- associations and sabhas;
- ashram discipline;
- press and bulletins;
- verified testimony;
- pledges;
- volunteer networks;
- local leadership;
- relief for families;
- training against panic and retaliation.

Thus the “moral” and “organisational” dimensions were inseparable.

### 7. Strongest criticisms and replies

| Criticism | Strongest reply | Residual limit |
|---|---|---|
| Self-suffering is disguised coercion | It limits injury and accepts cost personally | Revered leaders can exert unequal moral pressure |
| Non-violence protects the oppressor | It can widen participation and expose illegitimate force | It may fail against actors indifferent to legitimacy |
| Discipline silences autonomous mass action | It prevents retaliation from destroying the claim | Central authority may override local initiative |
| Trusteeship avoids structural redistribution | Moral restraint can reduce conflict and encourage social responsibility | It depends heavily on voluntary change by property holders |
| Truth language can become self-righteous | Fallibility should keep negotiation open | Followers may still treat the leader's judgment as final |

### 8. Gandhism and Marxism: careful comparison

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Both can criticise exploitation and imagine a social order in which coercive state power is no longer necessary. They diverge over class struggle, violence, property and moral transformation.

| Dimension | Gandhian approach | Marxist approach |
|---|---|---|
| Motor of change | Moral-political mobilisation and transformed conduct | Class struggle rooted in material relations |
| Means | Non-violence; means shape ends | Revolutionary coercion may be historically necessary |
| Property | Trusteeship and restraint | Abolition/socialisation of private productive property |
| Person | Ethical agency is central | Consciousness is deeply shaped by social relations |

Do not collapse either tradition into a single slogan.

Satyagraha should therefore be understood neither as saintly purity nor ordinary pressure politics, but as pressure deliberately bounded by ethical rules and organisational discipline.

### Visual: do not collapse committee, bills and law

```text
wartime revolutionary activity and repression
                    ↓
Rowlatt Committee investigates "sedition"/revolutionary crime
                    ↓
committee recommendations
                    ↓
TWO proposed bills in the Imperial Legislative Council
                    ↓

```

The distinction is exam-critical: a committee investigated and recommended; bills were introduced; one law was enacted. “Rowlatt Committee,” “Rowlatt Bills” and “Rowlatt Act” are related but not interchangeable.

### Plain-language intuition

Emergency powers are often defended as temporary responses to exceptional danger. The civil-liberties problem begins when temporary wartime logic becomes a normal peacetime instrument. A person can then lose liberty without the ordinary public process through which accusation must be proved.

### 1. The legal-political sequence

✅ **Fact:** Two bills, popularly called the **Rowlatt Bills** after the chair of the committee whose recommendations informed them, were introduced to curb revolutionary activity.

✅ **Fact:** One was enacted in March 1919 despite opposition from Indian members of the Central Legislative Council.

✅ **Fact:** Mohammed Ali Jinnah, Madan Mohan Malaviya and Mazhar-ul-Huq resigned from the Council in protest.

### 2. Civil-liberties mechanism

The law authorised imprisonment without ordinary trial and conviction. Its significance can be unpacked:

| Safeguard weakened | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Open accusation | Citizen may not fully know or contest the case |
| Ordinary trial | Executive suspicion substitutes for judicial proof |
| Legal defence | Capacity to challenge evidence narrows |
| Habeas corpus principle | Detention becomes harder to test before a court |
| Appeal and scrutiny | Error or abuse becomes less correctable |

The popular formula “no *dalil*, no *vakil*, no appeal” compressed this civil-liberties objection into mass language. Treat it as a political summary, not a complete statutory clause-by-clause description.

### 3. Why opposition became broad

The law offended different constituencies for different reasons:

- constitutionalists saw rule-of-law safeguards violated;
- wartime supporters saw promises betrayed;
- activists saw peacetime repression normalised;
- ordinary people could understand detention without trial as direct vulnerability.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Colonial justification:** Revolutionary violence had existed; government required preventive powers before conspiracies produced harm.

**Nationalist reply:** Preventive convenience cannot erase ordinary safeguards. Executive detention based on suspicion invites abuse, especially where the executive is unrepresentative and political dissent can be labelled sedition.

**Balanced assessment:** A state may confront real security threats, but the burden is to use reviewable, proportionate and time-limited law. The Rowlatt measure's political disaster lay in preserving exceptional coercion when India expected expansion of lawful participation.

### 5. UPSC use

- Never say the committee itself “passed” the Act.
- Never say both bills became law.
- Link the issue to civil liberties, executive detention and habeas corpus.
- Distinguish legal mechanism from the later protest chronology.

### Revision notes

1. The Rowlatt Committee preceded the legislation.
2. Its recommendations informed two proposed bills.
3. One measure was enacted in March 1919.
4. Indian members opposed the measure.
5. Jinnah, Malaviya and Mazhar-ul-Huq resigned.
6. The core objection was detention without ordinary trial safeguards.
7. Habeas corpus protects against unlawful detention.
8. Executive suspicion displaced judicial proof.
9. “No dalil, no vakil, no appeal” was a popular political summary.
10. Wartime emergency logic was being carried into peacetime.
11. Security claims do not remove requirements of proportionality and review.
12. Postwar expectations magnified the legitimacy cost.

### Visual: planned action versus actual movement

```text
PLANNED
Satyagraha Sabha
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

```

The gap between the two columns is the lesson of 1919: a popular call can travel faster than training, communication and control.

### 1. Satyagraha Sabha and pledge

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi founded the **Satyagraha Sabha** in February 1919. Members pledged to disobey the law and court arrest.

✅ **Fact:** Younger Home Rule workers joined; older Home Rule address lists and contacts assisted propaganda.

The organisation was innovative but thin. A pledge could declare discipline; it could not instantly produce it across a subcontinent.

### 2. Chosen repertoire

The programme combined:

- nationwide hartal;
- fasting and prayer;
- public meetings;
- planned civil disobedience of selected laws.

This repertoire translated an abstract legal issue into actions ordinary people could recognise.

### 3. The date problem and 6 April

✅ **Fact:** Delhi observed the hartal on **30 March** because of confusion over the date, and violence occurred.

✅ **Fact:** **6 April 1919** was the fixed all-India day of hartal. It produced a remarkable political awakening, strikes and demonstrations in many places.

The date confusion is not trivia. It reveals communication limits inside a rapidly expanding campaign.

### 4. Violence and regional crises

✅ **Fact:** Violence and disorder accompanied protest in several areas. Punjab reacted strongly; Bombay and Ahmedabad also experienced serious unrest.

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi attempted to travel to Punjab to calm the situation but was stopped and sent back toward Bombay. He stayed in western India to pacify unrest.

The common shorthand “Gandhi was deported” should be understood as governmental removal/prevention from proceeding to Punjab, not deportation from India.

### 5. Withdrawal on 18 April

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi withdrew the movement on **18 April 1919**, overwhelmed by the atmosphere of violence.

He acknowledged a major organisational error: people had been called into civil disobedience without sufficient training in non-violent discipline.

This admission matters:

```text
mass response proves reach
          but
violence proves inadequate preparation
          ↓
popularity ≠ organisational mastery
```

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Withdrawing after people had accepted risk abandoned participants and allowed one leader to override local action.

**Reply:** Continuing a movement whose defining condition had broken down would convert satyagraha into something else and invite wider repression. Yet the criticism remains partly valid: central withdrawal exposed the asymmetry between Gandhi's moral authority and local sacrifice.

### 7. UPSC use

- Separate 30 March Delhi confusion from 6 April all-India action.
- Mention Satyagraha Sabha, pledge and Home Rule contacts.
- Treat 1919 as both breakthrough and organisational failure.
- Do not claim the movement was a complete non-violent success.

The massacre was neither an isolated battlefield mistake nor the first violence in Amritsar. Prior civilian violence forms part of the chronology, but it does not justify firing without warning into an enclosed unarmed gathering.

### 1. Punjab's special pressure

✅ **Fact:** Punjab had suffered severe wartime repression, forcible recruitment and disease. Economic distress sharpened resentment.

The province mattered strategically because it was a major recruiting ground and was governed through an unusually security-centred wartime outlook.

### 2. Arrests and violence on 10 April

✅ **Fact:** Popular leaders **Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr Satyapal** were arrested on **10 April 1919**.

✅ **Fact:** Subsequent protest turned violent: public buildings were attacked, telegraph communications were cut and Europeans, including women, were assaulted.

A complete account must state this violence. A morally clear condemnation of the massacre does not require erasing preceding disorder.

### 3. Administrative handover and meeting ban

✅ **Fact:** The army was called in and Amritsar came under **Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer's** military authority. Dyer issued an order prohibiting public meetings and assemblies.

Communication of the prohibition was inadequate relative to the size and movement of the population, including rural visitors arriving for Baisakhi.

### 4. 13 April at the Bagh

✅ **Fact:** On **13 April 1919**, Baisakhi day, residents and visitors gathered at Jallianwala Bagh, including people attending a public meeting.

✅ **Fact:** The Bagh was enclosed by buildings and walls with restricted exits.

✅ **Fact:** Dyer ordered troops to fire without first giving the assembled crowd an effective warning or opportunity to disperse. Firing continued for approximately ten minutes.

No disputed casualty figure is necessary to establish the crime. Official and other estimates differ sharply; the examinable certainty is indiscriminate firing on a trapped, unarmed crowd without warning.

### 5. Dyer is not O'Dwyer

| Person | Position and responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer** | Military officer who ordered the firing at Jallianwala Bagh |
| **Sir Michael O'Dwyer** | Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab; bore political-administrative responsibility for the wider repressive regime |
| **Udham Singh** | Later assassinated Michael O'Dwyer in London; he did not shoot the crowd in 1919 |

This distinction is a frequent factual trap.

### 6. Martial law and humiliation

✅ **Fact:** Repression intensified and martial law subjected Punjabis to public humiliations.

✅ **Fact:** The **crawling order** forced people to crawl along a street associated with an assault on a British woman. Other punishments included public flogging, forced salutes, coercive roll calls, arbitrary detention and collective humiliation.

The political purpose was not merely restoring order. It was exemplary subordination—teaching a population to fear and obey.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Colonial defence:** Amritsar had seen lethal violence; Dyer believed he faced rebellion and sought to prevent a wider uprising.

**Reply:** Security necessity does not explain the absence of warning, firing into an enclosed crowd or the punitive humiliations that followed. The method was exemplary terror rather than proportionate crowd dispersal.

**Qualification:** Historical analysis should acknowledge 10 April violence without creating false equivalence. Attacks by protesters require condemnation; state authority carried a higher duty of restraint and law.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Reconstruct the sequence leading to Jallianwala Bagh and explain why the massacre cannot be defended as ordinary crowd control.

**Model answer**

Jallianwala Bagh arose within a wider Punjab crisis. Wartime repression, forcible recruitment, disease and economic distress had produced exceptional resentment. Dyer entered with troops and ordered firing without an effective warning or opportunity to disperse. The enclosed ground and restricted exits made escape extremely difficult; firing continued for about ten minutes.

Prior violence explains official fear but does not justify the act. Proportionate crowd control requires communication, opportunity to disperse and minimum necessary force. Dyer instead used exemplary terror against an unarmed trapped crowd. Subsequent martial-law humiliations—including the crawling order, flogging and forced salutes—confirm a punitive logic of subordination.

Responsibility must be distinguished: Reginald Dyer ordered the firing; Lieutenant-Governor Michael O'Dwyer bore wider political-administrative responsibility. The massacre was therefore not an accidental excess but the extreme expression of a coercive governing mentality.

The political rupture came not only from the firing but from the perceived inadequacy and division of the response.

### 1. Hunter inquiry and its limits

✅ **Fact:** The Government appointed the **Hunter Committee** to inquire into the Punjab disturbances.

✅ **Fact:** Congress conducted its own inquiry and regarded the official report as inadequate in light of documented Punjab brutalities.

The official process did criticise Dyer and his military career ended, but condemnation and removal were not equivalent to full legal or political accountability.

The committee did not speak with a single undivided moral voice. Its Indian members took a more critical position than the official majority. This distinction should be used cautiously: the essential point is that “the Hunter report” was not a complete national consensus.

### 2. British institutional and public response

✅ **Fact:** The House of Lords voted in support of Dyer's action.

✅ **Fact:** The British *Morning Post* organised a public fund that raised £30,000 for Dyer.

These responses mattered because they transformed the issue from “one officer's excess” into evidence, for many Indians, of a wider imperial moral failure.

### 3. Tagore and Gandhi

✅ **Fact:** Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in **1919** after the Punjab atrocities.

His action converted honour into shame: a badge granted by the state could no longer be retained without appearing to accept the humiliation of fellow Indians.

✅ **Fact:** Gandhi returned the **Kaiser-i-Hind medal in 1920**, linking his repudiation to the Punjab wrongs and the next political phase. This is a bridge only; the later programme belongs to Topic 20.

### 4. Why Gandhi rose

Gandhi's rise can be explained through four interacting causes:

```text
LOCAL CREDIBILITY
Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda produced visible results
               +
MASS IDIOM
truth, suffering, hartal, fasting, prayer, fearlessness
               +
ORGANISATION
ashram cadres, local leaders, Sabha, press, inherited networks
               +
POSTWAR CONJUNCTURE
hardship, repression, expectation and Punjab wrongs
               ↓
national leadership becomes plausible
```

### 5. Local agency prevents single-man causation

| Arena | Local and organisational agency |
|---|---|
| Champaran | Raj Kumar Shukla, Rajendra Prasad, Brij Kishore, J.B. Kripalani, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parikh |
| Ahmedabad | Anasuya Sarabhai, workers' meetings and collective discipline |
| Kheda | Vallabhbhai Patel, Indulal Yagnik, Gujarat Sabha and village organisers |
| Rowlatt | Younger Home Rule workers, inherited lists, local hartal organisers |
| Punjab | Kitchlew, Satyapal and locally rooted political unity |

Gandhi's leadership was real because it coordinated and symbolised these energies. It was not solitary authorship.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Gandhi's rise” centres one man and converts diverse struggles into stages of his biography.

**Reply:** The criticism is valid against hagiographic narration. A causal account must begin with grievances and local actors. Yet Gandhi's contribution cannot be dissolved entirely into structure: he supplied a recognisable method, accepted personal risk, connected local success to national language and became a focal point across regions.

### 7. Gandhi, Tagore and difference without hostility

The 2023 Mains question requires a wider intellectual comparison. Within this topic, retain only the anchor:

- Gandhi linked education to productive labour, ethical discipline and national self-reliance.
- Tagore opposed colonial imitation but feared narrow, coercive nationalism; his educational imagination stressed freedom, creativity, nature and universal humanism.
- Their disagreement was not nationalist versus anti-nationalist. It concerned the form nationalism and education should take.

### 8. Topic 20 bridge—strictly bounded

Punjab wrongs and the organisational lessons of 1919 made a later all-India programme thinkable. Gandhi now knew:

- mass response could be summoned;
- non-violence required deeper training;
- local grievances needed permanent organisation;
- imperial inquiries might not restore trust;
- withdrawal of cooperation could express lost legitimacy.

The programme, Khilafat alliance, Nagpur reorganisation and later chronology are not taught here.

One later chronology marker is retained only for the routed 2019 objective demand: Gandhi's 1930 breach of the Salt Law opened the Civil Disobedience phase, and the colonial repression that followed included declaring the Indian National Congress illegal. The movement's programme, regional forms and later negotiations remain outside this topic.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Explain Gandhi's rise between 1915 and 1919 without reducing Indian nationalism either to personal charisma or impersonal conditions.

**Model answer**

Gandhi's rise emerged from an interaction between leadership, local agency, organisation and a favourable but volatile conjuncture.

South Africa gave him a repertoire of petition, publicity, pledge, non-violent refusal, self-suffering, community support and negotiation. After returning in 1915, Gokhale's advice encouraged observation before intervention, while the Ahmedabad ashram trained disciplined cadres.

Local campaigns then built credibility. Raj Kumar Shukla brought Gandhi to Champaran, where Rajendra Prasad, Brij Kishore, Kripalani, Mahadev Desai and Narhari Parikh sustained inquiry. Anasuya Sarabhai helped organise Ahmedabad workers. Vallabhbhai Patel, Indulal Yagnik and Gujarat Sabha supported Kheda. Gandhi's contribution was to connect these local energies to a repeatable method and national moral language.

The postwar conjuncture widened opportunity: high prices, disease, repression, recruitment and frustrated expectations converged with Home Rule networks. Rowlatt transformed civil liberty into a mass issue, but violence exposed inadequate discipline. Punjab's massacre and humiliations then shattered faith in imperial self-correction; the weak accountability response deepened the rupture.

Thus Gandhi was neither solitary creator nor passive beneficiary. He became national leader because tested local credibility, accessible idiom and organisation met a legitimacy crisis—while 1919 also exposed the limits of centralised moral authority and under-trained mass action.

**Model answer**

Champaran and Kheda were peasant satyagrahas, but their grievance and political mechanism differed.

Champaran's cultivators were bound by tinkathia to grow indigo on three-twentieths of their holdings. As synthetic dyes weakened the market, planters demanded enhanced rent and illegal dues for release. Raj Kumar Shukla brought Gandhi; the campaign used refusal of an exclusion order, detailed testimony and an official inquiry. Tinkathia ended and a 25 per cent refund was accepted.

Kheda followed crop failure in 1918. Peasants claimed remission under revenue rules because yield was below one-fourth of normal. Gujarat Sabha, Vallabhbhai Patel and Indulal Yagnik organised collective withholding so that poorer peasants were not isolated. Repression included attachment of property. The government privately limited recovery to those able to pay, and Gandhi withdrew amid exhaustion.

Thus Champaran confronted planter exactions through inquiry, while Kheda confronted state revenue through solidarity-based non-payment. Both achieved limited redress and larger organisational learning.

**Rubric (10):** grievance distinction 2; method distinction 3; actors/evidence 2; outcomes 2; qualified comparison 1.

## 15-mark question

**Question:** Was the Rowlatt Satyagraha a failure? Give a reasoned assessment. (250 words)

**Model answer**

Judged by its immediate objective and non-violent discipline, the Rowlatt Satyagraha failed; judged as a demonstration of national reach and a source of organisational learning, it was transformative.

The Satyagraha Sabha asked members to pledge disobedience and accept arrest. Younger Home Rule workers and inherited contacts spread propaganda. A hartal, fasting and prayer translated detention without trial into a mass civil-liberties issue. The 6 April action produced strikes, demonstrations and unprecedented political awakening across regions.

However, scale exceeded preparation. Delhi observed hartal on 30 March because of date confusion, and violence followed. Punjab, Bombay and Ahmedabad experienced serious disorder. Gandhi was stopped while proceeding toward Punjab and returned toward Bombay to restore peace. He withdrew the movement on 18 April, admitting that people had entered civil disobedience without adequate training in non-violence. The Rowlatt law itself was not immediately nullified.

Yet “failure” alone misses the historical consequence. Gandhi emerged as a national rallying point, inherited networks were connected to a new mass idiom, and the limits of pledge without cadre discipline became unmistakable. Punjab wrongs then deepened the legitimacy crisis.

Therefore, it was an immediate tactical and organisational failure but a major political breakthrough. Its lesson was that popularity could summon a crowd, whereas satyagraha required trained organisation.

**Rubric (15):** objective/method 3; reach 3; violence/withdrawal 4; consequences 3; graded verdict 2.

## 20-mark question

**Question:** “Between 1917 and 1919 Gandhi converted local grievance into national moral politics, but never without local agency and political pressure.” Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer**

Between 1917 and 1919 Gandhi developed a repeatable method for connecting concrete grievance to a wider challenge to colonial legitimacy.

Champaran began with Raj Kumar Shukla's initiative and an existing indigo conflict. Gandhi's open refusal to leave, combined with testimony gathered by Rajendra Prasad, Brij Kishore, Kripalani, Mahadev Desai and Narhari Parikh, converted peasant complaint into public evidence and an official settlement. Ahmedabad used wage analysis, Anasuya Sarabhai's organisation, strike discipline and a fast to secure arbitration. Kheda used Gujarat Sabha, Patel and Yagnik to organise conditional revenue refusal and cross-class peasant solidarity.

These were moral campaigns because they insisted on truth, non-injury, self-suffering and acceptance of punishment. They were also pressure politics: production stopped, revenue was withheld, official orders were defied and repression imposed legitimacy costs. Negotiated settlements were therefore products of both moral claim and organised leverage.

The Rowlatt agitation carried this repertoire nationally through a pledge, hartal and inherited Home Rule contacts. Its violence and withdrawal exposed insufficient discipline. Punjab's massacre and weak accountability response then turned local and regional wrongs into a general crisis of imperial legitimacy.

Thus Gandhi neither created grievances nor acted alone. His achievement was to coordinate local agency through an accessible moral idiom and disciplined pressure; his limit was that national reach initially exceeded organisational control.

**Rubric (20):** thesis 2; three campaigns 6; named local agency 3; ethics-pressure analysis 4; Rowlatt/Punjab transition 3; limits and verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. The governing thesis

- Gandhi's rise was caused by **local credibility + mass idiom + organisation + postwar conjuncture**.
- He did not invent the grievances or pre-existing political networks.
- His innovation was to connect specific injustice, personal risk, disciplined non-cooperation, public communication and negotiation.
- 1919 established national reach but exposed insufficient preparation for non-violent mass action.

## B. South African formation

- 1893: Gandhi arrived in South Africa amid racial discrimination against Indians.
- 1894–1906: moderate phase—petitions, memorials, Natal Indian Congress and *Indian Opinion*.
- 1906: compulsory registration struggle; pledge, jail and certificate burning.
- Tolstoy Farm and Phoenix Settlement supplied material and ethical infrastructure.
- 1913: £3 poll tax and marriage judgment drew women, workers and ex-indentured labourers.
- Mass march and repression generated wider pressure.
- Negotiations secured concessions on tax, registration and Indian marriages.
- Limit: specific minority rights struggle in a settler colony, not an Indian-scale anti-colonial programme.

## C. Return and preparation

- January 1915: return to India.
- Gokhale advised observation before intervention.
- Gandhi travelled and initially avoided taking command of major political currents.
- Ahmedabad/Sabarmati ashram trained cadres in shared labour, simplicity, truth and non-violence.
- Ashram discipline produced credibility but could centralise moral authority.

## D. Champaran, 1917

- Tinkathia: indigo on three-twentieths of holdings.
- Synthetic dyes weakened indigo; planters monetised release through enhanced rents and illegal dues.
- Raj Kumar Shukla persistently brought Gandhi.
- Gandhi refused to leave and accepted possible punishment.
- Rajendra Prasad, Brij Kishore, Kripalani, Mahadev Desai and Narhari Parikh aided inquiry.
- Thousands of peasant statements established a public case.
- Official commission accepted abolition and compensation.
- Gandhi accepted a 25 per cent refund compromise.
- Significance: peasant grievance joined nationalist method.
- Limit: specific settlement, not agrarian revolution.

## E. Ahmedabad, 1918

- Plague bonus withdrawal clashed with continued wartime cost-of-living pressure.
- Gandhi supported a 35 per cent wage claim after studying costs and profits.
- Anasuya Sarabhai was a leading organiser.
- Arbitration failed initially; disciplined strike followed.
- Daily meetings and bulletin sustained morale.
- Fast fulfilled Gandhi's self-suffering promise but pressured millowners.
- Tribunal awarded 35 per cent.
- Limit: paternalism and moral-coercion concern.

## F. Kheda, 1918

- Crop yield below one-fourth supported remission under revenue rules.
- Gujarat Sabha, Patel and Yagnik organised villages.
- Better-off peasants withheld payment to protect poorer peasants.
- State attached cattle, goods and crops.
- Secret direction limited recovery to those able to pay.
- Movement ended amid exhaustion.
- Outcome: practical concession and cadre growth, but no public capitulation.

## G. Satyagraha doctrine

- Truth: specific claim, fact-finding and openness to correction.
- Ahimsa: no physical injury, hatred or crowd coercion.
- Self-suffering: accept penalty rather than inflict harm.
- Fearlessness: non-violence is not cowardice.
- Refusal: withdraw cooperation from a wrong.
- Means/end: present conduct must embody the desired future.
- Negotiation: compromise can acknowledge partial truth.
- Organisation: pledge, press, ashram, volunteers, relief and local leadership.
- Political mechanism: visible repression raises legitimacy costs.
- Limit: moral pressure, centralisation and weak leverage against legitimacy-indifferent repression.

## H. Rowlatt law and agitation

- Committee recommendations led to two bills; one was enacted in March 1919.
- Indian members opposed; Jinnah, Malaviya and Mazhar-ul-Huq resigned.
- Detention without ordinary trial weakened habeas corpus and judicial safeguards.
- Satyagraha Sabha formed in February 1919.
- Younger Home Rule workers and old contacts aided mobilisation.
- Delhi acted on 30 March through date confusion.
- 6 April was the nationwide hartal.
- Violence affected Punjab, Bombay and Ahmedabad.
- Gandhi was stopped from reaching Punjab and returned toward Bombay.
- 18 April: withdrawal because non-violent discipline had failed.

## I. Punjab and Amritsar

- Background: recruitment coercion, repression, disease and economic distress.
- 10 April: Kitchlew and Satyapal arrested.
- Violence followed; army entered and military authority expanded.
- Reginald Dyer prohibited meetings.
- 13 April: Baisakhi gathering in enclosed Jallianwala Bagh.
- No effective warning before sustained firing.
- Avoid disputed casualty figures.
- Martial law brought crawling order, flogging, forced salutes and humiliation.
- Reginald Dyer ordered firing.
- Michael O'Dwyer bore wider Punjab administrative responsibility.
- Udham Singh later assassinated Michael O'Dwyer.

## J. Inquiry, response and rupture

- Hunter Committee was the official inquiry.
- Congress conducted its own inquiry.
- Indian members of Hunter took a more critical position.
- Official criticism and Dyer's removal were not full accountability.
- House of Lords support and the *Morning Post* fund hardened Indian disillusionment.
- Tagore renounced his knighthood in 1919.
- Gandhi returned Kaiser-i-Hind in 1920.
- Punjab wrongs and organisational lessons made a later all-India programme thinkable.
