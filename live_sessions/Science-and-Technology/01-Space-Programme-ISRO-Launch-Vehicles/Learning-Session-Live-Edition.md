# Space Programme: ISRO, Organisation and Launch Vehicles — Live Learning Edition

An Indian weather satellite is useful only if it can be built, launched to an appropriate orbit, controlled, and its information delivered to users. A rocket alone is not a space programme. In this session, follow that complete chain from the national need for access, through orbital mechanics and launch engineering, to institutions, law and future capacity. **✅** denotes an attributable source-grounded statement; **⚠️** denotes an explanatory inference or conditional assessment. Dated developments are not statements about every later flight.

```text
Need: weather warning / Earth observation / communications / navigation
     ↓
Design satellite → choose useful orbit → build suitable launcher
     ↓                         ↓
Test stages and pad       launch + separate payload
     ↓                         ↓
Track/control satellite → distribute services to Indian users
     ↘ authorise, insure, supervise, sustain suppliers ↗
```

*The launcher solves access; satellite, ground system, legal oversight and user service complete the public benefit.*

**First imagine a cyclone warning.** A satellite can see cloud systems, but if an emergency replacement must wait for another country's launch schedule, service resilience depends on decisions outside India's control. Conversely, an Indian rocket without a working meteorological satellite and ground-distribution network does not warn anyone. The cyclone example establishes why *independent access* matters; it does **not** establish that every mission must be domestically launched or that local launches guarantee service.

1. **Access:** the ability to place a specified payload on a mission-suitable trajectory when needed. Autonomy means having viable domestic options and supply chains, not rejecting international cooperation. Payload mass, orbital destination, mission assurance and launch slots determine whether autonomy is real.
2. **Space segment:** satellites or spacecraft do the work in orbit. Earth-observation platforms supply imagery; communication platforms relay signals; navigation constellations supply position, navigation and timing. NavIC is India's own regional navigation system; GAGAN augments GPS for aviation and is not another independent navigation constellation; their applications are explored in the satellite-services topic.
3. **Ground and user segments:** command stations maintain spacecraft; receivers, standards and dissemination bring results to users. A failed receiver ecosystem can defeat an otherwise successful launch. One must distinguish a satellite's deployment from a functioning end-to-end service.
4. **Transport and institutions:** ✅ Department of Space (DoS) is the public-policy umbrella; Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) develops mission and launcher capabilities; Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota (SDSC-SHAR), integrates and conducts launches. Institutional authorisation and commercial services will be taught in Lesson 8.

**Challenge:** why not buy every launch cheaply overseas? ⚠️ Competition can lower costs and access to foreign specialists can add resilience. But export controls, unavailable launch slots, sensitive payloads and crisis priorities can undermine a wholly outsourced strategy. **Reply:** develop mission-critical national capability while purchasing or partnering where it serves cost and scientific goals; sovereignty is an option, not autarky. India-centric answer: connect an Earth-observation emergency need to *orbital access plus data delivery*, not merely to a rocket photograph.

**UPSC application:** GS-III's “indigenization of technology” and “awareness in ... Space” require both achievement and effect. Start an answer with the service need, show the launch-to-user chain, then qualify reliability and public cost. To choose a launch vehicle we first need to understand the destination.

### Revision notes

1. Independent access means retaining a viable domestic option to place a specified payload on a suitable trajectory when required.
2. Launch autonomy strengthens resilience but does not require rejecting international launch or scientific cooperation.
3. A useful space service needs a spacecraft, suitable orbit, ground control, processing and delivery to users; a rocket supplies only orbital access.
4. Earth observation, communication and navigation payloads perform different functions and therefore require different mission designs.
5. NavIC is India's regional navigation system, whereas GAGAN augments GPS for civil aviation.
6. DoS provides the governmental policy umbrella; ISRO develops mission and launcher capability; SDSC-SHAR integrates and conducts launches.
7. Deployment of a satellite is not equivalent to reliable operation or last-mile public service.
8. Strategic autonomy should be judged by mission-critical options, supply chains and delivered applications rather than by autarky.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is possessing a rocket neither necessary proof of an independent space service nor the whole reason to build sovereign access?

**Model answer:** A rocket delivers the spacecraft, but useful service also needs the right orbit, spacecraft, control stations and users; sovereign launch reduces dependence on external launch windows for critical missions while cooperation can remain useful.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Own rocket = guaranteed useful satellite service” ignores payload operation and ground delivery.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks (150 words)

**Original Mains prompt:** Examine how independent orbital access strengthens India's disaster-response capacity without itself guaranteeing a usable warning service. Answer in 150 words.

**Worked Mains response:** Independent access gives India a domestic option to launch or replace a suitable spacecraft on a mission-relevant schedule; it reduces exposure to unavailable foreign launch slots, not the need for cooperation. For cyclone response, an Earth-observation satellite can supply cloud imagery, but the selected orbit must provide useful repeat observations. ISRO's launch capability and Sriharikota's integration facilities address delivery; spacecraft health, ground command, image processing and distribution to emergency authorities turn imagery into a warning. NavIC supplies regional positioning rather than being a weather-imaging satellite; GAGAN augments GPS for aviation, illustrating why space services have distinct user chains. Overseas launches may still be economical for some missions. Thus launch autonomy strengthens resilience only when matched to payload, orbit, reliable operations and last-mile use.

**Scoring guide:** Credit the autonomy mechanism (2), named Indian launch/application evidence (2), orbit-to-ground-to-user causal chain (4), and the cooperation/service qualification with a reasoned verdict (2); a rocket-only essay cannot earn full credit.

- CA found (same sole current linkage): ISRO, “ISRO and NASA Celebrate One Successful Year of NISAR Mission on Orbit” (published 17 August 2026; anniversary event held 4 August 2026). It supplies a current mission anchor; the orbital definitions remain matters of mechanics.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
                           distant equatorial circle: GEO
                         ↗   (appears fixed from ground)
Earth ○ ----------- stretched ellipse: GTO ------------↗
     \ low near-Earth loop: LEO; tilted near-pole loop: polar
      \ near-polar track + matching Earth's yearly solar motion: SSO
```

*A destination, a plane and a transfer route are different descriptions; do not put them all in one “height” ranking.*

Picture taking repeated crop images. The important requirement is similar lighting at each pass, not hovering above one field all day. **Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO)** is a near-polar low-Earth orbit whose orbital plane precesses so that the spacecraft crosses a given latitude at approximately the same local solar time. The Sun still moves in the sky seasonally and lighting is not literally identical. A **polar orbit** passes close to the poles; not every polar orbit meets the Sun-synchronous precession condition.

| Term | Geometry and role | India-centric illustration and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LEO (low Earth orbit) | Relatively low altitude; rapid circuits; category of altitude, not a single inclination | Earth observation and human-orbit missions; any one LEO craft does not continuously see all India |
| Polar orbit | High-inclination plane gives successive swaths as Earth rotates beneath | Mapping land and coasts; “polar” is not synonymous with “sun-synchronous” |
| SSO | Special near-polar orbit with approximately stable local-solar-time passes | Compare monsoon-season imagery; still subject to cloud obstruction |
| GTO (geosynchronous transfer orbit) | Highly elliptical transfer trajectory that approaches geosynchronous height near apogee | Communication satellite separates here and uses its *own* propulsion for orbit raising |
| GSO / GEO | GSO: orbital period equals Earth's sidereal rotation; GEO: **circular equatorial** GSO near 35,786 km altitude | GEO appears fixed above one longitude, useful for communication/weather; GEO is a subset of GSO |

1. A satellite remains in orbit because its sideways motion makes it continually fall around Earth rather than straight down; higher orbit is not automatically “better.”
2. An Earth-observation payload often needs detailed, repeated passes; lower altitude improves potential ground resolution but reduces each spacecraft's instantaneous footprint.
3. A fixed-looking communications platform needs the Earth's rotation period *and* a circular equatorial orbit: an inclined geosynchronous craft traces a sky pattern rather than staying at one point.
4. **GTO → GEO:** after launch-vehicle separation near transfer orbit, onboard propulsion raises perigee and circularises/adjusts inclination. A launch to GTO is not a direct placement in GEO; extra propulsion, fuel and successful manoeuvres remain necessary.

**Objection:** “PSLV means only polar payloads; GSLV means it drops spacecraft into final GEO.” **Reply:** launcher names suggest design heritage, not hard orbit prohibitions; PSLV supports multiple orbit profiles. GSLV commonly injects a communication payload into *transfer* orbit. The 2018 Prelims GS-I Q95 concerns a PSLV/GSLV comparison, but its specific statements and answer are not reproduced here. **UPSC application:** describe the orbit's geometry before matching the vehicle; avoid calling all geosynchronous orbits geostationary. Orbit destination now sets the energy requirement addressed in Lesson 3.

### Revision notes

1. LEO describes a relatively low altitude region; it does not specify one orbital inclination or mission.
2. A polar orbit is defined by its high inclination and near-polar ground track.
3. An SSO is a special near-polar orbit whose plane precesses to preserve approximately the same local solar time on successive passes.
4. Similar local solar time improves image comparison but does not eliminate clouds or make illumination literally identical.
5. A geosynchronous orbit matches Earth's sidereal rotation period; it need not appear fixed in the sky.
6. GEO is the circular, equatorial subset of geosynchronous orbit and appears fixed above one longitude.
7. GTO is an elongated transfer orbit, not the final operational GEO.
8. After GTO separation, the spacecraft normally supplies orbit-raising, circularisation and any required inclination correction.
9. Mission matching begins with geometry and energy needs, not with assumptions drawn from a launcher's name.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a satellite delivered to GTO not immediately be described as “stationary above India”?

**Model answer:** GTO is elongated and transitional; the spacecraft must change its own orbit into a circular equatorial geostationary one, including inclination correction where required, before it appears fixed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating geosynchronous *transfer* with geostationary *operational* orbit erases spacecraft propulsion and mission risk.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks (250 words)

**Original Mains prompt:** Analyse why the terms low Earth, polar, Sun-synchronous, geosynchronous, geostationary and geosynchronous transfer cannot be used interchangeably when planning Indian satellite missions. Answer in 250 words.

**Worked Mains response:** These terms describe different properties: height, plane, lighting pattern, period or transfer path. Low Earth orbit describes an altitude region, not a fixed inclination; a near-polar orbit crosses high latitudes as Earth rotates beneath it. Sun-synchronous orbit adds a precessing near-polar plane that maintains approximately the same local solar time at successive passes. That improves comparability of Indian crop imagery, but neither removes clouds nor makes a single satellite continuously visible. Geosynchronous orbit instead matches Earth's sidereal rotation period; only its circular equatorial subset, GEO, appears fixed above one longitude and suits continuous-looking communications or weather coverage. A communication satellite injected by GSLV into elongated GTO has reached neither final GEO nor operational service: its own propulsion must raise perigee and correct inclination. PSLV's name likewise does not confine it to polar flights. Matching orbit geometry and energy to payload prevents a costly error in selecting launcher, onboard fuel and ground service. The final test is usable data or connectivity, not merely successful separation.

**Scoring guide:** Reward distinct definitions and SSO mechanism (5), GSO/GEO and GTO-to-GEO causality (4), Indian imaging/communication examples and launcher implications (4), and limitations/service qualification (2); do not credit a mere altitude ranking.

## Lesson 3 — Stages and propulsion

- CA found: None directly attributable in the last six months for a flown SE2000/SC120 stage; its development status is based on the dated technical sources, not search silence.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
lift-off → burn heavy lower stage → discard its empty tanks/motor
          → lighter vehicle accelerates → discard next stage
          → upper stage places payload on target trajectory
          → separate payload → spacecraft manoeuvres, if needed
```

*Discarding spent mass lets the later propulsion accelerate a lighter stack; separation is a risk as well as an efficiency gain.*

A rocket carries both its fuel and the substance needed for combustion because it cannot rely on atmospheric oxygen in space. The hot exhaust leaves backward; the rocket gains forward momentum. If we keep hauling a dry, empty lower stage, upper-stage fuel is spent accelerating useless mass. **Staging** solves this; it also creates new ignition and separation failure points.

| Engine family | Simple mental model | Mechanism and trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | Pre-filled motor, ignited like a controlled firework | Structurally straightforward, high initial thrust; after ignition normally cannot shut down/throttle at will. Used in boosters and several first/upper stages |
| Earth-storable liquid | Pump/feed fuel and oxidiser from separate tanks | Can provide controlled burns; hypergolic propellants such as UH25/UDMH and nitrogen tetroxide are toxic, requiring careful handling |
| Cryogenic liquid | Very cold liquid hydrogen plus liquid oxygen (LOX) | High specific impulse, especially valuable for high-energy upper stages; extreme cold, insulation, turbopumps and hydrogen's low density complicate engineering |
| Semi-cryogenic liquid | LOX plus refined kerosene | Denser fuel can favour high-thrust lower stages; LOX still requires cooling. ISRO's SE2000/SC120 development is not a currently flown replacement for LVM3's existing core |

1. **Thrust** is push; a launcher must produce sufficient thrust relative to weight at liftoff. **Specific impulse** expresses propellant-use efficiency, not the total amount of payload a vehicle can carry by itself.
2. **Boosters** augment initial lift; the *core* and upper stages then keep accelerating the stack. Count stages by propulsion sequence, not by the number of strap-on motors visible in a photograph.
3. **Why cryogenic upper stages?** High exhaust velocity allows more velocity change for a given propellant mass after heavy lower stages have fallen away. Hydrogen storage is bulky and engine startup difficult; it is not a universal first-stage solution.
4. **Why semi-cryogenic?** Dense kerosene and liquid oxygen can offer a different thrust/volume/cost trade-off. ✅ India's SE2000 engine and SC120 stage are an **under-development payload-enhancement proposal for LVM3**, not a flown stage; never swap this with the existing L110 core.

**Example with limit:** Think of a multi-leg hill journey where you discard empty supply crates between climbs. It explains mass-saving but does **not** explain orbital velocity, staging stresses, engine thrust or why a failed separation ends a mission. **Objection:** higher specific impulse alone must always win. **Reply:** liftoff thrust, density, cost, safety, restart capability, manufacturability and the mission's orbit all matter. **UPSC use:** specify propellants when comparing cryogenic (hydrogen + LOX) with semi-cryogenic (kerosene + LOX); solid ≠ unthrottleable rocket *as a whole* when later controlled stages exist. Now follow how India learned these engineering disciplines.

### Revision notes

1. A rocket carries both fuel and oxidiser because it cannot depend on atmospheric oxygen through the flight.
2. Staging improves performance by discarding spent structural and tank mass before later acceleration.
3. Every separation and upper-stage ignition adds a mission-critical failure point.
4. Solid motors provide strong thrust and comparatively simple storage but normally offer little post-ignition throttling or shutdown.
5. Earth-storable liquid stages permit controlled burns but use toxic propellants that complicate handling.
6. Cryogenic propulsion combines liquid hydrogen with LOX and offers high specific impulse at the cost of extreme-cold storage and complex machinery.
7. Semi-cryogenic propulsion combines refined kerosene with LOX; it is distinct from hydrogen-fuelled cryogenic propulsion.
8. Thrust measures push, while specific impulse measures propellant-use efficiency; neither alone determines payload capacity.
9. India's SE2000/SC120 proposal remains an under-development LVM3 enhancement, not the vehicle's presently flown L110 core.
10. Ground-tested hardware must still be qualified in the complete vehicle before it can be described as an operational stage.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a cryogenic upper stage improve a launcher without implying that every stage should use liquid hydrogen?

**Model answer:** Its high propellant efficiency is useful for later velocity gain, but bulky cold hydrogen tanks and complex ground/engine systems can make other propulsion better for high-thrust lower stages.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating specific impulse as a complete measure of cost, thrust and mission reliability.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks (250 words)

**Original Mains prompt:** Examine how staging and propellant choice jointly shape the performance and risks of an Indian launch vehicle. Answer in 250 words.

**Worked Mains response:** A launcher must accelerate its payload while carrying its own oxidiser; staging discards spent mass so later propulsion need not lift empty tanks. It improves achievable velocity but requires reliable separation and ignition. Solid motors provide strong early thrust and comparatively simple storage, as in LVM3's twin S200 boosters; their limited post-ignition control makes them unsuitable as the sole precision tool. Controlled liquid propulsion, such as LVM3's L110 core, offers another thrust-management choice, though earth-storable propellants impose handling risks. Its C25 liquid-hydrogen/liquid-oxygen cryogenic upper stage exploits high specific impulse where late velocity gain matters, but hydrogen's low density and extreme cooling complicate engineering. By contrast, the proposed LOX–kerosene SE2000/SC120 semi-cryogenic upgrade targets a denser lower-stage fuel combination; it is not a flown replacement for the L110. PSLV's solid–liquid–solid–liquid sequence illustrates a different mission balance and its liquid final stage aids insertion. Neither specific impulse nor motor count alone predicts payload delivered: thrust, mass, target orbit, qualification and mission assurance also decide. The best architecture is mission-specific, not uniformly cryogenic.

**Scoring guide:** Allocate staging mechanism and failure risk (3), accurate solid/liquid/cryo/semi-cryo mechanisms (6), named PSLV/LVM3/upgrade applications (4), and qualified performance verdict (2); penalise calling the proposed stage operational.

```text
SLV-3       → basic experimental orbital access
   ↓
ASLV        → added learning in vehicle control, staging and integration
   ↓
PSLV        → versatile operational mission class
   ├── GSLV → higher-energy GTO missions with cryogenic upper stage
   ├── LVM3 → heavier lift and human-flight base
   └── SSLV → dedicated smaller-payload/response market
```

*This is a capability ladder, not a claim that each successor made every predecessor obsolete.*

How do you learn to launch a heavy communication satellite? You do not begin with the heaviest mission. ✅ The **Satellite Launch Vehicle-3 (SLV-3)** was India's first experimental satellite launcher; the **Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle (ASLV)** extended technological learning before PSLV. These were test-and-learning systems, not the frontline commercial fleet. Repeated engineering work developed guidance, stage separation, launch operations and organisational competence—none of which follows automatically from drawing a larger vehicle.

1. **Mission differentiation:** PSLV established repeatable versatile launches; GSLV's indigenous cryogenic upper stage addressed heavier GTO-class needs; LVM3 expanded heavy-lift capability; SSLV addresses a different, smaller market. Detailed architecture follows in Lessons 5–7.
2. **Success as a process:** a launcher is qualified through ground testing, integration, flight evidence and anomaly investigation. “Operational” denotes a programme status, not a promise of a successful next flight. ISRO's historical Launchers page identifies PSLV, GSLV and LVM3 as active operational families; ✅ a DoS reply of 17 December 2025 said SSLV had completed developmental flights. ✅ Named successful flights include GSLV-F16/NISAR (30 July 2025) and LVM3-M5/CMS-03 (2 November 2025); neither certifies the outcome of later flights. ✅ ISRO's PSLV update published on 25 February 2026 said a national-level expert committee was reviewing the reason for the PSLV anomaly; this update date is distinct from the January 2026 flight event. These classifications, mission outcomes and review notices have different dates and scopes.
3. **Why a diverse fleet?** Suppose one low-mass ocean-monitoring satellite wants a dedicated timely flight while a heavy communications craft needs a GTO transfer. Optimising only one rocket produces avoidable cost or capability gaps. Payload size *and destination* decide the vehicle.

**Challenge:** is technological chronology merely trivia? **Reply:** each rung explains the next bottleneck: orbital access → reliable multi-mission flight → high-energy upper stage → heavier payload and crew-safety engineering → dedicated small payloads. ⚠️ However, a linear progression hides parallel projects and commercial/military constraints: do not claim SSLV is intrinsically superior to PSLV or that private participation began only after it.

**UPSC application:** in a lineage question show *what problem each family solves* and distinguish developmental vehicles from operational mission classes. A launch vehicle may have a mixed success record; avoid inflated “100% success” claims. Since “versatile” needs a mechanism, we examine PSLV rather than stop at its nickname.

### Revision notes

1. SLV-3 established India's experimental satellite-launch capability rather than a complete modern fleet.
2. ASLV extended learning in control, staging, integration and launch operations.
3. PSLV became the versatile operational branch for multiple mission profiles.
4. GSLV addressed higher-energy missions through an indigenous cryogenic upper stage.
5. LVM3 expanded the heavier-payload class and supplies the base for a human-rated derivative.
6. SSLV is a dedicated small-payload branch, not a more powerful successor to LVM3.
7. Vehicle qualification combines ground tests, integration, flight evidence and anomaly investigation.
8. “Operational” identifies an established programme role; it does not guarantee the result of the next mission.
9. Successful GSLV-F16/NISAR and LVM3-M5/CMS-03 flights are dated mission outcomes, not fleet-wide reliability certificates.
10. ISRO's 25 February 2026 PSLV update records an expert review after the January anomaly; publication date, flight date and eventual cause must remain separate.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is SSLV not simply the next, more powerful rung above LVM3 in a single rocket ranking?

**Model answer:** SSLV addresses small-payload, responsive launch demand, whereas LVM3 serves heavy missions; development is branching by mission need, not a monotonic size ranking.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading a chronological diagram as “every newer vehicle carries more mass to every orbit.”

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks (150 words)

**Original Mains prompt:** Discuss why India's development from SLV-3 and ASLV to its present launch-vehicle families is better read as capability differentiation than a single ladder of increasing rocket size. Answer in 150 words.

**Worked Mains response:** SLV-3 provided experimental orbital access; ASLV added learning in control, staging and integration. These developmental steps supported the engineering base for PSLV's versatile missions, but learning did not make every subsequent flight automatically reliable. Different demands then produced branches. GSLV Mk II's cryogenic upper stage serves more demanding transfer-orbit payloads; LVM3's different booster–core–upper-stage architecture supports heavier missions and a human-rated derivative. SSLV instead addresses dedicated smaller-payload timing, not a payload class above LVM3. For example, a small ocean-monitoring replacement may value its own launch window more than maximum rocket lift, whereas a heavy communication spacecraft requires appropriate mass and orbital energy. Recent named successes cannot establish the reliability of all future flights. India's technological progression is therefore a portfolio of qualified mission options; chronology explains bottlenecks, not a universal size ranking.

**Scoring guide:** Award the developmental distinction (2), PSLV/GSLV/LVM3/SSLV functions (4), a concrete payload–orbit comparison (2), and a non-teleological qualification (2); a list of names alone is insufficient.

## Lesson 5 — PSLV versatile missions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
- Book context: Queried — the PSLV architecture, variants and official launcher material were studied; no additional local space textbook evidence was available.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
PSLV lift:     solid first stage (+ optional strap-ons)
                    ↓
               liquid second stage
                    ↓
               solid third stage
                    ↓
               liquid fourth stage → accurate injection → payload separation
```

*Alternating propulsion types combine strong initial lift with controllable final placement; stage numbering is not a promise of mission success.*

“Polar” in Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle names a central design use, not an iron wall around its mission portfolio. ✅ ISRO presents PSLV as a workhorse with variants and missions to differing orbit profiles; Earth's resources monitoring, navigation and interplanetary missions demonstrate versatility. For example, a remote-sensing payload needs repeated observation from a near-polar orbit, while a different mission may use another trajectory; a mission profile is not inferred from the rocket's name alone.

1. The first solid stage and optional strap-on motors supply strong early thrust. Different strap-on configurations adapt to mass/mission requirements; a **core-alone** configuration is not a different space agency or a guarantee of lower total mission price.
2. The liquid second stage follows with a controlled burn; solid third stage supplies another velocity increment. The liquid fourth stage (PS4) refines the final trajectory and can support multi-payload deployment, subject to mission-specific constraints and approvals.
3. **Versatility has limits:** injection accuracy, fairing volume, payload mass and destination energy constrain what PSLV can deliver. It cannot replace every heavy GTO service of GSLV or LVM3. Nor is every “multiple satellite” launch simple: differing desired orbital planes/altitudes complicate deployment.
4. **Reliability is earned per flight.** PSLV-C61/EOS-09 (18 May 2025) was unsuccessful after a PS3-stage observation; PSLV-C62 experienced a third-stage anomaly during its January 2026 flight. *Public treaty responsibility does not vanish when a private firm builds the rocket; not all collision settings use the same liability rule.*

A launch can fail near inhabited areas or leave debris in orbit. Ordinary sale-of-service contracts cannot by themselves extinguish international responsibility. **✅ Outer Space Treaty (1967), Article VI:** States bear international responsibility for national space activities, including private ones; private activities require authorisation and continuing supervision. Article II prohibits national appropriation of outer space; Article I affirms freedom of exploration/use. “Peaceful” should not be rewritten as a complete treaty ban on every dual-use satellite.

1. **Liability Convention (1972):** a *launching State* may be a State that launches, procures launch, or from whose territory or facility an object is launched; more than one State can qualify. Surface-of-Earth and aircraft-in-flight damage attracts **absolute liability**, not a need to prove fault. Damage elsewhere than on Earth's surface to another State's space object/persons/property on it is **fault-based**. The international claimant addresses launching States; domestic contracts may allocate expenses internally, subject to applicable rules.
2. **Registration Convention (1975):** a launching State maintains a registry and supplies specified object information to the UN Secretary-General. Registration helps identification; it is not a substitute for collision avoidance, authorisation or compensation.
3. **Safety chain:** design/reliability qualification → trajectory and range exclusion planning → launch decision and flight-termination safeguards where applicable → track separated stages and space objects → mitigate debris and protect continuing operations. A local environmental or community concern is not settled by a successful flight alone.
4. **Indian regulatory problem:** ✅ Indian Space Policy 2023 supplies role separation and IN-SPACe processes, but no comprehensive domestic space statute was identified as of the stated verification date. ⚠️ Therefore statutory clarity on private liability allocation, insurance thresholds, review/appeal and supervision is a sensible reform question, **not** an assertion that no Indian law ever applies to space activity.
5. **Market reality:** customers price failure risk and schedule assurance. Licensing, spectrum/orbital coordination, export-control sensitivity, indemnities, insurance and quality control affect whether a cheap quoted launch actually becomes a viable commercial offer.

**Counterargument:** strict supervision can deter entry. **Reply:** proportionate, transparent authorisation and predictable insurance can lower uncertainty for serious firms; blanket deregulation transfers risk to the public and State. **Residual:** very high insurance requirements can exclude small startups, so calibrate requirements to mission-specific risk. **UPSC use:** distinguish absolute surface liability from fault-based in-orbit liability; treaty obligation from domestic policy; regulation from commercial marketing. ### Revision notes

1. Outer Space Treaty Article VI keeps States internationally responsible for national activities, including private activities.
2. Private national space activity requires State authorisation and continuing supervision.
3. Outer Space Treaty Article II prohibits national appropriation; Article I protects freedom of exploration and use.
4. A launching State can qualify through launch, procurement, territory or facility, so more than one State may be involved.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These are **original questions, not UPSC PYQs**. Decode the directive before drafting: *Examine* requires mechanisms and limits, *Discuss* requires connected dimensions and a weighed conclusion, and *Analyse* requires causal explanation and trade-offs. ## 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Examine why independent launch capability is not equivalent to a complete, independent space service. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:** Independent launch gives India a choice of launch window, payload and trajectory for missions; it is a necessary *option*, not a complete satellite service. For instance, ISRO's PSLV can deploy Earth-observation craft, but a weather-warning chain also requires working sensors, satellite control, data processing and delivery to disaster authorities. The launcher must match both mass and orbit: a Sun-synchronous observation platform needs repeated comparable passes, whereas a heavy communication craft may first enter GTO and use onboard propulsion to reach GEO. DoS and ISRO supply sovereign policy and technology, while IN-SPACe authorises private activity and NSIL commercialises services under the 2023 policy framework. This differentiated system can shorten dependence on foreign launch windows and deepen domestic supply chains. Yet domestic control does not eliminate launch failures, constellation degradation, user-device gaps or international cooperation. Real independence is the resilient ability to deliver the *application* reliably, not merely own a rocket.

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** direct thesis distinguishing launch access from complete service (2); launch-to-orbit-to-ground-to-user causal chain (3); named Indian vehicle, orbit and institutional evidence (3); cooperation, reliability and last-mile qualification with a reasoned verdict (2). **Total: 10.**

## 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Discuss how propulsion and orbit requirements shaped the differentiation of India's PSLV, GSLV, LVM3 and SSLV. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** The fleet is differentiated by the *payload–destination problem*, not by an ascending list of names. Low-Earth and Sun-synchronous imaging missions need repeated passes; higher-energy transfer trajectories and heavier satellites demand another propulsion and mass class. PSLV's solid–liquid–solid–liquid sequence and optional boosters make it a versatile platform for Earth-observation and other mission profiles. But neither its name nor versatility implies every possible payload or orbit. GSLV Mk II adds an indigenous liquid-hydrogen/liquid-oxygen cryogenic upper stage, helping with heavier communication payloads to **GTO**; the satellite, not the launcher, subsequently circularises to GEO. LVM3 couples twin S200 boosters, an L110 liquid core and C25 cryogenic upper stage for heavier missions. Its human-rated derivative is the Gaganyaan base, but human rating includes reliability and escape systems and does not mean an ordinary flight already carried a crew. SSLV instead meets small, responsive payload demand through three solid stages and terminal trimming; it can offer dedicated scheduling when a PSLV rideshare is poorly matched. ASLV and SLV-3 supplied earlier technology-learning steps. Semi-cryogenic SE2000/SC120 is under development, not a flown LVM3 core. Thus thrust, fuel efficiency, target orbit, payload mass and timing generate a **portfolio** of solutions. The qualification is operational: cost, quality control, safety and anomaly resolution determine whether theoretical capability becomes dependable access.

**Quantified rubric (15 marks):** payload–orbit framing and fleet-differentiation thesis (2); accurate PSLV and GSLV propulsion/orbit mechanisms (5); accurate LVM3 and SSLV architecture, role and human-rating distinction (4); developmental lineage and non-flown semi-cryogenic qualification (2); reliability, cost and mission-matching conclusion (2). **Total: 15.**

## 20 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Analyse whether India's emerging launch ecosystem can simultaneously advance strategic autonomy, commercial growth and public safety. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** These aims reinforce one another only when technology, institutions and risk allocation advance together. Independent PSLV/GSLV/LVM3 options reduce dependence on external launch windows for Earth observation, communications and heavier missions; ISRO retains research and strategic capability under the Department of Space. Indian Space Policy 2023 distinguishes this role from IN-SPACe's authorisation and continuing supervision of non-government entities and NSIL's commercial contracting. Skyroot's Vikram-S suborbital flight demonstrates private entry, not orbital delivery; SSLV–HAL technology transfer enables manufacturing, not a qualified HAL-built flight. Cabinet-approved NGLV and the Third Launch Pad indicate planned capacity, not operational service. **Commercial viability** depends on production, integration slots, pricing, insurance and repeated reliable flights: GSLV-F16/NISAR and LVM3-M5/CMS-03 succeeded in 2025, whereas PSLV-C61 and C62 experienced anomalies. ISRO's 25 February 2026 update confirms expert review of the PSLV anomaly but supplies no root cause; these outcomes establish no fleet-wide reliability rate. **Public safety** is not dispensable: Outer Space Treaty Article VI requires oversight of private activity; the Liability Convention makes launching States absolutely liable for surface/aircraft damage and uses fault-based liability for damage to another space object. Registration, debris mitigation, range protection and proportionate insurance make risk more predictable for credible firms. Yet heavy compliance can price out small entrants, and reuse saves money only with affordable refurbishment and adequate cadence. India should therefore pair competitive participation with transparent permissions, credible investigation and risk-based safety standards. Autonomy is measured by safely delivered services, not approvals.

**Quantified rubric (20 marks):** conditional thesis and strategic-autonomy mechanism (4); differentiated institutional roles, private participation and commercial milestone distinctions (5); treaty liability, authorisation, debris and safety analysis (5); dated mission/anomaly evidence with uncertainty control (3); proportionate regulation, cost/cadence trade-off and reasoned verdict (3). **Total: 20.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Access and mission chain

- National option for critical launches ≠ refusal of international cooperation. Space + ground + user segments produce applications; launcher alone produces orbital access.
- Mission chain: payload need → orbit → launch energy/vehicle → integration and range → injection → spacecraft operations → data/service. GS-III links space awareness to indigenisation and applications.
- NavIC is India's regional navigation constellation; GAGAN augments GPS for aviation. These are use-side links, primarily owned by topic 02.

## Orbit and propulsion discriminators

- LEO describes altitude; polar describes orbital inclination; SSO adds precession preserving approximate local-solar-time passes. GSO has Earth's sidereal period; GEO is circular and equatorial (~35,786 km); GTO is elliptical transfer, **not** final GEO.
- Staging discards dead mass; boosters are not necessarily separate serial stages. Solid: simple high thrust, little post-ignition control. Earth-storable liquid: controlled but toxic. Cryogenic: LOX–liquid hydrogen high specific impulse and storage complexity. Semi-cryogenic: LOX–kerosene, India's SE2000/SC120 LVM3 upgrade under development.
- PSLV: four stages solid–liquid–solid–liquid, optional strap-ons, mission versatility, not unlimited mass/energy. GSLV Mk II: solid core/liquid strap-ons, liquid second, cryogenic upper. LVM3: twin S200, L110 and C25; human rating is additional crew-safety work. SSLV: three solid stages plus terminal trimming for smaller responsive launches.

## Capability chronology and evidence

- SLV-3 and ASLV = developmental learning; PSLV = multi-mission workhorse; GSLV = indigenous cryogenic route; LVM3 = heavy lift; SSLV = small-payload branch, not a heavy successor.
- Dated missions: GSLV-F16/NISAR succeeded 30 July 2025; LVM3-M5/CMS-03 succeeded 2 November 2025. PSLV-C61 was unsuccessful on 18 May 2025; PSLV-C62 had a PS3 anomaly during its January 2026 flight. ISRO's separate update was published on 25 February 2026 and said an expert committee was reviewing the reason; it did not publish a cause. Mission date, update date and investigation conclusion are distinct.
- Skyroot's Vikram-S flew **suborbitally** 18 November 2022: first private rocket launch ≠ privately delivered satellite into orbit. 2026 Prelims Q46 has only a provisional local key; no official answer asserted. 2018 Q95 official key is unavailable locally; learn PSLV/GSLV mechanisms, not invented question statements.

## Roles, law and future readiness

- DoS policy umbrella; ISRO capability/R&D; IN-SPACe enables, authorises and supervises non-government entities; NSIL sells commercial services. Space Policy 2023 = policy, not comprehensive space statute. 2024 FDI automatic thresholds: 74% specified satellite segment; 49% launch vehicles/spaceports; 100% specified components/subsystems; check current notification before reuse.
- Outer Space Treaty Art. VI: State responsibility, private authorisation and continuing supervision; Liability Convention: launching-State *absolute* surface/aircraft and *fault-based* other-space-object damage; Registration Convention: registry and UN notification.
- SSLV–HAL agreement signed September 2025 ≠ HAL-built mission flown. NGLV approved September 2024 (~30 t LEO design class, partially reusable first stage) ≠ operational; Third Launch Pad approved January 2025 ≠ commissioned; a June 2026 government account described Kulasekarapattinam construction ≠ service. Targets are not achievements.
- **Answer spine:** define mission and orbit → select and explain launch architecture → place achievement in historical sequence → distinguish institutional roles → weigh safety, throughput, liability and market demand → conclude conditionally on *demonstrated* performance.
