| Suppose you create ... | Problem | Appropriate protection | What it does **not** give |
|---|---|---|---|
| A new drug-delivery mechanism | Others can copy the disclosed technical invention | Patent, if statutory tests pass | Ownership of every way to treat the disease |
| A distinctive label for that medicine | Buyers may confuse commercial origin | Trademark | Monopoly over the medicine's chemistry |

| Tea whose reputation depends on a producing region | Outsiders may free-ride on place reputation | Geographical indication (GI) | One firm's exclusive ownership of the place |
| A confidential manufacturing parameter | A rival could learn a valuable secret | Confidentiality / trade-secret protection | A registered right against independent discovery |

*The object of protection, rather than the owner's enthusiasm, decides the legal tool.*

**First intuition.** Imagine a Darjeeling producer develops a genuinely new tea-processing machine and prints an original illustration on its box. The machine, region-linked designation, commercial mark and picture present **four different questions**. An inventor's disclosure bargain is unlike a community's claim about geographical reputation. India therefore has several Acts, not one general monopoly in “ideas”.

1. **Patent:** the state gives a limited right to exclude others from specified acts involving the claimed **product or process** in return for disclosure; permission to manufacture or sell under all other laws is not thereby guaranteed. Its ordinary term is **20 years from filing**, subject to maintenance and the Act's limitations. An expired patent falls outside this exclusive term; a registered trademark may be renewed repeatedly.
2. **Trademark:** distinguishes one trader's goods or services from another's and protects source-identifying goodwill. A registered mark runs in renewable **10-year** periods. A GI instead identifies goods from a defined area when quality, reputation or another characteristic is attributable to that origin. A logo with a regional name does not magically acquire GI status.
3. **Copyright:** protects original *expression*, not an abstract idea, scientific fact or therapeutic principle. Registration is not a condition for copyright to arise. The commonly stated life-of-author-plus-60-years rule is for specified authorial works; terms vary by category.
4. **Design:** registered visual appearance, not technical function; the Designs Act's term is **10 years**, extendable **once by five**. **Semiconductor layout-design** has a separate 2000 Act and **10-year** term. Plant-variety protection and trade secrecy follow still different logics. Do not place every one of these under the Patent Office.

**Why grant exclusion at all?** ⚠️ A developer who expects immediate copying may underinvest; a disclosed patent can permit licensing and later public use. **Objection:** monopoly prices and overbroad claims can impede follow-on work. **Reply:** tests for patentability, a finite term, opposition, exceptions and licences limit the right; their real effectiveness depends on examination and enforcement. The example does not imply that every novel-looking object deserves a patent.

**UPSC application.** Prelims tests object–statute–term matching; a GS-III answer should first name the kind of knowledge or reputation at stake, then the instrument and its access cost. **Trap:** “IPR” does not mean “patent”; “registered” does not imply indefinitely renewable. **Mini recap:** invention ≠ brand ≠ expression ≠ geographical reputation.

**Revision notes.**

1. A patent protects a claimed technical product or process, not every useful idea around it.
2. Patent protection is time-bound; the ordinary term is 20 years from filing.
3. A trademark distinguishes commercial source and can be renewed in successive ten-year periods.
4. A GI protects qualifying place-linked goods for eligible regional producers; it is not one trader's freely assignable brand.
5. Copyright protects original expression and ordinarily arises without registration; it does not monopolise an idea or scientific fact.
6. A registered design protects visual appearance rather than technical function.
7. Semiconductor layout-design and plant-variety protection follow separate statutory routes.
8. A trade secret depends on maintained confidentiality and does not prevent lawful independent discovery.
9. Several rights may coexist in one product, but each has a different protected object, holder and limitation.
10. Next ask whether the technical proposal actually passes the patentability test.

### Concept check

**Question:** A company coins a tea brand, draws its box and invents a new mechanical leaf sorter. Would registering its brand protect all three assets?

**Model answer:** No. The brand can identify the business through trademark law, the original drawing may attract copyright or design protection, and only the sorter's technical invention could seek a patent if it meets patentability conditions.

**Misconception to avoid:** “A strong brand automatically patents the technology”; each right attaches to a different protected subject.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks; answer in 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish patents, trademarks and geographical indications using an Indian producer's product as an example.

**Model answer (within 150 words):** A right follows what is being protected, not merely the label “IP”. A patented tea-processing machine protects a disclosed technical invention for a limited term if it meets statutory conditions; it does not reserve the word “tea”. A firm's distinctive tea label identifies commercial source under trademark law and may be renewed in successive ten-year periods. Darjeeling Tea illustrates a GI: reputation is linked to origin and protection is available to eligible regional producers, not to a single company as owner of a transferable brand. These can coexist on one packet, but their tests and beneficiaries differ. GI registration needs authentic production and market enforcement; a patent does not certify regional origin. A credible IP policy protects each value without allowing one claim to swallow the others.

**Scoring guide (10):** correct protected objects and legal contrast 3; machine/brand/Darjeeling example applied correctly 3; term/holder distinction 2; limits and qualified conclusion 2.

```text
Technical proposal
    ↓ Is it a new product OR process? [s.2(1)(j)]
    ↓ Novel compared with prior public knowledge?
    ↓ Inventive step: non-obvious technical advance OR economic significance?
    ↓ Capable of being made or used in an industry?
    ↓ Not excluded by ss.3 or 4?
    ↓ Claims define scope; specification enables understanding
    ↓ Possible Indian patent: limited exclusion, NOT permission to market
```

*Failure at any required gate blocks the claimed patent, even if a company spent heavily on research.*

**In ordinary language:** an engineering team that merely describes a known filter cannot demand exclusivity. If it engineers a non-obvious membrane arrangement that can be manufactured, the claim may be eligible. The word **“invention”** in s.2(1)(j) covers a *new product or process involving inventive step and capable of industrial application*. Novelty asks “was this already disclosed?”; inventive step asks “would the advance have been obvious to a person skilled in that technical field?” These are independent questions: a trivial difference can be new yet obvious.

Section **2(1)(ja)** specifies technical advance over existing knowledge **or** economic significance **or both**, **and** non-obviousness. “Economic significance” alone does not erase non-obviousness. Section **2(1)(ac)** calls an invention industrially applicable when it can be made or used in industry. Sections **3 and 4** still filter subject matter: a technically clever claim may concern a legally excluded discovery, algorithm *per se* or atomic-energy-related invention. The Office's basics page expressly lists all three positive tests and these exclusions.

**Product/process contrast:** a product claim may cover a qualifying substance itself; a process claim covers the specified method. Before product-patent restoration for medicines and chemicals in **2005**, Indian pharmaceutical innovators and generic manufacturers worked under a process-only regime for those fields; product claims then became available subject to India's statutory filters. It is inaccurate to say all product patents were unavailable in India before 2005 or that all generics became unlawful after it. A generic may use an unpatented product, a lawful licence, an exception or expired claims.

**What the grant excludes under s.48.** Subject to the Act and s.47 conditions, a **product patent** lets the patentee prevent an unconsenting third party from **making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing for those purposes** the patented product in India. A **process patent** lets the patentee prevent unconsented **use of the process** and the **use, offer for sale, sale or import for those purposes of the product obtained directly by that process** in India. These are negative rights to prevent specified acts, not an affirmative licence to market, practise medicine or bypass safety, environmental or competition law. The word “importing” also does not erase statutory exceptions such as qualifying s.107A parallel import.

**Scope is written, not imagined.** The patent's **claims** set the legal boundary; the specification teaches the invention sufficiently for others to understand the disclosure. A claim to one membrane geometry cannot automatically fence off every water-purification method. An inventor or assignee (and qualifying legal representative) can apply. Application, grant, technical validity and actual marketability are separate events. **Objection:** disclosure lets rivals design around the claims. **Reply:** that is partly the bargain: genuine improvements and diffusion alongside a defined period of protection. The example cannot itself establish patentability without an actual prior-art search and examination.

**UPSC application.** Compare novelty and non-obviousness in a reasoned two-step answer, not as synonyms. **Trap:** a granted right does not override drug safety or other regulatory approvals. **Mini recap:** positive tests + negative exclusions + bounded claims.

**Revision notes.** s.2(1)(j) new product/process; s.2(1)(ja) technical advance/economic significance **and** non-obviousness; s.2(1)(ac) made/used in industry. Prior art matters; claims define exclusion; disclosure is the bargain. Twenty years is measured from filing, not grant. Product versus process is a distinction of claim subject, not a universal difference between old and new patents. Next: how does the Office test what the applicant asserts?

### Concept check

**Question:** A slight alteration to a known device has never been publicly described, but a skilled technician would make it routinely. Does novelty alone ensure a patent?

**Model answer:** No. Even if new, it must also involve inventive step, be industrially applicable and escape statutory exclusions; routine obvious adjustment fails the inventive-step test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating “new” and “inventive” as interchangeable tests.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks; answer in 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain why the Indian patentability test is more than a test of novelty.

**Model answer (within 150 words):** Novelty asks whether a claimed product or process was already disclosed; it does not establish an inventive contribution. Under the Patents Act, an invention must be new, have an inventive step and be capable of industrial application. Section 2(1)(ja) couples technical advance or economic significance with non-obviousness to the skilled person. A redesigned water filter that differs from existing models only in a routine fitting might be new but obvious. Even a technically strong claim must clear statutory exclusions under sections 3 and 4; patent claims delimit what is actually protected. These filters protect the disclosure bargain against trivial exclusivity, while valid claims encourage risky research. Whether the redesigned filter passes requires evidence of prior art and examination, not the applicant's assertion alone.

**Scoring guide (10):** correct three cumulative criteria 3; exact inventive-step reasoning 3; grounded filter example 2; statutory exclusions and evidentiary limit 2.

```text
Inventor/assignee → specification + claims filed
                   → publication (normally after 18 months; early route possible)
                   → examination ON REQUEST → objections / response
                   ├─ pre-grant representation: any person [s.25(1)]
                   ↓ compliant application → grant / publication
                   ├─ post-grant opposition: person interested,
                   │  within one year of publication of grant [s.25(2)]
                   └─ renewal-fee and statutory working/reporting duties
```

*Publication is not grant; opposition is not examination; the term is still normally counted from filing.*

An inventor might file a provisional specification to secure a filing date and later provide a complete specification within the applicable statutory period. The patent examiner searches earlier knowledge, considers the specification and claims, issues objections where appropriate, and the applicant responds. A **request for examination** is essential: filing alone does not force automatic grant. Publication opens knowledge to society before a final grant. The Patent Office grants only if legal requirements are met; an eventual validity challenge remains possible.

The two opposition windows give distinct standing. Before grant, **any person** may make a s.25(1) representation on statutory grounds such as anticipation and insufficient description; after grant, a **person interested** may oppose under s.25(2) within the prescribed year. A subsequent revocation route is separate. ⚠️ Quality advocates value challenges as protection against bad monopolies; applicants object that repeated challenges can add cost and delay. Strong examination, reasoned orders and manageable timelines are the response; deleting scrutiny is not synonymous with improving quality.

**Compliance does not end at grant.** The patent's statutory term is not perpetually renewable like a mark: maintenance fees matter. The working-of-patent reporting obligation is associated with **Form 27** under the applicable rules; older descriptions of “annual statements” must **not** be treated as a timeless filing frequency. The **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024** and a second 2024 amendment are listed by IP India, but the accessible official landing page does not establish their full operative effects or transitional application. Before quoting a present deadline or periodicity in an exam, inspect the controlling Gazette and rules; do not convert a draft, announcement or explanatory website into the Act.

**Institutional trap and PYQ.** The Intellectual Property Appellate Board **existed when the 2019 Prelims question was set**; it was later abolished under the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021. The question's historical tense and present institutional position must not be conflated. The Patent Office and CGPDTM administer patents; the post-IPAB dispute and appeal routes must be checked against the *current* Act and court jurisdiction for the precise kind of order. The CGPDTM does **not** administer every Indian IP right. Section 25 opposition is not an IPAB appeal.

**Mini recap / revision notes.**

1. Filing starts a legal process; it is not a grant.
2. Publication normally precedes grant and opens the application to public scrutiny.
3. Examination occurs on request and tests the claims, disclosure, positive criteria and statutory exclusions.
4. An examiner's objection may lead to clarification, amendment, refusal or compliant grant.
5. Under s.25(1), **any person** may make a pre-grant representation on statutory grounds.
6. Under s.25(2), a **person interested** may bring post-grant opposition within one year of publication of grant.
7. Opposition, examination and later revocation are distinct controls.
8. Patent-quality scrutiny can prevent weak monopolies, but unmanaged delay can also burden genuine applicants.
9. Maintenance and working-report obligations continue after grant; present forms and periods must come from operative rules.
10. Patent term ordinarily runs from filing, while the correct appellate-forum answer depends on the date asked.
11. Next: even flawless procedure cannot rescue subject matter excluded from invention.

### Concept check

**Question:** A patent application is published and no one has opposed it. Can its owner say the published application is necessarily a granted patent?

**Model answer:** No. Publication precedes grant; the request for examination, statutory patentability inquiry and the Office's decision still matter. Silence by opponents is not a grant.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating the opportunity to oppose with completion of examination.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks; answer in 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine how examination and the two opposition stages affect the quality of India's patents.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** A patent filing proposes an exclusive claim; it does not prove that the claim is deserved. After publication, examination on request assesses novelty, inventive step, industrial application, exclusions and disclosure. An examiner's objections allow the applicant to clarify or narrow claims. Opposition adds information the examiner may have missed: under s.25(1), any person can represent before grant; under s.25(2), a person interested can oppose within one year of publication of grant. In pharmaceutical cases, prior art and disclosure can be particularly important to access. This layered design can deter weak exclusivity and improve trust in valid patents. Its cost is procedural burden: poorly managed oppositions can delay both deserving inventors and follow-on producers. The appropriate reply is well-resourced examination, timely reasoned decisions and scrutiny that does not treat either applicants or opponents as automatically correct. Publication, grant and final legal validity should be kept separate. Working obligations and maintenance also make post-grant governance relevant; their exact forms and frequency must be taken from operative rules, not from an outdated procedural summary.

**Scoring guide (15):** process and examination 4; both opposition stages with correct standing/time 4; patent-quality/public-interest mechanism 3; delay criticism and practicable reply 3; careful rule-status qualification 1.

## Lesson 4 — The boundary of invention: Section 3(d) and other exclusions

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Claimed thing | Relevant rule | Decisive question |
|---|---|---|
| Natural fact or mere discovery | s.3(c) | Is there an invention beyond discovering what already exists? |
| New property or new use of a known substance | first limb, s.3(d) | Is the claim just a newly noticed property/use? |
| New **form** of a known substance | second limb, s.3(d) | Is a significant difference shown in properties **with regard to efficacy**? |
| A method of treating a patient | s.3(i) | Is the claimed process for medicinal, surgical, preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic treatment rather than a qualifying product? |
| Mere admixture, arrangement or traditional knowledge | s.3(e), s.3(f), s.3(p) as applicable | Does it claim more than aggregation/rearrangement/known traditional properties? |
| Mathematical/business method, computer programme **per se** or algorithm | s.3(k) | Does the claim, read as a whole, make a technical contribution rather than merely claim excluded subject matter? |

*A positive s.2 invention test never cancels a relevant s.3 exclusion.*

Suppose an old molecule is known to treat a disease. A researcher notices another use for it. That is a **new-use** claim, not automatically a new invention under the first limb of **s.3(d)**. That limb also addresses the mere use of a known process, machine or apparatus, subject to the Act's qualification where that known process results in a new product or employs at least one new reactant. Suppose instead the molecule has a newly produced crystalline form: this is the *new-form* limb. Its applicant must demonstrate significantly different properties *with regard to efficacy*. The statute's Explanation treats listed derivatives such as salts and polymorphs as the same substance unless they differ significantly in efficacy. These are **not interchangeable tests**: enhanced efficacy is the new-form route, not a blanket exception for mere new use.

In ***Novartis AG v. Union of India* (2013)** the Supreme Court read efficacy for a medicine as **therapeutic efficacy**, and did not accept improved physicochemical qualities alone as sufficient evidence of that improvement in the disputed claim. It did **not** prohibit all incremental advances or all pharmaceutical patents. **Evergreening** names the concern that minor modifications might extend effective exclusivity without commensurate therapeutic gain; it is a policy criticism, not a statutory synonym for any improvement.

**Method versus medicine.** Section **3(i)** excludes a *process* for medicinal, surgical, curative, prophylactic (preventive), diagnostic, therapeutic or other treatment of human beings; it also covers similar treatment of animals to free them of disease or increase their economic value or that of their products. A claimed method of administering a medicine as treatment is therefore different from claiming a newly invented drug product or a process for manufacturing it. The latter are **not categorically barred by s.3(i)**: they still need the ordinary tests and must clear other exclusions, notably s.3(d) when a known substance is involved. A diagnostic **product**, such as a novel test kit, must likewise be analysed by its actual claims rather than condemned simply because it helps diagnosis. The distinction protects clinical practice without imposing a blanket ban on pharmaceutical or diagnostic inventions; an applicant cannot evade the method exclusion by merely relabelling a treatment process as a product.

Other boundaries answer different questions. **s.3(b)** rejects inventions whose intended use offends public order/morality or seriously harms life, health or environment; **s.3(j)** separately addresses plants/animals and essentially biological processes (next lesson). **s.3(p)** rejects an invention that is in effect traditional knowledge or an aggregation/duplication of known traditional properties. **s.4** excludes inventions related to atomic energy as specified by law.

**Section 3(k) after the CRI Guidelines 2025.** The statutory exclusion remains for “a mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms.” The **Guidelines for Examination of Computer Related Inventions (CRIs), 2025** guide examination; they do not amend the Act or make a technology label patentable. The examiner should read the **substance of the claim as a whole**, identify the actual problem and contribution, and ask whether the claimed combination produces a **technical effect** or makes a **technical contribution** beyond the excluded subject matter. Merely adding “processor”, “network” or generic hardware to an abstract rule does not settle the issue. Conversely, software implementation is not rejected only because software is involved when the claim, properly construed, solves a technical problem through technical means—for example, a specifically disclosed improvement in computer operation, security, resource use, device control or another verifiable technical result.

```text
CRI claim under s.3(k)
        ↓ read the claim as a whole
What is the real problem and contribution?
        ├─ abstract calculation / business rule / algorithm as such
        │       or programme per se → excluded
        └─ specifically disclosed technical means solving a technical problem
                ↓ verify technical effect / technical contribution
                ↓ then still test novelty, inventive step, industrial application
                  and sufficiency of disclosure
```

**Emerging-technology treatment.** Artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, blockchain, cloud systems and quantum-computing claims receive the same disciplined inquiry; none gets an automatic exception from s.3(k). The 2025 guidance places particular weight on an enabling disclosure: the specification should explain the technical problem, architecture or processing steps, relevant inputs and outputs, and how the claimed result is achieved. For an AI/ML claim, merely naming a model or asserting improved accuracy is weaker than disclosing the implementation and the technical relationship that produces the asserted result. A claim may clear s.3(k) yet fail novelty, inventive step or sufficiency; “technical contribution” is not a substitute for every other statutory test. Equally, examination guidance remains subordinate to the Act and binding judicial interpretation.

**Strong objection:** a higher efficacy hurdle might discourage beneficial improvements, including manufacturing and stability changes. **Reply:** other genuine improvements may still be protectable on their properly framed merits, and the public-health filter targets weak extension claims; the hard case requires evidence of therapeutic benefit and careful claim construction rather than automatic rejection. The molecule example illustrates the legal fork, not a finding of patentability for a real drug.

**UPSC application.** Use a two-column “known use / new form” answer. Avoid a numerical claim of Indian drug patents unless a dated report supports it. **Mini recap:** first classify the claim, then apply the correct limb, then ask about proof of efficacy.

**Revision notes.** s.3(d): discovery of new property/use ≠ patentable invention; new form needs significantly different efficacy; *Novartis* = therapeutic efficacy for drug claims. s.3(i): human treatment processes and corresponding animal-treatment processes excluded, not all medicines or diagnostic products. s.3(c): discovery. s.3(e)/(f): mere aggregation/arrangement. s.3(k): computer programme per se. s.3(p): traditional knowledge. s.4: atomic energy. Each exclusion has its own subject. Next: does a discovery involving living material meet a *different* statutory boundary?

### Concept check

**Question:** A newly isolated polymorph of a known medicine is easier to store; the applicant has not shown greater therapeutic efficacy. Is its improved storage alone sufficient under the new-form limb of s.3(d)?

**Model answer:** No. A new form of a known medicine must meet the statute's significant-difference-in-efficacy requirement, understood as therapeutic efficacy in *Novartis*; easier storage alone does not establish that test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Applying the new-form efficacy exception to a mere new use, or equating a useful physical property with proven therapeutic efficacy.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks; answer in 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Why is it inaccurate to say that Section 3(d) bans pharmaceutical innovation in India?

**Model answer (within 150 words):** Section 3(d) distinguishes a mere discovery of a new property or use of a known substance from a new form of that substance. The former is not an invention under that limb; a new form may escape the exclusion if it differs significantly in properties relating to efficacy. For medicines, *Novartis* (2013) interpreted efficacy as therapeutic efficacy. A genuinely new drug product or process may still satisfy novelty, inventive step and industrial applicability and escape the exclusions. The provision targets weak extension claims, not pharmaceutical research itself. Industry's concern is that proving improved therapeutic effects may burden useful incremental work; the answer is careful examination of evidence and claims, not pretending that every minor alteration is patentable or that every improvement is prohibited. Patentability and affordability remain distinct issues.

**Scoring guide (10):** two-limb legal distinction 3; accurate *Novartis* application 2; new-invention pathway 2; serious objection/reply and qualification 3.

*“Life material” is not a single patent category, nor is every laboratory intervention an invented life form.*

**First decide what is claimed.** Section **3(j)** excludes plants and animals in whole or in any part **other than micro-organisms**, including seeds, varieties and species, and essentially biological processes for their production or propagation. The micro-organism exception removes one categorical bar; it does **not** grant a patent automatically. Other novelty, inventive-step, industrial-use and exclusion tests still apply. The **Budapest Treaty** provides for internationally recognised deposit of micro-organisms for patent-procedure purposes; depositing a sample is not a patent grant.

India's **Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights (PPV&FR) Act, 2001** is an alternative, *sui generis* (specially designed) system. It provides breeder protection while recognising farmers, including rights to save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share and sell farm produce including seed of protected varieties, subject to the statutory branded-seed restriction. The PPV&FR Authority is not CGPDTM. ⚠️ A breeder's right is not a conventional plant patent; the relationship between innovation incentives, seed access and farmers' autonomy must be argued, not assumed.

**Traditional knowledge (TK)** can be known within an Indian community but hard for a foreign patent examiner to discover. The **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)**, associated with **CSIR** and the Ministry of Ayush, translates and indexes documented medicinal knowledge so authorised patent offices can search it as **prior art**. It is **defensive**: showing the old turmeric use or a recorded formulation undermines an inappropriate novelty claim. TKDL is not itself a patent awarded to every traditional healer; nor does mere digitisation automatically ensure community consent, compensation or control. Indian examples involving **turmeric, neem and basmati** illustrate distinct biopiracy and misappropriation concerns; do not describe all three disputes as one identically decided patent case. Access to biological resources and **benefit sharing** under biodiversity law and applicable international arrangements such as the Nagoya Protocol are another, partly complementary question.

**Debate.** Preventing patents on old knowledge discourages appropriation; publishing sensitive knowledge may also risk disclosure or commercial exploitation. Restricted examiner access, appropriate community participation and fair benefit sharing respond to that concern; they do not eliminate it. Biotechnology patents can reward genuine engineering within the permitted legal boundary, without converting organisms, plant varieties or common inheritance into blanket private property.

**2019 Mains GS-III Q15:** “How is the Government of India protecting traditional knowledge of medicine from patenting by pharmaceutical companies?” **Discuss; 15 marks; 250 words.** **Unsolved route:** distinguish discovery/prior art and s.3(p) from novel invention; explain TKDL's defensive search function, supporting patent examination and biodiversity/benefit-sharing measures; acknowledge limits. **2024 Mains GS-III Q5** additionally asks about life materials and commercialisation; its second half belongs in Lesson 9. **2019 Prelims GS-I Q57** tests s.3(j) against IPAB's *historical* status; use Lesson 3's answer-neutral route. No official objective key is held locally.

**Revision notes / mini recap.**

1. Section 3(j) excludes plants and animals in whole or part, including seeds, varieties and species.
2. It also excludes essentially biological processes for producing or propagating them.
3. Micro-organisms are outside that categorical s.3(j) exclusion, but receive no automatic patent.
4. A micro-organism-related claim must still meet novelty, inventive step, industrial application and every other exclusion.
5. Budapest Treaty deposit supports patent procedure; it is not a grant.
6. Plant varieties use the PPV&FR Act's *sui generis* route rather than ordinary patents.
7. Farmers' qualified rights include saving, using, sowing, resowing, exchanging, sharing and selling farm produce including seed, subject to the branded-seed restriction.
8. TKDL turns documented traditional medicinal knowledge into searchable prior art for authorised examiners.
9. Section 3(p) and prior-art rules can defeat claims that merely appropriate known traditional properties.
10. Defensive rejection of a wrongful patent does not itself create consent, compensation or benefit sharing.
11. Turmeric, neem and basmati illustrate related but legally distinct misappropriation concerns.
12. Next: a valid patent may still face a public-health access problem.

**Question:** If an examiner finds a medicinal property described in TKDL, has TKDL awarded a new patent to the community?

**Model answer:** No. It supplies accessible evidence of existing knowledge to challenge an unjustified novelty claim; benefit sharing and community entitlements need separate legal and institutional treatment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Confusing defensive prevention of a wrongful grant with a positive exclusive patent.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks; answer in 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Discuss India's differentiated treatment of life-related inventions, plant varieties and traditional medicinal knowledge.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** Life material is not one legal class. Under s.3(j) of the Patents Act, plants and animals in whole or part, including seeds and varieties, and essentially biological production processes are excluded; micro-organisms are not categorically excluded, but their claimed inventions must pass the ordinary tests. | Access route | When / what | Why it is not the same as revocation |
|---|---|---|
| Voluntary licence | Patentee agrees contractual manufacture/use | Consent and negotiated terms |
| Compulsory licence, s.84 | Application **three years after grant** on any statutory ground: unmet reasonable public requirements, lack of reasonably affordable price **or** non-working in India | Patent remains; use authorised subject to conditions and remuneration |

| Regulatory-use exemption, s.107A(a) | Acts reasonably related to producing/submitting regulatory information | Preparatory use does not authorise ordinary commercial sale during a patent |
| Parallel import, s.107A(b) | Import from a person duly authorised under the relevant law | Statutory qualification, not blanket permission for counterfeit trade |

*A right can remain valid while the law limits the ways its owner can prevent access.*

Suppose a patented oncology medicine is unaffordable to many patients. One route is a negotiated licence to a generic producer. If that does not solve access, **s.84** permits an eligible applicant after **three years from grant** to seek a compulsory licence on **any one** of the three grounds in the table. The Controller considers statutory factors and licence conditions; this is not a patent transfer, an instant price-control order or general free copying. **Natco's 2012 licence concerning Bayer's sorafenib (Nexavar)** illustrates the ordinary s.84 route; do not present it as a universal result for every drug or assert an updated “only ever” count without a current official review.

**Section 92** addresses government notification for specified emergency, urgency or public non-commercial circumstances; do not incorrectly apply s.84's three-year waiting rule to every s.92 situation. **Section 107A** is yet another legal device: development and submission of regulatory information can allow preparations before expiry (often called the **Bolar** provision). The import limb concerns a duly authorised seller. None is equivalent to removing the patent or proving it invalid.

**Why the trade-off?** ⚠️ Patents can fund long-risk medicine development; monopoly pricing can exclude patients. A credible possibility of licence can change bargaining and support availability, but careless or unpredictable use can chill investment. The legal reply is **defined grounds, procedure, remuneration and proportionality**, alongside procurement, competition, regulatory safety and production capacity. Even a valid licence does not build a manufacturing plant overnight.

**International linkage.** The **2001 Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health** affirms WTO members' right to protect public health and use TRIPS flexibilities, including compulsory licensing and determining its grounds. It does not itself issue an Indian licence. **Exam trap:** evergreening filter s.3(d) asks *whether a claim deserves a patent*; s.84 asks *whether access to an existing valid patent warrants a licence*. They are not two names for the same device.

**Mini recap / revision notes.**

1. A voluntary licence rests on patentee consent and negotiated terms.
2. Section 84 becomes available after three years from grant, not filing.
3. Its three grounds are alternatives: unmet reasonable public requirements, lack of reasonably affordable price, or non-working in India.
4. A compulsory licence authorises conditioned use; it does not revoke or transfer the patent.
5. Natco–Bayer sorafenib (2012) is a dated illustration, not an automatic outcome for every medicine.
6. Section 92 is a distinct government-notified route for national emergency, extreme urgency or public non-commercial use.
7. Section 107A(a) protects acts reasonably related to developing and submitting regulatory information.
8. The Bolar route does not authorise unrestricted commercial marketing during the patent term.
9. Section 107A(b) permits qualified import from a duly authorised person; it is not permission for counterfeit trade.
10. The Doha Declaration recognises public-health flexibilities but does not itself issue an Indian licence.
11. Patent access tools cannot replace regulatory approval, manufacturing capacity, procurement or distribution.
12. Next: some livelihoods need collective or expressive protection, not access exceptions to a drug patent.

### Concept check

**Question:** A generic firm needs to prepare drug-regulatory submissions before patent expiry. Must it first prove all three s.84 grounds and wait three years after grant?

**Model answer:** No. Reasonably related regulatory uses have a distinct s.107A(a) exemption. That does not authorise unrestricted commercial sale; s.84 licensing is a separate route, with any one of its stated grounds after the prescribed period.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every lawful use without the patentee's ordinary consent as the same kind of compulsory licence.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks; answer in 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse whether Indian patent safeguards reconcile medicine innovation with public health.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** The patent bargain encourages disclosed pharmaceutical innovation, but an exclusive claim can restrict access. India addresses different failures with different tools. Section 3(d), read through *Novartis* (2013), tests certain new forms of known medicines for significantly different therapeutic efficacy; it does not ban all incremental work. Opposition can expose prior art or defective disclosure. For a valid patent, s.84 permits a compulsory-licence application three years after grant if public requirements are unmet, prices are not reasonably affordable or the invention is not worked in India. The Natco–Bayer sorafenib licence (2012) is a dated example, not an automatic template. Section 92 provides a distinct notified public-need route, while s.107A permits qualifying regulatory preparation and parallel import. Doha recognises members' public-health flexibilities under TRIPS. Critics warn that unpredictable licensing may reduce incentives; supporters stress timely affordable access. Reasoned orders, statutory conditions, remuneration and capable generic manufacture can protect both goals. Patent law alone cannot guarantee clinical approval, procurement or distribution; public-health infrastructure remains essential.

*One textile can embody several rights without turning their holders and tests into one monopoly.*

**GI mechanism.** The Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, **1999** concerns goods whose quality, reputation or other characteristics are essentially attributable to a defined geographical origin. An association or organisation representing producers can seek registration with the GI Registry (Chennai), supplying evidence of origin and link; eligible individual producers can seek **authorised-user** registration. Darjeeling Tea offers a familiar geographical example. GI registration lasts **10 years**, renewable; it is not a free-standing patent over tea-processing technology, and an outsider cannot obtain the place-linked entitlement merely by purchasing one producer's goodwill. A GI cannot be assigned, transmitted, licensed, pledged or mortgaged as an ordinary private mark can.

The **trademark** tells consumers whose goods or services they encounter; distinguishability and risk of confusion are central. A registered trademark lasts **10 years from application**, renewable in further ten-year periods; unregistered marks can sometimes be defended through *passing off*, which is not identical to infringement of a registered mark. An original label illustration may enjoy **copyright without registration**, but copyright does not protect an idea, a name as such or an independently invented manufacturing method. Industrial design protects registered ornamental appearance for **10 plus five years**, not the functional mechanism. Trade secrecy depends on effective confidentiality; India does not have a single comprehensive stand-alone trade-secret registration statute. A competitor who independently discovers a secret raises a different question from theft or breach of confidence.

**Rural-development claim tested.** A GI can help prevent false-origin marketing and coordinate producers, **but** a certificate alone does not establish quality control, producer bargaining power, export logistics or higher farmer/weaver incomes. ⚠️ The strongest objection is that better-resourced intermediaries can capture the value; the reply is producer organisations, authenticated supply chains, quality assurance, authorisation and marketing, with distributional scrutiny. Contrast a single firm's renewable brand: its proprietor can build goodwill, yet its success alone need not improve incomes across a region.

*The first diagram coordinates applications; the second concerns obligations. Neither manufactures a universal patent.*

**Why use the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)?** Filing separately in several jurisdictions is expensive and complex. The PCT lets an eligible applicant use a coordinated **international application** and receive an international search and written opinion before choosing where to continue. Publication ordinarily follows the relevant **18-month** point; applicants then enter national/regional phases within applicable periods and pay local costs. The WIPO FAQ expressly makes the national-phase decision central. A favourable search opinion is not a compulsory grant. India is a contracting state; an Indian grant remains territorial.

**TRIPS** (Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights) is a **WTO** agreement establishing minimum IP standards, enforcement obligations and flexibilities. **Article 27.3(b)** allows certain exclusions for plants and animals and requires protection of plant varieties by patents, an effective *sui generis* system or both. India's PPV&FR regime illustrates the specially designed alternative. The **Doha Declaration (2001)** reaffirms the members' public-health interpretive space, including compulsory-licence flexibility. ⚠️ A country cannot claim “TRIPS forces every plant variety to be patented”; equally, “flexibility” does not mean that an existing valid patent disappears automatically.

Related but distinct: the **Paris Convention** covers industrial-property principles including national treatment and priority; the **Berne Convention** concerns copyright; the **Madrid Protocol** is an international trademark-filing/registration route, not a world trademark free from national effects; the **Budapest Treaty** facilitates micro-organism deposits for patent procedure. The **GI Act 1999** connects to WTO/TRIPS obligations, whereas GI registration and enforcement in India are administered domestically. Do not confuse WIPO, a UN specialised agency, with the WTO's trade agreement and dispute mechanisms.

**Trade-off.** International coordination lowers duplicated paperwork and can help a small Indian innovator consider foreign markets. National-phase fees, translations, agents and local enforcement still cost money. ⚠️ Smaller firms may benefit more from selective foreign protection and strategic licensing than from filing everywhere. PCT's search helps decisions, not the affordability of every foreign grant.

| Faster digital examination | Predictability and lower waiting costs | Fast but poorly searched weak grants |
| Stronger patent enforcement | More secure legitimate licensing | Broad injunctions that ignore validity/access |
| Opposition and s.3(d) | Better quality and fewer weak extensions | Delay or misapplication against genuine research |
| GI promotion and IP literacy | More producers can recognise and monetise rights | Intermediary capture, no quality assurance |
| Secrecy, patents or open dissemination | Choose appropriate disclosure strategy | Closed know-how may impede diffusion; premature publication may defeat claims |

*Speed, quality, diffusion and affordability are related but cannot be collapsed into a single patent-count target.*

**A realistic decision problem.** A small biotech enterprise has a diagnostic invention, a confidential manufacturing parameter and a regional supply chain. A patent can disclose and claim the diagnostic if it passes the law; retaining a process as a **trade secret** avoids disclosure but is fragile if rivals discover it independently. A **trademark** can distinguish the firm's device, while a GI would be inapt unless a qualifying good genuinely owes a characteristic or reputation to geographical origin. ⚠️ This portfolio logic is strategic analysis, not a claim that one real firm possesses all those rights.

**Institutional capacity changes legal outcomes.** Digitising searches, applications and administration can lower transaction costs, but examiners need prior-art access, technical competence and reasoned decisions. MSMEs and traditional producer groups face different costs: one may struggle to fund foreign national-phase entry; another may struggle with GI verification and authorised-user uptake. A specialised institution does not guarantee accessibility. The National IPR Policy 2016 treats commercialisation and access to affordable healthcare as compatible objectives to pursue, not as measured achievements already attained.

**Fair disagreement.** More assertive protection may encourage high-risk research and foreign licensing. Counterargument: overly broad patents, costly litigation and poorly shared GI gains can concentrate rents. The reply is differentiated: high-quality claims and predictable remedies for genuine invention; s.3(d), opposition and s.84 where statutory conditions apply; producer-led GI governance; technology transfer and competition-sensitive licensing. **Residual problem:** no single legal safeguard can independently finance a clinical trial, prevent every act of TK exploitation, or make a rural brand competitive. Evidence of actual outcomes, rather than sweeping pro-/anti-IP slogans, must determine the mix.

### From exclusive right to infringement remedy: ss.48, 104A, 107 and 108

```text
s.48 specifies prohibited unconsented acts
        ↓ patentee alleges an act falls within a valid claim
Process patent + identical product?
        ↓ s.104A may shift proof of the process in defined cases
Defendant may invoke s.107 defences
        ↓ revocation grounds under s.64 and applicable s.47 conditions
Infringement proved
        ↓ s.108: injunction + plaintiff chooses damages OR account of profits
        └─ court may seize, forfeit or destroy infringing goods
           and predominantly used materials/implements, without compensation
```

**Section 48 fixes the conduct boundary.** For a product patent, the prohibited unconsented acts are making, using, offering for sale, selling and importing for those purposes in India. For a process patent, they are using the process and using, offering for sale, selling or importing for those purposes the product obtained **directly** by that process. Infringement therefore requires claim construction and proof of a specified act; resemblance, market competition or independent invention does not by itself answer the statutory question.

**Section 104A addresses the information problem in process patents.** Where a patent covers a process for obtaining a product and the defendant's product is identical to the product directly obtained by the patented process, the court may direct the defendant to prove that its process is different if the patented process obtains a **new product**, or if there is a **substantial likelihood** that the identical product was made by the patented process and the patentee could not determine the actual process despite reasonable efforts. The patentee must first prove product identity. The court must also protect manufacturing or commercial secrets against unreasonable disclosure. This is a conditional burden shift, not an automatic presumption in every process-patent suit.

**Section 107 preserves validity and statutory defences.** Every ground on which the patent may be revoked under **s.64** is available as a defence to infringement. For the specified machines, articles, processes, medicines and drugs, compliance with an applicable **s.47** condition is also a defence. A defendant may therefore contest validity or rely on a statutory condition; “granted” does not mean immune from challenge. These defences are distinct from the s.107A regulatory-use and parallel-import exceptions already taught in Lesson 6.

**Section 108 controls relief.** The court may grant an injunction on terms it considers fit and, at the **plaintiff's option**, either **damages or an account of profits**—not both as cumulative monetary recovery for the same infringement. It may also order infringing goods, and materials or implements predominantly used to create them, to be **seized, forfeited or destroyed without compensation**. A remedy remains judicial and fact-sensitive: validity, infringement, defences and the appropriate terms must be decided rather than inferred from the existence of a patent certificate.

**Status discipline as of 1 October 2026.** The IP India Patents Act HTML landing page says its e-version was updated through **23 June 2017** and that **Gazettes prevail**; its rules landing page says the HTML rules were updated to **15 March 2024**. Dates on the **15 and 16 March 2024** amendment notifications do not by themselves establish their operative effects. Neither a search result about a supposed **2026 effective date**, nor a discussion draft, proves present Form 27 intervals, examination limits or an intervening legislative change. Exact current operational compliance needs the Gazette and applicable transitional rule; this edition deliberately does **not** invent a deadline or filing total. WIPO's GII **2025** India **38th** is an edition-specific index rank, not a count of patents or evidence of licensing success.

**UPSC answer use.** For a 20-marker, choose a technology and carry it through the legal and commercial chain; test affordability, farmer/producer rights and institutional capacity, then qualify outcomes. **Trap:** “abolish public-interest exceptions to encourage innovation” and “grant a licence for every unaffordable product” are both unsupported extremes. **Mini recap / revision notes:** calibrated portfolio; high-quality scrutiny; legal entitlement ≠ capacity; regional producer inclusion; enforceability ≠ affordability; rank ≠ performance on every component; operative Gazette ≠ draft; keep measurable access and innovation in the same answer.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks; answer in 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Evaluate a differentiated IP strategy for Indian biotechnology, medicine access and region-linked producer livelihoods.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** A uniform “more IP” strategy confuses inventions, inherited knowledge and origin-based reputation. Biotechnology firms need reliable examination for genuinely new, non-obvious and applicable claims, including eligible micro-organism-related inventions; plant varieties instead have the PPV&FR route with qualified farmers' rights. For medicine access, s.3(d) filters certain weak new-form claims, while s.84 can permit conditioned use of an existing patent on statutory grounds. TKDL helps examiners identify recorded medicinal prior art; it cannot substitute for community participation or biodiversity benefit sharing. Producer groups can use GIs to challenge false-origin claims, but registered status without authorised users, quality systems or bargaining power may not raise incomes. Opponents of safeguards cite investment uncertainty; opponents of unchecked exclusivity cite prices and restricted follow-on research. Competent, timely and reasoned patent examination; proportionate licences and remuneration; genuine technology-transfer support; and producer-centred branding are better than either extreme. Monitor validated products, patient availability and producers' returns with dated evidence. Digital filing and high counts alone prove none of those outcomes.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These are additional retrieval questions **after** the ten lesson-local checks, not replacements for them.

1. **Question:** Can s.3(d) and s.84 be used as synonyms? **Model answer:** No; s.3(d) decides whether certain claims to known substances count as inventions, whereas s.84 conditionally licenses use of a granted patent. **Likely error:** assuming every access issue concerns patentability.
2. **Question:** A new plant variety is valuable to a breeder. Does its economic value override s.3(j)? **Model answer:** No; examine the separate PPV&FR protection and farmers' rights rather than claiming an ordinary plant-variety patent. **Likely error:** treating commercial value as sufficient for patent eligibility.
3. **Question:** Which extra link turns a patent grant into a usable medical product? **Model answer:** Validated production, regulatory clearance as applicable, transfer/licensing, finance and distribution; a patent alone does none of these. **Likely error:** counting grants as commercialised products.
4. **Question:** Does a PCT filing let a GI producer license a “world GI”? **Model answer:** No; PCT concerns patent applications and neither creates a world patent nor protects a GI. **Likely error:** conflating rights and international regimes.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Integrative 10 marks — answer in 150 words

**Question:** Explain why filing a patent is not proof either of patentability or commercial viability.

**Model answer (within 150 words):** Filing begins a legal examination, not a certification of novelty or market demand. The claimed product or process must be new, involve a non-obvious inventive step and be industrially applicable; sections 3 and 4 can still exclude it. For example, a newly described medicine form may need evidence of significantly different therapeutic efficacy under s.3(d), rather than an applicant's assertion. Even after grant, a diagnostic prototype needs validation, an industrial licensee, relevant approval, manufacturing capability and buyers. A university can therefore hold a valid patent without a commercial product. Patent quality, technology transfer, access and adoption require different evidence. Grant and sales statistics should be dated and have different denominators; neither follows automatically from an application.

**Scoring guide (10):** filing/positive criteria 3; exclusions and appropriate example 2; concrete post-grant chain 3; evidence qualification 2.

## Integrative 15 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** Discuss how India's treatment of traditional knowledge and GIs protects different community interests.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** Communities may hold inherited medicinal knowledge or sustain a place-linked product reputation. These are different legal problems. A pharmaceutical patent on already documented traditional healing knowledge raises a prior-art and misappropriation concern; s.3(p) and the CSIR–Ayush TKDL help examiners identify existing knowledge and resist undeserved claims. Defensive documentation is not a new community patent, nor does it itself assure consent or benefits from biological-resource use. A GI, by contrast, identifies goods whose reputation, quality or characteristic is linked to a defined region. Under India's 1999 GI Act, eligible producers, including registered authorised users, can defend regional identity against false-origin use; Darjeeling Tea illustrates the kind of interest involved. Yet a GI does not reward every member without producer organisation, quality verification and fair market access. Biodiversity benefit-sharing and community participation are complementary to defensive TK protection. The objection that restrictions can hinder research has force; genuine inventions can still seek examination, while preventing appropriation of existing knowledge and producer reputation protects a different public interest. Neither tool should be mistaken for a general patent on culture.

**Scoring guide (15):** TK prior-art mechanism 4; GI rights and example 4; independent benefit/income limitations 3; criticism/reply and community-centred conclusion 4.

## Integrative 20 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** Analyse how domestic patent safeguards, treaty flexibilities and technology-transfer institutions can jointly advance Indian health innovation.

**Model answer (within 250 words):** Health innovation requires both incentives for genuine research and access to the resulting products. India's patent test protects new, non-obvious, industrially applicable inventions while s.3(d), read through *Novartis* (2013), examines efficacy claims for certain new forms of known medicines. Opposition can reveal prior art. Once a valid patent exists, s.84 permits a conditioned licence after three years on specified unmet-need, affordability or non-working grounds; s.92 supplies a separate public-need route and s.107A protects qualifying regulatory preparation. WTO TRIPS sets minimum standards, but the Doha Declaration recognises public-health flexibilities. WIPO's PCT coordinates foreign patent filings, not prices or international grants. On the production side, a university patent may need proof of concept, a technology-transfer office, a capable licensee, regulatory approval and distribution before patients benefit. Critics say licences can deter risky investment; advocates of safeguards note that monopoly without affordability can deny access. Predictable reasoned decisions, statutory remuneration where applicable, industrial scale-up and public procurement can narrow the conflict. Measure validated innovations and patient access with dated evidence, not the number of filings alone.

**Scoring guide (20):** accurate domestic mechanisms and distinctions 6; treaty roles/Doha 3; sequential transfer-to-access chain 4; serious objection/reply 4; named evidence and disciplined measurement 3.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Sort the asset before reaching for a right

- Invention → **Patents Act, 1970**; disclose and claim a product/process; term ordinarily **20 years from filing**. Patent is exclusion, not a guarantee of regulatory approval or business success.
- Commercial identifier → **Trade Marks Act, 1999**; distinctive brand, renewable ten-year terms. Original expression → **Copyright Act, 1957**; protection generally arises without registration; duration depends on category.
- Place-linked goods → **GI Act, 1999**; qualities/reputation linked to origin, representative registration and individual authorised users, renewable ten-year registration; no ordinary assignment to a trader outside the producing region. Darjeeling Tea: illustrative origin case, not proof of every producer's income.
- Ornamental appearance → **Designs Act, 2000** (10 plus five years); layout-design → separate 2000 Act (ten years); confidential know-how → secrecy and legal obligations, no ordinary universal trade-secret register.

## Decide whether a patent can exist

- s.2(1)(j): new product or process, inventive step, industrial application. s.2(1)(ja): technical advance **or** economic significance **and** non-obviousness; s.2(1)(ac): made or used in industry.
- Positive tests do not bypass ss.3/4. s.3(c) discovery; s.3(b) public order/morality/serious harm; **s.3(i) human medical/diagnostic/surgical treatment processes and corresponding animal treatments**, not a blanket ban on pharmaceutical products or manufacturing processes; s.3(e)/(f) mere admixture or rearrangement; s.3(k) computer programme **per se**; s.3(p) traditional knowledge; s.4 atomic energy.
- Under the **CRI Guidelines 2025**, read a computer-related claim as a whole: identify the real problem and contribution, require a specifically disclosed technical effect/technical contribution, and then separately test novelty, inventive step and sufficiency. AI, ML, blockchain, cloud and quantum labels create no automatic patentability.
- **s.48:** product patent—prevent unconsented making, using, offering for sale, selling and importing for those purposes; process patent—prevent use of the process and those acts concerning the product obtained directly by it.
- **s.104A:** conditional process-patent burden shift after the patentee proves identical product, with new-product or substantial-likelihood/reasonable-efforts conditions and protection for unreasonable disclosure of secrets.
- **s.107:** s.64 revocation grounds remain infringement defences; applicable s.47 conditions are also defences. **s.108:** injunction plus the plaintiff's choice of damages **or** account of profits; possible seizure, forfeiture or destruction without compensation.
- s.3(d): mere new property/use of known substance is not an invention under that limb; mere use of known process/machine is also covered, with a qualification for a new product or new reactant in the known-process case. **New form** of known substance requires significantly different efficacy. *Novartis* (2013): therapeutic efficacy in the medicine context; improved physicochemical properties alone insufficient on those facts. Do not say all new medicines or meaningful improvements are forbidden.
- s.3(j): plants and animals in whole or part (other than micro-organisms), varieties/seeds and essentially biological processes excluded. Micro-organism exception does **not** bypass other tests. Budapest deposit helps patent disclosure procedure.
- PPV&FR Act, 2001 protects varieties through a specially designed route and preserves qualified farmers' seed rights (not branded seed sold as protected variety). TKDL (CSIR–Ayush) supplies defensive search evidence; biodiversity access/benefit sharing is distinct.

## Navigate filing and challenge

- Inventor/assignee files; claims define scope, specification discloses; publication ≠ grant. Examination **on request**; pre-grant representation **any person**, s.25(1); post-grant opposition **person interested** within **one year of publication of grant**, s.25(2).
- Maintain a granted patent and check applicable working-statement rules. Form 27 frequency and procedural deadlines are **rule- and time-sensitive**: consult operative Gazette, not an undated note. The IPAB existed in **2019**; it was abolished in **2021**. Historical PYQ wording is not present-tense institutional fact.
- Product versus process claims: product patent restoration in pharmaceutical/chemical fields from **1 January 2005**, alongside Indian safeguards; no universal proposition about all product patents before or all generics after 2005.

## Keep access instruments separate

- s.84: compulsory-licence application **three years after grant** if public requirements unmet **or** price not reasonably affordable **or** not worked in India. Dated **Natco/Bayer sorafenib (2012)** example; licence ≠ revocation.
- s.92: government-notified national emergency, extreme urgency or public non-commercial use; not every route has the s.84 waiting period.
- s.107A(a): use reasonably related to regulatory submission; **(b)** qualified parallel import. Doha Declaration **2001** reaffirms public-health flexibilities under WTO TRIPS; none of these provisions automatically manufactures a generic medicine.

## Cross borders, then cross the lab-to-market gap

- **WIPO PCT** coordinates filing/search and later national or regional phase decisions; **no world patent**. **WTO TRIPS** establishes IP minimum standards with flexibilities; Art.27.3(b) permits alternative effective plant-variety protection. Paris: industrial-property priority/national treatment; Berne: copyright; Madrid: international marks; Budapest: micro-organism deposits.
- **DPIIT/CIPAM** coordinate National IPR Policy **2016**, including awareness, capacity and commercialisation. **CGPDTM**: patents, designs, trademarks, GIs; copyright and PPV&FR have different offices/authority.
- Commercialisation chain: validated claim → prototype → pilot → TTO/ownership/licence → finance → producer → regulatory authorisation where needed → distribution and access. More filings ≠ more products. GII **2025** India's **38th** is an edition-specific innovation-index rank, not a patent conversion measure; the **2024 GS-III** “second” claim is its dated premise only.
- PYQ spine: **2018 Prelims Q26** GI/international-body link (cross-owned); **2019 Prelims Q57** s.3(j)/2019 IPAB; **2019 GS-III Q15** TKDL and TK; **2024 GS-III Q5** life-material choices + grouped commercialisation barriers. Answer objective stems without guessing a missing official key.
