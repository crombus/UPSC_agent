## Lesson 1 — Joining nuclei: promise and barrier

Progress: 1 / 9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Binding energy and the Coulomb barrier

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Two light positively charged nuclei
      ├─ at ordinary distance: electrical repulsion pushes them apart
      └─ brought extremely close: short-range nuclear attraction takes over
                                    ↓
                         more tightly bound product
                                    ↓
                 mass difference becomes released energy
```

*The diagram separates the obstacle to joining from the energy released after joining.*

Imagine pushing two magnets together with like poles facing: the initial push costs effort even if a much stronger latch eventually catches. The nuclei are **not** magnets; the useful analogy is only the distinction between an entry barrier and the energy outcome. Positively charged nuclei repel electrically. Extremely close together, the attractive strong nuclear force can bind them. Heating increases encounter energies; quantum tunnelling allows some pairs to react even when their classical energy is below the barrier. Neither fact means heating alone suffices: the ions must encounter one another often enough and stay confined.

**Binding energy** is the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its constituents. Light nuclei can form a more tightly bound product; the total mass of the products is less than that of the initial particles, with released energy **E = Δmc²**. The binding-energy-per-nucleon curve rises sharply for light nuclei towards the iron region. Hence fusion of sufficiently light nuclei can release energy; this does *not* imply that joining any arbitrary nuclei releases energy. Heavy nuclei can instead release energy by splitting. Stars establish fusion as a natural process; a terrestrial device cannot simply reproduce a star’s enormous gravitational confinement.

**Step by step:** positive charges repel → sufficiently hot ions acquire high collision energies → some close approaches/tunnelling events permit nuclear binding → the mass difference emerges as energetic products. The objection “If it releases energy, why supply energy?” confuses an initial barrier with final energy yield. The reply is that both must be assessed, and a practical reactor must recover more useful energy than it consumes across the whole system. A further qualification: at laboratory pressures, merely copying the Sun’s temperature would not reproduce its long confinement and huge volume.

**India connection:** the Institute for Plasma Research (IPR) studies the conditions in which heated, charged fuel can be controlled. Its research apparatus is not a small version of a power station. **UPSC use:** begin an explanation with repulsion, binding-energy difference and confinement, not “extremely high heat creates energy from nothing.” A Prelims trap is that both fusion and fission can release nuclear binding energy through *different directions* of change.

### Revision chain — 8 points

1. The **Coulomb barrier** is the electrical repulsion between positively charged nuclei.
2. The attractive strong nuclear force becomes decisive only at extremely short range.
3. Heating raises collision energies; quantum tunnelling raises reaction probability below the classical barrier.
4. Tunnelling helps but does not remove the need for adequate temperature, density and confinement.
5. More tightly bound products have less mass than the initial particles; the difference appears as **E = Δmc²**.
6. Fusion releases energy only for suitable light-nucleus combinations, not for arbitrary nuclei.
7. Fission and fusion move in different directions on the binding-energy curve while both can release nuclear energy.
8. Stellar gravity and volume cannot be copied directly by a terrestrial reactor; engineered confinement is essential.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a fusion reaction release energy even though the fuel must first be heated?

**Model answer:** Heating helps nuclei approach through repulsion; once suitable light nuclei join into more tightly bound products, their mass difference is released as energy. The entry barrier and net reaction energy are different quantities.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Energy released” does not mean any reactor automatically has net output after heating and other plant loads.

**PYQ link (2024 Prelims GS-I Q32, cross-topic):** The exact stem and all four choices are printed answer-neutrally before Lesson 1. The question tests giant versus dwarf stars, their lifetimes and nuclear reactions; this lesson supplies only the stellar-fusion connection. Distinguish a star's gravitational confinement from a tokamak's magnetic confinement before considering the stellar comparison. No key or elimination cue is supplied.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words maximum

**Question (Explain):** Why is the Coulomb barrier compatible with energy release from light-nucleus fusion, and why does this not guarantee useful power on Earth?

**Mains model answer:** Positive nuclei repel at a distance: a reacting pair must approach closely enough for the short-range strong force to bind it. Heating raises collision energies, while quantum tunnelling allows some lower-energy encounters to succeed. If suitable light nuclei form more tightly bound products, the products have less mass than the initial particles; that difference appears as energy, expressed by E = Δmc². Thus energy spent in overcoming an entry barrier is not identical to the energy released per successful reaction. The Sun provides an example of fusion, but its gravitational confinement and size cannot be assumed for a laboratory device. At IPR, plasma research instead confronts the terrestrial requirement to hold enough fuel hot and dense for long enough. Even a positive reaction yield is not a power-station balance: heating, confinement and energy recovery also consume resources.

## Lesson 2 — Follow a deuterium–tritium reaction

Progress: 2 / 9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Fuel, products, plasma and breeding

Book context: no topic-relevant OCR textbook was available; canonical fusion material and ITER's mission page were used.

CA found: No confirmed new breeding achievement in the last six months; ITER's published mission remains to **test** breeding.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
             DEUTERIUM (²H) + TRITIUM (³H)
                           ↓ fuse
        HELIUM-4 (alpha, 3.5 MeV) + NEUTRON (14.1 MeV)
          stays charged: heats plasma     escapes magnetic field:
                                         heats blanket, strikes structures
                           ↓
              lithium blanket + neutron → tritium (to recover)
```

*An alpha helps keep the fire hot; a neutral neutron can leave it, carrying most reaction energy.*

The fuel is hydrogen with extra neutrons: **deuterium** has one neutron, **tritium** has two. In the widely studied D–T reaction, ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + **17.6 MeV**. The helium nucleus has two positive charges and is called an **alpha particle**. It carries about 3.5 MeV; the electrically neutral neutron carries about 14.1 MeV. This energy split explains two design jobs: alpha self-heating within the magnetic field, and neutron-energy capture outside the plasma. “17.6 MeV” is energy **per reaction**, not a claim of net electrical generation.

At very high temperature, electrons separate from nuclei: the charged-particle mixture is **plasma**, not an ordinary hot gas. A reaction **cross-section** measures the effective probability that an encounter at a given energy will produce the nuclear reaction. D–T is favoured for early terrestrial machines because its cross-section becomes comparatively large at lower temperatures than other candidate fusion fuels. ITER’s operating context is plasma near **150 million °C**, roughly ten times the Sun’s core temperature; that does not mean every ion has the same energy or that temperature alone guarantees sufficient reactions. Its attraction is qualified. Deuterium can be extracted from water, but tritium is radioactive, not naturally available in useful quantities for a plant and decays with an approximately 12.3-year half-life. An ample hydrogen supply does not mean an ample ready-to-burn tritium supply.

In a proposed power reactor, neutrons would heat a surrounding **blanket**; that heat might ultimately drive a turbine, while neutron capture in lithium-bearing material would produce new tritium. A simplified route is ⁶Li + n → ⁴He + ³H. This does not prove a viable closed fuel cycle: extraction, recovery, inventory, leakage and neutron losses matter. ITER intends to test **test blanket modules**, not demonstrate that it is itself a tritium-self-sufficient electricity plant. Do not confuse lithium *breeding tritium for fusion* with thorium *breeding uranium-233 for India's fission programme*. Both involve neutron capture but serve different reactions and fuel chains.

**Objection and qualification:** “Because the reaction has no carbon at its point of generation, it has no waste.” D–T fusion makes helium rather than fission products, and operation has no self-sustaining fission chain reaction. Yet fast neutrons can activate surrounding materials and tritium requires containment; upstream construction and fuel systems also have footprints. **UPSC use:** distinguish fuel availability, reaction energy, heat recovery and tritium breeding in separate sentences.

### Revision fuel-and-energy ledger — 9 points

1. D–T fusion is **²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV**.
2. The charged alpha carries about **3.5 MeV** and can contribute to plasma self-heating.
3. The neutral neutron carries about **14.1 MeV** and crosses magnetic fields into blanket and structures.
4. A fusion cross-section expresses reaction probability for encounters at a given energy.
5. D–T is preferred initially because its cross-section is comparatively large at lower temperatures than alternative fuels.
6. ITER’s relevant temperature context is about **150 million °C**; temperature alone does not satisfy a reactor criterion.
7. Plasma is ionised matter, not merely an ordinary hot gas.
8. Deuterium is available from water, whereas tritium is scarce, radioactive and decays.
9. Lithium breeding is a proposed route to tritium renewal; a test module is not a proven closed commercial fuel loop.

### Concept check

**Question:** If deuterium is available from water, why does that not settle a D–T plant’s fuel-supply problem?

**Model answer:** A D–T plant also consumes scarce radioactive tritium; it must breed, extract and recycle enough tritium from lithium using fusion neutrons, accounting for losses and decay.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the existence of lithium or deuterium as proof that tritium breeding already works at plant scale.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words maximum

**Question (Analyse):** Analyse why the deuterium–tritium reaction creates two different engineering circuits, and assess whether a lithium blanket alone resolves fuel security.

**Mains model answer:** Deuterium and tritium fuse to produce helium-4, a neutron and 17.6 MeV per reaction. The charged alpha particle carries about 3.5 MeV and can remain in magnetically confined plasma, contributing to self-heating. The neutral 14.1 MeV neutron crosses magnetic fields; a surrounding blanket must capture its heat for possible future electricity generation while protecting structures. Lithium can also capture neutrons to make tritium: for example, lithium-6 plus a neutron yields helium and tritium. This is attractive because deuterium can come from water but tritium is scarce, radioactive and decays. Yet a breeding reaction in a test module is not a closed fuel cycle. Neutrons can be lost or captured in other materials, and the bred tritium must be extracted, contained and returned to the plasma with allowances for decay and reserve inventory. ITER plans test blanket modules, not a self-sufficient electricity station. Hence the same neutron enables prospective heat recovery and fuel renewal, but usable plant-scale fuel security still requires an integrated, loss-accounted system.

**Quantified lesson rubric — 15 marks:** complete D–T reaction and 17.6 MeV yield **3**; alpha/neutron 3.5/14.1 MeV circuit distinction **3**; cross-section and ~150-million-°C context **3**; lithium-6 breeding mechanism **3**; test-module versus closed-cycle qualification **3**. **Total: 15/15.**

## Lesson 3 — Hold the fuel without touching it

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Question | Magnetic confinement | Inertial confinement |
|---|---|---|
| Where is the fuel? | Diffuse hot plasma inside magnetic geometry | Tiny dense target or pellet |
| What keeps it together? | Fields guide charged particles away from walls | Rapid compression outruns expansion for a brief moment |
| Typical route | Tokamak or stellarator | Powerful lasers or particle beams |
| Main difficulty | Confinement, instabilities and sustained control | Symmetric compression, energy coupling and repetition |
| ITER belongs here? | Yes: tokamak | No |

*These routes trade a lower-density, longer-held plasma against a denser, fleeting fusion event.*

```text
Tokamak: magnet-guided charged plasma → sustained-control problem
Pellet:   driver-compressed dense target → repetition problem
```

You cannot put such hot fuel in an ordinary saucepan: contact would cool the plasma and damage the vessel. A **magnetic field** bends charged-particle trajectories, keeping much of the plasma away from the wall. A **tokamak** has a doughnut-shaped, or **toroidal**, chamber. Toroidal field coils and a field associated with the current flowing through the plasma together produce helical paths. A central solenoid helps induce the plasma current; additional coils shape and position it. External heating can use radio-frequency waves or neutral beams. Diagnostics measure conditions; feedback controls position and instability. A **divertor** at the edge removes heat and helium ash. Fields do not perfectly block heat or neutral neutrons: wall, divertor and blanket engineering remain essential.

A **stellarator** also confines magnetically but uses elaborate twisted external coils to produce the confining configuration without relying on a large transformer-driven plasma current. This may facilitate steadier operation, at the cost of complex coils and manufacturing. A tokamak is therefore *one* magnetic design, not a synonym for every fusion machine. ITER's large chamber is intended to study long pulses; size by itself neither eliminates disruptions nor makes electricity.

An **inertial** experiment instead squeezes a tiny fuel target quickly. Its inertia briefly delays expansion, allowing reactions during a short interval; repeated, efficiently manufactured and fired targets plus conversion into electricity would be additional power-plant challenges. The distinction is not “magnetic fusion versus laser fission”: both can pursue fusion. Nor does a successful short-shot target establish a continuous-grid electricity source.

### Basic Lawson criterion — learn this before the advanced gain lesson

```text
useful fusion conditions require
enough reacting particles (density n)
        × sufficient thermal energy (temperature T)
        × slow enough energy loss (confinement time τE)
```

The **Lawson criterion** is the canonical minimum-performance idea: a fusion system must combine density, temperature and energy-confinement time strongly enough for fusion heating to compete with losses. Magnetic confinement pursues lower density for longer confinement; inertial confinement pursues very high density for a very short interval. This Basic statement comes before Lesson 7’s Advanced treatment of triple-product interpretation, alpha-dominated burning and gain boundaries.

**India example and its limit:** SST-1 at IPR tests superconducting, longer-pulse tokamak operation; ADITYA-U is another experimental tokamak. Their lessons on control and instrumentation are relevant to ITER-class science, not evidence that either exports fusion electricity. **Objection:** magnets hold charged particles, so why does ITER need walls? **Reply:** fields guide charged fuel but do not imprison neutrons; plasma edge losses, thermal loads and protection still demand engineered boundaries. **UPSC use:** describe the mechanism before naming the facility; do not equate inertial compression and tokamak operation.

### Revision confinement map — 10 points

1. A tokamak confines charged plasma in a toroidal magnetic geometry.
2. Toroidal field and plasma-current-related field combine to guide particles helically.
3. The central solenoid helps induce plasma current; shaping coils position and control plasma.
4. External radio-frequency or neutral-beam heating raises plasma energy.
5. Diagnostics and feedback are essential because confinement is dynamically unstable.
6. A divertor exhausts helium ash and concentrated edge heat.
7. A stellarator uses complex external coils and reduces reliance on a large induced plasma current.
8. Inertial confinement compresses a small target to very high density for a very short time.
9. The Basic Lawson criterion combines **density n, temperature T and confinement time τE**.
10. ITER is a magnetic-confinement tokamak; neither a long pulse nor a successful pellet shot proves grid electricity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a tokamak need both magnetic control and a divertor?

**Model answer:** Fields guide charged plasma away from the main walls; unavoidable edge heat and helium ash still have to be removed safely by the divertor. Neutral fusion neutrons also cross fields.

**Misconception to avoid:** A field is not a solid wall that catches neutrons or removes all heat.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words maximum

**Question (Compare):** Compare how a tokamak and inertial confinement create fusion conditions, and explain one distinct practical limitation of each.

**Mains model answer:** Both routes must bring repelling light nuclei together while preventing the fuel from cooling before enough reactions occur. ITER's tokamak holds a relatively diffuse, ionised fuel in a toroidal chamber: coils and plasma current produce guiding magnetic fields; external heating raises temperature and control systems counter instabilities. Its difficulty is sustaining useful confinement while handling edge heat and disruptions; magnets guide charged particles but do not stop neutral neutrons. India's superconducting SST-1 helps investigate longer-pulse operation, not electricity production. Inertial confinement instead uses intense laser or beam energy to compress a tiny target quickly. Its high density permits reactions briefly before the target expands; compression symmetry, driver efficiency and rapid repetition become critical if many shots are to yield usable power. A stellarator offers another magnetic configuration, not a variety of inertial compression. Neither a long plasma pulse nor a successful pellet shot on its own shows that heat can be recovered efficiently and repeatedly for the grid. Each technique solves a different part of the density–confinement trade-off, leaving substantial engineering work.


*Assembly, first experimental operation, D–T burning plasma and a grid plant are four distinct milestones.*

ITER is an international experimental tokamak involving the **European Union, India, China, Japan, the Republic of Korea, Russia and the United States**. Its aims include a mainly self-heated D–T plasma, integrated testing of heating, control, diagnostics and maintenance, tritium-breeding tests, and a safety case. It will not convert its fusion heat into electricity. The seven partners contribute much of the equipment **in kind**, so progress in manufacturing one component does not itself commission the integrated machine.

ITER’s specified plasma target is **500 MW fusion power for 50 MW externally injected heating power**, expressed as **Q = 10**; this describes *plasma gain*, not output of a generating station. Sustained self-heating by alphas is another key scientific ambition. The mission includes long pulses, not perpetual power production. Think of a laboratory engine test: showing a powerful process in a test rig does not demonstrate a market-ready vehicle. The analogy stops at integrated thermonuclear, radiological and fuel-cycle conditions that cars do not face.

**Read the schedule carefully.** ITER’s revised baseline replaced an earlier symbolic “First Plasma” emphasis with a more complete start to research operations. The [press-conference summary](https://www.iter.org/sites/default/files/media/2024-07/baseline_press_conference_summary_july-2024_b.pdf) linked from ITER’s **8 July 2024** baseline release explicitly envisions **2034** for start of research operations; the release and summary target **2036** for full magnetic energy and **2039** for D–T operations. These are schedule-dependent *plans*, not completed milestones; the ITER Council in June 2026 discussed continued delivery under Baseline 2024, not a newly accomplished plasma event. On **29 July 2026**, ITER reported **six of nine sector modules in the pit** after a lift completed the day before. ITER's **7 September 2026** update reports Indian contractor INOX-CVA's thermal-shield repairs completed in **July 2026** and bellows-installation work involving L&T; module #9's transfer was still planned. A sector is a constituent of the vacuum vessel, not a successfully operating reactor. Do not interpret a publication date as an operation date; recheck all projected dates before future examination use.

**Objection:** a target Q of 10 proves ten times as much electricity as the facility consumes. **Reply:** the denominator counts power delivered *to the plasma*, not total electricity used by heating equipment, refrigeration, magnets and pumps. ITER has no turbine. Success would still be scientifically exceptional and could inform later demonstration plants; it does not displace Indian fission, solar or efficiency policies now. **UPSC use:** specify goal → India’s contribution → conditional long-term global implication; never put 2039 in the “achieved” column.

**Revision notes:** seven ITER members; site in France; experimental tokamak; mission includes burning plasma, integrated systems, blanket tests and safety; Q = fusion power / injected plasma heating; target Q = 10 or 500 MW / 50 MW; no electricity production; 2034/2036/2039 revised targets are planned; July module installation and September-reported Indian-contractor repair/installation work are assembly, not operation.

### Concept check

**Question:** What does ITER’s Q = 10 target demonstrate, and what would it not demonstrate?

**Model answer:** It targets a plasma releasing ten times the external heating power deposited in it; it does not establish net plant electricity, since auxiliary loads and thermal conversion are excluded and ITER has no generator.

**Misconception to avoid:** Mistaking a scheduled or construction milestone for a completed burning-plasma experiment.

**PYQ link (2025 GS-III Q5, 10 marks / 150 words; unsolved):** The question asks candidates to **mention India's contributions to ITER** and assess the implications **if the project succeeds** for future global energy. For ITER's mission portion, identify it as an experimental D–T tokamak, distinguish the planned 500 MW / 50 MW plasma Q target from electricity generation, then hand the answer to India's named contributions and conditional later power prospects. Do not treat assembly progress, a target Q or the revised operating dates as proof of achieved grid supply.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words maximum

**Question (Examine):** Examine why ITER's Q = 10 target and its reported assembly milestones should be described as distinct types of progress.

**Mains model answer:** ITER is an experimental tokamak in France, not a power station. Its intended Q = 10 expresses a future plasma target: 500 MW fusion power against 50 MW heating delivered into the plasma. This excludes the electricity consumed by cryogenics, magnets, pumps and inefficient heating, and ITER has no generating turbine. Separately, ITER reported six of nine sector modules installed in July 2026. That records physical assembly, not an achieved plasma gain or a fusion discharge. The revised baseline targets start of research operations in 2034 and D–T operation in 2039, both still plans subject to delivery. Assembly progress enables testing, and demonstrating a burning plasma would be significant science; neither alone proves a self-sufficient, grid-connected plant. UPSC answers should attach the correct date and energy-accounting boundary to each claim.

**Quantified lesson rubric — 10 marks:** ITER mission and seven-member experimental status **2**; Q numerator/denominator **2**; 29 July six-sector assembly fact **2**; 2034/2036/2039 planned-versus-achieved ladder **2**; no-turbine/DEMO qualification **2**. **Total: 10/10.**

## Lesson 7 — A burning plasma is not a power station

Progress: 7 / 9 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Lawson refinement, alpha heating and gain boundaries

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: no topic-relevant OCR textbook was available; advanced canonical material and ITER's mission page were used.
CA search: "site:iter.org burning plasma Q 10 ITER commissioning June July 2026"
CA found: No additional current-affairs anchor. The **June 2026** Council item remains status-docket evidence of construction and cold-test commissioning, not a burning-plasma result.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
MORE REACTIONS NEED THREE THINGS TOGETHER
   enough ions (density n)
   × sufficiently energetic ions (temperature T)
   × time that energy stays in fuel (τE)
                    ↓
        useful n·T·τE / Lawson-type threshold
                    ↓
  retained alpha heating → possible burning plasma
                    ↓
   plasma gain Q → whole-plant energy gain → net grid electricity
             (three different tests)
```

*Improving one parameter while the others deteriorate need not improve a reactor.*

Lesson 3 established the Basic Lawson idea: density, temperature and energy-confinement time must work together. The Advanced refinement asks how to interpret and measure that combined performance. Suppose India’s SST-1 holds plasma longer, but at insufficient density or temperature: it has achieved a useful research result, not automatically a burning plasma. The **energy-confinement time τE** measures how long stored plasma energy would take to leak away at its loss rate; it is not simply the clock length of an experimental pulse. The shorthand **nTτE** is a useful triple-product comparison, not a single universal numerical threshold independent of fuel, temperature convention and operational assumptions. Increasing temperature can raise D–T reactivity, but also intensifies instability and heat losses; stronger magnets and control can improve confinement but raise engineering costs.

**Burning plasma** means fusion-born charged alphas make a dominant contribution to heating, reducing reliance on external heating. The neutron's energy largely exits the field region; it cannot replace alpha heating inside plasma simply by being energetic. ITER aims to study this condition at scale. **Plasma Q = fusion power / power injected for plasma heating.** A plasma can exceed Q = 1 even if the electric heating system uses more input electricity than the energy recovered; ITER’s Q = 10 target is specifically **500 MW / 50 MW** at the plasma boundary, *not* “450 MW net electricity.” To calculate engineering performance you must also consider conversion efficiency and power for refrigeration, pumps, current drive, control and heating inefficiency. A system can scientifically break even and economically fail.

**Counterpoint:** achieving a burning plasma would not be trivial; it validates a critical missing regime of self-heating and integrated control. **Qualification:** its future transfer into long-lived, high-availability plant designs remains experimental. In a tokamak, the solenoid’s transformer-like induced plasma current tends toward pulsed operation; non-inductive current drive and stellarator alternatives pursue longer steady operation with different penalties. “SST” denotes research intent, not solved continuous operation. **UPSC use:** define each boundary before citing a power number and distinguish confinement time from pulse duration.

### Revision gain boundaries — 10 points

1. The Basic Lawson criterion requires density, temperature and energy-confinement time together.
2. The Advanced triple product **nTτE** compares combined plasma performance.
3. **τE** is an energy-loss time scale, not simply the duration of a pulse.
4. A longer discharge can remain below the required density or temperature.
5. D–T reactivity rises strongly over the relevant temperature range but temperature alone is insufficient.
6. A burning plasma is heated predominantly by retained fusion-born alpha particles.
7. The 3.5 MeV charged alpha aids self-heating; the 14.1 MeV neutron largely leaves the field region.
8. Plasma **Q** compares fusion power with heating delivered to the plasma.
9. Q > 1 or ITER’s Q = 10 target does not establish whole-plant net electricity.
10. Long-pulse or steady-state operation additionally depends on current drive, control, heat exhaust and engineering cost.

### Concept check

**Question:** Could an experiment meet a high plasma Q and still consume electricity overall? Explain the accounting boundary.

**Model answer:** Yes. Q compares fusion power with heating deposited into plasma; electric demand for generating that heating, cooling magnets and operating the plant, plus heat-to-electricity conversion losses, lies outside that ratio.

**Misconception to avoid:** Subtracting 50 MW from 500 MW and calling the difference grid-exported electricity.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words maximum

**Question (Analyse):** Analyse why improving plasma confinement time need not deliver a burning plasma or net electric output, even if a high Q is reported.

**Mains model answer:** Energy-confinement time is the time scale for plasma energy loss at the measured loss rate, not simply the duration of an experimental discharge. The Lawson-type triple product combines density, temperature and this confinement time: if an SST-1 experiment holds plasma longer while its temperature or density remains inadequate, the result advances control science but cannot demonstrate sustained fusion burning. In D–T fusion, charged 3.5 MeV alpha particles can deposit heat in plasma, whereas most energy travels outward in neutral 14.1 MeV neutrons. A burning plasma relies substantially on that retained alpha heating. ITER's future Q = 10 target compares 500 MW fusion power with 50 MW heating deposited in plasma, not input electricity with delivered electricity. A power plant would need to convert recoverable heat, cover heating inefficiency and the demands of cooling, pumping and current drive, and then export a surplus. Its fuel cycle and components must also endure. High confinement, alpha self-heating, plasma gain and net grid output are therefore successive, non-equivalent achievements.

Even a well-confined burning plasma cannot keep running without fuel and durable structures.

*The same escaping neutron is an energy carrier, prospective fuel breeder and materials hazard.*

```text
NEUTRON → useful heat / lithium breeding / wall damage
                       ↓
             one particle, three engineering jobs
```

**Tritium breeding ratio (TBR)** is produced tritium divided by tritium consumed. A future plant needs a ratio above unity with practical margin for extraction, decay, reserves and losses; writing “TBR > 1” describes an engineering *requirement*, not an ITER result. Blanket modules tested at ITER may help evaluate breeding in a fusion environment, but they are not a proof of full-coverage, closed-cycle self-sufficiency. The lithium reaction also needs neutron supply and suitable blanket geometry: neutrons are not an infinitely recyclable resource.

The fast **14.1 MeV neutron** is uncharged and crosses magnetic fields. Repeated bombardment displaces atoms, makes helium/hydrogen within materials, causes swelling or embrittlement and activates components. Candidate armour includes **tungsten**, while **reduced-activation ferritic-martensitic steels** are explored for supporting structures. “Reduced activation” does not mean “non-radioactive.” Qualifying these materials for a DEMO-class plant needs a dedicated high-flux neutron test source; that qualification facility itself remains a future prerequisite, not a completed validation. Material performance under the neutron flux, temperature gradients and operational life of a future plant still needs testing. The divertor, meanwhile, must handle intense local plasma-edge heat and particle exhaust. An apparent gain in confinement can intensify edge loads; solving core performance without exhaust can merely move the bottleneck.

ITER’s 2024 updated plans discussed changing the plasma-facing first-wall material from beryllium to tungsten and using a more complete machine at the start. That is a *design choice in the dated project plan*, not a finding that tungsten has passed a lifetime test. Remote maintenance is needed where activation and tight geometry prevent routine human access. Any proposed Indian DEMO-like plant would require integrated material manufacture, component replacement, tritium accounting, waste pathways and trained oversight, not just an ITER Q result.

**Objection:** if D–T gives abundant neutron energy, breeding must be easy. **Reply:** neutrons can miss useful lithium, be absorbed elsewhere, damage structures or generate heat that is hard to extract; tritium must then be safely recovered. **Counterpoint:** the requirements provide a testable engineering agenda rather than a reason to abandon fusion. **UPSC use:** list physics gain and *separate* fuel self-sufficiency, wall survival and exhaust as successive tests.

### Revision engineering loop — 10 points

1. Lithium-bearing blankets are intended to breed tritium from fusion neutrons.
2. **TBR = tritium produced / tritium consumed**.
3. A practical plant needs TBR above unity with margin for extraction, decay, reserves and losses.
4. ITER test blanket modules do not prove a full-coverage closed tritium cycle.
5. The 14.1 MeV neutron carries useful heat but also displaces atoms and activates structures.
6. Neutron exposure can cause swelling, helium/hydrogen production and embrittlement.
7. Tungsten is a candidate plasma-facing armour, not a lifetime guarantee.
8. Reduced-activation ferritic-martensitic steels reduce—not eliminate—activation concerns.
9. The divertor must remove helium ash and concentrated edge heat; improved core confinement can worsen exhaust loads.
10. High-flux qualification, remote maintenance and replaceable components are integral to a DEMO-class design.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a high tritium breeding ratio calculated for a blanket not by itself guarantee a self-sufficient reactor?

**Model answer:** A working cycle must extract and deliver tritium from the entire installed blanket while covering losses, decay and reserve needs; the blanket and structures must also survive neutron and thermal loading.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating a materials test module or design calculation with sustained plant-scale closed-loop operation.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words maximum

**Question (Assess):** Assess why a future D–T plant must solve tritium supply, neutron damage and edge exhaust together rather than optimise the plasma alone.

**Mains model answer:** Plasma performance can generate many D–T reactions, but each also yields an uncharged 14.1 MeV neutron. In a future plant, neutrons should deposit recoverable heat and react in a lithium-bearing blanket to replenish scarce tritium. A tritium breeding ratio above one, with margin, is needed to cover extraction delays, decay, leakage and inventory; ITER's test blanket modules are experiments, not proof that a full plant closes this loop. Meanwhile the divertor must remove helium ash and intense edge heat, which may worsen as core confinement improves. Losing that exhaust route threatens component life even if the core meets its physics target. Thus breeding geometry, usable heat recovery, survivable materials and maintenance must be designed as one system. Better plasma Q alone cannot certify continuous, fuel-self-sufficient or economically available electricity.

## Original 10-marker — 150 words maximum

**Question:** Explain why a high fusion gain in ITER would not mean that India can immediately use fusion electricity. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Directive — Explain:** show the causal difference between plasma gain, plant electricity and domestic deployment, then conclude with a qualified judgement. Avoid merely listing ITER’s components.

**Model answer (124 whitespace-delimited words):** ITER seeks to show that controlled deuterium–tritium plasma can release far more fusion power than the heating deposited in it. Its stated target is 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of injected plasma heating, a plasma Q of 10. But ITER will not convert that heat into electricity. A generating plant would additionally power cryogenics, magnets, pumps and heating equipment, recover neutron heat, breed scarce tritium from lithium and withstand damage to its blanket and divertor. India participates through ITER-India’s in-kind systems, including the cryostat, and through IPR’s ADITYA-U and SST-1 research. These build capability, not an Indian fusion station. ITER success would be a vital scientific step toward later demonstration plants, not an immediate alternative to existing Indian fission or renewables.

**Why this earns marks:** Answers the precise “why not” through separate energy-accounting, engineering and India-specific evidence; the conclusion neither trivialises ITER nor advertises nonexistent generation.

## Original 15-marker — 250 words maximum

**Question:** Discuss how magnetic and inertial confinement address fusion’s physical barrier, and assess which additional problems both routes must solve before becoming power sources. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Directive — Discuss and assess:** explain each mechanism, compare its distinct trade-off, then evaluate shared power-plant requirements. Do not claim that the two devices operate identically.

**Model answer (183 whitespace-delimited words):** Light positive nuclei repel; fusion needs enough close encounters and a sufficiently long period of useful conditions. Magnetic confinement, as in ITER’s tokamak, bends the paths of charged hot plasma in a toroidal chamber. Heating, shaped fields and feedback aim to sustain a relatively dilute plasma; disruptions, current drive and edge heat limit performance. IPR’s superconducting SST-1 contributes longer-pulse research, not grid power. Inertial confinement instead compresses tiny fuel targets extremely rapidly; the target’s inertia allows brief reactions before it disperses. Repetition, uniform compression and efficient drivers are its distinct obstacles.

Both routes must ultimately close a larger energy balance, not merely report an isolated plasma or target gain. For deuterium–tritium fuel, scarce tritium must be recovered from lithium breeding, and fast neutrons can damage and activate structures. Useful heat must drive conversion equipment without being consumed by auxiliary systems; components must survive and be replaceable at an acceptable cost. ITER’s proposed Q = 10 demonstrates a magnetic-plasma ambition, not that these shared engineering conditions have been met. The routes are scientifically promising but commercial readiness cannot be inferred from an experimental shot.

**Why this earns marks:** Distinguishes time/density trade-offs with a named Indian example, connects a common D–T reaction to fuel/material barriers and qualifies the Q claim without dismissing research.

**Quantified final rubric — 15 marks:** magnetic-confinement mechanism **3**; inertial-confinement mechanism **3**; Lawson density–temperature–confinement comparison **3**; shared tritium/material/energy-conversion barriers **3**; qualified power-source verdict **3**. **Total: 15/15.**

## Original 20-marker — 250 words maximum

**Question:** Evaluate India’s participation in ITER as an industrial and scientific strategy in the light of fusion’s safety, fuel-cycle and commercial limitations. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Directive — Evaluate:** weigh current capability gains against technical and governance costs; end with a conditional verdict. Avoid treating a future grid promise as a delivered benefit.

**Model answer (222 whitespace-delimited words):** India’s ITER membership is a long-horizon research and industrial strategy, not an electricity purchase. The Institute for Plasma Research in Gandhinagar builds domestic experimental skills through ADITYA-U and superconducting SST-1. Its dedicated domestic agency, ITER-India, manages in-kind contributions to the international tokamak in France. Nine packages span the cryostat, shielding, cooling water, cryogenics, ion- and electron-cyclotron RF heating, diagnostic neutral beam, power supplies and diagnostics. Manufacturing and integrating these systems give Indian industry experience with precise vacuum, cryogenic and plasma-control engineering. However, supplying components does not establish an operational Indian reactor, and international component delivery exposes the programme to shared schedule risk.

ITER targets burning-plasma research and a plasma gain of 500 MW fusion power for 50 MW injected heating; it will not generate grid electricity. A future D–T plant must breed scarce tritium in lithium blankets, recover neutron heat, withstand activation and embrittlement from energetic neutrons, and exhaust heat through a durable divertor. Fusion avoids a self-sustaining fission chain reaction, yet tritium containment, activated waste and worker safety demand oversight. Plant-wide energy balance, reliability and cost remain unproved. ITER’s 2026 sector-module installation is construction progress, not a commercial milestone.

Thus the defensible near-term return is scientific, engineering and industrial learning. Possible low-carbon firm power is a conditional longer-term prospect dependent on later demonstration machines, robust safety governance and commercially credible operation.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Barrier, binding and usable fuel

- Light positive nuclei repel (Coulomb barrier); close approach permits strong-force binding, helped by heating and tunnelling. Greater product binding yields released mass-difference energy, E = Δmc²; not all nuclear combinations are exoergic.
- D–T: ²H + ³H → ⁴He (alpha, 3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV); total 17.6 MeV per reaction. Charged alpha heats plasma; neutral neutron heats blanket, enables breeding and damages walls.
- Fusion cross-section means effective reaction probability at a given collision energy. D–T is favoured because its cross-section is comparatively large at lower temperatures than alternative fuels; ITER’s operating context is about **150 million °C**, not a claim that temperature alone meets reactor conditions.
- Plasma is ionised fuel; deuterium is extractable from water, tritium is radioactive and scarce. Lithium + neutron can breed tritium, but test blankets ≠ proven closed fuel cycle.

## Confinement and energy accounting

- Tokamak = toroidal magnetic device with helical guiding field, induced plasma current, external heating, diagnostics, control and divertor; stellarator uses complex external coils to ease reliance on induced current.
- Inertial route compresses a pellet rapidly; longer-held low-density magnetic plasma and short-lived high-density pellet are different solutions to confinement.
- Basic Lawson criterion: density, temperature and energy-confinement time must work together. Advanced **nTτE** comparison refines that idea; τE is not pulse length. Burning plasma relies heavily on alpha self-heating.
- Q = fusion power / injected plasma-heating power. ITER target 500/50 MW = Q 10; no ITER electricity generation. Engineering gain must include loads and conversion.

## Facilities, status and institutions

- ITER in France: EU, India, China, Japan, Republic of Korea, Russia, US; experimental tokamak with burning-plasma, systems, safety and breeding-test missions; planned later demonstration plants are distinct.
- ITER's 8 July 2024 baseline release links its press-conference summary envisioning research start in **2034**; the release and summary target full magnetic energy **2036** and D–T **2039**. These remain schedule-dependent future plans, not achieved operations.
- ITER release **29 July 2026** reports sixth of nine sector modules installed; installation is assembly, not first plasma.
- ITER report **7 September 2026**: Indian contractor INOX-CVA finished thermal-shield repairs in **July 2026**; L&T is pictured with ITER teams at first bellows welds. Bellows installation was continuing; module #9 pit transfer was planned, not accomplished.
- IPR (DAE-aided research institute, Gandhinagar) hosts ADITYA-U, SST-1 and the dedicated ITER-India domestic agency. ITER-India is not the entire IPR.
- Direct IPR evidence records ADITYA-U divertor expansion, stable pure-deuterium discharges, confinement/isotope studies, shaped-plasma operation and disruption-mitigation experiments; these are research achievements, not electricity generation.
- Nine Indian ITER packages: cryostat, in-wall shielding, cooling water, cryogenic system, ion-cyclotron RF heating, electron-cyclotron RF heating, diagnostic neutral beam, power supplies, selected diagnostics. Remember by function: enclosure → heat/cold → waves → measurement → electrical supply.
- Contractor progress complements rather than replaces the nine-package account: INOX-CVA's shield repairs and L&T's documented presence at first bellows welds illustrate construction/installation, not completed fusion operation.

## Risk, next steps and exam distinctions

- D–T reactors must breed more recoverable tritium than they use after practical losses, protect neutron-exposed reduced-activation ferritic-martensitic steels and tungsten-facing surfaces, exhaust edge heat, and enable remote maintenance. A dedicated high-flux neutron test source remains a prerequisite for DEMO materials qualification.
- Fusion has no runaway fission chain reaction or normal stream of fission-product waste; it does involve radioactive tritium, activated structures and industrial risk. “Safer in some respects” ≠ “risk-free.”
- India’s three-stage PHWR → fast-breeder → thorium/U-233 **fission** fuel strategy supplies a different answer from fusion research; 2026 PFBR/criticality PYQ is fission-owned.
- DAE officially records active feasibility interest in fusion-driven sub-critical/fusion–fission hybrid modes for electricity or radioactive-waste incineration. This is a feasibility concept, not an approved design, construction project or operating reactor.
- Status ladder: feasibility/proposal → construction → experiment → DEMO-type electricity demonstration → commercial deployment. Evidence at one rung cannot be promoted to the next.
- Direct 2025 GS-III Q5 route: India's domestic development → named in-kind ITER packages → conditional global energy implications → plasma gain versus plant electricity and tritium/material/cost limits.
- Mains verdict: scientific and industrial gains now are credible; commercial low-carbon electricity remains contingent on later engineering, regulation, economics and demonstration.
