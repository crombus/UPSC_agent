## Lesson 1 — From a trait to a genetic intervention

Progress: 1/8 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: DNA, gene expression, breeding and recombinant DNA

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
DNA sequence → gene expressed → protein / regulation → observable trait
     │
     ├─ cross two compatible parents → recombination of many existing variants
     ├─ insert designed DNA construct → recombinant-DNA GM plant
     └─ change a selected sequence → targeted editing (outcome depends on repair)
```

*The method of changing DNA and the final trait are different questions; neither alone settles safety or law.*

**Start with the farmer's question.** Suppose a crop loses yield to an insect. The intervention might combine naturally compatible parents, insert a bacterial gene, or alter a native plant gene. In each case the phenotype—observable performance—depends on gene expression, growing conditions and pest pressure. A gene is a DNA sequence involved in producing a functional molecule; a genome is the organism's complete genetic material. A promoter helps control *where and when* a gene is expressed. Thus possession of a construct and dependable field performance are not identical.

**1. The distinct routes.** Controlled hybridisation joins parent lines through sexual reproduction; its offspring inherit many shuffled genes. Recombinant DNA (rDNA) construction begins when restriction enzymes cut DNA at recognised sequences and DNA ligase joins compatible fragments. A plant vector commonly contains an origin of replication for maintenance in bacteria, a promoter, the gene of interest, a transcription terminator, a selectable marker and transfer-border sequences where the delivery system requires them. *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* can transfer the engineered T-DNA region from a modified Ti-plasmid vector; a gene gun (biolistics) physically delivers DNA-coated particles.

```text
trait and gene choice
        ↓
restriction cutting + ligase joining
        ↓
vector: promoter → gene → terminator
        │          + selectable marker / transfer borders
        ↓
delivery by Agrobacterium or biolistics
        ↓
transformation of competent plant cells
        ↓
selection of candidate transformed cells
        ↓
tissue-culture regeneration: callus → shoots → roots → plant
        ↓
molecular validation: locus, copy number, integrity, backbone/residual DNA
        ↓
expression + trait testing + stable inheritance in progeny
```

Transformation introduces DNA into a cell; selection enriches candidate transformed cells but does not prove correct integration. Regeneration converts a transformed cell or callus into a fertile plant, after which insertion, expression, phenotype and inheritance must be validated. A transgenic plant contains DNA from an unrelated species; a cisgenic plant receives an intact gene from a sexually compatible gene pool; intragenic design recombines compatible-pool elements. These distinctions describe donor provenance, not a blanket exemption from India's GMO oversight.

**2. What the comparison proves.** Traditional hybridisation is not itself transgenesis. Conversely, a hybrid can carry a transgenic trait if its parent lines were genetically engineered. A marker gene helps select transformed cells; its presence can have separate assessment implications. Insertion location, copy number, expression stability and unintended effects must be characterised rather than assumed from the label “GM”. Precision of target selection does not automatically mean precision of the trait under every field condition.

**Example and limit.** Crossing two conventional rice lines may combine drought tolerance and yield but also move unwanted linked traits. Inserting a single characterised construct can focus on a chosen mechanism, yet integration and ecological consequences still require study. This comparison explains *how variants arise*, not which method is universally safer.

**Objection → reply → residual.** An objector notes that a laboratory construct may disrupt a plant gene or alter expression. The reply is molecular characterisation, compositional comparison and staged ecological testing, not the claim that all inserted genes are harmless. Conventional breeding also changes genomes; that observation alone does not remove the duty to assess a new GM trait.

**UPSC use.** For GS-III, explain mechanism before claimed farm benefits; for Prelims distinguish hybrid, transgenic and edited outcomes. There is no claim that artificial chromosomes are ordinary GM crop varieties: an artificial chromosome is an engineered chromosome-like DNA carrier with replication/segregation elements. **Verified PYQ linkage: 2019 Prelims GS-I Q93** tests developments in artificial chromosomes/DNA science; objective demand: identify the technology, not assume any artificial chromosome is an approved Indian crop. Approach: identify carrier function, then separately ask whether stable plant deployment is claimed; do not infer an official answer key.

**Revision notes:** Gene → functional product → trait; restriction enzymes cut and ligase joins DNA; vector architecture includes promoter–gene–terminator plus selectable marker and necessary maintenance/transfer elements; hybridisation recombines parental variants; delivery → transformation → selection → regeneration → molecular validation → expression/trait → inheritance; *Agrobacterium* or biolistics delivers constructs; selection marker ≠ proof of correct stable insertion; transgenic ≠ cisgenic ≠ intragenic; organism, trait and approval stage must be named independently.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can a mustard plant simultaneously be a hybrid and a transgenic GM crop? Explain the two different descriptions.

**Model answer:** Yes. “Hybrid” describes a cross between parent lines; “transgenic” describes introduced DNA in the parent/hybrid lineage. These labels concern different features of the same plant.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling all hybrids GM confuses sexual crossing with laboratory gene transfer.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, answer in 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish conventional hybridisation from recombinant-DNA crop development, explaining why neither label alone proves safety.

**Model response (approximately 110 words):** Conventional hybridisation crosses compatible parents and recombines many inherited variants; recombinant-DNA development assembles a chosen construct and introduces it into plant cells, often via *Agrobacterium* or biolistics. The former can transfer linked unwanted traits, whereas the latter requires scrutiny of insertion site, copy number, stable expression and unintended effects. A hybrid may also be transgenic: the terms describe breeding and DNA provenance, respectively. Indian crop appraisal must therefore identify the specific trait and exposure pathway. Bt insect resistance, for example, raises a resistance-management question, while a food crop raises consumption questions. Neither familiarity with a cross nor molecular targeting substitutes for comparative, stage-specific evidence; testing must fit the product and its use.

**Scoring guide (10):** crossing versus construct mechanism (3); overlap and correct vocabulary (2); distinct sources of unintended effects (2); named crop/example (1); reasoned trait-specific safety verdict (2).

## Lesson 2 — Bt cotton: an insect trait, not an herbicide licence

Progress: 2/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Bt mechanism, resistance stewardship and biotechnology's farmer-welfare route

```text
Bt bacterium's cry gene → cotton expresses Cry protein
                                  ↓
susceptible bollworm larva ingests plant tissue → gut action → larval harm
                                  ↓
repeated exposure selects survivors → reduced effectiveness
                                  ↑
                    refuges + monitoring + integrated pest control
```

*The useful trait creates an evolutionary selection pressure; managing the second arrow is part of deploying the first.*

**Why this works.** *Bacillus thuringiensis* produces crystal (Cry) proteins. A cotton plant engineered to express a suitable *cry* gene exposes susceptible lepidopteran larvae, including bollworms, when they feed. Activation and binding in a susceptible insect gut cause toxicity; species-specific biological conditions matter. This is **insect resistance**, not tolerance of an herbicide. Bollgard I uses a Bt trait associated with Cry1Ac; Bollgard II stacks Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab. Different targets reduce reliance on one mode of action, but stacking is not a guarantee against resistant pests.

**Follow the population, not just the gene.** Initially the protein may suppress susceptible larvae. Repeated planting favours variants that survive exposure; those survivors reproduce. A refuge—non-Bt host plants maintained under the applicable stewardship plan—helps preserve susceptible insects that can mate with resistant survivors, delaying fixation of resistance. Appropriate configuration and compliance matter. Monitoring pest populations, avoiding indiscriminate pesticide substitution and integrated pest management remain necessary. A fall in spraying for a target pest in one setting cannot be extrapolated into a guaranteed nationwide yield or income gain: weather, secondary pests, seed cost, credit and market price also matter.

**Keep the cotton traits separate.** Official PIB material dated **6 August 2024** describes Bt cotton as India's only GM crop approved for commercial cultivation at that time. This dated legal-status statement is not a 2026 cultivation census. Herbicide tolerance is a different trait; unauthorised HT cotton planting would not itself confer regulatory approval. A seed marketed as “Terminator” (genetic use restriction causing sterile seeds) should not be confused with Bt cotton; no commercial use of that technology in Indian Bt seed is established here.

**From technology to living standards (core GS-III).** Start with a *specific farm constraint*, not a list of exciting techniques. Bt cotton targets susceptible bollworms: where target pressure is material, avoided crop injury or fewer target-pest sprays may reduce losses or costs; resistance, secondary pests, seed expense and local prices can offset the gain. A disease-free plant produced by tissue culture addresses infected planting stock through clonal propagation and screening, but does not guarantee protection against later infection. A suitable microbial biofertilizer can improve nutrient availability under appropriate soil conditions, but cannot replace every nutrient requirement or work identically in every field. An edited rice line intended for saline soils addresses a different constraint; ICAR's 2025 variety announcement is not evidence that farmers everywhere obtained seeds or higher net income. In each case trace **mechanism → credible field comparator → yield or input cost → market price and access → farmer net return**. Farm benefits can also depend on irrigation, extension, credit and affordable planting material. The mechanism is a *possible pathway*, not an observed India-wide welfare statistic.

**Verified Mains PYQ — 2019 GS-III Q16:** biotechnology's contribution to improving farmers' living standards (**Discuss; 15 marks; 250 words**; neutral demand summary, not verbatim question text). **Unsolved approach:** open with constraint-specific mechanisms, use distinct crop/planting-material or soil-input examples, explain the route to income, test risks and affordability, and close with evidence, access and stewardship; a single Bt success claim or a catalogue of scheme names cannot satisfy “living standards.” No PYQ model answer is given here.

**Objection → reply → residual.** The strongest ecological objection is that pest resistance or altered non-target pressure could erode benefit. Trait stacking, refuges, pest surveillance and site-sensitive review answer part of it. They do not eliminate evolutionary change or farmer compliance problems; post-release performance is empirical.

**UPSC use and PYQ.** **2021 Prelims GS-I Q67** concerns Bollgard I and II technology; the exact neutral stem and options appear in the verified PYQ section before the approach. Mains chain: trait → target → benefit conditional on pest ecology → resistance management → farmer income qualification.

**Revision notes:** Bt is *B. thuringiensis*; Cry proteins act on susceptible insect larvae; Bollgard II stacks Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab; Bt ≠ herbicide tolerance; refuge maintains susceptible populations; resistance evolves by selection; 2024 PIB commercial-status claim is date-limited; farm-welfare answer = mechanism + comparator + net return + affordability/access; tissue culture addresses planting material, microbial inoculants address soil nutrient availability; 2019 GS-III Q16 requires multiple benefit channels and qualifications.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a two-Cry-gene cotton variety still require refuge and pest monitoring?

**Model answer:** Two active traits may delay resistance, but surviving insects still undergo selection. A refuge helps retain susceptible mates; surveillance detects declining effectiveness.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Stacked genes stop evolution” overlooks selection and compliance.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, answer in 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain how Bt cotton suppresses its target pest and why durable gains depend on stewardship rather than the trait alone.

**Model response (approximately 112 words):** Bt cotton expresses a Cry protein derived from *Bacillus thuringiensis*. Susceptible bollworm larvae ingest it while feeding; action in their gut reduces target-pest damage. Bollgard II combines Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab, reducing dependence on a single protein. However, repeated exposure selects resistant survivors. Non-Bt refuges help maintain susceptible insects, while monitoring and integrated pest management detect and manage emerging resistance or secondary pests. India's PIB identified Bt cotton as the only commercially approved GM crop in its 6 August 2024 statement; that approval does not prove a uniform income gain in every locality. Seed cost, pest mix, climate, extension and prices condition farmer benefit. Policy should assess observed outcomes and enforce proportionate stewardship.

**Scoring guide (10):** Cry source, feeding and target specificity (3); stacking distinction (1); selection mechanism (2); refuge/monitoring (2); dated India anchor and qualified farmer verdict (2).

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Biological step | Gene or process | What it achieves | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent generation | *barnase* in one line | Male sterility enables controlled cross | Commercial farmer cultivation |
| Fertility restoration | *barstar* in partner line | Restores fertility in the hybrid | Absence of GMO regulatory scrutiny |
| Selection | *bar* marker | Glufosinate tolerance used for selecting transformed lines | A mandate to apply herbicide in fields |
| Field stage | Conditional environmental release for testing/seed | Further assessment under conditions | Uncontested commercial release |

*The first three rows explain a breeding system; the last describes a separate legal stage.*

**The breeding obstacle.** Mustard tends to self-pollinate. Reliable commercial-scale crossing needs a way to prevent pollen from the intended female parent fertilising itself. The *barnase* protein disables pollen formation in one parent; its partner *barstar* inhibits barnase and restores fertility in the resulting hybrid. DMH-11 is therefore a **hybrid created using transgenic parent lines**. The *bar* gene provides glufosinate tolerance as a selectable marker. Its presence is relevant to assessment, but the stated hybridisation purpose is not equivalent to an approval of field-wide herbicide application.

**Why this becomes political.** Advocates point to hybrid breeding and possible yield or oilseed benefits; critics ask whether local comparators and biosafety data justify the claimed advantage, whether gene flow could affect nearby mustard, whether herbicide tolerance could alter weed control or labour, and whether farmer choice and labelling are credible. The appropriate reply is transparent comparator trials, exposure-specific biosafety review, conflict-of-interest control and monitoring—not either a blanket “GM is safe” or a claim that *bar* alone proves inevitable herbicide use. Residual questions depend on field evidence and the eventual legal outcome.

**Timeline: read verbs literally.**

```text
18 Oct 2022  GEAC 147th meeting: environmental-release recommendation
25 Oct 2022  Union decision concerning conditional environmental release
07 Feb 2023  PIB explains environmental release for seed production/testing
             under conditions, *prior to* commercial release
23 Jul 2024  Supreme Court: two opposing judgments + common directions;
             divided question sent for an "appropriate Bench"
01 Oct 2026  No newer dispositive official order established in sources used here
```

Justice B. V. Nagarathna held the recommendation and Union decision vitiated and quashed them; Justice Sanjay Karol reached the contrary conclusion, allowing the conditional process subject to directions. **A split verdict is not a final unified holding approving or prohibiting commercial DMH-11 cultivation.** The common directions address national GM-crop policy consultation, safeguards against conflicts of interest, and **section 23 labelling compliance for imported GM food, particularly edible oil**, under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006; this specific direction is not a general judicial label order for all GM crops. Do not automatically call an “appropriate Bench” a larger bench; nor claim that the policy has been completed without an official status source.

**Contrast case.** The Government's **9 February 2010** Bt-brinjal release used a *moratorium*—release withheld at that stage pending further evidence—not permanent statutory prohibition and not approval. Continuation or withdrawal as of October 2026 could not be verified afresh here. Thus Bt cotton's commercial status, mustard's challenged conditional release, brinjal's dated moratorium and an experimental field trial represent four different propositions.

**Revision notes:** Self-pollinating mustard → controlled hybridisation problem; *barnase* male sterility; *barstar* restoration; *bar* selection marker; hybrid + transgenic simultaneously; GEAC recommendation 18 October 2022; conditional environmental release ≠ commercial cultivation; Supreme Court 23 July 2024 split; common directions ≠ resolution; Bt-brinjal moratorium ≠ ban.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why cannot the 2023 governmental statement about DMH-11 be cited as proof that farmers had approval for ordinary commercial cultivation?

**Model answer:** It described conditional environmental release for seed production and testing before commercial release; the later Supreme Court split left the challenged decision unresolved.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating “environmental release” as a synonym for unrestricted commercial cultivation erases a necessary approval stage.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, answer in 250 words

**Mains question:** Analyse why DMH-11 is simultaneously a breeding innovation and a difficult biosafety-governance case in India.

**Model response (approximately 180 words):** Mustard self-pollination limits reliable controlled crossing. DMH-11 uses transgenic *barnase* to make one parent male sterile and *barstar* in another to restore fertility in the hybrid; *bar* serves as a glufosinate-tolerance selection marker. The biological innovation is hybrid production, not an automatic entitlement to herbicide-tolerant field management. Potential gains must be tested against relevant Indian comparators, while gene flow, herbicide-management incentives, non-target effects and farmer access require separate evaluation. GEAC's October 2022 recommendation and the Union decision led to a conditional environmental-release position explained by PIB in February 2023 for seed production and testing before commercial release. In *Gene Campaign* (23 July 2024), Justices Nagarathna and Karol disagreed on the legality of that decision; their common directions called for GM policy consultation, conflict safeguards and section 23 labelling compliance for **imported GM food, particularly edible oil**. A divided ruling cannot be recast as settled commercial approval. India should publish trial evidence, protect independent assessment, specify permitted uses and provide traceable seed and monitoring arrangements. Innovation and precaution can coexist only if each legal stage and ecological claim is stated accurately.

**Scoring guide (15):** breeding mechanism with all three genes (4); benefit and evidence limits (2); two concrete ecological/social risks and matched safeguards (3); differentiated 2022–24 legal sequence (4); reasoned conclusion (2).

## Lesson 4 — CRISPR: targeting is not the same as editing outcome

Progress: 4/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: CRISPR repair, RNAi, DNA markers and reproductive-technology mechanisms

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available.
CA search: "ICAR 4 April 2025 genome edited rice CRISPR Cas foreign DNA India".
CA found: ICAR announced two genome-edited rice varieties on 4 April 2025.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
guide RNA ≈ address → matching genomic sequence beside compatible PAM
                                        ↓
                              Cas9 nuclease cuts both DNA strands
                                        ↓
               ┌────────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
          NHEJ joins ends                                  donor template + HDR
          often small indels                           specified substitution/insertion
          gene knockout possible                       less efficient in many settings
```

*An address, a cut and cellular repair are three separate causal steps; the guide does not write the final sequence.*

**Everyday intuition.** Finding a typo in a long document is not the same as correcting it. Guide RNA helps locate a complementary DNA sequence (often represented as roughly 20 target bases); Cas9 is the cutting protein. For standard SpCas9, a neighbouring **PAM**, commonly NGG, must be present. A guide's near match elsewhere can produce an **off-target** edit; neither a PAM nor a good guide proves zero risk.

**Repair decides the result.** Non-homologous end joining (**NHEJ**) rejoins broken ends and can create short insertions/deletions (indels), often knocking out a gene. Homology-directed repair (**HDR**) copies from a supplied homologous template: a short template can specify a small substitution, while a gene-sized donor can give targeted insertion. HDR is not uniformly efficient in plant cells. A targeted cut alone does not guarantee a specified allele. A designer checks target sequence, final locus, possible unintended insertions and performance in progeny.

**The plant bottleneck comes before and after the cut.** Editing cargo may be delivered as plasmid DNA, viral or non-viral nucleic acid, messenger RNA plus guide RNA, or a preassembled Cas–guide ribonucleoprotein (**RNP**). Delivery may use *Agrobacterium*, biolistics, protoplast transfection or other crop-specific systems. The chosen cargo changes the chance of residual foreign DNA and the duration of nuclease activity, but no format guarantees success. Many crop cells are difficult to transform, and a successfully edited cell must still regenerate into a fertile plant. Regeneration depends on species, variety or genotype, explant type and tissue-culture response; a protocol efficient in one rice or maize line may perform poorly in another. Low editing efficiency, chimeric or mosaic regenerants, tissue-culture mutations and failure to transmit the edit through the germline can therefore limit practical breeding. Screening must identify the intended allele in a stable, fertile progeny line rather than merely detect a cut in cultured cells.

```text
editing design → cargo choice → delivery into responsive cells
       ↓              ↓                    ↓
 guide/PAM       DNA, RNA or RNP      genotype + explant dependence
       ↓
edited cell → selection/screening → regeneration → fertile plant
       ↓                                  ↓
on/off-target validation          mosaicism, culture effects,
       ↓                           inheritance and trait testing
stable edited progeny
```

**Why editing is not always cutting.** Base editors typically combine a targeting system with a base-modifying enzyme to convert a base without a double-strand break. Prime editors use a guide/template arrangement and reverse-transcriptase chemistry to write small specified edits without the ordinary Cas9 double-strand-break/HDR sequence. Both still require assessment of unwanted changes; neither is accurately described simply as “NHEJ”. The familiar SDN categories describe repair outcomes from site-directed nuclease systems and should not automatically be pasted onto every newer editor without examining the actual product.

**Comparison that matters.** RNA interference (**RNAi**) uses short RNA-mediated recognition to suppress a messenger RNA and thereby reduce gene expression; it need not rewrite genomic DNA. CRISPR-Cas9 edits DNA through target recognition, cleavage and repair. An artificial chromosome is a carrier, not an editing nuclease. This matters when a question lists several technologies in one stem.

**Part B — when the question changes from plants to inheritance.** First identify *what is being changed*: a short repeated DNA sequence, a chromosome-like carrier, mitochondrial contents or nuclear DNA. This prevents a correct explanation of Cas9 from being wrongly applied to a technique that never uses it.

| Biological problem | Core mechanism | Decisive limit |
|---|---|---|
| Trace genetic variation or relatedness | Microsatellites are short tandem DNA repeats whose lengths can differ among people or species | A marker of relatedness is not itself proof of a causal trait gene |
| Carry an engineered sequence | An artificial chromosome incorporates chromosome-like maintenance/segregation components | Carrying DNA is not the same act as cutting another chromosome |
| Reduce maternally transmitted mitochondrial disease | Pronuclear transfer relocates the intended parents' nuclear pronuclei to an enucleated donor zygote with healthier mitochondria | It does not correct a disease-causing nuclear allele or guarantee zero residual affected mitochondria |
| Modify an embryo's nuclear allele | Genome editing alters DNA at a chosen locus; an embryo edit may enter future germ cells | Changing an embryo is not merely replacing mitochondria |
| Treat a patient's cells | Somatic editing or stem-cell transfer acts on cells in that patient's body | Cell transfer need not edit DNA; somatic effects are not automatically inherited |

*Substrate and heritability separate a DNA marker, a delivery platform, a mitochondrial intervention and nuclear editing.*

**Work through the pronuclear example.** Mitochondria contain their own DNA and are transmitted principally through the mother. Before the earliest cell divisions, nuclear pronuclei of the intended parents may be moved into donor cytoplasm containing healthier mitochondria. The purpose is to reduce risk from a **specified mitochondrial disorder**, not to rewrite the parents' nuclear genes. Conversely embryo nuclear editing seeks to change a genomic sequence and can affect the germline; a somatic stem-cell intervention may treat one person's tissue without changing future offspring. “Stem-cell transfer” is a cellular intervention, not evidence by itself of embryo editing, cloning or lawful clinical use. Microsatellite patterns support population comparisons but cannot prove why a rice trait or human disease occurs. The distinction works even when the same newspaper article calls all of these “genetic technology.”

**Verified objective PYQ routes:** Exact neutral stems and options for **2019 Prelims GS-I Q93, 2020 Q37/Q44, 2021 Q66 and 2023 Q70** appear in the verified PYQ section before their unsolved approaches.

**Objection → reply → residual.** Critics point to off-target cuts and on-target rearrangements. Better guide design, high-fidelity enzymes, sequencing, breeding away delivery constructs where feasible and line-by-line testing reduce uncertainty. Sequencing is a characterisation method, not evidence of universally harmless field performance.

**Other PYQ routes.** Exact neutral stems and options for **2018 Prelims GS-I Q64** and **2019 Q96/Q99** likewise appear before their unsolved approaches in the verified PYQ section.

**Revision notes:** Guide = targeting; Cas9 = nuclease; PAM = adjacent recognition requirement; NHEJ → variable indels; HDR + donor → template-directed change; cargo may be DNA, RNA or RNP; delivery and regeneration are separate bottlenecks; genotype and explant affect transformability; edited cells may be chimeric and must yield fertile, stably inheriting plants; base and prime editors can avoid double-strand breaks; RNAi changes expression, not necessarily DNA; artificial chromosome = carrier; microsatellite = repeated-length marker; pronuclear transfer ≠ nuclear-gene repair; embryo editing ≠ stem-cell transfer.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Cas9 guide finds its target and a cut occurs, but the investigator supplies no repair template. Why is a precise prescribed base substitution not guaranteed?

**Model answer:** The cut only initiates repair. Without a suitable template, NHEJ generally makes variable indels rather than copying a specified substitution.

**Misconception to avoid:** Attributing the final written DNA sequence to guide RNA ignores cellular repair.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, answer in 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain CRISPR-Cas9 targeting and distinguish two DNA-repair outcomes relevant to crop editing.

**Model response (approximately 112 words):** A guide RNA directs Cas9 to a complementary genomic sequence adjoining a suitable PAM. Cas9 creates a double-strand break, but the cell determines the edit when repairing it. Non-homologous end joining may introduce small variable insertions or deletions, potentially disabling a gene. Homology-directed repair uses a supplied sequence as a template to produce a specified change or targeted insertion, though this is often less efficient in plants. A guide alone cannot dictate a precise new allele. India's rice-editing work illustrates the potential to improve crops without leaving foreign DNA in the final plant. Nevertheless, both off-target changes and unexpected on-target outcomes require molecular characterisation and trait-specific field assessment.

**Scoring guide (10):** guide/PAM/Cas9 sequence (3); correct NHEJ result (2); HDR donor and precision qualification (2); relevant Indian example (1); safety limitation and assessment (2).

| SDN-3 | Gene-sized donor at selected locus | Substantial insertion can be cisgenic **or** transgenic | Not within that exemption; GMO-type scrutiny applies |
| Base/prime editing | Modified enzymes, often no double-strand break | Characterise the *actual final product* | Do not assume classification solely from tool name |

*The legal fork concerns the edit category and absence of exogenous introduced DNA, not a magical “CRISPR exemption”.*

```text
No donor → SDN-1 (indel) ┐
Short donor → SDN-2       ├→ check final line for exogenous introduced DNA
Gene-sized donor → SDN-3 ┘     └→ first two may qualify for the 2022 exception
```

*Both the size of the change and the final DNA matter.*

**Reconstruct the rule.** The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 supports the 1989 GMO Rules. The MoEF&CC Office Memorandum of **30 March 2022** exempted specified **SDN-1 and SDN-2 genome-edited plants free of exogenously introduced DNA from Rules 7–11** of those Rules. DBT notified the **Guidelines for Safety Assessment of Genome Edited Plants** on **17 May 2022**, as PIB explained on 19 July 2022. “Exempt from Rules 7–11” is not “unregulated in all respects”: prior characterisation, relevant institutional and seed/food/agricultural requirements, and product assessment questions must be considered on their own terms. An editing reagent may have been present during development but must not be mistaken for permanent foreign DNA in the final eligible line.

**Biological test case.** ICAR on **4 April 2025** announced genome-edited rice **DRR Rice 100 (Kamla)**, developed from Samba Mahsuri, and **Pusa DST Rice 1**, developed from MTU 1010, without adding foreign DNA. The former was described as having more grains per panicle and earlier maturity; the latter as improving performance on saline/alkaline soils. These are *announced varieties*, not proof here of acreage, seed availability everywhere, farm-income change, or approval of transgenic rice. Their trait performance must still be interpreted against controls and field conditions.

**The difficult boundary.** A donor from a sexually compatible relative inserted as a *gene-sized sequence* remains SDN-3; absence of an unrelated-species transgene alone does not convert that outcome into SDN-2. Conversely a small edit does not automatically count as exempt if exogenous introduced DNA remains. In a Mains answer, identify the molecular outcome and regulatory stage before inferring risk. India's partial product-sensitive exception exists within an otherwise process-oriented GMO scheme; other jurisdictions' rules and import tolerances may differ, creating traceability and trade questions.

**Objection → reply → residual.** Opponents worry that lighter review overlooks ecological effects of even precise traits. Proponents note that a small endogenous change can resemble a conventionally occurring mutation. A defensible middle path verifies sequence and absence of residual foreign DNA, then tests the trait's plausible exposure pathways; equivalence of sequence is not automatic equivalence of field outcome.

**UPSC use.** Distinguish SDN class, donor, residual DNA and rule number; connect public-sector rice work with food and soil constraints without inventing adoption data. A probable Prelims trap is to call all genome editing SDN-1 or every SDN-3 transgenic.

**Revision notes:** 30 March 2022 OM; 17 May 2022 DBT guidelines; 19 July 2022 PIB explanation; exemption specifically Rules 7–11 and qualifying SDN-1/2; SDN-1 NHEJ; SDN-2 short donor; SDN-3 gene-sized insertion even if cisgenic; 4 April 2025 two ICAR rice varieties; “announced” ≠ “widespread cultivation”.

### Concept check

**Question:** A targeted edit inserts an intact gene from a sexually compatible rice relative. Does lack of an unrelated-species donor by itself secure India's SDN-1/SDN-2 exemption?

**Model answer:** No. A gene-sized targeted insertion is SDN-3 even when cisgenic; the exemption is for qualifying SDN-1/SDN-2 products without exogenous introduced DNA.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating “not transgenic” with “SDN-2” ignores insertion size and regulatory classification.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, answer in 250 words

**Mains question:** Critically examine India's differentiated treatment of genome-edited plants after 2022.

**Model response (approximately 168 words):** Genome editing is not a single outcome. SDN-1 commonly produces small indels without a donor; SDN-2 uses a short template for a defined edit, while SDN-3 inserts a gene-sized sequence, whether cisgenic or transgenic. MoEF&CC's 30 March 2022 memorandum exempted eligible SDN-1/2 plants without exogenously introduced DNA from Rules 7–11 of the 1989 Rules, and DBT's 17 May 2022 guidelines set out safety-assessment procedures. This differentiation can help public breeders improve locally relevant traits: ICAR announced DRR Rice 100 (Kamla) and Pusa DST Rice 1 in April 2025 without adding foreign DNA. Yet a targeted edit can still yield unintended molecular effects, and an intended trait may alter ecological exposure or trade treatment. Exemption from specified GMO provisions does not prove no oversight is needed, nor does a variety announcement establish broad seed access. India should confirm final-product identity, use trait-specific risk pathways and transparent performance evidence, and distinguish breeding release, food rules and market access. Proportionate regulation improves innovation only when its category boundaries and safeguards remain credible.

**Scoring guide (15):** accurate three SDN mechanisms (4); two legal dates, condition and Rules 7–11 (4); named varieties and restrained status (2); ecological/trade critique (2); practicable differentiated safeguards and judgement (3).

## Lesson 6 — Who gives which permission?

Book context: not available.

```text
Advisory framework (RDAC, DBT)
         ↓
Institutional work (IBSC) → research / contained trials (RCGM, DBT)
         ↓
Proposed field / environmental release → GEAC, MoEF&CC
         ↓
Conditional monitoring (SBCC at state + DLC at district)
         ↓
Agronomic evaluation / varietal or seed pathway (ICAR-linked as applicable)
         ↓
Commercial cultivation only where separately authorised

```

*Arrows show stages, not a claim that every application gets every permission automatically.*

**Start from risk exposure.** A contained experiment has different opportunities for environmental spread than a farmer's open-field crop. Hence the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** and the **1989 Rules on hazardous microorganisms/genetically engineered organisms or cells** use staged supervision. The Recombinant DNA Advisory Committee (**RDAC**) advises under DBT; an Institutional Biosafety Committee (**IBSC**) checks laboratory work; the Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation (**RCGM**, DBT) oversees research and contained stages. The Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (**GEAC**, MoEF&CC) appraises large-scale use and environmental release; it can deal with field proposals—so “field trial” is not inherently identical to contained lab work. State Biotechnology Coordination Committees (**SBCC**) and District Level Committees (**DLC**) support subnational monitoring. Assigning every field exposure to RCGM or all crop decisions to GEAC is wrong.

**The parallel track.** The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (**FSSAI**) acts under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 for food safety and relevant labelling. The 2024 Supreme Court common **section 23** direction specifically addressed **imported GM food, particularly edible oil**; it was not a blanket labelling order for all genetically engineered crops. FSSAI is not another name for GEAC; environmental release alone does not decide whether a food product meets a separate food-law requirement. ICAR-related varietal/agronomic testing and seed systems concern performance and deployment, not retroactive substitution for biosafety clearance.

**Read an application file in stages.** A proposal, an expert request for more data, authorisation for a confined field trial, environmental-release recommendation, conditional environmental release, seed production/testing and ordinary commercial cultivation are distinguishable events. GEAC's **159th (20 March 2026)** and **161st (14 July 2026)** meeting minutes record requests for additional India-specific data and further expert consideration for BGII Roundup Ready Flex HT cotton. This procedural status is not commercial approval. Likewise DMH-11's environmental-release history cannot be collapsed into commercial authorisation.

**Objection → reply → residual.** A multi-body framework can produce fragmented responsibility and delay. Stage-specific written conditions, public reasons, interoperable monitoring and independent conflict checks provide a reply. Coordination does not dispense with evidence on actual field performance or give states a single magical veto in every legal situation; distinguish statutory competence from political acceptance.

**UPSC use.** In GS-III, write a stage-wise institutional map and illustrate with one crop. The 2019 Mains farmer-welfare PYQ (Lesson 2) needs this as one limiting dimension, not as an entire answer. Prelims traps: GEAC ≠ DBT; RCGM ≠ commercial clearance; FSSAI ≠ environmental biosafety body.

**Revision notes:** EPA 1986 → Rules 1989; RDAC advice; IBSC institution; RCGM–DBT research; GEAC–MoEF&CC environmental release/large scale; SBCC state; DLC district; ICAR agronomic stage; FSSAI food track; 2024 Court s.23 imported GM food, particularly edible oil, not all crops; trial ≠ environmental release ≠ seed/testing ≠ commercial cultivation.

### Concept check

**Question:** A company receives permission for a crop field trial and later reports promising yields. Which two further conclusions cannot be drawn from those facts alone?

**Model answer:** Neither unrestricted environmental/commercial release nor food-law clearance follows automatically; each depends on its relevant subsequent appraisal and legal requirements.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating positive trial performance as an approval or as a substitute for environmental and food assessment.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, answer in 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain the roles of RCGM, GEAC and FSSAI in the regulation of an engineered food crop in India.

**Model response (approximately 121 words):** The 1989 Rules under the Environment (Protection) Act provide staged oversight. RCGM, located in DBT, handles research and contained-stage review; GEAC, under MoEF&CC, appraises environmental release and large-scale use, including relevant field proposals. FSSAI operates a distinct food-safety and labelling track under the Food Safety and Standards Act. Institutional and state/district bodies also supervise work and monitoring. DMH-11 illustrates why conditional environmental release for seed production and testing is not the same as unrestricted commercial cultivation. The Supreme Court's 2024 common section 23 direction concerned imported GM food, particularly edible oil; it did not order labelling of every GM crop. Therefore a satisfactory framework must track what was approved, by whom and for which stage; it must publish evidence and monitor outcomes after release.

## Lesson 7 — Deciding benefits and risks without false certainty

Progress: 7/8 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: ecological mechanisms, farmer distribution, regulatory philosophy

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available.
CA search: "GM crop food feed environmental risk assessment sequence India".
CA found: No new development beyond the 4 April 2025 ICAR rice announcement.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Question | Specific pathway to investigate | Strong response | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can the trait spread? | Pollen/gene flow to compatible relatives | Reproductive ecology, distance and monitoring | Local variation |
| Will protection weaken? | Selection of resistant pests or weeds | Refuge/IPM or herbicide stewardship | Compliance and evolution |
| Could others be affected? | Exposure of non-target organisms or biodiversity | Targeted field/ecotoxicology studies | Long-term uncertainty |
| Who captures gains? | Seed price, licensing, credit, labour and market access | Public breeding, choice, extension | Unequal bargaining power |
| Can it be traded? | Different importing-country definitions and traceability | Product characterisation, export compliance | Detection of subtle edits |

*Each claimed benefit or hazard becomes testable only when linked to a trait, exposure and comparator.*

```text
Intended trait → measurable benefit → farmer's net return
       └──────→ molecular and ecological exposure → mitigation → residual risk
```

*Neither the benefit chain nor the risk chain can be replaced by a technology label.*

**Extend the farm-benefit test from Lesson 2.** Bt cotton's target-pest channel, tissue-cultured planting stock and microbial inoculants have different comparators; edited rice addresses another constraint again. A policy evaluation now asks *which regions and farmers* can afford planting material, withstand variable yield and sell produce at a remunerative price. Compare avoided losses, input costs, yield variability and market prices against locally suitable alternatives; pooling unlike traits into one “GM success/failure” number would conceal both winners and losers.

**Ecological chain.** Pollen moves only where compatible recipients and routes exist; assess the crop's reproductive biology and landscape before claiming either inevitable contamination or zero gene flow. Bt trait exposure can select resistant insects; herbicide-tolerance deployment can intensify selection for resistant weeds, alter weed ecology and affect weeding labour depending on actual practice. A laboratory off-target edit and a field ecological impact are different propositions: sequence characterisation screens the former, relevant field studies examine the latter. Precision narrows some uncertainties but never pre-answers a separate risk pathway.

**Use one ordered risk-assessment sequence.** Begin with problem formulation: define the crop, trait, receiving environment, intended use and plausible harm pathways. Then characterise the inserted or edited locus, expression and inheritance. For food and feed, compare key nutrients, anti-nutrients and other composition with an appropriate comparator; assess the newly expressed product or altered constituent for toxicity and allergenicity; estimate dietary exposure; and integrate nutritional consequences rather than treating substantial compositional difference as proof of harm by itself. For environmental assessment, examine persistence or invasiveness, gene flow to compatible recipients, effects on target and non-target organisms, soil or biogeochemical interactions, resistance evolution and consequences of the associated management practice. Finally characterise risk by combining hazard with exposure, prescribe mitigation such as refuge or use conditions, and plan post-release monitoring where residual uncertainty warrants it.

```text
problem formulation
  → molecular and inheritance characterisation
  → food/feed: composition → toxicity → allergenicity → dietary exposure/nutrition
  → environment: persistence → gene flow → target/non-target → soil → resistance/management
  → risk characterisation
  → mitigation, decision conditions and monitoring
```

**The regulation argument.** A *process-based* system flags how an organism was made; a *product-based* system emphasises the final organism's characteristics. India's GMO Rules begin with engineered-organism oversight; the 2022 qualifying SDN-1/2 exemption creates a narrower differentiated route. The strongest pro-innovation case is faster, less burdensome improvement, including public-sector lines. The strongest critical case is that a light-touch category may miss consequential traits or on-target effects and undermine trust if records are opaque. A reasoned reply is a proportionate, transparent assessment keyed to molecular outcome **and** actual trait exposure, with public reasons, conflict safeguards, monitoring and farmer access.

**International bridge.** The Cartagena Protocol focuses on biosafety in transboundary movement of living modified organisms, with advance informed agreement and risk-assessment/precaution dimensions. The Nagoya Protocol addresses access to genetic resources and fair benefit sharing, a *different* question. India's Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (amended 2023) and National Biodiversity Authority belong in the access/benefit-sharing frame; none of these instruments independently grants a crop commercial approval. Domestic seed governance and transparent communication shape public trust alongside international trade compliance.

**UPSC extension:** The **2019 GS-III Q16** farm-welfare demand and unsolved answer approach are taught in Core Lesson 2. At the advanced level, test whether a positive plot-level result survives across smallholder seed prices, labour changes, geographic exposure, export rules and long-term stewardship; these qualifications strengthen a Discussion answer without replacing the basic benefit chain.

**Revision notes:** trait + comparator + exposure + beneficiary; problem formulation precedes testing; molecular identity/inheritance precede food/feed and environmental branches; food/feed sequence = composition → toxicity → allergenicity → exposure/nutrition; environmental sequence = persistence/invasiveness → gene flow → target/non-target → soil → resistance/management; hazard + exposure → risk characterisation; mitigation and monitoring address residual risk; farmer welfare includes seed cost and markets; 2022 exception is conditional; Cartagena ≠ Nagoya.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can genome sequencing be necessary but insufficient evidence for a claim that an edited salt-tolerant rice variety will benefit all Indian farmers?

**Model answer:** It helps verify the edit and unwanted genomic changes; it cannot establish locally variable field performance, seed access, costs or prices.

**Misconception to avoid:** Collapsing molecular precision into proven agronomic and distributive outcomes.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, answer in 250 words

**Mains question:** “The appropriate unit of GM-crop policy is a trait, its exposure pathway and its beneficiary—not the label of the technology.” Critically analyse with Indian examples.

**Model response (approximately 201 words):** Crop technologies vary in mechanism and consequences. Bt cotton expresses *Bacillus thuringiensis* Cry proteins against susceptible bollworms; its benefit depends on pest pressure, refuge compliance and resistance surveillance. DMH-11 uses *barnase–barstar* to enable mustard hybridisation and a *bar* selection marker; its conditional environmental-release decision became the subject of the Supreme Court's split 2024 judgment, not settled commercial permission. ICAR announced two foreign-DNA-free genome-edited rice varieties in 2025, but an announcement alone cannot measure farm access or income. Risks must follow plausible pathways: pollen transfer where compatible recipients exist, selection of resistant pests or weeds, potential unintended genomic outcomes, and consequences for seed choice and labour. Critics correctly insist on long-term ecological and conflict-of-interest safeguards; advocates correctly resist equating a small endogenous edit with every foreign-gene insertion. Publish comparable field evidence, apply proportionate testing and post-release stewardship, and assess seed cost and regional performance. Innovation is defensible only when the intended public benefit is observed and those bearing the risk can scrutinise it.

|---|---|---|
| RNAi can silence an mRNA | Can the signal reach its intended cells without silencing other transcripts? | In-vitro knockdown ≠ clinically effective delivery |

*First identify the mechanism in Lesson 4, then match the evidence and ethical safeguard to the person or organism exposed.*

```text
Core mechanism and biological target (Lesson 4)
                ↓
Human exposure / descendants / donor contribution
                ↓
Evidence, independent ethics review and legally permitted use
```

*The advanced inquiry begins after—not instead of—the core mechanism.*

**From mechanism to evidence.** RNAi silences messenger RNA without necessarily editing a genome (Lesson 4); designing a target sequence does not itself solve tissue delivery, duration or unintended transcript suppression. A microsatellite marker may track relatedness; even a strong association with a condition cannot, without additional causal evidence, be called a therapeutic target. An artificial chromosome may carry DNA but needs separate evidence of stable maintenance and controlled expression. Different laboratory successes have different clinically important failure modes.

**What the reproductive distinction costs.** Pronuclear transfer aims to reduce a specifically mitochondrial disease risk; small amounts of affected mitochondria may persist, so follow-up and clear discussion of donor contribution matter. Editing the *nuclear* genome in an embryo raises a different possibility: mosaicism (not every developing cell carries the same edit) and unintended changes may be inherited if germ cells are affected. An adult's somatic-cell treatment usually confines direct genome changes to that recipient, though long-term safety and unequal access still deserve study. These are distinct uncertainty profiles; no assertion that one is lawfully available clinically in India follows from feasibility.

**Objection → reply → residual.** A proponent argues that precise intervention could prevent serious inherited illness. The strongest objection concerns uncertain off-target or mosaic changes and the inability of future persons to consent. Rigorous preclinical evidence, independent ethics review and applicable law are necessary; this answer does not imply any Indian clinical authorisation. Similarly, importing the Nagoya access/benefit-sharing framework into clinical consent or the Cartagena living-modified-organism regime into every medical procedure confuses separate legal purposes.

**UPSC use beyond the Core routes.** The answer-neutral mechanism and unsolved approaches for **2019 Prelims GS-I Q93/Q96, 2020 Q37/Q44, 2021 Q66 and 2023 Q70** appear in Lesson 4 (Q93 also in Lesson 1). Here ask an additional question when an assertion sounds overly confident: does the proposed intervention change a marker, somatic cell, cytoplasm or heritable embryo genome, and what evidence or consent would make its *use* acceptable? No objective answer key or clinical authorisation is implied.

**Revision notes:** RNAi delivery and transcript specificity; marker correlation ≠ causation; artificial-chromosome maintenance needs testing; pronuclear transfer can leave residual affected mitochondria and raises donor-disclosure questions; embryo nuclear editing can cause mosaicism and affect descendants; somatic treatment ≠ inheritable germline change; safety evidence ≠ ethical consent ≠ legal clinical permission; Cartagena ≠ Nagoya.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would evidence that pronuclear transfer may reduce a mitochondrial-disease risk fail to prove that it cures a disorder caused solely by a nuclear mutation?

**Model answer:** It changes the cytoplasmic mitochondrial environment while transferring the intended parents' nuclear pronuclei; a disease-causing nuclear allele remains unless independently addressed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating all inherited conditions as mitochondrial or all reproductive technologies as CRISPR editing.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, answer in 250 words

**Mains question:** Differentiate RNA interference, embryo genome editing and pronuclear transfer by biological target and ethical consequence.

**Model response (approximately 152 words):** RNA interference uses small RNAs to reduce expression of a complementary messenger RNA; it usually regulates expression rather than rewriting DNA. Embryo genome editing can change a nuclear genomic sequence at an early developmental stage, potentially affecting future descendants. Pronuclear transfer relocates the intended parents' nuclear pronuclei to donor cytoplasm with healthier mitochondria to reduce specified maternally transmitted mitochondrial-disease risks; it does not cure a nuclear-gene disorder. The biological target therefore determines both the appropriate evidence and the ethical problem. RNAi applications must demonstrate effective, specific delivery. Embryo editing raises off-target and mosaicism risks, germline consent and long-term oversight; pronuclear transfer raises residual-mitochondrial and donor-contribution questions. Clinical feasibility is not regulatory authorisation. A sound Indian policy discussion requires distinct safety endpoints, independent ethical review and truthful information to affected families rather than treating all three as one “gene-editing” intervention.

**Scoring guide (15):** RNAi target/mechanism (3); embryo hereditary mechanism (3); accurate pronuclear/mitochondrial mechanism (3); distinct evidence and ethical risks (4); legal-feasibility boundary and conclusion (2).

4. **Question:** Can a small edit be called automatically harmless because no foreign DNA remains? **Model answer:** No; molecular characterisation and plausible ecological/food exposure depend on the product and trait. **Repair:** Distinguish regulatory exemption from zero-risk proof.
5. **Question:** Would a successful gene edit and a successful variety announcement together demonstrate that FSSAI has approved a food product? **Model answer:** No; biological result, varietal/agronomic stage and food-law requirements are separate. **Repair:** Name the competent body and the exact decision.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These are **new practice questions**, not PYQs; model answers are separate from the unsolved PYQ approaches above.

### 10 marks — answer in 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish genetic modification, targeted genome editing and RNA interference using an Indian crop application.

**Model answer (approximately 113 words):** Recombinant-DNA modification introduces a designed construct into a plant; Bt cotton expresses bacterial Cry proteins that act against susceptible bollworms. Targeted genome editing changes a chosen genomic site: a guide and Cas protein can initiate a cut, and cellular repair determines whether an indel or template-directed change results. ICAR announced two genome-edited rice varieties without added foreign DNA in April 2025. RNA interference instead guides suppression of messenger RNA and need not alter the underlying genomic sequence. These mechanisms have different evidentiary questions. Bt cotton needs pest-resistance stewardship; an edited rice line needs sequence and trait-performance characterisation. India's conditional 2022 exemption concerns qualifying SDN-1/2 products without exogenous DNA, not every CRISPR-derived product. Molecular identity alone does not establish field benefit.

**Scoring guide (10):** three non-conflated mechanisms (5); accurately dated crop applications (2); correct conditional legal scope (2); measured limitation (1).

### 15 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate how India's biotechnology regulation can encourage crop improvement without treating biosafety as a one-time permission.

**Model answer (approximately 175 words):** Crop innovation should be judged by a named trait and its exposure. Bt cotton's Cry proteins can reduce injury by susceptible bollworms, but resistance and secondary-pest dynamics require refuges and monitoring. DMH-11's *barnase–barstar* system facilitates mustard hybridisation; the 2023 government explanation referred to conditional environmental release for seed and testing before commercial release, followed by a divided Supreme Court judgment in 2024. ICAR's 2025 announcement of foreign-DNA-free edited rice shows a different route; its field distribution and income effects should not be presumed. Under the 1989 Rules, RCGM oversees research/contained stages and GEAC environmental release; FSSAI handles food-law questions. The 2022 exception from Rules 7–11 for eligible SDN-1/2 products differentiates small edits from gene-sized SDN-3 insertions. Critics reasonably demand independent trials, transparent conflicts management and monitoring of gene flow and resistance. Proponents reasonably note that excessive identical burdens on unlike products can hinder public breeding. Publish comparable trait-specific evidence, enforce stewardship and labelling duties where applicable, and assess farmer seed access. Regulation remains credible when follow-up evidence can revise an earlier decision.

**Scoring guide (15):** three cases with correct stage/status (4); regulatory division/exemption (4); two biosafety pathways and responses (3); innovation critique and counterargument (2); adaptive policy verdict (2).

### 20 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** “Molecular precision cannot stand in for institutional credibility.” Discuss for GM crops and genome-edited plants in India.

**Model answer (approximately 217 words):** CRISPR-Cas targeting can locate a genomic sequence beside a PAM, but NHEJ yields variable indels and HDR depends on a donor template. Even a verified intended edit leaves questions about trait performance and ecological exposure. Classical transgenics present different pathways: Bt cotton's *cry* proteins require resistance management, while DMH-11's *barnase–barstar* system raises questions about mustard hybridisation, gene flow and the *bar* selection marker. Regulatory credibility therefore cannot be inferred from the precision of a laboratory instrument. India separates RCGM's research remit from GEAC's environmental appraisal and FSSAI's food-law obligations. Its March–May 2022 framework exempts eligible foreign-DNA-free SDN-1/2 plants from Rules 7–11 of the 1989 Rules; it does not give all edited plants blanket clearance. The 2024 Supreme Court split on DMH-11 and common directions on national consultation, conflicts of interest and section 23 labelling **for imported GM food, particularly edible oil** underline why a decision's procedure and scope matter. A critic fears that lighter oversight misses subtle harm; an innovator fears that identical barriers for dissimilar outcomes slow public crop improvement. Disclose molecular characterisation and comparable field data, apply exposure-proportionate testing and accessible monitoring, and account for seed prices, state-level acceptance and trade rules. Institutional trust rests on contestable evidence and stage-specific, revisable permissions.

**Scoring guide (20):** molecular mechanism and precision limitation (4); distinct India crop mechanisms (4); exact institutional and 2022 distinctions (4); split-verdict nuance/common directions (3); opposing cases with matched safeguards (3); coherent judgement (2).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Molecular routes and editable outcomes

- **DNA → expression → trait:** environment changes phenotype; do not infer farm impact from an edit alone.
- **rDNA construction:** restriction enzymes cut recognised sequences; DNA ligase joins fragments. A plant vector normally links promoter → gene of interest → terminator, with a selectable marker and required replication/transfer elements.
- **Complete GM workflow:** design/assemble construct → deliver by *Agrobacterium* or biolistics → transform competent cells → select candidates → regenerate callus/shoot/root into a fertile plant → validate locus, copy number, integrity and residual vector DNA → test expression, trait and inheritance.
- **Conventional hybrid:** crosses sexually compatible parents, reshuffling many variants. Selection identifies candidate transformed cells; it does not itself prove correct insertion or stable inheritance.
- **Transgenic:** unrelated-species DNA; **cisgenic:** intact gene from compatible pool; **intragenic:** recombined compatible-pool elements. A crop may be simultaneously **hybrid and transgenic**.
- **CRISPR-Cas9:** guide RNA targets sequence adjacent to PAM; Cas9 cuts; NHEJ makes variable indels; HDR plus donor specifies a template-directed edit. Cargo may be DNA, RNA or RNP; transformation efficiency, genotype/explant dependence, regeneration, mosaicism and stable germline inheritance are practical bottlenecks. Base/prime editing can avoid a double-strand break.
- **RNAi:** mRNA silencing rather than necessary DNA editing; **microsatellite:** short-repeat variation marker; **artificial chromosome:** DNA carrier.
- **Core exam chain:** 2019 GS-III Q16 (15 marks; 250 words) needs mechanism-linked Bt, tissue-culture, microbial-input or edited-crop examples → local comparator → seed/input cost and output price → conditional farm income. For 2020 Q37/Q44, 2021 Q66 and 2023 Q70, first distinguish nuclear from mitochondrial material, embryo from somatic treatment, and marker from causal gene; then consider ethics (Lessons 2/4 and 8).

### Three Indian crop cases — never exchange their statuses

- **Bt cotton:** bacterial Cry1Ac/Cry2Ab target susceptible lepidopteran larvae; Bollgard II stacks traits. Selection drives resistance → refuge + IPM + monitoring. PIB's **6 August 2024** statement identifies it as India's only commercially approved GM crop **on that date**; no later nationwide restatement established here.
- **DMH-11:** self-pollinating mustard → *barnase* male sterility + *barstar* fertility restoration; *bar* glufosinate-tolerance selection marker. GEAC **18 October 2022**, Union **25 October 2022**, PIB **7 February 2023** conditional environmental release for seed/testing before commercial release. Supreme Court **23 July 2024** split: Nagarathna J. quashed recommendation/decision; Karol J. did not. Common policy-consultation and conflict directions; FSS Act **s.23** direction is specifically about **imported GM food, particularly edible oil**, not a blanket crop-label rule. Question referred to an **appropriate Bench**. No later decisive order established here.
- **Bt brinjal:** **9 February 2010** release moratorium; dated restraint, not proved perpetual ban.
- **Edited rice:** ICAR **4 April 2025** announced **DRR Rice 100 (Kamla)** from Samba Mahsuri and **Pusa DST Rice 1** from MTU 1010 without added foreign DNA; first described with more grains/earlier maturity, second with saline/alkaline-soil performance. Variety announcement ≠ nationwide seed availability or verified farmer returns.
- **2026 HT cotton:** GEAC's 159th and 161st meeting minutes record additional data/expert consideration of BGII Roundup Ready Flex, **not commercial approval**.

### Law, institutional ladder and judgment traps

- **EPA 1986 → GMO Rules 1989.** RDAC = DBT advisory; IBSC = institution; RCGM = DBT research/contained work; GEAC = MoEF&CC environmental appraisal; SBCC/DLC = state/district monitoring.
- **MoEF&CC OM 30 March 2022 + DBT guidelines 17 May 2022**: qualifying SDN-1 (no donor/NHEJ) and SDN-2 (short template) products **without exogenous introduced DNA** exempt from **Rules 7–11**, not all laws; SDN-3 gene-sized insertion, including cisgenic, stays outside.
- **FSSAI** handles food safety and relevant labelling under **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006**; the 2024 Court's specific s.23 direction concerns **imported GM food, particularly edible oil**. ICAR-linked agronomic/varietal decisions and field trials do not substitute for GEAC/FSSAI functions.
- **Order of permission:** contained trial → authorised field exposure → conditional environmental release → seed/testing/agronomic decisions → separate commercial status. None of these words is interchangeable.
- **Cartagena:** biosafety/LMO transboundary movement; **Nagoya:** genetic-resource access and benefit sharing; **Biological Diversity Act 2002**, amended 2023, and **NBA** relate to the latter domestically.

### Evaluation and answer spine

- **Food/feed risk path:** problem formulation → molecular/inheritance characterisation → comparative composition → toxicity → allergenicity → dietary exposure and nutritional assessment → integrated risk characterisation.
- **Environmental risk path:** receiving environment → persistence/invasiveness → gene flow where compatible relatives exist → target/non-target effects → soil interactions → pest/weed resistance and management effects → mitigation and monitoring.
- **Decision rule:** hazard must be combined with plausible exposure; molecular off-target findings and ecological effects are distinct questions.
- **Farmer path:** trait → suitable comparator → avoided damage or improved tolerance → seed/input costs → prices, labour and access → net welfare. Public breeding, extension, seed sovereignty and traceability matter.
- **2018 Q63/Q64; 2019 Q93/Q96/Q99/Q16; 2020 Q37/Q44; 2021 Q66/Q67; 2023 Q70:** exact neutral wording precedes each unsolved approach; identify the specific mechanism without turning the approach into a revealed key. **No confirmed directly relevant 2024–26 PYQ**; 2026 GS-III Q5 addresses processing-stage biotechnology rather than GM-crop development.
- **10 marks:** mechanism + example + one limiting condition. **15 marks:** compare pathways + evidence + two counterarguments. **20 marks:** molecular mechanism → distinct Indian cases/status → staged institutions → ecological/equity critique and reply → proportionate qualified verdict.
