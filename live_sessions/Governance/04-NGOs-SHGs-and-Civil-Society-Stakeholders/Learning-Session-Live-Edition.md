## Lesson 1 — Outside government does not mean outside governance

Progress: 1/8 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Civil society, NGOs, SHGs and responsibility

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Actor | What holds it together | Its characteristic contribution | Accountability question |
|---|---|---|---|
| NGO / voluntary organisation | Organised nonprofit activity, potentially incorporated as a trust, society or section 8 company | Service, advocacy or research | To users as well as donors? |
| SHG | Members' voluntary pooled savings and regular collective action | Mutual support, credit access, livelihoods | Can every member speak and control her income? |
| Cooperative | Voluntary member-owned enterprise with a distinct legal form and democratic control | Joint production, credit, procurement or marketing | Is control genuinely member-oriented rather than manager- or elite-dominated? |
| Community-based organisation (CBO) | Residents organised around a locality, identity or shared local problem; formal or informal | Last-mile knowledge, mutual aid, monitoring and voice | Are non-members and weaker local groups heard? |
| Movement, union, residents' association, faith-based body | Shared cause, constituency or place; may be formal or informal | Voice, bargaining, scrutiny | Whom does it really represent? |
| Public authority | Law, budget and public mandate | Entitlement, equitable coverage, enforceable remedy | Who can be compelled to correct failure? |

*Civil society is wider than NGOs; organisation, funding and public powers vary even where all these actors work on the same problem.*

A remote village needs a health service. A volunteer may tell families how to obtain it; a charitable trust may operate a clinic; an SHG may bring women's experiences to a village meeting. None thereby becomes the state official answerable for a statutory entitlement. **Civil society** names the sphere of collective activity outside formal government (and distinct from a profit-seeking business), including NGOs but also associations and informal collectives. The boundary can be mixed: an NGO may receive a public grant without becoming a government department.

**A self-help group** is a small, voluntary savings and mutual-action group, predominantly of women in India's livelihoods programmes. It is not itself a bank, MFI, NGO or incorporated company. An **NGO** describes organisational purpose and practice rather than one exclusive legal form. A **CBO** is organised from within a community and is defined chiefly by its local constituency, not by one mandatory incorporation form. A **cooperative** is a member-owned economic institution: depending on its territorial operation, it is governed under the applicable state cooperative law or the Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002. Its surplus-producing enterprise and member control distinguish it from a donor-led NGO, even when both pursue development. Article 43B directs the State to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies; this does not turn every cooperative into a government body. Nor does a **pressure group** necessarily seek elected governmental power: unlike a political party, its defining aim is to influence decisions.

| NGO legal vehicle | Controlling source | Governance feature | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public charitable trust** | Trust deed, applicable State public-trust/endowment law and general law; Section 1 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 excludes public/private religious or charitable endowments | Trustee-held purpose and deed-defined administration | One uniform national Trusts Act incorporation route |
| **Registered society** | Societies Registration Act, 1860 or applicable State variant | Membership, general body and governing body | Section 8 company status or FCRA permission |
| **Section 8 company** | Companies Act, 2013, Section 8 | Companies Act governance; income/profits applied to objects; no dividend to members | Automatic CSR implementing eligibility, tax approval or grant sanction |

*The legal vehicle answers how the body is constituted; funding permission, tax status, CSR implementing status and public accountability remain separate.*

**Mechanism:** scattered citizens → collective organisation → information, trust or voice → contact with banks, administrators or other groups → influence over delivery or decisions. ⚠️ This chain is possible, not guaranteed. An NGO paid by a district to help families file health claims can improve access; it cannot eliminate the district's legal duty to fund, supervise and provide a grievance route. An informal women's collective may represent people missed by the contractor, though an organised group's members are not necessarily the poorest residents.

**Objection:** Why involve non-state organisations if elected government has the mandate? **Reply:** local knowledge, experimentation and an independent channel for complaints can correct blind spots. **Residual:** an unelected actor claiming to speak for everyone must disclose whom it represents and how excluded residents can challenge it. Collaboration improves governance only if public responsibility remains identifiable.

**UPSC use:** The GS-II development-processes clause expressly includes NGOs, SHGs, groups, donors, charities and other stakeholders. Begin any answer by naming the actor and distinguishing legal duty from assistance; do not turn “civil society” into a synonym for “NGO”. A probable unfamiliar-actor stem can be decoded by asking: structure, funding, representation and accountability. Next, ask what a group itself *produces* before any grant or bank loan arrives.

### Revision notes

- Civil society is wider than NGOs and includes formal and informal collective action outside government.
- An NGO is a purpose-and-practice category, not one exclusive incorporation form.
- An SHG builds savings and mutual action; it is not an MFI or automatically an incorporated NGO.
- Public charitable trust, registered society and Section 8 company are legally distinct vehicles.
- The Indian Trusts Act, 1882 is not a uniform national incorporation statute for public charitable trusts.
- A CBO is rooted in a community constituency and must still answer for exclusion within that community.
- A cooperative is a member-owned economic enterprise governed by applicable cooperative law, not a donor-led charity.
- A pressure group seeks influence; a political party seeks governmental office.
- A delivery partner may assist an entitlement, but the public authority retains universal duty and remedy.
- Classify an unfamiliar stakeholder by structure, finance, representation and accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** If a charity operates a publicly funded clinic, what makes it part of governance without making it the state?

**Model answer:** The charity participates in delivery and may supply local knowledge, but the public authority retains the mandate to secure coverage, supervise quality and remedy wrongful exclusion; a grant does not turn the charity into an elected or statutory duty-holder.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Non-government” describes institutional location, not absence of public accountability for work done with public money.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish NGOs, SHGs and other civil-society groups in the governance of an Indian village. Why does the distinction matter for accountability?

**Model (approximately 113 words):** Civil society comprises organised collective activity outside government; an NGO is one form, not the whole sphere. A women's SHG builds mutual savings and livelihood capacity, while a residents' association or movement may articulate a shared claim. In a hypothetical district health programme an NGO can help households reach a clinic, and an SHG can report access barriers. Their different memberships and funding create different representation and accountability tests. Neither a grant nor partnership transfers the state's obligation to ensure eligible residents receive a service and a remedy. The district must identify a responsible officer and hear non-members, including people excluded from every group. Civic participation helps delivery when it complements—not displaces—public duty.

**Scoring guide (10):** actor distinctions 3; specific Indian delivery mechanism 3; representation and public-duty distinction 3; qualified conclusion 1.

                 SHG  ←────────→  BANK

*The arrows show enabling conditions. NABARD, DAY-NRLM and DAY-NULM answer different questions; the shared abbreviation CLF has different rural and urban meanings.*

Imagine that ten women can each save a little but cannot individually persuade a bank to transact with them. Regular meetings establish a reliable record and mutual confidence; cooperation between groups improves scale; a federation can then negotiate with a bank or government office. **Social capital** means usable networks, trust and reciprocity, not money in the account. It can help acquire *financial* capital without becoming financial capital.

In the vocabulary commonly used with **Putnam's social-capital account**, **bonding** describes ties within a relatively similar group, **bridging** ties across groups, and **linking** vertical ties to institutions with authority or resources. These are analytical categories, not a law that every federation progresses in sequence. A woman who keeps savings records gains a skill; a rural village organisation (VO) might coordinate several SHGs; a rural cluster-level federation may bring collective demands to a bank. An urban area-level federation and city-level federation perform a different mission-specific aggregation role. A group can also visit a **Gram Sabha** or an urban local body to request a service correction. Formal invitation alone does not show that women have an effective voice.

✅ **NABARD** evolved the savings-led **SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (SHG-BLP)** and supports its ecosystem through policy advocacy, capacity building, SHG-promoting institutions, promotional support and refinance. Banks provide the accounts and credit; NABARD is not the retail lender to every SHG. ✅ **DAY-NRLM**, under the **Ministry of Rural Development**, uses bank linkage inside a rural livelihoods architecture with SHG → VO → Cluster-Level Federation, revolving-fund and community-investment support. ✅ **DAY-NULM**, under the **Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs**, uses an urban SHG → Area Level Federation → City Level Federation ladder; its full urban-poverty architecture belongs to Social Justice Topic 16. None of these institutional designs proves that a particular member controls income or that a federation is inclusive.

The Ministry of Rural Development/PIB **2 September 2026** announcement of a **3 September** meeting with banks and State Rural Livelihoods Missions establishes a coordination agenda on SHG-bank linkage and enterprise finance. It does **not** establish that the meeting changed income, credit quality or empowerment.

| Indian illustration | Institutional design | What it teaches | Limit to remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kudumbashree**, Kerala's State Poverty Eradication Mission | Women organised through neighbourhood groups, Area Development Societies and Community Development Societies, linked with local self-government | Federation can connect livelihood action, local planning and public voice | Wide membership or a three-tier structure does not prove equal control by every woman |
| **SEWA** (Self-Employed Women's Association) | A trade-union movement of women in informal employment, supported by member-oriented institutions including cooperatives | Collective organisation can combine labour voice, social security, finance and enterprise support | It is neither a conventional charity nor proof that one organisational model fits every worker |

Kudumbashree and SEWA therefore expand the SHG lesson without collapsing categories. The first foregrounds a state-supported community network connected to local government; the second begins from workers' association and builds supportive economic institutions. Both show that **organisation plus institutional linkage** can enlarge agency, while the distribution of voice and benefit must still be tested.

⚠️ **Why gender equity might follow:** regular records improve financial literacy; federation leadership teaches negotiation; interaction with officials can strengthen women's public voice. **Counterargument:** repeated meetings may impose unpaid time; privileged members may monopolise leadership; the non-member poor gain nothing from an inaccessible group. **Reply:** rotate leadership, disclose decisions, deliberately include marginalised households and link federations to an actual complaint and participation channel. The remaining test is whether agency, control of earnings and participation improve for disadvantaged women—not merely the number of groups.

**UPSC use:** **2025 GS-II Q6** asks about women's social capital and gender equity (10 marks, 150 words; “Explain”) and is primarily Social Justice-owned. **Unsolved approach:** identify which network stage yields which kind of access, distinguish NABARD's linkage role from the rural/urban mission ladders, ask how voice differs from credit, and test inclusion; do not assume an income effect. Its exact wording appears in the opening PYQ table. This potential access invites the harder question: what do loans actually achieve?

### Revision notes

- Social capital means usable networks, trust and reciprocity, not the money saved.
- Bonding ties operate within a group; bridging ties connect groups; linking ties reach institutions.
- NABARD evolved and facilitates SHG-BLP; banks provide the accounts and loans.
- DAY-NRLM is under MoRD: rural SHG → VO → Cluster-Level Federation.
- DAY-NULM is under MoHUA: urban SHG → ALF → City Level Federation.
- Rural and urban “CLF” are close-option traps, not the same institution.
- Federation can improve bargaining with banks, markets and public offices, but does not guarantee member agency.
- Kudumbashree illustrates a three-tier women's community network linked with local self-government.
- SEWA illustrates worker organisation supported by member-oriented economic institutions, including cooperatives.
- Test leadership rotation, control of earnings, excluded non-members and actual influence before claiming empowerment.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it incorrect to say that DAY-NRLM created SHG-Bank Linkage and that every CLF is the same institution?

**Model answer:** NABARD evolved SHG-BLP and supports its ecosystem, while banks provide accounts and loans; DAY-NRLM later uses linkage inside its rural SHG–VO–Cluster-Level-Federation architecture. DAY-NULM separately uses an urban SHG–ALF–City-Level-Federation ladder. The identical abbreviation does not erase different ministries, territories or institutional roles.

**Misconception to avoid:** “SHG programme” is not one interchangeable label for NABARD linkage, rural community institutions and urban community institutions.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse how NABARD's SHG-Bank Linkage and DAY-NRLM's community institutions may advance women's economic and public agency while remaining vulnerable to exclusion.

**Model (approximately 168 words):** NABARD evolved the savings-led SHG-Bank Linkage model and supports banks, SHG-promoting institutions and capacity building; banks remain the lenders. DAY-NRLM, administered by the Ministry of Rural Development, uses that linkage inside a rural community architecture in which SHGs may federate into village organisations and cluster-level federations. Repeated saving and record-keeping can generate within-group trust; cooperation across groups permits pooling and negotiation; interaction with banks and public officials may convert organisational scale into credit and a voice in local decisions. Thus a member may gain financial literacy and confidence as well as an opportunity to speak in a Gram Sabha. Yet access to a loan is not control of income, and a meeting is not proof of influence. Better-connected members might hold federation offices; non-members may remain outside. Rotation, transparent internal selection, inclusion outreach, alternative public access for non-members and grievance channels are necessary. DAY-NULM's urban SHG–ALF–City-Level-Federation ladder should not be merged into this rural sequence. The test is whether disadvantaged women's control over resources and actual participation improve.

## Lesson 3 — The credit instrument is not the poverty solution

Progress: 3/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: SHG–bank linkage, micro-finance, poverty and nutrition

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Question | SHG–bank linkage | MFI / NBFC-MFI lending |
|---|---|---|
| First organising principle | Group savings and records, followed by NABARD-evolved bank linkage | Specialised lender supplies small loans |
| Institution that scales | SHG and federation, then bank | Lender and loan portfolio |
| Chief promise | Financial access plus collective capability | Reach borrowers conventional banking may miss |
| Main danger | Exclusion and capture within groups; unviable enterprise | Over-indebtedness, multiple lending and recovery abuses |

*Two delivery routes can serve similar borrowers yet create different incentives; neither guarantees sustained poverty exit.*

If a household's earnings arrive unpredictably, a small loan may pay for inputs at the right time or smooth an emergency. **Micro-finance** includes small-value savings, credit, insurance and remittance services for people underserved by ordinary banking; **micro-credit** is only its loan component. The **NABARD-evolved SHG-Bank Linkage** route begins with group savings and records and is used within programmes including DAY-NRLM and DAY-NULM; it is not identical to a **microfinance institution (MFI)** or an **NBFC-MFI** lending route. The specialised-lender route builds a lender's loan book; the SHG route may also build group capability and member voice. Neither route should be romanticised.

**Poverty mechanism:** access to a savings buffer and suitable credit → less reliance on expensive informal borrowing → time to invest in a viable livelihood or withstand a shock. ⚠️ The strongest near-term case is **consumption smoothing and reduced vulnerability**; structural poverty exit additionally needs remunerative work, skills, buyers, social protection and health/education services. If many borrowers enter the same saturated local market, marginal sales can be too low to repay; taking one new loan to service an older loan is not income growth. Remote small-ticket lending also has genuine transaction costs, so a high apparent cost cannot be judged without examining alternatives and consumer protection.

**Gender and nutrition require separate causal accounts.** When a woman controls savings or enterprise proceeds, bargaining over household spending may change. Federation office-bearing may strengthen her voice with banks and officials. Better nutrition is **indirect**: resources under women's effective control plus health/nutrition messaging and convergence with public services may improve choices; credit by itself cannot establish a measured reduction in malnutrition. **Objection:** Joint repayment discipline can pressure women, and multiple loans may leave them worse off. **Reply:** review all borrowing, require non-coercive recovery, promote viable markets and non-credit support; assess outcomes for both members and those still outside SHGs.

**UPSC use:** **2020 GS-II Q17** (“Evaluate”, 15 marks, 250 words) asks about micro-finance, asset creation, income security **and women's empowerment** through SHGs: define evaluative criteria for each, identify NABARD SHG-BLP without converting NABARD into the retail lender, compare immediate protection and durable assets, then qualify the verdict. **2021 GS-II Q8** (“Explain with examples”, 10 marks, 150 words) asks whether women's SHG micro-financing can break a cycle involving gender inequality, poverty and malnutrition: test each channel, including the conditions required for nutrition. Economy Topic 06 owns the banking/financial-inclusion treatment; this lesson owns the governance and social-capital route. Both complete verified stems appear in the opening PYQ table, without solved answers.

### Revision notes

- Micro-finance includes savings, credit, insurance and remittances; micro-credit is only the loan component.
- NABARD evolved/facilitates SHG-Bank Linkage; banks lend; an MFI route begins with a specialised lender.
- Timely finance can smooth consumption and reduce dependence on expensive informal credit.
- Poverty exit additionally needs viable demand, skills, markets, public services and social protection.
- A loan used to repay another loan is not evidence of income growth.
- Credit in a woman's name does not prove her control over the money or enterprise proceeds.
- Nutrition effects require a chain through net resources, control, information and health/nutrition services.
- Assess debt sustainability, recovery conduct, member distribution and non-member exclusion separately.

### Concept check

**Question:** A woman uses an SHG loan to stock a shop, but a neighbouring household reports no increase in food quality. Is this a contradiction of the SHG mechanism?

**Model answer:** No. Credit can provide working capital, but income depends on demand and repayment costs, while nutrition depends on control of earnings, health information and services. Neither a loan nor group membership establishes the food outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a causal possibility as a proven programme impact hides the market and household decision points.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Evaluate micro-finance as a tool of poverty reduction in rural India, distinguishing its SHG and specialised-lender channels.

**Model (approximately 174 words):** Micro-finance comprises savings, small loans and other accessible financial services; micro-credit alone is not the whole instrument. NABARD evolved the savings-led SHG-Bank Linkage model and supports its ecosystem, while banks provide accounts and credit. DAY-NRLM uses linkage inside rural SHG federations; specialised microfinance lenders primarily build loan portfolios. For a rural household, timely working capital can sustain an enterprise and savings can smooth a shock. This makes micro-finance especially credible as vulnerability reduction. Yet a small loan does not create demand for products, assets or secure employment. Repeated borrowing from several lenders can produce over-indebtedness; harsh recovery or group pressure can also undo the benefit. A woman obtaining credit may not control the resulting income. The suitable test is whether net earnings, repayment sustainability and decision-making improve, not loan disbursal alone. Link credit to skills, viable markets, public health and social protection, while monitoring multiple borrowing and including non-member households. Micro-finance is a useful instrument inside a livelihoods system, not a substitute for that system; SHG-BLP, DAY-NRLM and MFI lending should remain institutionally distinct.

**Scoring guide (15):** complete finance/credit distinction 2; two institutional channels 3; anti-poverty mechanisms 3; over-indebtedness/market/gender limits 4; qualified verdict and concrete complement 3.

*Partnership has an accountable public spine; a useful partner is not a substitute for the institution citizens can compel.*

Suppose a district contracts a charitable trust to explain eligibility for a public health scheme. Families may trust a local worker more than an unfamiliar office. A private supplier may improve logistics; local SHGs can flag missing households. This is **co-production** of a public result, not permission to hand an entitlement to the best-funded local charity. Public charitable trusts can bring donations, facilities and continuity, a registered society supplies a membership/governing-body route, and a Section 8 company supplies Companies Act governance with no member dividend. None is automatically the superior delivery form. A CBO may map isolated households because its members live with the risk. A cooperative can aggregate producers through a member-owned enterprise and should be judged by member control, not NGO grant criteria.

|---|---|---|
| Which companies are covered? | Any one immediately-preceding-year threshold: net worth ≥ ₹500 crore, turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore, or net profit ≥ ₹5 crore | That every company or donation is governed by Section 135 |

*Topic 03 owns the complete development-industry, donor and CSR analysis. Here CSR is taught only far enough to prevent legal-form, funding-source and implementation-status conflation.*

**Disaster-response partnership:** before a flood or cyclone, local NGOs, SHGs, CBOs, faith-based groups and cooperatives can support risk communication, shelter preparation, community kitchens, needs assessment and last-mile distribution. Businesses can add transport, communications and supply chains. During response, however, the district administration and statutory disaster-management system must coordinate standards, avoid duplication, protect affected people and publish a grievance route. After relief, community participation in damage assessment and recovery planning helps prevent a rapid partnership from becoming an unaccountable parallel system.

**Compare the models by what each can and cannot promise.** The state can tax, legislate and be held to a universal public mandate; a project-funded NGO may pilot an innovation, mobilise marginalised groups and help a household complain, but its geographical reach and future funding may be limited. A business can provide technology or markets but its commercial incentive is not the same as universal access. Philanthropic donors finance experiments, yet their reporting demands may pull NGOs toward countable activities instead of residents' actual needs. A rights-holder needs a recognisable public addressee when the partner fails.

Six practical difficulties explain why the **alternative delivery model** is incomplete: (1) uneven geographic **scale**; (2) **project-cycle sustainability** rather than a permanent entitlement; (3) **upward donor/CSR reporting** displacing downward citizen accountability; (4) uneven staff **capacity and standards**; (5) **substitution risk** when government ceases to improve its own service; (6) **regulatory dependence**, including CSR eligibility or foreign-contribution permissions where applicable. **Reply to “never use NGOs”:** facilitation, legal assistance, pilots and community monitoring may reach people a standard bureaucracy misses. Specify a local-plan connection, published service standards, citizen complaint pathway, independent review of equity and an exit/continuity plan. Participation without a remedy is a consultation exercise, not a delivered right.

*Do not answer one regulatory question with a certificate issued for a different question.*

A public charitable trust may be validly constituted and still lack permission to receive foreign contribution or implement a CSR project. The controlling **Act** is the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010**, substantially **amended in 2020**; the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Rules, 2011** were further amended in **2024, 2025 and on 23 June 2026**. MHA administers this foreign-contribution framework; it does not create every NGO under trust, society or company law.

Under FCRA, an eligible association may use **registration** for continuing receipts, subject to a five-year validity/renewal framework, or **prior permission** for a specific source and purpose where it does not have registration. Renewal is sought within the statutory pre-expiry window; MHA's 30 September 2025 notice advised sufficiently early filing because a certificate can cease on expiry while a late application remains pending. Refusal, expiry/cessation, surrender, suspension and cancellation are distinct legal statuses; an allegation is not a finding.

✅ The 2020 amendment introduced the designated receipt **FCRA Account at State Bank of India, New Delhi Main Branch**, prohibited transfer of received foreign contribution to another person, and lowered the ordinary administrative-expense ceiling from **50% to 20%** of annual foreign contribution, subject to the statutory prior-approval route. The SBI account is the receipt gate: another FCRA account and one or more **utilisation accounts** may be opened in scheduled banks for keeping or using transferred foreign contribution, but non-foreign-contribution funds cannot be mixed into them.

✅ Annual **Form FC-4** is filed within nine months of financial-year closure with the prescribed income/expenditure, receipt/payment and balance-sheet material. The 2024 Rules permit reasoned carry-forward of an unspent part of the allowable administrative-expense amount; the 2025 Rules strengthened registration, prior-permission, renewal, bank-account and project/activity/location-wise FC-4 documentation; the 23 June 2026 Rules added current purpose/territorial, renewal-utilisation and disclosure requirements. The official PIB FAQ describes the renewal-utilisation condition as at least ₹10 lakh in foreign contribution over the preceding two-year period.

✅ **NGO Darpan** is a NITI Aayog identification database whose Unique ID is used or required in covered Union grant processes; it is not a licence to receive foreign funds. **12A/12AB** concerns an organisation's income-tax exemption route; **80G** concerns qualifying donor deductions. **CSR-1** registers an eligible Rule 4 implementing entity where required. A Darpan identifier, income-tax status, CSR-1 and FCRA permission may all be relevant without being equivalent. A CSR label also does not by itself decide whether a contributor is a “foreign source” under FCRA; apply the statutory definition and exclusions.

**Proposal boundary:** the official PIB FCRA FAQ dated 22 July 2026 discusses an amendment **Bill**. Unless enacted and commenced, its proposed asset/penalty/investigation changes are not current law. **Objection:** separate records burden small organisations. **Reply:** legal existence, grant identity, tax privilege, CSR implementation and foreign-receipt oversight answer different risks. A proportionate process can reduce duplication while preserving disclosure; arbitrary delay can undermine lawful work.

- FCRA registration and case-specific prior permission are distinct routes.

- Rules were amended in 2024, 2025 and on 23 June 2026; a proposed Bill is not enacted law.

*These are trade-offs to adjudicate, not grounds for either idealising or outlawing civic institutions.*

One kind of social capital may work against another. A close SHG of already-powerful families can be excellent at internal savings (**bonding**) but poor at welcoming isolated women (**bridging**). A federation with a bank relationship (**linking**) may amplify that exclusion if office-holders control access. This is **elite capture**, a risk rather than a verdict on every federation. Rotating leadership, disclosing decisions and checking members' actual ability to speak are design remedies; a non-member still needs a public service route.

The same independence that enables a watchdog to criticise a government programme can lead officials to perceive it as opposition. If funds come from abroad, the current FCRA Act/Rules supply a legal oversight gate; the public concern about outside influence is real, but indiscriminate suspicion can close useful feedback. A fair rule can combine financial traceability with reasoned, reviewable and proportionate decisions. Registration is not proof of public benefit, and expiry, refusal, suspension, cancellation, allegation and proposed statutory change must never be conflated. Likewise, an NGO's polished professional accounts may impress donors while frontline workers spend less time listening to beneficiaries. The remedy is **downward** feedback in addition to upward reporting, not a choice between professionalism and voice.

**Boundary test:** imagine an environmental NGO opposing an officially backed project while an SHG federation supports the employment it promises. Both may claim a public interest; neither represents every resident. Publish the evidence, hear displaced people and prospective workers, disclose funders and conflicts, and compare alternatives. The unresolved residual is political: even transparent groups disagree about values, so procedure does not manufacture consensus. ⚠️ The analytical claim that civic autonomy produces *both* empowerment and state friction links the 2025 social-capital and anti-State questions, but is an inference, not an official finding.

**UPSC use:** A 20-mark answer should distinguish deliverer, advocate, watchdog and community institution, compare collaboration with disagreement, identify internal legitimacy and external scrutiny, and make a conditional judgment. - Audited accounts establish financial information, not downward accountability or equitable outcomes.
- Foreign-funding scrutiny should be lawful, reasoned, proportionate and reviewable.
- Fast contracting can improve reach but weaken local ownership and continuity after the grant.

- Safeguards must be function-specific: rotation, disclosure, public remedies, consultation records and independent access for non-members.

The state cannot easily obtain all this information through its own hierarchy. Yet independence does not make each actor representative: federation leaders can control credit, a project NGO can answer to donors rather than users, and a well-funded pressure group can dominate consultation. Foreign-contribution oversight under FCRA addresses traceability and possible external influence, but broad or poorly reasoned restrictions may deter lawful scrutiny. The appropriate response differs by function. Rotate and disclose federation leadership; make a contracted provider answerable to beneficiaries through a public officer and grievance route; publish consultation and funding records for advocacy; use clear, proportionate and reviewable funding decisions. Check non-member households and dissenting local voices rather than treating organisational reach as community consent. The public authority must retain its legal entitlement duties throughout. Civic independence is valuable when its benefits and risks are both visible; it loses democratic justification if it is either treated as immunity from scrutiny or dismissed as disloyalty without evidence.

**Scoring guide (20):** disaggregate four actor functions 4; three explained benefits 4; capture, donor and FCRA objections 5; function-specific feasible safeguards 5; conditional, rights-aware judgment 2.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

**10 marks; 150 words — Question:** Explain why SHG membership is not a sufficient measure of women's empowerment.

**Model (approximately 114 words):** NABARD-evolved SHG-Bank Linkage connects savings groups to banks, while DAY-NRLM places rural women's SHGs within village organisations and cluster-level federations. Membership records therefore indicate an opportunity for bonding and institutional access, not a final outcome. A member may have credit without deciding how to spend it, while office-bearers alone may negotiate with the bank. Repeated meetings may also burden women whose unpaid care work remains unchanged. Measure control of earnings, leadership rotation, effective participation in public decisions and debt sustainability, while checking non-members. DAY-NULM's separate urban SHG–ALF–City-Level-Federation ladder should not be merged into this rural route. SHGs can expand agency when their institutions are inclusive and markets viable; membership alone cannot prove gender equity.

**Scoring guide (10):** programme mechanism 2; two distinct agency tests 3; member and non-member limits 3; qualified judgment 2.

**15 marks; 250 words — Question:** Assess whether NGOs should be treated as an alternative to the state in delivering public entitlements.

**Model (approximately 154 words):** NGOs can mobilise communities, innovate and assist people who find a district service difficult to access. For example, a charitable trust may help eligible families navigate a public clinic while SHG members report missed households. Such functions can improve information and trust. An alternative *duty-holder*, however, is a different claim: an NGO's donor-financed project may end, its geographic reach may be selective, and its reporting may reward outputs rather than a beneficiary's effective remedy. Elected government retains the universal mandate, funds and coercive legal powers needed to guarantee an entitlement. A sensible partnership specifies quality and equity standards, discloses selection and finance, supplies a complaint route to an identifiable public officer, audits excluded households and plans continuity. A credible NGO pilot can justify adapted public practice; it cannot prove the whole district will be covered. Therefore NGOs can complement, test and scrutinise delivery, while the state remains answerable for enforceable rights and corrective action.

**Scoring guide (15):** clear state/NGO functional comparison 4; three challenges with causal explanation 4; two concrete safeguards 4; India example and conditional conclusion 3.

**20 marks; 250 words — Question:** “Civil-society participation is only as democratic as the design of its representation and oversight.” Critically examine with reference to SHGs, NGO partnerships and advocacy.

**Model (approximately 184 words):** Organising citizens outside government can make governance more responsive, but organisation does not itself prove representation. DAY-NRLM SHGs may build internal savings trust and federate so women can negotiate with banks. Yet leadership held by a few well-connected members may exclude poorer women and non-members. An NGO assisting a district health scheme can offer local knowledge; donor reporting may nevertheless count visits rather than repaired exclusions. An environmental pressure group may supply evidence a regulator lacks, but privileged access or opaque funders could mute other residents. These objections justify differentiated oversight, not a ban on civic action. Rotate and disclose federation offices, test the distribution of credit and voice, publish service contracts, allow beneficiary complaints to a public officer, and make consultation records and conflicts visible. FCRA compliance is relevant when foreign contribution is involved; an allegation of non-compliance is not a finding against every critic. Public agencies must respond to well-grounded objections and preserve their own entitlement duties. If these conditions are absent, civic participation can reproduce hierarchy; if they function, independent groups can improve information, delivery and correction without becoming the state.

**Scoring guide (20):** three differentiated actor mechanisms 6; three causal objections 5; tailored safeguards 5; legal-status and public-duty qualification 2; defended verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Civil society's scope and the public boundary

- Civil society encompasses NGOs, SHGs, trusts, unions, cooperatives, resident groups, faith-based bodies, professional associations and informal movements; civil society ≠ NGO.
- Public charitable trust, registered society and Section 8 company are distinct legal vehicles. Trusts depend on the deed and applicable State public-trust/endowment framework; societies use the Societies Registration Act, 1860 or State variant; Section 8 companies use the Companies Act, apply income to their objects and cannot distribute dividends to members.
- Section 1 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 excludes public/private religious or charitable endowments; do not cite it as one national public-charity incorporation statute.
- A CBO may be formal or informal. An SHG, movement and cooperative are not synonyms for an incorporated NGO.
- Cooperatives are member-owned enterprises under applicable state or multi-state cooperative law; Article 43B emphasises voluntary formation, autonomy, democratic control and professional management. Test real member control and weaker-member inclusion.
- State retains entitlement coverage, funding, oversight and citizen remedy when an NGO helps implement a public service. A partner is not automatically the legal duty-holder.
- A pressure group aims to influence a policy, not to form a government as a party does. Judge structure, funding, representativeness and downward accountability for novel actors.

### SHG mechanism and its failure points

- Social capital = networks, trust and reciprocity; financial capital = resources. Bonding inside SHG → bridging across groups → linking to bank/market/state, contingent on functional institutions.
- NABARD evolved/facilitates SHG-BLP through policy, SHG-promoting institutions, capacity, promotion and refinance; banks supply the accounts and loans.
- DAY-NRLM: Ministry of Rural Development; rural SHG → Village Organisation → Cluster-Level Federation; revolving/community-investment/livelihood support and bank linkage.
- DAY-NULM: Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs; urban SHG → Area Level Federation → City Level Federation. Its “CLF” is not DAY-NRLM's Cluster-Level Federation; Social Justice 16 owns the full urban scheme.
- Kudumbashree shows a women's neighbourhood-group → ADS → CDS community network linked with Kerala local self-government; SEWA shows an informal-worker union movement supported by member-oriented institutions, including cooperatives. Neither example proves uniform outcomes.
- Mechanisms: savings and records → literacy and confidence; federation → bargaining; bank/office/Gram Sabha access → possible income and voice. Test control of earnings and real influence.
- Vulnerable points: meeting burden, coercive repayment, inaccessible bank, dominant office-holders, excluded non-member poor. Rotate roles, disclose decisions, reach marginalised residents.
- Micro-finance includes savings, credit, insurance, remittances; micro-credit is only credit. SHG–bank group route differs from specialised MFI/NBFC-MFI lender route.
- Credit most credibly smooths shocks; sustained poverty exit needs skills, viable demand, markets and services. Multiple loans, recovery abuse and saturated markets can erase gains.
- Nutrition follows indirectly through control of resources, information and convergence with public health/nutrition services; do not assert a measured nutrition effect from loan access.

### NGOs, charitable trusts and public services

- NGO strengths: local trust, facilitation, innovation, monitoring, complaint support. State strengths: universal mandate, financing, standards, enforceable public duty.
- Six challenges of an NGO alternative: uneven reach; expiring grants; donor-over-citizen accountability; variable standards; substitution of state capacity; funding/regulatory contingency.
- Section 135 CSR thresholds are alternative: net worth ≥ ₹500 crore, turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore, or net profit ≥ ₹5 crore in the immediately preceding financial year. Spending floor: at least 2% of average net profits of the three immediately preceding financial years, or available preceding years for a newer company.
- Rule 4 controls eligible CSR implementing routes; qualifying independent Section 8/trust/society bodies require prescribed 12A/80G status and a three-year track record. CSR-1 is the implementing registration, not incorporation, FCRA, tax approval or impact certification. Topic 03 owns full CSR/development-industry analysis.
- Government–NGO–business collaboration allocates roles: public authority guarantees standards/remedies, civic organisation brings community access, business adds logistics/market capacity. In disasters, NGOs, SHGs, CBOs, faith groups and cooperatives can support warnings, shelters, kitchens, needs assessment and distribution under statutory public coordination.
- Public charitable status does not prove inclusive access. Publish partner criteria, integrate local plan, give beneficiaries a named public remedy, measure non-member coverage and plan continuity.

### Funding and regulatory discrimination

- Legal constitution → trust/society/Section 8 route; FCRA → foreign-contribution receipt/use (MHA); 12A/12AB and 80G → tax; CSR-1 → prescribed CSR implementing route; Darpan → covered Union grant identity.
- FCRA Act: 2010, amended 2020. Rules: amended in 2024, 2025 and on 23 June 2026. A separately discussed 2026 Bill is a proposal unless enacted and commenced.
- Registration permits continuing receipts subject to the five-year renewal framework; prior permission is source-and-purpose-specific. Distinguish expiry/cessation, refusal, surrender, suspension and cancellation.
- Receipt account: SBI New Delhi Main Branch. Another FCRA account and one or more utilisation accounts may be used in scheduled banks after receipt; no non-foreign-contribution funds may be mixed.
- FC-4 is the annual return within nine months of financial-year closure. Current rules add project/activity/location and later purpose/territorial, renewal-utilisation and disclosure requirements.
- 2020 amendment: transfer prohibition and ordinary administrative-expense ceiling reduced from 50% to 20%. 2024 Rules permit reasoned carry-forward of unspent allowable administrative expense; 2025 Rules strengthen applications/accounts/FC-4; 2026 Rules add current controls.
- CSR is not automatically FCRA or non-FCRA; apply the statutory “foreign source” definition and exclusions. Do not attach changing national registration/cancellation or SHG/credit counts without a dated official source.

### Advocacy, representation and conditional judgment

- Watchdog work may challenge policy without being unlawful. Non-state = structure; anti-State = contested label. Test evidence, funding, whom the group speaks for and the state's reasoned response.
- Pressure-group influence: representation, information, scrutiny, risk of capture. Consultation quality requires hearing less organised and dissenting residents.
- Federation leadership can train women for public life; party tickets, election resources and electoral choice block automatic conversion to state legislative seats.
- Optional refinement: dense networks can capture a community; professionalisation can suppress grassroots feedback; security screening can chill lawful advocacy. Remedy with inclusion, downward accountability, proportionate review and reasoned public decisions.
- Related topics divide the material deliberately: Topic 03 covers legal vehicles, the development industry and CSR; Topic 14 covers participation quality; Governance 08 covers statutory social audit; Social Justice 05 covers gender outcomes and the primary 2023/2025 routes; Social Justice 16 covers the full DAY-NULM architecture; Economy 06 covers banking and financial inclusion; Polity covers pressure-group typology and the 2025/2026 pressure-group demands.
- PYQ route: 2020 evaluate finance ≠ poverty exit; 2021 distinguish gender/income/nutrition and compare delivery models; 2023 distinguish training from legislative selection; 2024 test partnership and charity inclusion; 2025 distinguish structural status from political perception.
- For 10 marks: position + two distinct mechanisms + one limitation. For 15: compare institutions, test competing outcomes, specify safeguard. For 20: disaggregate actors, trace both benefits and capture, address regulation and state duty, end with a conditional criterion.
