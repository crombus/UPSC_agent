# Administrative Reforms and the Second ARC — Live Session Edition

## Roadmap

An expert commission can identify an administrative fault; it cannot by itself repair the office. Follow the difference between diagnosis, recommendation, government decision and a citizen's experience.

| Lesson | Stage | Learning question | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundation | What changed between the First and Second ARCs? | 25 min |
| 2 | Core | Which key recommendations from the Second ARC answer which problem? | 35 min |
| 3 | Core | When has a recommendation actually become a reform? | 30 min |
| 4 | Core | Why does a sound recommendation stop short of a citizen outcome, and how do we write about it? | 35 min |
| 5 | Advanced | What remains valuable in an old diagnosis when the instruments change? | 30 min |
| 6 | Advanced | How do federal authority, incentives and attribution change the verdict? | 30 min |

**Learning route:** eras and mandates → complete report map with key recommendations → evidence of action → implementation bottlenecks and exam use → diagnostic endurance → federal and political-economy stress test. The first four lessons supply a complete core answer; the last two sharpen its qualifications. Each lesson closes with a concept check and original Mains practice. Practise the 2021 Prelims question without looking for its answer below; later Mains questions show how to apply a report's diagnosis to a named governance problem.

## Lesson 1 — Why two commissions asked different questions

Progress: 1/6 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: First ARC, Second ARC and reform mandates

| Question | First ARC | Second ARC |
|---|---|---|
| Historical setting | Post-Independence administrative machinery, 1966–70 | Commission constituted in 2005; reports issued 2006–09 |
| Chair | Initially Morarji Desai; later K. Hanumanthaiah | M. Veerappa Moily |
| Central problem | How should a growing state organise its personnel, ministries and Centre–State administration? | How can existing government become answerable, capable, citizen-facing and technology-enabled? |
| Example | Proposal for grievance institutions, including Lokpal/Lokayukta | Fifteen reports across transparency, personnel, local governance and more |

*The comparison shows different emphases, not mutually exclusive mandates: each also examined issues beyond the shorthand in its column.*

```text
Build machinery (First ARC) → inspect how established machinery serves citizens
                            → improve institutions and processes (Second ARC)
```

*This historical sequence is a difference of emphasis rather than a replacement of every older concern.*

Imagine opening an office in a new district: first decide who employs staff, allocates responsibility and coordinates departments. Decades later, ask whether the office tells citizens why an application failed and whether they can appeal. The first question illuminates **machinery of government**, the second **governance quality**. The First Administrative Reforms Commission belonged to a state still building its administrative architecture. The Second examined a more developed apparatus confronting transparency demands, digital service possibilities, decentralisation and entitlements. Neither commission itself legislated.

**Administrative reform** means deliberate change to the structure, rules, capabilities, incentives or public interface of administration to improve a stated public purpose. A structural change might merge overlapping desks; a process change might remove a needless approval; a personnel change might train a grievance officer; an accountability change might provide reasons and review. These can complement one another, but a merged office that still rejects applications without reasons has not achieved process or accountability reform.

The First ARC's grievance-institution proposal illustrates the status distinction already: the book documents its recommendation concerning Lokpal and Lokayukta, but that historical proposal must not be mistaken for today's statutory design. The Second ARC's *Local Governance* report examines panchayat revenue problems (Laxmikanth, PDF p. 810), but its diagnosis cannot itself transfer finance to a panchayat. A commission proposes; a competent government chooses an instrument and acts.

**Comparison with neighbouring bodies:** an Administrative Reforms Commission investigates administration and recommends changes; a Law Commission focuses on legal reform, while a Finance Commission has a constitutional fiscal-transfer mandate. Similar use of the word *commission* does not make their advice legally identical. Nor is the Second ARC a permanent administrative regulator capable of ordering a ministry to comply.

**Objection:** The contrast “structure then quality” sounds too neat: the First ARC also addressed citizens' grievances, and the Second addressed organisational structure. **Reply:** Treat the contrast as an account of dominant context and examination strategy, not a claim of exclusive subjects. The real test is to identify a particular report and proposed instrument, not to infer its content merely from the era. The residual challenge is proving how much an earlier commission caused a later change.

**UPSC application:** GS-II tests the structure, organisation and functioning of the executive and the quality of governance. For a comparison answer, give dates/chairs accurately, contrast the problem each confronted, illustrate one proposal from each and qualify the contrast. **Prelims trap:** a statement about the 1966 ARC must not be silently transferred to the 2005 Second ARC; a commission's recommendation, the creation of a department and its placement under a minister are three separate claims requiring separate evidence.

**2021 Prelims GS-I Q78 — question from the official paper (unkeyed):**

> With reference to the Union Government, consider the following statements:
>
> 1. N. Gopalaswamy Iyengar Committee suggested that a minister and a secretary be designated solely for pursuing the subject of administrative reform and promoting it.
> 2. In 1970, the Department of Personnel was constituted on the recommendation of the Administrative Reforms Commission, 1966, and this was placed under the Prime Minister's charge.
>
> Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
>
> (a) 1 only
>
> (b) 2 only
>
> (c) Both 1 and 2
>
> (d) Neither 1 nor 2

**Demand:** assess the committee claim and each linked part of the departmental-creation claim independently. **Approach:** distinguish an earlier committee from the First ARC; check recommendation, date of creation and ministerial charge against administrative history before choosing an option. The year **1970** here is part of the question, not independently established by this lesson. No answer or elimination is supplied.

### Revision notes

- The First ARC operated during **1966–70**.
- Morarji Desai was its initial chair; K. Hanumanthaiah later chaired it.
- The Second ARC was constituted in **2005** under M. Veerappa Moily.
- Its fifteen reports appeared during **2006–09**.
- “Building structure” versus “improving governance quality” is a contrast of emphasis, not an exclusive division.
- An ARC recommends; it does not legislate, regulate permanently or compel every government.
- Administrative Reforms Commission, Finance Commission and Law Commission have different mandates.
- A recommendation, a government decision and an actually constituted or operating institution are separate claims.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would a question about creating a personnel department be misread if the two ARCs were treated as interchangeable?

**Model answer:** They arose in different periods. The personnel-department claim refers to the First ARC's institutional era; the Second ARC's later personnel report concerns quality and reform of an existing service. Even for the First ARC, proposal and actual creation must be proved separately.

**Misconception to avoid:** The word “personnel” in both eras does not prove that the Second ARC created the earlier department.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish the historical reform problems faced by the First and Second Administrative Reforms Commissions, without overstating their differences.

**Model (approximately 125 words):** The First ARC (1966–70), chaired first by Morarji Desai and later by K. Hanumanthaiah, addressed the organisation and personnel of a post-Independence state. Its proposal for Lokpal and Lokayukta shows that even an institution-building agenda included citizens' grievances. The Second ARC, constituted in 2005 under M. Veerappa Moily, produced fifteen reports in 2006–09. Its *Citizen-Centric Administration* and *Promoting e-Governance* reports ask how established offices can serve and answer citizens more effectively. The contrast is principally one of context and emphasis: the Second also studied central-government organisation, and the First did not ignore accountability. Neither commission made law by publishing recommendations. Assess each proposed change against the subsequent government instrument and its observed operation.

**Scoring guide (10):** accurate chronology/chairs 2; distinct administrative problems 3; named examples 2; explicit overlap 1; recommendation/status distinction 2.

*The numbered map is a search tool for the correct analytical problem, not proof that any recommendation in a report was adopted.*

```text
Information [1] → integrity [4] → service and correction [12]
Local authority [6] ↔ district coordination [15] ↔ Union organisation [13]
Staff [10] + digital redesign [11] + financial systems [14] support delivery
```

*The branches show why one real public-service failure may call for more than one report.*

You need not memorise fifteen independent miniature books, but titles alone earn few marks. Read reports **1, 4 and 12** as an information–integrity–service chain: proactive disclosure and disciplined secrecy make power inspectable; ethics codes, integrity bodies and whistle-blower protection constrain abuse; charters, measurable service capability and grievance correction make administration answerable to a citizen. Reports **6 and 15** examine the territorial end of the chain: clear activity mapping and the 3Fs must coexist with accountable district coordination. Report **13** concerns central organisation, while **14** asks whether financial controls and resource use work. Reports **2 and 9** illuminate entitlements and social cooperation.

The GS-III-heavy reports also contain concrete administrative design. Report **3** shifts crisis management upstream towards prevention, mitigation, early warning, community capacity and a clear incident command. Report **5** seeks professional investigation and independent police accountability rather than treating every public-order failure as a demand for more force. Report **10** connects competency, domain placement, tenure, training and performance management; Report **11** insists on business-process re-engineering and interoperable standards before a portal is celebrated. These are recommendation clusters, not claims that every measure was accepted, enacted or uniformly operational.

For example, if a panchayat runs a water service without adequate own resources, *Local Governance* is a more precise intellectual starting point than *Organisational Structure of Government of India*. A district officer coordinating departments belongs naturally with *State and District Administration*. Local government and district administration interact, but an elected body's financial autonomy and a district executive's coordination are not the same reform. The book's panchayat-finance passage offers a concrete diagnostic example; it does not establish the present funding of any named panchayat.

Similarly, an online application with three avoidable approvals demands a process question: Report 11 is more apt than a bare reference to “digitisation”; if the problem is an unusable appeal after rejection, Report 12 is the closer lens. **Inference:** one case can draw on both, but name what each contributes rather than attributing an unsourced specific recommendation to either. Report 4 may illuminate an integrity failure without requiring a GS-II answer to reproduce the whole ethics syllabus.

**Objection:** A map of reports risks replacing real analysis with title-dropping. **Reply:** A title is only a route to the mechanism: state the problem, identify who acts, specify an instrument and test a citizen outcome. A report's name helps precision, but cannot substitute for knowing a provision or its present legal status. The remaining difficulty is that detailed recommendations vary within each report; verify the actual recommendation before quoting it.

**UPSC application:** In **2024 GS-II Q5 (10 marks, 150 words)**, the directive is *analyse*: assess local bodies' good-governance role and the advantages and disadvantages of merging rural and urban bodies. Report 6 frames the functions–funds–staff test; Report 15 adds the district-administration interface. Compare the two institutional options and test local capacity; neither title settles the merger question. In **2024 GS-II Q16 (15 marks, 250 words)**, the question asks candidates to *identify* why the Citizens' Charter has not realised its potential and *suggest* remedies. Report 12 frames service standards and correction, but the answer must assess actual accountability, not merely cite the report. **Trap:** report titles are historical document names; do not turn the 11th report into a current “Digital India Act” or treat the 4th as a statute.

### Revision notes

- Report 1: RTI-consistent secrecy, proactive disclosure and records management.
- Report 3: prevention, early warning, community capacity and coordinated incident command.
- Report 4: ethics code, integrity institutions, whistle-blower protection and accountability.
- Report 5: professional investigation separated from routine order work, plus independent police accountability.
- Report 6: subsidiarity, activity mapping and transfer of functions, funds and functionaries.
- Report 10: competency/domain placement, tenure, learning and performance management.
- Report 11: process re-engineering before digitisation, with standards, interoperability and inclusion.
- Report 12: Citizens' Charters, delivery capability, grievance correction and independent evaluation.
- Reports 2, 7, 8, 9, 13, 14 and 15 complete the entitlement, conflict, security, social-capital, Union, finance and state/district map.
- Use the sequence **problem → report → verified recommendation → decision → instrument → operation → outcome**.

### Concept check

**Question:** A district posts a new online grievance form, but no officer can correct wrongly rejected cases. Which reports offer different diagnostic angles, and why is a title not an implementation claim?

**Model answer:** Report 11 highlights process and digital design; Report 12 highlights citizen service and redress; Report 15 can examine district coordination. Their titles point to different questions. Whether a particular recommendation was adopted needs an identified government action and evidence of a working correction route.

**Misconception to avoid:** A programme addressing a similar theme is not automatically a direct implementation of the corresponding report.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Discuss how the Second ARC's reports help analyse a citizen's failed district-level digital service. Avoid treating the reports as implemented law.

**Model (approximately 174 words):** A failed online application is not only a technology problem. *Promoting e-Governance: The Smart Way Forward* supplies the process question: has the office removed avoidable approvals or merely placed them behind a screen? *Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance* asks whether applicants can learn the standard, receive a reason and seek correction. *State and District Administration: A Fresh Look* directs attention to coordination between offices responsible for the same application. If a local body must deliver the final service, *Local Governance* adds the question of actual functions, funds and staff. These reports are diagnoses and proposals, not proof of legal rights or current departmental practice. The responsible state or Union authority must adopt an appropriate instrument, equip staff and provide an effective review channel. A district's high portal-disposal count would establish transaction volume, not fair resolution: compare reversals, excluded applicants and citizen feedback. Thus the reports illuminate complementary failure points; a defensible verdict depends on documented government action and verified service outcomes, not a thematic match between old proposals and new technology.

**Scoring guide (15):** apt differentiated named reports 5; linked mechanism and district example 4; government authority/status distinction 3; outcome/appeal evidence and qualified verdict 3.

## Lesson 3 — The distance between recommendation and reality

Progress: 3/6 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: DARPG, implementation statuses and proof

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Laxmikanth PDF p. 1030 documents a First ARC proposal and later government acceptance concerning grievance institutions; this alone cannot establish present Second ARC acceptance or operation.
CA search: "site:darpg.gov.in 2026 2nd ARC reports government decisions on recommendations status DARPG September 2026"
CA found: No new recommendation-level acceptance or implementation decision was verified; no present status is inferred from a report title or programme resemblance.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Problem documented → ARC proposal → government decision
                                          │
                                accepted / modified /
                                rejected / pending
                                          ↓ if action proceeds
                              Act / rule / executive order
                                          ↓
                         staff + budget + working procedure
                                          ↓
                         reviewable citizen-level outcomes
```

*An arrow is a question to check, not evidence that the next stage happened.*

Suppose a commission proposes a deadline for certificates. A government may agree but never issue a rule; or it may issue a rule without appointing officers; or officers may close files on time while applicants still receive incorrect certificates. This is why **DARPG**—the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances—can coordinate and record follow-up to ARC material without itself compelling a state or a line ministry to deliver every recommendation. Each concerned authority has to make its own decision within its powers.

The **decision register** and the **implementation ladder** answer different questions. A government can accept, modify, reject or leave a recommendation pending; “accepted” is a response, not the outcome. Evidence of **enactment/notification** needs an operative Act, rule, order or scheme instrument—not a draft or a headline. **Operational** means trained people, usable processes and resources exist. **Evaluated** means evidence of quality or effects, preferably including users who could not access the service. One instrument can operationalise some components of a recommendation while others remain unadopted; therefore assign a status to the *particular recommendation, jurisdiction and date*, not to all fifteen reports.

| Claim in an answer | Minimum corroboration | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|
| “ARC proposed this” | Report's actual passage | Official acceptance |
| “Government accepted/modified this” | Dated decision by competent authority | Binding rule or implementation |
| “This is legally in force” | Enacted and commenced law / operative notified rule or order | Equal access or enforcement |
| “This is operating” | Budget, officials, procedures and records | That it benefits intended citizens |
| “It works” | Credible outcome, grievance and distribution evidence | That the ARC caused the reform |

Take a citizen charter posted outside an office. The notice is a real artefact but not automatically a statutory guarantee: inspect the jurisdiction's legal basis, standards, responsible officer and remedy. Conversely, a state service-guarantee Act may provide a legal remedy, but actual staffing and review must still be examined. Executive instructions can matter in practice without being identical to statutes; a constituted institution differs from an appointed, adequately staffed and functioning one.

**Outcome test:** A grievance-disposal count measures recorded closures, not whether complainants obtained a remedy or whether a recommendation in Report 12 was adopted. Compare the reasons for closure, independent citizen feedback and access to review; separately establish any government decision linking a reform to the report.

**Objection:** Requiring outcome evaluation for every report citation is impractical in a timed answer. **Reply:** Do not invent it: state the highest stage actually evidenced, and say “outcome not established” when relevant. This disciplined sentence is more valuable than a false claim of complete adoption. Residual uncertainty remains when administrative records do not reveal excluded users.

**UPSC application:** In a “how far implemented?” question, split recommendations by authority and stage; name one actual government instrument only when verified, then distinguish implementation from effect. Avoid an aggregate percentage across the commission without a dated, methodologically clear source. Report 10 cannot be relabelled “Mission Karmayogi implemented” merely because both concern civil-service capacity.

### Revision notes

- DARPG coordinates and preserves follow-up; competent ministries and states decide and act.
- A recommendation can be accepted, modified, rejected or left pending.
- Acceptance is a policy response, not an operative legal or administrative instrument.
- Enactment or notification does not prove staffing, funds or a usable procedure.
- Operation does not by itself prove fair access, quality or citizen benefit.
- A closed grievance file is not necessarily a resolved grievance.
- Status must attach to a particular recommendation, jurisdiction and date, not to all fifteen reports.
- Thematic resemblance between an old recommendation and a new programme does not establish causation.
- Where evidence stops, state the highest verified stage and preserve the uncertainty.

### Concept check

**Question:** If a ministry accepts a proposed service standard and later launches a complaint portal, what additional evidence is needed to say the recommendation is fully effective?

**Model answer:** Verify the particular accepted proposal and the portal's legal or administrative link, operational responsibility, staffing, actual access, quality of decisions, appeal and independent citizen outcomes. Acceptance plus a portal does not demonstrate a causal link or effective redress.

**Misconception to avoid:** An announcement or a disposal total proves neither completed adoption nor satisfactory remedy.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine why government acceptance of an ARC recommendation cannot be equated with its effective implementation.

**Model (approximately 165 words):** Acceptance records a policy response; it neither creates a binding entitlement nor supplies an operating service. DARPG may coordinate consideration of a Second ARC recommendation, but the competent ministry or state must issue any required rule, order or legislation. Take *Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance*: even if a government favours clearer service standards, a posted charter without a responsible officer, time limit and usable complaint route can leave rejected applicants without remedy. A notified service rule would establish an instrument; appointment, training and a working appeal would show operation; evidence of fair and timely outcomes would support effectiveness. Even a verified grievance-disposal count would measure recorded activity, not necessarily resolution or an ARC implementation link. Political resistance, resource shortages and jurisdictional limits can interrupt each stage. A fair assessment therefore labels the individual proposal accepted, modified, rejected or pending; verifies the operative instrument separately; and distinguishes administrative activity from outcomes for affected citizens. Where outcome evidence is unavailable, state that limitation instead of calling the entire report implemented.

**Scoring guide (15):** precise status ladder 5; named report and concrete mechanism 3; authority/capacity/incentive analysis 3; evidence limits and causal restraint 2; qualified conclusion 2.

## Lesson 4 — Why sound reform stalls, and how to answer a new stem

Progress: 4/6 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Implementation chain, stakeholders and mark-scaled answers

Book context: queried — Laxmikanth PDF p. 810 discusses PRI revenue constraints; useful for illustrating why a report cannot itself finance local responsibilities.
CA search: "site:darpg.gov.in 2026 August September CPGRAMS centre monthly report grievance disposal quality administrative reform"
CA found: No separate recent recommendation-specific adoption was verified; the official 3 April 2026 capacity-building anchor is used only as a present-day comparison, not as an ARC implementation claim.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Diagnosis → official acceptance → operative instrument → jurisdiction
                                                        │
                             state / Union authority + fiscal powers
                                                        ↓
                              staff + skills + funds + incentives
                                                        ↓
                           delivered service + review + outcomes
                             ↑
         concentrated costs of change can stop the chain at any step
```

*A brilliant proposal can fail at authorisation, capacity, incentives or verification without its diagnosis being wrong.*

Why would a sensible reform stall? A ministry that loses control over approvals may not favour a simpler process. An employee group may fear changed career rules. Citizens gain from quicker services, but their benefit is scattered across many people; a small group bearing concentrated costs can organise more easily. **This is a political-economy inference**, not proof that a particular ministry opposed a particular ARC proposal. Equally, local government cannot deliver a new function with no staff or money. The First and Second ARCs differed historically; both faced the same separation of advice from coercive power.

Walk through an unfamiliar proposal for faster district certificates. First ask what failure was diagnosed: delay, exclusion or lack of appeal? Second identify the competent government and whether the subject needs state action. Third specify the legal/administrative instrument, the office that must perform it and the money and skills it needs. Fourth identify who loses discretion and who benefits. Finally measure the error rate, appeals and user access as well as speed. **Example limit:** a hypothetical certificate service helps expose the chain, not establish that a named state enacted a particular ARC recommendation.

The reports direct answers without replacing the sector's own facts. Report 6 (*Local Governance*) brings the functions–funds–functionaries test into a devolution answer; Report 10 (*Refurbishing of Personnel Administration*) raises capacity and incentives; Report 13 (*Organisational Structure of Government of India*) raises coordination; Report 14 (*Strengthening Financial Management Systems*) asks whether expenditure systems support delivery. You can use more than one report, but explain the distinct work each does. A good public-finance system can report expenditure without guaranteeing clean water; a trained civil servant cannot fix a rule that rewards closure of complaints rather than correction.

**Strong objection:** Expert commissions produce long, expensive programmes that invite “commission fatigue”; perhaps incremental action is better. **Reply:** Small fixes are more politically achievable, but isolated fixes can be contradictory—for example, a new portal with the old paper approval chain. The best response is to sequence verifiable, jurisdiction-specific steps from the diagnosis while retaining the report's wider map of dependencies. The unresolved trade-off is which costs and delays a government is prepared to bear.

**UPSC answer method:** For **10 marks**, contrast eras or explain one report and one instrument/status caution. For **15**, state criteria, connect two named reports to a concrete governance problem, identify the bottleneck and give a defensible remedy. For **20**, trace three domains through decision, instrument, federal/capacity condition and outcome; add the changed technological context and a qualified verdict. Never claim a fixed implementation percentage, a recommendation number or a direct chain from Report 10 to Mission Karmayogi without documentation. Use a report title for historical diagnosis and a separately verified current instrument for present practice.

**PYQ link:** The 2021 objective demand from Lesson 1 illustrates precisely why “recommended,” “constituted” and “placed under charge” must not be merged. Questions asking about civil services, local bodies or citizens' charters apply these reform lenses but are examined in their own substantive domains.

### Revision notes

- Begin with the diagnosed failure: delay, exclusion, discretion, weak capacity or absent remedy.
- Identify the constitutionally and administratively competent Union, state or local actor.
- Specify the required Act, rule, order, procedure or other valid instrument.
- Check jurisdiction, finance, staff, skills and institutional coordination.
- A structural merger does not necessarily redesign a defective process.
- Concentrated losses of discretion can organise resistance more easily than diffuse citizen benefits.
- Capacity shortage is an alternative explanation to deliberate resistance and must be tested.
- Measure error, exclusion, appeal and correction alongside speed or disposal totals.
- Sequence feasible steps without losing the report's wider map of dependencies.
- The core answer is complete only when recommendation, authority, instrument and outcome remain distinct.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why might merging two certificate desks fail to shorten an applicant's wait, and how would you test a better reform?

**Model answer:** A merger changes organisation but may preserve duplicate approvals, weak staffing and no appeal. Map the procedure, identify the responsible authority, remove an unnecessary step through a valid instrument and compare actual waiting times, exclusion and correction rates, not only the number of merged desks.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reorganisation is not synonymous with a changed process or verified citizen outcome.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse why administratively persuasive Second ARC proposals may produce uneven reform outcomes across India. Suggest an evidence-based implementation strategy.

**Model (approximately 202 words):** The Second ARC's fifteen reports identify connected governance problems, but a published recommendation has no power to reorganise an office by itself. *Local Governance* exposes the gap between formal functions and local finance/staff: devolution needs state decisions, not just Union agreement. *Promoting e-Governance: The Smart Way Forward* supplies a process lens: an online form without removal of duplicate approvals can preserve delay. *Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance* demands service standards and usable correction; a posted charter without an accountable officer may not change an excluded applicant's experience. DARPG's coordination cannot compel every line authority. Sequence action: identify each proposal and competent government; record acceptance or modification; verify the rule, order or enacted instrument; budget and train the implementing office; publish accessible standards and appeals; then evaluate error correction, access and outcomes alongside throughput. Even a published grievance-disposal count would not alone prove resolution or an ARC causal link. Expert diagnosis remains useful if converted into such measurable, authority-specific commitments; another report without instruments and feedback will not suffice.

**Scoring guide (20):** three correctly differentiated reports and mechanisms 6; causal federal/incentive/capacity analysis 5; feasible stage-by-stage strategy 5; evidence qualification and conditional conclusion 4.

*A durable analytical category is not an automatically valid current policy prescription.*

```text
Old diagnosis → ask what persists → inspect today's rule and capacity
              → test new access/privacy risks → assess actual citizen outcome
```

*The report starts an inquiry; today's evidence finishes it.*

Think of an old diagnosis of traffic congestion: “the junction cannot handle how vehicles move” may still illuminate a problem after a new flyover, but you cannot claim that the old report built the flyover. The Second ARC is **time-bound diagnosis plus proposed design**. Later instruments such as Digital India, Mission Karmayogi and data-protection legislation may address similar issues, yet chronology and thematic resemblance do not prove that a specific ARC recommendation was accepted and implemented through them. To establish that stronger causal claim, trace the report's actual text through a dated government decision to a specified instrument. Without that trail, say **“consistent with an earlier diagnostic concern”** instead.

For an e-service, the useful enduring question from Report 11 is whether digitisation changes the underlying process. A contemporary digital identity or data-sharing architecture adds questions of exclusion, privacy and alternative access that an older prescription may not settle. The test is an actual service's authorised design and effects, not a generic assertion that technology solves bureaucracy. For personnel, Report 10 invites a capabilities question; a later training platform might provide courses but does not demonstrate that promotions, incentives or public behaviour changed. For district administration, Report 15 directs attention to coordination, while new dashboards need independent checks on what happens to a complainant after a file closes.

It describes Sādhana Saptah as a capacity-building initiative under Mission Karmayogi, records a shift from rule-based to role-based competency development and repeatedly connects learning with citizen-centric service outcomes. This supports a present-day illustration of the personnel-capacity problem. It does **not** prove that a specific recommendation in Report 10 was formally adopted, that Report 12 caused the initiative, or that course participation by itself changed frontline outcomes.

**Objection:** If specific instruments age, why read the ARC at all? **Reply:** It supplies a disciplined inventory of failures and dependencies, especially the gap between executive authority, public access and local capacity. The report's value as a benchmark survives even if an individual design is overtaken by a better one. **Residual:** categories themselves can obscure newly emergent harms, including digital exclusion; test old questions against contemporary evidence rather than treating them as a complete checklist.

**UPSC application:** In a “continuing relevance” answer, pair a named report's *diagnosis* with a separately dated *current* initiative where independently verifiable, then qualify causal attribution and examine coverage, rights and outcome. *Jurisdiction changes the implementing actor; politics and capacity change whether the actor can deliver.*

Suppose a report proposes giving panchayats a function. A Union department cannot by agreeing to a commission's suggestion make a state transfer its staff and money. Look for state legislation or rules, assignment of the function, adequate finance, trained personnel and a locally usable complaint channel. Report 6 concerns elected local governance; Report 15 concerns state and district administration. Their interaction matters when a district line department retains control of a function nominally devolved to a panchayat. The specific arrangement varies by state; do not describe every state as identical.

Personnel changes also have multiple principals: a Union cadre policy is not automatically a state service rule. Report 10 raises performance and cadre questions; the likely resistance from officials asked to surrender discretion is an **analytical explanation**, not an allegation against a named service association. Report 13's Union organisational question may be changed by executive action within legal limits, whereas some recommendations require statute or state action. Report 14's financial-management lens asks who controls funds, whether disbursement is timely and whether results justify expenditure. DARPG retains the archive and coordinates follow-up; it cannot substitute for these institutions' legal competence.

The **stakeholder asymmetry** is causal, not merely moral. A reform's gains may be spread across thousands of applicants while the affected approval office loses a visible power immediately. Offering training, transition time and auditable responsibilities can address some resistance, but cannot replace democratic choice where reform reallocates authority. Civil society can publicise failures; it cannot appropriate a government's spending or appoint its officials. A good verdict therefore differentiates a legitimate protection of due process from rent-preserving delay.

**Objection:** If state discretion produces uneven outcomes, should the Union impose one standard? **Reply:** A common minimum for transparent, reviewable service can help; implementation on state subjects still requires constitutionally competent action, context-specific staffing and finance. Uniform paperwork may conceal widely differing ability to comply. **Residual:** comparative assessment needs comparable outcome measures without rewarding states for excluding difficult cases from the denominator.

**UPSC application:** In a 20-mark evaluation, state what each of Reports 6, 10 and 15 diagnoses, identify whose approval and budget each reform needs, examine concentrated losses and diffuse benefits, then distinguish proposed, operational and evaluated changes. **2026 GS-II Q8 (10 marks, 150 words)** asks candidates to *analyse* the advantages and limitations of generalist civil services in contemporary governance. Report 10 provides personnel context; contrast generalists' coordination strengths with specialist needs and a feasible hybrid without claiming direct adoption of an ARC proposal. A new commission is not a substitute for documented accountability over earlier proposals. In Prelims never infer from DARPG's nodal role that it has coercive powers over state or Union line ministries.

- Union, state and local reforms require action by the legally competent authority.
- A Union endorsement cannot by itself transfer a state or local function.
- Functions without finances, functionaries and operational control produce weak devolution.
- Report 6 concerns elected local governance; Report 15 concerns state and district administration.
- Report 10's personnel lens is broader than participation in a current training platform.
- Civil-service placement, tenure and incentives may require different Union and state instruments.
- DARPG can track and coordinate but cannot compel every line ministry or state.
- Diffuse citizen gains and concentrated institutional losses shape reform coalitions.
- Compare authority, capacity, incentives, access, grievance correction and independently assessed outcomes.
- Preserve federal variation and evidentiary uncertainty rather than claiming uniform national implementation.

### 10 marks — comparison and precision (150 words)

**Question:** Explain why the Second ARC is a useful source for GS-II but an unsafe shortcut for claiming that governance reform has been completed.

**Model (approximately 117 words):** The Second ARC, constituted in 2005 under M. Veerappa Moily, produced fifteen reports in 2006–09 on problems spanning *Promoting e-Governance*, *Citizen-Centric Administration* and *Local Governance*. These titles help locate a diagnosis: whether a process is redesigned, whether a citizen has a remedy and whether an elected local body controls resources. But the reports are proposals. DARPG coordinates follow-up; competent ministries or states decide whether to accept, modify or reject a particular recommendation and must separately create any operative instrument. A charter, portal or devolution order may exist without trained officers, effective appeals or demonstrable inclusion. **Scoring guide (10):** accurate time/report context 2; three differentiated named examples 3; decision–instrument–outcome distinction 4; qualified judgment 1.

### 15 marks — implementation test (250 words)

**Question:** Examine the usefulness and limits of DARPG's follow-up role in evaluating the Second ARC's citizen-facing recommendations.

**Model (approximately 154 words):** DARPG provides continuity between the Second ARC's historical reports and government consideration of their proposals. Its report and government-decision listings help a reader begin with the correct question, rather than asserting that all recommendations were adopted. For *Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance*, the issue is whether an applicant receives intelligible standards and a working correction route; for *Promoting e-Governance: The Smart Way Forward*, it is whether digital delivery actually simplifies the underlying process. But DARPG's coordination cannot itself notify a state service rule or compel a line ministry. A recorded acceptance must be followed by the competent authority's instrument, staff, funds, service operation and evidence of inclusion and review. Even if grievance receipt and disposal were independently counted, those counts alone would not prove satisfactory resolution or that either report caused a specific improvement. State jurisdiction and different departmental capacities further limit a single national verdict. The best assessment labels each recommendation's actual decision status, then tests its operative authority and independently observed outcome.

**Scoring guide (15):** accurate coordinating role 3; two distinct named report mechanisms 4; implementation chain and jurisdiction 4; evidence limits 2; balanced conclusion 2.

### 20 marks — reform strategy and critique (250 words)

**Question:** “Another commission may improve diagnosis but not delivery.” Critically analyse this proposition using the Second ARC and propose a realistic reform sequence.

**Model (approximately 218 words):** Expert investigation clarifies public problems; it does not automatically change administrative incentives. The Second ARC's *Organisational Structure of Government of India* foregrounds coordination across Union offices; *Local Governance* asks whether local bodies control funds and functions; *Refurbishing of Personnel Administration* asks whether officials have capacity and appropriate incentives. Those are complementary diagnoses, not operative instructions. If a state devolves a service on paper but keeps the budget and staff with a district department, the local institution cannot answer citizens effectively. If a Union ministry adds a portal without changing the underlying approvals, technology may only hide delay. Concentrated losses of official discretion, divided federal competence and weak budgets can prevent adoption, whereas citizen benefits are dispersed. Yet the “commission fatigue” critique should not imply that diagnosis is useless: a new, evidence-led inquiry can identify emerging digital exclusion or changed legal risks that an older report could not anticipate. The implementation priority is to specify each earlier recommendation and responsible authority; record its decision status; issue a valid, funded instrument; prepare staff and appeals; and independently test exclusions, reversals and resolved grievances. DARPG's follow-up can make stages visible but cannot compel all actors. Thus commission analysis remains valuable if paired with dated action and accountable outcomes; it cannot be substituted for them.

**Scoring guide (20):** critical thesis and counterargument 4; three named report-specific mechanisms 6; federal/incentive causal explanation 4; sequenced accountable strategy 4; qualified conclusion 2.

## From report to a defensible current claim

- Ask of **each** proposal: exact historical report → acceptance/modified/rejected/pending decision → competent authority → valid instrument → working people/budget/process → appeal → distribution and independently assessed outcome.
- DARPG coordinates and archives ARC follow-up; it does not compel a line ministry or state. Do not attach one national “implemented” percentage to all reports.
- A state may have to transfer powers, staff and finances for local or district reform. Formal decentralisation without actual control is a testable gap.
- Newer programmes may fit an ARC diagnosis without being caused by the ARC. In particular, do **not** attribute Digital India to Report 11, Mission Karmayogi to Report 10, or a service-guarantee law to Report 12 without a documented direct decision trail.

## Writing routes and examiner traps

- **10 marks:** era/remit or one report + named illustration + status qualification. **15:** two reports and their distinct mechanisms + concrete bottleneck + authority and evidence. **20:** three domains, jurisdiction/capacity/incentives, dated instrument where verified, outcome and graded verdict.

- The old report can remain a good **diagnosis** when its preferred **instrument** is superseded. Technology can reproduce a bad process; training completion is not changed frontline behaviour; fiscal expenditure is not a citizen outcome.
- Balanced verdict: commission analysis maps problems; government decisions, lawful instruments, capable federal actors, incentives and remedies determine whether people experience reform.
