# Indian Society Topic 14 — Regionalism

Regionalism begins with attachment to a territory, but it becomes politically significant only when identity, representation, resources or administration are organised into a claim. The first discipline is therefore conceptual: cultural difference is not developmental disparity; statehood is not secession; autonomy is not necessarily a step toward statehood; and constitutional regional assertion is not automatically anti-national.

### 1. Region and regional identity

A **region** is a territory perceived as coherent through one or more of language, history, ecology, economy, culture or administrative experience. Its boundary may be socially meaningful even when it is not an official state boundary.

**Regional identity** is a sense of belonging to that region. Identity is descriptive and affective; it becomes political only when organised around a claim.

### 2. Regionalism

**Regionalism** is organised social or political assertion of a region’s distinct identity and interests. The claim may concern:

- recognition of language or culture;
- political representation;
- control over resources;
- administrative access;
- public investment;
- autonomy or statehood.

Regional attachment, cultural pride, regional parties and constitutional statehood demands are not automatically hostile regionalism.

### 3. Sub-regionalism

Sub-regionalism occurs at a smaller scale within an existing state or recognised region. A sub-region may allege neglect by the state capital, under-representation, cultural marginalisation or administrative distance.

### 4. Diversity and disparity

**Diversity** concerns difference. **Regional disparity** concerns unequal outcomes in income, infrastructure, health, education, connectivity or state capacity. A culturally distinct region may be prosperous; a culturally similar sub-region may be much poorer than its neighbour.

### 5. Demand forms are parallel

| Form | Central demand | Constitutional relation |
|---|---|---|
| Statehood | creation of a State within India | internal reorganisation under Article 3 |
| Autonomy | greater self-government without full statehood | councils, devolution or special constitutional/statutory arrangement |
| Secession | separation from the Union | incompatible with the constitutional Union |

One form does not mechanically develop into another. A movement may seek one form from the beginning or change according to negotiation, leadership and political opportunity.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Any strong regional assertion weakens national identity.

**Response:** Democratic federalism permits layered identities. A claim directed toward recognition or fair participation within the Union can strengthen legitimacy.

**Residual:** Regional assertion becomes exclusionary when it denies equal citizenship to internal minorities or treats outsiders as permanently illegitimate.

### UPSC application

- Define the exact concept before giving examples.
- Separate identity, representation and disparity.
- Present statehood, autonomy and secession in parallel.
- Test method, target and constitutional location before calling a movement anti-national.

### Revision notes

1. A region may be cultural, ecological, economic, historical or administrative.
2. Identity is not automatically political regionalism.
3. Regionalism is organised assertion of identity or interest.
4. Sub-regionalism operates within an existing state or region.
5. Diversity is difference; disparity is unequal outcome.
6. Statehood remains within the Union.
7. Autonomy means self-government short of full statehood.
8. Secession seeks exit from the Union.
9. The three demand forms are not an escalation ladder.
10. Regional parties are not inherently separatist.
11. Internal minorities must remain visible.
12. Diagnosis precedes response.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a statehood demand not equivalent to secession?

**Model answer:** Statehood seeks a new constituent State within the Indian Union through constitutional reorganisation. Secession seeks separation from the Union. Their legal location, political objective and implication for sovereignty are different.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not arrange autonomy, statehood and secession as mandatory stages.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish regionalism, sub-regionalism and regional disparity. Why does the distinction matter for policy?

**Complete model:** Regionalism is organised assertion of a region’s identity or interests. Sub-regionalism is a similar assertion at a smaller scale within an existing state or region. Regional disparity is an unequal development outcome in income, infrastructure, services or capability; it is not a cultural identity.

The distinction matters because grievances require different remedies. Linguistic or cultural marginalisation may need recognition and representation. Economic neglect needs investment, connectivity and fair fiscal allocation. Administrative distance may justify decentralisation or Article 3 reorganisation. A compounded sub-regional grievance may require more than one response.

Conflation produces error: an index score cannot settle an identity claim, and symbolic recognition cannot repair a service gap. Statehood, autonomy and secession must also be treated as parallel demand forms. Sound policy begins by identifying scale, driver, affected groups and constitutional method.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** three definitions 4; response logic 3; demand-form qualification 2; conclusion 1.

Once the categories are separated, the next problem is causal: why does one regional condition remain a sentiment while another becomes an organised demand?

Regional difference becomes regionalism through political work. People must interpret a condition as shared, identify a responsible authority, develop a language of claim and organise sustained action.

### 1. Grievance

The underlying condition may be:

- cultural non-recognition;
- uneven infrastructure or public services;
- under-representation;
- administrative remoteness;
- local-resource extraction without perceived benefit;
- migration and labour-market competition;
- historical memory of autonomy.

The condition alone is insufficient. Similar disparities can generate different political responses.

### 2. Framing

A frame converts experience into a public explanation: “our language is ignored,” “our resources leave while development lags,” or “the capital does not represent us.” Frames simplify reality, build solidarity and define the appropriate solution.

### 3. Leadership and organisation

Movements require organisers, networks, finance, symbols and repeated participation. Students’ unions, cultural bodies, tribal organisations, parties and civil-society groups can connect local grievances.

### 4. Political opportunity

Federal elections, coalition governments, commissions, negotiations and changes in ruling alliances can open or close opportunities. A demand may intensify when institutions ignore it and moderate when credible negotiation begins.

### 5. State response and feedback

Recognition, repression, development packages, autonomy, reorganisation or prolonged delay reshape mobilisation. Concession may integrate a movement, satisfy only one faction or produce new internal claims.

### Theoretical anchors

- **Relative deprivation:** mobilisation can arise from the gap between expected and experienced outcomes.
- **Resource mobilisation:** grievance needs organisation and resources.
- **Political opportunity:** institutional openings shape strategy.
- **Elite competition:** leaders may activate identity, but mobilisation cannot be reduced to manipulation when grievance is real.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Regionalism is manufactured by elites.

**Response:** Leadership matters, but successful mobilisation usually connects to recognised experience. Elite framing without a social base often fails.

**Residual:** A movement may represent a region while marginalising women, smaller ethnic groups, migrants or remote districts within it.

### UPSC application

- Use grievance → framing → organisation → opportunity → demand → response.
- Distinguish condition from mobilisation.
- Add counter-mobilisation and internal diversity.
- Explain why equal disparity does not produce equal regionalism.

### Revision notes

1. Difference does not mobilise automatically.
2. Grievance is interpreted experience.
3. Framing supplies a shared causal story.
4. Leadership coordinates.
5. Organisation sustains action.
6. Symbols make a territorial “we.”
7. Political opportunity affects strategy.
8. State response feeds back into movement form.
9. Concessions can integrate or fragment.
10. Elite action needs a social base.
11. Movements contain internal inequalities.
12. Counter-mobilisation may emerge.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two equally lagging regions display different levels of political mobilisation?

**Model answer:** Organisation, leadership, shared identity, framing and political opportunity may differ. Development gaps are conditions; they become movements only when actors convert them into a collective claim and sustain mobilisation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer movement intensity directly from an economic indicator.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain how regional grievance is converted into political mobilisation. Why do similar grievances produce different outcomes?

**Complete model:** A regional condition becomes political only through interpretation and organisation. Cultural non-recognition, disparity, administrative remoteness or resource conflict first generates grievance. Leaders then frame it as a shared regional injustice, identify an authority and propose recognition, redistribution, autonomy or statehood.

Resource-mobilisation theory explains the need for associations, finance, symbols and networks. Ted Gurr’s relative-deprivation framework explains why a gap between expectation and experience matters. Political-opportunity approaches show how elections, commissions, coalition bargaining and state response shape strategy.

Outcomes differ because regions possess different organisational capacity, internal cohesion, leadership and access to institutions. A development package may reduce one grievance but leave identity or representation unresolved. Repression can demobilise, radicalise or fragment. Concessions may also create new minorities within a recognised unit.

Elite competition matters, but it is not a complete explanation. Durable mobilisation normally connects with lived experience. A strong analysis therefore traces condition, frame, organisation, opportunity, response and feedback while examining who is excluded from the movement’s claimed regional “we.”

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** causal chain 4; named theories 3; variation 3; state feedback 2; internal diversity 2; conclusion 1.

Mobilisation explains political activation; the next question is the most visible frame it often uses—culture—and when cultural assertion crosses into exclusion.

Cultural assertiveness is the public affirmation of a group’s language, history, symbols or way of life. It becomes regionalism when organised around territorial recognition, representation or resources.

### 1. Assertiveness is not inherently hostile

Language movements can seek administration and education in a people’s own language. Such recognition can deepen participation and give regional elites a stake in federal institutions.

### 2. The causal pathway

```text
distinct culture
→ fear of neglect or assimilation
→ symbols and historical narrative
→ organisation and territorial framing
→ demand for recognition / autonomy / statehood
```

### 3. When it becomes exclusionary

Assertiveness becomes nativist when it treats fellow citizens as outsiders who lack equal claim to residence, work or public space. It becomes secessionist when sovereignty outside the Union is demanded.

### 4. Accommodation as integration

The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 illustrates recognition through internal federal redesign. It did not grant sovereignty; it aligned administration with linguistic identities while preserving the Union.

### Verified 2020 GS-I PYQ

> “Do you agree that regionalism in India appears to be a consequence of rising cultural assertiveness? Argue.”
> 10 marks · 150 words

**Demand:** accept the identity pathway but qualify it with disparity, representation, leadership and institutional response.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Cultural assertiveness explains regionalism completely.

**Response:** It explains identity-driven regionalism, but development disparity, resource control, representation and administrative distance may act independently or compound identity.

**Residual:** Recognition can satisfy the dominant group while producing insecurity among linguistic, tribal or migrant minorities inside the region.

### Revision notes

1. Cultural pride is not automatically political.
2. Assertiveness is public organised affirmation.
3. Regionalism adds territorial interest and demand.
4. Recognition can deepen integration.
5. Language is a major mobilisation resource.
6. Historical memory creates solidarity.
7. Statehood can remain constitutional.
8. Nativism targets fellow citizens.
9. Secession targets the Union.
10. Cultural assertion is one driver, not the only one.
11. Internal minorities matter.
12. Response can moderate or sharpen assertion.

### Concept check

**Question:** What turns cultural pride into regionalism?

**Model answer:** Political organisation links cultural identity to a territorial claim for recognition, representation, resources, autonomy or statehood. Pride alone is identity; sustained public demand makes it regionalism.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not call every use of regional language or culture regionalism.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Compare constitutional linguistic accommodation with sons-of-soil mobilisation. Why can one integrate regional identity while the other endanger equal citizenship?

**Complete model:** Constitutional linguistic accommodation recognises a region’s language in administration, education and state boundaries while retaining common Union citizenship. The 1956 reorganisation gave regional identities democratic arenas without conferring sovereignty, channelling assertion into elections, legislation and federal bargaining.

Sons-of-soil mobilisation instead converts territorial belonging into priority over other Indian citizens in jobs, housing, education or public space. It may arise from genuine unemployment or language anxiety, but it targets migrants rather than the structural cause. Its method can therefore be exclusionary even when the underlying grievance is real.

The decisive tests are target and safeguards. Integrative accommodation expands voice while respecting mobility, equality and internal minorities; exclusionary nativism makes origin a permanent test of entitlement. Policy should combine cultural recognition and local capability-building with equal citizenship and migrant protection. Regional identity strengthens integration when institutionalised as participation, not used to rank citizens.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** comparison of the two forms 4; named accommodation evidence 2; equal-citizenship test 2; qualification and policy response 1; verdict 1.

Cultural explanation alone cannot show whether a region is materially disadvantaged. We therefore next separate identity difference from unequal development outcomes.

### Visual: four possible combinations

```text
                         DISPARITY
                     LOW          HIGH
DIVERSITY   HIGH   distinct +   distinct +
                   comparable   lagging

            LOW    similar +    similar +
                   comparable   unequal

Diversity and disparity can overlap, but neither proves the other.
```

**Diversity** is the presence of distinct linguistic, cultural, ecological or historical identities. **Regional disparity** is unequal development outcome among states, regions or sub-regions.

### 1. Measurement objects

Disparity may concern:

- income and employment;
- health and education;
- infrastructure and connectivity;
- urbanisation and market access;
- fiscal and administrative capacity;
- gender, caste and tribal outcomes;
- rural-urban and district gaps.

### 2. Scale matters

An inter-state comparison can hide a lagging district cluster within a better-performing state. A state average can also hide tribal areas, border regions and unequal cities. “Regional” must specify state, sub-region, district or ecological zone.

### 3. Diversity without disparity

Two regions may differ in language and culture while showing comparable capability outcomes. Cultural difference does not itself establish deprivation.

### 4. Disparity without pronounced diversity

Two culturally similar sub-regions can diverge because of connectivity, historical investment, ecology, industrial location or state capacity.

### Verified 2024 GS-I PYQ

> “What is regional disparity? How does it differ from diversity? How serious is the issue of regional disparity in India?”
> 15 marks · 250 words

**Demand:** define, distinguish, assess seriousness through dated evidence and retain scale and causal cautions.

### Related 2024 GS-I support demand

**2024 GS-I Q20 — neutral demand:** critically analyse the correlation between cultural diversity and socio-economic marginalities (15 marks, 250 words). Apply a bounded test: never infer marginality from cultural difference without evidence, and disaggregate outcomes by territory and by class, caste, gender and tribe.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Cultural distinctiveness often coincides with backwardness, so the distinction is artificial.

**Response:** Overlap is empirical, not definitional. Keeping axes separate allows recognition and redistribution to be matched correctly.

**Residual:** Identity can affect development through discrimination or political bargaining; independence does not mean no interaction.

### UPSC application

- Define both terms before discussing seriousness.
- Specify geographic scale.
- Use outcome indicators, not stereotypes.
- Add sub-state and causation limitations.
- Match cultural recognition to diversity and redistribution to disparity.

### Revision notes

1. Diversity concerns difference.
2. Disparity concerns unequal outcome.
3. The axes are analytically independent.
4. All four high/low combinations are possible.
5. Scale must be stated.
6. State averages can hide sub-regions.
7. Composite scores do not reveal lived experience fully.
8. Cultural identity is not a poverty proxy.
9. Similar cultures can diverge economically.
10. Identity and disparity can compound.
11. Recognition and redistribution solve different problems.
12. A trend does not prove cause.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a state-level development average be inadequate for analysing sub-regionalism?

**Model answer:** It may conceal district, tribal, border or rural-urban gaps within the state. Sub-regionalism often arises precisely from unequal internal distribution, so evidence must match the smaller territorial scale.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use a state rank as proof about every locality.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Regional diversity and regional disparity are independent but interacting axes. Explain.

**Complete model:** Regional diversity describes differences in language, culture, ecology or historical experience. Regional disparity describes unequal outcomes in income, infrastructure, health, education or state capacity. One is descriptive identity; the other is measurable development.

Their independence is visible in four possible combinations: culturally distinct regions may be similarly developed; culturally similar regions may diverge sharply; both difference and disparity may coincide; or both may be limited. Colonial investment, connectivity, ecology, industrial location and administrative capacity shape economic trajectories separately from culture.

Interaction remains possible. A culturally distinct, lagging sub-region may frame economic neglect as denial of identity. Conversely, political recognition may improve voice without removing material gaps. State averages can also conceal district or tribal disparity.

The distinction changes policy. Recognition, language rights and representation answer diversity-related grievances; infrastructure, services and fiscal investment address disparity. Compounded cases require both. NITI Aayog’s SDG India Index can support state comparison, but sub-state and causal limits must be stated.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** definitions 3; independence matrix 4; interaction 2; scale limitation 2; response fit 3; conclusion 1.

Once diversity and disparity are separated, seriousness can no longer rest on impression; it must be measured with a stated indicator basket and at the correct territorial scale.

### 1. The NITI Aayog SDG India Index 2023–24

The 2023–24 edition covers **16 Goals, 70 targets and 113 indicators**. Raw indicators with different units are normalised to a 0–100 scale. Goal scores are arithmetic means of available normalised indicators with equal weight; the composite score is the arithmetic mean of 16 goal scores.

Categories are:

| Score | Category |
|---:|---|
| 100 | Achiever |
| 65–99 | Front Runner |
| 50–64 | Performer |
| below 50 | Aspirant |

### 2. What it can establish

- comparative state/UT performance on a stated indicator basket;
- areas where goal scores differ;
- a dated benchmark for discussing broad inter-state disparity;
- a common language for monitoring.

### 3. What it cannot establish alone

- disparity within a state or district cluster;
- causal effect of one programme;
- lived discrimination or political voice;
- fiscal capacity not represented by the basket;
- the grievance of a specific movement;
- permanent rank independent of edition and indicator choice.

### 4. Measurement dashboard

A serious regional-disparity answer should combine:

- income/employment;
- health and education outcomes;
- connectivity and infrastructure;
- fiscal and administrative capacity;
- rural-urban and district disaggregation;
- group differences by class, caste, gender and tribe;
- qualitative evidence of access and voice.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A composite score hides too much to be useful.

**Reply:** Aggregation is useful for overview and comparison if the basket, edition and scale are stated and the score is decomposed before diagnosis.

**Residual:** Indicator selection and equal weighting are normative choices; rankings must not replace analysis.

### UPSC application

- Cite “SDG India Index 2023–24,” not an undated index.
- State 16 Goals, 70 targets and 113 indicators only when methodology is relevant.
- Avoid state ranks unless directly needed and verified.
- Add sub-state, causation and indicator-choice limitations.

### Revision notes

1. Disparity requires measurable outcomes.
2. Edition and geography must be named.
3. SDG Index 2023–24 uses 16 Goals.
4. It covers 70 targets and 113 indicators.
5. Indicators are normalised to 0–100.
6. Goal and composite scores use arithmetic means.
7. Equal weighting is a methodological choice.
8. State scores can hide district gaps.
9. Composite scores do not prove cause.
10. Rankings do not equal lived experience.
11. Decomposition precedes policy.
12. Use quantitative and qualitative evidence together.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the SDG India Index compare states but not diagnose a particular sub-regional movement?

**Model answer:** The state/UT composite aggregates selected indicators at a broad scale. A movement may arise from district-level neglect, identity, representation or resource conflict that the state average and indicator basket do not capture.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer movement cause from a composite score.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How should regional disparity in India be measured without reducing it to one composite ranking?

**Complete model:** Regional disparity is unequal capability and opportunity across territories. Measurement must therefore identify geography, indicators, period and affected groups.

NITI Aayog’s SDG India Index 2023–24 offers a useful state/UT benchmark. It covers 16 Goals, 70 targets and 113 indicators, normalises values to 0–100 and aggregates goal scores. It permits comparable overview across health, education, poverty and other outcomes.

Its limits are decisive. Equal weighting and indicator choice shape the score; a state average can conceal a lagging district, tribal region or rural-urban divide; correlation does not identify cause; and political voice or cultural non-recognition may be absent from the basket.

A complete dashboard should add income and employment, infrastructure, fiscal and administrative capacity, district data, disaggregation by class, caste, gender and tribe, and qualitative access evidence. Trends should be decomposed by goal rather than read only from the composite.

The index is therefore a starting map, not a final diagnosis. Good policy follows granular evidence and distinguishes material disparity from identity grievance.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** definition/dashboard 3; exact NITI method 4; limitations 4; disaggregation 2; conclusion 2.

Measurement shows where gaps appear, not why they persist. The next lesson compares the mechanisms that can produce the same observed divergence.

## Lesson 6 — Why Regions Diverge

Progress: 6 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Relative deprivation, cumulative causation, centre-periphery, identity and institutions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Disparity and mobilisation theories offer competing explanations for regional divergence.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: five explanations of divergence

```text
REGIONAL DIVERGENCE
 |
 +-- RELATIVE DEPRIVATION: expectations exceed experienced outcomes
 +-- CUMULATIVE CAUSATION: growth attracts more capital/skills/connectivity
 +-- CENTRE-PERIPHERY: core controls resources and decisions
 +-- IDENTITY/RECOGNITION: culture lacks status or voice
```

### 1. Relative deprivation

Ted Gurr’s broad idea of relative deprivation focuses on the gap between expected and experienced outcomes. A region may mobilise after comparison with a neighbouring region even if its absolute condition improved.

### 2. Cumulative causation

Gunnar Myrdal’s cumulative-causation logic distinguishes **spread effects** from **backwash effects**. Growth centres attract capital, skills and markets; weaker regions may lose labour and investment unless connectivity and public policy generate countervailing spread.

### 3. Centre-periphery or internal colonialism

Michael Hechter’s internal-colonialism perspective highlights cultural division of labour and core control over peripheral resources and institutions. Applied cautiously, it helps analyse extraction grievances; it should not be asserted without evidence of power and unequal exchange.

### 4. Identity and recognition

A culturally distinct region may seek recognition even without severe economic disparity. Misrecognition can concern language, symbols, administration or political voice.

### 5. Institutional path dependence

Colonial infrastructure, princely-state legacies, terrain, conflict, land relations, education and state capacity can produce different development paths. Geography constrains but does not determine outcome.

### Comparison

|---|---|---|
| Relative deprivation | comparison-driven grievance | expectations difficult to measure |
| Cumulative causation | capital/skill concentration | policy can reverse trends |
| Centre-periphery | extraction and decision asymmetry | risk of overgeneralisation |
| Recognition | identity without poverty | may understate material interest |
| Institutional path | persistent historical variation | can become descriptive |

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Divergence is simply geography.

**Response:** Terrain and location matter through infrastructure, state capacity, markets and policy. Similar geographies can diverge institutionally.

**Residual:** Several theories may fit one case; evidence must establish the dominant mechanism.

- Distinguish absolute condition from comparative grievance.
- Explain backwash and spread effects.
- Use centre-periphery only with evidence of unequal control.
- End with a multilinear verdict.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Compare major theories of regional divergence and show why no single theory explains Indian regionalism.

**Complete model:** Regional divergence can be explained through several mechanisms. The North Eastern Council, created by the 1971 Act, has a separate regional planning and coordination role.

These forums can clarify data, coordinate infrastructure, discuss borders and linguistic minorities and involve states in Union decisions. Their repeated bargaining function is valuable even though recommendations are advisory.

Their weakness is institutional under-use. Infrequent meetings, weak follow-up, central agenda control and poor links to finance reduce trust. Nor can consultation substitute for cultural recognition, redistribution or autonomy where these are the actual grievances.

The institutions are therefore preventive federal infrastructure. Their effectiveness depends on regular convening, published follow-up, state agenda-setting and connection between dialogue and implementation.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** ISC 3; Zonal Councils 3; NEC distinction 3; grievance mechanism 3; limitations/reforms 2; verdict 1.

Coordination supplies forums, yet demands also need electoral carriers. The next question is how regional parties can federalise claims—or turn territorial identity inward against migrants.

### 1. Regional parties

Regional parties can:

- aggregate territorially concentrated preferences;
- raise language, autonomy and development concerns;
- bargain in coalitions;
- innovate in welfare and administration;
- increase accountability to state-specific conditions;
- check excessive centralisation.

They are not inherently anti-national. Participation in Union institutions can convert regional identity into federal bargaining.

### 2. Limitations

- patronage and personality-centred organisation;
- elite capture of regional identity;
- short-term bargaining over long-term development;
- marginalisation of smaller sub-regions;
- shifting autonomy positions depending on whether a party holds Union power.

### 3. Sons-of-soil politics

Sons-of-soil politics claims priority for locally defined “sons” in jobs, housing, education or public space. It often arises from urban migration, labour competition and cultural anxiety.

The Shiv Sena, founded in 1966 by Bal Thackeray, is the classical named example of a sons-of-soil platform. Its relevance is analytical: regional claims can turn inward against fellow citizens.

### 4. Legitimate concern versus exclusion

Local unemployment, language access and uneven urbanisation are legitimate issues. The constitutional problem begins when birth or origin is used to deny equal citizenship, mobility or safety.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** National parties alone protect integration.

**Response:** Regional parties have often integrated distinct identities through electoral competition and federal negotiation. National parties can also centralise or use regional rhetoric.

**Residual:** Institutional participation does not prevent nativist mobilisation; party form and political content must be assessed separately.

### UPSC application

- Separate regional party from regionalism as a phenomenon.
- Assess demands, method and treatment of internal minorities.
- Use coalition bargaining as integrative evidence.
- Use sons-of-soil as a distinct exclusionary form.

Sons-of-soil politics is analytically distinct. It claims priority for locally defined populations in jobs, housing or public space, often amid migration and labour competition. The Shiv Sena’s 1966 origins provide a named Indian example. Legitimate concerns about language access or unemployment become exclusionary when fellow citizens are denied mobility, security or equal opportunity.

The criterion is therefore method and target. Regional representation within constitutional citizenship strengthens federal democracy; nativism that turns regional identity against internal migrants weakens it.

### 1. Recognition

Use when language, culture, symbols or historical dignity are central. Recognition can include official use, education, cultural institutions and symbolic representation.

### 2. Representation and devolution

Use when decisions are remote or the group lacks voice. Councils, local government, administrative decentralisation and meaningful consultation can help.

### 3. Redistribution and capability

Use when disparity drives the grievance. Infrastructure, education, health, connectivity, jobs and fair fiscal support are relevant. A generic package without accountability may fail.

### 4. Reorganisation

Statehood or boundary change can address administration and representation through Article 3. It does not guarantee development and requires transition planning.

### 5. Autonomy

Use where territorial self-government and cultural institutions matter but full statehood is not the chosen form. Sixth Schedule, Article 244A and statutory arrangements must not be conflated.

### 6. Coordination

Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and NEC can address shared issues, but advisory discussion requires follow-up and finance.

### 7. Security and dialogue

Violence requires protection of citizens and rule of law. Security cannot replace political diagnosis; nor can dialogue excuse coercion.

### Mismatch failures

| Wrong response | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| development package for identity denial | material input does not supply recognition |
| symbolic recognition for service gap | grievance remains materially unresolved |
| statehood for every administrative issue | high-cost instrument used without alternatives |
| security frame for constitutional demand | securitises democratic assertion |
| autonomy without internal rights | reproduces exclusion inside the unit |

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Multi-instrument diagnosis delays decisive action.

**Response:** Mismatched decisive action can deepen grievance. Sequencing and combined responses are often faster than repeated failure.

**Residual:** Grievances evolve; policy needs feedback, local evidence and periodic review.

### UPSC application

- State the grievance category.
- Name the matching instrument.
- Add implementation and internal-minority safeguards.
- Use NITI measurement only for broad disparity diagnosis.
- Conclude with recognition plus fairness plus federal dialogue.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a large infrastructure package fail to reduce an identity-driven regional movement?

**Model answer:** The movement may seek recognition, language status or political voice rather than only material investment. Redistribution without recognition leaves the central grievance unanswered.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume every regional demand is caused by underdevelopment.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Design a diagnosis-based response framework for regionalism in India.

**Complete model:** Regionalism is driven by different combinations of identity, representation, disparity, administration and resource control. A uniform response therefore fails.

Cultural and linguistic grievances need recognition, language access and symbolic equality. Under-representation needs devolution, councils and effective consultation. Development disparity needs infrastructure, services, connectivity and rules-based fiscal support. Administrative distance may justify decentralisation or, after alternatives are tested, Article 3 reorganisation. Tribal self-government requires a precise choice among Fifth Schedule protection, Sixth Schedule councils and Article 244A; these are not one ladder.

Inter-state issues need the Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils or NEC according to legal and regional scope. Violence requires security and rule of law, but a security frame cannot replace political dialogue or fair development.

Every response needs disaggregated evidence, internal-minority rights, grievance redress and follow-up. NITI Aayog’s SDG India Index can indicate broad state gaps but cannot diagnose a movement’s cause.

Regional accommodation succeeds when recognition, representation and redistribution match the actual grievance rather than being offered as interchangeable concessions.

## Check 1 — Conceptual firewall

**Question:** A culturally distinct region has outcomes comparable to neighbouring states. Which concept is present and which is unproven?

**Model answer:** Diversity is present. Regional disparity is unproven because cultural difference does not establish unequal development.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why statehood, autonomy and secession should not be treated as stages of one regionalist ladder.

**Model answer:** The three forms have different objectives. Statehood creates a State within the Indian Union through Article 3. Autonomy grants self-government within an existing state or constitutional order, as through Sixth Schedule councils or the Article 244A route inside Assam. Secession seeks sovereign exit from the Union.

Movements may choose one form according to identity, scale, representation and institutional opportunity. Bodoland’s special council design cannot be read as an automatic step to statehood, and a constitutional statehood demand cannot be securitised as secession.

The distinction changes policy. Autonomy may fit a territorially concentrated ethnic claim; statehood may fit broad administrative and representational grievance; secession requires a different sovereignty and security analysis. Parallel classification prevents false escalation narratives.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three forms, uses named constitutional evidence and demonstrates why diagnosis changes response.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** definitions 4; named provisions 2; non-ladder logic 2; policy implication 1; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Regional disparity is a problem of scale as much as of measurement. Discuss.

**Model answer:** Regional disparity is unequal development outcome across territories. Its seriousness depends on the scale selected: inter-state, sub-state, district, tribal, border or rural-urban.

NITI Aayog’s SDG India Index 2023–24 provides a state/UT benchmark across 16 Goals, 70 targets and 113 indicators. Normalised 0–100 scores permit comparison, but equal weighting and indicator selection shape the composite. A state average can conceal a lagging sub-region.

Scale also affects causal explanation. Poor connectivity, historical investment, ecology and administrative capacity may produce district divergence that a state ranking cannot explain. Cultural identity and under-representation can compound disparity without being measured by the same indicators.

The answer should therefore combine composite overview with goal-level decomposition, district and group data, fiscal and infrastructure evidence and qualitative access or voice. A movement’s claim must be tested at its territorial scale.

Regional disparity is not captured by one rank. Measurement is useful when its geographic unit, method and limitations match the grievance being analysed.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses exact NITI methodology, explains scale mismatch and connects measurement to causal diagnosis.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** definition/scale 3; NITI method 4; limitations 3; granular dashboard 3; conclusion 2.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Build an integrated framework for reconciling regional assertion with national integration in India.

**Model answer:** Reconciliation begins with a conceptual firewall. Diversity, disparity, regionalism, autonomy, statehood and secession are distinct. The grievance must then be diagnosed as identity-based, representational, developmental, administrative or compounded.

Mobilisation links grievance to leadership, framing and political opportunity. Cultural assertion can be integrated through recognition; disparity requires capability and infrastructure; under-representation requires devolution and voice. Article 3 permits state reorganisation after consultation, not consent. Sixth Schedule councils and Article 244A offer distinct autonomy routes.

History supplies evidence. Andhra in 1953 and the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 show that linguistic recognition can strengthen unity. Regional parties channel territorial interests into elections and coalition bargaining. The Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and NEC provide consultative coordination.

Critical safeguards remain. New states can reproduce internal disparity; autonomous units can marginalise smaller groups; sons-of-soil politics attacks equal citizenship; composite scores mask sub-state grievance; security cannot replace dialogue.

National integration is therefore an institutional achievement: recognition, representation and redistribution must operate within common rights, constitutional method, internal-minority protection and accountable review.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates concepts, mobilisation, history, constitutional tools, coordination and critical limits in one executable framework.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Conceptual firewall

- Region: historically/culturally/economically/ecologically perceived territory.
- Regional identity: belonging; not automatically politics.
- Regionalism: organised assertion of territorial identity/interest.
- Sub-regionalism: smaller-scale assertion within a state/region.
- Diversity: cultural/ecological/historical difference.
- Disparity: unequal development outcome.
- Statehood: new State within Union under Article 3.
- Autonomy: self-government within existing order.
- Secession: sovereign exit.
- Statehood/autonomy/secession are parallel forms, not a ladder.

## Mobilisation

- Condition → grievance → framing → leadership → organisation → opportunity → demand → response.
- Drivers: identity, disparity, representation, administration, resources.
- Relative deprivation: expectation-experience gap.
- Resource mobilisation: grievance needs networks and resources.
- Political opportunity shapes strategy.
- Elites matter but cannot invent every grievance.
- Internal minorities and counter-mobilisation remain relevant.

## Cultural assertiveness

- Cultural pride becomes regionalism when organised territorially.
- Recognition demands can be integrative.
- Nativism targets fellow citizens.
- Secession targets the Union.
- Cultural assertion is one driver, not the only one.
- 1956 is the major accommodation example.

## Diversity versus disparity

- Independent axes; all four combinations possible.
- Specify inter-state, sub-state, district or ecological scale.
- State average can mask tribal/border/rural-urban gaps.
- Recognition addresses difference; redistribution addresses unequal outcome.
- Compounded grievance requires both.

## SDG India Index 2023–24

- 16 Goals, 70 targets, 113 indicators.
- Indicators normalised to 0–100.
- Goal scores: arithmetic mean with equal indicator weight.
- Composite: arithmetic mean of 16 goal scores.
- Achiever 100; Front Runner 65–99; Performer 50–64; Aspirant below 50.
- Use as dated state/UT benchmark.
- Not sub-state diagnosis, lived experience or causal proof.
- Disaggregate by territory and by class, caste, gender and tribe.
- Use the 2023–24 edition as a dated state/UT benchmark with the stated scale and method limits.

## Divergence theories

- Ted Gurr: relative deprivation.
- Myrdal: cumulative causation; spread versus backwash.
- Hechter: internal colonialism/centre-periphery, used cautiously.
- Recognition: identity and dignity.
- Path dependence: history, ecology and state capacity.
- India requires multilinear explanation.

## Linguistic chronology

- Odisha: separate province formed on linguistic lines in 1936.
- Dar Commission: 1948.
- JVP Committee: December 1948.
- Sriramulu fast: 19 October 1952; death after 58 days.
- Andhra: October 1953, first linguistic State created after Independence.
- SRC: 1953–55; Fazl Ali, Panikkar, Kunzru.
- Act + Seventh Amendment: 1956; 14 States, 6 UTs.
- Maharashtra/Gujarat: 1960.
- Official Languages Act: 1963; 1965 accommodation.
- Punjab/Haryana: 1966; Chandigarh UT/shared capital.
- Recognition strengthened unity but left minorities/borders/nativism.

## Sub-regionalism and new States

- Drivers: identity, neglect, representation, administration.
- Responses range from decentralisation to statehood.
- 2000: Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand.
- Telangana: 2014.
- Statehood may improve voice/access but not guarantee development.
- New states can reproduce internal disparity.
- Gorkhaland used only as undated statehood-demand example.

## Tribal and ethnic autonomy

- Sixth Schedule: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram.
- Autonomous district/regional councils with specified powers.
- Article 244A: autonomous State within Assam with legislature/CoM route.
- Fifth Schedule: protective/advisory model, not Sixth Schedule council.
- Bodoland has a special Sixth Schedule council design; no chronology asserted.
- Internal minorities and accountability required.

## Articles 1–4

- Article 1: Union of States.
- Article 2: admit/establish States.
- Article 3: form/alter State area, boundaries, name.
- President’s recommendation.
- State legislature gives views; views not binding.
- Consultation, not consent.
- Article 4: schedules/consequential provisions; simple-majority law, not Article 368 amendment.
- Berubari: foreign cession needs Article 368.

## Coordination institutions

- ISC: Article 263 provision; 1990 Presidential Order; PM chair; advisory.
- Zonal Councils: SRA 1956; five; Home Minister chair; statutory/advisory.
- NEC: NEC Act 1971; separate North-Eastern planning/coordination body.
- None is a legislature or court.
- Regularity, agenda-setting and follow-up matter.

## Parties and sons-of-soil

- Regional parties aggregate territorial preferences and federal bargaining.
- Electoral participation can integrate.
- Limits: elite capture, patronage, smaller-subregion neglect.
- Sons-of-soil claims local priority against migrants.
- Shiv Sena: founded 1966 by Bal Thackeray, classical named case.
- Equal citizenship is the test.

## Response formula

- Identity → recognition.
- Representation → devolution/voice.
- Disparity → redistribution/capability.
- Administrative distance → decentralisation/reorganisation.
- Tribal self-rule → correct Fifth/Sixth/244A form.
- Shared problems → ISC/Zonal/NEC.
- Violence → security plus rights and dialogue.
- Always add internal-minority protection, grievance and review.
