# Indian Society Topic 11 — Effects of Globalisation on Indian Society

Globalisation is often described as if an outside force simply enters India and makes society more “Western.” That picture is too crude. Cross-border flows of capital, goods, services, people, technology, images and ideas pass through Indian institutions. Families negotiate them, firms redesign them, platforms rank them, states regulate them and communities adapt or resist them. The outcome is therefore neither uniform nor one-directional.

The central question is:

> **Through which channels does globalisation alter Indian work, consumption, identity, family life, media, youth and access to resources—and why are its gains and costs distributed unevenly?**

## Visual: five processes that overlap but are not synonyms

| Process | Core movement | Main social question |
|---|---|---|
| **Internationalisation** | More interaction across states | Are cross-border links increasing? |
| **Liberalisation** | Reduction of state restrictions on economic activity | Are markets and investment more open? |
| **Globalisation** | Dense cross-border flows reorganise domestic relations | How are institutions and everyday life being reworked? |
| **Westernisation** | Adoption of institutions, values or styles associated with Western contact | Which external cultural influences are being adopted? |
| **Modernisation** | Structural differentiation, technology, rational-legal organisation | Are institutions and roles changing in form and function? |

*The processes may reinforce one another, but none can be used as a complete substitute for another.*

### 1. Begin with an ordinary object

Consider a smartphone assembled through a transnational supply chain, sold through an Indian platform, paid for through Indian digital infrastructure, used for regional-language videos and governed by domestic law. Is this merely a foreign product entering India?

No. The object carries several flows at once:

```text
global design and components
          +
Indian retail, payment and regulation
          +
local language and family use
          =
a socially embedded global connection
```

Globalisation is therefore not simply “more foreign things.” It is the intensification of cross-border flows that reorganises production, work, communication, consumption, identity and power within society.

### 2. A precise definition

**Globalisation** is a process through which cross-border flows of goods, services, capital, people, technology, information and cultural forms become denser and faster, increasing interdependence and altering domestic institutions and social relations.

Three elements matter:

1. **Flow:** something crosses or operates across borders.
2. **Mediation:** an Indian institution—firm, platform, state, family or community—receives and reshapes it.
3. **Social effect:** roles, opportunities, norms or power relations change.

If an answer lists foreign brands but never identifies mediation or social effect, it has described exposure, not analysed globalisation.

### 3. Liberalisation is a policy route, not the whole process

Liberalisation can accelerate globalisation by reducing trade, investment or market restrictions. Yet globalisation also proceeds through migration, communication networks, cultural circulation and transnational production.

```text
liberalisation
   -> opens selected economic channels
globalisation
   -> works through economic + social + technological + cultural channels
```

India’s post-1991 reforms are therefore an important context, but the effects of globalisation on family authority, language, food habits or digital identity cannot be derived from tariff policy alone.

### 4. Globalisation is not identical to westernisation

Westernisation names one direction of cultural or institutional influence. Globalisation is wider:

- Japanese production methods can shape an Indian factory.
- Korean popular culture can circulate among Indian youth.
- Indian yoga, cinema, cuisine and digital talent can travel outward.
- A global platform can carry Bhojpuri, Tamil or Khasi content.

The process is multi-centred and can strengthen a local form by giving it a larger audience.

### 5. Globalisation is not identical to modernisation

Modernisation refers to structural change such as bureaucratic organisation, technological systems, differentiated occupations and rational-legal institutions. A society can modernise through domestic policy without deep global integration. It can also globalise selectively while retaining institutions that are neither modernised nor egalitarian.

For example, an export-oriented firm may use advanced technology while recruitment still follows kin or caste networks. Global economic integration and internal social modernisation have moved at different speeds.

### 6. Example and limit

An Indian craft producer selling through an international e-commerce channel illustrates globalisation: market reach expands beyond national borders. If the producer redesigns motifs solely to meet platform trends, cultural and market power also shift.

The example does not prove that every artisan gains. Language, logistics, digital literacy, platform fees and bargaining power decide who can use the channel.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** “Globalisation” is so broad that it explains everything and therefore explains nothing.

**Reply:** The concept becomes testable when an answer names the flow, mediator, mechanism, affected group and outcome. “Global media altered youth” is vague; “algorithmically distributed transnational content changed the visibility and imitation of particular lifestyles among connected youth” specifies a mechanism.

**Residual issue:** Several forces act together. Urbanisation, education, domestic policy and technological change may accompany globalisation. Causal claims must therefore remain qualified.

## UPSC application

Use the formula:

```text
flow -> Indian mediator -> social mechanism -> differentiated outcome -> qualification
```

This prevents three common errors: equating globalisation with westernisation, treating liberalisation as the whole story, and asserting causation from mere temporal association.

1. Globalisation concerns intensified cross-border flows and domestic restructuring.
2. Internationalisation means more cross-border interaction; it need not imply deep restructuring.
3. Liberalisation opens selected economic channels but is not the whole process.
4. Westernisation is one possible cultural direction, not a synonym for globalisation.
5. Modernisation concerns structural-rational institutional change.
6. A global flow acquires social meaning through Indian mediators.
7. Goods, services, capital, people, technology, information and culture are distinct channels.
8. Globalisation can carry influences into and out of India.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the statement “India liberalised in 1991, therefore every later social change was caused by globalisation” analytically weak?

**Model answer:** Liberalisation opened important economic channels, but later social changes also reflect domestic policy, urbanisation, education, technology and group agency. A valid explanation must identify the particular global flow, its Indian mediator and the mechanism linking it to the claimed outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use chronology—one event occurring before another—as sufficient proof of causation.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks

**Mains question:** Distinguish globalisation from liberalisation, westernisation and modernisation. Why do these distinctions improve Indian Society answers? Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** Globalisation is the intensification of cross-border flows that reorganises domestic economic and social relations. Liberalisation is a policy process that reduces selected market restrictions and may accelerate global integration. Westernisation is the adoption of values, institutions or styles associated with Western contact. Modernisation is wider structural-rational change in institutions, technology and occupational roles.

The distinctions prevent false causal claims. India’s 1991 reforms opened trade and investment, but family change or digital identity cannot be inferred from tariff reform alone. Regional-language content on global platforms also shows that globalisation need not equal Western cultural replacement. Similarly, advanced production can coexist with caste- or kin-mediated recruitment, showing globalisation without complete social modernisation.

An answer should therefore trace a specific flow through an Indian mediator to a differentiated outcome. This turns “global influence” from a slogan into a sociological mechanism.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines four concepts, uses Indian illustrations, explains the analytical value of the distinctions and ends with a usable causal method.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** four accurate distinctions 4; Indian examples 2; causal significance 2; qualification 1; conclusion 1.

## Visual: the channel-and-filter model

```text
GLOBAL FLOWS
capital · jobs · migration · media · technology · consumer goods
                         |
                         v
                  ACCESS FILTERS
        income · skill · language · location · connectivity
                         |
                         v
                 INSTITUTIONAL FILTERS
       platform · employer · family · community · state
                         |
                         v
                   SOCIAL OUTCOMES
      opportunity / voice / mobility / insecurity / exclusion
                         |
                         v
               DIFFERENTIATED EXPERIENCE
        class · caste · gender · region · age · disability
```

*Globalisation does not arrive at an undifferentiated “Indian society”; it passes through unequal access and power.*

### 1. Why channels must be separated

Different global flows work through different mechanisms:

| Channel | Immediate mediator | Possible gain | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade and investment | Firm and supply chain | jobs, markets, products | displacement, weak bargaining |
| Labour mobility | Employer, recruiter, household | income, autonomy, skill | separation, insecurity |
| Digital platforms | algorithm, data, network | market and voice | surveillance, dominance, exclusion |
| Media and culture | platform, language market, peer group | exposure, expression | standardisation, stereotyping |
| Consumption | advertising, credit, retail, household | choice, convenience | status pressure, debt, waste |
| Technology | firm, school, state, household | productivity, access | skill divide, resource demand |

One channel cannot stand in for all others. A person may gain culturally from wider media access while losing economically through insecure work.

### 2. Access is not the same as capability

**Access** means that an opportunity or technology is available. **Capability** means that a person has the real freedom and resources to use it meaningfully.

An online market may technically be open to a rural producer. Capability additionally requires:

- a device and reliable connection;
- language and digital literacy;
- logistics and working capital;
- knowledge of platform rules;
- bargaining and grievance capacity.

Thus connectivity is a gateway, not an outcome.

### 3. Mediation changes the effect

Globalisation acts through institutions:

- A **family** may encourage a son’s migration but restrict a daughter’s.
- A **platform** may give a seller national reach while ranking products opaquely.
- An **employer** may value global skills while offering short contracts.
- A **community** may adapt global forms into local idioms.
- The **state** may build infrastructure, regulate competition or protect labour.

The same external flow can therefore generate different outcomes depending on the mediator.

### 4. Intersectionality

**Intersectionality** means that social positions overlap. Gender does not operate separately from class, caste, region or skill.

Compare two young women exposed to the same global service economy:

```text
Woman A: urban education + English + safe transport + networks
          -> greater chance of formal employment and autonomy

Woman B: weak schooling + care burden + unsafe commute + poor connectivity
          -> opportunity exists formally but remains unusable
```

The example shows distribution, not a universal rule. Individual pathways vary, and no single identity mechanically determines outcome.

### 5. Positive and negative effects can coexist

Globalisation may widen choice while deepening dependency:

- A platform worker receives flexible entry but faces algorithmic control.
- A migrant gains income but loses nearby care support.
- A local artist reaches global audiences but must adapt to platform visibility rules.
- Consumers gain variety but encounter status competition and persuasive marketing.

The correct unit of analysis is not “benefit versus harm” in the abstract. It is **who gains what, through which institution, under what conditions and at whose cost**.

### 6. Measurement discipline

Aggregate statistics can hide distribution. A national labour-force rate cannot identify a specific age, marital-status or migration stream. Platform transaction growth cannot reveal workers’ net earnings. Internet use cannot prove equal voice.

Use evidence in layers:

```text
aggregate indicator
        +
group disaggregation
        +
institutional mechanism
        +
qualitative experience
```

Each layer answers a different question.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Emphasising inequality makes it impossible to say anything general about globalisation.

**Reply:** A general theory can identify recurring mechanisms—access filters, institutional mediation and unequal bargaining—without claiming identical outcomes. Conditional generalisation is stronger than either anecdote or determinism.

**Residual issue:** Good disaggregated data are not always available. Answers should state the limit rather than substitute a convenient national average.

## UPSC application

For any effects question, build a two-column answer:

1. the channel and potential gain;
2. the filter that makes the gain unequal or converts it into risk.

This structure produces balance without artificial “pros and cons.”

1. Globalisation operates through multiple channels.
2. Each channel has a distinct mediator and mechanism.
3. Access does not equal capability.
4. Institutions filter and reshape external flows.
5. Intersectionality concerns overlapping social positions.
6. Class, caste, gender, region, age and skill alter outcomes.
7. One person may gain in one sphere and lose in another.
8. Flexibility can coexist with insecurity.
9. Visibility can coexist with weak bargaining power.
10. National averages cannot prove subgroup mechanisms.
11. Group disaggregation and qualitative evidence complement aggregate data.
12. The key question is distribution, not an abstract net verdict.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the same digital platform be empowering for one producer and exclusionary for another?

**Model answer:** The platform may expand market reach, but effective use depends on connectivity, language, digital literacy, logistics, capital and knowledge of platform rules. Different access and bargaining conditions convert the same formal opportunity into unequal capabilities.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat technological availability as proof of equal social benefit.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks

**Mains question:** “Globalisation produces differentiated rather than uniform social outcomes.” Explain using the channel-and-filter model. Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** Globalisation reaches society through distinct channels—trade, migration, media, platforms, consumption and technology. Each is filtered by income, education, language, location, gender norms and institutional power.

A digital marketplace, for example, can give an artisan wider reach, yet meaningful participation still requires connectivity, logistics, working capital and knowledge of platform rules. Similarly, global service employment can expand women’s income and mobility, while care burdens, unsafe transport or family restrictions keep the opportunity unusable for others. NITI Aayog’s 2022 gig-work estimate identifies the scale of an emerging labour form, but cannot establish secure earnings or social protection.

Thus the proper question is not whether globalisation is uniformly beneficial or harmful. It is who accesses a channel, which institution mediates it, how bargaining power is distributed and which group bears the risk.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains a mechanism, uses two Indian applications, distinguishes scale from welfare and gives a clear distributional verdict.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** channel model 2; access filters 2; institutional mediation 2; examples 2; evidence limitation 1; verdict 1.

## Visual: one global flow, four possible cultural results

```text
                         GLOBAL CULTURAL FLOWS
                                  |
           +--------------+-------+-------+--------------+
           |              |               |              |
           v              v               v              v
   HOMOGENISATION  HETEROGENISATION  GLOCALISATION  HYBRIDISATION
   similar forms   greater visible    local redesign  new recombined
   across places   differentiation    of global form  cultural form
```

*These are four parallel possible outcomes, not stages in a sequence. Several can operate simultaneously in different dimensions of the same cultural object.*

### 1. Homogenisation

**Cultural homogenisation** is increasing similarity in products, formats, tastes or practices across places.

Examples include:

- recognisable global retail and media formats;
- similar visual styles on social platforms;
- English-language aspiration in selected urban settings;
- standardised workplace practices.

Homogenisation does not require total sameness. It means convergence along a particular dimension.

### 2. Heterogenisation

**Heterogenisation** is the expansion or renewed visibility of cultural difference. Global networks may give small cultural producers access to audiences that local gatekeepers could not provide.

Regional cinema reaching national or overseas viewers, diaspora-supported cultural festivals, and digital discovery of local music illustrate possible diversification.

The limit is important: visibility can be unequal and commercially filtered. More available content does not ensure equal remuneration or long-term vitality.

### 3. Glocalisation

**Glocalisation** means adapting a global product, institution or format to local tastes, norms or languages.

```text
global form + local selection and redesign = glocalised form
```

An India-specific menu at a global restaurant chain or regional-language content on a global platform is not unchanged import. Local actors modify the form to secure legitimacy and demand.

Glocalisation shows agency, but not equality. A large firm may use local symbols while retaining control over profit, data and distribution.

### 4. Hybridisation

**Hybridisation** is the creation of a new form through recombination. Unlike simple glocalisation, the result may not be understood as a global template with local decoration; it develops a mixed identity of its own.

Fusion music combining Indian classical or folk elements with global genres is a familiar example. Urban speech mixing English with Indian languages can also become a stable communicative form.

The limit: “hybrid” should not be romanticised. Mixture may occur under unequal power, and marginal voices may be appropriated without receiving recognition.

### 5. Why the concepts are not rivals

Take one streaming platform:

| Dimension | Process |
|---|---|
| interface and recommendation format look similar worldwide | Homogenisation |
| many more genres and languages become visible | Heterogenisation |
| global reality-show format is redesigned for Indian audiences | Glocalisation |
| creators combine regional narratives with global production grammar | Hybridisation |

The same institution can produce all four outcomes. The analyst must specify the dimension being examined.

### 6. 2018 PYQ linkage

> **2018, GS-I, Q19 — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Globalisation is generally said to promote cultural homogenisation but due to this cultural specificities appear to be strengthened in the Indian Society. Elucidate.”

**Demand:** Explain the apparent paradox rather than choosing only homogenisation or only specificity.

**Answer approach:** define both movements; show global media and market convergence; explain local assertion, adaptation and wider reach; use Indian examples; end by locating power and distribution limits.

The learning focus is the paradox and the mechanisms that resolve it, not reproduction of a prepared answer.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Glocalisation is merely a marketing technique and therefore cannot count as cultural agency.

**Reply:** It can be commercially initiated, but local consumers, workers and creators also reinterpret forms beyond firm control. Adaptation is sociologically real even when profit remains concentrated.

**Residual issue:** Agency and appropriation may coexist. A local symbol can gain visibility while control over representation and revenue remains external.

## UPSC application

Never write “globalisation both homogenises and heterogenises” as a self-sufficient answer. State:

1. the domain;
2. the mechanism;
3. the local response;
4. the distribution of control.

## Revision notes

1. Homogenisation means convergence along a specified dimension.
2. It does not imply total cultural sameness.
3. Heterogenisation means expanded or renewed differentiation.
4. Availability is not equal vitality or remuneration.
5. Glocalisation adapts a global form to local conditions.
6. Hybridisation creates a recombined form.
7. The four processes are parallel possibilities, not stages in a progression.
8. One platform can generate all four processes in different dimensions.
9. Local adaptation proves agency, not equal power.
10. Cultural visibility may coexist with commercial concentration.
11. The 2018 PYQ asks for resolution of a paradox.
12. Always identify the dimension and the controller of distribution.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should homogenisation, heterogenisation, glocalisation and hybridisation be treated as parallel possible outcomes rather than a sequence?

**Model answer:** None of the four necessarily produces the next. The same global platform may standardise its interface, widen visible cultural variety, adapt formats locally and enable new hybrid forms at the same time. Their coexistence still leaves a separate power question about who controls visibility, data and revenue.

**Misconception to avoid:** Heterogenisation is not a later stage or by-product of homogenisation, and local-looking forms do not by themselves prove equal control.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks

**Mains question:** Compare homogenisation, heterogenisation, glocalisation and hybridisation as explanations of cultural change under globalisation in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Globalisation does not produce one cultural movement. **Homogenisation** describes convergence in formats, brands and selected aspirations. Similar platform interfaces and global entertainment grammar illustrate this tendency. **Heterogenisation** describes the expanded visibility of difference: regional cinema, music and language content can reach audiences beyond their original territory.

**Glocalisation** occurs when a global form is deliberately adapted to Indian tastes, as with local menus or vernacular versions of international media formats. **Hybridisation** goes further: interaction creates a relatively new mixed form, visible in fusion music, mixed-language urban speech and regional storytelling using global production techniques.

These processes coexist. A streaming platform may standardise recommendation architecture, diversify available languages, localise a programme format and enable hybrid creative forms. The 2018 GS-I question on homogenisation and strengthened cultural specificities is best answered through this simultaneity.

However, cultural agency does not remove power inequality. Platforms and firms may control visibility, data and revenue even while local symbols flourish. The strongest conclusion is therefore dimensional: globalisation standardises some infrastructures while multiplying, adapting and recombining cultural expression.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes four concepts, compares them within one institution, links the 2018 demand and qualifies cultural agency through power.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** four definitions 4; Indian examples 3; comparison and coexistence 3; 2018 linkage 2; power qualification 2; conclusion 1.

## Visual: identity is layered, not a single container

```text
PERSON
  |
  +-- local: neighbourhood · village · city
  +-- linguistic: mother tongue · literary sphere
  +-- regional: state · landscape · cuisine
  +-- religious/caste/tribal/community identities
  +-- national: citizenship · institutions · shared public life
  +-- global: profession · media · rights discourse · consumption

These layers may reinforce, compete or be strategically activated.
```

*Acquiring a global identity does not mechanically erase every local identity; the relationship depends on institutions, status and power.*

### 1. Identity as relationship

Identity answers “Who am I?” but also “To whom do I belong?” and “How am I recognised?” It is sustained through language, memory, institutions, ritual, work, territory and public representation.

A global professional identity may expand without replacing a regional language identity. A migrant may become more attached to local cuisine or festivals precisely because distance makes them markers of belonging.

### 2. What threatens local identity?

Globalisation can weaken local identity through:

1. **Market displacement:** local producers cannot match scale, advertising or distribution.
2. **Language hierarchy:** dominant languages gain educational or employment value.
3. **Format standardisation:** algorithms favour familiar, repeatable forms.
4. **Prestige inequality:** local practices are marked “backward” while imported forms signify status.
5. **Deterritorialisation:** migration separates practice from its ecological or community setting.

These are mechanisms, not automatic outcomes.

### 3. What can strengthen local identity?

The same process can support:

- wider markets for crafts, food and performance;
- digital archives and teaching;
- diaspora networks that fund or celebrate cultural forms;
- regional-language media;
- conscious identity assertion against perceived homogenisation;
- adaptation that makes a practice usable in new settings.

Strengthening may be symbolic rather than material. A craft can become internationally admired while artisans remain poorly paid.

### 4. Diversity and plurality

**Diversity** is the presence of multiple identities. **Plurality** requires coexistence with recognition and space for participation.

```text
many identities present
        ≠
equal voice and secure coexistence
```

Global content abundance may increase diversity of choice while a few platforms determine discoverability. Plurality therefore depends on institutional conditions, not numerical variety alone.

### 5. 2019 and 2020 PYQ linkages

> **2019, GS-I, Q20 — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Are we losing our local identity for the global identity? Discuss.”

**Demand:** Test a zero-sum premise. Explain loss mechanisms, adaptation and layered identity, then give a qualified verdict.

> **2020, GS-I, Q18 — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Is diversity and pluralism in India under threat due to globalisation? Justify your answer.”

**Demand:** Distinguish diversity from pluralism; show actual pressures and countervailing resilience; state the conditions under which threat intensifies.

Together, the two questions test layered identity, plural participation and the institutional conditions that resist one-way cultural loss.

### 6. Example and limit

Regional cinema distributed through a global streaming service can strengthen linguistic visibility and create hybrid production. Yet the platform’s recommendation system and commercial priorities can make some languages far more discoverable than others.

The example proves neither cultural decline nor cultural revival by itself. Distribution power must be analysed.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Celebrating layered identity underestimates how powerful global markets erase fragile traditions.

**Reply:** The risk is real, especially where a practice lacks institutional support or economic viability. But erasure is not inevitable: communities adapt, assert and circulate identity through the same networks.

**Residual issue:** Survival may come through transformation. Whether adaptation preserves a tradition or commodifies it is often contested within the community itself.

## UPSC application

Structure identity answers around:

```text
pressure -> local response -> institutional condition -> differentiated verdict
```

Avoid nostalgic claims that culture was once pure or static.

2. Global and local identities need not be mutually exclusive.
3. Market displacement can weaken local cultural producers.
4. Language hierarchy affects identity through opportunity.
5. Algorithms can standardise visibility.
6. Migration can weaken practice or intensify symbolic attachment.
7. Global networks can widen markets and cultural reach.
8. Symbolic visibility does not guarantee producer welfare.
9. Diversity is presence; plurality requires recognition and participation.
10. Content abundance can coexist with gatekeeper concentration.
11. The 2019 PYQ tests a zero-sum identity premise.
12. The 2020 PYQ requires a conditional threat assessment.
13. Cultural resilience often works through adaptation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the growth of regional-language content insufficient by itself to prove that Indian plurality is secure?

**Model answer:** More regional content demonstrates cultural visibility, but plurality also requires fair discoverability, producer voice, economic viability and freedom from hierarchy or exclusion. A few platforms may host diversity while controlling which identities become visible.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse the number of available cultural products with equal recognition among cultural groups.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks

**Mains question:** Does globalisation weaken local identities in India, or can it also strengthen them? Discuss the conditions that determine the outcome. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Globalisation can weaken local identities when scale, prestige and distribution favour dominant languages, brands and media formats. Local producers may be displaced, younger speakers may shift toward languages associated with employment, and platform algorithms may reward standardised content.

Yet identity is not passive. Global networks can widen the audience for regional cinema, crafts, cuisine and music. Migrants and diasporic communities may reinforce festivals and language as markers of belonging. Glocalisation and hybridisation permit local actors to adapt external forms rather than accept cultural replacement.

**Structural consumerism** concerns the material expansion of access: incomes, retail networks, credit, logistics and product variety.

**Cultural consumerism** concerns meaning: goods become signs of success, modernity, taste or belonging.

- labour demand and occupational aspiration;
- migration and household authority;
- information access and media production;
- language and cultural forms;

### 1. What digital globalisation changes

Digital networks reduce the cost of publishing, communicating and finding audiences across distance. They can support:

- regional-language creation;
- transnational professional and family networks;
- rapid circulation of ideas;
- access to educational and cultural material;
- visibility for groups excluded by older gatekeepers;
- direct market contact for small producers.

The gain is real but conditional.

### 2. Platformisation

**Platformisation** is the growing organisation of social and economic interaction through digital intermediaries that set rules, collect data and rank visibility.

Unlike a passive communication pipe, a platform:

- recommends some content;
- suppresses or moderates other content;
- rewards engagement;
- sells or uses attention and data;
- sets monetisation conditions;
- can change rules unilaterally.

Thus disintermediation—bypassing old gatekeepers—often creates **reintermediation** through new platform gatekeepers.

### 3. Vernacularisation and cultural reach

Regional-language content on global platforms illustrates glocalisation and heterogenisation. Creators can reach dispersed linguistic communities, and audiences can encounter forms ignored by national broadcasters.

Yet unequal discoverability persists. Major languages, high-production creators or advertiser-friendly content may receive more visibility. Availability is not equivalent to equal representation.

### 4. Echo chambers and polarisation

Algorithms often optimise engagement. Users also select like-minded networks. Together they can produce:

- repeated exposure to congenial views;
- reduced contact with disagreement;
- rapid circulation of sensational material;
- identity reinforcement;
- harassment or social pressure.

An **echo chamber** is not simply any group of similar people. It is an information environment where repetition and filtering systematically narrow exposure and strengthen prior belief.

### 5. Digital divide

The divide has several levels:

```text
device/connectivity
      -> skill and language
      -> meaningful use
      -> safety and privacy
      -> voice, income or service outcome
```

A connection without literacy, accessible design, trusted information or grievance support provides incomplete capability.

No NFHS-6 internet-use figure is used here. The concept does not require an unsupported numerical claim.

### 6. Example and limit

A regional-language creator may bypass a metropolitan broadcaster and reach viewers directly. Yet income still depends on platform rules, advertiser demand and algorithmic ranking.

The example shows widened entry and concentrated distribution power. It does not establish the fate of all creators or languages.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Algorithms merely show users what they prefer; platform harms are therefore individual responsibility.

**Reply:** Preferences matter, but platforms design ranking, defaults, friction and monetisation. Institutional design shapes which preferences are amplified and which risks are externalised.

**Residual issue:** Regulation can protect users but may also threaten expression or entrench incumbents if poorly designed. The balance requires transparency, contestability and rights protection.

*Technology can economise one resource while increasing total demand, dependence or waste elsewhere.*

### 1. The relationship is not “technology versus scarcity”

Technology can respond to scarcity by improving efficiency, substitution, monitoring and coordination. It can also create new resource demand through production, infrastructure and mass adoption.

Examples:

- remote communication can reduce some travel but increases device, network and electricity demand;
- precision systems can improve resource use but require capital, data and skills;
- digital services can widen access while relying on physical hardware and energy systems.

The correct relation is dynamic and distributive.

### 2. Efficiency and rebound

**Efficiency** means obtaining more output or service from a given input. But lower cost or easier use may increase total consumption. This is a **rebound effect**.

```text
greater efficiency
      -> lower cost per use
      -> wider or more frequent use
      -> part of expected resource saving is offset
```

Rebound is not inevitable or always complete. It is a caution against assuming technical efficiency automatically produces absolute conservation.

### 3. Material and ecological chains

Even apparently “virtual” technology rests on:

- mined materials;
- manufacturing;
- energy and cooling;
- data and telecom infrastructure;
- transport;
- repair and disposal.

Society analysis should ask who lives near extraction, manufacturing or waste sites and who controls high-value design, data and intellectual property. Precise mineral, energy or e-waste quantities should be drawn from appropriate specialist evidence rather than guessed here.

### 4. Scarcity is also social

Scarcity is not only physical shortage. A resource may exist but remain inaccessible because of price, location, ownership or institutional exclusion.

Technology can:

- reduce information scarcity;
- create dependence on proprietary systems;
- concentrate control through data and network effects;
- widen capability for skilled users;
- exclude those without devices, literacy or reliable infrastructure.

Thus the distribution of access can matter as much as aggregate supply.

### 5. India-specific analytical route

An India answer can organise the relationship through:

1. digital access to services and knowledge;
2. productivity and coordination;
3. import or supply-chain dependence;
4. energy, material and waste burdens;
5. regional and class access divides;
6. repair, reuse, circularity and public-interest regulation.

Avoid claiming that technology is inherently green or inherently extractive.

### 6. 2022 direct PYQ linkage

> **2022, GS-I, Q20 — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Elucidate the relationship between globalization and new technology in a world of scarce resources, with special reference to India.”

**Demand:** Explain the relationship, not merely list technologies. Link global diffusion and supply chains to efficiency, resource demand, unequal access and India-specific dependence or capability.

The central task is to explain the relationship among technological capability, material limits and unequal distribution.

### 7. Example and limit

A digital learning platform can distribute material widely at low marginal cost. Yet meaningful access requires a device, connection, electricity, language and a usable learning environment.

The example concerns educational capability. It does not prove learning outcomes or resource neutrality.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Innovation continually substitutes scarce resources, so social concern about material limits is pessimistic.

**Reply:** Substitution and efficiency are real, but transition requires materials, infrastructure and institutions. Gains may be delayed, unequally distributed or offset by rebound.

**Residual issue:** The balance varies by technology and life cycle. Society answers should give the analytical structure and draw precise technical claims from the relevant specialist evidence rather than fabricate them.

*The model converts a broad topic into a sequence of answerable sociological questions.*

### 1. Mediation defeats determinism

Globalisation does not act directly upon passive communities. Outcomes depend on mediators:

- states regulate access and protection;
- firms organise production and employment;
- platforms rank visibility and allocate tasks;
- families distribute permission, care and resources;
- communities reinterpret or resist cultural forms;
- individuals adopt, combine or reject them.

This is why similar global exposure produces different outcomes.

### 2. Agency is real but constrained

**Agency** is the capacity to act, interpret and choose. It is not unlimited freedom.

A local producer can adapt a global format, but platform rules shape visibility. A migrant woman can renegotiate family authority, but safety and care conditions constrain choices. Youth can form new issue publics, but information quality and institutional access affect influence.

The best answers hold agency and structure together.

### 3. Cumulative advantage

Initial resources can generate further advantage:

```text
better education/connectivity
       -> access to global skill and work
       -> higher income and stronger networks
       -> better future access
```

The reverse can also occur:

```text
weak connectivity/skill
       -> exclusion from new work and information
       -> lower income and bargaining
       -> reduced capacity to enter later
```

Globalisation can therefore widen gaps even when total opportunity grows.

### 4. Unevenness across spheres

One household may experience:

- economic gain from remittance;
- emotional cost from separation;
- cultural diversification through media;
- greater consumer pressure;
- new educational access;
- insecure platform work.

There is no single household-level “effect.” Social evaluation requires a multidimensional account.

### 5. Four recurring paradoxes

| Paradox | Resolution |
|---|---|
| common global formats and stronger local identities | homogenisation and local assertion operate together |
| greater labour flexibility and stronger control | platform scheduling freedom coexists with algorithmic power |
| women’s autonomy and continuing family ties | authority is renegotiated, not necessarily severed |
| health concern and fast-food growth | awareness competes with time, price, delivery, habit and aspiration |

These are not contradictions once separate mechanisms are identified.

### 6. What institutions can do

A socially defensible response does not attempt to stop all flows. It improves the terms of participation:

- universal and accessible digital infrastructure;
- multilingual capability and education;
- worker voice and contestable platform decisions;
- competition and interoperability;
- care, safety and housing support for migrants;
- support for local producers and cultural institutions;
- resource efficiency, repair and circularity;
- disaggregated evidence and grievance systems.

Policy design belongs partly to other subjects, but the Society test is whether institutions convert exposure into capability without externalising risk.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The “uneven effects” conclusion is evasive because it avoids deciding whether globalisation is good or bad.

**Reply:** A graded verdict is more demanding than a binary one. It identifies mechanisms, groups, gains, costs and correctives. A single moral label hides distribution.

**Residual issue:** Some harms may be systemic rather than correctable by small safeguards. Market concentration, ecological burden or cultural displacement may require structural reform, and the appropriate scale differs by domain.

### Concept check

**Question:** What makes “globalisation has uneven effects” an analytical conclusion rather than a vague compromise?

**Model answer:** It becomes analytical when unevenness is traced to specific channels, mediators, access filters and power relations. The claim must identify which group gains or bears risk, through what mechanism, and which institution could alter the distribution.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use “uneven” as a substitute for naming the actual source of inequality.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks

**Mains question:** “Globalisation expands opportunities but redistributes power and risk unevenly.” Critically analyse this proposition with reference to Indian society. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Globalisation expands cross-border flows of work, markets, technology, information and culture. Indian service workers, migrants, creators and producers may gain income, mobility, knowledge and wider audiences. Regional cultural forms can also travel through global platforms.

Yet flows are mediated by firms, platforms, families, communities and the state. Skill, language, class, caste, gender, region and connectivity determine usable access. Platforms may lower entry barriers while controlling ratings and visibility. Women’s migration may enlarge income and decision voice while producing safety, care and support risks. Global cultural formats can widen expression through glocalisation and hybridisation, even as concentrated distribution pressures fragile languages or producers.

Advantage can accumulate: education and connectivity open global work, generating income and networks that improve later access. Exclusion can similarly reproduce itself. NITI Aayog’s 2022 gig estimate indicates scale, while PLFS 2025 supplies aggregate labour context; neither proves secure work or a particular migration cause.

Therefore globalisation is neither an equalising force nor a uniform cultural invasion. Its social value depends on worker voice, accessible skills, fair platform governance, migrant support, cultural viability and resource responsibility. The decisive issue is the institutional distribution of capability, control and cost.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates economic, family, cultural and digital channels, explains cumulative inequality, uses named evidence cautiously and provides a critical institutional verdict.

**Quantified rubric — 20 marks:** opportunity channels 4; power and risk mechanisms 5; social differentiation 3; cultural/family/digital integration 3; named evidence with limits 2; critical response 2; conclusion 1.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Process

**Question:** A global product is sold in India but does not change work, identity, power or institutional relations. What has been shown, and what has not?

**Model answer:** Cross-border circulation has been shown. A significant social effect has not yet been established because the mediator, mechanism and outcome remain unidentified.

**Misconception to avoid:** Presence of a foreign product is not a complete sociological explanation.

## Check 2 — Culture

**Question:** Can homogenisation and heterogenisation occur on the same platform?

**Model answer:** Yes. The platform may standardise interface and recommendation grammar while expanding the number of visible languages, genres or local creators. The processes operate on different dimensions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat cultural theories as mutually exclusive total descriptions.

## Check 3 — Identity

**Question:** What additional evidence is needed before concluding that increased regional content has secured plurality?

**Model answer:** Evidence of discoverability, producer voice, viable livelihoods, equal recognition and freedom from exclusion is needed. Content count alone establishes availability, not plural participation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Numerical diversity is not automatically substantive plurality.

## Check 4 — Work

**Question:** Which facts would distinguish labour-market access from worker empowerment?

**Model answer:** Earnings predictability, control over tasks, bargaining capacity, social protection, grievance redress, care conditions and safety distinguish access from empowerment.

**Misconception to avoid:** A worker count or participation rate does not measure job quality.

## Check 5 — Family

**Question:** Why is “migration either liberates women or destroys family” a false binary?

**Model answer:** Migration can enlarge income, mobility and voice while remittance, affection, consultation and ritual ties continue. It can also create care and safety risks. Relationships are often renegotiated rather than erased.

**Misconception to avoid:** Autonomy is not identical to isolation.

## Check 6 — Youth

**Question:** What is the difference between global political exposure and verified political participation?

**Model answer:** Exposure concerns information, vocabulary and expression. Participation additionally requires observable, sustained engagement through institutions, organisation or other verified channels. One cannot be inferred automatically from the other.

**Misconception to avoid:** Online visibility does not establish turnout or membership.

## Check 7 — Technology

**Question:** Why must a digital service be analysed materially?

**Model answer:** Devices, networks, energy, manufacturing, repair and disposal underlie digital use. A virtual interface may reduce one cost while shifting or increasing other resource burdens.

**Misconception to avoid:** Digital does not mean resource-free.

## Check 8 — Synthesis

**Question:** Complete the sentence analytically: “Globalisation is uneven because…”

**Model answer:** “…flows pass through unequal access, institutional mediation and power relations, so groups differ in their capacity to use opportunities, resist risks and shape rules.”

**Misconception to avoid:** “Uneven” must name a mechanism, not merely signal balance.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question:** Why is glocalisation not a sufficient proof of cultural equality under globalisation? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:** Glocalisation is the adaptation of a global product or format to local language, taste or norms. It demonstrates that local culture is not passively replaced. India-specific menus and regional-language versions of global media formats illustrate this agency.

However, adaptation does not reveal who controls the process. A large firm or platform may retain ownership of data, branding, distribution and revenue while selectively using local symbols. Some languages and communities may also receive greater visibility than others because of purchasing power or advertiser demand.

Cultural equality requires more than local appearance. It includes producer voice, fair discoverability, viable livelihoods, recognition and the capacity to refuse appropriation. Thus glocalisation is evidence against a simple cultural-erasure thesis, but not evidence that cultural groups participate on equal terms.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the term, acknowledges its significance, exposes the power gap and gives a precise equality test.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** definition 2; Indian illustrations 2; power analysis 3; equality criteria 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-marker

**Question:** Evaluate how globalisation has altered the relationship among work, migration and family authority in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** Globalisation links Indian households to service employment, production networks and platform-mediated work. These channels can encourage migration, reward new skills and provide income outside inherited local occupations.

Migration changes family authority through distance, earnings and residence. A skilled woman working in a city may gain control over mobility, spending and marriage timing. Yet remittance, regular contact, ritual participation and consultation can persist, producing negotiated interdependence rather than complete family rupture. Men’s and women’s pathways differ because safety, care responsibilities and social permission affect usable opportunity.

Platform work adds another layer. It can lower entry barriers and permit flexible timing, while algorithms, ratings and variable demand shift control and risk. NITI Aayog’s 2022 gig estimate shows the scale of an emerging work form, not empowerment. MoSPI’s PLFS Annual Report 2025 gives aggregate labour context; it cannot identify a specific migration stream or prove globalisation as its cause.

Thus work, migration and family change through one another. Empowerment depends on secure income, safe housing and mobility, care support, worker voice and genuine household bargaining—not on employment or distance alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates three institutions, uses named evidence with limits, includes gender and platform mechanisms and ends with conditions for empowerment.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** work channels 3; migration-family mechanism 4; gender/platform depth 3; evidence control 2; institutional conditions 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 20-marker

**Question:** Globalisation simultaneously standardises social life and multiplies difference. Critically examine this proposition with reference to culture, media, consumption and youth in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** Globalisation standardises infrastructures and aspiration scripts. Common platform interfaces, brands, entertainment formats and consumer-status symbols can produce homogenisation. Algorithmic ranking and concentrated distribution may further reward repeatable, commercially familiar forms.

Yet the same networks multiply difference. Regional-language creators reach dispersed audiences; global formats are glocalised to Indian tastes; and youth produce hybrid music, speech and visual culture. Local identity can be consciously asserted when global pressure makes it more salient. Hence the 2018 homogenisation paradox and the 2019 local-identity question are best understood through simultaneous convergence and adaptation.

Consumption also has two faces. Market access and delivery expand choice, while brands organise status and social comparison. Youth are not only consumers: global education, work and political information reshape aspiration and peer networks. However, expression does not prove participation, and exposure does not prove capability.

Power resolves the apparent contradiction. Platforms can host diverse content while controlling visibility, data and monetisation. Class, language, gender, region and connectivity determine whose difference becomes viable.

Globalisation therefore standardises selected systems while diversifying and recombining cultural expression. Plurality depends on fair access, producer voice and institutional support, not cultural variety alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates four required domains, links verified demand families, identifies platform power and gives a genuinely critical resolution.

**Quantified rubric — 20 marks:** standardisation mechanisms 4; difference/adaptation mechanisms 4; consumption and youth integration 4; power and inequality 4; PYQ-linked critical balance 2; conclusion 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core definition and boundary

- Globalisation is intensified cross-border flow of goods, services, capital, people, technology, information and culture that reorganises domestic institutions and relations.
- Internationalisation is increased cross-border interaction; globalisation implies deeper interdependence and restructuring.
- Liberalisation is policy opening; westernisation is a direction of influence; modernisation is structural-rational change.
- Analytical formula: **flow → mediator → mechanism → group-specific outcome → qualification**.
- Never infer causation merely from post-1991 chronology.

## 2. Channel-and-filter framework

- Channels: trade/investment, labour mobility, platforms, media, consumption and technology.
- Mediators: state, firm, platform, family, community and household.
- Filters: income, skill, language, location, connectivity, gender norms and social networks.
- Access means availability; capability means real usable freedom.
- Intersectionality: class, caste, gender, region, age and skill overlap.
- One person or household can gain in one sphere and lose in another.

## 3. Cultural processes

| Concept | Rapid meaning | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Homogenisation | convergence of selected formats or practices | not total sameness |
| Heterogenisation | expanded or renewed visible difference | visibility is not viability |
| Glocalisation | local redesign of a global form | adaptation is not equal control |
| Hybridisation | creation of a recombined form | mixture may occur under unequal power |

- The four are parallel possible outcomes, not a progression from one to another.
- A single streaming platform can produce all four.
- Regional-language content is evidence of adaptation and reach, not automatic plurality.
- Platform control over ranking, data and revenue remains central.

## 4. Identity and diversity

- Identity is layered: local, linguistic, regional, community, national and global.
- Threat mechanisms: market displacement, language hierarchy, format standardisation, prestige inequality and deterritorialisation.
- Resilience mechanisms: adaptation, wider markets, digital archives, regional media and conscious identity assertion.
- Diversity is the presence of identities; plurality requires recognition, voice and secure coexistence.
- The 2019 demand tests whether global and local identity are zero-sum.
- The 2020 demand requires a conditional judgement about diversity and pluralism.

## 5. Consumer culture

- Consumer culture makes goods and brands vehicles of status, identity and participation.
- Structural consumerism: material access through income, retail, credit and logistics.
- Cultural consumerism: symbolic meaning and status.
- Mechanism: availability → advertising/data → reduced purchase friction → peer comparison.
- Consumers possess agency but operate within persuasive and unequal market structures.
- 2025 Q10: acknowledge consumerism, reject **only**, then show work, family, media and cultural channels.

## 6. Work and platforms

- Global services can expand occupations, skill premiums, migration and household bargaining.
- Platform market: digital intermediary matching distinct user groups.
- Algorithmic management: task allocation, ratings, dynamic pricing, monitoring and deactivation.
- Flexibility can coexist with platform control.
- NITI Aayog 2022: 7.7 million gig workers in 2020–21; 23.5 million for 2029–30 is a projection.
- Worker count does not establish earnings quality, autonomy or benefit coverage.
- Recognition of gig/platform categories is not automatic social protection.

## 7. Sole dated current linkage

- MoSPI PLFS Annual Report 2025 was released **27 March 2026**.
- Reference period: **January–December 2025**.
- Measure: usual-status female LFPR, age 15+: **40.0%**.
- Use: aggregate labour-market context.
- Do not use it to prove the size, age, marital status, destination or cause of a women’s migration stream.
- PLFS is contextual, not causal.

## 8. Women’s migration and family

- Gendered migration analysis includes safety, housing, care, permission, occupation and control over income.
- Freedom dimensions: mobility, spending, work identity, marriage timing/choice and household voice.
- Structural separation can occur without complete functional independence.
- Remittance, contact, visits, ritual participation and consultation may persist.
- Agency and vulnerability can rise together.
- 2024 Q19 requires both freedom and family relationship, not a generic female-work essay.
- Best verdict: negotiated interdependence, varying by class, caste, region and occupation.

## 9. Fast-food paradox in its wider social-change setting

- The fast-food question is primarily a broader social-change question.
- Globalisation contributes brands, aspiration and glocalised menus, but not the whole explanation.
- Full mechanism: time pressure, delivery, price, taste, habit, present bias, aspiration and household routines.
- Bounded rationality: decision under limited time, information and attention.
- Health awareness is one input, not the sole determinant of purchase.
- Do not invent consumption or disease figures.

## 10. Digital and media

- Digital networks lower the cost of publishing and communication.
- Platformisation subjects interaction to ranking, data and moderation rules.
- Disintermediation can become reintermediation through platform gatekeepers.
- Vernacularisation widens cultural reach but not necessarily equal discoverability.
- Echo chambers narrow exposure through selection and repeated reinforcement.
- Digital divide ladder: connection → skill/language → use → safety/privacy → outcome.
- Connectivity is not inclusion; upload capacity is not equal voice.
- No unsupported NFHS-6 internet-use figure is used.

## 11. Youth — SPEC framework

| Sphere | Gain channel | Risk/limit |
|---|---|---|
| Social | wider networks, identity choices, family bargaining | comparison, dependence, intergenerational tension |
| Political | information, rights vocabulary, issue publics | visibility is not participation; misinformation and harassment |
| Economic | global services, remote work, skills, enterprise | precarity, skill bias, aspiration gap |
| Cultural | wider genres, glocal and hybrid creation | status pressure, commercial gatekeeping |

- No general youth turnout, protest-scale or diaspora-effect claim is made.
- Youth data require defined age and measure.
- 2026 Q20 wording is provisional because the official paper scan was unavailable for verification.

## 12. Technology and scarce resources

- Technology can relieve scarcity through efficiency, substitution, monitoring and coordination.
- It can increase resource demand through production, infrastructure, mass adoption and waste.
- Rebound: efficiency lowers per-use cost, wider use offsets some savings.
- Digital systems depend on physical materials, energy, networks, repair and disposal.
- Scarcity may be physical or institutionally produced through price, location and ownership.
- Society analysis asks who gains capability, who controls technology and who bears life-cycle cost.
- Technical quantities belong to specialist Economy, Environment or Science and Technology evidence.
- 2022 Q20 requires an explained relationship with special reference to India.

## 13. Uneven-distribution synthesis

- Globalisation is mediated rather than deterministic.
- Agency means capacity to interpret and act within constraints.
- Cumulative advantage: education/connectivity → global opportunity → income/network → better future access.
- Cumulative exclusion follows the reverse path.
- Four paradoxes: homogenisation/local assertion; flexibility/control; autonomy/family continuity; health concern/fast-food growth.
- Institutional correctives: access, multilingual capability, worker voice, contestability, migrant support, cultural viability, repair and circularity.
- Master verdict: globalisation expands choice and interdependence while redistributing capability, control and cost unevenly.
