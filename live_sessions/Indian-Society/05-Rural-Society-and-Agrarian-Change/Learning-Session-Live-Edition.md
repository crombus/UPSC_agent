### Visual: the village is an open social system

```text
                    RURAL SOCIETY
                         │
       ┌─────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       │                 │                  │
   PRODUCTION         SOCIAL ORDER       INSTITUTIONS
 land · labour       caste · class       kinship · Panchayat
 credit · market     gender · status     school · state office
       │                 │                  │
       └─────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                         ↓
             FLOWS ACROSS THE VILLAGE
       migration · remittance · media · roads
       welfare · elections · commodity markets
```

*The village is neither only a farm nor a sealed community; it is a field of relationships connected to wider markets, towns and the state.*

### 1. Start with the correct unit

✅ **Rural society** is the network of relationships through which people in rural settlements organise production, family life, status, authority, exchange and collective decisions. Agriculture is important, but rural households may also depend on construction, transport, services, public employment, petty trade, craft work, care work and remittances.

That yields the first indispensable distinction:

| Rural | Agricultural |
|---|---|
| Refers to a settlement-linked social field | Refers to cultivation and allied production |
| Includes non-farm workers, institutions and households | Focuses on production relations around land and livestock |
| Contains political, kinship, caste and gender relations | Is one major economic sphere within rural society |

⚠️ **Analytical implication:** A rise in non-farm work does not make a village socially urban, and agricultural output alone cannot describe its hierarchy.

### 2. Rural structure is made of unequal relationships

Four relationships organise much of rural life:

1. **Land relation:** who owns, cultivates, leases, inherits or lacks land.
2. **Labour relation:** who hires, supervises, performs and bargains over work.
3. **Status relation:** how caste, gender, kinship and community affect dignity and access.
4. **Authority relation:** who controls local office, information, credit, dispute settlement and collective resources.

```text
asset control
   + labour dependence
   + social status
   + institutional access
              ↓
      bargaining power
              ↓
 income · security · voice · mobility
```

The same household can occupy different positions on these axes. A small owner may possess land but lack irrigation or political influence. A landless worker may obtain stable non-farm income yet remain socially excluded. A woman may perform major farm work without holding title or deciding crop choice.

### 3. Reject the timeless “village republic”

The harmonious, self-sufficient village image misses conflict and connection.

✅ Rural communities have long contained cooperation: shared irrigation, reciprocal labour, local knowledge, kin support and collective rituals.

⚠️ Yet cooperation coexists with unequal land, caste sanctions, gendered care burdens, labour dependence and exclusion from authority. Nor are villages self-contained: taxes, credit, procurement, schools, roads, elections, migration and media tie them to institutions outside the settlement.

> **Answer line:** The Indian village is a connected arena of cooperation and hierarchy, not a timeless republic outside markets and the state.

### 4. A relational answer asks “who depends on whom?”

Consider a paved road. It may:

- give cultivators access to a mandi;
- let workers commute to town;
- enable women’s mobility where safety permits;
- raise nearby land values;
- increase competition for local artisans.

The road is therefore not a social outcome by itself. Its effects depend on assets, mobility, caste networks, gender norms and the ability to bear market risk.

**Limit of the example:** One infrastructure improvement cannot establish equality, rural transformation or the disappearance of local hierarchy.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Rural society” is too broad; agricultural class should remain the decisive explanation.

**Reply:** Land and labour are foundational, but they do not exhaust rural power. Panchayat office, education, remittance income, credit networks, kinship and caste numbers can reinforce or counter land-based authority.

**Residual:** The relative weight of each resource is an empirical question. A good answer does not replace land with “networks”; it asks which combination carries power in the locality under study.

### UPSC application

- Open with the rural-versus-agricultural distinction.
- Organise the body around assets, labour, status, authority and external flows.
- Avoid romanticising community or treating every village as equally market-connected.
- Use one located mechanism, then state its regional and group limits.

### Revision notes

1. Rural society is a social field; agriculture is one economic sphere within it.
2. Land, labour, status and authority are the four basic relational axes.
3. Rural households can combine farm, non-farm, care and remittance incomes.
4. Ownership does not automatically imply effective control or security.
5. Wage change does not automatically erase caste or gender hierarchy.
6. Roads and markets create opportunities whose gains are asset-mediated.
7. The village is connected to towns, media, elections and state institutions.
8. Cooperation and hierarchy can coexist in the same community.
9. A case study demonstrates a mechanism, not national prevalence.
10. The best rural question is often: which resource now carries power?

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “rural society” not interchangeable with “agricultural society”?

**Model answer:** Rural society includes cultivation but also non-farm work, kinship, caste, gender, local institutions, migration and state-market linkages. Agricultural relations explain a major part of rural stratification, but they cannot by themselves explain authority, care, representation or remittance-based mobility.

**Misconception to avoid:** A village with substantial non-farm work has not necessarily become socially urban; settlement, institutions and social relations may remain distinctly rural.

### Mains practice — 10 marks

**Mains question:** “The village is neither isolated nor socially homogeneous.” Explain with reference to rural India. Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** Rural India is an open social field rather than a sealed agricultural community. Landowners, tenants, labourers, artisans and non-farm workers occupy unequal positions shaped by assets, caste, gender and kinship. Cooperation in irrigation, care or ritual can therefore coexist with dependence and exclusion.

Villages are also linked to towns and the state through migration, remittances, roads, commodity markets, schools, welfare and elections. A road may expand a cultivator’s market and a worker’s commuting options, yet households without assets, mobility or social networks may gain less. Likewise, non-farm income may reduce dependence on a landlord without ending caste stigma.

Thus neither the romantic “village republic” nor a uniform modernisation story is adequate. Rural change must be traced through the distribution of land, labour, authority and external connections, with region and group variation explicitly recognised.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** rural/agricultural boundary 2; relational structure 3; India-linked mechanism 2; heterogeneity qualification 2; direct conclusion 1.

### Visual: from customary obligation to negotiated service

```text
JAJMAN HOUSEHOLD
landholding/patron position
        │ recurring customary claim
        ↓
KAMIN/SERVICE HOUSEHOLD
barber · washerman · priest · carpenter · smith
        │
        ├── payment often grain/kind
        ├── sometimes cash or mixed payment
        └── hereditary, personalised tie

CHANGE PRESSURES
cash market + schooling + migration + non-farm jobs
        ↓
multiple clients + negotiated price + occupational choice
        ↓
TRANSFORMATION, WITH SOME RITUAL/PERSONALISED RESIDUALS
```

*Payment medium varied; the inherited personalised obligation is the decisive feature.*

### 1. What the institution meant

✅ The **jajmani system** was a historical and regionally variable patron-client arrangement. Patron households—often landholding—received recurring customary services from designated service households or groups. Services could include barbering, laundering, ritual officiation, carpentry and smithing.

The terms commonly used are:

- **jajman:** patron household;
- **kamin:** customary service provider;
- **customary remuneration:** often grain or kind, but sometimes cash or mixed payment;
- **hereditary tie:** expectations could pass across generations.

> **Decisive distinction:** Jajmani was not defined by “no cash.” It was defined by a relatively fixed, caste-linked and personalised service obligation.

### 2. Why it reproduced hierarchy

```text
birth-ascribed occupation
        ↓
fixed patron relationship
        ↓
restricted client choice
        ↓
customary payment and dependence
        ↓
caste occupation embedded in village economy
```

The arrangement could give patrons predictable services and service households a customary claim on payment. Yet it also restricted occupational mobility and bargaining because the relationship was inherited rather than freely renegotiated.

⚠️ **Balanced assessment:** Customary security and unequal dependence could exist together. Calling jajmani either pure exploitation or harmonious reciprocity erases regional variation and power differences.

### 3. Why jajmani changed

Four mechanisms weaken a fixed patron-service tie:

1. **Monetisation:** services receive negotiated cash prices rather than a customary share.
2. **Market expansion:** providers can serve multiple customers instead of one inherited patron.
3. **Migration and non-farm work:** town employment supplies alternative income and bargaining power.
4. **Occupational decoupling:** education and new work weaken the caste-occupation link.

Example:

```text
fixed family patron
      ↓
service provider gains town employment
      ↓
income no longer depends on customary grain payment
      ↓
service offered selectively or at a negotiated price
```

**Limit of the example:** One mobile worker does not prove that all household ties, caste sanctions or ritual obligations have disappeared.

### 4. Decline is not universal disappearance

Ritual services may remain personalised after other services become market-based. A household may hire laundry commercially but continue to call a familiar priest for a life-cycle ritual. Mixed forms are therefore sociologically important.

| Claim | Better formulation |
|---|---|
| Jajmani survives unchanged | Some personalised or ritual ties persist |
| Jajmani has vanished everywhere | Marketisation has transformed it unevenly |
| Remote village means jajmani survival | Remoteness alone proves neither survival nor form |
| Cash payment means modern labour market | Examine client choice, heredity and obligation |

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Jajmani” overstates order and reciprocity; it sanitises coercive caste labour.

**Reply:** The concept should indeed be used critically. It identifies a patterned relation, not a morally equal exchange. Bargaining power, exit options, stigma and local sanctions must be examined.

**Residual:** Historical forms differed. A strong answer treats jajmani as a bounded analytical model and avoids turning it into a uniform all-India village essence.

### UPSC application

- Define the relation before listing occupations.
- Contrast hereditary personalised obligation with negotiable market exchange.
- Explain change through monetisation, client choice, migration and occupational mobility.
- End with “transformation, not uniform disappearance.”

### Revision notes

1. Jajmani was a patron-client service arrangement, not a cash-free economy.
2. Jajman denotes the patron; kamin denotes the customary service provider.
3. Payments were often in kind but could include cash.
4. Heredity, personalisation and caste linkage are the defining features.
5. Customary claims could provide continuity while reproducing dependence.
6. Monetisation changes payment but does not alone prove institutional disappearance.
7. Multiple clients weaken a fixed patron’s control.
8. Migration and non-farm work expand exit options.
9. Occupational decoupling weakens caste-based service allocation.
10. Ritual ties may persist after ordinary services become contractual.
11. Remoteness cannot be used as proof of survival.
12. The correct verdict is uneven transformation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the presence of cash insufficient to decide whether a relationship is jajmani-like?

**Model answer:** Cash and in-kind payments could coexist. The stronger test is whether the service relationship remains inherited, personalised, caste-linked and tied to a relatively fixed patron rather than freely negotiated among multiple clients.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Paid in cash” does not automatically mean “modern impersonal labour market.”

### Mains practice — 10 marks

**Mains question:** Explain the jajmani system and account for its uneven transformation in contemporary rural India. Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** Jajmani was a regionally variable patron-client arrangement in which patron households received recurring services from designated caste-linked service groups. Remuneration was often grain or kind, but cash also occurred. Its defining feature was the hereditary, personalised obligation between a relatively fixed patron and provider.

Commercialisation and monetisation converted customary shares into negotiated prices. Schooling, non-farm work and migration enabled service providers to seek multiple clients, weakening occupational heredity and patron dependence. Yet transformation has not meant universal disappearance. Ritual officiation and other personalised services may survive after laundry, barbering or craft work becomes market-based.

The concept must also be used critically: customary continuity could coexist with unequal bargaining and caste sanction. Hence the defensible conclusion is that jajmani relations have been selectively monetised and contractualised, while residual ties persist according to service, region and local power.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** precise definition 2; institutional anatomy 3; change mechanisms 2; residual-form qualification 1; critical conclusion 2.

### Visual: the agrarian class ladder is about control and risk

```text
LANDOWNING CULTIVATOR
title/control + cultivation + claim on surplus
             │
             ↓
TENANT / SHARECROPPER
access to cultivation, but rent/share and tenure risk
             │
             ↓
LANDLESS AGRICULTURAL LABOURER
sells labour; seasonal work and bargaining vulnerability

CROSS-CUTTING AXES:
caste · gender · credit · irrigation · kinship · migration income
```

*Agrarian class is defined by relations to land, cultivation, surplus and labour—not ritual rank alone.*

### 1. Class positions are relational

✅ **Landowning cultivator:** controls land and ordinarily retains a major claim on output, though debt, input cost and market price affect the surplus.

✅ **Tenant:** cultivates land owned by another under an agreed or informal rent.

✅ **Sharecropper:** pays a share of output rather than a fixed cash rent; the actual distribution of input cost and risk varies.

✅ **Landless agricultural labourer:** depends substantially on wages and lacks secure cultivated land.

These are analytical types, not sealed identities. A household may own a small plot, lease additional land, work for wages and receive remittances in the same year.

Agricultural labour is also not synonymous with bonded labour. The first identifies a work relation; the second requires an additional condition of coercive or debt-linked unfreedom.

### 2. Ownership, cultivation and “farmer” are not synonyms

| Category | Controls title? | Cultivates? | Bears risk? | Main claim |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Owner-cultivator | Usually | Yes | High | Residual output after costs |
| Non-cultivating owner | Yes | No | Varies | Rent/share |
| Tenant/sharecropper | No | Yes | Often high | Contractual share after rent |
| Agricultural labourer | No | Works on farm | Employment risk | Wage |

⚠️ A policy or dataset using “operational holding,” “landowner,” “cultivator,” “agricultural worker” or “agricultural household” may refer to different populations. Never substitute one denominator for another.

### 3. How caste and class overlap

In many villages, historically advantaged castes acquired or retained land, credit and authority, while subordinated groups supplied insecure labour. This can make caste rank and class position reinforce each other.

```text
caste closure
   → restricted land/credit access
   → labour dependence
   → weak bargaining
   → reproduced class disadvantage
```

But caste and class are not identical. Education, migration, land reform, non-farm enterprise and electoral mobilisation can produce poor members within a locally dominant caste and prosperous members within historically subordinated castes.

> **André Béteille’s analytical use:** caste, class and power are related but distinct dimensions; the task is to examine their empirical alignment rather than assume identity.

### 4. The village is located within markets and the state

> **A.R. Desai’s analytical use:** agrarian relations cannot be explained as village customs alone; markets, state policy, commercialisation and wider class relations shape them.

This corrects a purely local account. A tenant’s bargaining position may change because of land records, crop price, credit access, migration alternatives or a public-employment option. Local hierarchy is therefore produced through both village institutions and wider political economy.

### 5. Risk reveals class difference

Suppose an input-intensive crop fails:

- a secure owner may absorb the loss through assets or formal credit;
- an insecure tenant may still owe rent and debt;
- a labourer may lose employment days;
- a woman worker may absorb additional unpaid care and food-management burdens.

**Limit:** The direction is not automatic. Large owners can be highly indebted, and tenants can possess knowledge, networks or diversified income. Class analysis identifies structured exposure, not individual destiny.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The landowner-tenant-labourer ladder is too static for a diversified rural economy.

**Reply:** The categories remain useful if treated as positions within a livelihood portfolio rather than permanent boxes. Non-farm income changes dependence without making land irrelevant.

**Residual:** Household-level categories can hide intra-household inequality. Title, labour and decision power may be distributed differently between men and women.

### UPSC application

- Separate ownership, operational cultivation, tenancy and wage labour.
- Show caste-class reinforcement, then add cross-cutting cases.
- Use Béteille to keep caste, class and power distinct.
- Use Desai to connect village relations with markets and the state.

### Revision notes

1. Agrarian class concerns control of land, cultivation, surplus and labour.
2. A tenant cultivates another’s land; a labourer primarily sells labour.
3. Sharecropping allocates output, input cost and risk through a crop share.
4. Ownership and cultivation may be separated.
5. Rural households can occupy multiple class positions simultaneously.
6. Caste and class often reinforce each other but are not identical.
7. Credit, irrigation and market access modify the effect of holding size.
8. Non-farm income can reduce patron dependence without ending social exclusion.
9. Béteille separates caste, class and power analytically.
10. Desai links agrarian structure to markets and the state.
11. Risk incidence is a strong way to explain class differentiation.
12. Household categories must be checked for intra-household gender inequality.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a tenant and an agricultural labourer not be treated as the same agrarian class?

**Model answer:** A tenant controls cultivation under a lease or share arrangement and bears production and rent-related risks, while a landless labourer mainly sells labour for wages and bears employment and bargaining risks. Their dependence, claims on output and exposure to crop failure differ.

**Misconception to avoid:** Lack of ownership does not make every rural worker “landless labour”; tenants may cultivate substantial land without title.

### Mains practice — 15 marks

**Mains question:** Analyse the relationship among caste, class and power in rural India. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Rural stratification is organised through overlapping but non-identical relations of caste, class and power. Agrarian class distinguishes landowning cultivators, tenants or sharecroppers and wage-dependent labourers by their control over land, surplus and risk. Caste can reinforce this order where historical closure restricts land, credit and dignified work for subordinated groups.

Yet ritual rank does not mechanically determine economic or political position. Migration, education, non-farm income, land reform and electoral mobilisation create class differentiation within castes. André Béteille’s distinction among caste, class and power therefore prevents one ladder from replacing three empirical questions. A wealthy individual need not command caste-wide support; a numerous caste may wield office despite middling ritual rank.

A.R. Desai further places village relations within commodity markets and the state. Crop prices, credit, land records, public employment and local institutions alter bargaining power. The correct conclusion is neither that caste equals class nor that markets have dissolved caste. Their overlap is locality-specific and must be tested through land, labour, networks and actual decision-making.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** agrarian-class taxonomy 3; caste-class mechanism 3; thinker use 3; regional/intersectional qualification 3; graded verdict 3.

### Visual: the dominant-caste test

```text
NUMERICAL STRENGTH
        +
SIGNIFICANT LOCAL LAND/ECONOMIC RESOURCES
        +
POLITICAL OFFICE AND NETWORKS
        +
ABILITY TO SECURE COMPLIANCE OR SET AGENDAS
        ↓
DOMINANT CASTE

NOT REQUIRED:
ritually highest rank
```

*Dominance is a local power configuration, not another name for ritual superiority.*

### 1. The concept

✅ **M.N. Srinivas’s dominant caste** refers to a caste that combines numerical strength, a significant share of local economic resources—especially land—and political influence. It may be ritually middle-ranked.

The concept emerged from Srinivas’s village sociology associated with **Rampura in Karnataka**. Its value lies in replacing a purely ritual map with a power map.

The same village-study tradition is also associated with his formulation of **Sanskritisation**, but dominant caste answers the different question of practical local power.

| Ritual rank asks | Dominant-caste analysis asks |
|---|---|
| Who is treated as higher or purer? | Who can influence work, credit, elections and decisions? |
| What is the status order? | Which resources converge in one caste? |
| Is hierarchy symbolically recognised? | Can the group translate resources into compliance? |

### 2. Why numbers matter

Numerical strength matters because democratic politics converts population into votes, office claims and mobilisation capacity. Numbers alone, however, are insufficient. A large but land-poor and internally divided caste may possess limited agenda power.

### 3. Why land matters

Land can provide:

- income and collateral;
- control over employment;
- social prestige;
- influence over settlement and water;
- resources for electoral mobilisation.

But landownership does not by itself constitute dominant-caste status. The concept concerns a caste-level configuration, not the largest individual landlord.

### 4. Why office and networks matter

```text
resources
   → campaign capacity and patronage
   → office-holding or influence over office
   → agenda-setting and implementation
   → reproduced local dominance
```

Political office can also challenge older power. A reserved-seat representative, an organised labour group or an SHG federation may create countervailing networks.

### 5. Dominant caste versus landowning class

| Dominant caste | Landowning class |
|---|---|
| Caste-level category | Economic class category |
| Requires collective numbers and influence | Requires relation to land and surplus |
| May include internally unequal households | May cut across several castes |
| Explains local social-political power | Explains economic position |

> **Answer line:** The two may overlap substantially, but treating them as identical converts an empirical relationship into a definition.

### 6. Contemporary relevance

Market change can alter the resources through which dominance operates. Land may remain important, but office, contracts, education, transport, remittance wealth and links to parties or bureaucracy may become additional carriers.

⚠️ The concept remains relevant when used as a test:

1. Which caste is locally numerous?
2. Who controls land, employment or credit?
3. Who holds or influences office?
4. Whose proposals enter budgets?
5. Can subordinated groups organise independently?

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The concept homogenises a caste and ignores poor members, women and internal factions.

**Reply:** That is a misuse, not a necessary feature. Dominance describes a group-level configuration; it does not claim that every member is affluent or equally influential.

**Residual:** Local power may be multi-polar rather than concentrated in one caste. A field-sensitive answer should allow competing dominant groups and shifting coalitions.

### UPSC application

- Attribute the concept to M.N. Srinivas and Rampura.
- Explain numbers, land and office as distinct but interacting resources.
- Separate dominant caste, ritual rank, largest landlord and landowning class.
- Test present relevance through actual agenda-setting rather than inherited labels.

### Revision notes

1. Dominant caste is a local power concept developed by M.N. Srinivas.
2. Rampura, Karnataka, is the standard fieldwork anchor.
3. Numerical strength supplies electoral and mobilisation capacity.
4. Land supplies surplus, employment control and collateral.
5. Office and networks translate resources into decisions.
6. Ritual superiority is not required.
7. Numbers alone do not create dominance.
8. The largest landlord is not automatically a dominant caste.
9. Dominant caste is not identical to landowning class.
10. Internal inequality within a caste must be recognised.
11. Reservation and collective organisation can contest older power.
12. Contemporary dominance may travel through land, office, contracts or networks.
13. Local power can be multi-polar and coalition-based.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a ritually middle-ranked caste be dominant in a village?

**Model answer:** Ritual rank measures status, whereas dominance depends on the convergence of local numbers, land or economic resources, political office and networks. A middle-ranked caste can therefore shape employment, elections and collective decisions more effectively than a ritually higher but numerically weak group.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Dominant” does not mean “ritually highest” or “containing the single largest landlord.”

### Mains practice — 15 marks

**Mains question:** Examine the continuing relevance and limitations of M.N. Srinivas’s concept of dominant caste. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** M.N. Srinivas’s dominant-caste concept, associated with Rampura in Karnataka, explains village power through the convergence of numerical strength, significant local resources—especially land—and political influence. It corrects a ritual-only view: a middle-ranked but numerous landholding caste may shape employment, credit and Panchayat priorities more effectively than the ritually highest group.

The concept remains relevant because democratic competition turns numbers into votes, while land, contracts, education and party-bureaucratic networks finance mobilisation. Yet it must not be equated with the landowning class or the largest landlord. A class can cut across castes, and a caste contains poor members, women and rival factions.

Its limitations are equally important. Reservation, migration, non-farm income, SHGs and subordinated-group mobilisation can create countervailing power. Some villages have competing influential castes rather than one dominant group. Therefore, “dominant caste” should be applied as a field test—who controls resources, office and agendas—not as a permanent label. It remains a powerful but locality-bound explanation of rural authority.

*A land rule matters sociologically when it changes recognised claims, dependence and bargaining power.*

### 1. What land reform includes

✅ Land reform is broader than redistribution. Its classic components include:

- abolition of intermediaries;
- tenancy regulation and security;
- ceilings on large holdings;
- consolidation of fragmented holdings;
- survey, settlement and land records.

In a Society answer, the question is not to reproduce every legal provision. It is to explain how these measures change relationships among owner, cultivator, tenant and labourer.

### 2. Tenure security changes incentives and voice

An unrecorded tenant may fear eviction after improving the land. Recognition can alter that calculation:

```text
recorded cultivation
      ↓
greater tenure security
      ↓
stronger incentive to invest
      ↓
better bargaining over rent and output
      ↓
less arbitrary dependence
```

✅ **Operation Barga in West Bengal** is a named example of recording sharecroppers. Its sociological significance lies in making the actual cultivator visible and improving bargaining security.

**Limit:** It is stronger evidence for tenancy security than for solving fragmentation, universal prosperity or every form of rural hierarchy.

### 3. Redistribution can alter authority

Where land concentration supports patronage, redistributive reform can weaken employment and credit dependence. The **Kerala land-reform experience** and **Jammu and Kashmir’s Big Landed Estates Abolition Act, 1950** are standard examples showing that political backing and implementation shape depth.

⚠️ Social effects may include:

- reduced landlord control over work and residence;
- wider asset ownership;
- changed caste-class alignment;
- stronger claim-making by former tenants;
- new fragmentation or viability problems.

No one outcome is automatic.

### 4. The implementation gap is itself sociological

```text
statutory reform
   − poor records
   − exemptions/evasion
   − political resistance
   − weak administration
            ↓
limited field outcome
```

Benami adjustment, informal leasing or exclusion of actual cultivators can preserve dependence beneath a changed legal surface. This is the norm-practice gap: formal reform is evidence of intended change, not proof of lived transformation.

### 5. Land reform and caste-class overlap

Land reform can loosen caste-class overlap where historically subordinated cultivators gain secure access. But caste stigma, social networks and local office may outlast ownership change.

Conversely, beneficiaries can become a new locally dominant agrarian group. Reform may therefore restructure the composition of power rather than abolish hierarchy.

### 6. Gender boundary

A household receiving land is not the same as every member controlling it. Ask:

- whose name is on the title;
- who decides crop, sale and credit;
- who contributes labour;
- who inherits;
- who can mortgage or lease.

⚠️ A gender-neutral household category can conceal unequal asset control.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Land reform is an Economy or Polity issue, not a Society topic.

**Reply:** Legal design belongs elsewhere, but the change in status, dependence, caste-class overlap, bargaining and household power is intrinsically sociological.

**Residual:** Land is not the only asset. Irrigation, credit, education, non-farm income and political connections can recreate inequality after redistribution.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is recording the actual cultivator socially significant?

**Model answer:** Recognition can strengthen tenure security, improve bargaining, support investment and reduce arbitrary eviction. **Mains question:** Land reform changes rural society only when legal intent becomes a change in bargaining power. Discuss. Answer in 250 words.

Kerala’s deeper reform and Jammu and Kashmir’s early 1950 abolition episode similarly show that political backing, records and administration affect outcomes. Thus legal intent becomes social transformation only when enforceable claims, accessible records, complementary credit and meaningful household control alter dependence in practice.

*The Green Revolution was a complementary input system, not a seed acting alone.*

### 1. Separate output from distribution

✅ The Green Revolution sharply raised selected crop yields where high-yielding varieties were combined with irrigation, fertiliser, credit, extension and procurement support. Early gains were concentrated in irrigated wheat-rice regions, notably Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.

Two questions must be kept separate:

1. Did aggregate food output increase?
2. Who accessed the package, captured the surplus and bore the risks?

An affirmative answer to the first does not settle the second.

### 2. Capital requirements create differentiation

Input-intensive cultivation needs cash or credit before harvest. Larger owners may possess collateral, irrigation and political connections. Tenants and smallholders may face:

- uncertain access to institutional credit;
- risk of rent or debt after crop failure;
- lower ability to wait for a favourable sale;
- weaker access to extension or assured procurement.

```text
unequal starting assets
        ↓
unequal technology access
        ↓
unequal productivity gains
        ↓
class differentiation
```

⚠️ This is a mechanism, not a universal result. Smallholders can gain where irrigation, collective access, extension, procurement and tenancy security reduce barriers.

### 3. Regional outcomes differ

| Setting | Relevant filter |
|---|---|
| Punjab-Haryana wheat-rice belt | irrigation, procurement and input depth |
| Eastern tenancy setting | tenure security, flood risk and labour relations |
| Dryland Deccan | rainfall variability, credit and crop suitability |
| Plantation region | estate labour and commodity-price dependence |
| Tribal area | land rights, market access and ecological dependence |
| Peri-urban agriculture | land conversion pressure and nearby markets |

Therefore, “the Green Revolution increased inequality” is too general unless the answer states how land, water, credit, crop and policy produced that effect in a specific context.

### 4. Caste and class channels

Where a locally dominant caste already controls land, irrigation and office, early access can reinforce its economic advantage. But technology alone does not prove political dominance: numbers, office, networks and counter-mobilisation remain necessary.

Likewise, not every member of the benefiting caste gains equally. Holding size, gender, tenancy and debt differentiate outcomes within the group.

### 5. Labour consequences

The input package can increase labour demand for some operations and seasons while mechanisation reduces demand for others. Higher output may create harvesting, transport and processing work, yet machines can displace particular tasks or weaken bargaining.

The correct approach is task-, crop- and season-specific rather than “technology creates jobs” or “technology destroys jobs.”

### 6. Ecological feedback

Production concentration can create groundwater depletion, soil stress, nutrient imbalance and residue-management problems. These are not external to society:

```text
ecological stress
      ↓
cost and yield instability
      ↓
debt and livelihood pressure
      ↓
migration · crop change · conflict over water
```

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The differentiation thesis undervalues the Green Revolution’s food-security achievement.

**Reply:** Distributional analysis does not deny aggregate achievement. It asks whether a successful production strategy imposed unequal social and ecological costs.

**Residual:** Later diffusion, public support and local institutions can modify initial inequality. Periodisation matters.

- Separate output, income, distribution and ecology.
- Explain unequal access through capital, water, credit and tenure.
- Compare at least two agrarian regions.
- End with a food-security achievement plus distributional qualification.

### Revision notes

1. The Green Revolution was a seed-water-fertiliser-credit-extension-procurement package.
2. Early gains were crop- and region-concentrated.
3. Aggregate output and distribution are separate analytical questions.
4. Upfront capital shapes access and risk.
5. Collateral and irrigation can advantage larger or secure cultivators.
6. Tenants may bear production risk plus rent obligations.
7. Smallholders can benefit where institutions reduce access barriers.
8. Caste advantage may be reinforced but is not mechanically determined.
9. Labour effects vary by operation, crop and season.
10. Ecological stress feeds back into debt and migration.
11. Regional comparison is indispensable.
12. Later diffusion may alter the initial distribution.
13. Food-security success and inequality can coexist.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Green Revolution’s aggregate output effect not sufficient to judge its social effect?

**Model answer:** Aggregate output does not reveal who accessed irrigation, credit and procurement, who bore crop and rent risk, how labour demand changed or which regions gained. Social evaluation requires distribution across land classes, castes, genders and regions, plus ecological feedback.

**Misconception to avoid:** Higher village yield does not prove higher income or stronger bargaining for every cultivator and labourer.

### Mains practice — 20 marks

**Mains question:** Critically examine the social consequences of the Green Revolution for rural stratification in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** The Green Revolution was a complementary package of high-yielding seed, irrigation, fertiliser, credit, extension and procurement. It strengthened food security, but its social consequences were filtered through unequal assets.

Secure and better-connected cultivators could access water, collateral and inputs earlier, capture marketed surplus and sometimes reinforce local caste-class dominance. Smallholders and tenants faced greater relative risk, especially where rent, informal credit or weak procurement reduced their share. Yet the result was not uniform: institutional support and later diffusion could widen smallholder gains.

Regional variation is decisive. Punjab-Haryana’s irrigated wheat-rice system differed from eastern tenancy zones and the rainfed Deccan. Labour effects also varied: intensive cultivation created some seasonal work while mechanisation reduced particular tasks. Women’s workload could rise without corresponding control over land or income.

Ecological path dependence—groundwater depletion, soil stress and cereal concentration—later fed into costs, livelihood risk and migration. Thus the Green Revolution should be judged as a major production achievement with unequal and region-specific distributive effects. Second-generation reform must combine productivity with secure tenure, inclusive credit, crop diversification and ecological resilience.

*Agrarian change is a bundle of transitions in work, risk, assets and identity.*

### 1. Commercialisation changes the objective of production

Commercialisation expands production for sale rather than primarily household use. It can create income opportunities through high-value crops, processing and larger markets. It also increases exposure to:

- price volatility;
- purchased-input costs;
- buyer concentration;
- quality standards;
- storage and transport constraints.

> **Answer line:** Market connection widens opportunity while shifting risk from subsistence shortage toward price, debt and contract failure.

### 2. Mechanisation changes tasks, not “labour” in the abstract

Machines may reduce labour demand for ploughing, transplanting, harvesting or threshing, depending on crop and scale. They may also create work in repair, transport, machine rental and processing.

| Weak claim | Better question |
|---|---|
| Mechanisation eliminates labour | Which task, season and worker group changes? |
| Mechanisation benefits all farmers | Who can buy or rent the service? |
| Mechanisation is only technological | How does it affect bargaining and migration? |

Small machine-rental services can spread access beyond owners, but cost, timing and local availability still matter.

### 3. Credit is a power relation

Credit enables investment before harvest. Its source and terms affect dependence:

```text
formal affordable credit
      → investment capacity and risk sharing

high-cost tied credit
      → sale obligation / debt rollover / weak bargaining
```

Debt should not be treated as proof of distress by itself; productive borrowing can raise income. The question is whether expected return, repayment terms and shock protection are viable.

### 4. Depeasantisation

✅ **Depeasantisation** refers to a decline or loss of direct cultivation as a household’s principal livelihood and social identity. It may involve leasing out land, land sale, wage work, migration or permanent exit.

It is not synonymous with:

- urbanisation;
- landlessness in every case;
- agricultural collapse;
- complete severance from village or kinship.

A household may retain a small plot and village membership while relying mainly on town wages.

### 5. Rural non-farm diversification

Non-farm work can:

- smooth seasonal income;
- reduce dependence on a landlord;
- finance cultivation;
- increase women’s or youth mobility;
- generate new inequality where skills and networks differ.

Thus diversification may weaken one hierarchy while creating another based on education, transport, digital access or contacts.

### 6. Ecological stress as a social mechanism

Drought, groundwater decline, soil degradation and crop loss redistribute risk. Secure owners may adapt through irrigation, insurance or diversification; tenants and labourers may face eviction, debt or job loss. Ecological change becomes social differentiation through unequal adaptive capacity.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Depeasantisation is too linear; many households move in and out of cultivation.

**Reply:** The concept should describe a tendency, not an irreversible stage. Circular migration, part-time farming and livelihood portfolios are common.

**Residual:** Household exit may conceal gendered continuity: men migrate while women continue cultivation without title or recognition.

### UPSC application

- Treat commercialisation, mechanisation, debt and migration as linked but distinct mechanisms.
- Explain task-specific labour effects.
- Define depeasantisation carefully.
- Show how ecological stress becomes inequality through unequal adaptive capacity.

2. Purchased inputs shift the timing and scale of financial exposure.
3. Mechanisation changes specific tasks, seasons and bargaining conditions.
4. Machine rental can widen access without equalising all gains.
5. Credit is enabling or exploitative according to terms and expected returns.
6. Debt alone is not proof of distress.
7. Depeasantisation means declining dependence on direct cultivation.
8. Exit from cultivation does not necessarily mean loss of village ties.
9. Rural non-farm work can reduce patron dependence.
10. Skills, transport and networks create new differentiation.
11. Ecological stress has class- and tenancy-specific effects.
12. Circular and part-time livelihood strategies challenge linear transition models.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should mechanisation be analysed by task and social group rather than by a single employment verdict?

**Model answer:** A machine may reduce labour demand for one operation while creating rental, repair, transport or processing work elsewhere. Owners, tenants, women workers and seasonal labourers face different access and displacement effects, so crop, scale, season and bargaining position must be specified.

**Misconception to avoid:** Mechanisation does not produce either universal unemployment or universal productivity gain.

### Mains practice — 20 marks

**Mains question:** Analyse how commercialisation, mechanisation and ecological stress are reshaping rural labour and livelihood in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Commercialisation links cultivators to wider buyers and high-value opportunities, but also exposes them to purchased inputs, price volatility, quality standards and storage constraints. Credit therefore becomes a power relation: affordable finance can support investment, while tied or high-cost debt can weaken bargaining.

Mechanisation changes tasks rather than labour uniformly. It may reduce demand for ploughing, transplanting or harvesting while creating machine-rental, repair, transport and processing work. Effects vary by crop, scale and timing; landless workers and small tenants cannot be treated like secure owners.

Ecological stress intensifies these differences. Groundwater decline, drought or soil degradation raises adaptation costs. Better-resourced cultivators may diversify or invest, whereas insecure tenants may exit and labourers may lose employment. Households respond through non-farm work, migration, leasing or depeasantisation. Yet exit is rarely complete: circular migrants often retain land, kinship and political ties.

The transition is therefore neither a simple march from farm to city nor a uniform agrarian crisis. It is a differentiated reorganisation of work, risk and identity requiring secure tenure, accessible services, diversified local employment and ecological resilience.

1. Who proposes the agenda?
2. Whose priorities enter the budget?
3. Who negotiates with officials and contractors?
4. Can the representative oppose a local elite?
5. Who supervises implementation?
6. Do marginalised residents attend and speak?

Seat counts answer none of these by themselves.

### 4. Two contrasting cases

**Independent authority:** An SC-reserved chairperson identifies a water-access problem, negotiates administrative support and supervises implementation despite elite resistance.

**Proxy authority:** A woman holds the chairperson’s office while a locally influential relative makes budget and contractor decisions.

These are mechanisms, not claims about every reserved representative.

### 5. Link with dominant caste

The Panchayat becomes a contested arena where inherited resources and democratic institutions meet:

```text
dominant caste:
numbers + land + networks
             ↕ contest
reserved office + mobilisation + state procedure
             ↓
outcome depends on independent capacity and countervailing organisation
```

SHGs, unions, community organisations and access to non-farm income can reduce dependence and support independent voice. They can also suffer elite capture.

### 6. Governance boundary

The detailed constitutional and administrative design of local bodies belongs to institutional analysis. For rural sociology, retain one core principle: representation, fiscal resources, functionary control and social freedom must align before office becomes effective authority.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The proxy narrative itself stereotypes women and marginalised representatives as incapable.

**Reply:** Correct. Proxy control is a risk to test, not a presumption. Many representatives exercise genuine leadership and alter local priorities.

**Residual:** Independent authority can be issue-specific. A representative may control welfare implementation but not land or contractor decisions.

### UPSC application

- Begin by acknowledging the real institutional opening created by representation.
- Define substantive power through agenda, resources and implementation.
- Present independent and proxy cases without universalising either.
- Connect outcomes to local dependence, organisation and administrative capacity.

### Revision notes

1. Formal representation is a real institutional change.
2. Office-holding and effective authority are distinct.
3. Agenda-setting is a direct test of substantive power.
4. Control of information and implementation also matters.
5. Dominant-caste networks may adapt to reservation.
6. Proxy control is possible but not universal.
7. Independent reserved leadership must be recognised.
8. Seat counts cannot measure effective decision power.
9. Non-farm income can reduce political dependence.
10. SHGs and collective organisation may create countervailing voice.
11. Elite capture can occur within participatory institutions too.
12. Authority may vary by issue rather than being all-or-nothing.

### Concept check

**Question:** What evidence would show that formal Panchayat representation has become substantive power?

**Model answer:** Independent agenda-setting, access to information, influence over budgets, ability to negotiate with officials, supervision of implementation and the capacity to resist informal elite direction are stronger evidence than holding a reserved seat alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Neither automatic empowerment nor automatic proxy control should be inferred from the category of the office-holder.

### Mains practice — 15 marks

**Mains question:** Critically examine the gap between formal representation and substantive power in rural local governance. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Formal representation creates an indispensable entry into rural authority. Reserved and elected representatives from historically excluded groups gain access to meetings, records, official communication and agenda-making. In some villages, independent leaders have redirected attention toward water, sanitation or excluded hamlets.

Yet office may coexist with informal control. A dominant local network can influence campaigns, contractors, officials and livelihoods, leaving the office-holder dependent or acting through a patron. Seat counts therefore measure presence, not agenda-setting or implementation power.

Substantive authority should be tested through who proposes projects, controls information, negotiates with staff, supervises spending and can oppose powerful interests. Non-farm income, SHGs, political organisation and administrative training may strengthen independence; intimidation, information asymmetry and fiscal dependence may weaken it.

The proxy narrative must not become a stereotype. Many women and SC/ST representatives exercise real leadership, and control can vary by issue. Representation is therefore necessary but insufficient: it becomes substantive power when institutional resources and social freedom permit independent decisions and accountable implementation.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** representation concept 2; informal-power mechanism 4; contrasting cases 3; dominant-caste linkage 2; institutional boundary 2; balanced verdict 2.

### Visual: a household across two spaces

```text
VILLAGE HOUSEHOLD
land · kinship · care · local politics
        │
        ├── worker migrates to town/city
        ├── remittance returns
        ├── goods and ideas circulate
        └── worker may return seasonally
        ↓
RURAL-URBAN CONTINUUM

NOT:
village = wholly traditional
town    = wholly modern
```

*Migration links places without making them identical.*

### 1. Continuum versus dichotomy

✅ A **rural-urban dichotomy** treats village and town as opposed types: traditional versus modern, agricultural versus industrial, intimate versus anonymous.

⚠️ A **rural-urban continuum** treats settlements as differently positioned within flows of labour, capital, goods, information and institutions.

| Dichotomy | Continuum |
|---|---|
| Sharp separation | Graduated connection |
| Migration ends rural ties | Migration may sustain origin ties |
| Village is self-contained | Village participates in wider markets |
| Urban influence equals urban identity | Linkage and local specificity coexist |

### 2. Migration is a household strategy

Migration may:

- diversify income;
- smooth agricultural seasonality;
- finance education, housing or inputs;
- reduce dependence on local patrons;
- create family separation and care burdens;
- transfer farm management to women or older persons.

The unit is often the household, not the isolated migrant.

### 3. Remittance changes bargaining—but unevenly

Remittance can reduce dependence on a landlord or local moneylender. It may finance cultivation or non-farm enterprise. Yet access to migration itself depends on networks, skills, money, safety and social permission.

```text
migration network
     ↓
lower job-search cost
     ↓
external earnings
     ↓
reduced local dependence

but:
unequal access to the network
     ↓
new differentiation
```

### 4. Rural non-farm economy

Construction, transport, retail, public services, repair, processing and digital work complicate an agriculture-only identity. Non-farm employment can:

- provide year-round income;
- alter youth aspirations;
- weaken hereditary occupation;
- increase land leasing;
- create new skill and gender divides.

### 5. Continuum does not erase place

Land relations, remoteness, ecology and local authority still matter. A peri-urban village facing land conversion differs from a tribal settlement with weak transport or a dryland village dependent on circular migration.

> **Answer line:** Continuity of flows does not mean uniformity of opportunity.

### 6. Social consequences

Migration can challenge hierarchy by enabling exit. It can also reproduce caste networks in destination housing and job referrals. It may strengthen origin politics when migrants finance campaigns or community institutions.

**Limit:** A road, mobile phone or remittance proves connection, not equality or complete cultural change.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The continuum model is so broad that it makes every settlement “partly urban.”

**Reply:** Its value is relational, not classificatory. It does not replace official settlement categories; it explains flows and interdependence.

**Residual:** Some settlements remain comparatively isolated, and some households within connected villages cannot use the available links.

### UPSC application

- Define continuum against dichotomy.
- Trace labour, remittance, market, media and institutional flows.
- Keep migration’s economic gains beside care, exclusion and network inequality.
- End with connection without sameness.

### Revision notes

1. The rural-urban continuum rejects a rigid traditional-modern binary.
2. It does not abolish official rural and urban categories.
3. Migration often operates as a household livelihood strategy.
4. Remittances can reduce local patron dependence.
5. Migration access is itself unequal.
6. Rural non-farm work diversifies income and identity.
7. External income can finance farming rather than replace it.
8. Migration may transfer farm and care work to women.
9. Caste networks can travel to destination labour and housing markets.
10. Peri-urban land pressure differs from remote rural isolation.
11. Infrastructure proves connection, not equality.
12. Continuum means interdependence with retained local specificity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does migration support the rural-urban continuum rather than prove a one-way transition from village to city?

**Model answer:** Migrants often retain land, kinship, care and political ties with the village while sending remittances, circulating information and returning seasonally. Their household operates across rural and urban spaces, so linkage—not complete severance—is the central process.

**Misconception to avoid:** A migrant wage or paved road does not make the origin village socially identical to a city.

### Mains practice — 10 marks

**Mains question:** Why is the rural-urban continuum a better sociological model than a rigid rural-urban dichotomy? Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** The rural-urban continuum explains villages and towns through flows rather than opposed types. Rural households may cultivate land while earning town wages, receiving remittances, buying inputs in urban markets and using media or transport networks. Migration often sustains, rather than ends, kinship and political ties with the origin village.

The model also captures change in power. External income can reduce dependence on landlords or hereditary patrons and finance education or cultivation. Yet the benefits are unequal because migration requires networks, skills, money and mobility. Women or older people may absorb additional farm and care work.

The continuum does not claim that every village is equally connected or that land, ecology and remoteness no longer matter. It is preferred because it explains interdependence while retaining local specificity—connection without social sameness.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** definition 2; flow mechanism 3; continuum-dichotomy distinction 2; inequality qualification 2; conclusion 1.

### 1. Four evidence types

| Evidence type | What it can establish | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Census | Stock and composition at a reference date | Ages between rounds |
| Sample survey | Estimate for a defined population and period | Not an exact village headcount |
| Administrative data | Registered activity or programme process | Excludes non-users and may change quickly |
| Field study | Mechanism, relation and lived practice | Cannot establish national prevalence alone |

### 2. What the PLFS Annual Report 2025 contributes

✅ **Fact:** The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation released the **Periodic Labour Force Survey Annual Report 2025** through a PIB release dated **27 March 2026**. The report covers **January–December 2025**, reflecting the calendar-year reference period under the revamped PLFS design.

⚠️ **Analytical use:** The report can update the employment context in which rural diversification, women’s work and movement away from agriculture are discussed.

❌ **What it cannot prove:** PLFS cannot by itself establish that jajmani has disappeared, identify a village’s dominant caste, measure land title or determine who actually controls a Panchayat budget.

> **Data line for an answer:** “PLFS supplies a dated labour-status estimate; village power requires relational and institutional evidence.”

### 3. Reference period is not decoration

Comparisons require:

- the same indicator definition;
- comparable age group and status concept;
- compatible rural/urban coverage;
- exact reference period;
- awareness of survey-design changes.

The PLFS 2023–24 annual round remains a historical comparator. Its older July–June frame must not be silently carried into the January–December 2025 report.

An undated labour percentage is not a structural constant.

### 4. Agriculture Census and land records

Agriculture Census data concerns operational holdings, not automatically legal ownership or household power. Land records may identify title but not who cultivates, controls income or makes decisions.

```text
operational holding ≠ legal title
legal title          ≠ effective household control
cultivation          ≠ sole livelihood
```

### 5. Situation Assessment evidence

Household surveys can illuminate income sources, cultivation, debt and living conditions. But “agricultural household” follows a survey definition and should not be casually replaced by “farmer,” “landowner” or “all rural households.”

### 6. Measuring power requires relational evidence

To test dominant caste or substantive Panchayat authority, combine:

- caste composition;
- land and employment relations;
- office-holding;
- budget priorities;
- who proposes and implements decisions;
- accounts from dissenting or excluded households.

This is triangulation: no single indicator settles the question.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Local qualitative evidence is subjective and inferior to a national survey.

**Reply:** Reliability depends on the question. A national survey estimates labour status well; it is not designed to observe informal authority. Multiple field sources and records can rigorously test a relational mechanism.

**Residual:** Local evidence remains vulnerable to selection and researcher bias, so transparent methods and comparison are necessary.

### UPSC application

- Name the evidence type and reference period.

- Separate labour status, landholding, household income and power.
- Use PLFS to establish labour-status context, then turn to relational evidence before making claims about village power.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can PLFS contextualise rural change but not prove a change in village power?

**Model answer:** PLFS estimates labour-force and employment characteristics for a defined population and period. It does not directly observe caste networks, land control, patron-client ties, budget agenda-setting or informal influence, which require village-level relational and institutional evidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** A national labour indicator cannot be used as a proxy for every dimension of rural hierarchy.

**Mains question:** How should evidence be used to analyse rural social change without causal overreach? Answer in 150 words.

**Mains model:** Rural social change requires matching each claim to the evidence that can test it. PLFS estimates labour status for a defined period; Agriculture Census describes operational holdings; Situation Assessment surveys examine specified agricultural households; land records concern recorded rights. Evidence must therefore retain source, definition and period. Survey association should not be presented as causation, and a case study should not become national prevalence. Triangulating compatible quantitative and field evidence produces the most defensible rural-society answer.

*The same reform or technology travels through different tenure, ecological and political structures.*

### 1. Why regional comparison is necessary

Rural India does not share one tenure regime, crop system, ecology or caste configuration. A mechanism must therefore be specified:

- irrigation and procurement can concentrate gains;
- insecure tenancy can weaken investment and bargaining;
- dryland risk can intensify debt and migration;
- plantation labour has estate-specific authority;
- tribal regions add land, forest and self-governance questions;
- peri-urban areas face land conversion and fragmented governance.

The comparison is analytical, not a claim that every location within a region is identical.

### 2. Three lenses that must work together

#### M.N. Srinivas: local power

The dominant-caste lens asks how numbers, land and office converge in a locality.

#### André Béteille: separate dimensions

The caste-class-power distinction prevents ritual status, economic position and political authority from being collapsed.

#### A.R. Desai: political economy

The agrarian-structure lens connects village relations with markets, state policy and wider class processes.

```text
Srinivas → who dominates locally?
Béteille → which axis—caste, class or power?
Desai    → how do market and state shape the locality?
```

Together they offer a stronger explanation than any one alone.

### 3. Transformation, persistence and replacement

Use three outcomes:

1. **Transformation:** jajmani becomes contractual or selective.
2. **Persistence:** caste exclusion or land concentration continues.
3. **Replacement:** a new network—party, contractor, SHG, remittance or bureaucracy—carries influence formerly tied mainly to land or ritual status.

This triad prevents the crude choice between “nothing changed” and “tradition disappeared.”

### 4. Collective organisations as countervailing power

SHGs, cooperatives, unions and producer organisations can create:

- bonding trust within a group;
- bridging links across groups;
- linking access to banks, markets and officials.

They may reduce caste-linked gatekeeping. But bonding without inclusion can reproduce elite control, and credit access does not guarantee profitable enterprise or political autonomy.

### 5. An integrated causal map

```text
INITIAL STRUCTURE
land + caste + labour + gender
        ↓ filtered by
REFORM / TECHNOLOGY / MARKET / MIGRATION
        ↓
NEW RESOURCE DISTRIBUTION
title · credit · skill · remittance · office · information
        ↓
COUNTERVAILING OR REPRODUCED POWER
        ↓
OUTCOME:
mobility / differentiation / exclusion / collective agency
        ↓
FEEDBACK:
new politics, migration, ecology and household strategy
```

### 6. Major criticisms and replies

| Criticism | Reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Village sociology is too local | Local studies reveal mechanisms hidden by aggregates | Generalisation needs comparison |
| Class analysis ignores caste | Class can be studied with status and closure | Weight varies by region |
| Dominant caste homogenises groups | Use it as a configuration, not a member-level claim | Factions complicate measurement |
| Continuum erases rural specificity | It explains flows, not identical settlement types | Isolation remains in some places |
| Representation equals empowerment | Test agenda, resources and implementation | Authority may be issue-specific |

### 7. The qualified synthesis

> **Master thesis:** Agrarian change in India has more often redistributed the resources through which rural power operates than abolished hierarchy itself. Land remains central, but credit, irrigation, office, migration income, skills, organisations and state access now interact with caste and gender in regionally distinct ways.

- Compare at least two regional trajectories.
- Name Srinivas, Béteille and Desai only for the analytical work they perform.
- End with restructuring rather than linear modernisation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “agrarian change has dissolved traditional hierarchy” an inadequate national conclusion?

**Model answer:** Change operates through different tenure systems, ecologies, caste configurations and institutions. Jajmani may decline while land concentration persists; migration may reduce patron dependence while gender burdens rise; reservation may create voice or face proxy control. Transformation, persistence and replacement must be assessed regionally.

**Misconception to avoid:** Regional variation is not an afterthought; it is part of the causal explanation.

### Mains practice — 20 marks

**Mains question:** “Agrarian change has restructured rural power more often than it has dissolved it.” Evaluate with reference to India. Answer in 250 words.

**Mains model:** Rural power historically rested on overlapping land, labour, caste and kinship relations. Agrarian change has altered these resources, but rarely removed hierarchy uniformly.

Jajmani ties have often become monetised and contractual as service groups gain alternative clients and non-farm work, while ritual relations sometimes persist. Land reform can improve tenant security, yet weak records or informal leasing preserve dependence. The Green Revolution raised output but channelled early gains through irrigation, credit and procurement, often strengthening better-resourced cultivators; effects differed across Punjab-Haryana, eastern tenancy zones and the dryland Deccan.

Migration and remittances reduce some landlord dependence and create a rural-urban continuum, but access to mobility is unequal. Feminisation can increase women’s workload without title or decision power. Panchayat reservation opens office to excluded groups, yet substantive authority depends on independent agenda-setting and implementation.

Srinivas identifies numbers-land-office dominance; Béteille separates caste, class and power; Desai connects village relations to market and state. Together they show that land now interacts with office, networks, credit, skill and remittance. Agrarian change is therefore best understood as a region-specific redistribution of the resources carrying power, not a linear disappearance of hierarchy.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** synthesis thesis 3; regional typology 4; thinker integration 3; transformation-persistence analysis 4; countervailing institutions 3; qualified verdict 3.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Unit of analysis

**Question:** A district reports rising non-farm employment. Does this establish the disappearance of agrarian hierarchy?

**Model answer:** No. It shows livelihood diversification, not who owns land, controls credit, holds office or sets village agendas. Non-farm income may reduce dependence, but caste, gender and land networks can persist or reorganise.

**Repair if missed:** Separate change in occupation from change in power.

## Check 2 — Jajmani boundary

**Question:** A customary priest receives cash from a long-standing patron family. Is the relationship necessarily non-jajmani?

**Model answer:** No. The payment medium is not decisive. Examine heredity, fixed patronage, personal obligation, caste linkage and freedom to serve multiple clients.

**Repair if missed:** Use structure of obligation, not cash-versus-kind, as the test.

## Check 3 — Class and caste

**Question:** Why can caste-class overlap loosen while caste remains socially important?

**Model answer:** Migration, education and non-farm income can create class differences within a caste, but endogamy, networks, stigma and local political mobilisation may continue. Economic cross-cutting does not automatically dissolve social closure.

**Repair if missed:** Distinguish economic position from status and boundary reproduction.

## Check 4 — Dominance

**Question:** What additional evidence is needed before calling a numerous landholding caste “dominant”?

**Model answer:** Evidence of political influence, office or network control, agenda-setting and the ability to translate numbers and resources into local decisions.

**Repair if missed:** Numbers plus land are important but do not complete the concept.

## Check 5 — Land reform

**Question:** Why can land reform alter ownership on paper yet leave dependence largely intact?

**Model answer:** Weak records, informal tenancy, credit dependence, local intimidation, unequal irrigation and poor administrative enforcement can preserve the effective power of prior elites.

**Repair if missed:** Insert the implementation and complementary-resource chain.

## Check 6 — Technology

**Question:** Can the same input technology benefit a small tenant and a secure owner differently?

**Model answer:** Yes. Credit terms, irrigation, rent, collateral, procurement and ability to absorb loss shape adoption and net gain. Technology is filtered through class and tenure.

**Repair if missed:** Move from the input to the institutional conditions surrounding it.

## Check 7 — Gender

**Question:** What would convert women’s greater farm responsibility into empowerment?

**Model answer:** Recognised title or secure lease, control of wages and sale proceeds, access to credit and extension, decision power, reduced time burden and effective voice in producer or local institutions.

**Repair if missed:** Participation is one indicator; control is the decisive test.

## Check 8 — Representation

**Question:** Why is a reserved Panchayat seat neither merely symbolic nor sufficient?

**Model answer:** It gives formal access, visibility and institutional standing, which are real gains. It is insufficient because agenda-setting, information, staff, funds and implementation may remain controlled by informal elites.

**Repair if missed:** Hold institutional opening and implementation gap together.

## Check 9 — Continuum

**Question:** How can migration simultaneously weaken and reproduce caste relations?

**Model answer:** External earnings can reduce dependence on local patrons, while caste networks may still organise destination jobs, housing, marriage and origin politics. Different functions of caste change at different speeds.

**Repair if missed:** Avoid treating migration as a single-direction modernising force.

## Check 10 — Evidence

**Question:** Which evidence would best test whether a caste controls village power: PLFS or a triangulated village study?

**Model answer:** A triangulated village study using caste composition, land and employment relations, office-holding, budgets and decision pathways. PLFS can contextualise labour status but is not designed to observe informal authority.

**Repair if missed:** Match the evidence to the claim.

## Check 11 — Regional variation

**Question:** Why can the Green Revolution not receive one uniform distributional verdict?

**Model answer:** Land distribution, tenancy security, irrigation, crop, credit, procurement, labour demand and later diffusion vary by region and period, changing who gains and who bears risk.

**Repair if missed:** Region is a causal variable, not a decorative example.

## Check 12 — Synthesis

**Question:** What does “rural power is restructured” mean?

**Model answer:** The resources carrying influence may shift from hereditary service and land alone toward office, contracts, remittances, skills, credit and organisations, while older caste, class and gender inequalities persist, adapt or face countervailing power.

**Repair if missed:** Identify both the changing resource and the residual hierarchy.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark practice

**Question:** Distinguish a dominant caste from a landowning class. Why does the distinction matter for analysing village power? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:** A dominant caste is a caste-level configuration identified by M.N. Srinivas through the convergence of local numerical strength, significant economic resources—often land—and political influence. A landowning class is an economic category comprising those who control substantial land and surplus; it may include several castes.

The two can overlap but are not identical. A wealthy landlord may lack caste-wide numbers or political support, while a numerous caste may exercise office and network power despite internal poverty. Ritual rank is a further separate axis.

The distinction matters because village authority is not explained by property alone. Analysis must ask who mobilises votes, controls employment and credit, occupies or influences office and sets collective agendas. Conflating caste and class hides internal inequality and falsely turns a local empirical relationship into a universal rule.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** definitions 3; overlap-versus-identity distinction 2; power mechanism 3; limitation 1; conclusion 1.

## Original 15-mark practice

**Question:** Examine how migration is changing gendered work and authority in rural households. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** Migration reorganises rural households across two locations. External wages and remittances can diversify income, reduce landlord dependence and finance cultivation or education. At the same time, male out-migration often transfers farm management, care and community responsibilities to women.

This can expand practical knowledge and operational autonomy, but feminisation is not automatically empowerment. A woman may select labour and manage sowing while land title, formal credit, machinery access and crop-sale decisions remain in a migrant husband’s or elder’s name. Irregular remittances can increase her financial risk, and combined farm-care work can deepen time poverty.

Effects vary by caste, class and tenure. Landless women face seasonal wage insecurity; tenant women may cultivate without recorded rights; women in owner households may manage assets they cannot inherit or mortgage. Migration access itself depends on money, networks and social permission.

Authority rises where responsibility is matched by title, income control, extension, collective organisation and voice in local institutions. It remains constrained where women are administratively treated as helpers. Migration therefore redistributes work immediately, but redistributes power only under supporting asset and institutional conditions.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** migration-household mechanism 3; feminisation distinction 3; asset-income-decision analysis 4; intersectional variation 2; enabling conditions 2; conclusion 1.

## Original 20-mark practice

**Question:** Market integration has transformed rural India without ending social stratification. Critically assess this statement across different agrarian settings. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:** Market integration links rural households to commodity prices, credit, technology, town employment and remittances. It has weakened some hereditary service ties, widened occupational choice and created non-farm income. Yet gains are filtered through existing land, caste, gender and institutional inequalities.

In irrigated Punjab-Haryana, the Green Revolution’s seed-water-credit-procurement package raised output but favoured actors able to access complementary inputs early. Eastern tenancy settings place greater weight on security and rent claims; dryland Deccan households face rainfall and credit risk; plantation labour remains shaped by estate authority; peri-urban villages experience land-value gains and displacement pressure.

Marketisation can convert jajmani services into negotiated work, but caste stigma and networks may persist. Migration reduces local patron dependence while destination jobs and housing can still follow caste contacts. Feminisation may increase women’s workload without title or sale control. Panchayat reservation introduces countervailing office, yet informal elites may retain agenda power.

Therefore market integration changes the resources through which stratification operates—land now interacts with credit, office, skill, remittance and networks. It neither preserves a timeless village nor produces automatic equality. Inclusive transformation requires secure tenure, accessible finance, non-farm work, gendered asset control, ecological resilience and effective local voice.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** clear thesis 3; multi-setting comparison 5; market mechanism 3; caste/class/gender integration 4; countervailing institutions 2; critical verdict 3.

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 — Treating rural as agricultural

**Why it fails:** It omits non-farm work, kinship, care, local politics, migration and state-market linkages.

**Repair:** Open with the distinction and use agriculture as one major subsystem.

## Error 2 — Defining jajmani by grain payment

**Why it fails:** Cash and mixed payment existed.

**Repair:** Test heredity, personalisation, caste linkage and fixed patronage.

## Error 3 — Equating caste, class and power

**Why it fails:** The axes overlap empirically but have different units and mechanisms.

**Repair:** Use Béteille’s separation and show the local pattern of alignment.

## Error 4 — Calling the ritually highest caste dominant

**Why it fails:** Dominance requires numbers, resources and political influence.

**Repair:** Apply the Srinivas test and investigate agenda-setting.

## Error 5 — Converting land-law intent into social outcome

**Why it fails:** Records, evasion, credit and enforcement mediate the result.

**Repair:** Trace rule → recognised claimant → bargaining → lived outcome.

## Error 6 — Treating technology as an independent cause

**Why it fails:** Irrigation, credit, extension, procurement, crop and tenancy condition adoption and gain.

**Repair:** Write the complementary-input and unequal-access chain.

## Error 7 — Assuming mechanisation has one employment effect

**Why it fails:** Tasks, crops, seasons and worker groups differ.

**Repair:** Name the operation replaced and the alternative work created or lost.

## Error 8 — Equating feminisation with empowerment

**Why it fails:** Workload may rise without title, income or decisions.

**Repair:** Use the asset-income-decision-time-voice test.

## Error 9 — Treating reservation as either automatic success or automatic proxy rule

**Why it fails:** Both genuine leadership and informal control occur.

**Repair:** Examine agenda, information, funds and implementation in the specific case.

## Error 10 — Using continuum as “village has become city”

**Why it fails:** Continuum explains flows while retaining local specificity.

**Repair:** Show migration, remittance and market links, then retain land and remoteness qualifications.

## Error 11 — Using PLFS to prove informal power

**Why it fails:** Labour-status surveys do not observe dominant caste, jajmani or budget control.

**Repair:** Use PLFS for labour context and field/institutional evidence for power.

## Error 12 — Adding “regional variation” only in the conclusion

**Why it fails:** Region changes the mechanism itself.

**Repair:** Compare at least two settings inside the body through tenancy, water, crop, market and institutional variables.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Topic boundary and master thesis

- Rural society includes agriculture, non-farm work, kinship, caste, gender, markets, local institutions and state linkages.
- Agrarian change covers tenure, technology, commercialisation, mechanisation, feminisation, migration, depeasantisation, non-farm diversification and ecological stress.
- Rural is not agricultural; landowner is not cultivator; farmer is not only a title-holder.
- Master thesis: agrarian change redistributes the resources through which power operates more often than it abolishes hierarchy.

## 2. Village society: structure and connections

- Basic axes: land, labour, status and authority.
- External flows: migration, remittance, media, roads, welfare, elections and commodity markets.
- Reject the self-sufficient harmonious-village model.
- Reject the opposite claim that market connection has erased local inequality.
- Infrastructure changes opportunity through existing assets, mobility and networks.

## 3. Jajmani system

- Historical, regionally variable patron-client service arrangement.
- Jajman: patron household; kamin: customary service provider.
- Common services: barbering, washing, ritual work, carpentry and smithing.
- Payment often involved grain or kind but could include cash.
- Defining feature: hereditary, personalised and caste-linked obligation to a relatively fixed patron.
- Change mechanisms: monetisation, multiple clients, education, migration, non-farm work and occupational decoupling.
- Correct verdict: uneven contractualisation with possible ritual or personalised residuals.
- Trap: neither remoteness nor cash alone proves the institution’s presence or absence.

## 4. Agrarian class structure

- Owner-cultivator: controls land and cultivation, bearing production and market risk.
- Tenant: cultivates another’s land under rent or informal arrangement.
- Sharecropper: transfers a crop share; input and risk distribution varies.
- Landless labourer: primarily sells labour and faces employment/bargaining risk.
- Households can combine ownership, leasing, wage work and remittances.
- Ownership, operational holding, cultivation and “farmer” are distinct categories.
- Caste-class overlap is empirical, locality-specific and historically produced.

## 5. Thinker toolkit

| Thinker | Use in the answer | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| M.N. Srinivas | dominant caste; Rampura; numbers-land-office power | locality-specific, not member-level uniformity |
| André Béteille | caste, class and power are related but distinct | requires empirical mapping in each setting |
| A.R. Desai | village relations shaped by market, state and wider class structure | macro lens must not erase local agency |

## 6. Dominant caste

- Numerical strength converts population into mobilisation and electoral capacity.
- Land supplies surplus, employment control, collateral and prestige.
- Office and networks translate resources into decisions.
- Ritual superiority is not required.
- Dominant caste is not the largest landlord or the landowning class.
- Test dominance through agenda-setting, budget influence, employment, credit and the ability to secure compliance.
- Internal class, gender and factional differences remain.
- Contemporary carriers may include contracts, education, remittance wealth and party-bureaucratic links.

## 7. Land reform: social effects

- Components: intermediary abolition, tenancy regulation, ceilings, consolidation and land records.
- Social mechanism: recognised cultivator → security → investment/bargaining → changed dependence.
- Operation Barga: sharecropper recording and tenure-security mechanism.
- Kerala: reform depth linked to political and administrative follow-through.
- J&K Big Landed Estates Abolition Act, 1950: early, distinctive reform episode.
- Formal rule is not implementation; weak records, evasion and informal leasing matter.
- Reform can loosen caste-class overlap without ending caste or gender inequality.
- Household title is not equal to women’s effective control.

## 8. Green Revolution and differentiation

- Complementary package: HYV seed, irrigation, fertiliser, credit, extension and procurement.
- Early concentration: irrigated wheat-rice regions, especially Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.
- Output, income and distribution must be separated.
- Upfront capital and collateral shape access.
- Tenants can bear crop risk plus rent obligations.
- Labour effect varies by operation, crop and season.
- Ecological feedback: groundwater, soil, nutrient and residue stress affect costs and migration.
- Regional and temporal variation prevents a single national distributional verdict.

## 9. Commercialisation, mechanisation and debt

- Commercialisation widens buyers and income opportunities while increasing price, input and contract risk.
- Mechanisation changes specific tasks, not “employment” uniformly.
- Machine rental may widen access but timing and cost remain unequal.
- Credit enables investment; terms determine whether it strengthens or weakens bargaining.
- Debt is not automatically distress; viability depends on return, repayment and shocks.
- Ecological risk becomes social inequality through unequal adaptive capacity.

## 10. Depeasantisation and non-farm work

- Depeasantisation: declining direct cultivation as the principal livelihood or identity.
- It may involve leasing, sale, migration, wage work or permanent exit.
- It is not synonymous with urbanisation or total severance from village ties.
- Rural non-farm work can smooth income and reduce patron dependence.
- Skills, transport, education and networks create new differentiation.
- Part-time and circular livelihoods challenge linear farm-to-city models.

## 11. Feminisation of agriculture

- May mean more women workers, tasks or farm-management responsibility.
- Five tests: assets, income, decisions, time and voice.
- Male migration can transfer workload without title.
- Remittance may improve security but can be irregular.
- Women differ by caste, class, tenancy, age and disability.
- Mechanisation can displace gendered tasks or reduce drudgery according to design and access.
- Administrative recognition as cultivator affects credit, extension, insurance and compensation.
- Participation is not empowerment without control.

## 12. Panchayat representation and village power

- Formal representation creates entry, visibility, information and claim-making.
- Substantive power requires agenda-setting, budget influence, staff access and implementation.
- Proxy control is a possible mechanism, not a universal assumption.
- Independent SC/ST/women leadership is equally real and must be recognised.
- Dominant-caste networks may adapt through campaign, contractor and official links.
- SHGs and other organisations can create countervailing voice but may suffer elite capture.
- Test who proposes, decides, spends, implements and can be held accountable.

## 13. Rural-urban continuum

- Continuum explains flows; it does not replace official settlement categories.
- Main flows: labour, remittance, goods, media, education, roads and administration.
- Migration is commonly a household strategy.
- External income can finance cultivation or non-farm enterprise.
- Access to migration is unequal and network-dependent.
- Caste can weaken as occupational allocation yet persist in marriage, housing and job contacts.
- Connection does not mean sameness; remoteness, land and ecology remain significant.

## 14. Regional agrarian comparison

| Setting | High-yield variables |
|---|---|
| Punjab-Haryana | irrigation, procurement, cereal path dependence, groundwater |
| Eastern India | tenancy security, labour relations, flood and land records |
| Dryland Deccan | rainfall, credit, crop risk, migration |
| Plantation | estate authority, labour dependence, commodity price |
| Tribal | land/forest rights, market-state relations, self-governance |
| Coastal | fisheries-farm interaction, climate and migration |
| Peri-urban | land conversion, property value, fragmented governance |

- Use regions to explain why mechanisms differ, not to stereotype entire populations.

## 15. Data discipline: using PLFS without causal overreach

- PLFS: labour-force and employment estimates for a defined period.
- Agriculture Census: operational holdings, not automatically title or household control.
- Land records: recorded rights, not necessarily actual cultivation or gendered authority.
- Situation Assessment: survey-defined agricultural households, income, debt and cultivation characteristics.
- Field study: mechanism and lived authority, not national prevalence.
- **27 March 2026:** MoSPI/PIB released PLFS Annual Report 2025 for January–December 2025.
- Use PLFS to contextualise work, not to prove jajmani decline, dominant caste or Panchayat control.
- Always state source, category and reference period.

1. Rural equals agricultural.
2. Jajmani means payment only in grain.
3. Jajmani vanished everywhere.
4. Caste equals class.
5. Dominant caste equals ritually highest caste.
6. Dominant caste equals landowning class.
7. Land statute equals implemented redistribution.
8. Higher output equals higher income for all.
9. Mechanisation has one employment effect.
10. More women’s work equals empowerment.
11. Reserved seat equals substantive power.
12. Proxy control is universal.
13. Continuum means villages are urban.
14. National survey proves local informal authority.
15. One case study proves national prevalence.
