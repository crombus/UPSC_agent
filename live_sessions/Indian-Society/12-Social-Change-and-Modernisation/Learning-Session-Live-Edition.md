# Indian Society Topic 12 — Social Change and Modernisation

Social change is not a straight road from “tradition” to “modernity.” Institutions may change while values persist; new values may spread without equal access to new institutions; local culture may reshape a pan-Indian form rather than simply receive it. The central task is therefore to identify **what changes, through which mechanism, at what level, for whom, and with what resistance or unintended effect**.

Social change is a durable alteration in social relations, institutions, norms, roles, identities or patterns of interaction. A fashion may change without transforming an institution. One person may move upward without changing the stratification system. A law may change while behaviour lags. The term therefore requires a **social object**, a **mechanism**, a **time dimension** and an **effect**.

### 1. Core distinctions

| Concept | Precise meaning | Frequent error |
|---|---|---|
| Social change | alteration in social relations, institutions, norms, roles or identities | treating every novelty as structural change |
| Social reform | deliberate attempt to correct a practice or institution | assuming intention guarantees outcome |
| Social mobility | movement of a person/group within stratification | equating positional mobility with structural equality |
| Modernity | a broad condition involving differentiated institutions, calculation, science, individual choice and rational-legal organisation | treating it as Western dress |
| Modernisation | process of institutional and cultural movement toward selected modern forms | assuming one universal route |
| Development | normative and material improvement in capabilities or wellbeing | assuming all change is improvement |
| Evolution | directional long-term transformation | assuming direction necessarily means progress |

Change can be planned or unplanned, gradual or abrupt, internal or externally stimulated, material or symbolic, progressive for one group and costly for another.

### 2. Level, pace and scope

```text
MICRO: identity, household practice, everyday interaction
MESO : organisation, community, school, market, association
MACRO: class/caste relations, state, economy, media system

Pace: slow accumulation ↔ rapid disruption
Scope: local ↔ regional ↔ national ↔ transnational
```

A digital payment tool can alter household bargaining at the micro level, business organisation at the meso level and state-market relations at the macro level. These effects need not occur together.

### 3. Direction is not value

“Change” is analytically neutral. Abolition of an exclusionary practice may expand equality; automated surveillance may be technologically new while reducing autonomy. Development and empowerment require an additional normative test: whose capabilities, dignity and voice improve?

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** The definition is so broad that almost any difference becomes social change.

**Response:** Require a clear unit, mechanism and durable relational or institutional effect. A temporary preference is insufficient unless it changes roles, rules or patterned interaction.

**Residual:** The beginning and endpoint of change are often contested. Periodisation is an analytical choice, not a naturally given boundary.

### UPSC application

- Define the specific object: caste status, family rule, cultural practice, institution or technology.
- Separate positional mobility from structural transformation.
- Do not call change “development” without an explicit evaluative criterion.
- Use the sequence: object → mechanism → differentiated effects → resistance/feedback → verdict.

### Revision notes

1. Social change alters patterned relations, institutions, norms, roles or identities.
2. Reform is intentional; change may be unintended.
3. Mobility can occur without structural transformation.
4. Modernisation is one type of social change, not its synonym.
5. Development adds a normative capability test.
6. Change operates at micro, meso and macro levels.
7. Different dimensions change at different speeds.
8. Novelty is not necessarily progress.
9. Law, practice and outcome must be separated.
10. Every answer needs object, mechanism, distribution and qualification.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the movement of one household into salaried work not by itself prove structural modernisation?

**Model answer:** It proves a change in occupation and perhaps mobility. Structural modernisation requires wider alteration in institutions, role differentiation, authority, technology or rules. The household may retain older kinship authority and status practices despite its new occupation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer system-wide change from one actor’s movement.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish social change, social reform and modernisation. Explain why the distinction matters for analysing Indian society.

**Complete model:** Social change is any durable alteration in institutions, relations, norms, roles or identities. Social reform is a deliberate effort to correct a practice; its intention may exceed its actual effect. Modernisation is a more specific process involving rational-legal organisation, differentiated institutions, technology and expanded choice.

The distinction matters because the three can diverge. A reform law may be enacted while custom persists. M.N. Srinivas’s Sanskritisation shows how a caste group may claim mobility without ending status hierarchy. A household may use modern technology while retaining unequal gender authority. Conversely, locally generated collective action may transform power without copying a Western institutional form.

An answer should therefore identify the object and mechanism of change, distinguish formal rule from lived practice and assess effects across groups. Indian society is best read as selective and uneven transformation, not as a single transition from tradition to modernity.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** three definitions 3; divergence mechanisms 3; Indian examples 2; qualified conclusion 1; clarity 1.

```text
WHY DID SOCIETY CHANGE?
 |
 +-- EVOLUTIONARY: What directional sequence occurred?
 +-- CYCLICAL: What rise-decline-renewal pattern recurred?
 +-- STRUCTURAL-FUNCTIONAL: What adaptation restored or renegotiated order?
 +-- CONFLICT: Which contradiction and struggle altered power?
 +-- DIFFUSION: Which idea, technology or practice spread across boundaries?
 +-- MULTILINEAR: Which context-specific pathway combined several mechanisms?
```

### 1. Evolutionary theory

Evolutionary approaches explain change as movement from simpler to more differentiated forms. Early unilinear versions associated all societies with one sequence and often converted Western experience into a universal scale.

**Use:** long-term differentiation of institutions, occupations and authority.

**Limit:** teleology, ethnocentrism and the assumption that later automatically means better.

### 2. Cyclical theory

Cyclical perspectives emphasise recurrence: civilisations, elites, institutions or cultural orientations may rise, decline and renew through different mechanisms.

**Use:** elite replacement, alternating cultural emphasis and institutional fatigue.

**Limit:** cycles can become descriptive metaphors without a testable mechanism.

### 3. Structural-functional theory

Structural-functionalism views society as an interconnected system. It emphasises differentiation and adaptive adjustment: change in one institution produces responses elsewhere.

Example: migration may reduce co-residence while remittance, ritual and elder-support functions continue through a translocal family.

**Limit:** an equilibrium lens can understate coercion, domination and change produced by movements from below.

### 4. Conflict theory

Conflict theory locates change in contradictions among groups with unequal resources or authority. Marx’s class conflict is the classical model; caste, gender and resource conflicts require their own institutional analysis.

**Use:** struggles over land, labour, status, representation, knowledge and rights.

**Limit:** not every institutional adjustment is the direct product of open conflict.

### 5. Diffusion

Diffusion explains the spread of ideas, technologies or practices through contact, trade, migration, media, conquest or networks. Western education, print and legal ideas entered India through colonial contact, but were selectively interpreted rather than mechanically copied.

**Limit:** adoption is unequal; recipients reshape what travels.

### 6. Multilinear theory

Multilinear approaches reject one universal ladder. Societies may follow multiple pathways shaped by ecology, technology and institutional history. For India, colonialism, caste, region, movements, law, markets and local culture combine differently.

### Comparison: choose the mechanism, not the label

| Episode | Functional reading | Conflict reading | Diffusion/multilinear qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| decline of untouchability practices | adaptation to constitutional norms and social integration | oppressed-group mobilisation against entrenched power | law, education, migration and local politics vary by region |
| family nuclear residence | functional adaptation to urban work | gender/generational bargaining over autonomy and care | translocal support prevents a simple joint-to-nuclear sequence |
| spread of new consumption | adaptation to time and markets | corporate power and unequal health burden | global forms are localised by class, region and taste |

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Combining theories makes the answer unfalsifiable.

**Response:** Combination is disciplined when each theory explains a distinct causal link and evidence identifies which mechanism dominates.

**Residual:** Evidence may support rival interpretations. A good answer explains the disagreement instead of forcing false certainty.

### UPSC application

- Evolutionary language requires an anti-teleology qualification.
- Use structural-functionalism for adaptation and conflict theory for power struggle.
- Diffusion must include selective adoption.
- End with a multilinear verdict when pathways vary by region and group.

### Revision notes

1. Unilinear evolution assumes one sequence; multilinear change permits many pathways.
2. Direction does not establish progress.
3. Cyclical theory stresses recurrence and renewal.
4. Structural-functionalism links change to differentiation and adaptive adjustment.
5. Functionalism can understate power.
6. Conflict theory centres contradiction and struggle.
7. Marx is the classical class-conflict anchor.
8. Diffusion is spread, not identical copying.
9. Recipients reinterpret imported forms.
10. Theory choice must follow the observed mechanism.
11. One episode can contain adaptation, conflict and diffusion.
12. India requires regionally differentiated, multilinear analysis.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the same anti-caste change be explained differently by structural-functional and conflict theories?

**Model answer:** Functionalism may interpret it as institutional adaptation that reduces tension and aligns society with constitutional norms. Conflict theory emphasises mobilisation by subordinated groups against interests sustained by hierarchy. Evidence about actors, pressure and enforcement determines which explanation is stronger.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not call the theories identical merely because both describe the same outcome.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Compare structural-functional and conflict explanations of social change. Can they be used together in analysing India?

**Complete model:** Structural-functional theory treats society as an interdependent system. Change occurs when institutions differentiate or adapt to disruption, often producing a new equilibrium. This lens helps explain how family, education and administration reorganise while retaining essential functions.

Conflict theory instead asks whose interests an arrangement serves and how unequal groups struggle over resources, status and authority. Marx’s class framework supplies the classical formulation, but Indian analysis must also examine caste, gender and community power. Anti-caste reform, labour organisation or women’s movements cannot be reduced to smooth adjustment.

The theories may be combined only with causal discipline. Urban migration can functionally produce smaller households and new care arrangements, while conflict within the household redistributes unpaid work and decision-making. Constitutional reform may increase integration, but its implementation can still reflect struggle between entrenched and subordinated groups.

Their limits are opposite: functionalism may normalise inequality; conflict theory may understate cooperation and gradual adaptation. A strong Indian answer therefore identifies the dominant mechanism in each stage and ends with a multilinear verdict rather than a universal theory.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** functional theory 3; conflict theory 3; theoretical precision 2; integrated Indian examples 4; limitations 2; verdict 1.

The official NCERT chapter records Satish Saberwal’s three aspects of the modern framework of change in colonial India: **modes of communication, forms of organisation and the nature of ideas**. The triad is causal. Ideas without transmission remain isolated; communication without organisation may produce awareness but little sustained action; organisation without legitimate ideas may defend old power.

### 1. Communication

Print, telegraph, steamship and railways widened the speed and geography of debate. Today, broadcasting and digital networks can perform a similar connecting function, but speed does not guarantee truth or equal access.

### 2. Organisation

Reform associations, schools, movements, unions, political parties, self-help groups and bureaucracies convert diffuse dissatisfaction into coordinated action. Organisation supplies leadership, resources, memory and bargaining power.

### 3. Ideas

Equality, individual rights, scientific reasoning and reinterpretations of tradition supplied new vocabularies for criticism. Indian reform was not simply Western import: reformers combined liberal ideas with fresh readings of religious and ethical traditions.

### 4. Wider agents

| Agent | Change mechanism | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| State and law | alter formal rights, incentives and institutions | enactment may outrun practice |
| Education | changes aspiration, skill and legitimate knowledge | access and quality remain unequal |
| Markets and work | reshape occupations, consumption and mobility | can deepen insecurity and inequality |
| Migration and urbanisation | expose people to new roles and networks | may preserve translocal authority |
| Technology and media | accelerate diffusion and coordination | can amplify exclusion and misinformation |
| Social movements | challenge legitimacy and redistribute voice | representation may be uneven |
| Demographic change | alters care, generation and labour relations | effects vary by class and gender |

### Census 2027 example: an announced digital design

PIB describes Census 2027 as India’s first digital enumeration, with mobile-based data collection, optional self-enumeration, near real-time administrative monitoring through CMMS and geo-referenced jurisdictions. Sociologically, this illustrates changing **communication technology**, **state organisation** and the **idea of data-driven governance**.

It does **not** yet prove faster availability, higher accuracy, complete inclusion or equal digital access. Those are outcomes requiring later evidence.

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** Technology is now the main agent; associations and ideas are secondary.

**Response:** A device changes society only through institutions, rules, users and meanings. The same mobile system can expand access or create a new barrier.

**Residual:** Agents interact recursively. Movements change law; law changes organisations; media changes movements.

### UPSC application

- Use the Saberwal triad for colonial reform and contemporary communication-led change.
- Treat state, market and technology as agents, not automatic causes.
- Separate announced institutional design from measured social outcome.
- Ask who can communicate, organise and define legitimate ideas.

### Revision notes

1. Saberwal’s triad is communication, organisation and ideas.
2. Communication expands reach and speed.
3. Organisation converts awareness into sustained action.
4. Ideas provide legitimacy and direction.
5. Reformers combined Western liberalism with reinterpreted traditions.
6. Law changes rules but not automatically behaviour.
7. Education changes aspiration and legitimate knowledge.
8. Markets produce opportunity and insecurity.
9. Technology operates through institutions.
10. Movements redistribute voice and public meaning.
11. Census 2027 digital features are announced design, not proven outcome.
12. Agents influence one another through feedback.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a communication technology insufficient by itself to explain social change?

**Model answer:** Technology expands possible contact, but organisation determines collective capacity and ideas determine meaning and legitimacy. Access, rules and power shape who uses the technology and whether it changes institutions or merely accelerates existing inequality.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer social outcome directly from a technical feature or policy announcement.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain Satish Saberwal’s three-part framework for modern social change and assess its contemporary relevance.

**Complete model:** Satish Saberwal identifies three connected aspects of the modern framework of change: modes of communication, forms of organisation and the nature of ideas. In colonial India, print, telegraph, steamship and railways connected reformers across regions. Associations and schools converted debate into sustained action, while ideas of equality, liberty and rational criticism supplied new legitimacy.

The framework remains useful. Digital communication can spread information rapidly; movements, parties and community organisations aggregate voice; constitutional values and rights language frame claims. The PIB design for Census 2027—mobile collection, optional self-enumeration and digital monitoring—also shows communication technology interacting with state organisation and an evidence-oriented governing idea.

Yet the triad is not deterministic. Digital reach may be unequal, organisations may reproduce elite power, and ideas may be selectively interpreted. Nor does an announced digital design prove improved coverage or accuracy. Markets, law, demography and material interests also influence change.

Saberwal’s framework therefore works best as a mechanism map: communication widens connection, organisation builds collective capacity and ideas justify action. Contemporary analysis must add unequal access, regulation and feedback.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** exact triad 3; colonial mechanism 3; contemporary applications 3; Census qualification 2; limitations 2; conclusion 2.

## Lesson 4 — Sanskritisation: Mobility Within an Unequal Structure

Progress: 4 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: M.N. Srinivas, regional variation, de-Sanskritisation and NCERT criticisms

CA search: Not required for this established sociological concept.
CA found: None used.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: position may move while structure remains

```text
GROUP SEEKS HIGHER LOCAL STATUS
              ↓
adopts customs · rituals · beliefs · ideology · lifestyle
of a high / often twice-born caste
              ↓
CLAIM TO RECOGNITION
              ↓
possible POSITIONAL mobility
              ≠
abolition of caste hierarchy
```

M.N. Srinivas coined **Sanskritisation** for the process by which a “low” caste, tribe or other group adopts the customs, ritual, beliefs, ideology and style of life of a high, especially twice-born, caste. It concerns a status claim within a local hierarchy.

### 1. What the concept explains

- ritual and lifestyle emulation;
- relation between improved economic/political position and status claim;
- local dominance as a cultural reference;
- language, music, ritual and everyday-style changes;
- the difference between group aspiration and legal equality.

Srinivas noted regional variation. Where Sanskritised castes were dominant, wider regional influence could follow. Where non-Sanskritic groups dominated, **de-Sanskritisation** could occur. Persian and other regional influences also prevent a uniform India-wide process.

### 2. Necessary conditions and limits

Sanskritisation may follow new land, education, political power, migration income or group self-consciousness. These resources make a claim possible; they do not guarantee acceptance. Recognition depends on local power and other groups.

### 3. The five NCERT criticisms

1. **Exaggerated mobility:** it may produce positional change for some without structural change; inequality persists.
2. **Upper-caste superiority:** the concept can naturalise the upper-caste way of life as superior and lower-caste practice as inferior.
3. **Hierarchy legitimation:** it may appear to justify purity-pollution and an unequal, exclusionary model.
4. **Gendered regression:** adoption of upper-caste practices can include seclusion, dowry and tighter control over women.
5. **Erosion of Dalit and labouring cultures:** artisanal knowledge, labour dignity, medicine, ecology and agricultural knowledge may be devalued.

Anti-Brahmin movements, regional self-consciousness and Dalit assertion reveal alternative mobility through political organisation, education and rejection of Brahmanical status standards.

### Example and boundary

A group adopts vegetarianism, a new marriage ritual and a higher-caste style of worship after gaining local economic power. This may support a status claim. It does not prove equal access to land, education, housing or intermarriage.

### Strongest defence and reply

**Defence:** Sanskritisation is descriptive, not an endorsement. It captures an empirically visible route of aspiration.

**Reply:** Description still needs a power and gender critique. Without it, the concept overstates mobility and hides the cost of treating dominant culture as the standard.

**Residual:** The concept remains valuable for some local status processes but cannot serve as a general theory of Indian modernisation.

### UPSC application

- Define it as status emulation, not structural equality.
- Name positional versus structural change.
- Include regional variation and de-Sanskritisation.
- Present all five criticisms, especially gender and cultural erasure.
- Compare with Westernisation and modernisation only after explaining it independently.

### Revision notes

1. M.N. Srinivas coined Sanskritisation.
2. The adopting group may be a caste, tribe or other group.
3. Customs, ritual, beliefs, ideology and lifestyle can be copied.
4. The reference is a high, often twice-born, caste.
5. Economic or political improvement may precede the claim.
6. Acceptance is locally negotiated.
7. Position can change without structure changing.
8. The concept may reproduce an upper-caste norm.
9. Purity-pollution hierarchy may be legitimated.
10. Effects can be regressive for women.
11. Dalit, artisanal and labouring knowledge may be devalued.
12. De-Sanskritisation and regional paths qualify uniformity.
13. Political assertion can reject rather than imitate hierarchy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is Sanskritisation not equivalent to caste abolition?

**Model answer:** Sanskritisation seeks improved position by adopting the practices of a higher caste. It normally accepts the hierarchy’s status language rather than removing inherited ranking, endogamy or unequal power. Positional mobility may occur while the structure persists.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat any ritual change as proof of material or structural equality.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine Sanskritisation as an explanation of social mobility in India.

**Complete model:** M.N. Srinivas defined Sanskritisation as the adoption by a lower caste, tribe or other group of the customs, rituals, beliefs, ideology and lifestyle of a high, especially twice-born, caste. It explains how economic or political improvement and group self-consciousness can generate a local claim to higher status.

Its value lies in identifying a real cultural route of mobility and its regional variation. Dominant groups can shape the reference culture; non-Sanskritic dominance may also produce de-Sanskritisation. Acceptance is negotiated, not automatic.

The official NCERT critique is decisive. Sanskritisation can exaggerate mobility because it often changes position, not structure. It treats upper-caste culture as superior, may legitimise purity-pollution, can encourage seclusion and dowry, and can erode Dalit, artisanal and labouring knowledge. It also underplays political assertion, law, education and anti-caste movements that reject hierarchy rather than imitate it.

The defence is that the concept is descriptive, not prescriptive. Even so, description must include power, gender and material outcomes. Sanskritisation remains a bounded account of ritual-status mobility, not a comprehensive theory of equality or modernisation.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** definition/mechanism 3; conditions/variation 2; five criticisms 5; defence 2; alternative mobility 2; verdict 1.

```text

         |
   ------+------------------------------
   |             |          |          |
TECHNOLOGY   INSTITUTIONS   IDEAS    LIFESTYLE

         \_______|___________|__________/
```

M.N. Srinivas defined Westernisation as the changes brought about in Indian society and culture through more than 150 years of British rule, including changes in **technology, institutions, ideology and values**.

### 1. Wider than imitation

Westernisation includes:

- modern transport and communication;
- English education and new professions;
- legal and administrative institutions;
- liberal, egalitarian and individual-rights ideas;
- art, literature, food, dress and domestic forms.

A minority subculture of early Western-educated intellectuals supported reform. Wider social groups later adopted selected technologies and styles.

### 2. External form is not modern value

NCERT’s crucial qualification is that Westernisation can involve external forms without democracy or equality. Western clothes, interiors or education can coexist with communal prejudice, patriarchal authority or discriminatory use of modern technology.

```text
western form ≠ egalitarian value
western education ≠ absence of prejudice
modern technology ≠ modern social relation
```

### 3. Not limited to upper castes

Srinivas suggested a broad pattern in which lower castes Sanskritised while upper castes Westernised. Regional studies make that generalisation difficult: non-upper-caste groups also adopted Western education and cosmopolitan practices as routes to mobility and critique.

### 4. Colonial paradox

Westernisation brought institutions and liberal ideas through colonial domination. The same educated middle class could draw on Enlightenment thought, oppose colonial rule and revalue Indian traditions. Thus external contact generated both imitation and cultural self-assertion.

### Comparison

| Sanskritisation | Westernisation |
|---|---|
| local status emulation | change through Western/colonial contact |
| often within caste hierarchy | can introduce equality and rights critiques |
| ritual/lifestyle emphasis | technology, institutions, values and lifestyle |
| dominant caste as reference | heterogeneous Western institutions and ideas |

### Objection, response and residual

**Objection:** The concept treats every contemporary change as Western influence.

**Response:** Establish a historical or institutional contact mechanism. Indigenous reform, market change and technological innovation cannot automatically be called Westernisation.

**Residual:** “Western” is internally diverse. The concept must not homogenise societies or assume exclusive origin.

### UPSC application

- Use Srinivas’s full technology-institution-ideology-value definition.
- Show the colonial paradox.
- Separate external form from modern egalitarian value.
- Avoid the upper-caste-only generalisation.
- Explain selective adaptation, not passive copying.

2. It concerns changes linked to prolonged British rule.
3. It includes technology, institutions, ideology and values.
4. Lifestyle imitation is only one layer.
5. Western form does not guarantee democratic values.
6. Education can coexist with prejudice.
7. Modern technology can serve discriminatory ends.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a practice be Westernised without being modern in the normative sense?

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Westernisation is neither mere lifestyle imitation nor a guarantee of modern values. Explain.

Modernisation refers to structural and cultural changes associated with rational-legal organisation, institutional differentiation, science, technology, occupational specialisation, achievement and expanded individual choice. The NCERT chapter, drawing on Rudolph and Rudolph, presents modernity as a shift toward universal commitments, calculation, science, chosen association and achieved identity.

### 1. Modernisation is not Westernisation

Westernisation identifies historical contact with Western institutions and culture. Modernisation identifies an institutional logic. A society can adopt modern administration or technology through locally generated reform and can Westernise in lifestyle without adopting equality or rational-legal conduct.

### 2. Modernisation is not a total condition

The same actor can be modern in employment and traditional in marriage, scientific in work and devotional in personal life. Institutions change unevenly.

### 3. Yogendra Singh — bounded use

Yogendra Singh’s idea of the **modernisation of Indian tradition** is best used as a bounded lens: modern institutions do not simply destroy tradition; they select, reinterpret and reorganise it. Tradition can supply symbols and legitimacy while law, education, bureaucracy, communication and markets alter its operation.

Do not convert this into a claim that every tradition automatically becomes democratic or that India has one harmonious path. Power, region, caste, class and gender shape whose tradition is retained and whose is marginalised.

### 4. Institutional mechanisms

- education converts birth-fixed expectations into credential competition, but unequal schooling persists;
- bureaucracy creates impersonal rules, but informal networks remain;
- urban work separates residence, kinship and occupation, but translocal family authority can continue;
- law changes legitimate norms, but behaviour may lag;
- technology expands coordination, but access and control are unequal.

**Criticism:** Modernisation theory is Western teleology disguised as sociology.

**Reply:** The criticism defeats unilinear versions, not every analysis of differentiation, rationalisation or technology. A multilinear account can study these mechanisms without prescribing one civilisation’s endpoint.

**Residual:** Modern institutions may reproduce old hierarchy through new credentials, platforms or markets.

Education and salaried work may alter occupation and aspiration while kinship continues to organise marriage and care. Robert Redfield distinguished Great and Little Traditions; McKim Marriott developed the framework for Indian village studies through **universalisation** and **parochialisation**.

### 1. Great Tradition

The Great Tradition refers to wider, textual, reflective and often institutionally organised cultural forms carried by scholars, religious centres, pilgrimage networks and canonical narratives.

### 2. Little Tradition

The Little Tradition consists of local, oral, folk and community practices embedded in everyday life.

### 3. Universalisation

A local deity, festival, story or practice gains pan-regional recognition through pilgrimage, textual incorporation, media, patronage or institutional networks.

### 4. Parochialisation

A pan-Indian form is translated into local language, simplified, combined with regional symbols or adapted to local resources and social structure.

### 5. Why the framework matters

It rejects the idea that elite culture only descends intact. Local society is creative. Repeated two-way exchange produces layered cultural forms.

### Criticisms

- Great/Little can oversimplify multiple traditions into a binary.
- “Great” may sound superior rather than wider in scale.
- Power, caste and gender affect whose local practice is universalised.
- Markets, media and the state influence cultural scale.
- Cultural borrowing does not prove social equality.

### Example and limit

A village deity linked to a pan-Indian deity through pilgrimage illustrates universalisation. A pan-Indian festival performed through regional language, food and local narrative illustrates parochialisation. The example identifies cultural flow, not the complete history of the practice.

- Explain why the framework is not elite imposition.
- Add power and binary-limit qualifications.

**Model answer:** Universalisation shows a local practice moving upward into wider recognition. Local communities therefore do not merely receive elite culture; their symbols and practices can reshape the wider tradition.

**Complete model:** Robert Redfield distinguished a wider textual and reflective Great Tradition from local, oral and everyday Little Traditions. McKim Marriott used universalisation and parochialisation to show their interaction in India.

Parochialisation occurs when a pan-Indian form is translated, simplified or recombined with local language, resources and symbols. Used carefully, the framework reveals Indian culture as layered exchange rather than uniform diffusion or sealed local isolation.

**Custom** is a socially transmitted practice supported by expectation and repetition. **Obscurantism** is the defence of belief or authority in ways that suppress inquiry, evidence or reasoned criticism. Customs may contain practical knowledge and meaning; they become obscurantist when authority blocks questioning, equal agency or evidence-based revision.

### 1. The NCERT reform lesson

Nineteenth-century reform occurred through debate, print, associations and reinterpretation. Reformers did not rely only on external denunciation: they used new liberal ideas and fresh readings of inherited texts. Opponents also organised and contested who could interpret tradition.

### 2. A four-part test

1. **Epistemic:** Can evidence and criticism be heard?
2. **Agency:** Can affected members consent, dissent or exit?
3. **Equality:** Does the practice impose status or gender hierarchy?
4. **Revisability:** Can interpretation change when harm is shown?

### 3. Avoid two extremes

- “All tradition is irrational” ignores knowledge, identity and internal reform.
- “All custom is culturally protected” can shield coercion and unequal authority.

Reformers combined liberal ideas with fresh readings of inherited texts, while women and subordinated groups increasingly challenged who could interpret custom. Change therefore came through internal dissent as well as external contact.

A defensible test asks four questions: can evidence be heard; can affected members consent or dissent; does the practice reproduce unequal status; and can interpretation change when harm is demonstrated? This avoids both extremes—calling every inherited practice irrational and treating community authority as immune from criticism.

The strongest objection is that “reason” can mask cultural domination. The reply is procedural rather than homogenising: require evidence, equal voice and revisability without prescribing one uniform way of life.

Reasoned reform preserves voluntary meaning while challenging hierarchy, harm and closed authority. Tradition remains living when it can be interpreted and contested.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** thesis/definitions 3; reform mechanism 3; four-part test 4; objection/reply 3; conclusion 2.

### 1. Six rules for synthesis

2. **Choose the mechanism:** adaptation, conflict, diffusion, organisation or technology.

4. **Locate level:** micro, meso or macro.
5. **Disaggregate outcome:** caste, class, gender, region and generation.

### 4. Strongest objections to modernisation theory

- Western experience is treated as universal.
- tradition and modernity are falsely polarised;
- colonial power is underplayed;
- social groups bear unequal costs;

Indian social change is **selective** because actors choose among practices; **uneven** because axes and regions move at different rates; **contested** because interests and identities conflict; **bidirectional** because local and wider traditions reshape one another; and **recursive** because outcomes alter future agents and institutions.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why must social mobility be distinguished from structural social change?

**Model answer:** Social mobility is movement by an individual or group within a stratification system; structural social change alters the rules, institutions or power relations that produce positions.

M.N. Srinivas’s Sanskritisation illustrates the distinction. A group may adopt dominant-caste practices and gain local recognition, yet endogamy, inherited ranking and unequal resources remain. Similarly, education may enable occupational mobility without removing caste networks in housing or marriage.

Structural change requires wider transformation: redistribution of authority, enforceable equality, altered institutional access or weakening of ascriptive closure. Mobility can contribute to that transformation by creating new organisations and expectations, but it does not prove it.

The distinction prevents exaggerated claims. A sound answer measures both changed position and changed structure, while asking whether gains are durable and shared.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both concepts, uses named evidence, explains the causal difference and gives a qualified relationship.

**Quantified rubric — 10 marks:** definitions 3; Sanskritisation example 2; structural tests 2; relationship/qualification 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Technology is an agent of social change only when mediated by organisation, ideas and power. Discuss.

**Model answer:** Technology creates affordances—speed, scale, storage, automation or new networks—but social outcome depends on institutions and users. Satish Saberwal’s triad clarifies the mediation: communication expands connection, organisation builds collective capacity and ideas give direction and legitimacy.

Print and railways aided reform because associations and equality-oriented ideas used them. Digital systems similarly may enable information, services and mobilisation, but unequal devices, literacy, language and institutional appeal can create exclusion. The PIB design for Census 2027 combines mobile enumeration, optional self-enumeration and administrative monitoring; these features describe a planned system, not proof of improved accuracy or inclusion.

Cryptocurrency supplies another example. Distributed-ledger affordances may support payment innovation and transnational networks, while speculation, fraud, energy cost and unequal financial literacy distribute risk unevenly. Regulation and platform power shape trust.

The strongest objection is technological determinism’s opposite: institutions alone matter. Yet technical design affects what institutions can do. The defensible verdict is relational—technology changes possibilities, while organisation, ideas and power select outcomes.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses a named framework, two contrasted technologies, evidence limits and a balanced causal verdict.

**Quantified rubric — 15 marks:** thesis and Saberwal 3; historical mechanism 3; digital/Census example 3; crypto example 3; objection 1; conclusion 2.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Construct an integrated sociological framework for analysing continuity and change in contemporary India.

**Model answer:** An integrated framework begins by specifying the object—an institution, relation, norm, identity or practice—and its micro, meso or macro level. It then selects causal lenses.

Evolutionary theory identifies long differentiation but requires an anti-teleology caution. Structural-functionalism explains adaptation; conflict theory reveals struggle over power; diffusion explains spread with reinterpretation; multilinear theory accommodates different regional paths. Saberwal’s communication–organisation–ideas triad maps agents.

India-specific concepts separate dimensions. Sanskritisation concerns status claims within hierarchy; Westernisation concerns contact-driven changes in technology, institutions and values; modernisation concerns structural rationalisation. Their unequal pace produces status inconsistency. Redfield and Marriott explain bidirectional Great–Little Tradition exchange, while secularisation identifies domain-specific institutional differentiation.

Continuity is actively reproduced through socialisation, interests and meaning, then reshaped by law, migration, education, media and markets. Cryptocurrency and fast food show the same logic: new infrastructures create possibilities, but class, gender, literacy, platforms and regulation distribute effects.

The framework must ask who gains, who bears cost, who can dissent and what feedback follows. Indian social change is selective, unequal and contested—not a single movement from tradition to modernity.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates macro theories, Indian concepts, agents, continuity mechanisms and contemporary applications within a clear evaluative method.

**Quantified rubric — 20 marks:** object/level 2; macro theories 5; Saberwal/agents 2; Indian processes 5; continuity and applications 3; power/feedback 2; verdict 1.

- Cyclical: rise, decline and renewal; demand a causal mechanism.
- Structural-functional: adaptation, differentiation and renegotiated equilibrium.
- Conflict: contradiction and struggle over power/resources.
- Diffusion: cross-boundary spread plus selective adoption.
- Multilinear: several ecological, historical and institutional pathways.
- Theory selection must follow evidence.

## Saberwal and agents

- Satish Saberwal: modes of communication + forms of organisation + nature of ideas.
- Agents: state/law, education, markets/work, migration/urbanisation, media/technology, movements, demography.
- Communication without organisation may not sustain action.
- Organisation without legitimate ideas may defend old power.
- Technology is mediated by access, rules and institutions.
- PIB Census 2027: announced mobile collection, optional self-enumeration, CMMS and geo-referencing; no outcome inference.

## Sanskritisation

- M.N. Srinivas: lower caste/tribe/group adopts customs, ritual, beliefs, ideology and lifestyle of a high, especially twice-born, caste.
- Resources and group self-consciousness may precede the claim.
- Recognition is local and negotiated.
- Regional variation and de-Sanskritisation matter.
- NCERT criticisms:
  1. positional, not structural, mobility;
  2. upper-caste superiority;
  3. legitimation of inequality/purity-pollution;
  4. seclusion, dowry and gender regression;
  5. erosion of Dalit, labouring and artisanal cultures.
- Alternative route: anti-caste assertion and rejection of hierarchy.

## Westernisation

- Srinivas: changes from prolonged British rule across technology, institutions, ideology and values.
- Wider than lifestyle.
- Western form does not ensure democratic or egalitarian value.
- Colonial modernity produced liberal critique and domination together.
- Non-upper-caste groups also Westernised.
- Adoption is selective and locally interpreted.

## Modernisation and Yogendra Singh

- Institutional differentiation, rational-legal rules, specialised roles, science, technology and choice.
- Not identical to Westernisation.
- Modern in one domain, traditional in another.
- Yogendra Singh: modernisation of tradition through selection, reinterpretation and reorganisation.
- Bounded use: do not claim automatic harmony, equality or one national path.
- Modern institutions can reproduce older hierarchy.

## Three-axis interaction

- Sanskritisation = status.
- Westernisation = contact-driven values/forms.
- Modernisation = structure.
- Axes can diverge.
- Status inconsistency: structural or value gain without equivalent recognition.
- No fixed sequence.

## Great and Little Traditions

- Robert Redfield: Great/Little distinction.
- McKim Marriott: universalisation/parochialisation in Indian village studies.
- Universalisation: local → wider/pan-Indian.
- Parochialisation: wider → local/adapted.
- Cultural flow is bidirectional.
- Critique: binary simplification, power and hierarchy.

## Secularisation

- Institutional differentiation of religious authority from other domains.
- Does not require loss of belief.
- Secularism = legal-political doctrine.
- Desacralisation = declining sacred meaning.
- Test by domain; visible religion does not settle the question.

## Continuity and change

- Continuity mechanisms: socialisation, institutional embedding, interest, meaning, adaptation.
- Change mechanisms: law, education, migration, work, media, market, movements.
- Persistence can occur through changed form.
- Evaluate consent, internal voice, burden and exit.

## Custom and reason

- Custom is socially transmitted practice.
- Obscurantism suppresses inquiry and reasoned criticism.
- Test evidence, consent, equality and revisability.
- Internal reform and dissent matter.
- Avoid “all tradition is irrational” and “all custom is immune.”

## Technology and cryptocurrency

- Affordance is possibility, not outcome.
- Cryptocurrency is a blockchain application, not blockchain itself.
- Private crypto differs from sovereign CBDC.
- Social channels: payments, networks, speculation, fraud, illicit finance, energy, trust and unequal literacy.
- Avoid unverified price, adoption or fresh regulatory claims.

## Fast food and consumption

- Drivers: urban time, commuting, dual-earner households, cold chains, franchising, platforms, advertising, aspiration.
- Indian menus localise global forms.
- Health awareness coexists with consumption because convenience, price and norms compete.
- Class, gender, children, workers and neighbourhoods experience different effects.
- Social-change analysis links time, household organisation, platforms, aspiration and localisation.
