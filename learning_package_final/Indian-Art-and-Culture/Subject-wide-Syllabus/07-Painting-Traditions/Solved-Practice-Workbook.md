## DIRECT SOLVED MAINS PYQS

### PYQ-M1. The 'Flute Playing Krishna' theme is very popular in Indian art. Discuss. (CSE, 2012)

**Model answer:**

The flute-playing Krishna theme became popular because it allowed painters to combine devotion, music, love, landscape and rasa in a single image. It travels across Rajput, Pahari, Pichhwai and other painted traditions, proving that Indian painting often spreads through shared themes but differentiated visual idioms.

First, Krishna as Venugopala or the flute-bearing divine lover fits Bhakti-era emotional culture. In Rajput and Pahari settings, the flute becomes the pivot of Radha-Krishna imagery, pastoral nature and longing. Second, the theme works especially well in miniature painting because lyrical line, costume detail and compressed landscape can all intensify mood. Third, in Pichhwai and devotional painted hangings, Krishna's image serves not only aesthetic delight but ritual viewing.

The popularity of the theme therefore comes from its multi-layered usefulness: it is sacred, musical, poetic and visually adaptable. A high-scoring answer should conclude that the theme's spread does not erase school differences; Kishangarh, Kangra and Nathdwara each handle Krishna differently through line, colour, audience and ritual function.

### PYQ-M2. While bringing out their salient features, distinguish between either 'Madhubani' Art and 'Manjusha' Art or 'Rajasthani' Schools of Painting and the 'Pahari' School of Painting. (CSE, 2011)

**Model answer - Route A: Madhubani and Manjusha**

Madhubani and Manjusha are both associated with Bihar, yet they differ in social base, layout and motif emphasis. Madhubani is strongly associated with women's ritual practice around Mithila and is marked by bold colour, dense filling, double borders and auspicious-deity imagery. Manjusha, linked to the Bhagalpur region, is especially recognisable through snake motifs and the Angika cultural setting.

Thus, Madhubani is best identified through domestic-ritual wall and paper practice with fertility and festival symbolism, whereas Manjusha stands out through its regional narrative-motif structure and recurring serpent imagery.

**Model answer - Route B: Rajasthani and Pahari**

Rajasthani and Pahari painting are related miniature worlds, but they are not identical. Rajasthani painting is better taught as a cluster of courts - Mewar, Bundi, Kota, Jaipur and Kishangarh - with bold colour, devotional themes, hunt scenes and strong regional variation. Pahari painting belongs to the Himalayan foothill courts and is best understood through internal sequence: Basohli's force, Guler's transition and Kangra's lyrical landscape and bhakti mood.

The major distinction lies in visual mood and landscape treatment. Rajput painting often projects courtly energy and stronger colour blocks, while Pahari painting, especially Kangra, heightens lyrical intimacy and softer natural surroundings. Both share Krishna and Ragamala themes, but their handling differs enough for close exam comparison.

### PYQ-M3. Write about the chief features of the Bundi School of Painting. (CSE, 1993)

**Model answer:**

The Bundi school is one of the most dynamic branches of the Rajput painting family. Its chief features include vigorous movement, rich colour, energetic treatment of courtly and hunting scenes, and a strong role for landscape as dramatic setting rather than passive background.

Bundi painters often animate forests, monsoon skies, animals and royal action with unusual vitality. This makes the school especially useful in comparison questions: unlike the inward lyricism of Kishangarh or Kangra, Bundi excels in kinetic narrative and environmental drama. It also shares the broader Rajput themes of devotion and court culture, yet retains a distinctive visual energy.

Hence, the Bundi school should be remembered as a regional Rajput style marked by motion, hunt imagery, vivid colour and expressive landscape integration.

### PYQ-M4. Write about the chief features of: (a) Kishangarh style of painting (b) Neo art movement of Rabindra Nath Tagore (c) Kangra School of Painting. (CSE, 1990)

**Model answer:**

**(a) Kishangarh style of painting:** Kishangarh is famous for lyrical elongation, refined profile, idealised Radha-Krishna imagery and the iconic Bani Thani association. The line is elegant, the mood devotional-romantic and the pictorial world highly stylised rather than naturalistically descriptive.

**(b) Neo art movement of Rabindranath Tagore:** Rabindranath's modern artistic direction belongs to the wider twentieth-century attempt to rethink Indian visual language beyond colonial academic realism. It privileges inward experimentation, personal imagination and freedom from strict academic finish, and it should be read alongside the wider Santiniketan climate of artistic reorientation.

**(c) Kangra School of Painting:** Kangra represents the lyrical peak of the Pahari tradition. It is marked by delicate landscape, emotional refinement and a strong association with Krishna-bhakti, love poetry and natural beauty. Compared with Basohli's intensity, Kangra appears softer and more atmospheric.

### PYQ-M5. Write a note on the origin and development of Oriental School of Art. (CSE, 1986)

**Model answer:**

The Oriental or Bengal-centred school of modern Indian art arose in reaction to dominant European academic conventions in colonial India. Its origin lies in the effort to recover a culturally rooted pictorial language through selective engagement with Indian and Asian sources rather than through simple imitation of western oil-naturalism.

Its development is associated with Abanindranath Tagore, the symbolic force of Bharat Mata, the wash effect and the larger pedagogic-cultural climate that later widened through Santiniketan. The movement matters not merely as a style school but as an aesthetic programme linked to national self-definition.

A balanced answer should add that this was not the only modern path. Later artists and groups, including post-independence modernists, took different routes, so the Oriental School marks a major beginning, not the final destination of modern Indian painting.

### PYQ-M6. What are the basic differences between Mughal Style of Painting and Rajput Style of Painting? (CSE, 1985)

**Model answer:**

Mughal and Rajput painting differ first in patronage and visual purpose. Mughal painting develops in an imperial atelier under rulers such as Akbar and Jahangir and therefore gives strong place to court chronicle, portraiture, flora-fauna study and the album page. Rajput painting grows across multiple regional courts and is comparatively more saturated with devotional, literary, musical and heroic themes.

Second, Mughal painting tends toward greater observational naturalism, individual likeness and controlled detailing, especially under Jahangir. Rajput painting is more openly stylised and regionally varied: Bundi and Kota emphasise movement and hunt scenes, Kishangarh idealises devotional romance and Mewar retains bold narrative energy.

Third, the viewing format differs. Mughal manuscript and album culture creates an intimate courtly-connoisseurial world, whereas Rajput painting, though also miniature-based, often channels local court identity, bhakti feeling and regional thematic preference more strongly. Therefore the safest contrast is imperial-naturalist-atelier versus regional-devotional-variant, without denying shared materials or occasional interaction.

## ORIGINAL MAINS ANSWERS

### Original Q1 (10 marks, 150 words)

**Question:** Explain why Ajanta and Bagh should be read together in UPSC preparation, but not treated as identical mural traditions.

**Model answer:**

Ajanta and Bagh should be studied together because they form the safest remembered pair for Gupta-era mural excellence in India. Both preserve painting on prepared cave surfaces, both help teach narrative and devotional wall painting, and both appear in objective routes that test site identification.

However, they should not be treated as identical. Ajanta is a multi-phase Buddhist mural complex with major Jataka, Bodhisattva and courtly scenes; Bagh is remembered as the other surviving Gupta-era mural anchor and is often described as more forceful in outline and more earthly in human presence. The Hallisalasya route also gives Bagh a separate exam identity.

Thus, the correct method is comparative reading: pair them for chronology and mural significance, then distinguish them by visual mood, site logic and source-supported evidence limits.

### Original Q2 (10 marks, 150 words)

**Question:** Distinguish mural, manuscript and album as three different viewing formats in Indian painting.

**Model answer:**

Mural, manuscript and album are not just media labels; they create different viewing habits. A mural is fixed to an architectural surface and is usually experienced collectively in a cave, temple or built interior. This makes scale, spatial integration and narrative spread especially important.

A manuscript painting is portable, small and often tied to monastic, scholastic or courtly reading cultures. Palm leaf and paper formats compress space and sharpen contour. An album folio is even more selective: it is curated for elite private viewing, portrait appreciation or connoisseurial study rather than for continuous narrative reading.

Therefore, the same theme changes when its support changes. Indian painting history becomes clearer when one tracks not only subjects and schools but also the movement from wall to folio to album.

### Original Q3 (15 marks, 250 words)

**Question:** Discuss the Mughal atelier as an institution and show how Akbar and Jahangir gave it different artistic priorities.

**Model answer:**

The Mughal atelier was not merely a place where pictures were made; it was an institution through which imperial power organised knowledge, taste and visual record. Its formation began with Humayun's Persian connection, especially through Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad, and reached administrative maturity under Akbar.

Akbar's priority was scale, collaboration and narrative energy. Through the Tasvir Khana, he salaried artists, encouraged teamwork and commissioned major illustrated manuscripts such as Tutinama, Hamzanama, Razmnama and Akbarnama. Painting thus entered the imperial system as a workshop activity tied to chronicle, translation and dynastic imagination.

Jahangir did not abolish this inheritance; he refined it. His priority shifted toward album culture, individual portraiture, flora-fauna studies and allegorical refinement. Named masters such as Ustad Mansur and Abu'l Hasan embody this new emphasis on close observation and connoisseurial finish.

Hence Akbar and Jahangir represent two linked but distinct phases of the same institution: Akbar built the expansive narrative atelier, while Jahangir deepened the intimate, selective and observational capacities of Mughal painting.

### Original Q4 (15 marks, 250 words)

**Question:** Compare Rajasthani and Pahari painting as related yet distinct regional miniature traditions.

**Model answer:**

Rajasthani and Pahari painting are related because both emerge within the wider post-Mughal regional miniature world, share portable formats and often revisit Krishna, Ragamala and courtly themes. Yet they are distinct enough that a strong UPSC answer must compare them on patronage, mood, line and landscape.

Rajasthani painting should be understood as a family of courts such as Mewar, Bundi, Kota, Jaipur and Kishangarh. It often displays bold colour, strong narrative energy, court-hunt subjects and marked sub-school variation. Kishangarh, through Bani Thani and the Nihal Chand-Sawant Singh association, is especially lyrical and stylised.

Pahari painting belongs to Himalayan foothill courts. Its safest internal teaching order is Basohli to Guler to Kangra. Basohli carries intensity and boldness, Guler marks transition, and Kangra becomes celebrated for lyrical landscapes, bhakti mood and emotional softness.

Thus, the key contrast is not religious theme - both may depict Krishna - but the handling of line, atmosphere and court ecology. Rajasthani is broader and more internally divergent as a Rajasthan court family; Pahari is more hill-court based and often more landscape-sensitive in its mature forms.

### Original Q5 (20 marks, 250 words)

**Question:** Evaluate how colonial patronage and modern nationalism changed the social life of painting in India.

**Model answer:**

Colonial and modern phases transformed painting in India not only through style but through the circulation of images, the identity of patrons and the expansion of audiences. The major change is that painting moved beyond cave interiors, temples and royal ateliers into markets, studios, institutions and print publics.

Company painting is the first major sign of this shift. Indian artists worked for European patrons and adapted to documentary taste, altered perspective and new subject demands. Kalighat and bazaar painting took the image into urban commodity circuits, making painting more immediate, portable and market-facing.

Raja Ravi Varma enlarged this public sphere further. By combining mythological themes with oil technique and print reproduction, he helped produce a mass visual culture in which epic and Puranic figures entered ordinary domestic viewing spaces.

The Bengal School then responded critically to colonial academic norms. Through Abanindranath Tagore, Bharat Mata and related circles, style itself became a cultural-national argument. Santiniketan expanded experimentation, while post-1947 groups such as the Progressive Artists Group reopened modern Indian art to broader modernist vocabularies.

Hence the deepest change was social: painting acquired new patrons, new institutions and new publics, while also becoming a site where India argued about aesthetics, identity and modernity.

### Original Q6 (20 marks, 250 words)

**Question:** "Folk and tribal painting traditions are not peripheral to Indian art history; they change the centre of the story." Discuss.

**Model answer:**

Folk and tribal painting traditions challenge the old habit of writing Indian art history only through courts, monasteries and high-style ateliers. They shift attention to community practice, ritual use, ecology and transmission, thereby changing both the evidence base and the meaning of artistic continuity.

Madhubani, for example, is linked to Mithila's ritual and domestic sphere and is strongly associated with women's practice. Pattachitra shows how painted cloth and palm leaf can remain embedded in devotional craft worlds. Kalamkari demonstrates the intimate connection between painting and textile preparation. Warli turns wall painting into a geometric ritual language, while Gond painting encodes the sacred-natural world through patterning and line.

These traditions also alter the idea of patronage. Instead of imperial workshops alone, we encounter households, communities, shrines, itinerant performers and later market intermediaries. They therefore reveal that Indian painting history is not a closed chain running only from Ajanta to Mughal to modern gallery art.

A balanced answer should add that these traditions are living and changing. Paper, tourism, institutional display and contemporary markets reshape them, but that does not make them less authentic. Rather, it proves that continuity in Indian art has always worked through adaptation.
