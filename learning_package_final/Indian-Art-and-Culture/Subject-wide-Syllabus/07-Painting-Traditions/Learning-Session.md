# Painting Traditions - Complete Learning Session

> Read this topic through surface, scale, patronage and audience: a cave wall, palm-leaf manuscript, imperial album, bazaar sheet and ritual wall painting are different viewing worlds, so they must not be collapsed into one vague story of "Indian painting".

## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### LEARNING ROUTE

1. Begin with how to read a painting historically before memorising schools.
2. Fix technical vocabulary: mural, fresco secco, tempera, miniature, manuscript and album.
3. Move chronologically from prehistoric rock art to Ajanta, Bagh and South Indian murals.
4. Then shift to portable painting: Pala, Jain / Western Indian, Sultanate transition, Mughal, Deccani, Rajasthani and Pahari schools.
5. End with colonial markets, Bengal School, Santiniketan, Progressive art and living folk-tribal continuities.

### ORIGIN AND DEVELOPMENT TIMELINE

- Prehistoric phase: rock shelters such as Bhimbetka and Lakhudiyar preserve painted surfaces linked to hunting, dancing and collective memory.
- Early historic to Gupta-era mural line: Ajanta develops across more than one phase; Bagh provides the other classic Gupta-period mural survival in exam memory.
- Early medieval South: Sittanavasal, Pallava sites, Armamalai and Chola murals show that mural painting also had strong southern trajectories, even if survival is patchy.
- Manuscript era: Pala Buddhist and Western Indian / Jain illustration shift painting from fixed walls to portable leaves and paper.
- Sultanate transition: Persianate contact and manuscript culture prepare the ground for later Mughal, Deccani and regional ateliers.
- Mughal era: Babur and Humayun connect India to Persian masters; Akbar institutionalises the atelier; Jahangir refines albums and natural history; later dispersal nourishes regional schools.
- Regional miniature flowering: Deccani, Rajasthani and Pahari courts adapt shared resources differently rather than forming sealed, isolated traditions.
- Colonial and modern turns: Company, Kalighat and bazaar painting address officials and markets; Ravi Varma uses oil and print; Bengal School and Santiniketan turn technique into cultural argument; Progressive artists reopen modern idioms after 1947.
- Contemporary continuity: Madhubani, Pattachitra, Kalamkari, Warli, Gond and related traditions show that painting remained embedded in community ritual, craft ecology and new markets.
- PYQ return points: Bagh (2009), mural-site discrimination (2013), Padmapani at Ajanta (2017), Bani Thani (2018), Jahangir album shift (2019) and Hallisalasya at Bagh (2026).

### SESSION 1 - FOUNDATION - How to read a painting historically

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> A painting is first a historical surface with a patron, material and audience; style comes after that, not before.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
PAINTING-READING CHAIN
surface / support
-> scale
-> technique / pigment
-> patron / workshop
-> audience / function
-> theme / iconography
-> stylistic comparison
-> explicit evidence limit
```

*This sequence prevents decorative description from replacing analysis.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **surface**
- **technique**
- **patronage**
- **audience**
- **workshop**
- **evidence limit**

#### CORE TEACHING

Painting traditions change not only in line and colour but also in viewing conditions. Rock art is tied to shelter walls and collective activity; Ajanta murals belong to monastic and courtly interiors; palm-leaf manuscripts serve monastic and scholastic use; Mughal album pages address elite connoisseurship; Kalighat sheets and prints enter the open market.

This is why UPSC answers improve when you classify paintings by format, function and patronage before naming the school. The same Krishna theme appears very differently in a mural, a Kishangarh miniature, a Pichhwai hanging and a bazaar chromolithograph because the intended viewer, scale and ritual setting are different.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Indian painting developed through changing visual formats rather than through a single linear style.
- **Evidence:** Bhimbetka is a rock shelter surface, Ajanta a mural interior, Pala art an illustrated manuscript, Mughal art an imperial atelier product and Kalighat a market-facing urban sheet.
- **Analysis:** So the history of painting is also a history of portability, patronage and audience. This explains why workshop practice, brush line and theme shift alongside political change.
- **Qualification / link:** Use this frame in Mains answers to avoid writing only a list of schools.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Bhimbetka and Lakhudiyar for fixed prehistoric painted surfaces.
- Ajanta and Bagh for mural interiors.
- Kalpasutra manuscripts for portable painted folios.
- Mughal muraqqa albums for elite viewing.
- Kalighat and Ravi Varma prints for market and public circulation.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not identify a school from theme alone; Krishna appears across many schools.
- Do not treat all paintings as court paintings; ritual and community forms remain crucial.

#### MAINS USE

Use the chain as an opening sentence before beginning chronology.

#### RECAP

- Support matters.
- Patronage matters.
- Audience changes style.
- Every answer needs one evidence limit.

### SESSION 2 - FOUNDATION - Principles, materials and techniques

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> In painting questions, technical vocabulary is not ornament; it is often the difference between a safe answer and a trap.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
KEY TERMS
Shadanga -> six classical limbs of painting
mural -> painting on wall / fixed architectural surface
miniature -> small-format painting, often manuscript or album based
fresco secco -> colour on dry plaster
true fresco -> colour on wet plaster
tempera -> pigment mixed with binder, often on prepared ground
album -> curated folio for private viewing
```

*Technique words are classification tools, not decorative jargon.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Shadanga**
- **mural**
- **miniature**
- **fresco secco**
- **tempera**
- **album**

#### CORE TEACHING

The classical six limbs of painting - Roopbheda, Pramana, Bhava, Lavanya Yojana, Sadrishya and Varnikabhanga - provide an indigenous conceptual vocabulary for form, proportion, expression, grace, resemblance and colour handling. Even when UPSC does not ask them directly, these terms sharpen descriptive answers.

Technique labels must be used carefully. The Ajanta murals are safer to describe as fresco secco or tempera on prepared dry plaster than as pure wet fresco. Likewise, "miniature" should signal small scale and portable format, not a single homogeneous school.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Technique is an evidentiary clue, not a side note.
- **Evidence:** Ajanta's dry-plaster execution differs from true fresco; palm-leaf manuscripts constrain scale and line; Tanjore painting uses a very different material language from Mughal paper miniatures.
- **Analysis:** Material and technique therefore shape figure type, detail density, durability and audience.
- **Qualification / link:** In Prelims, this helps eliminate close distractors; in Mains, it lets you move beyond vague praise.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Ajanta for prepared plaster and mineral pigments.
- Palm-leaf Pala manuscripts for elongated horizontal format.
- Tanjore for raised decorative surface and devotional display.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not call every mural a true fresco.
- Do not use "miniature" as a synonym for "Rajput painting".

#### MAINS USE

Use one technique term in each paragraph where relevant; it raises precision immediately.

#### RECAP

- Shadanga supplies vocabulary.
- Technique shapes style.
- Mural and miniature are formats before they are schools.

### SESSION 3 - FOUNDATION - Prehistoric rock art

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> The earliest Indian paintings are records of collective action and environment, not portraits of dynasties.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
ROCK-ART BASELINE
Bhimbetka / Lakhudiyar
-> hunting / dancing / animals / group scenes
-> mineral colours on rock surface
-> community memory, ritual and environment
-> no court atelier, no manuscript portability
```

*Rock art gives the earliest painted evidence, but interpretation must remain cautious.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Bhimbetka**
- **Lakhudiyar**
- **mineral colours**
- **group scenes**
- **environment**
- **caution**

#### CORE TEACHING

Prehistoric rock art introduces the longest duration in Indian painting. Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh and Lakhudiyar in Uttarakhand are remembered in UPSC because they preserve painted evidence of animals, hunting, dancing and collective action on shelter walls.

These scenes are invaluable for reconstructing environment and visual culture, but they do not license grand claims about doctrine or named kings. Their importance lies in showing that image-making preceded courts, manuscripts and classical treatises by a vast margin.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Rock art establishes painting in India as a social and spatial practice long before elite ateliers.
- **Evidence:** Bhimbetka and Lakhudiyar preserve animal and human scenes on shelter walls using mineral colours.
- **Analysis:** The paintings record movement, hunting and community life, showing that painting first emerged in inhabited landscapes rather than palace studios.
- **Qualification / link:** But the scenes should not be over-read into fully systematised theology or named ethnic identities.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Bhimbetka hunting and dancing imagery.
- Lakhudiyar as another key rock-art memory point.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Rock painting is not the same as petroglyph engraving.
- Prehistoric painted scenes cannot be assigned to classical schools.

#### MAINS USE

Use rock art as the chronological opening when asked about continuity in Indian painting.

#### RECAP

- Earliest layer: rock surface.
- Themes: life, motion, animals.
- Limit: context yes, dynastic certainty no.

### SESSION 4 - CORE - Ajanta murals

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Ajanta is not merely beautiful Buddhist art; it is India's great lesson in narrative mural painting, courtly refinement and technical restraint on a cave wall.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
AJANTA DOSSIER
2nd century BCE phase + 5th century CE flowering
-> Buddhist caves
-> Jataka / Buddha / Bodhisattva / court scenes
-> prepared plaster + earth/mineral pigments
-> fresco secco safer than true fresco
-> Vakataka-Harisena patronage in major later phase
```

*Ajanta is best remembered as multi-phase, narrative and technically careful.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Ajanta**
- **Jataka**
- **Bodhisattva**
- **Harisena**
- **fresco secco**
- **narrative mural**

#### CORE TEACHING

Ajanta developed in more than one phase, with an early phase going back to the late centuries BCE and a major flowering in the Vakataka context, especially under Harisena in the fifth century CE. This multi-phase chronology matters because Ajanta should not be described as a one-time artistic event.

Its importance lies in narrative range and emotional intelligence: Jataka stories, Buddha episodes, Bodhisattvas such as Padmapani, devotional imagery and courtly scenes coexist on carefully prepared cave surfaces. The work is admired for line, modelling and colour harmony, but a safe answer also notes that the technique is better treated as fresco secco or tempera on dry plaster rather than rigidly labelled true fresco.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Ajanta marks the mature integration of narrative, devotion and elite visual culture in mural form.
- **Evidence:** The caves preserve Jataka cycles, the celebrated Padmapani of Cave 1, refined courtly scenes and layered chronology extending from early to Vakataka phases.
- **Analysis:** This shows that mural painting at Ajanta was not merely decorative; it educated, moved and sacralised the interior space while also absorbing worldly detail.
- **Qualification / link:** Avoid reducing Ajanta to a single dynasty or to a generic wet-fresco formula.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Padmapani in Cave 1.
- Vajrapani and Manjushri as named Bodhisattva references in the source bank.
- Embassy/courtly scene associated in the source bank with Pulakeshin II-period diplomatic memory.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not say Ajanta is one chronological block.
- Do not equate Ajanta technique with pure true fresco.

#### MAINS USE

Ajanta is the best single example when asked to show how religion, narrative and worldly life coexist in Indian painting.

#### RECAP

- Multi-phase chronology.
- Buddhist setting, but not purely monastic austerity.
- Narrative wall painting with technical caution.

### SESSION 5 - CORE - Bagh murals and the Hallisalasya route

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Bagh is remembered not because it copies Ajanta, but because it proves that Gupta-period mural excellence also survived in a more earthy, strongly outlined register.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
BAGH DOSSIER
Dhar district, Madhya Pradesh
on the Baghni river
-> 5th-7th century CE range in source bank
-> "Ajanta of Central India"
-> stronger outline, more earthly human presence
-> Cave 4 = Rang Mahal
-> Hallisalasya cited in Bagh paintings generally
```

*Bagh must be linked to Ajanta, but never flattened into Ajanta's duplicate.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Bagh**
- **Baghni river**
- **Rang Mahal**
- **Gupta-period mural**
- **Hallisalasya**
- **qualification**

#### CORE TEACHING

Bagh lies in Dhar district of Madhya Pradesh on the Baghni river and is often called the "Ajanta of Central India". That shorthand is useful only if qualified: Bagh belongs to the same high mural universe, yet its figures and outlines are often described as more forceful and humanly immediate.

The source bank also preserves the Hallisalasya route. It states that the Bagh Cave paintings depict the Hallisalasya dance, a classical Indian dance form performed in royal courts, temples and ceremonial gatherings, and that in the Gupta period it was often depicted as a devotional or courtly dance in art and sculpture. The caution is critical: do not assign Hallisalasya to a modern named dance form, do not pin it to Cave 4 specifically and do not read beyond the source's "devotional or courtly" limit.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Bagh expands the Gupta-era mural field and sharpens exam discrimination.
- **Evidence:** It is the other classic Gupta-period cave-painting survival paired with Ajanta; the source bank also attaches Hallisalasya to the Bagh paintings generally and identifies Cave 4 as Rang Mahal.
- **Analysis:** So Bagh matters both as an art-historical site and as a question-setting trap: students must distinguish the secure site, the secure dance name and the insecure extra inferences.
- **Qualification / link:** In objective answers, state only what the source directly supports.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Bagh, Dhar district, on the Baghni river.
- Cave 4 as Rang Mahal.
- Hallisalasya as devotional or courtly in art and sculpture during the Gupta period.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not move Hallisalasya to a specific cave unless the source does so.
- Do not treat Bagh as Ellora or Nasik.

#### MAINS USE

Use Bagh to show regional variation within the Gupta-era mural horizon.

#### RECAP

- Paired with Ajanta, not identical to it.
- Stronger outline and more earthly register.
- Hallisalasya requires strict wording control.

### SESSION 6 - CORE - South Indian mural trajectories

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> The history of Indian mural painting does not end at Ajanta; it travels powerfully into Jain, Pallava, Chola and Vijayanagara-Nayaka spaces in the south.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
SOUTH INDIAN MURAL LINE
Sittanavasal
-> Pallava sites (Kanchipuram / Panamalai / Mamandur)
-> Armamalai bridge
-> Chola murals at Brihadisvara and related sites
-> Vijayanagara / Nayaka layers including Lepakshi
```

*Few survivals do not mean weak production; wall painting is especially vulnerable to damage and over-painting.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Sittanavasal**
- **Pallava**
- **Armamalai**
- **Brihadisvara**
- **Lepakshi**
- **survival bias**

#### CORE TEACHING

Sittanavasal is crucial for showing a southern Jain mural world, remembered especially for the lotus-pond imagery and the broader religious environment linked to Samavasarana themes in the source bank. The canonical material also preserves Pallava mural references at sites such as Kanchipuram, Panamalai and Mamandur, with Armamalai acting as a useful bridge between later Pallava and early Chola trajectories.

The Chola mural bank includes Brihadisvara, where the famous royal-ritual scene of Rajaraja I with Karuvur Devar is central. Later superimpositions and damaged survival make an important methodological point: the current wall is not always the original wall. Lepakshi then carries the story into the Vijayanagara-Nayaka world and also appears in the 2013 mural-site Prelims route.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Southern murals extend the history of painting through changing religious and political spaces.
- **Evidence:** Sittanavasal preserves Jain imagery; Pallava sites preserve early South mural evidence; Brihadisvara retains Chola mural memory; Lepakshi survives as a major later mural site.
- **Analysis:** Together these show continuity, adaptation and reworking rather than a single isolated golden age.
- **Qualification / link:** Because walls are reused, repainted and damaged, survival gaps must not be mistaken for production gaps.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Sittanavasal lotus-pond imagery.
- Pallava site bank: Kanchipuram, Panamalai, Mamandur.
- Brihadisvara: Rajaraja I with Karuvur Devar.
- Lepakshi mural tradition.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not move mural evidence to an unrelated site such as Sanchi.
- Do not use surviving quantity as proof of original output.

#### MAINS USE

This session is ideal when asked to de-centre north-west or Ajanta-only narratives.

#### RECAP

- South India has a long mural arc.
- Jain, Shaiva and courtly registers coexist.
- Survival bias is a must-write caution.

### SESSION 7 - CORE - Early manuscript painting: Pala and Western Indian / Jain lines

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> When painting moves from wall to manuscript, portability changes composition, scale, line and even the habits of seeing.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
EARLY MINIATURE / MANUSCRIPT SHIFT
wall mural
-> palm leaf format
-> illustrated manuscript culture
-> paper adoption in later phase
Pala -> Buddhist manuscript line
Apabhramsa / Western Indian -> Jain-dominant illustrated line
```

*Portable painting compresses space and sharpens linear emphasis.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Pala**
- **palm leaf**
- **Apabhramsa**
- **Jain manuscripts**
- **paper transition**
- **portable format**

#### CORE TEACHING

The Pala school flourished roughly between 750 and 1150 CE and is remembered for illustrated Buddhist manuscripts executed on palm leaf or vellum-like paper. Their compositions are relatively compact, often with subdued backgrounds, sinuous lines and single figures; the patronage context is strongly tied to Buddhist, especially Vajrayana, traditions.

The Western Indian / Apabhramsa line, associated with Gujarat and the Mewar region, became a major manuscript tradition between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries. Jain themes dominated early phases, later expanding into Vaishnava material. Red, yellow and ochre remain prominent, while the fish-shaped bulging eye, pointed nose and angular face became strong visual markers. Kalpasutra and Kalakacharya Katha are classic memory points.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Portable manuscript painting creates a new visual discipline in Indian art.
- **Evidence:** Pala works compress Buddhist imagery onto palm leaf, while Western Indian Jain manuscripts develop dense line, stylised figures and strong colour fields.
- **Analysis:** Limited surface area encourages compact design, sharper contour and iconographic economy; portability also expands circulation beyond the wall-bound monument.
- **Qualification / link:** Do not confuse all early miniatures with later Mughal naturalism.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Pala Buddhist manuscripts.
- Kalpasutra and Kalakacharya Katha in the Western Indian / Jain line.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Pala is not a Rajput school.
- Bulging fish-shaped eyes are not Mughal naturalist markers.

#### MAINS USE

Use this as the pivot point from mural to miniature history.

#### RECAP

- Portability changes composition.
- Pala and Jain lines are early but distinct.
- Palm leaf and paper matter as much as theme.

### SESSION 8 - CORE - Sultanate transition and Persian interaction

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Before the Mughals formalised a great atelier, manuscript illustration had already become a meeting ground of Persianate and indigenous habits.

```text
TRANSITIONAL FIELD
Persian contact
+ manuscript illustration
+ local idioms
-> Sultanate synthesis
-> Nimatnama at Mandu
-> pathways toward Mughal / Deccani / Rajput ateliers
```

*Transition does not mean formlessness; it means mixed resources before later court codification.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Sultanate**
- **Persianate**
- **indigenous synthesis**
- **Nimatnama**
- **Mandu**
- **transition**

#### CORE TEACHING

Painting during the Delhi Sultanate and related regional courts is best understood as a transition in which Persian elements met pre-existing Indian manuscript habits. The result was not a crude binary of foreign versus local, but a layered synthesis.

The Nimatnama of Mandu is the most cited example in the source bank because it demonstrates manuscript illustration, court taste and stylistic blending in a concrete object. This stage matters because it prepares the visual vocabulary later refined by Mughal, Deccani and regional courts.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Sultanate painting is a bridge, not a blank interval.
- **Evidence:** The Nimatnama shows Persianate manuscript conventions interacting with Indian figural and decorative tendencies in the Malwa world.
- **Analysis:** This hybridisation explains why later Indian miniature schools can share resources without losing distinct identities.
- **Qualification / link:** Avoid presenting Persian contact as total replacement of indigenous practice.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Nimatnama under Nasir Shah of Mandu.
- Lodi Khuladar as another named transitional style in the source bank.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not begin miniature history abruptly with Akbar.
- Do not reduce synthesis to communal binaries.

#### MAINS USE

A short Sultanate bridge paragraph makes Mughal emergence historically grounded.

#### RECAP

- Persian interaction predates Akbar's mature atelier.
- Mandu is a key transitional centre.
- Synthesis, not replacement, is the safe formula.

### SESSION 9 - CORE - Mughal painting from Babur to Akbar

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Mughal painting becomes historically decisive when Persian-trained masters meet imperial scale, Indian talent and Akbar's institutional workshop.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
MUGHAL FORMATION
Babur -> limited time, taste memory
Humayun -> exile in Persia -> Mir Sayyid Ali + Abdus Samad
Akbar -> Tasvir Khana -> collaborative manuscripts
Tutinama / Hamzanama / Razmnama / Akbarnama
```

*Akbar's atelier matters because it turns patronage into a system.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Humayun**
- **Mir Sayyid Ali**
- **Abdus Samad**
- **Tasvir Khana**
- **Hamzanama**
- **collaborative atelier**

#### CORE TEACHING

Babur admired visual culture but had little time to build a large Indian painting system. Humayun, after exile in Persia, returned with key painters such as Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad, bringing major Persianate discipline into the Indian court.

Akbar transformed this inheritance. By organising the Tasvir Khana, paying artists on salary and commissioning massive illustrated works such as Tutinama, Hamzanama, Razmnama and Akbarnama, he converted painting into an imperial knowledge industry. The style under Akbar becomes narrative, energetic and increasingly open to Indian artists and experiment, including foreshortening and crowded action scenes.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Akbar's greatest innovation was institutional, not merely aesthetic.
- **Evidence:** He created a salaried Tasvir Khana and commissioned large collaborative manuscripts after Humayun had already imported Persian masters.
- **Analysis:** This workshop system explains scale, diversity and the visible mixture of Persian training with Indian artistic participation.
- **Qualification / link:** So Mughal painting should be explained as an atelier process, not a single master's style.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad.
- Tutinama, Hamzanama, Razmnama, Akbarnama.
- Basawan and Daswanth as celebrated Indian painters in Akbar's orbit.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not attribute album-centred taste to Akbar; that shift is later.
- Do not forget Humayun's Persian bridge.

#### MAINS USE

This session supplies the best answer to "how did court patronage shape Mughal painting?"

#### RECAP

- Humayun imports painters.
- Akbar institutionalises production.
- Collaboration and narrative scale define the phase.

### SESSION 10 - CORE - Mughal refinement from Jahangir to Aurangzeb

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Jahangir does not merely continue Akbar; he redirects Mughal painting from crowded manuscript narration toward the album page, portrait and studied natural observation.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
MUGHAL REFINEMENT
Akbar -> large illustrated books
Jahangir -> album / portrait / flora-fauna / allegory
Shah Jahan -> court polish, stillness, richer finish
Aurangzeb -> reduced patronage -> dispersal of artists
```

*Later Mughal change is about viewing format as much as about style.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Jahangir**
- **album**
- **portraiture**
- **Ustad Mansur**
- **Abu'l Hasan**
- **artist dispersal**

#### CORE TEACHING

Jahangir marks the high point of refinement in the canonical source bank. Natural history studies, album folios, individual portraiture, decorated margins and allegorical compositions become more central. The names Ustad Mansur and Abu'l Hasan are crucial because they anchor this shift in identifiable workshop excellence.

Under Shah Jahan, painting acquires greater polish, brighter colour and ornamental richness, though some scholars and source summaries describe it as more still and courtly than Akbar's dynamism or Jahangir's observational curiosity. Aurangzeb's reduced patronage did not end painting in India; instead, artist mobility and dispersal helped regional courts flourish.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** The Jahangir phase is best understood as a shift in visual attention and viewing habit.
- **Evidence:** The source bank explicitly links Jahangir to albums, portraits, flora-fauna studies and famous masters such as Ustad Mansur.
- **Analysis:** This means Mughal painting becomes more intimate and selective; the viewer studies a folio rather than only following a long manuscript narrative.
- **Qualification / link:** Do not turn Aurangzeb into a simple death sentence for painting; dispersal is the more useful historical effect.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Jahangir embracing Shah Abbas image associated with Abu'l Hasan in the source bank.
- Ustad Mansur for natural history studies.
- Decorated album margins and muraqqa culture.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- The album and individual-portrait shift belongs to Jahangir, not Humayun or Akbar.
- Later Mughal decline is not the same as total artistic disappearance.

#### MAINS USE

A good answer can compare Akbar's narrative manuscript world with Jahangir's connoisseurial album world.

#### RECAP

- Jahangir = album and natural observation.
- Shah Jahan = polished splendour.
- Aurangzeb = reduced patronage, increased dispersal.

### SESSION 11 - CORE - Deccani painting and shared Persianate resources

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Deccani painting proves that shared Persianate inputs did not produce one uniform court style across India.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
DECCANI SIGNATURE
Persianate resource base
+ Deccan court culture
-> saturated colour
-> elongated figures
-> fantastic landscape
-> rich costume and atmosphere
-> distinct from Mughal naturalism
```

*Similarity of source is not sameness of result.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Deccani**
- **saturated colour**
- **elongated figures**
- **fantastic landscape**
- **court culture**
- **distinction from Mughal**

#### CORE TEACHING

The basic owner identifies the Deccani line through saturated colour, elongated figures, fantastic landscape and Persianate-Deccan court culture. This is enough to answer most UPSC questions safely without inventing an exhaustive monument-style chronology.

The essential analytical move is comparison. Mughal painting tends toward controlled imperial naturalism and observational detail; Deccani work often appears more atmospheric, lyrical and stylised. Shared Persianate inheritance therefore coexists with different regional tastes, materials and patronage cultures.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Deccani painting is a distinct regional transformation of shared cosmopolitan resources.
- **Evidence:** The source bank marks saturated colour, elongated figures and fantastic landscapes as Deccani identifiers.
- **Analysis:** These traits indicate that regional courts did not merely imitate Mughal models; they reworked Persianate and Indian elements into their own visual mood.
- **Qualification / link:** Avoid treating Deccani as simply "Mughal in the south".

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Deccani court culture as a Persianate-local synthesis.
- Contrast set: Mughal naturalism versus Deccani colour-field and elongated stylisation.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Shared Persian roots do not erase regional distinction.
- Deccani is not a synonym for Rajput.

#### MAINS USE

Use Deccani to challenge simplistic centre-periphery narratives.

#### RECAP

- Shared input, different output.
- Colour and figure-type matter.
- Regional courts are creative, not derivative.

### SESSION 12 - CORE - Rajasthani or Rajput schools

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Rajput painting is best read as a family of courts, not as one flat style label.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
RAJASTHANI FAMILY
Mewar -> bold court / devotional energy
Bundi / Kota -> dynamic hunt and landscape movement
Jaipur / Amber -> courtly refinement
Kishangarh -> lyrical elongation, Radha-Krishna world, Bani Thani
shared themes -> Krishna, Ragamala, court, hunt
```

*Sub-school attribution is the real exam skill here.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Mewar**
- **Bundi**
- **Kota**
- **Kishangarh**
- **Bani Thani**
- **regional variation**

#### CORE TEACHING

Rajasthani or Rajput painting is a cluster of related but distinct court traditions. Mewar retains bold colour and strong narrative force; Bundi and Kota are famous for energy, hunting scenes and landscape movement; Kishangarh is remembered for lyrical elongation and idealised Radha-Krishna imagery.

Bani Thani is the key Prelims anchor. The safe formula is not merely "Rajput painting" but "Kishangarh school, associated with Nihal Chand and the Sawant Singh milieu". This is a good example of why precise sub-school attribution matters more than broad labels.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Rajput painting gains richness through internal variation.
- **Evidence:** Bundi and Kota develop kinetic hunt scenes, while Kishangarh turns toward elongated lyrical devotion and Bani Thani.
- **Analysis:** So theme alone is insufficient; line, mood, landscape handling and court location all matter.
- **Qualification / link:** In Prelims, prefer the sub-school name when available.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Bundi and Kota hunt imagery.
- Kishangarh, Nihal Chand, Sawant Singh, Bani Thani.
- Ragamala and Krishna themes across Rajput settings.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Do not place Bani Thani in Kangra or Jaipur.
- Do not treat Bundi, Kota and Mewar as interchangeable.

#### MAINS USE

Rajasthani schools are excellent for demonstrating regionalisation after imperial dispersal.

#### RECAP

- Rajput is a family label.
- Kishangarh is a precise identity marker.
- Sub-school detail wins marks.

### SESSION 13 - CORE - Pahari schools: Basohli, Guler and Kangra

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Pahari painting turns miniature art toward lyrical landscape and bhakti intimacy, but its own internal sequence must still be distinguished carefully.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
PAHARI ARC
Basohli -> bold colour, expressive force
Guler -> softer naturalism, transition
Kangra -> lyrical Radha-Krishna, landscape sensitivity
wider hill network -> Jammu / Dogra / Kullu-Mandi variants
```

*Pahari is a region-cluster, not a single painterly formula.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Basohli**
- **Guler**
- **Kangra**
- **lyrical landscape**
- **bhakti**
- **transition**

#### CORE TEACHING

The Pahari schools of the Himalayan foothill courts are united by geography but differentiated by style. Basohli is remembered for bold colour, high emotional charge and strong drawing; Guler softens the visual field and helps mark transition; Kangra becomes the celebrated lyrical peak, especially for Radha-Krishna, love and nature-rich imagery.

This matters because students often treat "Pahari" as if it automatically means Kangra. The real analytical skill is to identify progression and variation within the hill-court network.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Pahari painting develops through internal stylistic movement rather than instant uniformity.
- **Evidence:** Basohli's boldness, Guler's transition and Kangra's gentle lyricism are separately named in the OCR source bank.
- **Analysis:** This sequence shows how patronage, devotional taste and aesthetic preference gradually reshape the miniature surface.
- **Qualification / link:** Distinguish Pahari from Rajput by mood, landscape handling and regional court ecology rather than by any crude ethnic formula.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Basohli, Guler and Kangra.
- Krishna-centred and Ragamala-linked imagery in hill settings.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Basohli is not Kishangarh.
- Pahari is not reducible to one court or one theme.

#### MAINS USE

Use Pahari in comparative questions with Rajasthani painting.

#### RECAP

- Bold -> transitional -> lyrical arc.
- Landscape matters strongly.
- Court ecology shapes style.

### SESSION 14 - CORE - Tanjore, Mysore and devotional painted surfaces

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Not every portable painting in India is a paper miniature; some traditions are devotional objects with relief, gold and display value built into the surface itself.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
DEVOTIONAL PAINTED OBJECTS
Tanjore -> wood support, gold, glass / gem-like embellishment
Mysore -> gesso, fine line, restrained elegance
Ganjifa -> painted playing-card tradition
```

*Here the painted surface often behaves like a sacred-display object, not just an illustrated page.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Tanjore**
- **Mysore**
- **gesso**
- **gold relief**
- **devotional display**
- **Ganjifa**

#### CORE TEACHING

Tanjore painting is identified by rich devotional imagery, bold central figures and a materially ornate surface, often with gold work and embellishment. Mysore painting shares a devotional environment but is visually more restrained, noted for fine lines and gesso-based detailing.

Both emerge from a larger South Indian court-temple world rather than from the Mughal manuscript logic. Ganjifa painting, meanwhile, shows that painting could also animate objects of play and circulation, widening the topic beyond murals and paper folios.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** South Indian painted traditions often merge pictorial and object value.
- **Evidence:** Tanjore uses a raised, gold-rich surface for devotional display, while Mysore develops delicate gesso-based finish.
- **Analysis:** Material treatment here is inseparable from religious use, domestic display and courtly craft patronage.
- **Qualification / link:** Do not force these traditions into the same classification box as Mughal or Pahari paper miniatures.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Tanjore gold-rich devotional surface.
- Mysore gesso work.
- Ganjifa as painted card tradition.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Gold-rich devotional panel is not a Mughal album folio.
- Gesso is a marker here, not a generic Indian painting term.

#### MAINS USE

This session helps explain how support and devotional use shape artistic language.

#### RECAP

- Tanjore and Mysore are related but not identical.
- Material texture is central.
- Painted objects can be ritual-display artefacts.

### SESSION 15 - CORE - Company, Kalighat, bazaar and Ravi Varma

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Colonial-era painting changes when officials, urban buyers and print consumers become major audiences alongside courts and temples.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
COLONIAL VISUAL TURN
Company painting -> documentation + hybrid taste
Kalighat / bazaar -> urban market sheets
Ravi Varma -> oil + print circulation + mythological public image
```

*The nineteenth century is about new publics as much as new styles.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Company painting**
- **open market**
- **Kalighat**
- **bazaar art**
- **Ravi Varma**
- **print circulation**

#### CORE TEACHING

Company painting emerges in the colonial encounter where Indian artists respond to European patrons, documentary demands and changing market conditions. The result is hybrid: local hands, new subjects, altered perspective and market-facing production.

Kalighat and bazaar painting push this urban market logic further. Raja Ravi Varma adds another turning point by combining mythological themes with oil technique and wide print circulation, thereby shaping a mass visual imagination beyond elite patrons. A safe answer notes change in audience, medium and distribution rather than writing only that "Western influence entered India".

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Colonial painting reorders the social life of images.
- **Evidence:** Company works address officials; Kalighat sheets circulate in urban temple-market environments; Ravi Varma's prints carry mythological figures into middle-class households.
- **Analysis:** Painting is no longer restricted to mural interiors or court albums; it now enters commodity, print and public culture.
- **Qualification / link:** This is change by adaptation and expansion, not simple cultural replacement.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Company paintings for colonial patrons.
- Kalighat sheets near the Kalighat temple and urban market.
- Ravi Varma's oil-and-print public image world.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Company painting is not synonymous with Bengal School.
- Nineteenth-century change is not only a technique story; it is also a market story.

#### MAINS USE

Use this session to connect art history with colonial urbanism and print culture.

#### RECAP

- New audience, new market, new circulation.
- Hybrid forms matter.
- Public visual culture expands sharply.

### SESSION 16 - CORE - Bengal School, Santiniketan and modern Indian directions

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Modern Indian painting is not one rebellion; it is a sequence of responses to colonial academic realism, nationalism and later global modernism.

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
MODERN TURN
Ravi Varma / academic-naturalist public image
-> Bengal School / wash / cultural-national response
-> Santiniketan experimentation
-> Progressive Artists Group after 1947
```

*Modernity in Indian painting is plural, argumentative and self-conscious.*

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Abanindranath Tagore**
- **Bharat Mata**
- **wash technique**
- **Santiniketan**
- **Nandalal Bose**
- **Progressive Artists Group**

#### CORE TEACHING

The Bengal School centred on Abanindranath Tagore and related circles turned technique into cultural politics. Bharat Mata became emblematic because it linked nationalist imagination with an alternative to dominant European academic norms. The school's wash effect, inward mood and selective recovery of Asian and Indian resources were central to this move.

Santiniketan widened the conversation through experiment, pedagogy and a broader relationship with folk, rural and pan-Asian energies. After 1947, the Progressive Artists Group reopened the field through a more international modern idiom. The safest line is that modern Indian painting develops through debate, not through a single victorious school.

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION - CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> QUALIFICATION / LINK

- **Claim:** Twentieth-century Indian painting uses style itself as argument.
- **Evidence:** Abanindranath's Bharat Mata becomes a nationalist image; Santiniketan artists such as Nandalal Bose rework pedagogy and visual sources; the Progressive Artists Group adopts a different modern language after independence.
- **Analysis:** Painting is now a conscious site of cultural self-definition, not merely patron-driven decoration.
- **Qualification / link:** Avoid reducing Bengal School to a simple anti-West slogan; it is a selective and strategic aesthetic response.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE / EXAMPLES

- Abanindranath Tagore and Bharat Mata.
- Nandalal Bose and Santiniketan.
- Progressive Artists Group founders named in the OCR source bank.

#### PRELIMS TRAPS

- Bharat Mata belongs to the Bengal School orbit, not to Ravi Varma.
- Modern Indian art is not exhausted by one nationalist school.

#### MAINS USE

Ideal for answers on art and nationalism or on continuity and change in modern visual culture.

#### RECAP

- Bengal School = cultural response.
- Santiniketan = experimentation.
- Progressive group = post-1947 reopening of modernism.

## ADVANCED ENRICHMENT - USE ONLY AFTER THE CORE IS SECURE

### 1. Survival bias and art history

Few surviving murals do not prove low original production. Wall paintings are especially vulnerable to weathering, plaster loss, repainting and architectural reuse. This is why South Indian mural history must be narrated with evidentiary humility.

### 2. Workshop mobility after imperial decline

The dispersal of artists after later Mughal contraction helps explain why regional courts could absorb miniature resources while still producing distinct styles. It is a movement of skills, not a collapse into sameness.

### 3. Painting as a format history

A high-quality Mains answer can track painting from cave wall to manuscript, album, market sheet, cloth panel and print. This format history explains many style changes better than dynastic labels alone do.

### 4. Modern painting as argument

Bengal School, Santiniketan and the Progressive Artists Group should be read as different answers to the question of what a modern Indian art language ought to be. This gives the modern section analytical force.
