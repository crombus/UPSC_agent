## ORIGINAL MAINS ANSWERS

### Original Q1. Why should cinema be treated as a modern Indian art form rather than as mere entertainment? (10 marks, 150 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** Cinema belongs in Indian Art and Culture because it does what earlier art forms did - combine narrative, performance, music and visual design - while adding camera, editing and mass reproducibility.

**Model solution**

Cinema is a composite modern art. It fuses story or literature, acting, moving image, sound, music, colour and production design, and editing into one integrated medium. Its distinctive feature is not only combination but circulation: once recorded, a film can travel across regions and generations, creating common memory and public debate.

This medium also has a specifically Indian cultural history. From mythological silent features such as *Raja Harishchandra* to parallel cinema's social realism and the multilingual field of regional cinemas, cinema became a major vehicle of identity, empathy and representation. It also developed its own institutions - certification, training, archiving, awards and festivals - which older performance forms did not require in the same way.

Cinema should therefore be treated not as entertainment trivia but as a modern art that mediates aesthetics, technology and society together.

**Conclusion:** In UPSC terms, cinema matters because it is simultaneously art, archive, institution and public culture.
### Original Q2. Trace the development of Indian cinema from exhibition to the talkie era. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** The development of Indian cinema is best understood through multiple firsts rather than one heroic starting point.

**Model solution**

Motion pictures entered India through Lumière screenings in Bombay in 1896. The process of indigenisation began quickly: *Coconut Fair and Our Indian Empire* appeared in 1897 as the first Indian-shot film, while Harishchandra Bhatavdekar, or Save Dada, made short films in 1899 as the first motion venture by an Indian. Cinema-house infrastructure developed with Major Warwick's Madras hall in 1900 and Jamshedji Madan's Elphinstone Picture House in Calcutta in 1907.

The silent era consolidated cinema as an Indian medium. *Pundalik* (1912) marked an Indo-British collaboration, and Dadasaheb Phalke's *Raja Harishchandra* (1913) became the first indigenous Indian silent feature. Silent films in India were accompanied by live music, showing early localisation of the medium. The 1930s then brought technical change: *Alam-Ara* (1931) became the first talkie.

**Conclusion:** Thus, Indian cinema evolved from imported exhibition to locally produced, institutionally grounded and linguistically transformative sound cinema within a few decades.
### Original Q3. Distinguish certification from censorship in the Indian film-regulatory framework. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** India calls its film regime certification, but the powers exercised under it mean that censorship cannot be wished away from the analysis.

**Model solution**

Certification refers to legal classification for public exhibition. Under the Cinematograph Act, 1952, films are examined by the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC), a statutory body under the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting. The Board decides which audience may view a film and uses categories such as U, A and S, alongside age-linked UA markers in the current scheme.

Censorship, by contrast, concerns suppression, modification or restriction of content. In India, the distinction blurs because the Board may direct cuts, excisions or other modifications before granting a certificate. It may even refuse sanction for public exhibition if required changes are not made. Therefore the system is certification in formal structure, but partly censoring in effect.

Institutional detail matters. CBFC has a Chairman and non-official members appointed by the Central Government and functions through regional offices. The federal split is also important: certification is a Union subject, but enforcement within territories has involved state governments. The current caution is legal as well: FCAT should not be treated as a live current tribunal; the safer current statement is that specified appeals move through the High Court route under the updated framework.

**Qualification:** The full constitutional doctrine on free speech belongs to Polity. In Art and Culture, the focus is on the medium's regulation and cultural consequences.

**Conclusion:** Indian film regulation should therefore be described as audience-classification backed by content-control powers, not as a pure and harmless labelling system.
### Original Q4. Discuss the institutional architecture of Indian cinema with special reference to training, preservation and development. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** Indian cinema has separate institutions because making films, certifying films, teaching filmmaking and preserving old films are different functions.

**Model solution**

The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting is the Union-level administrative anchor for the film sector. Under it, the CBFC handles statutory certification for public exhibition. But the institutional architecture widens beyond regulation.

Training is represented by Film and Television Institute of India (FTII), set up in 1960 under the Government of India, and Satyajit Ray Film and Television Institute (SRFTI), established in 1995 as an autonomous educational institution under the administrative control of MIB. Their function is pedagogic, not regulatory. They create trained film workers and filmmakers.

Preservation historically centred on the National Film Archive of India (NFAI), because film heritage is fragile and needs archiving, restoration, cataloguing and access. Development and promotion were associated with the National Film Development Corporation (NFDC), set up in 1975. Current official pages show that the functions of Films Division, Directorate of Film Festivals, NFAI and Children's Film Society of India were merged into NFDC under the consolidated architecture. This means that archive, documentary, children's cinema and festival functions now operate through a more integrated public structure.

The advantage of such consolidation is administrative synergy; the risk is dilution of specialist mandates, since archive logic differs from production logic.

**Conclusion:** Indian cinema's institutional map is therefore best written function-wise - certify, train, preserve, develop, exhibit and recognise - rather than as a flat acronym list.
### Original Q5. Analyse cinema as a medium of nation-building and social representation in India. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** Cinema helped India imagine itself, but it did so through representation and mediation, not through neutral social reporting.

**Model solution**

Post-independence Indian cinema acquired a nation-building role because of its reach across urban and rural audiences and its ability to produce shared stories, emotions and symbols. It shaped public memory and gave visual form to ideas of family, reform, modernity, region and nation. This made cinema more than entertainment; it became a recurring site of identity formation.

At the same time, cinema is a medium of representation. It shows how filmmakers and producers imagined social tensions and what they believed audiences would accept. This is why it can illuminate reformist themes such as child marriage, women's education or social injustice without being mistaken for a direct social survey. The source's own periodisation of women on screen shows this clearly: reform themes, later stereotype, and then more complex depictions in parallel cinema.

Parallel cinema deepened this representational role by foregrounding poverty, injustice and human suffering. Figures such as Satyajit Ray, Ritwik Ghatak, Guru Dutt and Shyam Benegal in the approved bank help explain how cinema could serve as critique rather than only consensus.

Yet representation has limits. Commercial structures, censorship pressures, language markets and unequal access shape what reaches audiences. Therefore cinema can show changing public imagination without mirroring society perfectly.

**Conclusion:** Indian cinema's nation-building importance lies precisely in this tension - it reflects, shapes and debates social reality, but never transparently reproduces it.
### Original Q6. Evaluate the role of festivals, awards and preservation in projecting Indian cinema globally. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Answer-grabbing introduction:** Global visibility in cinema is produced not only by famous films but by a chain of festivals, awards, archives and institutions that decide what travels and what survives.

**Model solution**

Festivals and awards give Indian cinema international visibility. The International Film Festival of India (IFFI) functions as a domestic international platform for exhibition, exchange and programming. National Film Awards and the Dadasaheb Phalke Award provide domestic recognition, while institutions such as BAFTA or the Academy Awards represent distinct international systems of recognition. These statuses are different: a certificate, a festival screening, a nomination and an award should never be merged.

The 2026 *Boong* route shows both possibility and caution. BAFTA's official category page verifies its win in the Children's & Family Film category, which is important for the visibility of Manipuri and Indian cinema. But the claim must remain category-specific; it should not be inflated into a general Oscar story or a proof of universal superiority. That distinction is analytically important.

Preservation is equally central to global projection. Films cannot travel across generations without archiving, restoration and digitisation. Here, institutions such as the NFAI legacy, NFHM and the current NFDC-linked preservation architecture matter because global presence depends not only on making films but also on keeping film heritage accessible.

However, external validation has limits. Festival circuits and juries recognise particular works in particular categories and do not exhaust the value of India's multilingual film cultures.

**Conclusion:** Indian cinema projects itself globally when recognition, curation and preservation work together - but prestige must always be analysed with category, institution and archival depth.
