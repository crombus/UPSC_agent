### SESSION 1 - FOUNDATION - NATYA SHASTRA, NATYA VEDA AND DRAMATIC MODES

#### Answer-grabbing line - write/adapt in the exam

> Indian theatre should begin with Natya Shastra because the tradition defines performance as an integrated art of text, enactment, music and rasa rather than as spoken dialogue alone.

#### Visual first

| Dramatic modes | Lokadharmi = realistic depiction of life; Natyadharmi = stylised, symbolic narration |
| Exam use | Use the pair to explain why Indian theatre cannot be written as mere script history |

*This frame prevents the answer from shrinking theatre into either literature alone or performance trivia.*

#### Core explanation

The core owner and Nitin's theatre opening make one point unavoidable: Indian theatre begins as a composite performance grammar. Bharata's layered `Natya Shastra` is presented as the first treatise on dramaturgy, and its Natya Veda idea brings together story, gesture, music and aesthetic effect. The Lokadharmi-Natyadharmi distinction then helps the student explain how realism and stylisation coexist inside the same theatre tradition. The safest chronology is broad because the text is layered; pseudo-precision is riskier than an honestly bounded date range.

#### How to write the paragraph (claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification/link)

**Model paragraph:** Indian theatre is best introduced through the composite logic of `Natya Shastra`. The source conventionally attributes the dramaturgic treatise to Bharata and explains theatre as Natya Veda, an art joining words, enactment, music and rasa. This matters because later Sanskrit drama, folk theatre, Kutiyattam and even puppetry all inherit a world in which performance is more than script recitation. The qualification is essential: cite a broad late-BCE/early-CE horizon instead of inventing one final composition year.

#### Named evidence/examples

- Bharata's `Natya Shastra` as the first treatise on dramaturgy in the source-owned bank.
- Lokadharmi and Natyadharmi as the precision pair most likely to be tested in theory questions.
- Natya Veda as the argument that theatre in India is composite rather than merely literary.

#### Prelims traps

- Do not give a falsely exact date for `Natya Shastra`.
- Do not treat Natya Veda as dance alone or literature alone.
- Do not assume textual prescription proves unchanged later practice.

#### Mains use

### SESSION 3 - CORE - STAGING, CONVENTION, SITABENGA-JOGIMARA CAUTION AND DECLINE

#### Answer-grabbing line - write/adapt in the exam

> The safest way to explain Sanskrit theatre is through its recorded conventions and decline mechanisms, while refusing to convert prescriptive rules or disputed archaeology into universal fact.

#### Visual first

| Stage | About 400 persons, two-storeyed stage, curtains used, masks not used in Bharata's prescription | Prescription is not archaeology |
| Archaeology | Sitabenga and Jogimara are linked by some scholars to performance | 'World's oldest amphitheatre' is disputed |
| Decline | Shift to poems/songs, difficult Sanskrit, changed patronage under later rulers | Treat causes as multiple and overlapping |

*This table separates what the source records from what the answer must qualify.*

#### Core explanation

The classical theatre bank is strong precisely where students often overclaim. Nitin records four- to seven-act plays, rare tragedy, a male protagonist who succeeds, and a structured opening-to-conclusion movement. He also records Bharata's stage prescription: around 400 people, upper celestial and lower terrestrial levels, curtains, and no masks. The same section then lists three reasons for decline - authorial shift to poems and songs, the difficulty and reduced reach of rule-bound Sanskrit, and changed patronage. Sitabenga and Jogimara can be noted as performance-linked archaeological spaces, but the popular 'world's oldest amphitheatre' tag should remain disputed, not asserted as settled fact.

#### How to write the paragraph (claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification/link)

**Model paragraph:** Sanskrit theatre should be written as a codified but historically bounded performance system. The source records multi-act structure, rare tragedy, a Sutradhar-led opening and a stage prescription that distinguishes celestial from terrestrial levels. These details show a designed dramaturgic world, yet they remain prescriptive rather than archaeologically universal. The same caution applies to decline: the book gives multiple causes - literary shift, linguistic difficulty and changed patronage - so the answer should present contraction as a process, not as a single-date collapse.

#### Named evidence/examples

- Four- to seven-act convention and structured dramatic progression.
- Stage prescription with two levels, curtains, and no masks in Bharata's classical summary.
- Named decline mechanisms in the source rather than a vague 'foreign invasion ended theatre' line.

#### Prelims traps

- Do not present the Bharata stage as a verified blueprint for every historical theatre.
- Do not write that Sanskrit plays were Greek-style tragedies.
- Do not state the Sitabenga-Jogimara amphitheatre claim as settled fact.

#### Mains use

Useful in questions on the nature of Sanskrit drama, reasons for its contraction, or evidence-and-interpretation discipline in cultural history.

#### Mini recap

- Prescription is not archaeology.

*Kutiyattam is the bridge between classical dramaturgy and living safeguarding.*

#### Core explanation

The source-owned file is unusually strong on Kutiyattam. It records the form as the oldest continuing theatre tradition of India, surviving in Kerala since the tenth century CE, performed in Sanskrit, Prakrit and Malayalam, and accompanied by Mizhavu and Edakka. Each character begins with Nirvahana, a recollection of past events, and the performance expands leisurely with social, philosophical and political commentary. The official UNESCO page strengthens the line by describing Kutiyattam as one of India's oldest living theatrical traditions, a synthesis of Sanskrit classicism and local Kerala tradition, with neta abhinaya and hasta abhinaya prominent. The result is an ideal answer bridge between historical continuity and living heritage.

#### How to write the paragraph (claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification/link)

**Model paragraph:** Kutiyattam is the strongest rebuttal to the claim that Sanskrit theatre is merely textual memory. UNESCO's official page confirms that it is one of India's oldest living theatrical traditions and a synthesis of Sanskrit classicism with local Kerala forms. The qualification to preserve is simple: Kutiyattam proves survival of a lineage, not unchanged continuity of every older Sanskrit stage convention everywhere.

### SESSION 5 - CORE - FOLK THEATRE AS REGIONAL PUBLIC CULTURE

#### Answer-grabbing line - write/adapt in the exam

> Folk theatre is best written as a changing public medium - rooted in locality, often devotional in origin, then romantic-heroic, and later adapted for social messaging.

#### Must-write keywords

- rural roots
- rustic style
- devotional
- local heroes
- social message
- public culture

#### Visual first

```text
FOLK THEATRE ARC
15th-16th century devotional themes
            ->
love stories / local heroes / regional idiom
            ->
post-Independence social lessons + entertainment

Rule: do not write all folk theatre as a frozen ancient survival.
```

*This three-step arc is the quickest way to turn a list of forms into an analytical answer.*

#### Core explanation

Nitin's foundation statement is precise: folk theatre reflects local lifestyle, social norms, beliefs and customs; it is rural in roots and rustic in style when compared with the sophisticated urban Sanskrit stage. Most forms begin in the fifteenth-sixteenth century devotional milieu, later absorb love stories and local heroes, and after Independence acquire explicit social-message roles. This arc matters because it keeps theatre historical and functional at once. It also disciplines geography: the present state labels used by modern summaries are convenient descriptors, not proof that identical state boundaries existed when the forms first emerged.

#### How to write the paragraph (claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification/link)

**Model paragraph:** The correct analytical move with folk theatre is to show transformation rather than timeless sameness. The source describes these forms as rural and community-facing, born largely in devotional settings, later widened to romance and heroic memory, and finally used for education and social messaging. This helps the answer explain why forms as different as Jatra, Nautanki or Bhaona remain performance publics rather than museum residues. The qualification is vital: current state labels are modern reference points, not evidence that the same political map existed at origin.

#### Named evidence/examples

- Local lifestyle, belief and custom as the social base of folk theatre.
- Devotional -> romantic/heroic -> social-message arc from the source-owned bank.
- Comparison with the urban Sanskrit stage as an analytical contrast rather than a ranking exercise.

#### Prelims traps

- Do not describe all folk theatre as timeless and unchanging.
- Do not project present state boundaries retrospectively as ancient certainties.
- Do not reduce folk theatre to 'village entertainment' with no ideological or social role.

#### Mains use

Use for questions on theatre as communication, community performance, and regional cultural publics.

#### Mini recap

- Folk theatre is local and historical.
- Origin, function and later adaptation must be separated.
- Modern geography is a locator, not a proof of ancient borders.
