### CROSS-OWNED VERIFIED GS-I PYQ ANCHORS — ORIGINAL STEMS, NOT CLAIMED AS THIS OWNER'S DIRECT ROUTES

**2024 GS-I Q5 (10 marks; 150 words):** "Why do large cities tend to attract more migrants than smaller towns? Discuss in the light of conditions in developing countries." The audited 2024–2025 Mains routing ledger assigns the question to the Geography migration-theories-and-patterns owner; the exact stem and analysis are reproduced in `upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/16_Urban-Poor-Homeless-and-Migrant-Workers.md`, §5. This is a cross-paper application, not extra direct PYQ ownership.

**Model (142 words):** Rural underemployment, seasonal uncertainty and limited non-farm work push people to seek urban incomes. Large cities attract them more strongly than small towns because factories, construction and services create varied formal and informal jobs. Earlier migrants provide networks that reduce search and settlement costs. Metropolitan hospitals, education and transport add pull, while wages may compensate for high rents and informal housing. In developing countries, investment and public services concentrate in metropolitan centres; smaller towns lack comparable industrial density and capacity. Migration does not guarantee welfare: insecure rentals, unsafe work and difficulties accessing rations at destination can coexist with better earning prospects. Decentralised investment and smaller-town services can ease concentration, but urban authorities must also meet migrants' needs through portable NFSA rations under ONORC, safe shelter and fair labour protection. Economic attraction and social inclusion are different tests of the same urbanisation process.

**2025 GS-I Q8 (10 marks; 150 words):** "How does smart city in India address the issues of urban poverty and distributive justice?" The audited 2024–2025 Mains routing ledger assigns this question to the Indian Society urbanisation owner. Original stem and analysis: `upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/16_Urban-Poor-Homeless-and-Migrant-Workers.md`, §5; included here only as a cross-owned anchor.

**Model (147 words):** The Smart Cities Mission can improve urban services, but a high-tech precinct is not itself distributive justice. Special Purpose Vehicles implement area-based upgrades and pan-city projects. Better public transport, water, sanitation and accessible digital services can lower costs for poor households where the projects extend into informal settlements. However, investment concentrated in selected districts may bypass slums, street vendors, migrants and people sleeping without shelter; rising land values or eviction can intensify insecurity. Assess who gets a reliable service and who bears displacement costs, not the number of sensors installed. Town Vending Committees should protect vendors' livelihoods; Shelter for Urban Homeless facilities and affordable rental housing must reach the unserved, while ONORC helps mobile households retain food access. Publish ward-level access and grievance data and include marginalised users in design. Smart-city infrastructure can advance distributive justice when pan-city benefits and explicit protections reach those outside showcase areas.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why urban poverty in India must be analysed as a housing, livelihood and portability nexus rather than as a housing shortage. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (148 words):**

Urban poverty links shelter, work and entitlements. A houseless person lacks a bed; a slum resident may lack secure tenure and services. DAY-NULM's Shelter for Urban Homeless offers all-weather access and referrals, while PMAY-U 2.0 includes rental and ownership routes. Neither alone ensures a worker can earn near a home. A street vendor's working-capital PM SVANidhi loan does not replace procedural protection under the 2014 Street Vendors Act. Seasonal migrants additionally need NFSA foodgrain portability through ONORC and enforceable labour safeguards under the commenced OSHWC Code. A remote sanctioned house can remain unoccupied when travel costs sever work; a portable ration cannot prevent eviction or make a shelter safe. Analyse occupied housing, nearby jobs, usable vendor pitches, cross-State ration transactions and appeals together. Housing matters greatly, but reducing poverty to unit construction ignores the daily costs of maintaining a livelihood and claiming benefits in a changing city.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the legal protection available to a street vendor from the credit available to one, and explain why the distinction is decisive. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (144 words):**

Vendors need regulated trading rights and affordable capital. The Street Vendors Act, 2014 provides Town Vending Committees, survey and certificate procedures, vending plans and safeguards against arbitrary eviction. PM SVANidhi, launched in 2020, instead offers collateral-free working-capital loans, with an interest subsidy and digital-payment incentive; timely repayment may unlock higher tranches. A loan is repayable credit, not a grant or a permanent pitch. If a worker repays every instalment but is removed without due procedure, fresh borrowing cannot restore livelihood security: the vending committee and applicable eviction rules must be tested. Conversely, a certificate does not finance inventory after a shock. Assess both whether eligible vendors obtain fair recognition and grievance review and whether credit reaches them on workable terms. Urban inclusion requires procedural protection against displacement alongside finance, without confusing either instrument with a guarantee of income, free housing or exemption from regulation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the implementation gaps in India's welfare architecture for the urban homeless and for migrant workers. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (235 words):**

Urban homelessness and migration involve different but overlapping failures. DAY-NULM's Shelter for Urban Homeless calls for permanent all-weather 24x7 facilities with beds, water, sanitation and health or livelihood referrals. The planning norm of one shelter per 100,000 urban population is not evidence of accessible beds. A facility far from casual work, unsafe for women or closed when a shift ends may exist on paper but be unusable. Housing schemes such as PMAY-U 2.0 add construction, rental, partnership and interest-subsidy routes, but sanction differs from completion, occupancy and affordable access for landless renters.

Circular migrant workers may lose food access when origin-bound delivery or failed authentication interrupts NFSA use; ONORC permits inter-State transactions through linked ration cards and ePoS, but requires working grievance and alternative access. The OSHWC Code commenced on 21 November 2025 and replaced the 1979 migrant-workmen law; final Central Rules followed in May 2026, while State rules and on-site enforcement still need checking. Formal status is not a paid journey allowance or equal treatment in practice.

These gaps interact: a worker can find employment yet sleep outside, or occupy a shelter while losing rations and wages. Audit bed locations and occupancy by group, completed and occupied rental units, successful and failed portable ration transactions, labour complaints and appeal outcomes. Build accessible near-work shelters, reliable portability, employer accountability and anti-displacement safeguards rather than claiming success from sanctioned units and digital registrations alone.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess how far the smart-cities approach addresses urban poverty and distributive justice. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (230 words):**

Smart-city investments can support inclusion, but the distributive outcome depends on where money goes and who can use the service. Special Purpose Vehicles implement both area-based development and pan-city interventions. Better transit, water and waste services can reduce poor households' costs beyond selected project wards when affordability, location and access are secured. Digital service information may help identify shelter beds or make vendor payments easier. These possibilities rebut a claim that every smart-city project necessarily harms the poor.

Yet attractive showcase districts can absorb investment while slums and peripheral settlements retain insecure tenure, deficient water and long commutes. A vendor displaced from an upgraded corridor cannot replace the lost pitch with a PM SVANidhi loan; rights under the Street Vendors Act require Town Vending Committee processes and fair eviction safeguards. A pavement sleeper cannot benefit from an online shelter locator if the bed is unsafe, distant or unavailable. Women, renters and circular migrants face different barriers that average service statistics conceal.

Assess the Mission through ward-level access, affordability, displacement, consultation and grievance outcomes. Align pan-city transport and basic services with SUH shelters, rental housing, regulated vending and ONORC food portability. Track both improvements outside the investment zone and who bears land-value and eviction costs. Smartness is an instrument of urban governance; distributive justice is the independently demonstrated condition that the least served obtain usable benefits without losing livelihood or shelter.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse why large cities attract migrants more strongly than small towns, and evaluate what that pattern requires of urban welfare policy. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer (276 words):**

Metropolitan migration responds to concentrated opportunity rather than a promise of comfort. Rural underemployment, uncertain farming incomes and scarce non-farm work push people out; large cities pull through dense construction, industry, domestic work and service markets. Agglomeration increases job matching and allows a worker who loses one employer to search nearby. Networks of earlier migrants supply temporary accommodation and job information, reducing the risk of entry. Smaller towns may have lower rents but fewer employers and less specialised infrastructure, especially in developing countries where public investment and services cluster in major cities.

The resulting urban labour supply does not ensure equal citizenship at destination. Circular migrants can hold ration cards tied to origin, live in unsafe rentals or miss shelter despite working in the city. ONORC can carry existing NFSA benefits through Aadhaar-linked ePoS transactions, but authentication failures require correction; it is not a housing or wage scheme. DAY-NULM's SUH can supply emergency all-weather shelter, subject to location and actual availability. The OSHWC Code, commenced in November 2025, creates a current migrant-worker legal route, though Central rules, State implementation and employer compliance must be assessed separately. Vendors may need both 2014 Act safeguards and PM SVANidhi credit.

A sound policy combines protection in major receiving cities with decentralised job creation and services in smaller centres. It should not assume coercive dispersal will reduce migration or that sanctioned remote housing meets workers' needs. Measure jobs reached, real wages after rent and travel, portable ration uptake and safe occupied shelter, while distinguishing a 2011 slum census denominator from present mobile households. Explain city size by agglomeration, but evaluate it by whether newcomers can live and work with dignity.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that urban inclusion requires portable person-based entitlements alongside place-based housing, services and livelihood rights. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer (278 words):**

Portable person-based entitlements and place-based urban services solve distinct failures. ONORC enables a seasonal migrant to draw existing NFSA foodgrains across States through seeded cards and ePoS; a failed authentication should trigger remedy, not the loss of food. The commenced OSHWC Code replaces the 1979 inter-State migrant-workmen law and supplies a current labour-rights route, though applicable rules and enforcement determine its practical reach. Neither portable food nor a legal labour status gives a family a safe bed at destination.

Place matters because work, homes and services occupy actual neighbourhoods. DAY-NULM SUH shelters should provide nearby all-weather beds and referrals; PMAY-U 2.0's affordable rental route can help mobile households where land ownership and loan finance are unrealistic. Construction sanction must be measured against occupation and job access. The 2014 Street Vendors Act supplies survey, Town Vending Committee and procedural safeguards where livelihoods depend on a specific pitch; PM SVANidhi lends working capital but does not give a vending right. A city can upgrade a smart zone while moving vendors and renters farther from customers and jobs, making formal improvements regressive.

Coordinate both levels through origin–destination records that a worker can correct, locally available grievance routes and fair consultation before eviction. Monitor successful ration transactions and failures, occupied shelter by user group, affordable near-work rentals, recognised vendors and resolved labour complaints. Include women and disabled migrants when checking whether shelters, digital transactions and safe transport actually work. Avoid assuming all slum dwellers are roofless or all migrant workers vendors; intersectional needs vary. Portability protects a moving person, while equitable city planning protects the places in which that person must earn and live. Neither can substitute for the other.
