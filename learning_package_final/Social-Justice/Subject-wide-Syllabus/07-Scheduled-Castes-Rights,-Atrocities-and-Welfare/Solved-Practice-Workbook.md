3. The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 is a special law providing enhanced penalties for caste-based offences committed against members of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and the owners record its distinctive architecture as Special Courts for trial, victim-relief provisions and a presumption of the offence in certain circumstances; the reason the design matters in an answer is that the same physical act may be an ordinary offence under the general penal law and an atrocity under this Act, and it is the identity of the victim, the identity of the accused and the listed nature of the act that convert the first into the second, which is why a general law-and-order framing misses what the statute is for.
4. The owners define an atrocity as an offence listed in the Schedule to the Act, and they name assault, wrongful dispossession, forced labour, sexual exploitation and public humiliation among the listed heads, committed against a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe person by a person who is not a member of those categories; the 2015 amendment expanded the list with new offences including garlanding with footwear and preventing entry to public places, so the offence set is not confined to physical violence and expressly reaches social and economic exclusion such as boycott, which is why an answer that treats the Act as a violence statute alone understates its coverage.
5. The owners set out the procedural chain as registration of a first information report for a listed atrocity offence, investigation by an officer of the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police or above, trial before a State-notified Special Court with a Special Public Prosecutor, and victim relief in the form of monetary compensation, shelter and travel expenses in accordance with the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Rules, 1995 as amended; the analytical value of setting out the chain in this order is that a failure can be located at exactly one joint, so an answer can say whether the defect is registration, investigation rank, court capacity, prosecutor quality or the payment of relief rather than asserting that the Act does not work.
6. The 2015 amendment expanded the list of atrocity offences, strengthened the rights of victims and witnesses and created the Special and Exclusive Special Court architecture, and the owners attach a precise caution to that architecture: such courts are notified by States under the conditions the Act lays down and with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court, so it must not be stated that an Exclusive Special Court automatically exists in every district; the examinable consequence is that court availability is a State-specific fact to be verified rather than a national entitlement to be assumed, and pendency in atrocity trials is therefore partly a notification and staffing question.
7. The owners record the sequence with care. A 2018 ruling of the Supreme Court diluted arrest and anticipatory-bail protection under the Act; Parliament responded by inserting Section 18A, under which no preliminary inquiry is required for the registration of a first information report, no approval is required for arrest and the statutory bar on anticipatory bail is restored notwithstanding that ruling; the constitutional challenge to the amendment was then decided in Prathvi Raj Chauhan in 2020, which upheld Section 18A while preserving an exceptional power in a constitutional court to intervene where the complaint discloses no prima facie case; the recurring trap is to say the 2018 amendment implemented a judicial direction when it in fact overrode one.
8. ### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the legal-protection channel of Scheduled Caste policy from its welfare-delivery channel, and explain why a complete answer must use both. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** Protection and welfare address different barriers faced by Scheduled Castes. The SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 penalises specified caste-based crimes and provides victim safeguards; after an FIR, proper investigation, prosecution before a Special or Exclusive Special Court and relief determine effective protection. Articles 17 and 23 address untouchability and forced labour, while Article 338 gives the National Commission for Scheduled Castes an oversight role, not a trial role. Welfare tackles exclusion from opportunities: post-matric scholarships support educational continuation, the National Scheduled Castes Finance and Development Corporation supports credit and enterprise, and PM-AJAY brings development components together. These do not substitute for a safe workplace or fair policing. Equally, a prosecution does not ensure a learner can complete a course or a borrower can build a viable enterprise. Justice requires a safe claim-and-remedy route alongside well-targeted education, finance and local services, with caste- and gender-sensitive monitoring of actual outcomes.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Examine what the 2015 and 2018 amendments to the atrocities statute changed, and what they could not change. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** The 2015 amendment to the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act widened listed offences, strengthened victim and witness rights, and created Special and Exclusive Special Courts. Following a 2018 Supreme Court ruling that diluted arrest and anticipatory-bail safeguards, Parliament's 2018 amendment responded with Section 18A: no preliminary enquiry is required for registration of an FIR under the Act, approval is not required for arrest when the law otherwise permits it, and anticipatory bail remains restricted in cases meeting the statutory threshold. The sequence matters: judicial interpretation prompted legislative correction, not automatic conviction or exemption from due process. Neither amendment alone can make a frightened witness report violence, produce an adequate investigation, fund relief promptly or secure a reasoned judgment. Registration and conviction are distinct stages. Effective reform needs trained investigators, witness protection and timely court processes, together with secure education and livelihoods that reduce dependence on alleged offenders.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that legal protection alone can secure justice for Scheduled Castes. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** The atrocities law makes targeted caste violence a special legal wrong rather than treating it as an ordinary quarrel. It lists offences including assault, dispossession and sexual exploitation, provides enhanced penalties, victim and witness rights and a pathway from FIR through investigation to a Special or Exclusive Special Court. Article 17 rejects untouchability and Article 338 establishes the National Commission for Scheduled Castes to monitor safeguards. These provisions matter because unequal local power can otherwise prevent a complaint from being heard at all. Yet law alone cannot ensure that police register the correct offence, that witnesses can testify without reprisals or that relief reaches survivors. A low case count may reflect underreporting; a high count may reflect better registration rather than a sudden change in underlying violence. Dalit women can encounter caste- and gender-based threats together, requiring confidential reporting and appropriate support. Beyond prosecution, education and credit channels matter: post-matric scholarships can prevent dropout, and finance through the National Scheduled Castes Finance and Development Corporation can expand economic options. Such instruments cannot replace accountability for attacks, but they can weaken the dependency that makes remedies risky to pursue. Reviewing action at each stage—registration, investigation, prosecution, relief and rehabilitation—offers a better test than counting statutes. Protection is necessary to restrain violence; durable justice also needs equal access to assets, schooling and institutions willing to enforce the law.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the implications of the 2024 judgment permitting sub-classification within the Scheduled Castes for welfare targeting. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** In State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024), the Supreme Court permitted sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for more equitable distribution of reservation benefits. Permission is not a command to every state, nor a declaration that the Scheduled Caste list itself can be rewritten by a state. The decision recognises that a broad constitutional category contains groups with different histories of exclusion and different access to educational and employment opportunities. A state considering a sub-quota should justify it with relevant evidence of under-representation and relative disadvantage, specify its purpose and maintain judicially reviewable criteria. The holding should not be confused with remarks in separate opinions about a creamy-layer-style approach; those observations do not themselves constitute an automatic nationwide exclusion rule. Targeting can help those least reached by post-matric scholarships, public employment and enterprise assistance, especially when benefits have repeatedly accrued to better-positioned groups. But a narrow sub-classification based on weak records may create new exclusions, political competition or litigation. Educational access and protection from atrocities remain needs shared across communities; redistributing a fixed quota cannot alone remove local discrimination or underfunded schools. Periodic publication of group-level access, scholarships, job-entry and retention data would test whether a measure reduces disadvantage rather than merely relabels it. In short, the judgment opens a constitutionally permissible route to targeted equity, subject to evidence, fairness, constitutional limits and ongoing review; it does not itself deliver welfare outcomes.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse why registered atrocity data are an unreliable measure of the incidence of caste-based violence, and what would improve the evidence base. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Registered atrocities are events captured by a reporting system, not a census of caste-based violence. A complaint may never be lodged because a survivor fears retaliation, depends economically on the alleged offender or cannot reach a police station. Where a complaint is made, incorrect recording of the caste element can keep it out of the special-law category. Investigation may be delayed, evidence lost or witnesses pressured; a prosecutor's case and a court's verdict then measure yet other stages. A higher registration count might signal more violence, improved trust in police or a change in recording practice. Conversely, a lower count can reflect silence rather than safety. National Crime Records Bureau annual statistics are indispensable for tracking recorded cases, but their denominator and year must be stated. Neither an FIR nor conviction proves the prevalence of violence in a community. Disaggregation by district, offence type, age, sex and social position is important because Dalit women can face combined caste- and gender-based harms. Evidence would improve with independent victimisation studies, qualitative community accounts and audited police-register data. Publish linked stage-wise indicators for complaints, FIRs, investigations, charge sheets, trials, outcomes and time taken, with privacy safeguards for survivors and witnesses. Compare patterns with access to legal aid, relief payments and functioning Special Courts rather than using a headline total to claim success. Any comparison over time should account for legal amendments expanding listed offences and for changes in willingness to report. This approach distinguishes incidence, reported exposure and institutional response. Improving protection requires not simply fewer registered cases but safer reporting, credible investigation, timely support and fewer acts of violence across places and groups.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that Scheduled Caste policy has succeeded at widening entry and failed at securing conversion. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Scheduled Caste policy has enlarged routes into schooling, credit and public decision-making, but an entry point is not the same as a sustained gain. Articles 15 and 16 permit affirmative measures, Articles 330 and 332 reserve legislative seats, and the post-matric scholarship channel helps students continue after school. PM-AJAY supports development initiatives, while the National Scheduled Castes Finance and Development Corporation provides targeted finance. These instruments create opportunities that would otherwise be blocked by inherited disadvantage. Yet ‘succeeded’ is too sweeping without evidence of actual take-up, retention, earnings and voice. A scholarship can reach a student who still lacks transport, safe accommodation or a functioning college; credit may be inaccessible without guidance or a viable market. Reservation opens a job or seat, but stigma and informal networks can shape promotion, workplace treatment and agenda-setting. Atrocities, including dispossession and sexual violence, can disrupt education and enterprise; the special Act's protections must be enforced from FIR to witness support and trial. Scheduled Castes themselves are heterogeneous. Davinder Singh (2024) allows states, subject to constitutional constraints and evidence, to consider sub-classification to reach groups that have obtained fewer reservation benefits. It is not a finding that all existing welfare programmes failed, nor a substitute for universal quality of public services. Dalit women may confront overlapping barriers that a caste-wide average conceals. Evaluation should track entry separately from conversion: admissions and disbursements on one side, completion, safe employment, control of assets and participation in decisions on the other. Link disaggregated administrative records to independent field evidence, while keeping complaint data distinct from actual violence prevalence. Policy must protect people from discrimination while investing in the means to use opportunities. The proposition is a valuable warning about conversion failures, but cannot be a verdict of uniform failure without appropriately measured outcomes.
