2. The owners organise tribal development around two axes, displacement and rehabilitation: the displacement axis carries mining and industrial projects, dams and irrigation submergence, protected-area notification and land alienation, while the rehabilitation axis carries forest-rights recognition, the convergence mission for the most vulnerable groups, minor-forest-produce livelihood and residential education; they then record an analytical extension, namely that the two-axis framing is substantially correct but incomplete, because it omits either livelihood and market integration, which asks whether a recognised title converts into income security, or cultural autonomy, which asks whether identity, language and self-governance survive material resettlement, and the position the owners recommend is a qualified yes that adds a third axis rather than a denial of the first two.
3. The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 recognises three categories that must be named separately: individual forest rights, community rights and community forest resource rights; the owners record that examiners test precisely whether a candidate knows the third category, because an answer that describes the statute as a land-titling law has reduced a three-part recognition of tenure, livelihood and governance to its first part alone, and the recurring trap is exactly that reduction.
4. Individual forest rights are rights to hold and live in forest land under individual or family occupation for habitation or for self-cultivation, and the owners record three limits that make the category examinable: the title is limited to the area actually under occupation, subject to a ceiling of four hectares per nuclear family; it is heritable, so it passes within the family; and it is not transferable or alienable, so it cannot be sold to an outsider; the design logic is that the statute secures tenure against eviction while deliberately withholding the marketability that would reopen the land-alienation problem it exists to close.
5. Community rights under the statute are the livelihood limb and include ownership of minor forest produce, community grazing, access to biodiversity, traditional rights over fish and water bodies and other customary rights of the community; the owners stress that this head is collective rather than individual, so it is not a second individual title but a recognition that the forest economy on which a village depends is jointly used, and its practical effect is that produce which was previously collected under permission becomes produce that is owned, which is the precondition for the value-addition and marketing instruments taught later in this topic.
6. Community forest resource rights are separate from both individual and community rights and constitute the governance limb of the statute, being the right of a Gram Sabha to protect, regenerate, conserve and manage the community forest resources it has traditionally protected; the owners describe this as the most empowering category and simultaneously the least operationalised, because awareness among claimants and administrative capacity among officials are limited, and their illustration of the category working as intended is a Gram Sabha exercising the right to regenerate a degraded forest, which converts a community from a claimant of produce into a manager of a resource.
7. The Ministry of Tribal Affairs page for the forest-rights statute, read on 2 September 2026, records that the Act recognises the rights of forest-dwelling tribal communities and other traditional forest dwellers to forest resources on which those communities were dependent for livelihood, habitation and other socio-cultural needs, and that forest-management policy in colonial and post-colonial India had not until this enactment recognised the symbiotic relationship of Scheduled Tribes with forests; the same page records that the Act encompasses rights of self-cultivation and habitation usually regarded as individual rights, and community rights such as grazing, fishing and access to water bodies in forests, habitat rights for particularly vulnerable tribal groups, traditional seasonal resource access for nomadic and pastoral communities, access to biodiversity, community rights to intellectual property and traditional knowledge, recognition of traditional customary rights and the right to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage any community forest resource for sustainable use, and that it also provides for allocation of forest land for developmental purposes to meet basic infrastructural needs of the community; it records that in conjunction with the land-acquisition, rehabilitation and resettlement statute of 2013 the Act protects the tribal population from eviction without rehabilitation and settlement, that the Act enjoins on the Gram Sabha and the rights holders a duty to conserve biodiversity, wildlife, forests, catchment areas, water sources and ecologically sensitive areas and to stop destructive practices, and that the Gram Sabha is a highly empowered body enabling a decisive say in local policies and schemes; the objectives it states are to undo the historical injustice to forest-dwelling communities, to ensure land tenure, livelihood and food security for forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, and to strengthen the conservation regime by placing responsibility and authority on rights holders for sustainable use, conservation of biodiversity and maintenance of ecological balance.
8. The owners set out the recognition procedure as a three-tier chain: the Gram Sabha receives claims, verifies them and makes its recommendation; the Sub-Divisional Level Committee examines and verifies the recommendation; and the District Level Committee is the final adjudicating authority, with the Rules specifying timelines and evidence requirements; the examinable consequence is that recognition quality is a function of Gram Sabha capacity, documentary evidence and committee behaviour rather than of the statutory text alone, which is why recognition rates and rejection rates vary sharply across States and why an implementation answer must name the tier at which the failure occurred.
9. The owners state a precise legal boundary that is frequently misremembered: the statute provides a process for recognising rights, and rejection of a claim does not erase due-process duties or justify automatic eviction; they record that the 2019 direction of the Supreme Court concerning evictions of claimants whose claims had been rejected was stayed, so that interim episode must not be treated as a standing rule of automatic eviction; the answer-writing consequence is that high rejection rates create eviction risk as a practical matter without creating eviction authority as a legal matter, and the two must be stated separately.
10. The owners record that Section 4(2) of the statute permits modification of rights in a critical wildlife habitat only after a set of cumulative safeguards is satisfied, namely completed recognition of rights, a scientific case for the modification, an assessment of whether coexistence is possible, the free informed consent of the Gram Sabha and a secure resettlement and livelihood package, and they state expressly that this is not a general relocation power; the significance is that the sharpest point of the displacement-rehabilitation tension, tribals living inside protected areas, is governed by a conditional provision with a consent requirement rather than by administrative discretion, so an answer must set out the conditions rather than assert either automatic protection or automatic relocation.
11. In Scheduled Areas the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 gives Gram Sabhas powers that the owners read here for their welfare and livelihood effect rather than for their institutional design, namely mandatory consultation before land acquisition and resettlement, ownership of minor forest produce, control over money-lending regulation and management of village markets; the boundary is doubly stated, first that the law applies only to Scheduled Areas notified under the Fifth Schedule and not to all rural areas, and second that the constitutional and Panchayati Raj institutional detail belongs to the Polity owner and is cross-linked rather than re-taught here.
12. A particularly vulnerable tribal group is a sub-category within the Scheduled Tribes identified using four criteria that the owners state exactly, namely a pre-agricultural or low level of technology, low literacy, a declining or stagnant population and economic backwardness relative to other tribal groups, and seventy-five such communities are officially identified across India; the two traps the owners flag follow directly from the definition, first that not all Scheduled Tribes are such groups because the sub-category is defined by these vulnerability criteria, and second that the identification is of communities rather than of individuals, so the criteria must be stated in full whenever the category is used.
13. Pradhan Mantri Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan was launched on 15 November 2023 as a convergence mission of the Ministry of Tribal Affairs for the seventy-five identified particularly vulnerable tribal communities across eighteen States and Union Territories, and the owners record its saturation design across housing through convergence with the rural housing scheme, road, electricity and water infrastructure, health services delivered through mobile medical units and Anganwadi outreach, and education through residential schools and hostels; the status caution recorded on 21 July 2026 is that a sanctioned-household or dashboard count must not be converted into proof of completed service delivery, so any figure used must state the component and the reporting date, and the mission must not be described as a universal tribal-welfare scheme.
14. **Model answer (independent):** The two-axis account captures tension in tribal development: projects and land transfers displace people, while law and schemes seek to restore security. Mines, dams and protected-area changes can interrupt access to land, minor forest produce and sacred sites. State tenancy restrictions seek to check alienation; Samatha illustrates controls on mining leases in Scheduled Areas. Rehabilitation includes social-impact assessment, compensation and resettlement under the 2013 land-acquisition law, and recognition of individual, community and community forest resource rights under the Forest Rights Act, 2006. PM-JANMAN aims to coordinate services for identified PVTG communities; Van Dhan initiatives support produce value addition, while Eklavya schools address educational access. None of these labels alone proves delivery or restores a lost cultural landscape. The two axes are useful but incomplete. A third axis should be meaningful self-government and durable livelihoods: in Scheduled Areas PESA requires consultation before acquisition and resettlement, while community forest resource rights put management with the Gram Sabha. In the Niyamgiri matter, Gram Sabhas determined community and religious forest claims; the example cannot be generalised into a veto over every project. Section 4(2) of the forest-rights law conditions modification of rights in critical wildlife habitats on completed recognition, scientific grounds, examination of coexistence, free informed Gram Sabha consent and a secure resettlement package. A meaningful opinion must ask not just who was displaced and compensated, but who consented, whether shared resources survive and whether families retain viable livelihoods. Development succeeds when affected communities retain agency over their future.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the three categories of right recognised by the Forest Rights Act, 2006, and explain what each secures. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** The Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognises tenure, livelihood and governance rather than one title. Individual forest rights secure habitation or self-cultivation on forest land under occupation by an eligible forest-dwelling claimant. The heritable, non-transferable title covers actual occupation within statutory limits. Community rights secure collective uses such as minor forest produce, grazing, fishing and access to biodiversity; ownership of produce is not the same thing as an individual plot. Community forest resource rights empower a Gram Sabha to protect, conserve, regenerate and manage the forest traditionally under its stewardship. They address decision-making over a shared resource, not merely permission to collect its output. The categories coexist: a family holds tenure, a village livelihood and its assembly management. Recognition depends on claims and verification through the Gram Sabha, sub-divisional committee and district committee; a paper right cannot substitute for informed and fair recognition.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Examine why particularly vulnerable tribal groups are treated as a distinct policy category. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** Particularly vulnerable tribal groups are a sub-category within the Scheduled Tribes, not an alternative name for all tribes. Identification considers a pre-agricultural or low level of technology, low literacy, stagnant or declining population and relative economic backwardness. The official category identifies communities, not any individual who happens to be poor. It addresses disadvantages hidden by tribal averages. PM-JANMAN, launched in 2023 under the Ministry of Tribal Affairs, is a convergence mission for the officially identified groups; it coordinates housing, roads, water, electricity, health outreach and education services. Such targeting may overcome disconnected departmental delivery, but a sanctioned house or reported connection is not proof a household has usable services. Dispersed settlements need accessible records and Gram Sabha participation. Policy must avoid reducing distinctive livelihoods and cultures to a checklist of infrastructure. Recognising exceptional vulnerability should sharpen access and autonomy, not isolate communities from wider constitutional safeguards.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the implementation of forest-rights recognition in India. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** The Forest Rights Act, 2006 converts long-standing occupation and customary use into recognisable rights, but administrative decisions determine whether claimants obtain them. A Gram Sabha receives and verifies claims and makes recommendations. A Sub-Divisional Level Committee examines them; the District Level Committee makes the final determination. Individual rights address tenure; community rights cover livelihoods such as minor forest produce; community forest resource rights concern forest management. Conflating the three can make a village's governance claim disappear behind individual title totals. Implementation falters when residents lack information, documentary standards ignore oral and customary evidence, Gram Sabhas have little support or committees reject claims without reasoned scrutiny. Differences in capacity across states can produce uneven recognition, but no national success rate should be invented from selected examples. Nor does a rejected claim automatically authorise eviction: the 2019 Supreme Court eviction direction concerning rejected claims was stayed, and legal due process remains necessary. Section 4(2) permits modification of rights in critical wildlife habitat only after cumulative safeguards, including completed recognition, scientific justification, consideration of coexistence, free informed Gram Sabha consent and secure resettlement and livelihood arrangements. It is neither absolute immunity from conservation decisions nor an open relocation power. Better recognition requires claim-level reasons, accessible appeals, transparent committee records and specific monitoring of community forest resource rights. The statute's promise is realised when residents exercise legitimate control over tenure and management, not when an administration merely disposes of applications.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess whether recognising rights over minor forest produce is sufficient to secure tribal livelihoods. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** Ownership of minor forest produce under the Forest Rights Act is a legal starting point for tribal livelihoods, not a guaranteed income. Community rights can establish collective claims to collection and use. In Scheduled Areas, PESA also recognises Gram Sabha interests in minor forest produce and village markets; its territorial limit should not be generalised to all villages. Control over produce protects customary livelihood space, but collectors still face weak storage, transport, processing capacity, price information and unequal bargaining power. A middleman can capture value even when ownership is legally recognised. TRIFED's Van Dhan Vikas Kendras support aggregation, processing, packaging and market links, while minimum-support-price arrangements address certain produce markets. Such instruments complement community rights; a scheme is not itself proof that all communities obtain a remunerative price, and village units are not universal. Local decisions about harvest methods and regeneration also depend on community forest resource rights, a distinct governance category. Without those rights, commercial pressure could undermine the very resource supplying income. Gram Sabha management, women collectors' participation and transparent payment practices can distribute benefits more fairly. Indicators should compare recognised collective claims, actual market access, net earnings and ecological condition over time, without assuming a causal gain from the number of units sanctioned. Recognising minor forest produce is thus necessary to shift control toward communities, but sufficient livelihoods require market power, sustainable management and accessible infrastructure.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse the displacement and rehabilitation axes of tribal development and evaluate whether a third axis is required. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Tribal development often moves along two linked axes. Displacement arises through mining, dams, industrial projects, conservation measures and loss of customary access; the injury can encompass forest livelihoods, commons, cultural sites and self-government, not only title to a house plot. Rehabilitation seeks to remedy those losses through the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, with social-impact assessment, compensation and resettlement provisions, and through recognition of forest rights under the Forest Rights Act, 2006. Yet cash or a relocated dwelling may not replace a community's access to sacred hills, water and collective decision-making. Section 4(2) of the forest-rights law makes modification of rights in a critical wildlife habitat conditional on completed recognition, scientific evidence, consideration of coexistence, free informed Gram Sabha consent and a secure rehabilitation and livelihood package. It is not a general power to evict. In Scheduled Areas PESA requires consultation before land acquisition and resettlement; applying it outside its Fifth Schedule area would be inaccurate. Samatha is relevant to mining and land transfers in Scheduled Areas, while the Niyamgiri proceedings demonstrate Gram Sabha determination of community and religious forest claims. Neither judgment proves that every project was stopped. A third axis is therefore justified: self-determination and consent, expressed in meaningful control over forest resources, recognition of community claims and institutions able to reject or reshape damaging proposals. Its content cannot be reduced to a ceremonial meeting held after decisions are final. Evaluate a project by the quality of consultation, rights recognition, viable post-relocation livelihoods and preservation of collective cultural space. The Xaxa Committee's diagnosis of displacement and weak self-government informs scrutiny but is not a binding order. Development should expand capabilities with communities as decision-makers, not count compensation payouts as evidence of justice.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that tribal land alienation is a failure of administration rather than of law. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Tribal land alienation persists despite legal controls, so administrative failure is central, but the proposition should not obscure legal limits and competing development pressures. State-specific restrictions in Scheduled Areas, such as the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act in Jharkhand and the Andhra Pradesh Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation, constrain transfers to non-tribals. Where informal purchases, manipulated records or leases bypass restrictions, the deficit lies in detection, correction and restoration. A rule on paper cannot return land when officials lack accurate records or claimants cannot afford proceedings. Gram Sabha involvement under PESA in Scheduled Areas, forest-rights recognition under the 2006 Act, and scrutiny of acquisition and rehabilitation under the 2013 law supply different protections; none is a single nationwide prohibition on all forms of transfer. Samatha anchors concerns about mining leases and transfers in Scheduled Areas. The Niyamgiri case shows the need to determine community and religious forest rights with the Gram Sabha, not merely calculate individual plot compensation. These examples demonstrate that how institutions interpret and apply rights matters as much as a formal ban. Some losses also arise through acquisition or displacement where a legally permitted project proceeds after process; calling every such loss illegal land alienation would conflate separate legal pathways. Compensation alone cannot restore a sacred landscape or shared livelihood. Improve cadastral records with community verification, provide accessible legal aid and timely restoration proceedings, publish reasons for transfer approvals and monitor post-project livelihoods. Women and particularly vulnerable tribal groups may be excluded even by apparently representative consultations, so participation should be checked rather than assumed. The Xaxa Committee provides a diagnosis of land loss and weak self-government, not a self-executing remedy. Alienation is therefore chiefly an enforcement and governance problem where restrictions already exist, but justice also requires strengthening consent, recognition and safeguards where existing legal processes permit profound disruption.
