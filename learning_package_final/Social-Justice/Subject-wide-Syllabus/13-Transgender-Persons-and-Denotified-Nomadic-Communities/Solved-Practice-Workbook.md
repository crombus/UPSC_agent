## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### VERIFIED MAINS PYQ — 2026 GS-II Q1 (10 marks; 150 words)

**Original printed question:** "Right to privacy relating to self-identity is very dear to every human being and well protected under Article 21 of the Constitution. In this context, examine the effect of the amendment in 2026, to the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019."

**Provenance:** Official 2026 GS-II paper OCR scan `mains-2026-ocr/QP-CSM-26-010926-GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER - II.txt`, as reproduced and attributed in `upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/13_Transgender-Persons-and-Denotified-Nomadic-Marginalised-Communities.md`, §3A / manual 2026 PYQ integration. The older 2018–2025 ledgers route no direct PYQ here; the 2021 gig and 2024 migration questions are cross-owned, not claimed.

**Direct model (150 words):** Privacy under Article 21 protects self-identity, not merely secrecy of records. *NALSA* recognised gender self-determination and equality under Articles 14, 15 and 21. The original 2019 Act included trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons and protected self-perceived gender under Section 4(2). The 2026 amendment, operative from 25 May 2026 under S.O. 2620(E), omits that subsection and narrows the definition. It requires a District Magistrate to examine a designated medical board's recommendation before certification; after surgery the revised certificate is mandatory and the treating institution must disclose information to authorities. These changes raise autonomy, exclusion and informational-privacy concerns, especially for people outside the amended definition. The amendment also provides document name-change access and stronger protection against coercive transitions: verification and protection are legitimate aims requiring proportionate means. A constitutional challenge remains pending; no judicial invalidation or stay should be asserted. Accessible, confidential and non-coercive recognition is the constitutional test.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the recognition and anti-discrimination provisions of the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019, and assess how far they convert recognition into access. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (150 words):**

Recognition does not guarantee access. The original Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 prohibited discrimination in education, employment, health and public services. It provided an identity-certificate route through the District Magistrate and required establishments to designate complaint officers. Thus a rejected patient or applicant had a statutory basis to challenge unequal treatment. But the route is only as effective as a safe application process, trained officials, accessible healthcare and independent redress. The National Council advises and monitors; it is not a substitute for an enforceable individual remedy. Importantly, the 2026 amendment, operative from 25 May 2026, narrowed the definition, removed express Section 4(2) self-perception language and introduced a medical-board recommendation before certification. An answer describing the original no-medical-test route as current law would therefore misstate eligibility. Evaluate actual certificates, refusals, complaint resolution and service access, with privacy protection; legal recognition matters only when it survives this delivery chain.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the constitutional recognition in NALSA v. Union of India from the statutory scheme of the 2019 Act, and explain why the distinction matters in an answer. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (144 words):**

*NALSA v. Union of India* (2014) affirmed self-identified gender and equality under Articles 14, 15 and 21 and called for welfare and backward-class consideration. The original 2019 Act was a framework, not a verbatim codification of every judicial direction. Its definition included trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons; it created a District Magistrate certificate route, prohibited discrimination and set up an advisory National Council. Nor did it enact an automatic nationwide horizontal-reservation entitlement. The distinction sharpened with the 2026 amendment, operative from 25 May: express Section 4(2) self-perceived-identity language was removed and a designated medical board recommendation entered basic certification. This statutory change does not itself extinguish the constitutional reasoning in *NALSA*. A pending Article 32 challenge puts compatibility, including Article 21 privacy, at issue without establishing that the law was stayed or struck down. Answers distinguish judicial principle, enacted rule and delivered access.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that recognitive justice for transgender persons in India has stopped at declaration without building delivery infrastructure. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (230 words):**

The gap between recognition and capability is institutional rather than semantic. *NALSA* recognised the constitutional claim to self-identified gender, and the original 2019 Act gave it a certificate process and prohibited discrimination across employment, education, healthcare and public facilities. Yet a right has little value if a person cannot obtain documents safely, enrol in a course without harassment or challenge a discriminatory refusal. Complaint officers within establishments may provide a channel, but the National Council's advisory remit cannot replace independent investigation, enforceable directions or accessible remedies. SMILE's transgender sub-scheme and Garima Greh can support persons lacking shelter; a shelter bed, however, neither creates secure employment nor establishes sustained healthcare access.

The legal baseline also moved. The 2026 amendment was notified to commence on 25 May 2026; it narrowed the definition, deleted express Section 4(2) language and made basic certification depend on examination of a designated medical board recommendation. It added a document name-change route after certification and stronger penalties against coercive transition. Those protective purposes must be weighed against privacy, exclusion and involuntary disclosure; the constitutional challenge remains pending on the verified record. Rather than claim recognition has entirely failed, test rejection and appeal of certificates, complaint disposal, safe housing transitions and discrimination outcomes, disaggregated by region and identity. The answer is accountable, privacy-respecting procedures plus real remedies, not either symbolic statute or an unqualified rejection of every intervention.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse the historical roots of the invisibility of Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic communities and evaluate the adequacy of recent policy responses. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (233 words):**

DNT exclusion arose from a punitive history, not from the 2019 transgender statute. The Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 labelled whole communities as hereditary criminals, with registration, surveillance and restrictions on movement. Repeal and denotification in 1952 removed the formal category but could not automatically remove policing suspicion or the administrative consequences of disrupted residence and livelihoods. Nomadic and semi-nomadic communities also differ from each other and from the communities specifically denotified: mobility is not proof that every family was once classified under the Act. State lists and community categories vary, so a confident all-India headcount without a sound denominator is not evidence.

The Renke and Idate diagnostic work identified classification and welfare gaps. The Idate Commission proposed a central list and permanent statutory commission; the Development and Welfare Board, established in 2019 under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, coordinates welfare but is not that statutory commission. SEED targets socioeconomic support, yet an announcement does not prove that a family with no permanent address can produce a required certificate or receive benefits. Improve outcomes by harmonising verifiable records without erasing State-specific status, accepting alternative proof for mobile households, preventing police profiling, and publishing disaggregated enrolment, rejection, payment and grievance data. Include independent review of rejection decisions so a household moving between districts can contest a wrong classification. Mobility-friendly delivery and accountability, rather than a fresh universal label, must correct the colonial legacy.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that transgender persons and Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic communities share one failure mode of recognitive justice while requiring different remedies. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer (289 words):**

The two populations share an identity-to-entitlement gap but not a single legal history. Transgender persons gained constitutional recognition of self-identified gender through *NALSA* (2014); the original 2019 Act prohibited discrimination, created certificates and an advisory National Council. For DNT communities, the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 imposed collective criminal stigma; repeal in 1952 did not remove profiling or fixed-address obstacles. Nomadic and semi-nomadic identities are not identical to denotified status. Calling both groups merely 'undocumented' conceals the former's gender-autonomy claim and the latter's history of collective surveillance.

Recent changes deepen the need to distinguish remedies. The 2026 amendment, operative from 25 May, narrowed the statutory transgender definition, removed express self-perceived-identity language and required a medical-board recommendation before the District Magistrate's basic certificate. Its strengthened penalties against coercive transition and document name-change route must be assessed alongside Article 21 privacy risks and eligibility exclusions; the constitutional challenge remains pending. Transgender protection requires confidential accessible recognition, safe complaint channels, effective anti-discrimination remedies, employment and healthcare. DNT policy requires reliable but flexible community-list verification, mobile enrolment, policing safeguards and routes across State boundaries. Idate recommended a permanent statutory commission; the existing welfare board coordinates but does not automatically deliver that reform.

SMILE/Garima Greh and SEED can support different pathways, but allocations or registrations cannot prove sustained access. For a transgender member of a nomadic household, both pathways may be needed without substituting one certificate for another. Compare acceptance, denial, appeal, service uptake and exclusion across regions; a shared recognition–delivery framework should guide evaluation, while the remedies remain genuinely group-specific. In particular, compare post-shelter housing and employment with initial admissions, and compare SEED disbursements with eligible mobile families, not just enrolments. Neither indicator can substitute for the other population's missing institutional protection.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess what a state must build, beyond legal recognition, before an identity-based entitlement becomes a capability for the person who holds it. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer (277 words):**

A state converts recognition into capability only when law, procedure, service and remedy work together for the same person. *NALSA* provides transgender persons a constitutional self-identity benchmark under Articles 14, 15 and 21. The 2019 Act introduced prohibitions and administrative recognition, yet the operative 2026 amendment narrows statutory eligibility and routes certification through a medical-board recommendation examined by the District Magistrate. A medically intrusive or inaccessible procedure may prevent a constitutional claimant from reaching a school, clinic or job; the pending challenge to the amendment does not by itself suspend its operation. Confidential handling of surgery information and reasoned, appealable decisions therefore matter as much as an identity document.

For a denotified family, formal repeal of the Criminal Tribes Act in 1952 was only the first step. Uneven State classifications, absent fixed residence, profiling and incomplete documentation can still defeat SEED and ordinary welfare access. The Development and Welfare Board coordinates responses, while Idate's proposal for a permanent statutory commission remains a distinct institutional demand. Officials should accept appropriate alternative proofs, operate mobile enrolment, reconcile lawful category records and prohibit collective suspicion, without declaring all nomadic families legally identical.

Capability is demonstrated by safe certificate access, actual payments, school and healthcare entry, fair work and usable grievance resolution, not counts of enacted provisions, portals or shelters. Some safeguards require verification and public funds; these legitimate concerns should be addressed through proportionate evidence standards, data minimisation, review and budgeting rather than blanket denial. For an intersecting DNT and transgender claimant, a single-window referral may coordinate distinct entitlements while protecting consent and privacy. Rights must be evaluated at the last mile where both categories and life chances are decided.
