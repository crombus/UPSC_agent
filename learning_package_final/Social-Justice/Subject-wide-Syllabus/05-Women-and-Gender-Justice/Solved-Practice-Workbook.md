### Source-evidence dossier (dated claims retain their original qualifiers)

1. This topic owns the gender half of the rights-and-capability argument, namely protection from domestic violence and from workplace sexual harassment, maternity support, economic empowerment, statutory oversight through the National Commission for Women and allocation tracking through the Gender Budget Statement, and its distinctive feature is the densest verified Mains record in the folder: the audited routing ledgers route seven General Studies Mains demands to it, from 2019, 2020, 2021, 2023 and 2025, together with one objective demand from 2019 whose official key the ledger records as not held locally; the self-help-group social-capital mechanism is owned by the Governance owner, women's workforce-participation macroeconomics by the Economy owner, and the 2024 General Studies Paper I demand on gender equality, gender equity and empowerment is routed by the audited ledger to the Indian Society owner, so this topic argues protection, empowerment and accountability without claiming those routes.
2. The owners define gender equality as the same legal treatment, rights and opportunities regardless of gender, applied through outcome-neutral rules; gender equity as differentiated treatment that compensates for structural disadvantage so that fair outcomes become possible, which is a recognition that starting conditions are unequal; and women's empowerment as the expansion of women's agency, capability and voice, that is, the outcome that appears when protective and affirmative instruments actually combine; the examinable value of the trichotomy is architectural rather than lexical, because equality is the constitutional baseline, equity is the instrument layer of protective law and affirmative schemes, and empowerment is the measured outcome, so an answer that treats the three as synonyms has collapsed a causal chain into a definition.
3. The owners record the constitutional bank for this topic as Articles 14, 15(1), 15(3), 16, 21, 39(a) to 39(d), 42 and 51A(e), where Article 14 supplies equality before the law, Article 15(1) prohibits discrimination on the ground of sex, Article 15(3) expressly permits the State to make special provision for women and is therefore the enabling clause for differentiated instruments, Article 16 governs equality of opportunity in public employment, Article 21 carries life and personal liberty including protection from violence, the directive principles in Article 39 and Article 42 speak to livelihood, equal pay and just and humane conditions of work with maternity relief, and Article 51A(e) makes renouncing practices derogatory to the dignity of women a fundamental duty; the discipline is that Article 15(3) is an enabling power and not itself a scheme, so a constitutional citation is the start of an argument rather than a substitute for one.
4. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 is recorded by the owners as primarily a civil and protective statute rather than a general penal code for domestic violence: an aggrieved woman may approach a Protection Officer or a Magistrate for a protection order, a residence order and monetary relief, and the implementing arm named in the Act is the Protection Officer; the criminal edge is precise and narrow, because breach of a protection order is an offence under Section 31, while cruelty may separately attract prosecution under the general penal law, so the accurate formulation is that the Act supplies civil remedies backed by a criminal consequence for breach, and describing it either as wholly civil or as a general criminal statute for domestic violence is the trap the owners flag.
5. The owners separate the two principal reliefs by function: a protection order restrains the respondent from committing domestic violence, from contacting the aggrieved woman and from related acts, so its object is physical safety, whereas a residence order secures the aggrieved woman's right to reside in the shared household regardless of whether she holds any ownership interest in it, so its object is housing security; monetary relief is a third and distinct head; the boundary the owners record is that the shared-household residence right is contested where the property belongs to parents-in-law rather than to the husband, which is why a well-marked answer names the relief it means instead of writing about the Act in the abstract.
6. The owners record that the Act reaches an aggrieved woman who is a wife, a qualifying live-in partner, a sister, a mother or any woman in a domestic relationship, and that the statute expressly covers a relationship in the nature of marriage, so a woman in a live-in relationship is within scope subject to factual determination by the Magistrate; the significance is that the Act is drafted around the household relationship rather than around marital status alone, which is why the recurring trap that the Act protects only legally married women is false, and why an answer that argues access to justice must attach the scope of the relationship to the capacity of the Protection Officer who actually operates it.
7. The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 defines sexual harassment as unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature, and requires an employer to constitute an Internal Committee wherever employee strength is ten or more; the owners record that a complaint is to be inquired into within ninety days, that non-compliance attracts penalties and that repeat default can lead to cancellation of licence or registration; they also record the recurring compliance failure that the required external member with experience of women's issues is often not appointed, which means the statutory design and the delivered committee are two different objects and must be argued separately.
8. The Local Committee is constituted at district level by the District Officer to receive complaints from women working in establishments with fewer than ten employees and from women in the unorganised sector, and the owners record that a dwelling place or house is included within the meaning of workplace, which is the route by which a domestic worker may bring a complaint; the same owners record that Local Committee capacity varies across districts and that practical awareness remains weak, so the correct statement is that coverage was extended in law to the unorganised sector while delivery depends on a district body whose functioning is uneven, and the trap that the statute applies only to the organised sector is false.
9. Mission Shakti was restructured and launched as an umbrella scheme of the Ministry of Women and Child Development in 2021, subsuming erstwhile standalone schemes into two sub-schemes: Sambal, the safety and security track, which the owners illustrate with One Stop Centres, the Women Helpline, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao and Nari Adalat, and Samarthya, the empowerment track, which the owners connect to shelter and rehabilitation, maternity benefit, childcare, working-women accommodation and empowerment support; the durable examinable fact is the two-sub-scheme architecture rather than any dashboard count, and the owners instruct that current ministry guidelines be checked before a component is attached to a named sub-scheme.
10. The Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 as amended in 2017 is a labour-law entitlement enforced against the employer: the owners record twenty-six weeks of paid maternity leave for the first two surviving children in covered establishments with ten or more employees; the entitlement therefore runs with covered employment rather than with motherhood, which is precisely why it does not reach a woman in unorganised or own-account work, and why an answer about women's workforce participation must state that the strongest maternity instrument in Indian law is available only where the employment relationship itself is formal and covered.
11. Under the second version of the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana the owners record a conditional direct benefit transfer of 5,000 rupees in two instalments for the first child and 6,000 rupees in a single instalment for a second child if that child is a girl, subject to health, birth and immunisation conditions, with exclusions turning on regular government or public-sector employment or receipt of a similar benefit rather than on a simple organised and unorganised divide; the transfer compensates for lost wages around childbirth but creates no leave entitlement binding on any employer, so a statute and a budgetary transfer must never be merged into one instrument in an answer.
12. The National Commission for Women was established under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990 with a Chairperson and Members as notified, and the owners record its mandate as investigating complaints of deprivation of women's rights, taking suo motu cognisance of matters relating to women, reviewing existing legislation and recommending amendments, advising on policy and inspecting institutions such as jails and remand homes that affect women; the decisive limitation the owners state is that the Commission recommends rather than enforces, because it cannot make an order that binds like a court, so follow-up depends on executive or judicial action and any answer that treats a recommendation as an enforceable direction has overstated the institution.
13. The owners insist on a status distinction that a routed Mains demand tests directly: the National Commission for Women is a statutory body created by ordinary legislation and can therefore be altered by ordinary legislation, whereas the National Commission for Scheduled Castes under Article 338 and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes under Article 338A are constitutional bodies whose mandate is entrenched in the Constitution; constitutionalisation of a commission accordingly means moving its existence, composition and duties out of the reach of an ordinary amending majority and, in the pattern of Articles 338 and 338A, giving it a constitutionally stated duty to investigate and monitor safeguards and to report, so the question of whether such a step would deliver greater gender justice is a question about entrenchment, capacity and follow-up rather than about renaming.
14. The Gender Budget Statement in the Union Budget is recorded by the owners as an accountability and transparency instrument with two parts, Part A listing schemes with a hundred per cent women-specific allocation and Part B listing schemes with a thirty to ninety-nine per cent pro-women allocation, and it is expressly not a separate budget; the examinable limitation is that the Statement tracks the input, so allocation reported in either part can rise while survey-measured outcomes do not, and the owners record an analytical reform proposal that a third part track outcome indicators rather than allocation alone, which is a recommendation and not an existing feature.
15. The owners record that Articles 243D and 243T reserve seats for women in panchayats and municipalities, that the advanced companion states this local reservation as a one-third share under the 73rd Amendment settlement, and that the 106th Amendment creates a one-third reservation in the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assemblies which becomes operational only after the census and delimitation sequence contemplated by Article 334A; the analytical limit the owners state is decisive for a routed demand, namely that numerical presence does not by itself defeat proxy control, party gatekeeping or caste and class differences among women, so a comment on limited impact on patriarchy must argue the gap between presence and power rather than deny the reservation.
16. The owners supply a case bank with an express instruction to use each holding narrowly: Vishaka linked equality and dignity to workplace protection and is the doctrinal ancestor of the statutory Internal Committee route, Shayara Bano scrutinised a discriminatory personal-law practice and is authority about that practice rather than about personal law at large, and Joseph Shine rejected a paternalistic adultery offence and is authority about the constitutional treatment of a woman as an autonomous person rather than as marital property; the constitutional-perspectives demand routed to this topic is answered by pairing provisions with holdings at that level of precision instead of listing case names.
17. The owners record Time Use Survey evidence as the instrument that makes cooking, cleaning and care burdens visible as work, and they classify childcare, water supply, transport and safe work as economic infrastructure rather than as welfare extras, because each of them converts unpaid time into available time; the argument that follows is that a woman's participation is constrained by time poverty and mobility as much as by any formal rule, which is why gender-sensitive programme design targets those constraints, and why an intervention answer that stops at education and empowerment schemes has not yet reached the recorded structural barrier.
18. The owners carry a dated access baseline from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey, namely that 78.6 per cent of women aged fifteen to forty-nine reported having a bank or savings account that they themselves used and 54.0 per cent reported owning or using a mobile phone, and they state expressly that these are access indicators rather than proof of equal control over assets, income or decision-making; they further record that the sixth round for 2023-24 was released on 29 May 2026 and that its indicators must be cited separately rather than mixed with the fifth round, which makes round discipline an examinable rule and not a stylistic preference.
19. The owners record from the sixth round of the National Family Health Survey for 2023-24 that 20.1 per cent of women aged twenty to twenty-four reported marriage before the age of eighteen, down from 23.3 per cent in the fifth round, and they attach the boundary that this is a prevalence indicator and is not proof that every such marriage was detected, reported or prosecuted; the analytical use is that a falling prevalence measured by survey is consistent with weak enforcement measured by case data, so a complete answer joins the survey trend to schooling, safety and household economic security rather than to punishment alone.

**Model answer (independently reasoned; no official key claimed):** Reservation has transformed access to local institutions more decisively than it has transformed the patriarchal distribution of power. Articles 243D and 243T require women's representation in panchayats and municipalities. Elected women now occupy seats from which they were previously excluded; visibility and the experience of managing local public goods can create future leaders. It would be mistaken to dismiss this constitutional entry point merely because discrimination persists. Yet a reserved seat need not entail independent control over decisions. A male relative may act as proxy, party networks may dictate candidates, and officials may treat women members as signatories rather than decision-makers. These are limits on effective authority, not arguments against reservation. Training in budgets and procedures, transparent meeting records, childcare and safeguards against proxy functioning can turn presence into agency. Thus the statement is substantially valid if ‘limited’ denotes incomplete change in political practice, but not if it denies the importance of guaranteed entry.

**Model answer (independently reasoned; no official key claimed):** Constitutionalising the National Commission for Women requires a constitutional amendment, not simply a change in its title. Articles 338 and 338A, establishing the commissions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, illustrate the architecture of monitoring safeguards, investigating complaints and reporting to constitutional authorities. **Model answer (independently reasoned; no official key claimed):** Constitutional gender justice is not confined to identical treatment. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law; Article 15(1) prohibits discrimination on grounds including sex; Article 16 addresses equal opportunity in public employment. Article 15(3) expressly permits special provision for women, so a measure addressing unequal starting conditions need not contradict equality. Article 21 protects life and liberty, grounding claims to dignity, bodily integrity and autonomy. Directive principles add a social dimension: Article 39 addresses livelihood and pay, and Article 42 requires just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief. Article 51A(e) asks citizens to renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity. These latter provisions guide public policy without operating as identical, independently enforceable remedies. Case law illustrates the move from formal guarantees to concrete protection. Vishaka linked workplace sexual-harassment prevention to equality and dignity where a legislative gap existed; the subsequent 2013 workplace statute provides the complaint architecture. Shayara Bano scrutinised instant triple talaq in a personal-law setting; it should not be described as striking down every personal law. Joseph Shine invalidated the adultery offence's paternalistic treatment of married women; it did not abolish all consequences of marital infidelity. The domestic-violence law operationalises safety through civil protection and residence orders; maternity law supports work continuity for covered employees. Constitutional permissions and court holdings establish directions, but enforcement, accessible forums and control over resources decide practical equality. Thus provisions and cases together support substantive gender justice, not an assertion that favourable judgments alone have achieved it.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the protective instruments of Indian gender-justice policy from its empowerment instruments, and explain why a complete answer needs both. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** Protection addresses coercion; empowerment enlarges agency and livelihoods. Under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, a woman in a domestic relationship may seek protection, residence and monetary orders with an officer's help; breach of a protection order is an offence. ### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Examine why the Gender Budget Statement is an accountability instrument for allocation rather than evidence of women's empowerment. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** A Gender Budget Statement classifies expenditure; it does not measure whether women gained agency. Part A identifies programmes whose allocation is entirely women-specific, while Part B records programmes with a substantial but not exclusive women-specific component. This is useful: Parliament can ask which ministry funds childcare, safe transport or shelter, compare allocations over time and demand an explanation for underfunding. Yet an earmark may remain unspent; spending may reach intermediaries rather than intended women. Take-up cannot prove women control benefits or that marginalised groups can access them. Evaluation requires expenditure and service-delivery records alongside gender-disaggregated access, control over assets and time-use evidence. Time Use Survey evidence can test whether childcare funding relieves unpaid care. The Statement opens an accountability question, not a causal verdict: empowerment must be demonstrated in decisions and outcomes.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 secures effective access to justice for aggrieved women. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 offers unusually wide entry to civil protection. Domestic relationships include wives and, where the facts establish a relationship in the nature of marriage, partners; violence includes economic and emotional abuse, not just assault. A woman may seek a protection order against further harm, a residence order for the shared household without proving ownership, monetary relief and custody-related protection. Protection Officers and service providers should connect her to the Magistrate, shelter and medical assistance. Breach of a protection order has a criminal consequence; the entire Act is not simply a criminal prosecution scheme. This matters when dependence on the household makes an ordinary assault complaint insufficient. Yet a legal route is not equivalent to access. A woman may not know an officer exists, may face pressure to return home, or may lack safe travel, documentation and independent income. Delays in serving orders and limited capacity for counselling and shelter weaken the practical value of relief. Live-in eligibility also depends on factual assessment rather than automatic recognition of every relationship. Conversely, a residence order can make legal autonomy real even before property rights change. Staffing Protection Officers, legal aid, prompt hearings and monitoring compliance are thus as important as drafting broad rights. The test is safe access to enforceable protection. Independent legal aid and follow-up on monetary relief must also be accessible to a woman whose financial dependence otherwise prevents her from pursuing the case.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess whether the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013 has extended workplace safety beyond the organised sector. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013 extends protection beyond a conventional office: a workplace can include the dwelling where a domestic worker works, and the statutory definition covers unwelcome physical, verbal and non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. The employer must establish an Internal Committee where there are ten or more employees. For smaller establishments, including many unorganised workplaces, the District Officer constitutes a Local Committee; that route also matters where a complaint is against the employer. Thus the law has a deliberate institutional path outside the organised sector, rather than excluding informal workers. Its inquiry is time-bound, and employers have preventive and reporting duties. But the district body cannot receive an effective complaint if domestic workers, street-linked workers and women in tiny enterprises cannot locate it, leave work to testify or remain employed after complaining. A remote committee without language support or safe confidentiality can reproduce workplace dependence. Employers may constitute nominal committees with untrained members, while informal employment obscures who bears preventive responsibility. Effective extension needs public information on district access points, training and independence for committees, protection against retaliation and monitoring of actual inquiries rather than committee counts. This is especially salient when employers control housing or wages as well as work. Legal coverage is broad; delivery remains conditional on reachable forums, enforceable procedures and a credible route to safety for workers with least bargaining power.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse how maternity support in India is divided between a labour-law entitlement and a targeted welfare transfer, and what that division means for women's workforce participation. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Indian maternity policy separates an employment entitlement from a conditional transfer. The Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, as amended in 2017, obliges a covered employer to provide paid leave, with twenty-six weeks for the first two surviving children and a shorter entitlement thereafter; it also provides a crèche requirement for establishments meeting the statutory threshold. The entitlement protects continuity of employment and income when a woman has an identifiable covered employer. Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana, by contrast, channels a conditional cash benefit to eligible mothers outside a simple employer-payroll claim. Its second version provides a benefit for a first child and a distinct provision for a second child if that child is a girl, subject to scheme eligibility. It is not twenty-six weeks of wages. The instruments leave different gaps. A domestic worker or casual labourer may have no enforceable paid leave from an employer, yet still need food, rest, antenatal care and help with care work. Employer-only finance may discourage hiring. Exclusion and delay weaken transfers. Childcare and safe transport shape whether either mother can return to paid work; Time Use Survey evidence makes unpaid care visible but does not prove a particular reform succeeded. Policies should ensure timely benefits and accessible crèches, consider financing designs that do not penalise recruitment, and protect work continuity alongside health. A transfer can cushion consumption but cannot substitute for a job-protected right; a right tied to formal employment cannot cover every mother. Frontline workers must explain eligibility in local languages and support claims without imposing informal documentation barriers. Comparable data should separately track covered employees, transfer recipients and women excluded from both; otherwise a scheme may look successful even while mothers leave the workforce. Workforce participation depends on closing that structural divide and redistributing care.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the claim that statutory oversight and reserved political presence together secure gender justice in India. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Statutory oversight can expose injustice, and reservation can open political doors; neither automatically redistributes authority. The National Commission for Women, constituted under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990, can investigate rights complaints, take suo motu notice, review safeguards and advise governments. It can inspect institutions and bring neglected cases to public attention. It is statutory, not a constitutional commission with a binding judicial remedy: its recommendations depend on executive and institutional response. The 2020 Mains question about constitutionalising it is therefore about entrenchment and accountability, not a magic transfer of court powers. Constitutional amendment could specify duties, appointments and reporting; enforceable follow-up, independent resources and accessible complaint routes would still matter. Political reservation addresses another bottleneck. Articles 243D and 243T ensure women's seats in panchayats and municipalities. The 106th Amendment envisages reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, but the sequence in Article 334A links operation to a census and delimitation; it must not be described as an already functioning legislative quota. Entry can foster a leadership pipeline. Yet proxy control, party ticket allocation, caste hierarchies and unpaid domestic work can deprive a representative of agenda-setting power. Civil society can train representatives and scrutinise budgets; it cannot itself compel parties to nominate diverse candidates. Evaluation therefore requires separate evidence: timely action on Commission recommendations, the effective ability of women to speak and decide, and the distribution of safety, paid work and care. Counts of complaints and seats cannot prove justice. Scrutiny should include whether commissioners represent marginalised women and whether recommendations receive reasoned replies. Transparent reporting can reveal when formal inclusion coincides with blocked access to services. Oversight plus representation is necessary institutional scaffolding; equality in practice depends on response to complaints and autonomous exercise of voice.
