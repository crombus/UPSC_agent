**Model answer:**

Skill programmes influence employment through a sequence: basic learning enables training; training builds usable competencies; employers recognise those competencies; available jobs allow workers to use them. Failure at any link explains why certification and placement figures may diverge.

NIPUN Bharat's emphasis on foundational literacy and numeracy by Grade 3 matters even for later vocational learning: reading instructions and handling measurement are prerequisites in many trades. Skill India training and NCVET's oversight of qualifications and assessment can signal standards to employers, but standardisation does not guarantee course quality. NAPS apprenticeships add supervised workplace practice and feedback on real tasks. Where trainers use outdated equipment or courses have weak employer input, the qualification may not match vacancies. Where local businesses cannot create jobs, even excellent trainees may migrate or remain underemployed.

Employment is also shaped by the demand side, not just workers' skill supply. Enterprise finance, local industry clusters and infrastructure create opportunities; mobility, transport and safe workplaces determine whether women and disabled people can take them. Self-employment needs credit and customers, not only entrepreneurship certificates. Informal placement can mask low pay, unsafe conditions or quick job loss. Employers should help design curricula while being prevented from using apprentices as unpaid substitutes for staff. Training providers should offer recognised assessments and pathways between vocational and higher education.

Measure independently verified skill acquisition, completion, employment after a sustained interval, wages and job quality by gender and district. Do not equate initial placement with durable livelihood or assume an economic slowdown proves training failure. Programmes are necessary bridges from education to work, but credible employment depends equally on labour demand, sustained demand for skills and fair access.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess whether the statutory right to elementary education has been converted into a right to learning. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

Article 21-A and the RTE Act, 2009 guarantee free compulsory elementary education for children aged 6–14. A right to enter school is an indispensable foundation, but a right to learning requires teaching, inclusion and accountability after admission.

RTE clarifies public duties and allows families to contest denial; its provision for eligible disadvantaged children in private unaided schools can broaden access where properly implemented. Yet enrolment records do not reveal whether a pupil attends regularly, reads a passage or understands arithmetic. NIPUN Bharat responds to foundational-literacy and numeracy deficits by Grade 3, and Samagra Shiksha supports teachers and school infrastructure. ASER's rural household surveys assess learning independently; UDISE+ records administrative enrolment and facilities. Neither dataset on its own explains why a child struggles, but their contrast reveals why universal registration is an inadequate success metric.

Substantive learning also depends on functioning teachers, accessible classrooms, appropriate language of instruction, safe travel and nutrition. A disabled pupil barred by stairs, or a remote child unable to reach school, cannot benefit from a formally available seat. School-management participation and usable grievances can expose absenteeism or discriminatory treatment. NEP 2020's 5+3+3+4 design brings preschool and secondary development into policy planning, but does not itself extend the RTE statute beyond ages 6–14. The RTE 2019 amendment restored state discretion concerning regular examinations and holding back pupils; testing powers should not be confused with proof of learning.

The statutory entitlement has expanded the enforceable access framework, not guaranteed mastery for every pupil. Provide teachers, accessible facilities and remedial instruction, audit attendance and actual Grade 3 competencies by district and group, and intervene on identified deficits. Only then does the right to schooling approach a meaningful right to learn.
