### Authoritative legal, judicial and current checks, accessed 27 September 2026

- **Information disorder:** UNESCO distinguishes misinformation (false, shared without harmful intent), disinformation (false and deliberately created/shared to deceive or harm) and malinformation (genuine information used to cause harm, often through context or disclosure). These are analytical categories, not standalone Indian penal offences.
- **IT Rules base:** the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 replaced the 2011 intermediary rules. Rule 3 contains due diligence/grievance duties; Rule 4 imposes additional duties on significant social media intermediaries; Rule 7 links non-observance to loss of section 79 safe-harbour protection.
- **First-originator rule:** Rule 4(2) applies to a significant social media intermediary primarily providing messaging services. It permits a competent-court order or section 69 competent-authority order for listed sovereignty/security/friendly-relations/public-order/incitement or rape/sexually-explicit/CSAM offences punishable with not less than five years; bars an order where effective less intrusive means exist; does not itself require content unless separately ordered under section 69; and treats the first originator within India as the originator where the source is abroad. WhatsApp LLC v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 7284/2021, remained unresolved on merits in the reviewed record, including an 11 August 2026 procedural order.
- **2022 amendment:** Rule 3A created one or more Grievance Appellate Committees. A user may appeal a platform Grievance Officer decision within 30 days; the GAC endeavours to resolve the appeal within 30 calendar days. It is not a section 69A blocking committee or criminal court.
- **2023 Fact Check Unit amendment:** the provision concerning content about the business of the Central Government was struck down by the Bombay High Court on 26 September 2024. No later official judgment overturning that result was located; it is not presented as operative.
- **2025 amendment:** G.S.R. 775(E), notified 22 October and effective 15 November 2025, confined Rule 3(1)(d) “actual knowledge” to a competent-court order or a reasoned written intimation by a properly authorised senior government/police officer, requiring the legal basis and specific URL/identifier, with monthly Secretary-level necessity/proportionality review.
- **2026 amendment:** G.S.R. 120(E), notified 10 February and effective **20 February 2026**, shortened Rule 3(1)(d)’s compliance period from 36 hours to **within three hours**; introduced “synthetically generated information” (SGI), lawful-SGI labelling/provenance duties for relevant intermediaries, and user-declaration/technical-verification/labelling duties for significant social media intermediaries. Synthetic content is not automatically unlawful.
- **Later 2026 proposal:** MeitY’s 30 March/21 April 2026 “Second Amendment” papers were **draft consultation documents** with blank Gazette/commencement particulars. No final notification located in the reviewed official sources makes their proposed continuous-label or Ministry-advisory due-diligence clauses operative; they are excluded from the current-law account.
- **Blocking and suspension:** IT Act section 69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules govern blocking; Telecommunications Act, 2023 section 20 and the 2024 Temporary Suspension Rules govern telecom-service suspension. These are different routes.
- **Constitutional safeguards:** *Shreya Singhal* (2015) struck down section 66A and upheld section 69A’s structured process; *Puttaswamy* (2017) recognised privacy; *S. Rangarajan* (1989) rejected restrictions based on remote or conjectured public-order danger; *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020) rejected indefinite internet suspension and required publication, necessity, proportionality and review.
- **Online radicalisation:** official MHA/UN material identifies propaganda, incitement, recruitment, training, financing and operational communication as distinct terrorist uses of digital platforms. Exposure or radical belief alone is not proof of recruitment or criminal intent.
- **OSINT:** the OHCHR Berkeley Protocol supplies a verification and preservation model for public digital information. Public availability does not eliminate authenticity, context, privacy or chain-of-custody duties.

**Official links used:** [current IT Rules page](https://www.meity.gov.in/documents/act-and-policies/information-technology-intermediary-guidelines-and-digital-media-ethics-code-rules-2021-it-rules-2021-IjM5QjMtQWa?pageTitle=Information-Technology-Intermediary-Guidelines-and-Digital-Media-Ethics-Code-Rules-2021-IT-Rules-2021) · [2025 amendment](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/10/90dedea70a3fdfe6d58efb55b95b4109.pdf) · [2026 amendment notification](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/02/f55fe52418b03f58b0669f6a8bc03b6d.pdf) · [2026 consolidated rules](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/02/550681ab908f8afb135b0ad42816a1c9.pdf) · [WhatsApp Rule 4(2) proceeding, 11 August 2026](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/115213663/) · [Rule 3A/GAC amendment](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/02/IT-Intermediary-Rules-2021-updated-on-28.10.2022-2.pdf) · [Anuradha Bhasin](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2019/28817/28817_2019_2_1501_19350_Judgement_10-Jan-2020.pdf) · [Puttaswamy](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2012/35071/35071_2012_Judgement_24-Aug-2017.pdf) · [MHA CTCR](https://www.mha.gov.in/en/divisionofmha/counter-terrorism-and-counter-radicalization-division) · [UN information integrity](https://www.un.org/en/information-integrity/global-principles) · [OHCHR Berkeley Protocol](https://www.ohchr.org/en/publications/policy-and-methodological-publications/berkeley-protocol-digital-open-source).

### Evidence limits

1. “Fake news” is an imprecise umbrella; falsity, intent, context, coordination and harm must be proved separately.
2. Virality, reach, impressions, belief and offline behaviour are different metrics. Removal counts do not prove reduced harm.
3. Encryption, anonymity and metadata are different properties. First-originator identification is not textually the same as routine content decryption, but its technical feasibility and systemic cost remain contested.
4. A coordinated information operation may use true, false and synthetic material. One inaccurate post does not establish a foreign campaign.
5. The 2023 government Fact Check Unit provision is not treated as operative after the 2024 Bombay High Court judgment.
6. The 2026 SGI amendment’s notification date is 10 February; commencement is 20 February. The three-hour rule applies to a legally valid actual-knowledge route, not every private complaint.
7. The March/April 2026 proposed Second Amendment remained a draft on the official documents reviewed; draft text is not current law.

## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### Analytical map — content becomes a security problem through a pathway

```text
actor + objective + information object
            ↓
platform/encrypted channel + targeting + amplification
            ↓
exposure → belief/fear/anger → coordination or mobilisation
            ↓
public-order, recruitment, institutional-trust or strategic harm
            ↓
content + platform + investigation + civic + external response
            ↓
legality + necessity + proportionality + transparency + remedy
```

### SESSION 1 — Social media is dual-use infrastructure

Speed, low publication cost, network effects and personalisation can widen participation,
emergency communication and accountability. The same properties allow rumours, harassment,
terror propaganda and coordinated influence to scale. End-to-end encryption protects
citizens, commerce, officials and journalists, while also frustrating access to some criminal
communications.

The correct unit of analysis is not "social media" as a single threat. Ask: who is acting;
what is the objective; what content or conduct is involved; which platform feature amplifies
it; which audience is vulnerable; and what measurable offline effect follows?

**Named evidence:** the 2012 panic-driven movement of people from the North-East and the
2013 Muzaffarnagar false-video episode illustrate online-to-offline harm. They are examples,
not proof that every viral item causes violence.

**Trap:** a platform's scale does not convert every false statement into an internal-security
offence or justify a network-wide shutdown.

### SESSION 2 — Misinformation, disinformation and malinformation require different remedies

| Category | Truth/intent | Example pattern | First-line response |
|---|---|---|---|
| misinformation | false/inaccurate; no harmful intent at sharing | mistaken warning | rapid correction, friction, literacy |
| disinformation | false/misleading; deliberate deception or harm | fabricated communal incident | network/actor analysis, platform action, investigation where offence exists |
| malinformation | substantially genuine; harmful disclosure/context | doxxing or selective leak | privacy/safety action, context and narrow lawful remedy |

"Fake news" is an umbrella phrase, not a standalone Indian offence. Satire, error, synthetic
media, manipulated evidence, impersonation and incitement have different legal elements.
The response must first establish truth value, intent, context, coordination and likely harm.

**Exam line:** information disorder is an analytical classification; criminal liability still
needs a valid provision, attributable conduct and evidence.

### SESSION 3 — Amplification, not falsity alone, converts content into public-order risk

A harmful message may pass through seed accounts, closed groups, recommender systems, bots,
influencers and trusted local-language peers. Repetition and identity cues can turn uncertainty
into fear or anger and then into harassment, panic movement, vigilantism or riot.

```text
item → network coordination → reach/exposure → belief → behaviour → harm
```

Each arrow needs evidence. Reach is not belief; belief is not action; temporal correlation is
not sole causation. Useful interventions differ by rung: provenance and verification at the
item; coordinated-inauthentic-behaviour detection at the network; trusted corrections at the
belief stage; police and peace-committee action where an imminent public-order risk appears.

**Evidence use:** cite a named case to demonstrate a mechanism, then qualify that social,
political and offline conditions mediate impact.

### SESSION 4 — Radicalisation and recruitment are not synonyms

Radicalisation is a contested, non-linear process through which beliefs may become more
extreme. Recruitment is purposive mobilisation into an organisation, network or task.
Public propaganda can frame grievance and identity; private or encrypted groups can support
grooming, vetting, role assignment, finance or operational communication.

Investigators must distinguish viewing, forwarding, endorsement, direct contact, material
support, training and operational intent. Belief—even objectionable belief—is not automatically
membership or an offence. A calibrated response combines prevention, credible community
voices, off-ramps and rehabilitation with targeted evidence-led action against recruitment
and prohibited support.

**Answer move:** protect the route away from violence while proving the later conduct that
crosses a legal threshold.

### SESSION 5 — Information warfare is a campaign, not one inaccurate post

An information operation joins an actor, strategic objective, narrative, vector, target
audience and intended effect. Hybrid warfare blends political, technological, military,
economic and social instruments. State-linked data harvesting or coordinated influence is
qualitatively different from organic error, though local actors can amplify a foreign
campaign.

| Question | Evidence sought |
|---|---|
| Who? | linked accounts, infrastructure, finance, control or tasking |
| Why? | coercion, polarisation, recruitment, distrust, market/political influence |
| How? | synchronised narratives, bots, leaks, influencers, synthetic media |
| Against whom? | community, institution, election, security force or diaspora |
| With what effect? | belief, behaviour, disruption or strategic advantage |

Domestic intermediary law can reduce reach and preserve evidence; it cannot by itself compel
every offshore actor or prove State responsibility. Intelligence, cyber defence, diplomacy
and partner cooperation are necessary for the external layer.

The canonical **Zhenhua Data** example illustrates state-linked collection of cross-platform
relational data about people and institutions. Use it as evidence of scale and targeting,
not as a blanket finding about every foreign technology company or every data broker.

### SESSION 6 — Encryption, anonymity, metadata and endpoint security

**Encryption** controls who can read data. **End-to-end encryption (E2EE)** keeps message
content unavailable to intermediaries between secure endpoints. **Anonymity/pseudonymity**
concerns whether activity maps to a real person. **Metadata** describes communication context:
account, device, time, routing or relationship information. It can be highly revealing even
without message text.

```text
secure channel
   ├─ protects content in transit
   ├─ does not secure a compromised sender/recipient device
   ├─ does not guarantee anonymity
   └─ may still leave architecture-dependent metadata
```

Weakening encryption for all users creates a systemic vulnerability while determined actors
may migrate to other tools. Lawful investigation can also use endpoint, account, financial
and traffic evidence under valid authority. The policy question is targeted necessity, not
"privacy versus security" as an all-or-nothing choice.

### SESSION 7 — Rule 4(2): mechanics, triggers, limits and unresolved challenge

Rule 4(2) applies to a **significant social media intermediary primarily providing messaging
services**. It must enable identification of the **first originator** of specified information
when required by:

1. a judicial order of a competent court; or
2. an order under **IT Act section 69** by the competent authority under the 2009
   interception/monitoring/decryption rules, supported by a copy of the information in
   electronic form.

The order is limited to prevention, detection, investigation, prosecution or punishment of
an offence related to India's sovereignty and integrity, State security, friendly relations
with foreign States, public order or incitement to those offences, or rape, sexually explicit
material or child sexual abuse material, where the offence is punishable with **not less than
five years' imprisonment**.

The safeguards/limits are equally examinable:

- no order where **less intrusive means are effective**;
- compliance does not require message-content or other information disclosure unless
  separately ordered under section 69;
- if the first originator is outside India, the first originator **within India** is treated
  as the first originator for the rule.

The legal text frames targeted identity attribution. Platforms argue that an E2EE service
may need a protocol-wide provenance mechanism affecting all users to answer a later
case-specific order. **WhatsApp LLC v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 7284/2021**, remained
unresolved on merits in the reviewed record; an order dated 11 August 2026 recorded ongoing
submissions. Do not declare either technical feasibility or constitutionality finally settled.

### SESSION 8 — Safe harbour, takedown, appeal and blocking are separate routes

Section 79 gives conditional protection for qualifying third-party content. Rules 3 and 4
specify due diligence; Rule 7 makes protection unavailable on non-observance, but does not
itself convict the intermediary.

| Route | Trigger and actor | Legal effect |
|---|---|---|
| private moderation | platform terms/community standards | platform action, not State blocking |
| Rule 3(1)(d) | court order or valid reasoned government intimation | remove/disable the identified information within current period |
| section 69A + 2009 Rules | competent blocking process | public-access blocking |
| Rule 3A GAC | user appeal from platform grievance decision | binding intermediary direction; court remedy remains |
| criminal process | police/prosecutor/court | individual liability and sentence |

The **2025 amendment (G.S.R. 775(E))** requires written, reasoned, item-specific government
intimations from authorised senior officers, with legal basis, URL/identifier and monthly
Secretary-level necessity/proportionality review. The **2026 amendment** shortened the valid
Rule 3(1)(d) response period from 36 hours to **within three hours**.

The 2023 Union-government Fact Check Unit provision was struck down by the Bombay High Court
on 26 September 2024 and is not operative on the reviewed record. GAC and FCU are not the
same body. The **Sahyog** portal may transmit government–intermediary notices, but a delivery
channel does not create an independent legal power; the underlying notice still needs its
statutory basis and safeguards.

### SESSION 9 — The 2026 SGI amendment regulates provenance, not truth itself

**G.S.R. 120(E)** was notified on 10 February 2026 and commenced on
**20 February 2026**. It defines **synthetically generated information (SGI)** as
computer-created or materially altered audio, visual or audio-visual information appearing
authentic and depicting a person or event as real. It excludes specified routine good-faith
editing, document/presentation creation, accessibility, quality, translation and similar
uses that do not materially misrepresent the underlying content or create false records.

Relevant creation/dissemination intermediaries must use reasonable technical measures against
unlawful SGI, label lawful SGI prominently and embed permanent metadata or other provenance
mechanisms to the extent technically feasible; they must not enable removal of those markers.
Significant social media intermediaries must seek user declarations, take reasonable
technical steps to verify them and label confirmed SGI.

**Three distinctions:** synthetic ≠ false; false ≠ automatically unlawful; detector score ≠
proof. Labels tell users about origin, not whether the claim is true. The later March/April
2026 "Second Amendment" papers reviewed were consultation drafts and are not treated as law.

### SESSION 10 — Network shutdown, OSINT and public communication complete the response

Network suspension follows **Telecommunications Act, 2023 section 20** and the current
temporary-suspension rules, not Rule 3(1)(d) or section 69A. *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of
India* (2020) bars indefinite suspension and requires publication, necessity,
proportionality and review. A shutdown may slow mobilisation briefly but also removes
emergency communication, fact-checking and ordinary economic activity.

OSINT must follow a disciplined chain: preserve original file, URL, time and context; assess
source; reverse-search and geolocate/chronolocate; corroborate; record confidence; protect
victims and chain of custody. A public post is not authenticated evidence merely because it
is visible.

A durable response combines pre-bunking, rapid evidence-linked official correction, trusted
local messengers, independent journalism/research, platform transparency and appeal, targeted
investigation, foreign-partner cooperation and victim remedy. Measure corrected belief,
disrupted coordination and reduced offline harm—not takedown volume alone.

India's participation in the **Christchurch Call** and the UNSC Counter-Terrorism Committee's
**Delhi Declaration** illustrate international cooperation against terrorist use of digital
technologies; they do not themselves create domestic offences. *S. Rangarajan* adds that the
public-order danger invoked against speech cannot remain remote, conjectural or far-fetched.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### ADVANCED BRANCH A — Information operations are socio-technical systems

Content, platform incentives, social cleavages, trusted intermediaries and offline conditions
interact. Removing one post may leave the network and audience vulnerability intact. Effective
response joins content action with community conflict prevention, institutional credibility
and platform-design friction.

The 2012 North-East exodus panic and 2013 Muzaffarnagar false-video episode are useful
mechanism evidence: digital content interacted with pre-existing fear and identity tension.
**Trap:** attributing the full outcome to the platform or the item alone without proving
coordination, audience response and offline conditions.

### ADVANCED BRANCH B — Rule 4(2) contains a scale mismatch

The order is case-specific, but an E2EE provider may need system-wide provenance capability
to identify an originator later. The constitutional assessment must therefore examine the
architecture imposed on all users, not only the immediate target. Less intrusive means,
data minimisation, security of originator data and independent challenge are substantive
requirements.

Rule 4(2)'s five-year punishment threshold and listed offence classes constrain the trigger,
while W.P.(C) 7284/2021 keeps the architectural/privacy issue open. **Mechanism:** targeted
order → platform capability → identity output → separate investigation. **Trap:** treating
the rule's textual content limit as proof that implementation creates no system-wide cost.

### ADVANCED BRANCH C — Metadata can reveal associations as powerfully as content

Timing, device, location and communication graphs can identify networks even when text remains
encrypted. Calling Rule 4(2) an identity/metadata measure correctly distinguishes it from
routine content decryption, but does not eliminate privacy risk. Retention, access, secondary
use and breach exposure require tight controls.

Apply *Puttaswamy*'s legality, legitimate aim, necessity and proportionality logic to the
dataset created, not only the final disclosure. **Evidence test:** who can query the data,
for how long, for which case and with what audit trail? **Trap:** assuming metadata is harmless
because it is not plaintext.

### ADVANCED BRANCH D — Provenance and detection answer different questions

A detector estimates whether media is synthetic; provenance records a creation/edit history
when present. Both can fail through compression, new models, stripping or spoofing. Verification
must still compare source, context and external evidence. Human review and appeal are needed
because a synthetic label neither proves falsity nor decides legality.

G.S.R. 120(E) supplies the current label/provenance duties, while the Berkeley Protocol
supplies a broader preservation and corroboration method. **Trap:** presenting one automated
score as proof of authorship, event authenticity or criminal intent.

### ADVANCED BRANCH E — Safe-harbour incentives can produce over-removal

Rule 7 makes due diligence consequential. A short three-hour response period can rationally
push a platform to remove borderline speech. Reasoned item-specific notices, monthly review,
notice where lawful, restoration, transparent error data and independent judicial review
reduce this chilling incentive without eliminating urgent action.

G.S.R. 775(E) supplies the senior-authority, legal-basis, URL/identifier and monthly-review
safeguards; G.S.R. 120(E) supplies the shorter period. **Mechanism:** urgent compliance plus
later correction/appeal. **Trap:** measuring effectiveness only by removals while ignoring
false positives and restored content.

### ADVANCED BRANCH F — Foreign operations exceed domestic platform law

An offshore campaign may combine foreign infrastructure, local amplifiers, stolen data,
synthetic media and multiple services. Domestic due diligence can reduce distribution and
preserve evidence, but attribution and response also need intelligence, cyber defence,
diplomacy and cross-border legal cooperation. Public confidence requires calibrated claims;
an anonymous viral account is not proof of a foreign State operation.

The Christchurch Call is an example of voluntary international cooperation against terrorist
and violent-extremist content; it is not domestic coercive law. **Evidence ladder:** account
coordination → operator → sponsor → State responsibility. **Trap:** treating a foreign-hosted
server or shared narrative as conclusive attribution.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Concepts and harm chain

- Misinformation: false without harmful intent at sharing; disinformation: deliberate
  deception/harm; malinformation: genuine information weaponised through context/disclosure.
- Fake news is an umbrella, not a standalone offence.
- Harm chain: item → coordination/amplification → exposure → belief → behaviour → offline harm.
- Radicalisation is non-linear belief change; recruitment requires purposive mobilisation/evidence.
- Information operation = actor + objective + narrative + vector + audience + effect.
- Encryption protects content; anonymity concerns identity; metadata describes communication.

### Rule 4(2) and platform law

- Applies to an SSMI primarily providing messaging services.
- Order: competent court or section 69 competent authority; electronic copy accompanies it.
- Trigger: listed sovereignty/security/friendly-relations/public-order/incitement or
  rape/sexually-explicit/CSAM offence, punishable with not less than five years.
- Limits: less intrusive means ineffective; content not required unless separately ordered;
  foreign originator → first originator in India for the rule.
- WhatsApp challenge W.P.(C) 7284/2021 remained unresolved on merits in reviewed 2026 record.
- Section 79 = conditional safe harbour; Rule 7 non-observance affects protection, not automatic guilt.
- Rule 3(1)(d), section 69A blocking, private moderation, GAC appeal and criminal prosecution are
  separate processes.
- 2023 FCU provision struck down 26 Sep 2024; do not cite as operative.

### Current amendment chronology

- 2022: Rule 3A GAC appeal.
- 2025 G.S.R. 775(E): reasoned, written, authorised, item-specific intimation + monthly review.
- 2026 G.S.R. 120(E): notified 10 Feb; effective 20 Feb; Rule 3(1)(d) within three hours;
  SGI definition, prevention, declaration, verification, label and provenance duties.
- SGI ≠ false ≠ unlawful; routine non-misrepresentative edits/educational/accessibility uses may be excluded.
- Telecom shutdown: separate section 20 route; *Anuradha Bhasin* requires publication,
  necessity, proportionality and review.

### Final answer spine

Classify the information and actor, prove the amplification-to-harm chain, match the narrow
legal/institutional tool, preserve encryption and evidence, give correction and civic
resilience measures, and audit both offline harm and wrongful restriction.
