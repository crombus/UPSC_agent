# Maritime and Coastal Security — Solved Practice Workbook

## Evidence and use note

The Topic 07 Basic and Advanced owners, audited routing ledgers, local official papers, statutes and dated Government sources control this workbook. The forty questions are a new workbook-only bank. The current coastline is 11,098.81 km; 7,516.6 km is retained only as a superseded measurement. Awareness, interception, arrest, prosecution, conviction and recovery remain distinct.

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

This is a completely rebuilt bank of **40 distinct questions**. It uses single-best
answer, matching, chronology, statement, scenario, law-location, evidence and application
formats. Keys rotate `A → B → C → D` exactly ten times.

### Q1. [Current measurement] Which coastline figure is correct for a September 2026 answer?

A. 11,098.81 km from 29 April 2025; 7,516.6 km is superseded.
B. 7,516.6 km, because an official geographic measurement cannot later change.
C. 15,106.7 km; land borders and coastline use one combined denominator that counts the shoreline twice for national planning.
D. 200 nautical miles, because the EEZ breadth is India's coastline length.

**Answer: A.**

**Option-by-option explanation:**
- **A — correct:** The MoPSW circular supplies the current figure and date; the change reflects methodology.
- **B — incorrect:** The earlier figure remains historical evidence but is no longer the current official measure.
- **C — incorrect:** 15,106.7 km is the land-border total; it is neither a coastline planning denominator nor a double-counting rule.
- **D — incorrect:** EEZ breadth is a legal offshore limit measured from baselines, not shoreline length.

**Question-specific trap:** Use the revised official figure and explicitly mark the old one as superseded.

**Question-specific trap:** Methodological revision is not territorial acquisition or altered maritime breadth.

### Q3. [Match the scope] Which set is correctly matched?

A. Coastal security—high-seas naval war only; port security—fisher welfare only.

- **C — correct:** This preserves the nested scope and distinct port-system focus.
- **D — incorrect:** Boundary diplomacy and EEZ resource jurisdiction do not define port or coastal security for operational enforcement.

**Question-specific trap:** Do not merge broad maritime security, near-shore security, port resilience and naval defence.

### Q4. [Zone order] From a coastal baseline, arrange the legal spaces correctly.

A. EEZ → territorial sea → contiguous zone → internal waters.
B. Contiguous zone → internal waters → high seas → territorial sea, before any coastal baseline is drawn.
C. Territorial sea → EEZ → internal waters → contiguous zone.
D. Internal waters → territorial sea → contiguous zone → EEZ → high seas.

**Answer: D.**

**Option-by-option explanation:**
- **A — incorrect:** The EEZ lies beyond both territorial and contiguous zones.
- **B — incorrect:** The baseline anchors the sequence; internal waters lie landward, not between the contiguous zone and high seas.
- **C — incorrect:** The ordering displaces internal waters and the contiguous zone.
- **D — correct:** This is the correct spatial ladder for jurisdictional analysis.

**Question-specific trap:** Draw zones from the baseline outward before discussing powers.

### Q5. [Territorial sea] Which proposition is legally sound?

A. India has sovereignty in the territorial sea, subject to the international-law regime of innocent passage.
B. The territorial sea is high seas because foreign ships may navigate through it.
C. Innocent passage authorises intelligence collection and weapons exercises prejudicial to the coastal State during routine territorial-sea transit.
D. Only ships of coastal States may claim innocent passage.

**Answer: A.**

**Option-by-option explanation:**
- **A — correct:** This states both coastal-State sovereignty and the applicable navigation qualification.
- **B — incorrect:** Passage rights do not erase the territorial sea's legal status.
- **C — incorrect:** Intelligence collection or weapons exercises prejudicial to the coastal State make passage non-innocent under the Convention.
- **D — incorrect:** The right is not restricted to ships of States possessing a coastline.

**Question-specific trap:** Innocent passage qualifies sovereignty; it does not erase it.

**Question-specific trap:** Private ends and statutory location distinguish piracy.

### Mains PYQ M1 — 2022 GS-III Q10 — DIRECT

**Exact official wording:** “What are the maritime security challenges in India? Discuss the organisational, technical and procedural initiatives taken to improve the maritime security.” *(150 words, 10 marks)*

**Demand:** classify challenges and give initiatives under the three requested heads.

**Model answer:**

India faces sea-borne terrorism, piracy/armed robbery, arms and narcotics smuggling, trafficking, IUU fishing, attacks on ports/offshore assets, and cyber disruption of navigation and logistics.

**Organisationally**, the Navy has overall maritime-security responsibility; the Coast Guard performs statutory constabulary and coordination functions; State Marine Police patrol the close-coast layer; JOCs, the National Maritime Security Coordinator and port agencies connect them. **Technically**, coastal radar, AIS, NC3I and IMAC create the national picture, while vessel registration, identity systems and transponders improve small-craft visibility. **Procedurally**, joint SOPs, Sea Vigil/Sagar Kavach exercises, port-facility security plans, intelligence sharing, boarding and evidence protocols connect detection to action.

The remaining gap is not awareness alone. Serviceable boats, trained crews, clear command, cyber recovery and fisher trust determine whether an alert becomes timely, lawful interdiction. Inputs should therefore be audited against response time and case outcomes.

### Mains PYQ M2 — 2023 GS-II Q20 — APPLICATION

**Exact official wording:** “‘Sea is an important Component of the Cosmos.’ Discuss in the light of the above statement the role of the IMO (International Maritime Organisation) in protecting environment and enhancing maritime safety and security.” *(250 words, 15 marks)*

**Demand:** explain IMO’s norm-setting/cooperation role without treating it as India’s operational coastal command.

**Model answer:**

The sea is a connected ecological, commercial and security space: pollution, unsafe shipping or unlawful interference in one area can affect distant coasts and supply chains. The International Maritime Organization provides the principal multilateral rule-making forum for international shipping.

Its safety role centres on conventions and standards such as SOLAS, including ship construction, equipment, navigation and emergency requirements. The ISPS Code under SOLAS structures security assessments, plans and responsible officers for covered ships and port facilities. Environmental protection is advanced through MARPOL’s pollution-prevention regime and rules addressing oil, chemicals, sewage, garbage and air emissions. IMO instruments also support training, certification, search-and-rescue cooperation, casualty learning and facilitation of international maritime traffic.

These standards strengthen security by reducing unsafe or anonymous operations, improving port–ship preparedness and enabling common procedures across flags and jurisdictions. In India, DG Shipping and other competent authorities translate international obligations into ship and port regulation, while the Navy, Coast Guard, Marine Police, port operators and Customs retain their domestic operational mandates.

However, IMO is not a global navy or criminal court. Enforcement depends on flag, port and coastal States, capacity and compliance. Effective Indian implementation therefore requires credible audits, trained personnel, interoperable information, pollution preparedness and sanctions under domestic law, while preserving lawful navigation.

### Mains PYQ M3 — 2024 GS-II Q20 — APPLICATION

**Official wording, obvious OCR spelling normalised:** “Discuss the geopolitical and geostrategic importance of Maldives for India with a focus on global trade and energy flows. Further also discuss how this relationship affects India's maritime security and regional stability amidst international competition?” *(250 words, 15 marks)*

**Demand:** connect geography and partnership to SLOC security without assigning domestic coercive powers to a foreign partner.

**Model answer:**

Maldives lies astride important sea lanes in the central Indian Ocean, near routes connecting West Asian energy sources, Africa and Europe with South and East Asia. Its island geography therefore affects surveillance, search and rescue, disaster response and the security of commercial and energy flows approaching India.

For India, a stable partnership can widen maritime domain awareness through information sharing, coordinated patrols, hydrography, training and capacity support. It can also improve response to piracy, trafficking, illegal fishing and disasters. India’s Navy-hosted IFC-IOR supplies a wider regional information-sharing framework into which willing partners can connect. Economic and people-to-people ties support stability beyond a narrow military lens.

International competition creates risks of strategic mistrust, opaque infrastructure dependence and domestic politicisation of external partnerships. India should avoid treating Maldivian sovereignty as an instrument of its own security. Respectful consultation, demand-led capacity building, transparent projects and adherence to international law make cooperation more durable.

The domestic-security boundary is clear: Maldives can share information and cooperate at sea, but Indian Navy, Coast Guard, police, port, Customs and judicial authorities act under Indian law. Partnership strengthens the regional information and response environment; it does not transfer sovereignty or enforcement jurisdiction.

### Mains PYQ M4 — 2025 GS-III Q20 — DIRECT

**Exact official wording:** “Why is maritime security vital to protect India's sea trade? Discuss maritime and coastal security challenges and the way forward.” *(250 words, 15 marks)*

**Demand:** connect sea-trade continuity to a differentiated threat and institutional response.

**Model answer:**

India’s trade, energy access, ports, offshore assets and submarine communications depend on safe sea lanes and functioning coastal gateways. Maritime security therefore protects economic continuity as well as sovereignty and life at sea; a fragile current trade-share percentage is unnecessary to establish this dependence.

Challenges operate at several levels: naval coercion and strategic competition; sea-borne terrorism; piracy and armed robbery; arms, narcotics and human trafficking; IUU fishing and fisher straying; attacks on offshore installations and cables; and cyber disruption of ports, navigation and logistics. Creeks, islands, small craft and the sea-to-hinterland hand-off create persistent vulnerabilities.

India’s layered response combines Navy overall responsibility and defence, Coast Guard constabulary and safety functions, State Marine Police, port/CISF/Customs/immigration controls, JOCs and the NMSC. NC3I and IMAC provide the national picture; IFC-IOR supports regional information sharing. Vessel registration, identity documents, transponders and fisher engagement improve local awareness. The way forward is to complete awareness-to-interdiction links: serviceable boats and aircraft, trained crews through institutions such as NACP, clear SOPs, joint exercises with corrective closure, port cyber recovery, small-craft coverage and evidence protocols. Fisher livelihoods, privacy, innocent passage and humane treatment must remain safeguards. Success is faster lawful response and resilient trade—not sensor or meeting counts.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the roles of the Indian Navy, Indian Coast Guard and State Marine Police in coastal security. *(Answer in 150 words.)*

**Model answer:**

The institutions form a coordinated architecture. The **Indian Navy** bears overall maritime-security responsibility, including military defence and sea control. The **Indian Coast Guard**, an armed force under the Coast Guard Act, performs constabulary, safety and environmental duties: protecting offshore installations, assisting fishers, supporting Customs against smuggling, enforcing maritime law and coordinating coastal security. **State/UT Marine Police** police the close coast up to 12 nautical miles and link landings to local investigation.

Roles overlap in incidents. IMAC may fuse a track, the Coast Guard intercept it in territorial waters, and State police register the case; port, Customs and immigration authorities may also act. The key is clear incident command, communications, joint SOPs and evidence transfer—not exclusive geography. Overall Navy responsibility does not make it ordinary police, while Coast Guard coordination does not extinguish State jurisdiction.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Differentiate IMAC from IFC-IOR and explain why the distinction matters. *(Answer in 150 words.)*

**Model answer:**

The **Information Management and Analysis Centre (IMAC)** is India’s national coastal-maritime information hub. It correlates NC3I, coastal-radar, AIS and agency feeds into a common domestic operating picture for tasking.

The **Information Fusion Centre–Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR)**, established at Gurugram on 22 December 2018 and hosted by the Navy, develops a regional picture with partner countries and liaison officers on piracy, trafficking, IUU fishing and maritime terrorism.

This distinction prevents two errors: regional sharing does not give IFC-IOR arrest, boarding or prosecution powers; and receiving a foreign or sensor track does not complete domestic verification. Competent Navy, Coast Guard, police, port and investigative authorities must classify threats, interdict suspects and preserve evidence. IMAC and IFC-IOR are complementary awareness nodes, not interchangeable command or adjudicatory bodies.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the dual role of fishing communities as stakeholders and security partners in India’s coastal architecture. *(Answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:**

Fishing communities live within the operational space that formal sensors and patrols seek to monitor. Their local knowledge of vessels, crews, landing points, weather and unusual movement can provide early warning. Registration through systems such as ReALCraft, crew identity documents, transponders where fitted, community interaction and distress channels can improve both safety and maritime domain awareness.

However, the community cannot be reduced to “eyes and ears.” Fishers hold livelihood, mobility, dignity and privacy interests. Registration identifies a declared vessel, not every present occupant; an identity card proves identity, not intent; a transponder works only if fitted, powered, maintained and used. Small boats and informal landing sites remain hard to cover. Boundary alerts also do not solve depleted stocks, prohibited gear, debt or the Palk Bay livelihood transition.

A balanced policy should simplify registration and correction of records, subsidise reliable safety/communication equipment, train users, protect data, maintain rescue credibility and create non-punitive reporting channels. Harbour committees should include fishers, police, Coast Guard, fisheries officials and local government. Enforcement must distinguish straying, illegal fishing, trafficking and hostile infiltration, with humane treatment and consular mechanisms across settled boundaries.

Thus, trust is an operational capability. When identification produces harassment without safety or livelihood benefit, compliance and reporting decline; when benefits and accountability accompany regulation, communities strengthen the entire sea-to-shore security chain.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish piracy, armed robbery at sea and maritime terrorism. Why does the distinction matter for India? *(Answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:**

The labels differ by purpose, location and legal route. **Piracy**, under the Maritime Anti-Piracy Act’s UNCLOS-based formulation, involves specified violence, detention or depredation for private ends in the statutory high-seas setting, including the EEZ as defined by the Act, against another ship or persons/property aboard. **Armed robbery at sea** describes comparable conduct within internal or territorial waters and is not automatically piracy. **Maritime terrorism** is politically or ideologically motivated violence using or directed against ships, fixed platforms, ports or coastal targets; the SUA Act, UAPA and other applicable domestic provisions may become relevant.

The distinction determines who may board or seize, which court has jurisdiction, what international cooperation is available and what elements prosecutors must prove. Calling every boat attack “piracy” can misapply a high-seas statute; calling private predation “terrorism” can distort motive and evidence. A State-directed naval act belongs to defence and international responsibility, not ordinary piracy.

India should train boarding teams and investigators to record position, flag, victim vessel, purpose, weapons, communications and custody; preserve digital and physical evidence; notify competent agencies and States; and protect crew/victims. International reach remains bounded by UNCLOS, statutory jurisdiction and fair-trial safeguards. Precise classification is therefore not semantics—it is what converts interception into a sustainable prosecution and legitimate maritime response.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Ports are simultaneously trade gateways, critical infrastructure and security targets. Design an integrated port-security and resilience framework for India. *(Answer in 300 words.)*

**Model answer:**

A port concentrates vessels, cargo, crew, fuel, dangerous goods, warehouses, road/rail links and digital control systems. Disruption can therefore produce physical harm, supply-chain delay and strategic effects even without a conventional attack.

**Risk mapping:** classify naval/terror threats, smuggling and trafficking, insider access, sabotage, hazardous cargo, drones, underwater intrusion and cyber compromise of terminal, gate or vessel-traffic systems. Prioritise consequences and dependencies rather than applying identical controls to every port.

**Mandate architecture:** port operators and Port Facility Security Officers maintain facility plans; DG Shipping administers applicable ship/port security standards; CISF protects facilities where deployed; Customs controls goods; immigration controls persons; police investigate crime; Coast Guard and Navy provide seaward security and intelligence. The Bureau of Port Security design under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 should be assessed through notified rules, staffing and audit performance rather than announcement alone.

**Capability:** integrate access control, cargo and vehicle screening, waterside patrols, underwater and drone detection where risk-justified, vendor and insider controls, segmented operational technology, offline manifests, redundant communications and tested backups. Connect port/VTS feeds to JOCs and IMAC under clear incident command.

**Response and recovery:** exercise hijack, fire, cyber, pollution and mass-casualty scenarios; pre-designate evidence, rescue and continuity leads; protect workers and passengers; restore priority berths, navigation and data; and conduct independent after-action review.

**Safeguards and metrics:** use proportionate surveillance, labour consultation, data minimisation and grievance processes. Measure detection-to-response time, equipment uptime, audit closure, recovery time, case outcomes and cargo continuity—not procurement alone.

Integrated resilience keeps trade moving while preserving law, safety and accountability across the ship–shore–hinterland chain.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically evaluate India’s post-26/11 maritime and coastal security architecture and suggest the next generation of reforms. *(Answer in 300 words.)*

**Model answer:**

Post-26/11, India built a denser layered architecture: the Navy received overall maritime-security responsibility; Coast Guard coordination and State Marine Police were strengthened; four Joint Operations Centres, NC3I and IMAC improved surveillance and fusion; and Sagar Prahari Bal, vessel/fisher identification and exercises expanded operational coverage. IFC-IOR added regional information sharing, the first National Maritime Security Coordinator was appointed in 2022, and the Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022 supplied a dedicated high-seas legal route. The 2025 NMDA contract and 2026 National Academy of Coastal Policing target data fusion and training.

Yet capacity remains uneven. Awareness can outrun interdiction where craft, crews or authority are unavailable. Navy–Coast Guard–police hand-offs may delay command and evidence transfer. Small craft, informal landings and inaccurate identity records create blind spots. Ports and offshore assets also face cyber-physical disruption requiring continuity and recovery, not perimeter protection alone.

Next-generation reform should establish risk-based incident command; connect national and regional information without treating IFC-IOR as an enforcement body; complete maintainable small-craft communication coverage; improve marine-police recruitment, sea time and investigation; and exercise port cyber, drone, underwater and supply-chain contingencies. Each exercise should assign corrective owners and retest failures. Fisher participation must combine safety and livelihood benefits with data safeguards and non-harassing enforcement. Piracy, terrorism, smuggling and naval threats should follow their distinct laws and jurisdictions.

Thus, reform should be judged by response time, serviceability, lawful case outcomes and trade recovery—not by announced sensors or institutions. India has moved from fragmentation towards integration, but the next step must be anticipatory, audited and rights-conscious.
