### Full examiner-grade model answers to ORIGINAL MAINS 1–6

**Mains 1 (10 marks) — 1964 and 1966 successions.** India's parliamentary democracy passed two tests of leadership change without abandoning constitutional government. On Nehru's death in June 1964, Lal Bahadur Shastri took office through the Congress parliamentary process: succession depended on party institutions rather than a personal heir or military intervention. Shastri then led India during the 1965 Pakistan war and signed the Tashkent Declaration with Ayub Khan on 10 January 1966; his death there on 11 January triggered another transition. Indira Gandhi defeated Morarji Desai 355–169 in the Congress leadership vote and became Prime Minister in January. That contest demonstrates institutional resilience precisely because the choice was contested, not automatic. Yet the Syndicate's expectation that it could manage the new leader reveals an unresolved tension between elected executive and party organisation. These transitions established continuity of government amid external conflict and food pressure; they did not, by themselves, establish that party democracy or executive accountability would remain secure.

**Mains 2 (10 marks) — compare 1965 and 1971.** The wars differed in origin, settlement and regional consequence. In 1965, Pakistani infiltration into Kashmir escalated into war; neither side secured an unambiguous decisive military settlement. The Tashkent Declaration of January 1966 ended the confrontation diplomatically and was followed almost immediately by Shastri's death. In 1971, the crisis in East Pakistan and refugees entering India formed the immediate context for intervention. Indian military victory was decisive and Bangladesh emerged as an independent state: this was a transformation of the subcontinent's political map, not merely a return to the pre-war position. The different results also affected political authority: 1965 raised Shastri's standing but gave way to a succession, while 1971 magnified Indira Gandhi's national authority after her electoral victory. Neither comparison should collapse humanitarian crisis into the Kashmir dispute or treat Tashkent as the settlement of the later conflict.

**Mains 3 (15 marks) — measures and party consolidation.** Indira Gandhi's 1969–71 consolidation combined policy change, factional struggle and a new source of electoral legitimacy. After the 1967 election reduced Congress's parliamentary majority and cost it eight states, its federal party machinery no longer guaranteed easy dominance. Nationalising fourteen banks in 1969 supplied a substantive claim about wider access to credit while placing the leadership to the left of its Syndicate opponents. The V.V. Giri presidential contest exposed the leadership struggle, and the split into Congress (R) and Congress (O) formalised it. The Supreme Court's 1970 reversal of the first bank-nationalisation instrument reminds us that an announced policy still had to survive constitutional scrutiny. Abolition of privy purses through the 26th Amendment in 1971, after executive derecognition failed in court, further connected social symbolism to legal change. Finally, the 'Garibi Hatao' appeal won Congress (R) 352 of 518 seats in 1971, converting a factional claim into a national mandate. But policy motives and tactical value are not mutually exclusive; electoral consolidation also bypassed intermediate party checks.

**Mains 4 (15 marks) — the 1967 election.** The fourth general election was a watershed without being a change of government at the Centre. Congress retained a reduced Lok Sabha majority but lost power in eight states. This distinction matters: it was the state-level base of the Congress system that first cracked. Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalitions illustrated united anti-Congressism despite ideological differences; frequent floor-crossing, remembered as 'Aya Ram Gaya Ram', exposed the fragility of these arrangements. State politics became an arena of negotiation rather than automatic transmission of central party authority. The weakened organisational machine also sharpened conflict between Indira Gandhi and the Syndicate, culminating in the 1969 split and a leader-centred 1971 national campaign. Nevertheless, the later Congress (R) landslide did not erase regional differences or restore the pre-1967 state system. Thus 1967 inaugurated competitive state politics and a changed relationship between national leadership and party organisation; it did not end Congress rule nationally.

**Mains 5 (20 marks) — mandate and institutional erosion.** The proposition identifies a real tension, but electoral legitimacy and institutional costs must be judged together rather than treated as alternatives. Shastri's strengthened Prime Minister's Secretariat signalled increasing executive coordination before Indira's ascent. The 1967 loss of eight state governments weakened the federated Congress machine and encouraged coalition politics and defections. Indira's bank nationalisation in 1969, support for V.V. Giri and confrontation with the Syndicate offered policy direction while shifting authority from party organisation to leader. The 1969 Congress (R)/(O) split turned that rivalry into competing institutions. The Court's interventions against the first bank measure and privy-purse derecognition in 1970 show that legal checks still operated: renewed banking legislation and the 26th Amendment followed distinct judicial setbacks. 'Garibi Hatao' then gave Congress (R) a sweeping 1971 mandate, while the Bangladesh victory further strengthened Indira. Popular approval was genuine electoral evidence, not merely a facade. Yet bypassing organisational mediation concentrated power and reduced internal accountability. The result was a democracy still capable of elections and judicial review but increasingly dependent on personalised authority. This was a background vulnerability for the later crisis, not proof that the Emergency was inevitable.

**Mains 6 (20 marks) — trajectory and costs.** The years from Shastri's accession to Pokhran-I connected democratic continuity to a new, more centralised political order. Shastri took office in June 1964, led India through the inconclusive 1965 Pakistan war, signed the Tashkent Declaration and died in January 1966. Congress then elected Indira Gandhi over Morarji Desai, sustaining constitutional succession despite an unsettled economy. War expenditure, harvest failures and external aid dependence preceded the politically costly June 1966 rupee devaluation. In 1967 Congress retained the Centre but lost eight state governments; coalitions and defections weakened its former organisational dominance. Indira answered the party contest with fourteen-bank nationalisation, the Giri presidential candidacy and the 1969 Congress split. Judicial reversals in 1970 required a revised banking route and, for former rulers' privy purses, eventual abolition through the 26th Amendment in 1971. Her 'Garibi Hatao' victory delivered 352 of 518 seats, while the Bangladesh war created a new state and transformed India's regional standing. The 1972 Simla agreement belongs to the diplomatic aftermath; Pokhran-I in May 1974, officially termed a peaceful nuclear explosion, lies just beyond the 1964–73 core. These changes displayed resilience and strategic capacity, but leader-centred mobilisation hollowed out internal party checks. De-institutionalisation helps explain later vulnerability without treating the 1973–75 crisis as predetermined.
