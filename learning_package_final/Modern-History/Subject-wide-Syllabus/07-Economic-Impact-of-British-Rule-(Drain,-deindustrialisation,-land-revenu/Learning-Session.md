# Economic Impact of British Rule — Complete Learning Session

**Subject:** Modern Indian History | **GS Paper:** GS-I

British rule reorganised land, trade, production, finance and infrastructure around imperial needs. The central analytical problem is therefore not whether change occurred, but who controlled it, who carried its risks and where its surplus moved.

## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### Session 1: Periodisation And The Extraction-To-Underdevelopment Frame

```text
PHASE 1  c.1757-65 -> late 18th c.
  DOMINANT MECHANISM : tribute-taking + trade monopoly
  INSTRUMENT         : Bengal revenue after Diwani (Bengal revenue after Diwani)
        |
        v
PHASE 2  1813 / 1833 -> mid-19th c.
  DOMINANT MECHANISM : free-trade imperialism
  INSTRUMENT         : asymmetric tariffs + machine-made imports
        |
        v
PHASE 3  c.1850s -> 1940s
  DOMINANT MECHANISM : integration without autonomy
  INSTRUMENT         : railways, commercialisation, guaranteed capital
        |
        v
CUMULATIVE OUTCOME : structural underdevelopment
  (phases OVERLAP - they are shifts of dominance, not sealed eras)
```

**Plain definition:** Periodisation here means dividing British economic impact into phases defined by the dominant extraction mechanism of each phase, not by dynasty, war or Governor-General.

**Technical definition:** The extraction-to-underdevelopment frame is the analytical claim that conquest-period plunder, institutionalised drain and structural transformation of land, craft and trade are three distinct but linked mechanisms whose cumulative effect was modernisation of circulation without autonomous development.

**Exam-ready opening:** British economic impact is best assessed not as one act of impoverishment but as a sequence in which the dominant mechanism changed -- conquest-period revenue extraction, nineteenth-century free-trade restructuring after the 1813 end of the Company's Indian trade monopoly, and late-colonial railway-borne integration -- while the direction of resource transfer stayed constant.

**Must-write keywords:** Company plunder | continuing drain | structural underdevelopment | free-trade imperialism | dominant mechanism | 1813 monopoly end

**How to deploy them:** Open by naming the dominant mechanism of each phase to justify the periodisation; keep Company plunder and continuing drain as separate ideas; date the free-trade turn to the 1813 monopoly end and the 1833 Charter Act; close on structural underdevelopment as the cumulative outcome.

#### Core explanation

This chapter studies how British rule restructured the Indian economy through revenue, trade, production, finance and infrastructure. The single organising idea this chapter returns to at every subtopic is that colonial rule tied the Indian economy to the needs of British capitalism -- as a source of revenue, a supplier of raw materials, a market for manufactured goods and a field for the profits of British capital -- and that this restructuring, not any single act of plunder, is what "economic impact of British rule" means at examination level.

FACT: three broad phases are usable without over-schematising a genuinely continuous process. An early phase of direct revenue extraction and mercantile monopoly runs roughly from the 1757-65 conquest of Bengal through the late eighteenth century, when the Company behaved substantially as a tribute-taking and trade-monopolising power. A nineteenth-century phase of free-trade imperialism and structural deindustrialisation follows the end of the Company's trade monopoly (1813) and the 1833 Charter Act, when British manufactures entered India on asymmetric terms while Indian raw materials and cash crops were drawn outward. A late-colonial phase from around the 1850s onward adds railway-borne market integration, expanding commercialisation of agriculture, and limited, foreign-capital-dominated colonial industrialisation, continuing to the structural famine and wartime pressures of the early 1940s. ANALYSIS: these phases overlap rather than replace one another -- revenue extraction did not stop in 1813, and deindustrialisation continued alongside colonial industrialisation later -- so a defensible answer states the periodisation as a shift in the *dominant* mechanism, not as three separate, sealed eras.

FACT: this chapter distinguishes three related but non-identical ideas that a weak answer often collapses into one. Company plunder is the early, direct, often coercive extraction of Bengal's revenue and treasure following 1757-65 (the fiscal origin of Company rule, used here only as backdrop). Continuing drain is the ongoing, institutionalised unilateral transfer of resources to Britain via home charges, salaries, pensions, interest and remittances (Session 12 below), operating throughout the colonial period once Company rule and later Crown rule were established. Structural underdevelopment is the broader, longer-run outcome -- deindustrialisation, a revenue-driven agrarian order, commercialisation without protection, and infrastructure built for imperial rather than autonomous purposes -- that outlasted any single episode of extraction. ANALYSIS: a strong "assess the economic impact of British rule" answer names all three separately and shows how the first fed into the second, and the second and the agrarian/industrial mechanisms together produced the third, rather than treating "extraction" as one undifferentiated fact.

TRAP: do not write "British rule impoverished India through a single mechanism" -- land revenue, drain, deindustrialisation, commercialisation, infrastructure design and famine policy are distinct, mutually reinforcing mechanisms, and the strongest Mains answers name at least three of them working together rather than resting on one alone. TRAP: do not compress this chapter's periodisation into "1757 conquest, then decline" -- the nineteenth-century free-trade/deindustrialisation mechanism and the late-colonial infrastructure/commercialisation mechanism are analytically distinct stages, each independently tested.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** British economic impact must be periodised by dominant mechanism rather than narrated as continuous decline.
- **Named evidence:** The Company's Indian trade monopoly ended in 1813 and its China monopoly in 1833; railway construction from the 1850s onward and the commercialisation of agriculture belong to a later phase again.
- **Analysis:** Each statutory or infrastructural turn changed which mechanism carried most of the transfer: monopoly profit gave way to tariff-mediated import penetration, which in turn gave way to market integration plus guaranteed returns on British capital.
- **Qualification:** The phases overlap: revenue extraction did not stop in 1813, and craft decline continued alongside colonial industrialisation after the 1850s, so the periodisation states a shift of dominance, not a replacement.

**Recall line:** The Company's Indian trade monopoly ended in 1813 and its China monopoly in 1833; railway construction from the 1850s onward and the commercialisation of agriculture belong to a later phase again.

### Session 2: The Pre-Colonial And Early-Colonial Baseline, Without Romanticism

```text
WHAT THE BASELINE CLAIMS            WHAT IT DOES NOT CLAIM
-----------------------------       -----------------------------
leading export of cotton      | NO universal prosperity
textiles, raw silk, indigo,   | NO social equality
saltpetre, opium, sugar,      | NO absence of poverty/famine
rice, spices, precious stones | NO pre-colonial industrial
developed merchant capital    |    revolution
and artisan production        | NO claim of rising per-capita
        |                     |    income
        v
LOGICAL FUNCTION: a base that exists CAN be eroded
  => 'deindustrialisation' is the correct word
  => 'failure to industrialise' is the wrong word
```

**Plain definition:** The baseline is the narrow, comparative statement of what the Indian economy actually was on the eve of colonial restructuring, stated as an export-sector fact rather than as a general condition of prosperity.

**Technical definition:** A non-romantic baseline is a bounded empirical claim -- India held a large, globally competitive handicraft export sector supported by merchant capital and artisan production -- deliberately stated without any accompanying claim about universal pre-colonial welfare, equality or growth.

**Exam-ready opening:** Any argument about deindustrialisation depends on establishing a baseline, and the defensible baseline is narrow: India was a leading exporter of cotton textiles and allied goods within a developed merchant-artisan system, which is what makes 'deindustrialisation' the correct term rather than 'failure to industrialise'.

**Must-write keywords:** export baseline | merchant-artisan system | cotton textiles | golden-age fallacy | deindustrialisation precondition | raw silk and indigo

**How to deploy them:** State the baseline as an export-sector claim, list the actual commodities (cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, opium, sugar, rice, spices, precious stones), explicitly reject the golden-age fallacy, and then use the baseline only as the precondition that makes deindustrialisation logically possible.

#### Core explanation

Any "how far did British rule cause X" question requires a defensible baseline -- what India's economy looked like before the mechanism being assessed took hold -- and this chapter's baseline must be stated carefully, without sliding into an uncritical "golden age" claim that a good answer should avoid.

FACT: before the nineteenth-century reversal, India's most important export was cotton textiles, supported by raw silk and silk fabrics, indigo, saltpetre, opium, sugar, rice, spices and precious stones, with this trade historically balanced by inflows of precious metals into India rather than out of it. This is the precise evidentiary basis for calling India, at this baseline, an exporting manufacturing and craft economy, not merely a raw-material-producing region waiting to be developed. ANALYSIS: this baseline is exactly what makes "deindustrialisation" the analytically correct term for the nineteenth-century reversal (Sessions 9-11) rather than "failure to industrialise" -- a country cannot be "deindustrialised" unless it held a substantial manufacturing and craft base to begin with, and the pre-colonial textile-export baseline is the evidence that it did.

FACT: this baseline economy rested on flourishing handicrafts and textile production (cotton, silk, and specialised regional crafts), an active merchant and banking community engaged in long-distance and maritime trade, and an agrarian order that, while regionally variable and already stratified by caste, landholding status and local power, supported both subsistence and a surplus sufficient to sustain courts, towns and craft production. ANALYSIS: naming merchants, artisans and agrarian producers together, rather than describing only "village India," is necessary to avoid the opposite error of treating pre-colonial India as a static, purely agrarian subsistence economy with no urban or commercial sophistication.

ANALYSIS: the correct baseline claim is narrow and specific -- a substantial, internationally competitive textile-export economy with an active merchant class existed before the colonial reversal -- not a broad claim that pre-colonial India was uniformly prosperous, egalitarian or free of internal exploitation. Regional stratification, caste-linked occupational structures and considerable variation in agrarian conditions across kingdoms and regions existed well before 1757, and this chapter does not require, and must not present, an idealised pre-colonial economy in order to make its deindustrialisation and drain arguments; the analytical claim is comparative and sectoral (a competitive export-manufacturing base existed and was structurally reversed), not a claim about a lost utopia.

TRAP: never present the pre-colonial baseline as a "golden age" of universal prosperity -- the defensible claim is narrower: a substantial textile-export and craft economy existed, and its later reversal is what "deindustrialisation" describes; broader claims about pre-colonial welfare, equality or growth rates are not supported by the evidence this chapter holds and must not be asserted.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** The pre-colonial baseline must be asserted narrowly, as an export-sector fact, to remain defensible.
- **Named evidence:** The commodity list itself -- cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, opium, sugar, rice, spices and precious stones -- is a trade fact, not a welfare measure.
- **Analysis:** A narrow baseline is sufficient for the argument that follows: erosion of a real export sector is what deindustrialisation names, and no claim about average welfare is needed to establish it.
- **Qualification:** Nothing here asserts pre-colonial prosperity, equality, or freedom from famine; a wide golden-age claim would be both unevidenced and unnecessary.

**Recall line:** The commodity list itself -- cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, opium, sugar, rice, spices and precious stones -- is a trade fact, not a welfare measure.

### Session 3: The Permanent Settlement, 1793: Cornwallis, Zamindar Property And Fixed Demand

```text
STATE SIDE                      ZAMINDAR SIDE
--------------------------      --------------------------
demand FIXED in perpetuity  ->  cannot be raised on him
revenue certainty absolute  ->  but is due on a FIXED DATE
        |                               |
        v                               v
NO share in rising rents        SUNSET LAW: miss the date
or improved output          ->  => estate itself sold
        |                               |
        v                               v
LONG-RUN FISCAL LOSS            TURNOVER OF PROPRIETORS
to the colonial state           + absentee landlordism
                                + layered intermediaries
                                        |
                                        v
                        CULTIVATOR: rent NOT fixed by 1793;
                        occupancy security addressed only
                        later and incompletely
```

**Plain definition:** The Permanent Settlement is the 1793 arrangement by which the state's land-revenue demand in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa was fixed in perpetuity on zamindars recognised as proprietors.

**Technical definition:** It is a proprietary-revenue settlement in which the assessment unit is the zamindari estate, the demand is fixed permanently rather than periodically revised, and default is enforced by the Sunset Law through compulsory sale of the estate itself.

**Exam-ready opening:** The Permanent Settlement's defining feature is asymmetry of risk: by fixing the state's demand in perpetuity, Cornwallis gave the colonial exchequer certainty while transferring the whole burden of harvest, price and collection failure onto the zamindar, and through him onto the cultivator.

**Must-write keywords:** Cornwallis 1793 | zamindar as proprietor | fixed in perpetuity | Sunset Law | estate sale on default | Bengal-Bihar-Orissa

**How to deploy them:** Name Cornwallis and 1793 first, then the settlement unit (the zamindari estate) and the region; make the Sunset Law the pivot of any mechanism question; and close by separating the zamindar's secured proprietorship from the cultivator's unsecured occupancy.

#### Core explanation

Land revenue is the mechanism through which the colonial state converted agrarian India into a predictable, extractable, cash-and-calendar revenue base, and the Permanent Settlement of 1793 is the first, most consequential, and most heavily tested of the three settlement systems this chapter must compare.

FACT: the Permanent Settlement was introduced in 1793 by Lord Cornwallis in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. It made the existing zamindars -- until then revenue collectors and intermediaries under Mughal and Company practice, not landowners in an unqualified sense -- into proprietors of the land, and it fixed the state's revenue demand on them permanently, in perpetuity, at the level then assessed. ANALYSIS: "permanent" describes the *state's demand on the zamindar*, not the rent the zamindar could charge cultivators, which is a distinct and uncapped relationship; this distinction is the single most tested conceptual point on this settlement and a frequent source of error.

FACT: the mechanism enforcing the fixed demand was strict and calendar-bound: failure to pay the assessed revenue by a specified date exposed the zamindari estate to sale, a mechanism conventionally described as the "Sunset Law" arrangement, and a substantial number of older zamindari houses did lose their estates to auction and to a newly emerging class of purchasers (including merchants and moneylenders who acquired zamindari rights) in the settlement's early decades. ANALYSIS: without stating a specific proportion or rate for this turnover (no precise, sourced figure is supported by the evidence), the mechanism itself is the examinable fact: a fixed, calendar-enforced cash demand, unresponsive to harvest conditions, that treated the zamindari right itself as a saleable, foreclosable asset.

FACT: the state's own stated aims combined fiscal predictability (a permanently known, easily budgeted revenue base, ending the earlier system's periodic and often disputed reassessments) with a Cornwallis-era belief, influenced by contemporary British agrarian thought, that a secure landed proprietorship would give zamindars an incentive to invest in agricultural improvement, mirroring the improving-landlord model then current in Britain. ANALYSIS: the outcome diverged sharply from this stated aim in the aggregate: because the state's own share was frozen while agricultural output and prices over time were not, the zamindar's *residual* income (rent collected minus the fixed state demand) could grow without any corresponding investment obligation, so the "improvement incentive" argument is best treated as a stated aim that produced, at most, an uneven and geographically patchy investment record rather than a systematic transformation.

FACT: below the zamindar, the actual cultivating peasantry held no correspondingly fixed or secure right; because the state's demand on the zamindar was frozen but the zamindar's own demand on tenants was not correspondingly capped in practice across most of the settlement's operation, an incentive toward rack-renting and the growth of layered sub-infeudation (intermediate rent-receiving interests inserted between zamindar and actual cultivator) followed over the nineteenth century, later addressed only partially and much later by tenancy legislation (a subject that belongs to this chapter only as an outcome, the detailed tenancy legislation belonging to administrative and legal history). ANALYSIS: describing the Permanent Settlement as having secured "peasant rights" is a direct error -- it secured zamindari property and state revenue; whatever tenancy protection peasants eventually gained came from later, separate legislative correction of the very insecurity this settlement created, not from the 1793 settlement itself.

TRAP: do not say the Permanent Settlement fixed the rent that peasants paid, or that it made peasants proprietors -- it fixed only the *state's* demand on the zamindar and made the *zamindar* the proprietor; do not state a specific turnover rate or percentage for Sunset Law estate sales, since no precise, sourced figure for this is supported by the evidence.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** The Permanent Settlement secured the state's revenue while shifting all agrarian risk downward.
- **Named evidence:** The demand was fixed in perpetuity for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1793 under Cornwallis, and the Sunset Law made non-payment by the appointed date grounds for sale of the estate.
- **Analysis:** Because the state could neither raise the demand in good years nor relax it in bad years, the zamindar absorbed every fluctuation and had strong incentive to pass it on as rent enhancement and sub-infeudation.
- **Qualification:** The settlement was not uniformly ruinous for zamindars: those who could meet the fixed demand gained long-run security from a demand that never rose, which is why the outcome is turnover and differentiation rather than uniform dispossession.

**Recall line:** The demand was fixed in perpetuity for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1793 under Cornwallis, and the Sunset Law made non-payment by the appointed date grounds for sale of the estate.

### Session 4: The Ryotwari Settlement: Read, Munro, And Direct Settlement With Uncertainty

```text
STATE  --- direct settlement --->  INDIVIDUAL RYOT
                                        |
        (no zamindar intermediary)      |
                                        v
                        PERIODIC SURVEY AND REASSESSMENT
                                        |
                        +---------------+---------------+
                        |                               |
                        v                               v
              CASH DEMAND ON A FIXED             POSSESSION IS
              CALENDAR, REVISABLE                CONDITIONAL ON
                        |                        PAYMENT
                        v                               |
              BAD SEASON -> borrow                      v
              -> moneylender -> land           NOT absolute,
                 alienation                    unconditional
                                               proprietorship
```

**Plain definition:** The Ryotwari settlement is the arrangement in which the state settled revenue directly with the individual cultivator, the ryot, without a zamindar intermediary.

**Technical definition:** It is a direct, individual-holding settlement in which the assessment unit is the ryot's holding, the demand is periodically revised by survey and assessment, and continued possession is conditional on payment rather than vested as unconditional ownership.

**Exam-ready opening:** Ryotwari removed the intermediary but not the pressure: by settling directly with the ryot on a periodically revised cash assessment, it made the cultivator the state's debtor of record and therefore the first person exposed to a bad season.

**Must-write keywords:** Thomas Munro | Alexander Read | individual ryot | periodic revision | Madras and Bombay | conditional occupancy

**How to deploy them:** Credit Read and Munro and place the system in Madras and Bombay; make the assessment unit (the individual ryot) and the revision pattern (periodic, by survey) the two load-bearing facts; and refuse the common overstatement that the ryot became an absolute proprietor.

#### Core explanation

FACT: the Ryotwari system, associated with Alexander Read's earlier experiments and developed and extended by Thomas Munro, was applied chiefly in the Madras and Bombay presidencies. Unlike the Permanent Settlement, it dispensed with a zamindar intermediary and settled the revenue demand directly with the individual cultivator (ryot), who held a registered right to the land subject to regular payment of the assessed revenue. ANALYSIS: "direct settlement with the cultivator" is the system's defining institutional feature and its most tested identifying fact, distinguishing it sharply from both the zamindar-intermediary model of the Permanent Settlement and the community/village-based model of Mahalwari.

FACT: Ryotwari revenue demand was not fixed in perpetuity; it was subject to periodic measurement (survey) and revision at fixed intervals, meaning the ryot faced recurring reassessment cycles rather than a permanently frozen demand. ANALYSIS: this periodic-revision feature is precisely why the state, not a zamindar, retained the ability to raise the demand over time, and why Ryotwari's own history includes recurring complaints of over-assessment and rigid collection regardless of harvest outcome in specific periods and regions.

FACT: because the demand was reassessed periodically and often set with limited responsiveness to actual harvest conditions in a given year, cultivators under Ryotwari faced high-demand and uncertainty problems of their own kind -- a fixed cash obligation due on a calendar regardless of the season's yield -- which, combined with the removal of any zamindar buffer, exposed the ryot directly to moneylender dependence in poor years. ANALYSIS: describing the ryot as an "absolute proprietor" free of colonial pressure is a direct overstatement; the ryot held a registered occupancy right conditional on regular payment, not an unconditional freehold, and the periodic-revision mechanism preserved the state's ability to extract a high demand over time.

TRAP: do not describe Ryotwari as pro-peasant simply because it removed the zamindar -- direct settlement removed one intermediary but retained, and in some respects sharpened, the state's own demand-setting power through periodic revision; do not assume that Ryotwari cultivators received automatic remission in every bad harvest. The officially dropped 2024 Prelims Q57 remains only a demand-control; the periodic-revision and high-demand mechanism above explains why blanket remission cannot be inferred.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** Ryotwari changed who bore the demand, not how heavy or how rigid the demand was.
- **Named evidence:** Associated with Alexander Read and Thomas Munro and applied largely in the Madras and Bombay presidencies, it assessed the individual ryot's holding and revised the assessment periodically by survey.
- **Analysis:** Removing the zamindar removed a layer of rent extraction but exposed the cultivator directly to a cash demand on a fixed calendar, which in a poor season converted into borrowing, indebtedness and land alienation.
- **Qualification:** Ryotwari is therefore not automatically pro-peasant, and the ryot's tenure was conditional on payment rather than an unconditional property right.

**Recall line:** Associated with Alexander Read and Thomas Munro and applied largely in the Madras and Bombay presidencies, it assessed the individual ryot's holding and revised the assessment periodically by survey.

### Session 5: The Mahalwari Settlement: Holt Mackenzie'S Framework And Bird'S Implementation

```text
THE MAHAL (village / estate)
                            |
        +-------------------+-------------------+
        |                   |                   |
   co-sharer            co-sharer           co-sharer
        \                  |                  /
         +-----------------+-----------------+
                           |
                 JOINT AND SEVERAL LIABILITY
                 for the whole mahal's demand
                           |
                           v
           PERIODIC REVISION by survey and record
                           |
                           v
   FRAMEWORK: Holt Mackenzie  ->  IMPLEMENTATION: R. M. Bird
   REGION: North-Western Provinces, and later Punjab
                           |
                           v
   RISK: a defaulting co-sharer's burden falls on the rest
```

**Plain definition:** The Mahalwari settlement is the arrangement in which revenue was settled on the village or estate -- the mahal -- as a unit, with the village body jointly responsible for payment.

**Technical definition:** It is a joint-liability settlement in which the assessment unit is the mahal, the demand is periodically revised, and liability falls collectively on the village community or its recognised co-sharers rather than on a single proprietor or a single cultivator.

**Exam-ready opening:** Mahalwari is best defined by its unit and its liability: assessment on the mahal with joint responsibility, which distributes risk across the village body instead of concentrating it on a zamindar or an individual ryot.

**Must-write keywords:** Holt Mackenzie | R. M. Bird | mahal as unit | joint liability | North-Western Provinces | periodic revision

**How to deploy them:** Attribute the framework to Holt Mackenzie and the large-scale implementation to Bird; name the mahal as the assessment unit and joint liability as the distinguishing feature; place it in the north-western provinces and Punjab; and note that periodic revision, not permanence, governed the demand.

#### Core explanation

FACT: the Mahalwari settlement originated in Holt Mackenzie's 1822 framework and was substantially implemented and revised by R.M. Bird, applied chiefly in the North-Western Provinces, Punjab and parts of Central India. Its defining unit of settlement was the village or mahal (estate) as a joint, collectively responsible body, rather than an individual zamindar-proprietor or an individual ryot. ANALYSIS: "village/mahal community, jointly liable" is the identifying fact that distinguishes Mahalwari from both other systems, and it is the fact most frequently tested in a three-way comparison question.

FACT: under Mahalwari, the revenue demand was assessed on the mahal as a whole, with the village community or its representative co-sharers (often drawn from dominant landholding lineages within the village) jointly and severally responsible for the total demand, and the assessment was subject to periodic revision in the same general manner as Ryotwari, rather than being fixed permanently as under the Permanent Settlement. ANALYSIS: joint liability meant that if some co-sharers defaulted, the remaining members of the mahal could be held liable for the shortfall, transferring collection risk onto the internal solidarity (or coercive capacity) of the village community itself, rather than onto a single zamindar or a single ryot.

ANALYSIS: Mahalwari is sometimes loosely described as "retaining traditional village community structures," and there is a defensible sense in which it preserved existing co-sharing bodies as the unit of assessment rather than replacing them with either a single zamindar or a mass of atomised individual ryots; but the periodic-revision mechanism and the colonial state's own role in fixing and enforcing the mahal's aggregate demand mean this preservation operated within, and in the service of, an externally imposed cash-revenue framework, not as an untouched survival of pre-colonial village autonomy.

TRAP: do not say Mahalwari settled directly and solely with individual peasants -- its defining unit is the village/mahal community under joint liability; do not confuse Holt Mackenzie's 1822 framework (the design) with R.M. Bird's implementation and revision (the operational rollout) -- both names are independently testable, and a question may ask for either the framework's originator or its principal field administrator.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** Mahalwari's distinguishing features are its assessment unit and its liability rule, not its geography alone.
- **Named evidence:** Holt Mackenzie supplied the framework and R. M. Bird carried out the large-scale settlement operations in the North-Western Provinces; the mahal was the unit and liability was joint.
- **Analysis:** Joint liability spread default risk across the village body, which could buffer an individual bad year but also meant that one co-sharer's failure raised the effective burden on the others.
- **Qualification:** Mahalwari was periodically revised, not permanent, and did not restore any pre-colonial village autonomy; it was a colonial revenue instrument using a village-level unit.

**Recall line:** Holt Mackenzie supplied the framework and R. M. Bird carried out the large-scale settlement operations in the North-Western Provinces; the mahal was the unit and liability was joint.

### Session 6: Comparative Synthesis Of The Three Land-Revenue Systems

```text
AXIS          PERMANENT      RYOTWARI        MAHALWARI
-----------   ------------   -------------   --------------
architect     Cornwallis     Read / Munro    Mackenzie/Bird
region        Bengal,        Madras,         North-Western
              Bihar,         Bombay          Provinces,
              Orissa                         Punjab
unit          zamindari      individual      mahal (village
              estate         ryot holding    / estate)
revision      fixed in       periodic        periodic
              perpetuity
intermediary  zamindar       none            village body /
                                             co-sharers
risk carrier  zamindar,      ryot            village body
              then tenant                    jointly
-----------------------------------------------------------
COMMON CORE: a secure CASH demand on a fixed calendar
NO system covered all India; all changed in practice
```

**Plain definition:** Comparative synthesis means holding the three settlements apart on the same set of axes so that each can be matched to its own architect, region, unit, revision pattern and risk carrier.

**Technical definition:** The comparison is conducted on five controlled variables -- architect, region, assessment unit, revision pattern and the party bearing residual risk -- with a common core, the secure cash revenue demand, held constant across all three.

**Exam-ready opening:** The three settlements differ in unit, revision and intermediary structure, but converge on one purpose: a secure, calendar-bound cash revenue demand for the colonial state, with the residual risk placed on a different shoulder in each case.

**Must-write keywords:** assessment unit | revision pattern | risk carrier | intermediary structure | common cash core | no all-India system

**How to deploy them:** Answer any comparative question on the five axes in a fixed order -- architect, region, unit, revision, risk carrier -- then state the common cash core, and finish by rejecting the claim that any one settlement covered all India.

#### Core explanation

FACT: the three systems differed on every structural dimension that matters for an examination comparison -- the legal unit settled with (zamindar-proprietor; individual ryot; village/mahal community), the presence or absence of an intermediary (zamindar present; no intermediary; village co-sharers acting collectively), the revision pattern (permanently fixed; periodically revised; periodically revised), and the region of application (Bengal-Bihar-Orissa; Madras-Bombay; North-Western Provinces-Punjab-parts of Central India). ANALYSIS: despite these structural differences, all three agreed on the same underlying function -- a demand that was cash-denominated, calendar-bound and enforceable ultimately by sale or dispossession of the defaulting party's interest, whether that party was a zamindar, an individual ryot, or a village mahal.

FACT: each system's own "incentive" rationale, as stated by its colonial architects, diverged from its dominant recorded outcome. The Permanent Settlement's improving-landlord incentive produced an uneven investment record alongside rack-renting and sub-infeudation below the zamindar; Ryotwari's direct-settlement rationale (removing a supposedly extractive zamindar intermediary) produced its own high-demand and over-assessment pressure through periodic revision; Mahalwari's community-based rationale produced joint-liability pressure that could fall hardest on weaker co-sharers within the same village. ANALYSIS: the strongest comparative Mains answer states this convergence explicitly -- three different intermediary structures, one shared cash-calendar-sale logic, and in each case an outcome that diverged from the architects' own stated incentive claim.

FACT: regional variation compounds this comparison -- the permanently settled eastern zone, the ryotwari south and west, and the mahalwari north together produced a genuinely uneven agrarian map, later overlaid by distinct commercial-crop belts (Session 7) that did not map neatly onto the revenue-system boundaries. ANALYSIS: a "compare the three systems" question rewards naming this regional overlay explicitly, since revenue-system boundaries and commercial-crop-belt boundaries are two separate maps that a strong answer keeps analytically distinct even while noting where they interact (for example, the Deccan cotton belt, discussed in Session 8, sat within Ryotwari-administered territory).

TRAP: never present any one of the three settlements as simply "good for peasants" or "bad for peasants" in an undifferentiated way -- each produced a different specific mechanism of peasant vulnerability (sub-infeudation and rack-renting under Permanent Settlement; high/uncertain demand under Ryotwari; joint-liability exposure under Mahalwari), and naming the specific mechanism, not a generic verdict, is what earns marks.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** The three settlements are differentiated by unit, revision and risk carrier, but unified by a cash revenue imperative.
- **Named evidence:** Permanent Settlement fixed the zamindari estate demand in perpetuity in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; Ryotwari assessed the individual ryot periodically in Madras and Bombay; Mahalwari assessed the mahal periodically with joint liability in the north-west.
- **Analysis:** Holding the axes constant exposes the real pattern: the colonial state varied its instrument by region and administrative theory while never varying its requirement for reliable cash revenue.
- **Qualification:** None of the three covered the whole subcontinent, and all three were modified in practice by later legislation and revision, so a static all-India map of settlements is historically wrong.

**Recall line:** Permanent Settlement fixed the zamindari estate demand in perpetuity in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; Ryotwari assessed the individual ryot periodically in Madras and Bombay; Mahalwari assessed the mahal periodically with joint liability in the north-west.

**Plain definition:** Commercialisation of agriculture means the shift from cultivation primarily for subsistence and local exchange towards cultivation for distant and export markets, paid for and settled in cash.

**Technical definition:** It is the reorganisation of cropping decisions around market price and credit obligation rather than household consumption, driven by cash revenue demand, advance-based contracts and export demand, and transmitting price and harvest risk to the cultivator.

**Exam-ready opening:** Commercialisation in colonial India was not simply the arrival of markets; it was the arrival of markets on terms where the cultivator carried the price and harvest risk while the revenue claim and the creditor's claim remained fixed.

**Must-write keywords:** cash revenue demand | advance system | cash crops | agrarian indebtedness | land alienation | price and harvest risk

**How to deploy them:** Start from the cash revenue calendar as the compulsion, then name the advance system and the specific cash crops, then show the credit chain, and close on indebtedness and land alienation as the measurable outcome rather than on 'exploitation' as a label.

#### Core explanation

The cash-and-calendar revenue demand established across all three land systems (Sessions 3-6) is the direct structural reason cultivators needed a reliable source of cash, and commercialisation of agriculture -- the shift from subsistence-oriented cultivation toward market-oriented, often export-oriented, cash-crop production -- is the mechanism through which peasants across very different regions were drawn into that need.

FACT: indigo, cotton, opium, jute and tea were the major commercial crops linking Indian cultivation to global and imperial markets, alongside continuing food-crop production; indigo was concentrated in Bengal and Bihar, cotton chiefly in the Deccan (Ryotwari-administered territory, Session 6), opium chiefly in eastern India (Bihar/Bengal, under a colonial state monopoly system geared toward the China trade), jute in lower Bengal, and tea in Assam under a plantation-labour system distinct from peasant cash-cropping. ANALYSIS: naming this regional crop map is necessary to avoid treating "commercialisation" as one undifferentiated national process -- the coercion, credit relationship and market risk each crop's cultivators faced differed by crop and region, even though the underlying cash-need mechanism was shared.

FACT: commercialisation was not inherently harmful in principle -- market access can raise cultivator incomes where prices are favourable and credit terms fair -- but the colonial-era terms under which it occurred systematically skewed risk onto the cultivator: advance (dadan) systems tied cultivators to specific crops and specific buyers (often European planters or their agents) before a harvest was in, planter- or trader-set prices frequently did not track the cultivator's own production costs or global price movements, and global-market dependence exposed cultivators to price collapses (a shock originating entirely outside their own control) with no corresponding downward flexibility in their revenue obligation to the state. ANALYSIS: the defensible thesis is therefore not "commercialisation was good" or "commercialisation was bad" in the abstract, but that colonial-era market integration combined with a rigid, externally-fixed revenue demand and coercive advance/credit arrangements to concentrate price and production risk on the cultivator while limiting the corresponding upside.

FACT: credit dependence deepened alongside commercialisation -- cultivators borrowed against a standing crop or against land itself to meet advance obligations, revenue due-dates, or subsistence gaps between harvests, and moneylenders (often, though not exclusively, distinct from planters and traders) became a structural feature of the commercialised countryside. ANALYSIS: this credit relationship, layered onto a fixed cash-revenue demand and a price-taking position in volatile commodity markets, is the direct structural link between land-revenue policy (Sessions 3-6) and the specific agrarian protests examined next (Session 8) -- indebtedness, not any single crop failure, is the common thread connecting otherwise distinct regional movements.

TRAP: do not state that colonial commercialisation was uniformly exploitative with no cultivator gain, nor that it was a neutral, mutually beneficial modernisation -- the examinable claim is that colonial-era terms (coercive advances, price-setting power concentrated with planters/traders, and a rigid revenue demand that did not fall when crop prices fell) skewed risk onto the cultivator; commercialisation as such is not being asserted here as inherently harmful.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** Commercialisation shifted risk downward rather than simply expanding opportunity.
- **Named evidence:** Cash revenue fell due on a fixed calendar, advances tied the cultivator's crop choice in advance of harvest, and cash crops such as indigo, opium, cotton, jute and oilseeds were grown for distant markets.
- **Analysis:** Because the revenue claim and the credit claim were fixed in nominal terms while the cultivator's realisation depended on price and yield, any adverse shock was absorbed entirely at the bottom of the chain.
- **Qualification:** Commercialisation was not uniformly impoverishing: some regions, crops and cultivators with better irrigation, tenure or market access did benefit, so the defensible claim concerns the distribution of risk, not a blanket ruin.

**Recall line:** Cash revenue fell due on a fixed calendar, advances tied the cultivator's crop choice in advance of harvest, and cash crops such as indigo, opium, cotton, jute and oilseeds were grown for distant markets.

```text
      (unequal access to British

```

**Plain definition:** Deindustrialisation means a decline in the output, employment or bargaining position of Indian handicraft manufacture relative to its earlier weight, not the disappearance of Indian industry.

**Technical definition:** It is a multi-causal outcome produced by the interaction of mechanised British production, asymmetric tariff and market access, the collapse of courtly and elite patronage, and later railway-borne penetration of interior markets, with substantial regional and craft-level variation.

**Exam-ready opening:** Deindustrialisation is best argued as a causal web rather than a single cause: mechanised British production supplied the capacity, tariff asymmetry and political power supplied the access, court decline removed the high-end demand, and the railways delivered the imports inland.

**Must-write keywords:** causal web | tariff asymmetry | machine-made yarn and cloth | loss of court patronage | railway penetration | uneven and craft-specific

**How to deploy them:** Present deindustrialisation as a causal web, not one cause: name tariff asymmetry, competition from machine-made yarn and cloth, loss of court patronage and railway penetration as separate strands, attach a mechanism to each, then close on decline that was uneven and craft-specific, which is what refuses the total-extinction claim.

#### Core explanation

Given the pre-colonial baseline established in Session 2 -- a substantial, export-competitive textile and handicraft economy -- deindustrialisation names the specific nineteenth-century reversal of that position, and this chapter must present it as a policy-structured encounter, not a story of technology alone.

FACT: several mechanisms combined. Machine-made British textiles, produced at falling unit costs, entered the Indian market under a trade regime that, following the end of the Company's monopoly (1813) and subsequent free-trade policy, did not protect Indian producers with a comparable tariff; Indian exports of manufactured cotton goods correspondingly lost ground both in external markets and, more significantly for employment, within India's own domestic market to imported cloth. ANALYSIS: this asymmetry -- Indian goods entering Britain facing British tariffs and constraints while British goods entered India with minimal protective duty -- is the specific "asymmetric tariff" mechanism that a strong answer must name rather than leaving as a vague reference to "free trade."

FACT: alongside trade policy, the political conquest and decline of Indian courts (Mughal successor states, regional kingdoms and their aristocracies) progressively removed a major source of demand for specialised, high-value artisanal production that such courts had previously patronised, an independent cause of craft decline operating even where import competition was not the immediate mechanism. ANALYSIS: this loss-of-patronage channel is important precisely because it shows deindustrialisation was not solely a trade-competition story -- political conquest itself removed demand directly, a mechanism the Industrial-Revolution "how far" framing (Session 10) must explicitly credit as a colonial-political variable, not an English-industrial one.

FACT: railway expansion and improved internal communications, discussed fully as infrastructure in Sessions 14-15, had a further, double-edged deindustrialising effect once built from the mid-nineteenth century onward: they widened the reach of imported machine-made goods into the Indian interior, intensifying competition faced by local artisans who had previously been partially insulated from coastal import competition by high internal transport costs. ANALYSIS: this places the railway network's deindustrialising role later in the century than the initial 1813-onward tariff-asymmetry mechanism, and a precise chronological answer should distinguish the earlier trade-policy phase from this later transport-driven intensification phase rather than treating "deindustrialisation" as a single-dated event.

TRAP: do not attribute deindustrialisation to "machine competition" alone -- tariff asymmetry, the loss of court patronage, and later railway-driven market penetration are three separate, independently examinable mechanisms; do not assert a specific employment or output percentage for the decline -- Morris D. Morris and, on census evidence, Daniel Thorner questioned the scale of the decline (Session 20), so exact magnitudes remain debated and must not be invented.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** Deindustrialisation is a causal web, and a single-cause answer is analytically weaker than a multi-strand one.
- **Named evidence:** Mechanised British production, asymmetric tariff and market access, the loss of court and elite patronage after the decline of Indian courts, and railway penetration of interior markets each operated through a distinct channel.
- **Analysis:** The strands are complementary rather than competing: capacity without access would not have displaced Indian cloth, and access without inland transport would have left interior markets largely untouched.
- **Qualification:** The decline was uneven by region, craft and period; coarse cloth and many village crafts persisted, so the claim is relative decline, not extinction.

**Recall line:** Mechanised British production, asymmetric tariff and market access, the loss of court and elite patronage after the decline of Indian courts, and railway penetration of interior markets each operated through a distinct channel.

FACT: England's Industrial Revolution supplied a genuine, necessary external condition -- mechanised spinning and weaving that sharply lowered the unit cost of cloth production, alongside British industry's own growing need for Indian raw materials (raw cotton in particular) and for an expanding overseas market for its finished goods. ANALYSIS: this is the "necessary but not sufficient" half of the balanced verdict -- English mechanisation set the price at which Indian handicrafts had to compete, which is a real, independent, non-colonial-policy variable that any answer must credit.

FACT: what converted this technological/commercial pressure into structural Indian decline was colonial political power: an asymmetric tariff regime that did not protect Indian producers the way Britain protected its own emerging industries in earlier phases of its own development; the absence of any Indian industrial or protective-tariff policy under colonial administration; the loss of court patronage (Session 9); and, later in the century, railway-borne market penetration into the Indian interior. FACT: the decline was not uniform across crafts or regions -- some crafts adapted to surviving niche or local markets, some regions retained stronger craft continuity than others, and the debate over the decline's exact scale (Session 20) means a defensible answer avoids an absolute "total destruction of Indian handicrafts" claim. TRAP: an answer that says only "the Industrial Revolution destroyed Indian handicrafts" fails to engage the demand word "how far," which explicitly asks for a distribution of responsibility, not a single-cause narrative; an answer that credits English industrialisation alone, without naming at least the tariff-asymmetry and lost-patronage colonial variables, will not earn full marks on this exact, previously-asked question.

**Recall line:** The Industrial Revolution supplied cheaper machine-made goods, but their effect on India ran through tariff asymmetry, colonial control of commercial policy, the decline of court patronage and railway access to interior markets.

**Plain definition:** The drain of wealth is the unilateral transfer of Indian resources to Britain for which India received no equivalent economic return.

**Technical definition:** It is a transfer concept, not a trade concept: it names identifiable institutional channels -- home charges, official salaries and pensions, interest and profit on British capital, stores purchases and imperial military expenditure charged to Indian revenues -- through which a claim on Indian resources was discharged abroad.

**Exam-ready opening:** The drain argument is strongest when it names channels rather than totals: home charges, remittances of official income, guaranteed returns on British capital and imperially determined expenditure charged to India constitute an obligation that left India without a corresponding inward return.

**Must-write keywords:** unilateral transfer | home charges | official remittances | interest and profit | stores and military charges | channels not totals

**How to deploy them:** Define the drain as a unilateral transfer, then list its channels one by one -- home charges, official remittances, interest and profit, stores and military charges -- showing that each was a claim discharged abroad out of Indian revenue or Indian export earnings; argue channels not totals, and never attach an invented figure.

#### Core explanation

FACT: the drain of wealth names a unilateral transfer of Indian resources to Britain without a corresponding economic return to India -- a transfer for which India received no goods, services or investment income back in exchange, as distinct from an ordinary commercial export for which India would receive payment. ANALYSIS: this "unilateral transfer without equivalent return" definition is the precise technical content of "drain," and an answer that merely says "the British took wealth from India" without specifying the *unilateral, unrequited* character of the transfer is incomplete.

FACT: this chapter distinguishes early plunder from systemic drain as two related but different phenomena. Early plunder refers to the direct, often coercive extraction of Bengal's treasury and revenue in the conquest and Dual Government period following 1757-65 -- the Bengal-conquest background's own material, named here only as the drain mechanism's historical origin point. Systemic drain refers to the ongoing, institutionalised transfer that continued throughout Company and then Crown rule, operating through regular administrative and financial channels rather than direct seizure. ANALYSIS: a strong answer explicitly separates these two -- the early period supplied the drain's origin and its first, most visible form, while the systemic mechanism is what made the drain a continuing structural feature of the colonial economy for well over a century, not a one-time event.

FACT: the drain's channels, as documented in the evidence used here, included home charges (payments made in Britain on India's account, covering costs of the India Office and related administration), pensions and salaries of British officials (both those serving in India and, on retirement, drawing pensions remitted to Britain), profits and interest payments on British capital invested in India, the cost of stores purchased in Britain for Indian administrative and military use, and military/imperial expenditure charged to Indian revenues (including costs connected to Britain's wider imperial military commitments, where charged to India). ANALYSIS: naming this specific channel list -- home charges, official salaries/pensions, capital profits/interest, stores, military-imperial expenditure -- rather than a vague reference to "extraction," is what converts a general drain claim into an examinable, evidence-based answer.

FACT: the exact quantum of the drain is genuinely disputed among historians and was disputed even among contemporary estimators, and the evidence used here explicitly withholds any specific percentage or amount rather than asserting one. ANALYSIS: the defensible position is to write about the drain's *channels and political effect* -- never a percentage or a sum -- and to note that the drain argument concerns the *net direction* of transfer (a persistent, unrequited outflow), not a claim that no investment of any kind ever flowed into India.

TRAP: do not treat "drain theory" as referring only to the early conquest-period plunder -- it names an ongoing systemic transfer operating through home charges, salaries/pensions, capital profits/interest, stores and military-imperial expenditure across the colonial period; do not state any specific percentage, amount, or proportion of Indian income as "the drain" -- no such figure is sourced here, and the exact quantum remains a disputed question this chapter does not resolve with an invented number.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** The drain is defensible as a channel argument and indefensible as an arithmetic one.
- **Named evidence:** The named channels -- home charges and India Office costs, official salaries and pensions, private remittances, interest and profit on British capital including guaranteed returns, stores purchased abroad, and imperial military and external expenditure charged to Indian revenues -- are institutionally documented categories.
- **Analysis:** Each channel represents a claim on Indian resources settled outside India, which is what distinguishes a transfer from ordinary two-way trade in which value returns in goods.
- **Qualification:** The magnitude of the drain remains disputed among historians and depends heavily on what is counted as a legitimate administrative cost, so the size is left unquantified here by deliberate discipline, not by omission.

**Recall line:** The named channels -- home charges and India Office costs, official salaries and pensions, private remittances, interest and profit on British capital including guaranteed returns, stores purchased abroad, and imperial military and external expenditure charged to Indian revenues -- are institutionally documented categories.

ANALYSIS: the exam-safe move is to name the mechanism -- export surplus financing home charges via a bill/remittance system rather than returning as bullion -- without attaching an invented number to it.

FACT: on currency and exchange policy specifically, this chapter's own verified evidence is narrower and more precise than a general "the rupee was manipulated" claim: Gandhi's eleven-point ultimatum to Irwin (31 January 1930) explicitly demanded lowering the rupee-sterling exchange rate to 1 shilling 4 pence, alongside protective tariffs on foreign cloth and reservation of coastal shipping for Indian companies. ANALYSIS: the safe general claim is that the colonial fiscal system combined a land-revenue base with growing indirect and, later, direct taxation, financed a public debt serving both infrastructure and general imperial purposes, and operated throughout within the same drain/remittance logic already established.

- **Qualification:** Conceding real unintended benefits does not concede developmental intent; the defensible verdict is modern infrastructure without autonomous developmental priority.

**Recall line:** Railways were financed through the Guarantee System, under which private British capital received an assured return with any shortfall met from Indian revenues; forest control was built through the 1864 Forest Department, the Government Forests Act of 1865 and the Indian Forest Act of 1878, with reserved, protected and unclassified categories.

**Exam-ready opening:** Famine analysis must separate the trigger from the amplifier: drought or monsoon failure supplied the shock, but rigid revenue demands, commercialised risk, price-directed grain movement and a restrictive relief doctrine converted scarcity into mass mortality.

#### Core explanation

This subtopic builds the causal frame needed to solve the exact 2022 GS-I Mains demand -- "Why was there a sudden spurt in famines in colonial India since the mid-eighteenth century? Give reasons." -- solved as PYQ 01 in the PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE section.

FACT: colonial-era famines were failures of entitlement and governance, not simply failures of rainfall; this chapter's own sourced position explicitly frames famine causation as combining a genuine climatic trigger (drought, harvest failure) with a set of colonial-era structural vulnerabilities that converted a harvest shock into mass suffering on a scale earlier, less commercialised and less rigidly taxed agrarian regimes had not experienced in the same way. ANALYSIS: "spurt in famines since the mid-eighteenth century" is best explained as the arrival of new, colonial-era vulnerability mechanisms layered onto a pre-existing climatic risk that South Asian agriculture had always faced, rather than as evidence that droughts themselves became more frequent or severe for climatic reasons alone.

FACT: the specific vulnerability mechanisms this chapter names are: a rigid, cash-denominated revenue demand (Sessions 3-6) that did not fall when harvests failed, forcing continued extraction of payment precisely when cultivators' capacity to pay was lowest; commercialisation and export-oriented cropping (Sessions 7-8) that could reduce the local food-grain buffer available in a bad year; railway-enabled market integration (Sessions 14-15) that, under a price mechanism rather than a needs-based allocation, could move grain toward paying demand (including export demand) even from a locality experiencing scarcity, rather than guaranteeing food moved toward the area of greatest need; and a laissez-faire relief ideology that regarded direct interference in grain trade and markets, and generous relief provision, as economically unsound and likely to encourage dependency. ANALYSIS: naming all four mechanisms -- rigid revenue, commercialisation/export exposure, the transport-paradox (railways moving grain by price signal, not by need), and laissez-faire relief doctrine -- together is what distinguishes a genuine entitlement-and-governance argument from a vague "colonialism caused famines" claim.

FACT: administrative delay in recognising and responding to a developing famine, and the interaction between famine conditions and public health (epidemic disease accompanying and compounding famine mortality, a documented feature of major colonial-era famines), further shaped outcomes once a climatic shock had occurred; this chapter explicitly withholds any mortality figure for any famine, following its own factual-risk caution. ANALYSIS: because relief practice, administrative capacity and epidemic risk all varied across the different famine episodes examined in Session 18, a single fixed "colonial famine mechanism" should be understood as a recurring combination of these elements, whose relative weight differed from famine to famine and region to region.

FACT: an "export caused the famine" argument taken alone is a monocausal oversimplification this chapter must avoid; export-oriented cropping and grain movement toward paying demand are genuine contributing mechanisms within the broader entitlement failure, operating alongside the revenue-rigidity and relief-doctrine mechanisms above, not as a single, sufficient explanation on their own. ANALYSIS: the strongest famine-causation answer states climatic trigger plus a named set of colonial-era structural amplifiers, explicitly avoiding both a purely climatic ("just bad rains") account and a purely commercial ("food was simply exported away") account.

TRAP: do not cite a mortality figure, a population-share figure, or a precise export-volume figure for any famine discussed in this chapter -- this chapter's own sourced position explicitly withholds these; do not argue that grain exports alone "caused" any famine -- state the fuller entitlement/governance mechanism (rigid revenue, commercialisation exposure, transport-by-price-signal, laissez-faire relief doctrine) instead of a single-cause export argument.

#### Named-evidence paragraph

- **Claim:** Famine mortality is explained by entitlement failure, not by absolute food disappearance alone.
- **Named evidence:** Cash revenue fell due on a fixed calendar irrespective of the harvest; commercialisation had replaced local food buffers with cash crops and credit; grain moved along price signals rather than toward deficit areas; and relief doctrine restricted and delayed intervention before later reform.
- **Analysis:** Each amplifier attacks a different buffer -- savings, stored food, local availability and public support -- so their combination removes every fallback simultaneously and converts a survivable shortfall into a catastrophe.
- **Qualification:** This does not claim colonial rule invented famine in India, nor that climate was irrelevant; and no mortality figure is asserted, because the contemporary estimates vary widely and are disputed.

**Recall line:** Cash revenue fell due on a fixed calendar irrespective of the harvest; commercialisation had replaced local food buffers with cash crops and credit; grain moved along price signals rather than toward deficit areas; and relief doctrine restricted and delayed intervention before later reform.

ANALYSIS: this paradox statement is the most efficient single-sentence answer to any "was British rule good or bad for the Indian economy" framing, since it avoids both an uncritical "modernisation was purely beneficial" reading and an equally uncritical "nothing modern happened" reading.

### Q1. Which statement best expresses the defensible periodisation of British economic impact?

A. The dominant extraction mechanism changed across three overlapping phases -- conquest-period revenue extraction, free-trade restructuring after 1813, and late-colonial railway-borne integration -- while the direction of transfer stayed constant
B. A single unchanging extraction pattern operated from 1757 to 1947 with no internal phase distinction
C. An initial developmental phase was followed by an extractive phase only after 1857
D. Economic impact was confined to the twentieth century and had no eighteenth or nineteenth-century roots

**Answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The periodisation is stated as a shift in the dominant mechanism, dated to the 1813 end of the Company's Indian trade monopoly and to railway construction from the 1850s, with the phases explicitly overlapping.

**Trap logic:** Do not convert 'overlapping phases' into 'sealed eras'; revenue extraction did not stop in 1813 and craft decline continued after the 1850s.

### Q2. Which statement accurately describes the pre-colonial economic baseline?

A. As a claim that India had no significant textile production before colonial rule
B. As a narrow, comparative export-sector claim -- India was a leading exporter of cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, opium, sugar, rice, spices and precious stones -- without any claim about universal prosperity
C. As a claim that the pre-colonial agrarian economy involved no market exchange at all
D. As a golden age of universal prosperity and social equality across India

**Answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The baseline is stated narrowly because only a real export base can be eroded; that is what makes 'deindustrialisation' the correct term rather than 'failure to industrialise'.

**Trap logic:** A golden-age framing is both unevidenced and unnecessary -- it weakens the argument it is meant to support.

### Q3. Which feature of the Permanent Settlement of 1793 converted the state's revenue certainty into dispossession risk for the zamindar?

A. A statutory ceiling on the rent that zamindars could demand from cultivators
B. The periodic resurvey and reassessment of every zamindari estate
C. The Sunset Law, under which failure to pay the fixed demand by the appointed date exposed the estate itself to sale
D. A requirement that zamindars reside permanently on their estates

**Answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Cornwallis fixed the demand in perpetuity for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, and enforced it through sale of the estate on default -- security for the exchequer, risk for the proprietor.

**Trap logic:** Do not attribute periodic reassessment to the Permanent Settlement; permanence of the demand is its defining feature.

### Q4. What is the assessment unit under the Ryotwari settlement?

A. The zamindari estate
B. The mahal, that is the village or estate taken as a whole
C. The district treasury circle
D. The individual ryot's holding, settled directly with the state without an intermediary

**Answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Ryotwari, associated with Alexander Read and Thomas Munro and applied largely in Madras and Bombay, settled directly with the individual cultivator on a periodically revised assessment.

**Trap logic:** Removing the zamindar did not make the ryot an absolute proprietor: possession remained conditional on payment.

### Q5. Which feature distinguishes the Mahalwari settlement from the other two?

A. Assessment on the mahal with joint liability of the village body or its co-sharers
B. A demand fixed in perpetuity and never revised
C. Direct settlement with each individual cultivator with no collective element
D. Assessment by the Company's commercial residents rather than revenue officers

**Answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Holt Mackenzie supplied the framework and R. M. Bird carried out the large settlement operations in the North-Western Provinces; the mahal was the unit and liability was joint.

**Trap logic:** Mahalwari was periodically revised, not permanent, and did not restore pre-colonial village autonomy.

### Q6. On which set of axes should the three land-revenue settlements be compared?

A. Rate of assessment, currency used, and language of the record
B. Architect, region, assessment unit, revision pattern and the party bearing residual risk
C. Crop grown, irrigation source and caste of the cultivator
D. Date of enactment alone

**Answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Holding these five axes constant exposes the real pattern: the instrument varied by region and administrative theory while the requirement for secure cash revenue did not.

**Trap logic:** No settlement covered all India and all three were modified in practice, so a static all-India settlement map is historically wrong.

### Q7. What is the defensible charge against the commercialisation of agriculture?

A. That markets for agricultural produce did not exist in India before colonial rule
B. That commercialisation uniformly impoverished every cultivator in every region
C. That it shifted price and harvest risk downward, because the cash revenue claim and the creditor's claim stayed fixed while the cultivator's realisation varied
D. That cash crops were physically incapable of being grown in Indian conditions

**Answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The compulsion begins with a cash demand on a fixed calendar; advances and moneylender credit then tie the crop, so any price or harvest shock is absorbed entirely at the bottom of the chain.

**Trap logic:** Some regions, crops and cultivators genuinely gained -- the defensible claim concerns the distribution of risk, not blanket ruin.

### Q8. Which pairing of episode, adversary and repertoire is correct?

A. Deccan 1875 -- European planters -- refusal to sow
B. Indigo 1859-60 -- moneylenders -- destruction of debt bonds
C. Pabna from 1873 -- European planters -- refusal to sow
D. Indigo 1859-60 -- European planters -- collective refusal to sow; Pabna from 1873 -- zamindars -- leagues and litigation; Deccan 1875 -- moneylenders -- seizure of debt bonds

**Answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Each repertoire is the rational form of action against that specific adversary: a crop boycott defeats an advance contract, litigation defeats an illegal rent demand, and destroying bonds defeats a documentary debt.

**Trap logic:** These three are economic-response case studies here; their fuller political history belongs to the wider peasant and worker movement context and should be handed off, not narrated.

### Q9. Which formulation of deindustrialisation is examination-safe?

A. A relative decline in the output, employment or bargaining position of Indian handicrafts, produced by mechanised British production, tariff and market asymmetry, loss of court patronage and railway penetration, and varying by region, craft and period
B. The complete extinction of all Indian manufacturing by the mid-nineteenth century
C. A synonym for India's failure to experience an industrial revolution
D. A process caused solely by the arrival of the railways

**Answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Deindustrialisation is a causal web: capacity without access would not have displaced Indian cloth, and access without inland transport would have left interior markets largely untouched.

**Trap logic:** The total-extinction claim is the single most common way this answer loses marks; coarse cloth and many village crafts persisted.

### Q10. How should a 'how far' question such as the 2024 GS-I handicrafts question be structured?

A. By rejecting the premise outright and arguing that the stated factor had no effect
B. By conceding the stated factor as a necessary condition, naming the transmission mechanisms through which it operated, weighing the independent contribution of colonial policy, and returning a graded verdict
C. By accepting the premise fully and attributing the entire outcome to it
D. By narrating the chronology of British industrialisation without reaching a verdict

**Answer: B.**

**Explanation:** 'How far' asks for a distribution of responsibility. Conceding necessity and then applying the counterfactual of tariff autonomy is what allows independent weight to be assigned to colonial policy.

**Trap logic:** A binary yes/no verdict fails the directive however well-informed the content is.

### Q11. Why must artisan displacement and de-urbanisation be kept as separate claims?

A. Because de-urbanisation occurred only after 1900 and displacement only before it
B. Because displacement is a claim about towns and de-urbanisation is a claim about crafts
C. Because displacement is a claim about occupational structure and de-urbanisation is a claim about settlement pattern, and the same colonial pressures produced urban decline in some centres and urban growth in port and railway towns
D. Because only one of the two is mentioned in the official syllabus

**Answer: C.**

**Explanation:** A merged claim cannot express simultaneous decline of court and craft towns alongside the growth of port and railway towns, which is precisely what the evidence shows.

**Trap logic:** Contemporary data for both are thin and regionally uneven, so argue qualitatively and do not invent workforce shares or town populations.

### Q12. Which list correctly names the institutional channels of the drain of wealth?

A. Customs duties, excise duties, land revenue and stamp duty
B. Famine relief expenditure, canal construction and primary education grants
C. Local municipal taxation, district board cess and village common funds
D. Home charges and India Office costs, official salaries and pensions, private remittances, interest and profit on British capital including guaranteed returns, stores purchased abroad, and imperial military and external expenditure charged to Indian revenues

**Answer: D.**

**Explanation:** The drain is a transfer concept: each channel is a claim on Indian resources discharged in Britain, which is what distinguishes it from ordinary two-way trade in which value returns as goods.

**Trap logic:** Name the channels and stop. The magnitude of the drain is disputed among historians and no total, annual figure or percentage should be asserted.

### Q13. Which attribution of works to the nationalist economic writers is correct?

A. Dadabhai Naoroji wrote 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'; R. C. Dutt wrote 'The Economic History of India'; M. G. Ranade argued for Indian industrial development
B. R. C. Dutt wrote 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'; Dadabhai Naoroji wrote 'The Economic History of India'
C. M. G. Ranade wrote both 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' and 'The Economic History of India'
D. Dadabhai Naoroji wrote 'The Economic History of India'; M. G. Ranade wrote 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'

**Answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Naoroji centres the drain, Dutt reconstructs land-revenue and agrarian pressure from official records, and Ranade argues for industrial development and a constructive state role -- three distinguishable arguments, not one.

**Trap logic:** Swapping the two books between Naoroji and Dutt is a standard Prelims distractor; the organised political use of this critique belongs to the nationalist-politics context.

### Q14. What does the Council Bill mechanism demonstrate about India's export surplus?

A. That the surplus proves India gained from colonial trade
B. That sterling raised in London against rupee funds in India allowed the surplus to settle home charges and other external obligations, so a recorded surplus need not mean resources remained available in India
C. That India ran a persistent deficit in visible merchandise trade throughout the colonial period
D. That the surplus was invested entirely in Indian irrigation and education

**Answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The mechanism closes the loop between the drain's channels and the trade account by showing how a surplus visible in trade statistics could be consumed by invisible obligations before reaching the Indian economy.

**Trap logic:** Do not attach an adopted rupee-sterling ratio to this answer. The 1s 4d figure belongs only to Gandhi's 1930 eleven-point demand; the Hilton-Young adopted rate is treated here as unverified.

### Q15. Which pairing correctly describes colonial infrastructure and forest administration?

A. Railways were financed entirely from Indian private capital with no assured return
B. The Indian Forest Act of 1878 preceded the creation of the Forest Department
C. Railways were financed through the Guarantee System, under which private British capital received an assured return with shortfalls met from Indian revenues; forest control was built through the Forest Department of 1864, the Government Forests Act of 1865 and the Indian Forest Act of 1878
D. Canal irrigation and the telegraph were built by Indian princely states alone

**Answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Design purpose and consequence must be answered separately: the network served imperial control and commodity movement on financing terms that transferred risk to Indian taxpayers, yet also produced genuine market integration.

**Trap logic:** Conceding real unintended benefits does not concede developmental intent; the safe verdict is modern infrastructure without autonomous developmental priority.

### Q16. Which account of nineteenth-century famine causation and policy is correct?

A. Famine mortality is fully explained by absolute disappearance of food from the subcontinent
B. Colonial famine policy was codified before any major nineteenth-century famine occurred
C. Export of grain was the sole cause and climate played no part at all
D. A climatic trigger was amplified by revenue rigidity, commercialisation exposure, price-directed transport and a restrictive relief doctrine, producing entitlement failure; the 1876-78 famine was followed by the Strachey Commission of 1880 and codified relief, yet severe famine recurred in 1896-1900

**Answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Separating trigger from amplifier is the core discipline, and recurrence after codification shifts the analytical target to the doctrinal limits of relief -- work tests, cost ceilings and delayed declaration.

**Trap logic:** Reject both the climate-only and the export-only explanation, and assert no mortality figure: contemporary estimates vary widely and are disputed.

#### PYQ 01 — UPSC 2022, GS-I Mains, Q3 (10 marks, 150 words)

**Question:** "Why was there a sudden spurt in famines in colonial India since the mid-eighteenth century? Give reasons." **10 marks · 150 words.**

##### Solved model answer

**Directive control.** "Give reasons" requires named causal mechanisms in
sequence, not a general narrative of colonial hardship.

A climatic harvest shock — drought or monsoon failure — was usually the
immediate trigger, but colonial-era conditions converted scarcity into mass
mortality through four identifiable amplifiers.

First, revenue rigidity: a cash demand falling due on a fixed calendar under
the Permanent Settlement and Ryotwari could not be deferred in a bad year,
forcing distress sales of grain reserves at exactly the wrong moment.

Second, commercialisation exposure: advances and credit had tied cultivation to
cash crops for distant markets, thinning the household and village food buffers
that had previously absorbed a single failed season.

Third, price-directed transport: expanding rail capacity moved grain along
price signals rather than toward deficit areas, so food could leave a famine
district that could no longer bid for it.

Fourth, relief doctrine and administrative delay: restrictive relief
principles, work tests and late official declaration compounded distress before
the Strachey Commission of 1880 codified relief — and severe famine recurred in
1896–1900 even after that codification.

**Qualification.** This does not claim colonial rule invented famine in India,
nor that climate was irrelevant. It claims that specific colonial mechanisms
removed the buffers that might otherwise have absorbed a recurring shock.

**Why this earns marks.** Naming at least three of the four amplifiers, and
keeping the trigger analytically separate from them, is what converts a
descriptive answer into a causal one. No mortality figure is asserted.

#### PYQ 02 — UPSC 2024, GS-I Mains, Q13 (15 marks, 250 words)

**Question:** "How far was the Industrial Revolution in England responsible for the decline of handicrafts and cottage industries in India?" **Discuss · 15 marks · 250 words.**

##### Solved model answer

**Directive control.** "Discuss" on a causal proposition requires the premise
to be engaged on both sides and a graded verdict returned, not a one-sided
narration.

The Industrial Revolution was a necessary condition for the decline of Indian
handicrafts: mechanised spinning and weaving in England produced yarn and cloth
at unit costs no hand process could match, and the resulting capacity had to
find markets.

It was not, however, sufficient on its own. The shock reached Indian producers
through four colonial transmission mechanisms. Tariff and market asymmetry gave
British manufactures access to Indian markets on terms Indian goods did not
enjoy in Britain. Political control of commercial policy meant India could not
adjust duties in response. The decline of Indian courts removed the elite
patronage that had sustained the finest crafts. Railway penetration from the
1850s carried machine-made imports into interior markets and carried raw
materials outward.

The counterfactual clarifies the weighting: an economy holding tariff autonomy
could have slowed, buffered or redirected the same technological shock, as
other nineteenth-century economies did. The independent weight of colonial
policy therefore stands alongside the technological cause rather than behind
it.

**Qualification.** The outcome varied by region, craft and period. Coarse
cloth, village crafts and several specialised trades persisted, so no claim of
total extinction survives the evidence, and the aggregate magnitude of craft
decline remains disputed — Morris D. Morris and Daniel Thorner challenge its
scale and measurability.

**Verdict.** The Industrial Revolution was substantially responsible, but it
became decisive only through colonial policy instruments.

### ORIGINAL SOLVED MAINS PRACTICE

Six original questions — two at 10 marks (150 words), two at 15 marks
(250 words) and two at 20 marks (250 words). None of these is a PYQ; the

### Mains 10M-1 — Examine the institutional mechanism through which the Permanent Settlement of 1793 combined absolute revenue certainty for the state with genuine dispossession risk for the zamindar.

*Original practice question — 10 marks | 150 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Examine" requires the precise mechanism to be named and tested, not the settlement merely described.

#### Model answer

Cornwallis's settlement of 1793 fixed the state's land-revenue demand in
perpetuity across Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, recognising zamindars as
proprietors in exchange for that permanently fixed obligation. The state's
security was absolute: the demand could never be revised upward however much a
zamindar's income improved.

The corresponding risk operated through one rule. Under the Sunset Law, failure
to pay the fixed demand by the appointed date exposed the zamindari estate
itself to sale, transferring the proprietary right to a purchaser. A permanent
demand meeting a variable harvest therefore produced turnover of proprietors,
absentee tendencies and layered rent-collecting intermediaries.

Cultivator rights were a separate and less protected question: 1793 fixed the
state's claim on the zamindar, not the zamindar's rent on the ryot, and
occupancy security was addressed only later and incompletely.

**Qualification.** It was not uniformly ruinous: zamindars who met the demand gained lasting security, so the outcome was differentiation.

**Why this earns marks.** Naming the Sunset Law explicitly, and separating zamindar proprietorship from cultivator occupancy, is the precision this question rewards.

### Mains 10M-2 — Distinguish the target and the method of collective action in the Indigo disturbances of 1859-60 from those of the Pabna agrarian league movement from 1873.

*Original practice question — 10 marks | 150 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Distinguish" requires a paired comparison on controlled variables, not two separate descriptions.

#### Model answer

The two episodes differ on both variables that matter.

Target. The Indigo disturbances of 1859-60 in Bengal confronted European indigo
planters directly. The Pabna movement from 1873 in eastern Bengal confronted
Bengali zamindars attempting rent enhancement beyond legally recognised limits.

Method. Against planters, the effective weapon was a collective refusal to sow
indigo, because the planters' power rested on advance-based nij and ryoti
cultivation obligations that a crop boycott nullified at source. Against
zamindars, the effective weapons were organisational and legal — agrarian
leagues, pooled funds, rent resistance and litigation — because the grievance
was an unlawful rent demand that courts could be made to test.

Each repertoire is therefore the rational form of action against that specific
economic relationship.

**Qualification.** Both are used here strictly as economic-response case studies; their fuller political and organisational history belongs to the wider history of peasant and worker movements.

**Why this earns marks.** Stating target and repertoire as paired variables, and explaining why each repertoire fitted its adversary, converts description into comparison.

### Mains 15M-1 — Analyse the drain of wealth as a transfer mechanism rather than as a quantified total, and assess what this framing gains and what it concedes.

*Original practice question — 15 marks | 250 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Analyse" requires the concept to be broken into its components and the analytical consequences of that framing to be stated.

#### Model answer

The drain of wealth is a transfer concept, not a trade concept. It names a
unilateral movement of resources from India to Britain for which India received
no equivalent economic return, and it is strongest when argued through its
channels.

Those channels are institutionally identifiable. Home charges and India Office
costs were met from Indian revenues. Salaries, pensions and private remittances
carried official income out of India. Interest and profit on British capital,
including the assured returns of the railway Guarantee System, were claims
settled abroad. Stores were purchased in Britain on Indian account, and
imperial military and external expenditure was charged to Indian revenues.

The Council Bill mechanism connects these claims to the trade account. Bills
sold in London against rupee funds in India allowed India's recorded surplus of
exports over visible imports to service sterling obligations rather than return
as goods or investment. A surplus, on this reading, is not evidence of national
gain.

What the framing gains is evidential security: each channel can be named and
dated, and the argument survives the statistical objection. What it concedes is
magnitude. Revisionist scholarship disputes how much of this was a genuine
transfer rather than a payment for administration and infrastructure actually
delivered, and the surviving series cannot settle the question.

**Qualification.** The concession is deliberate. No total, annual figure or percentage of national income is asserted here, because the aggregate remains disputed and depends on what is counted as a legitimate administrative cost.

**Why this earns marks.** Naming at least four channels, adding the Council Bill link, and openly conceding the measurement problem is what distinguishes this from a slogan-level drain answer.

### Mains 15M-2 — Compare the Permanent, Ryotwari and Mahalwari settlements on assessment unit, revision pattern and risk carrier, and assess whether any of them can properly be called pro-peasant.

*Original practice question — 15 marks | 250 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Compare and assess" requires controlled comparison followed by an explicit verdict on the stated characterisation.

#### Model answer

The three settlements differ on three controlled axes.

Assessment unit. The Permanent Settlement assessed the zamindari estate;
Ryotwari assessed the individual ryot's holding; Mahalwari assessed the mahal,
the village or estate taken as a whole.

Revision. The Permanent Settlement fixed the demand in perpetuity under
Cornwallis in 1793 for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Ryotwari, associated with
Alexander Read and Thomas Munro in Madras and Bombay, revised the assessment
periodically by survey. Mahalwari, framed by Holt Mackenzie and implemented at
scale by R. M. Bird in the North-Western Provinces, was likewise periodically
revised.

Risk carrier. Under the Permanent Settlement the zamindar carried residual
risk, and passed it downward as rent. Under Ryotwari the cultivator carried it
directly, as the state's debtor of record. Under Mahalwari the village body
carried it jointly, so one co-sharer's default raised the burden on the rest.

On the verdict: none was pro-peasant. Ryotwari removed an intermediary but
substituted a direct, periodically revisable cash demand on a fixed calendar,
which in a poor season produced borrowing, mortgage and land alienation.
Mahalwari's joint liability buffered an individual bad year but transmitted
default across the village.

**Qualification.** No settlement covered the whole subcontinent and all three were modified by later legislation and revision, so a static all-India map of settlements misstates the position.

**Why this earns marks.** Answering on fixed axes in a fixed order, then returning an explicit verdict on the 'pro-peasant' characterisation, satisfies both halves of the directive.

### Mains 20M-1 — Critically examine the proposition that British rule produced modernisation of circulation in India without autonomous development.

*Original practice question — 20 marks | 250 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Critically examine" requires the proposition to be tested against contrary evidence before a qualified verdict is returned.

#### Model answer

The proposition holds, but only after the contrary evidence is conceded in
full.

What supports it. Revenue and property were redesigned through the Permanent,
Ryotwari and Mahalwari settlements to secure a cash demand on a fixed calendar.
Commercialisation pushed price and harvest risk downward through advances and
credit. Handicrafts declined under mechanised British production, tariff
asymmetry, lost court patronage and railway penetration. Drain channels — home
charges, remittances, guaranteed returns, stores and imperial expenditure —
settled claims abroad. Railways were financed through the Guarantee System,
which assured British capital a return while Indian revenues met the shortfall.

What resists it. Modernisation was real. Railways, ports, telegraph, canal
irrigation and codified law were built. Plantations, jute, cotton mills, coal
and later iron and steel constituted a genuine modern sector. Market
integration and mobility were real, and later served the nationalist movement
itself.

The reconciliation lies in direction and control. Circulation was modernised —
goods, information and people moved faster — while tariff policy, currency
policy, the composition of public expenditure and the terms of external
settlement were determined outside Indian hands. Craft decline and modern-sector
growth coexisted because they occupied different products, places and labour
markets, so the new sector never absorbed the displaced.

**Qualification.** Revisionist scholarship, including Morris D. Morris and Daniel Thorner, disputes the scale and measurability of decline, and regional outcomes varied sharply; the proposition therefore rests on the distribution of benefit and the location of control, not on any unquantified claim of total loss.

**Why this earns marks.** Conceding the modernisation case in detail before returning the located-control verdict is what 'critically examine' rewards; a one-sided answer forfeits the critical component.

### Mains 20M-2 — Evaluate the view that nineteenth-century Indian famines were failures of entitlement and governance rather than of food availability.

*Original practice question — 20 marks | 250 words | GS-I.*

**Directive control.** "Evaluate" requires the stated view to be weighed against its alternative and a reasoned judgement delivered.

#### Model answer

The view is largely sustainable, provided the climatic trigger is conceded
rather than denied.

The case for it. Drought or monsoon failure supplied the shock, but four
mechanisms converted scarcity into mass mortality. Revenue rigidity meant a cash
demand fell due on a fixed calendar irrespective of the harvest, forcing distress
sales of reserves. Commercialisation had replaced local food buffers with cash
crops and credit obligations. Transport followed price rather than need, so
grain could move out of a district that could no longer bid for it. Relief
doctrine imposed work tests, cost ceilings and late declaration. Together these
destroy the ability to command food even where food exists in the wider market,
which is precisely what entitlement failure means.

The governance case is strengthened by chronology. The 1876-78 famine produced
the Strachey Commission of 1880 and a codified relief framework — yet severe
famine recurred in 1896-1900. Recurrence after codification points to the
doctrinal limits of relief rather than to the absence of policy.

The case against it. Climate cannot be written out: the shocks were real,
recurrent and independent of policy. Nor is colonial rule shown to have invented
Indian famine, which predates it.

Judgement: availability failure triggered these famines; entitlement and
governance failure determined their scale.

**Qualification.** No mortality figure is asserted for any famine in this sequence, because contemporary estimates vary widely and are disputed; the 1770 and 1943 famines are used only as bounded bridges.

**Why this earns marks.** Conceding the climatic trigger, then separating trigger from amplifier and using the post-1880 recurrence as the governance test, is what converts a moral claim into an evaluated one.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### Competing interpretations

| Interpretation | Central emphasis | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| Nationalist — Naoroji and R.C. Dutt | Drain, revenue pressure, poverty and deindustrialisation | Anchor the critique in named channels and policies. |
| Marxist / structural | Dependent integration into world capitalism | Explain how land, trade, finance and industry formed one colonial structure. |
| Revisionist — Morris D. Morris and Daniel Thorner | Questions scale and measurability | Qualify employment, town and drain claims; do not deny all structural change. |
| Regional / social | Outcomes varied by region, tenure, crop, caste, gender and ecology | Prevent an all-India narrative of uniform loss. |

### Advanced refinements

- The drain is strongest as an institutional transfer argument and weakest as a precise aggregate.
- Deindustrialisation and modern industry can coexist because they affected different products, regions and labour markets.
- Commercialisation should be evaluated through risk distribution rather than a simple market-versus-subsistence binary.
- Railways and canals had real integrative effects while remaining imperial in design and finance.
- Famine analysis separates climatic trigger from entitlement, revenue, transport, relief and disease amplifiers.
- The Hilton-Young exchange-rate demand remains bounded: the qualitative remittance rationale is usable, but no exact adopted rate is asserted; `1s 4d` belongs to Gandhi's 1930 demand.

### Consolidated written register

#### REGISTER 01 — Scope, governing proposition and the three separated ideas

- This chapter answers what British rule did to the Indian **economy**.
- Governing proposition: **modernisation of circulation without autonomous
  development** — faster movement of goods, information and people, with
  surplus direction, risk placement and policy control located outside Indian
  hands.
- Keep three ideas separate: **Company plunder** (episodic, conquest-linked),
  **continuing drain** (ongoing, institutional, external), **structural
  underdevelopment** (the long-run outcome).
- Periodisation by dominant mechanism: tribute and monopoly (c.1757-65 onward);
  free-trade imperialism (after the 1813 Indian and 1833 China monopoly ends);
  integration without autonomy (c.1850s onward). Phases overlap.

#### REGISTER 02 — The pre-colonial baseline, stated narrowly

- Claimed: leading export of **cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre,
  opium, sugar, rice, spices and precious stones**, on a developed
  merchant-artisan base.
- Not claimed: universal prosperity, equality, absence of famine, or a
  pre-colonial industrial revolution.
- Function: a base that existed can be eroded — which is why
  **deindustrialisation** is the right word and **failure to industrialise** is
  a different claim.

#### REGISTER 03 — The Drain: mechanism, channels and evidential limits

- Definition: a **unilateral transfer** of resources to Britain without
  equivalent economic return. A transfer concept, not a trade concept.
- Channels: home charges and India Office costs; official salaries, pensions
  and private remittances; interest and profit on British capital including the
  **Guarantee System's assured returns**; stores purchased abroad on Indian
  account; imperial military and external expenditure charged to Indian
  revenues.
- Financial link: **Council Bills** sold in London against rupee funds in India
  allowed the recorded export surplus to settle sterling obligations rather
  than return as goods or investment — so a surplus is not evidence of gain.
- Limit: the **aggregate is disputed** and depends on what counts as a
  legitimate administrative cost. Assert **no total, annual figure or
  percentage**. No adopted Hilton-Young rate is stated; 1s 4d belongs only to
  Gandhi's 1930 eleven-point demand.
- Nationalist critique: **Naoroji, 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'**
  (drain); **R. C. Dutt, 'The Economic History of India'** (land revenue and
  agrarian pressure from official record); **M. G. Ranade** (industrial
  development and a constructive state role). Never swap the titles. Political
  deployment belongs to the nationalist-politics context.

#### REGISTER 04 — Deindustrialisation: evidence, causal web and the debate

- Formulation: **relative decline** in craft output, employment or bargaining
  position — uneven by region, craft and period; **never total extinction**.
- Four strands: mechanised British production (capacity); tariff and market
  asymmetry plus colonial control of commercial policy (access); decline of
  court and elite patronage (demand collapse for fine crafts); railway
  penetration from the 1850s (distribution inland).
- 'How far' architecture: concede necessity -> name transmission mechanisms ->
  apply the **tariff-autonomy counterfactual** -> admit variation -> return a
  **graded verdict**.
- Keep **artisan displacement** (occupational structure) apart from
  **de-urbanisation** (settlement pattern): court and craft towns contracted
  while port and railway towns grew.
- Debate: **Morris D. Morris** and **Daniel Thorner** dispute the scale and
  measurability of decline, not the existence of structural change. Contemporary
  series are thin — argue qualitatively, invent no share or percentage.
- Colonial industry was real but constrained: plantations, jute, cotton mills,
  coal and mining, later iron and steel; foreign capital and managing agencies
  powerful; craft decline and modern-sector growth coexisted because they
  occupied different products, places and labour markets.

#### REGISTER 05 — Land revenue systems compared

| Axis | Permanent | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architect | Cornwallis | Alexander Read, Thomas Munro | Holt Mackenzie (framework), R. M. Bird (implementation) |
| Year / region | 1793; Bengal, Bihar, Orissa | Madras, Bombay | North-Western Provinces, later Punjab |
| Unit | zamindari estate | individual ryot's holding | the mahal |
| Revision | fixed in perpetuity | periodic, by survey | periodic |
| Intermediary | zamindar | none | village body / co-sharers |
| Risk carrier | zamindar, then tenant | the ryot | village body, jointly |

- Permanent Settlement enforcement: the **Sunset Law** — default by the
  appointed date exposed the **estate itself** to sale, producing proprietor
  turnover, absentee tendencies and layered intermediaries. It fixed the
  state's claim on the zamindar, **not** the zamindar's rent on the ryot.
- Ryotwari: no intermediary, but a revisable cash demand on a fixed calendar
  with **possession conditional on payment** — not absolute proprietorship, and
  not automatically pro-peasant.
- Mahalwari: **joint and several liability** buffers an individual bad year but
  transmits a defaulter's burden to the rest.
- Two compulsory qualifiers: no system covered all India; all three were
  modified in practice by later legislation and revision.

#### REGISTER 06 — Commercialisation, credit risk and agrarian protest

- Chain: cash revenue on a fixed date -> need for money before harvest ->
  **planter/merchant advance** or **moneylender credit** -> cash-crop commitment
  (indigo, opium, cotton, jute, oilseeds, sugarcane, tea) -> price or harvest
  shock -> claims stay fixed while income does not -> **indebtedness, mortgage,
  land alienation**, often with the former holder continuing as tenant or wage
  labourer.
- Qualifier: some regions, crops and cultivators gained; the charge concerns the
  **distribution of risk**, not blanket ruin.
- Three protest cases, matched on target / relationship / repertoire:
  **Indigo 1859-60** — European planters — advance-based **nij and ryoti**
  cultivation — collective **refusal to sow**;
  **Pabna from 1873** — zamindars — rent enhancement beyond legal limits —
  **leagues, rent resistance and litigation**;
  **Deccan 1875** — moneylenders — debt and mortgage on written bonds —
  **seizure and destruction of the bonds**.
- Boundary: economic-response cases here; fuller political history lies beyond this economic chapter.

#### REGISTER 07 — Infrastructure, forest law and the Guarantee System

- Purpose and consequence are answered **separately**: design served troop
  movement, administrative control, raw-material evacuation and import
  distribution; consequences included genuine market integration, mobility and
  later nationalist communication.
- **Guarantee System**: private British capital received an assured minimum
  return, with shortfalls met from Indian revenues — risk socialised in India,
  return secured in Britain. This is why railways appear in both the
  infrastructure and the drain answers.
- Forest sequence: **1806** oak and teak reserved for the Navy; **1864** Forest
  Department; **1865** Government Forests Act; **1878** Indian Forest Act with
  **reserved / protected / unclassified** categories. Curtailment of customary
  use links this to tribal resistance.
- Safe verdict: modern infrastructure **without autonomous developmental
  priority**.

#### REGISTER 08 — Famine causation and administrative response

- Separate **trigger** from **amplifiers**. Trigger: drought or monsoon
  failure. Amplifiers: revenue rigidity (cash due on a fixed calendar);
  commercialisation exposure (cash crops and credit replacing food buffers);
  **price-directed transport** (grain leaves a district that cannot bid); and
  **relief doctrine** (work tests, cost ceilings, late declaration).
- Result: **entitlement failure** — people cannot command food that exists.
  Reject both the climate-only and the export-only explanation.
- Sequence: **1770** Bengal (bounded bridge); recurrent nineteenth-century
  regional famines; **1876-78** great southern/Deccan famine; **1880 Strachey
  (Famine) Commission** and codified relief; **1896-1900** renewed severe
  famines; **1943** Bengal (bounded bridge).
- Examinable pivot: recurrence **after** codification tests the **doctrine**,
  not the chronology.
- **Assert no mortality figure** for any famine in this sequence.

#### REGISTER 09 — Variation, historiography and the answer spine

- Variation axes: region (irrigated vs dry), tenure (secure vs tenancy-at-will
  vs sharecropping), caste access to credit, land and commons, gender
  entitlement, and ecology (forest and pastoral users under the 1865 and 1878
  Acts). Aggregates conceal distribution.
- Historiography: **nationalist** (drain and deindustrialisation);
  **Marxist/structural** (dependent integration into world capitalism);
  **revisionist** (**Morris D. Morris**, **Daniel Thorner** — scale and
  measurability); **regional** (outcomes varied by place, sector and group).
  Synthesis assesses extraction and transformation together.
- Answer spine: revenue and property redesign -> commercialisation and credit
  risk -> craft decline versus drain -> infrastructure and dependent industry ->
  famine vulnerability -> regional and social variation -> attributed
  historiography and a qualified verdict.
