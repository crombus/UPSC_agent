### Full examiner-grade model answers to ORIGINAL MAINS 1–6

**Mains 1 (10 marks) — immediate trigger.** The Emergency followed a developing political and economic crisis, but its immediate trigger was a judgment against the Prime Minister's election. On Raj Narain's petition, Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court set aside Indira Gandhi's election on 12 June 1975. Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer of the Supreme Court granted a conditional stay on 24 June; this was neither the original decision nor an unconditional vindication. At his Delhi rally on 25 June, Jayaprakash Narayan called for countrywide civil disobedience, adding political urgency. The national Emergency proclamation under Article 352 was dated 25 June and announced publicly on 26 June; arrests of opposition leaders under MISA began. Economic distress and mobilisation created a difficult setting, but neither automatically required national suspension of liberties. The executive choice transformed a contested personal electoral position into a national constitutional crisis. A dissolution and early election remained a possible constitutional alternative, even if politically risky.

**Mains 2 (10 marks) — JP movement's base and limits.** The JP movement gave diverse protests a shared moral leader and an ambitious language of 'Total Revolution', but its social reach and methods require qualification. Gujarat's Nav Nirman agitation began in January 1974; students mobilised in Bihar in March before Jayaprakash Narayan assumed leadership. The movement drew prominently on students, middle classes, traders and intellectuals. This coalition challenged corruption and governmental unresponsiveness but did not establish a comparably broad rural-poor or organised working-class base. A twenty-two-day railway strike in May 1974 sharpened the broader political confrontation, yet labour action should not be conflated with JP's entire programme. Calls for officials to resist unlawful orders and the later civil-disobedience appeal posed contested constitutional questions. These concerns do not prove JP intended authoritarian government, nor do they justify censorship and detention in response. His movement's strength lay in moral opposition; its uneven base and reliance on extra-parliamentary pressure limited the kind of governing alternative it could construct.

**Mains 3 (15 marks) — converging crises.** The Emergency arose when economic, political and legal pressures reinforced each other; describing any one as a complete explanation erases the choices made in June 1975. Failed monsoons, shortages and the oil shock worsened inflation and unemployment, eroding the authority of the 1971 'Garibi Hatao' mandate. Gujarat's Nav Nirman movement and JP's Bihar-led agitation translated this distress and dissatisfaction with corruption into organised opposition; the railway strike of May 1974 demonstrated the ability to disrupt essential services. Institutional weakening within Congress reduced space to process dissent. The immediate legal blow was the Allahabad High Court ruling of 12 June 1975 on Raj Narain's petition setting aside Indira Gandhi's election. The Supreme Court's conditional stay of 24 June left a live political dispute, while JP called for wider civil disobedience. The executive then chose an Article 352 proclamation dated 25 June, announced the next day. No drought, protest slogan or adverse election judgment legally compelled that choice: dissolution and early polls offered another constitutional route. Convergence explains vulnerability; it does not remove governmental responsibility for the decision.

**Mains 4 (15 marks) — instruments and excesses.** The Emergency combined formal legal instruments with coercive and extra-constitutional practice. Following the Article 352 proclamation dated 25 June 1975, publicly announced on 26 June, opposition figures were detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, public liberties were curtailed and press censorship blocked scrutiny. Elections were postponed; the 42nd Amendment of 1976 enlarged central and executive power. The Twenty-Point Programme of July 1975 offered an official reform-and-order agenda, but this should not be treated as equivalent to Sanjay Gandhi's later four-point programme. Sanjay exercised considerable power without constitutional office or the accountability attached to it. Coercive sterilisation and slum demolition became especially grave abuses, experienced unevenly across regions, notably in the north. Some initially approved the restoration of order and price stability, which explains early acceptance without excusing the violations that followed. The Shah Commission later investigated abuses. Neither disputed casualty counts nor the assertion that every administrative action was coercive is needed to establish the central failure: unchecked detention, censorship and unaccountable intervention undermined constitutional freedoms.

**Mains 5 (20 marks) — democracy failed and passed.** The formulation is persuasive only if we identify different agents and moments of failure and recovery. Economic distress, JP's extra-parliamentary mobilisation and an adverse election judgment combined in 1975, but an early electoral appeal remained an alternative to emergency rule. Instead, the Article 352 proclamation dated 25 June and announced on 26 June inaugurated detention under MISA and press censorship. Parliament, the ruling party and significant judicial protection of personal liberty failed to contain this restriction of rights; the 42nd Amendment of 1976 widened executive-central authority. Sanjay Gandhi's extra-constitutional influence and coercive sterilisation and demolitions revealed the costs of power without public accountability. Yet the initial popularity of order and price stability complicates any narrative of immediate universal opposition. Elections called in January 1977 were held in March. Voters defeated Congress, ending the Emergency that month, with pronounced regional differences reflecting uneven exposure to excesses. The Shah Commission inquired into abuses; the 44th Amendment of 1978 subsequently rebuilt constitutional safeguards, a process owned in doctrinal detail by Polity. Thus voters decisively passed a test many institutions had earlier failed. A later constitutional repair should not be mistaken for evidence that liberties were adequately protected during the crisis itself.

**Mains 6 (20 marks) — trajectory from distress to verdict.** A weakened party system and mounting economic distress formed the background to a crisis resolved electorally, but only after profound injury to constitutional freedoms. Failed monsoons, inflation, scarcity and the 1973 oil shock undermined the expectations generated by the 1971 mandate. Gujarat's Nav Nirman agitation began in January 1974; Bihar's March agitation gained JP's leadership and his 'Total Revolution' appeal. The railway strike of May 1974 intensified confrontation, though neither student nor labour mobilisation represented all social groups equally. On 12 June 1975 the Allahabad High Court invalidated Indira Gandhi's election; a Supreme Court judge granted a conditional stay on 24 June. After JP's 25 June Delhi appeal, an Article 352 Emergency was proclaimed, dated that day and publicly announced on 26 June. MISA detentions, censorship and election postponement were followed by the 42nd Amendment in 1976; Sanjay Gandhi's unaccountable influence and coercive sterilisation and demolitions discredited promises of discipline. The January 1977 election call led to Congress's defeat in March and the Emergency's end. The electorate, not a court order, reversed the regime; subsequent Shah Commission inquiry and 1978 constitutional safeguards did not erase earlier institutional failures. Democratic machinery proved recoverable, but it had not reliably prevented authoritarian practice.
