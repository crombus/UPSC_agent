**Question:** Why did the Janata experiment of 1977-1980 fail? Analyse its internal contradictions. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model thesis:** Janata proved that a coalition united only by anti-Indira, anti-Emergency sentiment could win power but not exercise it: absent a shared programme, a single leader or a common organisational culture, the dual-membership dispute supplied the fault line on which the government collapsed by 1979.

**Evidence spine:**

- The Janata Party was formed in January 1977 as a merger of Congress (O), the Jana Sangh, the Bharatiya Lok Dal and the Socialists, uniting the anti-Emergency opposition into a single electoral front.

- Charan Singh headed a caretaker government from July 1979, after Morarji Desai resigned, without ever facing Parliament for a vote of confidence.

### Full examiner-grade model answers to ORIGINAL MAINS 1–6

The six original questions and their prior evidence notes remain above; these are full answers with named evidence and qualifications.

**Mains 1 (10 marks) — Janata's failure.** Janata achieved alternation at the Centre but could not convert opposition to the Emergency into a stable programme of government. Congress (O), Jana Sangh, Bharatiya Lok Dal and Socialists merged in January 1977, winning 330 of 542 Lok Sabha seats in March and installing Morarji Desai as India's first non-Congress Prime Minister. Their common electoral grievance concealed conflicting ideologies, organisational habits and claims to leadership. The Jana Sangh–RSS 'dual membership' controversy exposed a structural question: could members of a merged governing party retain a separate organisational commitment? Rivalries among Desai, Charan Singh and others aggravated this fault line. Desai resigned in July 1979 and Charan Singh briefly headed a caretaker government; the coalition could not sustain its parliamentary basis. Yet Janata restored civil liberties and passed the 44th Amendment in 1978. Its failure was not proof that non-Congress government was impossible, only that negative unity without negotiated institutional rules could not stabilise this particular coalition.

**Mains 2 (10 marks) — regional assertion.** Regional politics in the early 1980s expressed diverse claims to electoral power, autonomy and identity rather than one uniform separatist impulse. Tamil Nadu's DMK and AIADMK under M.G. Ramachandran exemplified established Dravidian electoral competition. N.T. Rama Rao founded Telugu Desam in 1982 and swept Andhra Pradesh in 1983, making Telugu self-respect an electoral resource. The Left Front under Jyoti Basu governed West Bengal from 1977 through an ideologically distinct state coalition. Elsewhere, AASU mobilised migration and identity grievances in Assam, while Sheikh and later Farooq Abdullah's politics in Jammu and Kashmir were affected by central intervention. Punjab's Akali demands for autonomy and resource questions followed a more dangerous path of escalation. These cases differ in social coalition and central response: electoral regionalism could deepen representation, whereas prolonged mistrust and militancy posed grave problems. Federal accommodation, not an assumption that every regional demand endangered unity, is the more discriminating conclusion.

**Mains 3 (15 marks) — Punjab up to October 1984.** Punjab's crisis cannot be explained as religion alone; demands for political autonomy, territory and river waters interacted with competitive politics and escalating violence. The Akali Dal's Anandpur Sahib Resolution framed a federal claim, including greater state autonomy. These political grievances needed negotiation without denying that serious security threats subsequently emerged. The rise of Bhindranwale and militant actors changed the context and narrowed space for moderate settlement; historical interpretations disagree over the weight of central political management, federal grievances and security pressures. Operation Blue Star took place at the Golden Temple, Amritsar, in June 1984. Its profound political and social consequences preceded Indira Gandhi's assassination on 31 October, after which anti-Sikh violence occurred. None of these events licenses collective blame of Sikh citizens, nor does an account require unverified casualty totals or assertions about actors' private intentions. The tragedy demonstrates the cost of allowing political demands and violent escalation to become inseparable while negotiation was delayed; security action alone could not supply the political accommodation that federal stability required.

**Mains 4 (15 marks) — contrasting regional responses.** Central handling of regional politics varied because the grievances and mobilising organisations were not interchangeable. In Andhra Pradesh, N.T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam translated Telugu self-respect into an electoral victory in 1983; regional assertion could be processed through competitive federal politics. The Left Front under Jyoti Basu had already governed West Bengal from 1977, showing the coexistence of a distinct state ideological coalition with a different party at the Centre. In Assam, AASU's agitation around migrants and the identification of 'foreigners' generated prolonged conflict; it was a political-identity issue, not simply a policing problem. In Punjab, the Akali Dal's Anandpur Sahib demands for greater autonomy and resource arrangements became entangled with militant escalation and the June 1984 Blue Star operation. An eventual 1985 settlement lies beyond this owner's core. One cannot infer that all delays caused all violence: actual security threats also required a response. The contrast supports timely political accommodation alongside lawful security measures, without attributing identical central intentions or outcomes to four distinct states.

**Mains 5 (20 marks) — anti-Congressism and return.** The Janata experience shows both the electoral power and governing limits of a coalition defined principally by what it opposed. Congress (O), Jana Sangh, BLD and Socialists formed Janata in January 1977 after the Emergency; voters awarded it 330 of 542 seats in March and Morarji Desai headed the first non-Congress central government. That transfer was a democratic achievement, as was the government's restoration of freedoms and the 44th Amendment of 1978. Yet its components lacked a settled common programme, leadership hierarchy or organisational identity. Disputes over former Jana Sangh members' continuing RSS links made 'dual membership' a test of loyalty to the merged party; personal rivalries compounded the division. Desai resigned in 1979, Charan Singh's government was brief, and Congress (I) won 353 of 529 seats in January 1980. Indira's return demonstrated dissatisfaction with Janata's inability to govern, not a retrospective electoral endorsement of Emergency censorship or coercion. Thus opposition unity successfully secured a change of government but did not, without institutions for coalition bargaining, endure as a governing arrangement.

**Mains 6 (20 marks) — trajectory and federalism.** Janata's formation in January 1977 and victory in March established electoral alternation at the Centre. Morarji Desai led a coalition of disparate anti-Emergency parties, restoring freedoms and passing the 44th Amendment in 1978, but factional rivalry and the Jana Sangh–RSS dual-membership dispute undermined unity. Desai resigned in July 1979; Charan Singh briefly headed the government. Indira Gandhi's Congress (I) returned with 353 of 529 seats in January 1980. Sanjay Gandhi died that June and Rajiv entered politics, while state politics followed paths that central electoral strength could not simply override. Tamil Nadu's DMK/AIADMK competition, West Bengal's Jyoti Basu-led Left Front and N.T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam victory in Andhra in 1983 demonstrated electoral incorporation of distinct regional identities. Assam's AASU agitation over migration, central interference in the Abdullah-led politics of Jammu and Kashmir, and Punjab's unresolved Akali federal claims developed less stable trajectories. The rise of militancy in Punjab preceded Operation Blue Star in June 1984; Indira was assassinated on 31 October and anti-Sikh violence followed. These developments do not prove regionalism was inherently disintegrative: electoral federalism broadened participation. Equally, security threats and central–state distrust made accommodation difficult. The qualified verdict is a maturing federation where regional demands could be represented, alongside crises where negotiation and constitutional trust failed.
