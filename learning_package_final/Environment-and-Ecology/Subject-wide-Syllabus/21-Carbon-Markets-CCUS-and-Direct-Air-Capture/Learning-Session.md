### 1. One visual: where each instrument acts

```text
CLIMATE MITIGATION HIERARCHY

AVOID demand/emissions
        ↓
REDUCE through efficiency, electrification, renewables and process change
        ↓
PRICE remaining emissions
        ├─ carbon tax
        ├─ cap-and-trade / ETS
        └─ baseline-and-credit
        ↓
CAPTURE difficult point-source emissions
        └─ CCS / CCUS
        ↓
REMOVE residual CO2 already in the atmosphere
        ├─ DACCS / BECCS
        ├─ afforestation / soil carbon / biochar
        └─ enhanced weathering / ocean approaches
```

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A carbon price changes incentives, CCUS intercepts a source, and carbon dioxide removal draws CO2 from the atmosphere; none is a substitute for rapid direct reduction.

### 2. Carbon-pricing taxonomy

#### 2.1 Tax, ETS, baseline-credit and offsets

| Instrument | What government or programme fixes | Unit or payment | Main strength | Main design risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbon tax** | A tax rate per tCO2e or on the carbon content of fuel | Tax liability; no allowance is necessary | Predictable marginal price | Emissions quantity remains uncertain; rate may be too low or coverage narrow |
| **ETS / cap-and-trade** | An absolute emissions cap for covered sources | **Allowance** authorising a defined quantity of emissions | Quantity discipline within the cap | Overallocation, price volatility, free-allocation leakage and weak enforcement |
| **Baseline-and-credit** | A performance baseline, benchmark or intensity target | **Credit/certificate** for verified performance beyond the target | Rewards outperformance and can suit growing output | A generous baseline over-credits; absolute emissions may still rise |
| **Offset use** | Rules permitting an entity to compensate for emissions with external credits | Credit generated outside the buyer’s own inventory boundary | Mobilises finance beyond the regulated source | Additionality, leakage, reversal, double claiming and mitigation deterrence |

**Key distinction:** an offset is a **use of a credit**, not a synonym for every carbon market. A tax or ETS may permit limited offsets; a voluntary buyer may also retire credits outside a legal obligation.

#### 2.2 Price certainty versus quantity certainty

```text
CARBON TAX                      CAP-AND-TRADE
Price fixed by law             Quantity fixed by cap
        ↓                              ↓
Emissions response uncertain   Allowance price market-determined
```

Price floors, ceilings, allowance reserves and tax-adjustment rules can soften this textbook contrast, but they do not erase it.

#### 2.3 Allowance versus credit

| Test | Allowance | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Legal/economic origin | Created under a capped compliance system | Created by verified reduction, avoidance or removal against a methodology/baseline |
| Environmental meaning | Permission to emit within the programme limit | Claimed mitigation outcome relative to a counterfactual or target |
| Typical issuance stage | Allocated or auctioned before/within a compliance period | Normally issued after monitoring and verification |
| Core risk | Cap too loose or allowances overallocated | Baseline inflated, non-additional activity, leakage or reversal |
| Final use | Surrender for compliance | Surrender, cancellation or retirement as rules permit |

> **UPSC trap:** tradability does not make an allowance and a credit the same instrument.

#### 2.4 Compliance versus voluntary market

- A **compliance market** exists because law imposes an obligation: hold/surrender units, meet a target or pay a tax.
- A **voluntary market** exists because a non-obligated buyer chooses to purchase and retire credits or finance mitigation.
- The distinction follows the **source of demand**, not whether the unit is electronic, privately certified or recorded in a registry.
- A government can operate a voluntary crediting mechanism; a private credit can sometimes be admitted into a compliance system if law permits.

### 3. From activity to credible credit

#### 3.1 The integrity equation

```text
Creditable mitigation
≈ conservative baseline
− project emissions
− leakage
− uncertainty deductions
− reversals not remedied
```

This is a conceptual audit, not a universal legal formula. Each programme’s approved methodology controls the actual calculation.

#### 3.2 Baseline and additionality

- **Baseline:** the defensible reference level or counterfactual—what emissions/removals would occur without the credited intervention.
- **Additionality:** the credited outcome would not have occurred in the claimed amount without the activity and crediting incentive.
- Common tests examine legal requirements, investment barriers, prevailing practice, technology penetration and project start dates.
- A baseline can be project-specific, standardised, benchmark-based or dynamic. A baseline that is too high creates “hot air”; one that is too stringent can suppress worthwhile projects.

> **Close-option distinction:** a monitored reduction can still be non-additional; accurate measurement does not prove that the credit caused the outcome.

#### 3.3 Leakage

**Leakage** is an emissions increase outside the accounting boundary caused by the credited activity.

Examples:

- Protecting one forest shifts logging to another area.
- A bioenergy plantation displaces food production, inducing land conversion elsewhere.
- A plant closes in a regulated jurisdiction and production moves to a higher-emitting jurisdiction.

Leakage is different from **physical storage leakage**, where stored CO2 escapes from a reservoir. The first is activity displacement; the second is loss of containment.

#### 3.4 Permanence and reversal

| Storage form | Principal durability issue |
|---|---|
| Forest/soil carbon | Fire, drought, pests, harvesting or land-use change can reverse storage |
| Biochar | Stability varies with feedstock, production conditions, soil and time |
| Products | Carbon may be released at disposal or combustion |
| Geological storage | Requires site integrity, well control, plume/pressure monitoring and post-closure stewardship |

- **Permanence** asks how long carbon remains out of the atmosphere.
- **Reversal** is the subsequent release of previously credited stored carbon.
- Common programme responses include buffer reserves, insurance, replacement obligations, discounting and long monitoring periods. These controls reduce risk; they do not make all storage identical.

#### 3.5 MRV, validation and verification

```text
METHODOLOGY
   ↓
PROJECT/ENTITY BOUNDARY + BASELINE + MONITORING PLAN
   ↓
VALIDATION: ex-ante assessment of design and eligibility
   ↓
MONITORING + REPORTING by participant
   ↓
VERIFICATION: independent ex-post assessment of monitored result
   ↓
ISSUANCE in registry
   ↓
TRANSFER → SURRENDER / RETIREMENT / CANCELLATION
```

- **Monitoring** gathers activity data, emission factors, meter readings and storage evidence.
- **Reporting** presents the calculation and evidence under the methodology.
- **Verification** tests whether reported outcomes conform to rules; it is not the same as programme approval.
- A **registry** records serialised units, ownership, transfers and final use. It prevents many bookkeeping errors but cannot by itself prove additionality or permanence.
- **Surrender** normally fulfils a compliance obligation; **retirement** normally supports a final voluntary claim; **cancellation** makes a unit unavailable for use.

#### 3.6 Double counting has several forms

| Risk | Meaning | Main control |
|---|---|---|
| Double issuance | Two units issued for the same mitigation outcome | Unique project/unit identifiers and programme coordination |
| Double use | The same unit used more than once | Registry lock and retirement/surrender controls |
| Double claiming | Two actors or countries claim the same outcome | Claim rules and, for authorised Article 6 transfers, corresponding adjustments |

> **Crucial limit:** a corresponding adjustment addresses international accounting; it does not cure a false baseline, non-additionality or reversal.

### 4. Paris Agreement Article 6

#### 4.1 The three branches

```text
ARTICLE 6
├─ 6.2 COOPERATIVE APPROACHES
│    Parties define cooperation under UNFCCC accounting guidance
│    → authorised mitigation outcomes may become ITMOs
│    → corresponding adjustments prevent double claiming
│
├─ 6.4 PARIS AGREEMENT CREDITING MECHANISM
│    UNFCCC-supervised mechanism and registry
│    → A6.4 emission reductions
│    → authorisation determines international use/accounting
│
└─ 6.8 NON-MARKET APPROACHES
     Cooperation without transfer of mitigation outcomes
     → mitigation, adaptation, finance, technology and capacity-building
```

#### 4.2 Article 6.2 and ITMOs

- **Article 6.2** permits voluntary cooperative approaches between Parties.
- An **Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcome (ITMO)** is an authorised mitigation outcome used internationally under the Article 6.2 accounting framework. It is an accounting status, not simply any privately issued credit.
- The participating Parties must authorise relevant uses, report information, track outcomes through registries and apply **corresponding adjustments**.
- A corresponding adjustment means the transferring Party cannot also count the authorised outcome toward the same NDC achievement, while the acquiring Party accounts for its authorised use.
- Article 6.2 guidance strongly encourages adaptation contributions and delivery of overall mitigation, but does not impose the Article 6.4 percentages.

#### 4.3 Article 6.4 mechanism

- Article 6.4 establishes a UNFCCC-supervised mechanism under a Supervisory Body.
- It can credit reductions and removals under approved methodologies, host-Party approval and mechanism rules.
- An **authorised** A6.4 emission reduction used toward another Party’s NDC or another authorised international mitigation purpose is treated through Article 6.2 accounting and corresponding adjustment.
- A non-authorised **mitigation-contribution A6.4ER** contributes to host-country mitigation; it must not be marketed as if the host has transferred its NDC claim.

#### 4.4 SOP and OMGE

| Rule | Article 6.4 | Article 6.2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Share of proceeds (SOP) for adaptation** | Mandatory: 5% of issued A6.4ERs transferred to the Adaptation Fund, plus the mechanism’s monetary contribution rules | Parties are strongly encouraged to contribute resources for adaptation |
| **Overall mitigation in global emissions (OMGE)** | Mandatory cancellation of at least 2% of issued A6.4ERs | Parties are strongly encouraged to cancel outcomes or otherwise deliver OMGE |

> **Exam trap:** SOP is not the same as OMGE. SOP finances adaptation; OMGE ensures some credited mitigation is not used by anyone.

#### 4.5 Article 6.8

Article 6.8 covers **non-market approaches**: integrated cooperation on mitigation, adaptation, finance, technology transfer and capacity-building without trading or transferring mitigation outcomes. Decision 4/CMA.3 established the work programme and the Glasgow Committee on Non-market Approaches.

#### 4.6 Kyoto/CDM transition firewall

> **“Firewall” is a study mnemonic, not UNFCCC treaty language.**

There are two separate transition lanes:

1. **CDM activity transition**
   - Participants had to request transition by **31 December 2023**.
   - The host-Party approval deadline, originally 31 December 2025, was extended to **30 June 2026**.
   - Transition is conditional; a CDM activity does not automatically become an Article 6.4 activity or automatically receive Paris units.

2. **Limited legacy CER use**
   - Eligible CERs must come from CDM activities registered on or after **1 January 2013**.
   - They are identified as pre-2021 reductions and transferred to the Article 6.4 mechanism registry.
   - They may be used only toward a Party’s **first or first updated NDC**, not later NDC periods.
   - The host Party does not apply a corresponding adjustment and the Article 6.4 SOP is not levied on these legacy CERs.

**Therefore:** Kyoto units, transitioned activities, new A6.4ERs and Article 6.2 ITMOs are not interchangeable labels.

### 5. India’s Carbon Credit Trading Scheme

#### 5.1 Statutory and regulatory ladder

```text
ENERGY CONSERVATION (AMENDMENT) ACT, 2022
  ├─ section 14(w): Central Government may specify CCTS
  └─ section 14AA: Government/authorised agency may issue CCCs;
                   registered entities may buy or sell under the scheme
          ↓
CCTS, 2023 — S.O. 2825(E), 28 June 2023
          ↓
CCTS AMENDMENT — S.O. 5369(E), 19 December 2023
  └─ expressly adds offset mechanism, validation and project registration
          ↓
MoEFCC GEI TARGET RULES under Environment (Protection) Act, 1986
          ↓
CERC CCC TRADING REGULATIONS, notification dated 27 February 2026
```

#### 5.2 Institutional map

| Institution | Exact core role |
|---|---|
| **National Steering Committee for Indian Carbon Market (NSC-ICM)** | Governs/oversees the ICM; recommends procedures, regulations, sectors, targets, methodologies and CCC issuance. It is chaired by the Secretary, Ministry of Power and co-chaired by the Secretary, MoEFCC. |
| **Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE)** | Scheme Administrator; develops trajectories, targets, procedures, methodologies, accreditation and IT systems; registers offset projects; issues CCCs after the prescribed recommendations/approvals. |
| **Ministry of Power** | Administers the parent scheme; after BEE/NSC-ICM inputs, recommends GEI targets to MoEFCC. |
| **MoEFCC** | Notifies entity-specific GEI targets under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; coordinates climate-policy and Article 6 matters within its mandate. |
| **Grid Controller of India Limited** | Registry operator; registers entities, maintains CCC accounts, checks holdings and updates transfers. |
| **CERC** | Regulates purchase and sale/trading; approves exchange rules, price-discovery arrangements and market safeguards. |
| **Accredited Carbon Verification Agency (ACVA)** | Independently validates offset project design and/or verifies monitored outcomes under approved procedures. |

> **Mnemonic:** **NSC recommends → BEE administers/issues → Grid Controller records → CERC regulates trading.**

#### 5.3 Compliance mechanism

- It is an **intensity-based baseline-and-credit** system, not an economy-wide cap-and-trade ETS.
- MoEFCC notifies entity-specific **greenhouse gas emission intensity (GEI)** targets in tCO2e per unit of equivalent product/output.
- An obligated entity performing better than its target becomes eligible for CCCs after monitoring, verification, assessment and approval.
- An entity missing its target must purchase/surrender the equivalent CCC shortfall.
- Absolute emissions can rise if output grows faster than intensity falls; therefore CCTS is not, by itself, an absolute emissions cap.
- PAT is the institutional precursor, but an ESCert measures energy savings in tonnes of oil equivalent; a CCC represents one tCO2e of reduction, removal or avoidance under CCTS rules.

#### 5.4 Final GEI notifications and draft boundary

| Instrument | Final/draft status | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| G.S.R. 739(E), 8 October 2025 | **Final** | Aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, pulp and paper; entity schedules for compliance years 2025-26 and 2026-27 |
| G.S.R. 25(E), 13 January 2026 | **Final amendment** | Secondary aluminium entities, petroleum refinery, petrochemical and textile |
| Gazette draft, 26 June 2026 | **Draft only** | Iron and steel |

- The initial 2025-26 targets were proportionately calculated for **September 2025-March 2026**.
- The second-schedule 2025-26 targets were proportionately calculated for **January-March 2026**.
- The current ICM portal lists eight compliance sectors: aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, pulp and paper, textile, iron and steel, petrochemical and refinery. A sector’s presence on that list must still be distinguished from a final entity target schedule.

#### 5.5 Offset mechanism

- It is a voluntary, project-based **baseline-and-credit** mechanism for non-obligated entities.
- Projects may claim eligible reduction, removal or avoidance only under a published sectoral methodology, validation, registration, monitoring and verification.
- Ten approved sectoral scopes are listed: energy; industries; agriculture; waste handling and disposal; forestry; transport; fugitive emissions; construction; solvent use; and CCUS/other removals.

**Approved methodologies visible on BEE’s official page on 30 September 2026:**

1. Grid-connected renewable electricity
2. Hydrogen from electrolysis of water
3. Industrial energy efficiency and fuel switching
4. Hydrogen using methane extracted from biogas
5. Landfill methane recovery
6. Flaring or use of landfill gas
7. Methane recovery from livestock/manure at households and small farms
8. Afforestation/reforestation of degraded mangroves
9. Afforestation/reforestation of land except wetlands
10. Improved rice-cultivation management
11. Compressed biogas production
12. Electricity and heat from biomass

**Current boundary:** CCUS is an approved sectoral scope, but no approved CCUS methodology appeared in that list. Sector approval therefore cannot be cited as evidence of a registered CCUS project or issued CCUS CCC.

#### 5.6 CCC trading framework and evidence-safe status

The CERC 2026 regulations provide that:

- Grid Controller of India functions as the registry for exchange.
- BEE formulates the detailed transaction procedure for CERC approval.
- There are separate **compliance** and **offset** market segments.
- CCCs are dealt with through power exchanges unless CERC permits another mode.
- Transactions are monthly or at another approved periodicity.
- Power exchanges require CERC-approved rules, eligibility and price discovery.
- The compliance segment operates within a CERC-approved **floor** and **forbearance** price proposed by BEE.
- One CCC equals one tCO2e reduction, removal or avoidance under the scheme.

📰 **Status statement safe for an answer dated 30 September 2026:** the legal, target, verification, registry and exchange frameworks are substantially notified. No verified public CCC issuance total, exchange price, traded volume or completed-trade record was located on the reviewed official pages. A regulation, portal registration counter or draft PDD is not proof of issuance or trading.

### 6. Voluntary standards and claims — examples, not endorsement

Private programmes such as the [**Verified Carbon Standard (VCS)**](https://verra.org/programs/verified-carbon-standard/) and [**Gold Standard**](https://www.goldstandard.org/) publish methodologies, approve validation/verification bodies and operate registries. Integrity initiatives such as the [**ICVCM Core Carbon Principles**](https://icvcm.org/assessment-framework/) assess programmes and credit categories; the [**VCMI Claims Code**](https://vcmintegrity.org/claims-code-of-practice/) addresses buyer claims.

This package does **not** endorse any standard, project, credit or claim. Apply five firewalls:

1. Programme registration ≠ government compliance eligibility.
2. Verification ≠ automatic additionality or permanence.
3. Retirement ≠ Article 6 authorisation.
4. Article 6 authorisation ≠ a guarantee of every project-quality dimension.
5. Purchasing a credit ≠ reducing the buyer’s own inventory emissions.

| Claim type | Proper interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Compensation/offset claim** | Buyer says retired credits balance specified emissions; requires especially clear ownership, boundary and double-claim rules |
| **Contribution claim** | Buyer finances mitigation without subtracting it from its own inventory |
| **Net-zero neutralisation** | Durable removals counterbalance residual emissions only after deep value-chain reductions |

The UN High-Level Expert Group’s *Integrity Matters* framework and VCMI both place direct emissions reduction before voluntary credit use.

### 7. CCUS: complete chain, not just a capture machine

#### 7.1 Chain visual

```text
SOURCE CHARACTERISATION
        ↓
CAPTURE / SEPARATION
        ↓
DEHYDRATION + PURIFICATION + COMPRESSION
        ↓
TRANSPORT: pipeline / ship / road or rail for small volumes
        ↓
USE ---------------------------------- STORAGE
fuels, chemicals, materials, EOR       site appraisal → injection
        ↓                                      ↓
duration + lifecycle test              plume/pressure monitoring
                                               ↓
                                      closure + long-term liability
```

Climate value depends on the weakest link. A high capture percentage at the stack does not equal the same percentage of lifecycle emissions avoided.

#### 7.2 Point-source capture methods

| Method | Where separation occurs | Core mechanism | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Post-combustion** | After fuel combustion | Solvents/sorbents separate CO2 from flue gas | Dilute stream and solvent regeneration impose an energy penalty |
| **Pre-combustion** | Before combustion after fuel conversion | Fuel becomes hydrogen-rich gas plus CO2; CO2 is separated | Best suited to integrated processes; not a simple retrofit |
| **Oxy-fuel** | During combustion in near-pure oxygen | Flue gas is mainly CO2 and water; water is condensed | Oxygen production is energy-intensive |
| **Process separation** | Industrial stream already rich in CO2 | Physical/chemical separation, membranes or compression | Sector- and stream-specific economics |

IEA identifies chemical absorption, physical separation, oxy-fuel, membranes and looping/direct-separation approaches at different readiness levels.

#### 7.3 Transport

- CO2 is normally conditioned and compressed before transport.
- Pipelines suit large, continuous flows; ships can connect dispersed sources to offshore storage; road/rail are generally smaller-scale options.
- Shared hubs can lower unit costs but create coordination and common-carrier questions.
- Impurity specifications, corrosion, pressure management, rupture/asphyxiation risk and cross-border rules matter.

#### 7.4 Utilisation is not automatically storage

| Use | Likely climate character |
|---|---|
| Synthetic fuel | CO2 is normally re-released when fuel burns; may recycle carbon but is not durable removal |
| Beverages/urea and many chemicals | Usually short-lived storage |
| Concrete curing/mineral products | Can provide longer storage, but quantify the retained fraction and lifecycle emissions |
| Enhanced oil recovery | Requires full lifecycle accounting because additional oil may later be combusted |

#### 7.5 Geological storage

IEA identifies deep saline formations and depleted oil/gas reservoirs as major storage types; reactive basalt can mineralise CO2 but remains less developed. At suitable depths—typically greater than about **800 metres**—compressed CO2 can remain in a dense state.

Storage security relies on:

- reservoir capacity, injectivity and caprock integrity;
- legacy-well identification and remediation;
- injection-pressure management;
- measurement of injected mass and impurities;
- plume and pressure monitoring;
- leak detection, corrective action and closure plans;
- financial security and allocation of long-term liability.

Trapping strengthens through structural, residual, solubility and mineral mechanisms. “Theoretical capacity” is not the same as appraised, permitted, bankable storage.

#### 7.6 CCS, CCU, CCUS and CDR

| Term | What it means | Removal? |
|---|---|---|
| **CCS** | Capture at a source plus durable storage | Usually emissions avoidance, not atmospheric removal |
| **CCU** | Capture plus use in products/processes | Depends on product lifetime and lifecycle; often temporary |
| **CCUS** | Umbrella covering use and/or storage | Not automatically negative emissions |
| **CDR** | CO2 is removed from ambient atmosphere and durably stored | Yes, if lifecycle net removal is positive |
| **DACCS / BECCS** | DAC or sustainable bioenergy paired with durable storage | Potential CDR |

> **Prelims trap:** fossil point-source CCS prevents a new emission; it does not remove a past atmospheric emission.

### 8. Direct Air Capture

#### 8.1 Operating logic

```text
AMBIENT AIR
   ↓ fans/contactors
CO2 BINDS TO SORBENT OR SOLVENT
   ↓ heat / pressure / vacuum regeneration
CONCENTRATED CO2
   ↓ compression
GEOLOGICAL STORAGE → DACCS removal
OR
PRODUCT/FUEL USE → duration and re-emission decide the claim
```

#### 8.2 Solid and liquid pathways

| Pathway | Capture medium | Regeneration |
|---|---|---|
| **Solid DAC (S-DAC)** | Solid sorbents | Lower-temperature heat plus electricity; may use heat pumps, geothermal or waste heat |
| **Liquid DAC (L-DAC)** | Liquid alkaline solvents | Current designs can need high-temperature heat, up to about 900°C, plus electricity |

Both need fans, contactor area, heat, electricity, sorbent/solvent management, compression and transport/storage infrastructure.

#### 8.3 Cost and climate test

- IEA’s **2022** report estimated future capture cost for a large-scale plant built with then-current technology at roughly **USD 125-335 per tCO2**, with a wide and uncertain range.
- This is a dated **capture-cost estimate**, not a universal transaction price and not necessarily the full delivered cost including transport, storage, financing and long-term monitoring.
- DAC’s low atmospheric CO2 concentration raises energy needs relative to many point sources.
- Low-carbon energy is essential; otherwise the energy supply can erode or negate net removal.
- Siting near low-carbon energy and suitable storage reduces infrastructure burdens.
- IEA assesses DACCS as having a relatively small direct land/water footprint compared with many land-based removals, but indirect energy, material and infrastructure footprints still require lifecycle assessment.

#### 8.4 DAC is removal only under strict conditions

```text
Gross CO2 captured from air
− energy and material lifecycle emissions
− transport and compression emissions
− any storage leakage/reversal
= net atmospheric removal
```

Using air-captured CO2 for synthetic aviation fuel can reduce reliance on fossil carbon when paired with low-carbon hydrogen and energy, but combustion returns the CO2 to the atmosphere. It is carbon recycling, not durable removal.

### 9. Carbon dioxide removal portfolio

| Method | Removal mechanism | Storage | Main limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Afforestation/reforestation** | Photosynthesis increases biomass/soil carbon | Biosphere | Land rights, food/biodiversity trade-offs, fire/drought/reversal |
| **BECCS** | Biomass absorbs CO2; conversion CO2 is captured and stored | Geological | Sustainable biomass, land/water, supply-chain emissions, capture/storage infrastructure |
| **Biochar** | Biomass is pyrolysed and stable carbon applied to soil | Soil/product | Feedstock sustainability, variable durability, air pollution and soil effects |
| **DACCS** | Chemical capture from ambient air | Geological | Energy, cost, materials, storage access and infrastructure |
| **Enhanced weathering** | Crushed alkaline silicate minerals react with CO2 | Mineral/bicarbonate | Mining, grinding energy, dust/metals, reaction-rate and MRV uncertainty |
| **Ocean alkalinity enhancement** | Adds alkalinity so ocean can hold more inorganic carbon | Ocean chemistry | Ecological uncertainty, material scale, transboundary governance and MRV |
| **Ocean fertilisation/biological approaches** | Stimulates biomass and hoped-for deep-ocean export | Ocean | Export fraction, permanence, food-web/deoxygenation risk and governance |

#### 9.1 BECCS is not automatically negative

BECCS is net-negative only if biomass regrowth, land-use change, cultivation, processing, transport, conversion, capture rate and storage are accounted for. Unsustainable biomass or indirect land-use change can eliminate the benefit.

#### 9.2 Afforestation is not avoided deforestation

- **Afforestation/reforestation:** increases carbon stocks and can be a removal.
- **Avoided deforestation:** prevents a counterfactual emission; it is generally an avoided-emissions claim, not removal of previously emitted CO2.

#### 9.3 Biochar is not permanent by label

Biochar durability varies with feedstock, pyrolysis conditions and soil environment. Credit only the stable fraction under a methodology, after deducting feedstock, process and transport emissions.

### 10. Limits: mitigation deterrence, resources and justice

#### 10.1 Mitigation deterrence

**Mitigation deterrence** occurs when the promise of future offsets, CCS or removals delays feasible emissions cuts today. The risk is greatest when:

- uncertain future removal is treated as guaranteed;
- temporary biological storage is equated with fossil-carbon permanence;
- CCUS extends high-emitting assets where cleaner substitution is feasible;
- corporate “neutrality” claims obscure rising gross emissions.

#### 10.2 Resource and justice questions

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who controls land and biomass? | BECCS/afforestation can affect food, water, customary tenure and Indigenous/local rights |
| Who bears local risk? | Capture plants, pipelines, injection wells and mining for enhanced weathering create place-specific burdens |
| Who owns the unit and claim? | Communities, project developers, governments and buyers may contest benefit sharing and carbon rights |
| Who carries reversal/liability? | Short crediting periods can shift long-term risk to future taxpayers or communities |
| Who pays for removal? | Historic responsibility, ability to pay and access to technology shape climate justice |

#### 10.3 The defensible hierarchy

```text
1. Avoid unnecessary emissions
2. Reduce gross emissions rapidly
3. Use carbon pricing to strengthen incentives
4. Reserve CCUS mainly for difficult process/residual emissions
5. Use high-integrity CDR for residual emissions
6. Keep separate ledgers for reductions, avoided emissions and removals
7. Disclose durability, uncertainty, ownership and corresponding adjustments
```

### 11. UPSC traps and rapid tests

| Wrong shortcut | Correct boundary |
|---|---|
| Every carbon market is cap-and-trade | Tax, cap-and-trade and baseline-credit are distinct |
| Every tradable unit is an allowance | Credits arise from mitigation performance; allowances authorise emissions |
| A registry guarantees quality | It tracks units; it does not prove additionality |

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Market integrity is a stack, not a label

```text
PROGRAMME GOVERNANCE
   + METHODOLOGY QUALITY
   + PROJECT ADDITIONALITY
   + CONSERVATIVE QUANTIFICATION
   + INDEPENDENT VERIFICATION
   + DURABILITY / REVERSAL CONTROL
   + REGISTRY AND CLAIM ACCOUNTING
   = possible high-integrity outcome
```

Failure at any layer can invalidate the claim. A premium label or high market price is not proof of atmospheric benefit.

### B. Authorisation and claim separation under Article 6

Three questions must be answered separately:

1. **Was a mitigation outcome issued?**
2. **Did the host Party authorise international use?**
3. **What claim may the buyer make?**

An issued credit can remain a host-country mitigation contribution. Conversely, authorisation/corresponding adjustment does not prove the project was socially just or technologically additional.

### C. Baseline uncertainty and asymmetric risk

Over-crediting has asymmetric consequences: one doubtful credit can permit one real tonne of emissions elsewhere. Conservative baselines, uncertainty deductions and ex-post correction are therefore not bureaucratic detail; they protect the atmosphere against counterfactual error.

### D. Intensity markets in a growing economy

⚠️ India’s intensity design can improve emissions per unit of output while allowing total emissions to rise. This can support industrial growth and technology diffusion, but it means:

- target stringency and output definitions are decisive;
- product-mix changes can distort comparability;
- absolute emissions must be reported alongside intensity;
- CCTS should complement, not replace, sectoral technology and energy policy.

### E. CCUS hubs and cross-chain liability

Shared transport/storage networks can reduce cost, but create allocation problems:

- Who is liable if an off-spec CO2 stream damages common infrastructure?
- Does capture stop when transport or storage is unavailable?
- Who bears post-closure liability?
- How are cross-border CO2 transfers classified?

The climate claim should follow measured CO2 through the entire chain, not stop at the capture plant gate.

### F. Removal equivalence is false

One tonne stored in a forest for decades, one tonne mineralised, and one tonne injected into a well are not automatically equivalent in durability, monitoring burden or reversal risk. A scientifically credible market must disclose storage form and expected duration rather than hide them behind a common “one tonne” label.

### G. Portfolio governance

IPCC assesses CDR as necessary in pathways reaching net zero, particularly for residual emissions, but also warns that methods differ in cost, maturity, side effects and sustainability. A prudent portfolio:

- avoids dependence on one speculative method;
- protects food, water, biodiversity and rights;
- funds monitoring for the full storage period;
- separates near-term reductions from future removals;
- never treats announced future capacity as already delivered mitigation.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Carbon-pricing identity

- Tax fixes **price**; emissions quantity responds.
- Cap-and-trade fixes **covered quantity**; allowance price responds.
- Baseline-credit rewards performance beyond a benchmark; no necessary absolute cap.
- Offset = external credit used to compensate; it is a **use**, not a complete market taxonomy.
- Allowance authorises emissions; credit certifies a claimed reduction/avoidance/removal.
- Compliance/voluntary depends on the source of demand.

### Integrity chain

- Baseline = counterfactual/reference.
- Additionality = would not occur in claimed amount without intervention/incentive.
- Leakage = displaced emissions beyond boundary.
- Permanence = storage duration; reversal = later release.
- Validation is ex ante; verification is ex post.
- Registry tracks issuance-transfer-final use; it does not prove project quality.
- Double issuance, double use and double claiming are separate.

### Article 6

- 6.2: cooperative approaches, ITMOs, authorisation, reporting and corresponding adjustments.
- 6.4: UNFCCC-supervised crediting mechanism.
- 6.8: non-market cooperation.
- 6.4 SOP: 5% in-kind A6.4ER share for Adaptation Fund; OMGE: minimum 2% cancellation.
- 6.2 adaptation contribution/OMGE are strongly encouraged, not the same mandatory percentages.
- Corresponding adjustment prevents international double claim; it does not cure non-additionality.
- CDM requests closed 31 December 2023; host approval deadline closed 30 June 2026.
- Eligible legacy CERs: activity registered on/after 1 January 2013; first/first updated NDC only.

### India CCTS

- Legal base: Energy Conservation Act sections 14(w), 14AA.
- CCTS: S.O. 2825(E), 28 June 2023; amendment S.O. 5369(E), 19 December 2023.
- NSC recommends; BEE administers/issues; Grid Controller records; CERC regulates trading.
- Compliance = GEI intensity baseline-credit; offset = project-based voluntary crediting.
- Final targets: four sectors on 8 October 2025; secondary aluminium + refinery + petrochemical + textile on 13 January 2026.
- Iron/steel schedule dated 26 June 2026 remained draft in the reviewed official set.
- CERC trading regulations dated 27 February 2026 created two exchange segments.
- No verified public CCC price/volume/issuance tally was found as of 30 September 2026.
- Twelve approved offset methodologies were listed; none was a CCUS methodology.

### CCUS and storage

- Chain: capture → condition/compress → transport → use/store → monitor → close/liability.
- Post-combustion, pre-combustion and oxy-fuel are distinct.
- Capture rate ≠ lifecycle emissions avoided.
- Short-lived use is not permanent storage.
- Saline formations and depleted reservoirs are major storage options; basalt mineralisation is emerging.
- MRV must cover injected mass, plume, pressure, leakage and closure.
- Fossil point-source CCS = avoidance; DACCS/credible BECCS can be removal.

### DAC and CDR

- DAC captures dilute ambient CO2; it needs heat, electricity, materials, compression and storage.
- S-DAC uses lower-temperature heat; L-DAC can require heat up to about 900°C.
- IEA 2022 large-plant capture-cost estimate: USD 125-335/tCO2, highly uncertain and not full delivered cost.
- DAC + synthetic fuel = carbon recycling; DAC + durable storage = potential removal.
- BECCS needs sustainable biomass and full lifecycle accounting.
- Afforestation is reversible; avoided deforestation is generally avoided emissions.
- Biochar durability varies.
- Enhanced weathering and ocean approaches face material, ecological and MRV uncertainty.

### Final answer line

> Carbon markets can lower the cost of mitigation, CCUS can address difficult point-source emissions, and CDR can neutralise residuals; their legitimacy depends on direct reductions first, conservative accounting, durable storage, transparent claims and just allocation of risk.
