## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. The language firewall: weather, climate, variability, warming and change

```text
ATMOSPHERIC CONDITION
├─ minutes to weeks at a place --------------------------> WEATHER
├─ statistical distribution over a long reference period -> CLIMATE
├─ fluctuation around that distribution -----------------> CLIMATE VARIABILITY
└─ persistent shift in the distribution -----------------> CLIMATE CHANGE

GLOBAL WARMING = long-term rise in global mean surface temperature
CLIMATE CHANGE = warming + altered rainfall, circulation, extremes, ocean and ice
```

| Term | Precise meaning | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| **Weather** | Atmospheric conditions at a time and place: temperature, rain, wind, humidity and pressure. | One hot day is not a climate trend. |
| **Climate** | The statistical distribution of weather, including averages, variability and extremes, over a long period. NOAA notes that 30-year normals are often used. | Climate is not merely “average weather”; tails and variability matter. |
| **Internal variability** | Fluctuation generated within the coupled atmosphere-ocean-cryosphere system, such as ENSO-related variation. | A cool year does not cancel long-term warming. |
| **Climate change** | A persistent change in the state or statistical properties of climate, caused by natural drivers, internal processes or external forcing; present global warming is predominantly human-caused. | It is not synonymous with every individual disaster. |
| **Global warming** | The rise in global mean surface temperature. | It is narrower than climate change. |

**Answer-grabbing line:** Climate evidence is statistical and scale-sensitive: weather is an event, climate is a distribution, variability moves conditions around that distribution, and climate change shifts the distribution itself.

**Must-write keywords:** reference period; distribution; mean; variability; extremes; trend; internal variability; scale.

**Claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification:** NOAA distinguishes short-term weather from long-period climate and uses 30-year normals operationally. Therefore, an unusual Indian monsoon season may be societally important without alone establishing a long-term climatic trend; attribution requires a longer record and causal analysis.

**UPSC traps**

- “Cold day disproves warming” confuses local weather with a global long-term trend.
- “Natural variability causes all observed warming” ignores attribution evidence across temperature, ocean, ice and vertical atmospheric fingerprints.
- “Climate change means only higher temperature” omits water-cycle, ocean, cryosphere and extreme-event changes.

### 2. Earth’s energy balance, albedo and the greenhouse mechanism

```text
SUN
  │ predominantly shortwave radiation
  v
TOP OF ATMOSPHERE: about 340 W/m² global average
  ├─ about 29% reflected by clouds, atmosphere and bright surfaces -> SPACE
  └─ remainder absorbed by atmosphere and surface
                           │
                           v
                    SURFACE WARMS
                           │ emits longwave infrared
                           v
       GHGs absorb and re-emit selected infrared wavelengths
              ├─ upward -> space
              └─ downward -> warmer surface/lower atmosphere
```

NASA’s global energy-budget description uses about **340 W/m²** incoming solar energy and about **29%** reflection. In long-term equilibrium, absorbed shortwave energy is balanced by outgoing reflected shortwave plus emitted longwave energy. A forcing disturbs this balance; temperature and other responses evolve until outgoing energy again balances incoming energy at a warmer or cooler state.

**Albedo** is the reflected fraction of incoming radiation. Snow, ice and bright clouds have high albedo; ocean and many vegetated or soil surfaces are darker. Albedo is a property of the surface-atmosphere system, not a greenhouse gas.

#### Natural versus enhanced greenhouse effect

| Natural greenhouse effect | Enhanced greenhouse effect |
|---|---|
| Water vapour, CO2, CH4, N2O, ozone and other gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation. | Human activities raise long-lived GHG concentrations and alter ozone, aerosols and land cover. |
| Keeps Earth roughly **33°C warmer** than without the terrestrial greenhouse effect; it is life-enabling. | Produces positive net radiative forcing and an energy imbalance, causing warming and wider climate change. |
| Not a problem to “remove”. | The policy problem is the anthropogenic enhancement. |

**Answer-grabbing line:** The greenhouse effect is not a lid that simply “traps heat”; it is a wavelength-selective radiative process that raises the altitude from which Earth effectively emits energy to space, requiring a warmer surface-troposphere system to restore balance.

**Must-write keywords:** shortwave; longwave; infrared absorption; re-emission; albedo; top-of-atmosphere balance; energy imbalance; natural versus enhanced.

**Claim -> evidence -> analysis -> qualification:** NASA explains that the natural greenhouse effect sustains an average habitable surface climate, while IPCC AR6 assesses positive human-caused forcing. The correct causal chain is concentration change -> radiative forcing -> energy imbalance -> climate response, not the imprecise claim that all “pollution” heats Earth.

### 3. Forcing agents: gases, water vapour, ozone and aerosols

#### 3.1 Source-sink-role map

| Agent | Important anthropogenic sources | Main removal/sink logic | Climate role and lifetime discipline |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CO2** | Fossil-fuel combustion, cement calcination, land-use change | Ocean uptake, photosynthesis and long geological processes | Long-lived stock pollutant; no single atmospheric lifetime because multiple reservoirs act on very different timescales. Cumulative emissions largely determine warming. |
| **CH4** | Fossil-fuel systems, livestock, rice cultivation, waste | Mainly oxidation by tropospheric hydroxyl radicals; some soil uptake | Stronger per unit mass than CO2 over short horizons but much shorter-lived. IPCC AR6 assessed lifetime: **11.8 +/- 1.8 years**. Oxidation also produces CO2 and water. |
| **N2O** | Agricultural soils and nitrogen fertiliser, manure, industry | Mainly destruction in the stratosphere | Long-lived warming gas and ozone-depleting substance. IPCC AR6 assessed lifetime: **109 +/- 10 years**. |
| **Fluorinated gases** | Refrigeration, electronics, industrial processes and electrical equipment, varying by gas | Chemical destruction or very slow removal, varying by gas | Often high GWP; lifetimes range widely. Never assign one value to the entire group. |
| **Water vapour** | Evaporation and rapid hydrological cycling; direct human emissions are usually locally important but not the main global long-lived forcing | Condensation and precipitation within days | Most abundant greenhouse gas, but principally a **feedback**: warming raises atmospheric moisture, which amplifies warming. |
| **Tropospheric ozone** | Not emitted directly; formed from NOx, VOCs, CO and CH4 in sunlight | Chemical reactions and deposition | Short-lived warming agent and pollutant. |
| **Stratospheric ozone** | Formed and destroyed photochemically; depleted by ozone-depleting substances | Photochemical cycle | Absorbs ultraviolet radiation and affects stratospheric temperature. Tropospheric and stratospheric ozone changes must not be conflated. |
| **Sulphate and many other aerosols** | Combustion and precursor gases; also natural dust, sea salt and volcanoes | Rainout and deposition, generally days to weeks | Mostly cooling through scattering and cloud effects; spatially uneven and short-lived. |
| **Black carbon** | Incomplete combustion: diesel, biomass, cookstoves and brick kilns | Deposition | Warming aerosol; absorbs sunlight and darkens snow/ice, reducing albedo. It is not a well-mixed GHG. |

#### 3.2 Concentration is not emission

```text
EMISSION FLOW
     │
     v
atmospheric chemistry + sinks + lifetime + transport
     │
     v
CONCENTRATION / BURDEN
     │
     v
RADIATIVE FORCING
```

An **emission** is a flow during a period; a **concentration** is atmospheric abundance; a **stock** is the accumulated burden. Falling annual CO2 emissions still add to the stock unless net emissions reach zero. By contrast, sustained CH4 emission cuts can lower CH4 concentration over decades because methane is removed much faster.

#### 3.3 Radiative forcing, GWP and GTP

- **Radiative forcing (RF):** imposed change in Earth’s net energy balance, conventionally in W/m².
- **Effective radiative forcing (ERF):** includes rapid atmospheric and land-surface adjustments before global surface temperature substantially changes.
- **Global Warming Potential (GWP):** time-integrated radiative forcing from a pulse emission of a gas relative to the same mass of CO2 over a stated horizon.
- **Global Temperature-change Potential (GTP):** global mean surface-temperature change at a chosen future time after a pulse emission, relative to CO2.

| IPCC AR6 metric | CH4, fossil | CH4, non-fossil | N2O |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| GWP-20 | 82.5 +/- 25.8 | 79.7 +/- 25.8 | 273 +/- 118 |
| GWP-100 | 29.8 +/- 11 | 27.0 +/- 11 | 273 +/- 130 |
| GTP-100 | 7.5 +/- 2.9 | 4.7 +/- 2.9 | 233 +/- 110 |

These are **AR6 Chapter 7 assessment values**, not timeless constants. The methane distinction reflects downstream CO2 from fossil methane. A bare statement that “methane has a GWP of X” is incomplete without assessment report, gas category and time horizon.

**Answer-grabbing line:** GWP compares integrated radiative influence, while GTP compares temperature at an endpoint; neither metric is an intrinsic, horizon-free property of a gas.

**UPSC traps**

- Water vapour is not ignored; it is treated mainly as a fast feedback to long-lived forcing.
- Ozone can warm in the troposphere while stratospheric ozone depletion has a different radiative effect.
- Aerosols are not uniformly cooling: black carbon warms.
- A gas with shorter lifetime can have a high near-term GWP.

### 4. Carbon cycle, airborne fraction and sinks

```text
ANTHROPOGENIC CO2 EMISSIONS (2010-2019 average)
                 100%
          ┌────────┼────────┐
          v        v        v
 ATMOSPHERE 46%  OCEAN 23%  LAND 31%
   warming        sink       sink
                  +          +
              acidification  variable storage
```

IPCC AR6 assessed that during **2010-2019**, about **46%** of anthropogenic CO2 emissions accumulated in the atmosphere, **23%** entered the ocean and **31%** was stored by terrestrial vegetation and soils. Across **1959-2019**, the mean airborne fraction was about **44%**, with large year-to-year variability. The SPM summarises this as land and ocean taking up about **56% per year** over six decades.

#### Four distinctions

1. **Gross flux versus net flux:** Natural photosynthesis and respiration are individually large; net imbalance determines whether land is a sink or source.
2. **Sink versus permanent storage:** A forest sink can weaken or reverse through fire, drought, harvest or land-use change.
3. **Ocean heat uptake versus carbon uptake:** Both involve the ocean but are different processes. CO2 uptake lowers pH; heat uptake raises temperature and contributes to thermal expansion.
4. **Airborne fraction versus concentration:** The fraction is a ratio of annual/cumulative partitioning; concentration is atmospheric abundance.

#### Carbon feedbacks

- Higher CO2 can stimulate photosynthesis under some conditions (**carbon fertilisation**), but nutrient, water, temperature and ecosystem constraints limit the response.
- Warming generally raises soil respiration and can weaken land storage.
- Ocean warming and stratification can reduce uptake efficiency.
- Fire, drought, ecosystem dieback and permafrost thaw can release carbon.
- IPCC projects land and ocean sinks to become **less effective proportionally** as cumulative emissions increase, although absolute uptake can still rise under high emissions for a time.

**Answer-grabbing line:** Natural sinks slow atmospheric accumulation; they do not cancel emissions, and their future effectiveness is itself climate-sensitive.

**Must-write keywords:** gross and net; airborne fraction; land sink; ocean sink; carbon fertilisation; respiration; saturation; reversibility.

### 5. Forcing, feedback and response: do not mix the arrows

```text
EXTERNAL DRIVER / FORCING
CO2, CH4, solar change, volcanic aerosol, land-use albedo
                       │
                       v
INITIAL TEMPERATURE / CLIMATE RESPONSE
                       │
          ┌────────────┴────────────┐
          v                         v
 POSITIVE FEEDBACK             NEGATIVE FEEDBACK
 amplifies initial change      damps initial change
                       │
                       v
        NEW ENERGY-BALANCE STATE + IMPACTS
```

| Process | Usual global sign | Mechanism | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Planck response** | Negative | A warmer body emits more longwave radiation to space. | Fundamental stabilising response, not normally called a policy feedback. |
| **Water-vapour feedback** | Positive | Warmer air holds more water vapour; additional vapour strengthens infrared absorption. | Water vapour responds rapidly and is not the initial long-lived forcing. |
| **Lapse-rate feedback** | Negative globally | Vertical warming structure changes outgoing radiation; tropical upper-tropospheric warming tends to increase emission to space. | Regional sign and interaction with water vapour are complex. |
| **Surface-albedo feedback** | Positive | Snow/ice loss exposes darker surfaces, increasing absorption. | Strongest where snow and ice change. |
| **Cloud feedback** | Net positive in AR6, high confidence | Changes in cloud amount, altitude and optical properties alter shortwave reflection and longwave trapping. | Largest contribution to overall feedback uncertainty; individual cloud effects differ. |
| **Carbon-cycle feedback** | Generally positive under warming | Soil respiration, fires, ocean stratification and ecosystem stress leave more CO2 in the atmosphere. | CO2 fertilisation is a negative carbon-concentration feedback but constrained. |
| **Permafrost feedback** | Positive | Thaw exposes frozen organic carbon to decomposition, releasing CO2 and CH4. | Magnitude and timing are uncertain; avoid instant “methane bomb” claims. |

**Forcing-feedback-response firewall**

- CO2 emitted from fossil fuel is a forcing agent.
- Water vapour increase caused by warming is mainly a feedback.
- Higher temperature is a response.
- Sea-level rise is an impact/response pathway, not a forcing.
- Adaptation is a societal response to impacts, not a climate-system feedback.

**Answer-grabbing line:** A forcing initiates an energy imbalance; a feedback changes the size of the resulting response; an impact is the consequence that follows.

### 6. Climate sensitivity: ECS and TCR

| Metric | Question answered | IPCC AR6 assessed value |
|---|---|---|
| **Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity (ECS)** | Eventual global surface warming after atmospheric CO2 is doubled and the fast climate system reaches equilibrium, with slow ice-sheet/carbon-cycle changes generally excluded. | Best estimate **3°C**; likely **2.5-4.0°C**; very likely **2.0-5.0°C**. |
| **Transient Climate Response (TCR)** | Global surface warming near the time CO2 doubles in an idealised experiment where concentration rises 1% per year. It includes ocean heat uptake delaying warming. | Best estimate **1.8°C**; likely **1.4-2.2°C**; very likely **1.2-2.4°C**. |
| **TCRE** | Warming per cumulative CO2 emitted. | AR6 best estimate **0.45°C per 1000 GtCO2**, likely **0.27-0.63°C**. |

ECS exceeds TCR because the deep ocean delays surface warming; equilibrium takes much longer. Neither is “the warming forecast for 2100.” Actual future warming depends on emissions, non-CO2 forcing, carbon-cycle response and scenario.

**Answer-grabbing line:** TCR governs warming during rising forcing; ECS describes the longer-run response after ocean heat uptake has had time to equilibrate.

**UPSC trap:** High model ECS does not automatically invalidate a model, but AR6 projections are constrained using multiple evidence lines rather than adopting the raw CMIP6 spread without assessment.

### 7. Detection, attribution, event attribution and projection

```text
OBSERVATION
   │
   ├─ DETECTION: is the change distinguishable from expected variability?
   │
   ├─ ATTRIBUTION: which human and natural drivers best explain it?
   │
   └─ EVENT ATTRIBUTION: how did forcing alter an event's probability/intensity?

MODEL + ASSUMED FUTURE FORCING
   └─ PROJECTION: conditional future outcome, not a prediction of policy choices
```

#### Detection and attribution

Detection establishes that an observed change is unlikely to be explained by internal variability alone. Attribution evaluates the relative contributions of anthropogenic GHGs, aerosols, solar/volcanic drivers and internal variability using observations, physical understanding and model experiments.

IPCC AR6’s central finding is not based on temperature alone. It uses:

- land and ocean warming;
- tropospheric warming with stratospheric cooling;
- ocean heat accumulation;
- cryosphere loss;
- spatial and seasonal patterns;
- simulations with natural forcing alone versus natural plus anthropogenic forcing.

#### Event attribution

An individual heatwave, rainfall event or drought still has immediate meteorological causes. Event attribution compares the observed climate with counterfactual model ensembles to estimate how human influence changed probability or intensity. Strong conclusions are generally more available for heat extremes than for events heavily controlled by circulation, local exposure or sparse data.

**Correct phrasing:** “Human-caused warming increased the likelihood or intensity of this class of event, according to an event-specific study.”

**Incorrect phrasing:** “Climate change single-handedly caused this cyclone/flood.”

#### Internal variability

ENSO and other coupled modes redistribute heat and moisture and can amplify or temporarily mask regional/decadal trends. They do not create the sustained planetary energy gain observed across the ocean-atmosphere-cryosphere system.

**Answer-grabbing line:** Detection asks whether a signal exists; attribution asks why; projection asks what follows under stated assumptions.

### 8. Climate models, RCPs, SSPs and uncertainty

#### 8.1 What a climate model does

Climate models numerically solve conservation laws and represent atmosphere, ocean, land, ice, radiation, chemistry and biogeochemical processes. Ensembles sample internal variability and model differences. Models are tested against observations, process behaviour and paleoclimate evidence, but regional rainfall and clouds remain harder than global temperature.

#### 8.2 Scenario family map

| Term | Meaning | Exam-safe formulation |
|---|---|---|
| **RCP** | Representative Concentration Pathway, named by approximate radiative forcing in 2100, such as 2.6 or 8.5 W/m²; central to CMIP5/AR5. | A concentration/forcing pathway, not a probability statement. |
| **SSP** | Shared Socioeconomic Pathway: a socioeconomic development narrative. | SSP number alone does not specify forcing. |
| **SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, etc.** | AR6/CMIP6 scenario combining a socioeconomic pathway with an approximate 2100 forcing level. | Same forcing suffix does not make it identical to an older RCP. |

IPCC AR6 assessed five illustrative scenarios: SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5. They are conditional “if-then” pathways, not forecasts of which social future will occur.

#### 8.3 Four uncertainty sources

1. **Internal variability:** especially important regionally and in the near term.
2. **Model response uncertainty:** clouds, feedbacks, ocean uptake and regional processes.
3. **Scenario uncertainty:** future emissions and land-use choices; grows later in the century.
4. **Observational and structural uncertainty:** incomplete records, resolution and parameterisation.

Confidence is not ignorance. IPCC calibrated language separates evidence and agreement; a range can be decision-relevant even when exact local timing remains uncertain.

**Answer-grabbing line:** A climate projection is conditional on a forcing pathway; uncertainty broadens the range but does not erase robust directional findings.

### 9. Indicators: how a warming planet is diagnosed

| Indicator | Physical mechanism | Current authoritative anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **Global surface temperature** | Positive forcing raises surface-troposphere temperature. | WMO: 2025 was the second or third warmest year, **1.43 +/- 0.13°C above 1850-1900**. A single year above 1.5°C would not by itself mean the Paris long-term threshold has formally been crossed. |
| **Earth energy imbalance** | More energy enters/stays than leaves; warming restores outgoing radiation only gradually. | WMO added energy imbalance as a key indicator in its 2025 report and stated it reached a new observed high. |
| **Ocean heat content** | Ocean stores most excess climate-system heat. | IPCC: ocean warming accounted for **91%** of climate-system heating during 1971-2018. WMO: 2025 ocean heat content was the highest in the 66-year record. |
| **Sea level** | Ocean thermal expansion plus land-ice loss; regional land motion and circulation affect relative sea level. | WMO: 2012-2025 rate **4.75 mm/year**, versus **2.65 mm/year** in 1993-2011; 2025 remained near the 2024 record. |
| **Cryosphere** | Atmospheric and ocean warming reduce glaciers, snow, sea ice and ice sheets. | WMO: 2025 Arctic maximum sea-ice extent was the lowest annual maximum in the observed record; reference-glacier balance in 2024/25 was among the five most negative. |
| **Ocean acidification** | Dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid, shifting carbonate chemistry and lowering pH. | It is caused by CO2 uptake, not by warming alone. |
| **Extremes** | A warmer baseline and intensified hydrological cycle alter distributions. | AR6 finds stronger attribution for hot extremes and heavy precipitation than for every regional drought or cyclone metric. |

#### Sea-level impact chain

```text
GHG forcing
  ├─ ocean warming -> thermal expansion
  └─ land-ice melt -> ocean mass increase
                         │
                         v
GLOBAL MEAN SEA-LEVEL RISE
                         │
        tides + surge + waves + subsidence/uplift
                         │
                         v
relative local flooding, erosion, salinisation and habitability risk
```

Sea ice melt has little direct effect on sea level because it already floats; land ice does. However, sea-ice loss amplifies warming through albedo feedback.

### 10. India: monsoon, heat, ocean and cryosphere—qualified correctly

#### 10.1 Current observation anchor

IMD’s **Statement on the Climate of India during 2025** reports:

- all-India annual mean land-surface air temperature **0.28°C above the 1991-2020 average**;
- 2025 was the **eighth warmest** year since nationwide records began in 1901;
- 2024 remained warmest at **+0.65°C** on that baseline;
- 2016-2025 was the warmest decade, with a **+0.32°C** decadal anomaly.

These are Indian land observations with a 1991-2020 baseline. They cannot be directly compared as if identical to WMO’s global 1850-1900 anomaly.

#### 10.2 Monsoon

Warming increases atmospheric moisture and can intensify heavy precipitation, but the Indian monsoon also responds to aerosols, land-sea thermal contrast, Indian Ocean conditions, ENSO, circulation and land-use change. Therefore:

- distinguish seasonal total rainfall from short-duration extremes;
- distinguish all-India means from regional trends;
- do not treat one flood or deficient monsoon as proof of a long-term trend;
- use event-specific attribution for individual extremes.

The MoES assessment of climate change over India reports warming over India, substantial tropical Indian Ocean warming, changing monsoon characteristics and increasing heavy-rainfall risk. These are observed/assessed regional statements, not simple downscaling of global averages.

#### 10.3 Heat

A higher mean shifts the temperature distribution, making previous high thresholds easier to exceed. Urban heat islands, humidity, land cover, power access and occupational exposure shape actual harm. A heatwave is thus a hazard produced by climate and weather, while disaster severity also depends on exposure and vulnerability.

#### 10.4 Indian Ocean, coasts and Himalaya

- Indian Ocean heat affects marine heatwaves, evaporation and monsoon interactions.
- Sea-level rise combines with storm surge, waves, erosion and subsidence along Indian coasts.
- Himalayan snow, glaciers and permafrost respond to warming, but basin impacts vary by glacier, season and catchment.
- Black-carbon deposition can reduce snow/ice albedo, but it is one driver among temperature, precipitation and topography.

**Answer-grabbing line:** India’s climate risk is a compound-risk problem: global forcing sets the background, regional ocean-atmosphere dynamics shape the hazard, and exposure and vulnerability determine the disaster.

### 11. Response firewall: mitigation, adaptation, loss and damage, CDR and SRM

```text
CAUSES --------------------> IMPACTS --------------------> RESIDUAL HARM
  │                           │                              │
  v                           v                              v
MITIGATION                 ADAPTATION                  LOSS AND DAMAGE
reduce sources /           adjust systems to          adverse impacts not
enhance sinks              actual/expected effects    avoided by mitigation
                                                       and adaptation
```

| Response | Scientific boundary | Detailed owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitigation** | Reduce GHG sources or enhance sinks. CO2 mitigation limits cumulative warming; rapid CH4 cuts affect near-term warming. | Topics 19-21 and 25 |
| **Adaptation** | Adjustment to actual or expected climate and effects; reduces exposure or vulnerability but has limits. | Topics 20, 24 and 26 |
| **Loss and damage** | Adverse residual impacts, including extreme and slow-onset effects, that are not or cannot be fully avoided. It is not a synonym for adaptation. | Topic 19 negotiations; disaster topics |
| **Carbon dioxide removal (CDR)** | Anthropogenic removal of atmospheric CO2 with durable storage. It falls within mitigation but varies greatly in permanence, land, energy and ecological effects. | Topic 21 |
| **Solar radiation modification (SRM)** | Intentional planetary-scale alteration of the radiative budget to limit warming. IPCC does **not** classify SRM as mitigation or adaptation. | Topic 21 |

#### Geoengineering firewall

- CDR addresses atmospheric CO2 only if removal is additional and durably stored; it cannot substitute for unlimited emissions.
- SRM could cool surface temperature but would not stop CO2 accumulation or ocean acidification under continued emissions.
- Abrupt sustained SRM termination could cause rapid warming.
- SRM and CDR must never be merged into one undifferentiated “geoengineering solution”.

UNEP’s **Emissions Gap Report 2025** estimates warming of **2.3-2.5°C** under full NDC implementation and **2.8°C** under current policies. These are conditional policy-path projections, not observed warming and not physical forecasts independent of human choices.

**Answer-grabbing line:** Mitigation changes the hazard’s future magnitude, adaptation changes vulnerability and exposure, and loss and damage concerns the residual that remains.

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1

Which one of the following most accurately distinguishes weather from climate?

A. Weather is the state of the atmosphere over short periods, whereas climate is the statistical distribution of weather over a long period.
B. Weather concerns only rainfall, whereas climate concerns only temperature.
C. Weather is local, whereas climate can only be global.
D. Weather is observed, whereas climate is produced only by models.

**Answer: A**

**Option explanations**

- **A — Correct.** It preserves both time-scale and statistical-distribution distinctions.
- **B — Incorrect.** Both weather and climate include temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity and other variables.
- **C — Incorrect.** Climate can be local, regional or global.
- **D — Incorrect.** Climate is derived from observations as well as analysed and projected with models.

### MCQ 2

Consider the following statements:

1. Internal climate variability can temporarily amplify or offset a long-term trend in a region.
2. A single unusually cool year cannot by itself disprove long-term global warming.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

A. 1 only
B. Both 1 and 2
C. 2 only
D. Neither 1 nor 2

**Answer: B**

**Option explanations**

- **A — Incorrect.** Statement 2 is also correct.
- **B — Correct.** Internal variability changes short-period outcomes around the forced trend; one year is insufficient trend evidence.
- **C — Incorrect.** Statement 1 correctly describes variability.
- **D — Incorrect.** Both statements follow the weather-climate and variability-trend distinction.

### MCQ 3

Which sequence best represents the basic terrestrial energy-budget mechanism?

A. Longwave sunlight -> surface reflection only -> ozone depletion -> cooling
B. Shortwave sunlight -> complete atmospheric absorption -> no surface heating -> longwave escape
C. Shortwave solar input -> reflection and absorption -> surface longwave emission -> greenhouse absorption and re-emission
D. Infrared sunlight -> ocean acidification -> cloud removal -> surface warming

**Answer: C**

**Option explanations**

- **A — Incorrect.** Solar input is predominantly shortwave, and the sequence omits absorption and surface emission.
- **B — Incorrect.** The atmosphere does not absorb all incoming shortwave radiation; the surface absorbs a substantial share.
- **C — Correct.** It captures incoming shortwave, albedo/absorption, outgoing longwave and GHG interaction.
- **D — Incorrect.** Ocean acidification is a carbon-chemistry impact, not a step in the radiative sequence.

### MCQ 4

Which statement about the greenhouse effect is correct?

A. Any greenhouse effect is necessarily anthropogenic.

### Prelims PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-I Q65

**Question:** Which of the following statements best describes “carbon fertilization”?

C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Official key: D**

**Solution:** SRI uses younger, widely spaced seedlings and less seed; intermittent irrigation reduces prolonged anaerobic conditions and methane formation; reduced pumping can lower electricity use. Site practice matters, and poorly managed drying can affect N2O, but all three listed outcomes form the official answer.

### Mains PYQ 1 — 2022 GS-III Q17 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Discuss global warming and mention its effects on the global climate. Explain the control measures to bring down the level of greenhouse gases which cause global warming, in the light of the Kyoto Protocol, 1997. (Answer in 250 words)

**Ownership:** Topic 17 directly owns mechanism/effects; Topic 19 owns Kyoto architecture.

**Model answer (241 words):**

Global warming is the long-term rise in global mean surface temperature caused mainly by anthropogenic enhancement of the natural greenhouse effect. Fossil-fuel use, cement production, land-use change and agriculture raise CO2, CH4 and N2O concentrations, producing positive radiative forcing. IPCC AR6 estimated human-caused warming at 1.07°C for 2010-2019 relative to 1850-1900.

The effects extend beyond temperature. Ocean heat uptake causes thermal expansion; land-ice loss adds water, raising sea level. A warmer atmosphere intensifies the water cycle, increasing hot extremes and heavy precipitation while altering regional drought risk. Glaciers, snow and sea ice decline; ocean CO2 uptake causes acidification; ecosystems and food systems face shifting climatic envelopes. Internal variability shapes individual seasons, so no single disaster should be attributed without event-specific evidence.

Control requires a gas- and timescale-sensitive strategy: rapid CO2 reduction for cumulative warming; methane abatement in energy, waste and agriculture for near-term benefit; N2O reduction through efficient nitrogen management; protection and restoration of durable natural sinks; and limits on high-GWP fluorinated gases. Adaptation cannot replace mitigation.

The **Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997**, translated CBDR-RC into binding quantified commitments for developed Annex I parties. Its flexibility mechanisms were **emissions trading, Joint Implementation and the Clean Development Mechanism**, which enabled lower-cost reductions and projects in developing countries. However, limited country coverage and uneven participation constrained global impact.

Thus, Kyoto supplied an early legal and market architecture, but physical stabilisation ultimately requires net-zero CO2 and sustained cuts in other forcing agents.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers mechanism, effects and Kyoto measures separately, uses named evidence, and qualifies event attribution and treaty limits.

### Mains PYQ 2 — 2023 GS-I Q4 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Discuss the consequences of climate change on the food security in tropical countries. (Answer in 150 words)

**Model answer (149 words):**

Climate change affects all four pillars of tropical food security: availability, access, utilisation and stability. Higher baseline temperatures accelerate crop development and can exceed reproductive heat thresholds; warmer nights also reduce yields. IPCC-assessed intensification of heat and heavy rainfall raises crop failure, erosion and post-harvest loss, while altered rainfall and drought disrupt sowing and irrigation.

Tropical fisheries face marine heatwaves, deoxygenation and shifting stocks; ocean acidification harms sensitive food webs. Livestock suffer heat stress, disease and reduced productivity. These supply shocks raise prices and wages may not adjust, worsening access for poor households. Floods can contaminate water and increase food-borne disease, reducing nutritional utilisation.

CO2 fertilisation cannot be treated as a full offset because nutrient, water and heat constraints limit gains and food quality may decline. Responses require heat- and drought-tolerant varieties, diversified farms, water management, climate services, storage and safety nets, alongside mitigation; adaptation limits rise with warming.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer follows a four-pillar structure, links mechanisms to outcomes and avoids presenting carbon fertilisation as unlimited.

### Mains PYQ 3 — 2025 GS-I Q4 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** How are climate change and the sea level rise affecting the very existence of many island nations? Discuss with examples. (Answer in 150 words)

**Model answer (148 words):**

Climate change threatens island nations through compound rather than sea-level-only risk. Ocean warming causes thermal expansion and land-ice melt adds mass; WMO reports that global mean sea level rose at 4.75 mm/year during 2012-2025. A higher mean allows tides, waves and storm surges to flood farther inland.

Low-lying atoll states such as **Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives** face erosion, saline intrusion into freshwater lenses and soils, infrastructure loss and shrinking habitable land. Coral bleaching and ocean acidification weaken reefs that provide food, tourism revenue and wave protection. Displacement can strain culture, land tenure and sovereignty even before complete inundation.

Risk varies with elevation, reef health, sediment supply, subsidence, protection and migration options; therefore “disappearance” is not a single-date prediction. Adaptation includes freshwater security, ecosystem restoration, resilient infrastructure and planned mobility, but hard limits emerge as warming and sea level rise continue. Deep global mitigation remains indispensable.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives mechanism, examples, compound impacts, variation, adaptation limits and a qualified conclusion.

### 15. Six original Mains questions with model answers

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish weather, climate variability and climate change. Why can a single extreme event neither prove nor disprove climate change?

**Model answer (141 words):**

Weather is the atmospheric condition at a particular time and place. Climate is the long-period statistical distribution of weather, including means, variability and extremes. Climate variability is fluctuation around that distribution due to processes such as ENSO; climate change is a persistent shift in the distribution caused by external forcing or longer-term internal change.

A single event arises from immediate meteorological conditions and local exposure. It may occur without anthropogenic forcing and therefore cannot alone establish a changed climate. Conversely, one cold spell cannot negate global warming because internal variability redistributes heat regionally and temporarily.

Event attribution instead compares observations and model ensembles representing the present climate with a counterfactual climate without human forcing. It estimates how warming changed an event’s probability or intensity. Thus, trends establish changing risk, while event-specific studies connect that altered background risk to a particular episode.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three terms and explains the counterfactual logic rather than using a slogan.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain Earth’s energy balance and distinguish the natural greenhouse effect from its anthropogenic enhancement.

**Model answer (134 words):**

Earth receives predominantly shortwave solar radiation. NASA estimates a global average near 340 W/m², of which about 29% is reflected by clouds, atmosphere and bright surfaces. The remainder is absorbed, warming the system. Earth emits longwave infrared radiation; greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit selected wavelengths, including downward, while the planet ultimately loses energy to space.

The **natural greenhouse effect** raises surface temperature by roughly 33°C and makes Earth habitable. The present problem is its **anthropogenic enhancement**: fossil-fuel combustion, cement manufacture, land-use change and agriculture increase CO2, CH4 and N2O, while aerosols and land-cover changes modify other radiative terms.

IPCC AR6 assessed net human-caused effective radiative forcing at 2.72 W/m² in 2019 relative to 1750. This creates an energy imbalance; warming then increases outgoing radiation until balance is approached at a higher temperature.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects shortwave, albedo, longwave, forcing and equilibrium with an exact sourced figure.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Differentiate forcing, feedback and response. Examine the major feedbacks that determine the magnitude of global warming.

**Model answer (227 words):**

A **radiative forcing** is an externally imposed change in Earth’s energy balance, such as increased CO2, volcanic aerosol or solar variation. A **feedback** is a process triggered by the climate response that changes its magnitude. A **response** is the resulting temperature, precipitation, ocean, ice or ecosystem change.

The fundamental stabiliser is the **Planck response**: a warmer Earth emits more longwave radiation. Against it operate several feedbacks:

- **Water vapour:** warming raises atmospheric moisture, strengthening infrared absorption; this is positive.
- **Lapse rate:** the vertical warming pattern, especially tropical upper-tropospheric warming, generally increases emission to space and is globally negative.
- **Surface albedo:** snow and ice loss exposes darker surfaces, increasing absorbed sunlight; positive.
- **Clouds:** changes in height, amount and optical properties alter both reflected shortwave and trapped longwave radiation. IPCC AR6 assesses net cloud feedback as positive, but clouds remain the largest feedback uncertainty.
- **Carbon cycle:** soil respiration, fire, ecosystem stress, ocean warming and stratification can leave more CO2 airborne. CO2 fertilisation partly opposes this but is nutrient- and water-limited.
- **Permafrost:** thaw exposes organic carbon to decomposition, adding CO2 and CH4; positive but uncertain in magnitude and timing.

These distinctions prevent category errors: fossil CO2 is a forcing; water-vapour increase is mainly a feedback; sea-level rise is a response. The warming outcome therefore depends not merely on emissions but on how feedbacks and ocean heat uptake shape the system’s adjustment.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the three categories, examines sign and mechanism, and qualifies clouds and permafrost.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Distinguish detection, attribution and projection. How should scenarios and uncertainty be used in climate-risk decisions?

**Model answer (201 words):**

**Detection** asks whether an observed change is distinguishable from expected internal variability. **Attribution** evaluates the relative contributions of anthropogenic greenhouse gases, aerosols, natural forcing and variability. **Projection** is a conditional model estimate of future climate under a stated emissions, concentration or forcing pathway.

Attribution is strongest when multiple fingerprints agree: surface and ocean warming, ocean heat gain, glacier and sea-ice loss, tropospheric warming with stratospheric cooling, and simulations in which natural forcing alone cannot reproduce observations. Event attribution is narrower: it estimates how human influence changed a particular event’s probability or intensity, not whether climate change was its sole cause.

Scenarios organise uncertainty. RCPs describe concentration/forcing pathways; AR6 SSP labels combine socioeconomic pathways with approximate 2100 forcing. They are “if-then” experiments, not predictions that governments will choose a specified future.

Risk decisions must separate:

1. internal variability, important regionally and near term;
2. model-response uncertainty, including clouds and regional rainfall;
3. scenario uncertainty, increasingly important later;
4. observational and structural limits.

Uncertainty is not a reason for paralysis. Robust decisions use ranges, test performance across scenarios, prioritise low-regret adaptation and preserve flexibility. They also distinguish observed Indian evidence from global findings and avoid converting a projection into a current fact.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates evidence, scenario taxonomy and decision-making rather than merely defining terms.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Climate mitigation must be gas-specific and carbon-cycle-aware. Analyse with reference to greenhouse gases, aerosols, sinks and emissions metrics.

**Model answer (238 words):**

Climate forcing is produced by agents with different sources, lifetimes and radiative effects; therefore a single undifferentiated “emissions cut” frame is inadequate.

**CO2** from fossil fuel, cement and land-use change is a cumulative stock problem. It has no single lifetime, and stabilising CO2-driven warming requires net-zero anthropogenic CO2. **Methane** from energy, livestock, rice and waste is stronger per unit mass over short horizons but lasts about 11.8 years; rapid cuts can reduce near-term warming. **N2O**, largely linked to nitrogen management, lasts about 109 years and also affects stratospheric ozone. Fluorinated gases require compound-specific treatment.

Metrics preserve different policy questions. GWP integrates radiative forcing over a stated horizon; GTP compares temperature at a future point. IPCC AR6 gives fossil methane GWP-20 of 82.5 and GWP-100 of 29.8, illustrating why a horizon-free value misleads.

Carbon-cycle awareness is equally necessary. During 2010-2019, IPCC assessed 46% of anthropogenic CO2 remaining in the atmosphere, 23% entering the ocean and 31% entering land. These sinks slow accumulation but are climate-sensitive and not guaranteed permanent storage.

Aerosols add a transition complication: sulphate cooling has masked some greenhouse warming, while black carbon warms and darkens snow. Clean-air policy brings major health benefits but must be accompanied by rapid GHG cuts to avoid unmasking without mitigation.

Hence an effective portfolio combines durable CO2 reduction, rapid methane abatement, efficient nitrogen use, F-gas control and protected high-integrity sinks, while reporting metric, horizon, permanence and uncertainty explicitly.

**Why this earns marks:** It links gas physics, exact metrics, sink limits and the aerosol transition trade-off.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess the major indicators of contemporary climate change and explain how they translate into differentiated risks for India.

**Model answer (243 words):**

Contemporary climate change is diagnosed through a coherent system of indicators rather than temperature alone. WMO reports that 2025 was about 1.43°C above 1850-1900 and that ocean heat content reached a record in the 66-year series. Rising ocean heat demonstrates continued planetary energy gain even when surface temperature fluctuates.

Thermal expansion and land-ice loss raise sea level; WMO estimates 4.75 mm/year during 2012-2025, compared with 2.65 mm/year during 1993-2011. Glacier mass loss, record-low Arctic maximum sea ice and changing snow/permafrost corroborate cryosphere warming. Ocean CO2 uptake lowers pH, while a warmer atmosphere intensifies hot extremes and can strengthen heavy precipitation. Event attribution, however, must remain event- and region-specific.

India’s risks are differentiated. IMD found 2025 Indian land temperature 0.28°C above the 1991-2020 mean and the eighth warmest since 1901. Heat hazard combines this warmer baseline with humidity, urban heat islands, outdoor work and unequal cooling access. Monsoon outcomes reflect greater moisture alongside aerosols, ENSO, Indian Ocean conditions and circulation; seasonal totals and short-duration extremes can move differently. Coastal risk combines sea-level rise with surge, erosion and subsidence. Himalayan effects vary across glaciers and basins.

Policy should therefore join mitigation with heat-health plans, climate services, resilient water and urban systems, coastal ecosystem/infrastructure measures and social protection. Yet adaptation has limits: continuing emissions raise irreversible ocean and ice commitments. Global metrics must not be mechanically downscaled; Indian observations and local vulnerability determine the final risk.

**Why this earns marks:** It moves from multiple indicators to qualified India-specific pathways and a mitigation-adaptation conclusion.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Attribution is a causal comparison, not a correlation

Detection-and-attribution studies compare spatiotemporal fingerprints under natural-only and anthropogenic-plus-natural forcing, while event studies compare factual and counterfactual ensembles. Confidence varies because observational length, model fidelity and physical mechanisms differ by event. Heat has a direct thermodynamic signal; precipitation and drought depend more strongly on circulation, soil moisture and regional data.

### B. Effective radiative forcing budget

IPCC AR6 assessed **net anthropogenic ERF of 2.72 [1.96-3.48] W/m² in 2019 relative to 1750**. Increased GHG concentrations dominate positive forcing; aerosols offset part of it. The total aerosol ERF for 1750-2019 was assessed at **-1.1 [-1.7 to -0.4] W/m²**. Cleaner air therefore removes harmful particulate pollution and some masking, making simultaneous GHG mitigation essential.

### C. Sensitivity is evidence-constrained

Raw CMIP6 models span sensitivities wider than the AR6 assessed range. AR6 combines process understanding, instrumental warming, paleoclimate and emergent constraints. The assessed ECS best estimate is 3°C, while TCR is 1.8°C. This is why “model spread” and “assessed uncertainty” are not identical.

### D. Carbon budget and sink weakening

AR6 estimated a remaining carbon budget from the start of 2020 of **500 GtCO2** for a 50% chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C. This figure is assessment- and date-specific and has been depleted by subsequent emissions; it must not be presented as a current 2026 balance. The robust principle is cumulative CO2 proportionality: delayed cuts consume finite budget and require steeper later reductions or removals.

### E. Carbon-cycle asymmetry and permanence

Removing one tonne of CO2 later does not perfectly reverse every effect of emitting one tonne earlier because ocean and land responses are state-dependent. CDR claims therefore require additionality, durability, leakage, energy/land effects and monitoring. Natural restoration can provide biodiversity and adaptation co-benefits, but fire or land-use reversal threatens permanence.

### F. Current-policy projection is not physical observation

UNEP’s 2025 estimate of 2.3-2.5°C under full NDC implementation and 2.8°C under current policies is a conditional policy assessment. WMO’s 1.43°C for 2025 is an observed annual temperature estimate. Combining them in one sentence without labels commits an observation-projection error.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Definition and scale map

- Weather = short-term atmospheric state; climate = long-period statistical distribution.
- Variability moves conditions around a distribution; climate change shifts the distribution.
- Global warming is temperature rise; climate change is the broader system change.
- One event neither proves nor disproves a trend; event attribution estimates changed probability/intensity.

### Energy-balance spine

- About 340 W/m² incoming solar energy; about 29% reflected (NASA).
- Absorbed shortwave warms Earth; Earth emits longwave infrared.
- GHGs absorb/re-emit selected longwave wavelengths.
- Natural greenhouse effect is life-enabling and about 33°C; anthropogenic enhancement is the problem.
- Albedo: bright snow/ice/clouds reflect more; dark ocean/land absorb more.

### Agent map

- CO2: fossil fuel, cement, land use; cumulative stock; no single lifetime.
- CH4: energy, livestock, rice, waste; OH sink; 11.8 +/- 1.8 years.
- N2O: agricultural soils/fertiliser; stratospheric sink; 109 +/- 10 years.
- Water vapour: fast positive feedback, not primary long-lived forcing.
- Tropospheric ozone: secondary warming pollutant; stratospheric ozone protects from UV.
- Sulphate/many aerosols cool; black carbon warms and darkens snow.
- GWP = integrated forcing; GTP = endpoint temperature; always state horizon/report.

### Carbon-cycle ledger

- 2010-2019: 46% atmosphere, 23% ocean, 31% land.
- Six-decade mean airborne fraction about 44%; annual variation is large.
- Sinks slow accumulation but can weaken/reverse; gross flux is not net storage.
- Ocean carbon uptake causes acidification; ocean heat uptake causes warming/expansion.
- Carbon fertilisation is constrained by water, nutrients, heat and permanence.

### Forcing-feedback-response map

- Forcing initiates imbalance: CO2, CH4, aerosols, solar/volcanic change.
- Feedback changes response: water vapour +; ice-albedo +; lapse rate generally -; clouds net + but uncertain; carbon/permafrost +.
- Response/impact: warming, rainfall change, ocean heat, ice loss, sea-level rise.
- Planck response is the fundamental negative stabiliser.

### Sensitivity, models and scenarios

- ECS: best 3°C; likely 2.5-4°C.
- TCR: best 1.8°C; likely 1.4-2.2°C.
- TCRE: best 0.45°C per 1000 GtCO2.
- RCP = concentration/forcing pathway; SSP = socioeconomic pathway; SSPx-y combines both.
- Projection = conditional; not forecast. Separate variability, model, scenario and observational uncertainty.

### Indicator and India map

- WMO 2025: 1.43 +/- 0.13°C above 1850-1900; second/third warmest.
- WMO 2025: record ocean heat; sea-level rate 4.75 mm/year in 2012-2025; Arctic maximum sea ice lowest.
- IMD 2025 India: +0.28°C versus 1991-2020; eighth warmest since 1901.
- Indian monsoon: moisture thermodynamics + aerosols/ocean/ENSO/circulation/internal variability.
- Heat risk = hazard + humidity/urban form/work exposure/vulnerability.
- Coastal risk = mean sea level + surge/waves/erosion/subsidence.

### Response and PYQ route

- Mitigation reduces sources/enhances sinks; adaptation adjusts to effects; loss and damage is residual harm.
- CDR removes and durably stores CO2; SRM changes radiative balance and does not stop acidification.
- Prelims anchors: carbon fertilisation; methane hydrates; rice CH4/N2O; SRI; cement calcination; India emissions metrics.
- Mains route: definition -> mechanism -> indicators -> impacts -> India evidence -> response -> baseline/confidence qualification.
