## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Ecological succession: a trajectory, not any ecological change

#### Visual gateway

```text
STARTING CONDITION
new substrate or disturbance
        |
        v
arrival of propagules -> establishment -> population increase
        |                    |                 |
        +---------- competition/coaction ------+
                             |
                             v
community modifies site + external forces continue
                             |
                             v
replacement / coexistence / patch turnover
                             |
                             v
RELATIVELY PERSISTENT STATE — still disturbance-sensitive
```

✅ **Ecological succession** is directional change through time in community composition,
structure and ecosystem processes after a new substrate appears or an earlier community is
disturbed. “Directional” does not mean that every site follows one fixed sequence, reaches a
forest, or becomes permanently stable.

✅ A **sere** is the complete succession sequence at a site. A **seral community/stage** is
one community within that sequence. Succession is different from:

| Change | Why it is not automatically succession |
|---|---|
| seasonal leaf fall or migration | recurrent phenology without community replacement |
| short population fluctuation | abundance changes may occur without directional community change |
| zonation across space | a spatial gradient; it becomes succession only if one site changes through time |
| evolution | genetic change across generations, not community replacement as such |

#### Classical process vocabulary

| Stage | Exact meaning | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Nudation** | creation/exposure of a bare or severely disturbed area | secondary succession need not begin fully bare |
| **Migration/invasion** | arrival of seeds, spores, larvae or other propagules | arrival does not ensure establishment |
| **Ecesis** | successful establishment and initial growth | controlled by substrate and stress |
| **Aggregation** | increase of established populations | raises interaction and resource demand |
| **Competition/coaction** | interactions among growing populations | facilitation can occur alongside competition |
| **Reaction** | organisms modify soil, light, litter, nutrients or microclimate | a major autogenic pathway |
| **Stabilisation** | relative persistence under the prevailing regime | modern ecology rejects permanent finality |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Succession is a time-ordered reorganisation of a community,
> but its direction is constrained by substrate and climate and made contingent by
> disturbance, dispersal and history.

**Must-write keywords:** direction · trajectory · community composition · sere · seral stage ·
propagule · ecesis · reaction · relative stability.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** succession is directional but not deterministic.
**Named evidence:** a bare rock lithosere requires colonisation and soil formation, whereas an
abandoned field retains soil and seed sources. **Analysis:** different starting legacies alter
speed and possible pathways. **Qualification:** neither pathway guarantees one permanent
forest climax.

### 2. Primary and secondary succession: use the soil-and-legacies test

#### Side-by-side comparison

| Test | Primary succession | Secondary succession |
|---|---|---|
| Initial surface | newly exposed or biologically impoverished substrate | previously occupied, disturbed site |
| Developed soil | absent or rudimentary | usually retained |
| Biological legacies | few; colonisation mainly from outside | seed bank, roots, microbes, litter or survivors may remain |
| Early bottleneck | substrate stabilisation and soil development | competition, dispersal and disturbance legacy |
| Usual relative speed | slower | faster, other conditions equal |
| Indian application | fresh landslide scar, exposed rock, severe mine spoil: often **primary-like** | jhum fallow, abandoned field, many post-fire sites |

✅ Primary succession begins where a developed soil is absent. Lichens, microbial crusts,
mosses or stress-tolerant plants may act as early colonists, but the exact pioneer depends on
moisture, chemistry and propagule supply. A lichen is a symbiotic association, not a plant.

✅ Secondary succession follows disturbance where soil and usually some **biological
legacies** survive. It is commonly faster because nutrient pools, microbes, roots or a seed
bank shorten the establishment pathway.

⚠️ A fire does not automatically mean primary succession. If soil and propagules survive,
recovery is secondary. Only exceptionally severe disturbance that removes or sterilises the
substrate can make the pathway primary-like.

⚠️ Mine spoil should also be described carefully. Some spoil lacks developed soil and behaves
like a primary site; other spoil contains soil fragments, seeds or contaminants. “Primary-like”
is safer than declaring every mine site a textbook primary sere.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The decisive distinction is not whether the site looks empty,
> but whether soil and biological memory survived the disturbance.

**Must-write keywords:** developed soil · seed bank · surviving roots · microbial community ·
biological legacy · substrate · primary-like · recovery rate.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** secondary recovery is generally faster.
**Named evidence:** a Northeast Indian jhum fallow retains soil and underground/nearby
propagules, unlike newly exposed rock. **Analysis:** retained legacies bypass the long
soil-building phase. **Qualification:** repeated short fallows, erosion or invasive dominance
can still arrest or redirect recovery.

### 3. Named seres, hydrarch and xerarch pathways

#### Starting-substrate map

```text
ROCK -------- lithosere \
SAND -------- psammosere \
SALINE SITE - halosere ----> named by STARTING CONDITION,
FRESH WATER - hydrosere /     not by a guaranteed endpoint
DRY SITE ---- xerosere   /
```

✅ **Hydrarch succession** begins in a wet or aquatic setting. In the classical hydrosere,
organic matter and sediments progressively alter depth and rooting conditions:

```text
open water
 -> plankton/submerged plants
 -> floating-leaved plants
 -> reed-swamp
 -> sedge/meadow
 -> scrub/woodland or another regional community
```

The useful direction is **hydric toward more mesic terrestrial conditions**. It is wrong to
say hydrarch succession necessarily moves toward a wetter climax. It is equally wrong to treat
lake infilling as inevitable: flushing, depth, wave action, sediment supply, grazing and human
water management can interrupt or reverse the sequence.

✅ **Xerarch succession** begins on a dry substrate. A classical lithosere may move through
crustose and foliose lichens, mosses, herbs and shrubs as weathering, litter and moisture
retention improve. The exact sequence varies; a dryland grassland, scrub or desert community
may itself be the appropriate regional endpoint.

| Close pair | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| hydrosere / hydrarch | sequence beginning in fresh water / moisture-defined pathway |
| xerosere / xerarch | sequence beginning on a dry site / dry-start pathway |
| lithosere / psammosere | rock start / sand start |
| halosere / hydrosere | saline start / freshwater start |
| sere / seral stage | whole sequence / one community within it |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A sere name identifies the ecological starting point; it does
> not pre-write one universal sequence or endpoint.

**Must-write keywords:** lithosere · psammosere · halosere · hydrosere · xerosere · hydric ·
xeric · mesic · infilling · substrate.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** hydrarch and xerarch pathways can converge toward
more mesic conditions under the same regional climate. **Named evidence:** sediment and litter
can make a pond shallower, while lichens and mosses can improve water retention on rock.
**Analysis:** both pathways modify the moisture regime. **Qualification:** climate, hydrology
and disturbance can preserve open water, grassland or scrub instead of producing forest.

### 4. Who drives change? Autogenic and allogenic succession

| Driver | Source of change | Examples | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autogenic** | the community changes its own environment | litter, humus, shade, nitrogen fixation, soil binding, altered microclimate | not every internal change favours later species |
| **Allogenic** | forces external to the community alter the site | flooding, sediment input, erosion, fire regime, storm, climate shift, grazing or land conversion | organisms may mediate the response without becoming the original driver |

✅ Autogenic and allogenic are causal labels, not separate chronological stages. They can act
together. Reeds may trap externally supplied sediment; the sediment source is allogenic, while
the trapping and organic-matter accumulation are partly autogenic.

#### Connell-Slatyer establishment models

| Model | Effect of early occupants on later species | Replacement logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Facilitation** | improve conditions for later colonists | pioneers prepare the site and are later replaced |
| **Tolerance** | neither necessary help nor decisive suppression | later species establish because they tolerate available conditions/resources |
| **Inhibition** | suppress establishment or growth of later species | replacement follows death, damage or removal of incumbents |

✅ These are alternative mechanisms, not a compulsory three-step sequence. A landscape can
show facilitation in one patch and inhibition in another.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Successional replacement depends not only on who arrives first,
> but on whether early occupants facilitate, merely coexist with, or inhibit later colonists.

**Must-write keywords:** autogenic · allogenic · reaction · facilitation · tolerance ·
inhibition · establishment · priority effect · external forcing.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** pioneer presence has three possible effects.
**Named example:** a nurse shrub may moderate heat and facilitate seedlings; a dense grass mat
may instead inhibit tree establishment. **Analysis:** the sign of the interaction changes the
trajectory. **Qualification:** the same species can facilitate under severe stress yet compete
under milder conditions.

### 5. Climax theory, non-equilibrium ecology and patch dynamics

#### From one endpoint to a shifting mosaic

```text
CLASSICAL VIEW
regional climate -> orderly sere -> single climatic climax
                         |
                         v
MODERN QUALIFICATION
climate envelope + soil/topography + species pool + dispersal
+ disturbance regime + chance/history
                         |
                         v
multiple pathways, persistent mosaics and sometimes alternative states
```

✅ A **climax community** is best used as a relatively persistent community under a prevailing
environment and disturbance regime. It is not immutable.

✅ Modern ecology treats disturbance as a normal structuring process. Windthrow, fire,
flooding, herbivory and treefall create patches of different ages. **Patch dynamics** explains
how a landscape can remain broadly persistent while local patches continually change.

✅ A **disturbance regime** includes type, frequency, intensity/severity, season, spatial
extent and interaction with other disturbances. The same “fire” can maintain a fire-adapted
savanna, trigger secondary forest recovery, or cause severe degradation depending on regime.

✅ **Alternative stable states** may occur where feedbacks maintain different persistent
configurations under similar external conditions. Crossing a threshold can make return
difficult; **hysteresis** means simply reversing the original pressure may not immediately
restore the earlier state.

| Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|
| climate is irrelevant to climax | false: it constrains the broad envelope |
| climate uniquely fixes one permanent climax | false: history and local controls matter |
| disturbance always represents ecosystem failure | false: many systems are disturbance-structured |
| every change proves a new stable state | false: persistence and feedback evidence are needed |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A mature landscape is often not one uniform climax, but a
> disturbance-maintained mosaic whose stability lies in persistence of processes, not
> stillness of every patch.

**Must-write keywords:** dynamic equilibrium · disturbance regime · patch mosaic · gap phase ·
multiple pathways · alternative stable state · threshold · hysteresis.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** disturbance can maintain rather than merely destroy
ecosystems. **Named evidence:** periodic fire and grazing can help retain savanna structure.
**Analysis:** suppressing or intensifying the regime changes tree-grass competition and patch
age. **Qualification:** a regime outside the historical range can still erode resilience and
push the system across a threshold.

### 6. Resistance, resilience, recovery and ecological restoration

#### Response to disturbance

```text
STATE / FUNCTION
  ^
  | baseline ----------- resistance = small immediate change
  |          \__
  |             \____ recovery trajectory
  |                  \____ reorganised persistent state
  +-------------------------------------------------> time

resilience = capacity to absorb/reorganise and retain identity/functions
recovery   = observed movement after disturbance
```

| Term | Exam-safe meaning |
|---|---|
| **Resistance** | degree to which a system changes little during a disturbance |
| **Resilience** | capacity to absorb disturbance, reorganise and retain essential identity, functions and feedbacks |
| **Recovery** | actual post-disturbance trajectory; it may be slow, partial or redirected |
| **Restoration** | assisting recovery toward a defensible reference ecosystem and ecological integrity |
| **Rehabilitation** | restoring selected functions/services without necessarily restoring reference composition |
| **Reclamation** | making severely disturbed land stable or useful; ecological fidelity may be limited |
| **Afforestation** | establishment of tree cover on non-forest land; not automatically ecosystem restoration |
| **Reforestation** | re-establishment of tree cover on formerly forested land; quality still depends on composition and process |

✅ The FAO/SER/IUCN CEM 2024 standards make restoration a cycle: assess the site and reference
ecosystem, set measurable goals, design and implement, manage, monitor, evaluate and adapt.

#### Succession-informed restoration ladder

```text
1 diagnose reference ecosystem + degradation driver
2 protect remnants, soil, hydrology and propagule sources
3 remove the continuing pressure
4 choose passive recovery / assisted natural regeneration / active rebuilding
5 match species and intervention to the current seral stage
6 monitor composition + structure + process + connectivity + resilience
7 adapt; do not declare success from hectares planted
```

✅ Planting late-successional trees directly on unstable mine spoil can fail because substrate
stabilisation, organic matter, microbes and microclimate are missing. Conversely, a site with
strong seed sources may recover better through protection and assisted natural regeneration
than through dense planting.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Restoration should repair the process that permits succession,
> not merely install the appearance of a later stage.

**Must-write keywords:** resistance · resilience · recovery · reference ecosystem · assisted
natural regeneration · native composition · ecological process · adaptive management.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** planting is an intervention, not an outcome.
**Named evidence:** on mine spoil, stabilising substrate and building soil may precede native
woodland establishment. **Analysis:** stage-matched intervention removes the limiting factor.
**Qualification:** the target must be the native ecosystem—grassland restoration may require
fewer trees, not more.

### 7. What is a biome? Fix scale before using the word

#### Scale ladder

```text
BIOSPHERE
  └─ BIOME: broad climate-physiognomy category
       └─ ECOREGION/LANDSCAPE: geographically coherent expression
            └─ ECOSYSTEM: bounded functional interactions
                 └─ HABITAT: place/resources used by a species
```

✅ A **biome** is a broad regional ecological category characterised chiefly by climate and
dominant vegetation structure on land, or by salinity, flow, depth/light, substrate and
productivity in water. It contains many ecosystems.

#### Control hierarchy

| Scale | Major controls | What they explain |
|---|---|---|
| continental/global | temperature, precipitation, seasonality, latitude, altitude, continentality, ocean circulation | broad biome envelope |
| regional | monsoon/orography, soil parent material, drainage, fire and herbivory | forest-grassland-desert expression |
| landscape/site | slope/aspect, groundwater, substrate, disturbance history, fragmentation and land use | local patches and ecotones |

✅ Climate is the first approximation, not a complete deterministic map. Similar rainfall can
produce different vegetation where seasonality, soil, fire, grazing or land-use history differ.

| Close term | Do not confuse it with |
|---|---|
| biome | one bounded ecosystem |
| habitat | the full regional biome |
| ecotone | a cartographic line |
| vegetation type | legal forest status |
| forest cover | mature native forest or a known successional stage |
| “wasteland” | an ecological diagnosis |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Climate draws the broad biome envelope; soil, disturbance and
> history decide how that envelope is expressed on the ground.

**Must-write keywords:** temperature · precipitation · seasonality · physiognomy · edaphic ·
orographic · disturbance regime · spatial scale · vegetation structure.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** biome maps are generalisations.
**Named evidence:** the Indian monsoon gradient supports wet evergreen, deciduous, thorn and
montane formations within one country. **Analysis:** moisture seasonality and relief alter the
regional climate envelope. **Qualification:** local fire, grazing and soils can preserve open
vegetation even where a simple climate map predicts more tree cover.

### 8. Major terrestrial biomes: compare structure and limiting factor

#### Tropical and dry/open systems

| Biome | Climatic rhythm | Structure/adaptation | Dominant constraint | Indian illustration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tropical rainforest** | warm and wet through most/all of year | evergreen, broad-leaved, multi-layered canopy; lianas and epiphytes | light below canopy; nutrients often cycle rapidly in biomass/litter | Western Ghats, Northeast, Andaman-Nicobar wet evergreen formations |
| **Tropical seasonal/deciduous forest** | warm with a marked dry season | leaf fall reduces dry-season water loss; canopy more open seasonally | length/severity of dry season | moist and dry deciduous belts of central and peninsular India |
| **Savanna/tropical grassland** | warm; alternating wet and dry seasons | continuous grasses with scattered drought/fire-tolerant trees | seasonal moisture plus fire, herbivory and soil | natural grassland-savanna mosaics of semi-arid and peninsular India |
| **Desert/xeric shrubland** | precipitation persistently below evaporative demand | sparse shrubs/grasses; small/waxy leaves, thorns, storage tissue or deep/spreading roots | water | Thar and Kutch thorn-desert landscapes |
| **Mediterranean shrubland/woodland** | dry summer, mild wet winter | sclerophyllous evergreen scrub/woodland; leathery leaves, thick bark | summer drought and fire | no extensive classic Mediterranean biome in India |

#### Temperate and cold systems

| Biome | Climatic rhythm | Structure/adaptation | Close-option distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temperate grassland** | continental; moderate but often seasonal moisture | grasses, deep fibrous roots, fertile humus-rich soils in many regions | not treeless because soil is universally poor |
| **Temperate deciduous forest** | adequate moisture with cold/warm seasonality | broad leaves shed during the unfavourable season | deciduous is not synonymous with tropical monsoon forest |
| **Boreal forest/taiga** | long cold winter, short growing season | conifers; needle leaves and conical form common | taiga is forest, not tundra |
| **Tundra** | very short cool growing season; permafrost widespread in Arctic tundra | mosses, lichens, sedges, grasses and dwarf shrubs; treeless | low precipitation can coexist with waterlogged summer surface |
| **Alpine** | cold controlled by altitude rather than latitude | low herbs, grasses and dwarf shrubs above climatic treeline | alpine and Arctic tundra are analogous, not identical |

✅ Tropical rainforest soils need not be nutrient-rich. Warmth and moisture speed
decomposition and uptake; intense leaching and rapid biological cycling can leave a relatively
small readily available soil pool despite luxuriant biomass.

✅ Savanna is not merely “degraded rainforest.” Fire, herbivory, seasonal rainfall and soil can
maintain an open tree-grass state. Conversely, altered fire or grazing can also degrade it; the
history and regime must be specified.

✅ Tundra is treeless because the growing season, cold soils, wind, snow and rooting constraints
restrict tree establishment. It is not a coniferous forest with fewer trees.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A biome is recognised by climate rhythm and vegetation
> physiognomy together; rainfall total alone cannot distinguish rainforest, seasonal forest,
> savanna and desert.

**Must-write keywords:** evergreen · deciduous · sclerophyll · xerophyte · grass-fire feedback ·
conifer · permafrost · growing season · physiognomy.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** savanna openness is a maintained ecological state.
**Named evidence:** the 2021 Prelims demand identifies fire, grazing herbivores and seasonal
rainfall as checks on forest development. **Analysis:** these controls regulate tree
recruitment and grass competition. **Qualification:** not every treeless tropical site is a
natural savanna; land degradation must be tested separately.

### 9. Major aquatic systems: classify by water regime, not tree form

#### Aquatic classification rail

```text
FRESH WATER
  ├─ lentic: lakes and ponds — standing water, depth/light zonation
  ├─ lotic: rivers and streams — current, oxygen and longitudinal gradient
  └─ wetlands — saturated/inundated soils, hydroperiod and hydrophytes

BRACKISH / TRANSITIONAL
  └─ estuaries and lagoons — river-sea mixing, tides, salinity variability

MARINE
  ├─ intertidal/coastal/neritic — light, substrate and nutrient inputs important
  ├─ oceanic pelagic — open water; production often nutrient-limited
  └─ benthic/deep sea — sea floor; darkness, pressure and detrital/chemosynthetic pathways
```

| Aquatic system | Main controls | Characteristic feature | Indian example/use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lentic freshwater** | depth, thermal mixing, light, oxygen, nutrient loading | littoral-open-water-deep/benthic differentiation | lakes and ponds; hydrosere questions |
| **Lotic freshwater** | flow velocity, discharge, substrate, connectivity | organisms adapted to current; conditions change from headwater to floodplain | Ganga/Brahmaputra and peninsular river systems |
| **Wetland** | hydroperiod, saturation, sediment and nutrient regime | water/soil transition; filtering, storage and nursery functions | Loktak-type and floodplain wetland applications |
| **Estuary/lagoon** | salinity gradient, tides, freshwater/sediment inflow | productive, variable ecotone; brackish rather than simply marine | Sundarbans estuary; Chilika lagoon |
| **Mangrove** | tropical/subtropical intertidal salinity, inundation and sediment | salt-tolerant woody community; nursery, shoreline buffer and blue carbon | Sundarbans and other Indian coasts |
| **Coral reef** | suitable temperature, light, clear shallow water and carbonate balance | coral-algal symbiosis and high structural diversity | Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep, Andaman-Nicobar |
| **Open ocean/deep sea** | light-depth, nutrients, circulation, pressure and substrate | pelagic and benthic are positions, not trophic levels | Arabian Sea/Bay of Bengal applications |

✅ **Pelagic** means the water column; **benthic** means the bottom/substrate. **Intertidal**
means alternately exposed and submerged between tidal levels. **Estuarine** refers to
freshwater-seawater mixing. None of these words is a synonym for “marine producer.”

✅ Mangroves are both coastal forest ecosystems and land-sea ecotones. Coral reefs are marine
ecosystems built by coral colonies. Wetlands include freshwater and saline/brackish forms; a
Ramsar designation is a governance status, not an aquatic-biome category.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Terrestrial biomes are read chiefly through climate and plant
> form; aquatic systems are read through salinity, flow, depth, light, substrate and
> hydroperiod.

**Must-write keywords:** lentic · lotic · hydroperiod · brackish · estuarine · intertidal ·
pelagic · benthic · photic · salinity gradient.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** an estuary is controlled by variability.
**Named evidence:** the Sundarbans receives river flow, sediment, tides and saline water.
**Analysis:** fluctuating salinity and inundation select tolerant organisms and create nursery
habitats. **Qualification:** mangrove condition cannot be inferred from mapped area alone;
freshwater and sediment regimes also matter.

### 10. Ecotones and treelines: boundaries are zones and filters

#### Boundary logic

```text
FOREST CORE ---- EDGE / ECOTONE ---- GRASSLAND CORE
specialists         overlap + stress       specialists
                    + edge species

edge effect may raise local richness
BUT may also raise heat, wind, invasion, predation or generalist dominance
```

✅ An **ecotone** is a transition zone between adjoining ecological communities, not a line.
Species from both systems and edge specialists may overlap, producing an **edge effect**.
Higher local richness is common, not universal proof of ecological quality.

✅ A **treeline** is the climatic/ecological limit beyond which upright tree growth and
recruitment cannot maintain a forest form. Alpine treelines occur with elevation; latitudinal
treelines occur toward polar regions. Individual krummholz or stunted trees do not necessarily
mean that a closed forest has crossed the treeline.

| Treeline control | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| growing-season temperature | limits tissue formation and seedling establishment |
| wind/frost/snow | causes mechanical and physiological stress |
| moisture and soil | can override a simple warming response |
| seed supply and dispersal | creates lags even after climate becomes suitable |
| grazing, fire and cutting | can depress or fragment the observed treeline |

⚠️ An upslope treeline at one site is not, by itself, proof of climate change. IPCC AR6 WGII
reports warming-related range and boundary shifts, but Himalayan and other mountain responses
can be upslope, stable or locally downslope because climate and land use interact.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Ecological boundaries move through recruitment and mortality,
> not by a biome map sliding uniformly across space.

**Must-write keywords:** ecotone · edge effect · gradient · treeline · recruitment · krummholz ·
range shift · lag · fragmentation.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** treelines are multi-causal climate sentinels.
**Named evidence:** IPCC AR6 notes mountain boundary shifts but also mixed local responses.
**Analysis:** warming may relax thermal limits while grazing, snow, drought or dispersal slow or
reverse recruitment. **Qualification:** repeated site-level monitoring is required before
attribution.

### 11. India: succession and biome examples without category errors

| Indian setting | Correct ecological use | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| fresh landslide scar/exposed rock | primary or primary-like recovery where developed soil is lost | calling every landslide secondary because vegetation once existed nearby |
| abandoned cultivation/jhum fallow | secondary succession; fallow length and repeated clearing matter | assuming retained soil guarantees full forest recovery |
| fire-affected forest/grassland | usually secondary if soil and propagules remain | treating all fire as ecosystem destruction or all fire as beneficial |
| mine spoil | staged stabilisation, soil rebuilding and native reference ecosystem | instant planting of late-stage trees |
| pond/wetland | hydrosere concepts plus hydrology/sediment management | assuming inevitable conversion to terrestrial forest |
| Western Ghats/Northeast | tropical wet evergreen examples | equating all high-rainfall sites with undisturbed rainforest |
| central/peninsular India | deciduous forest and open woodland-grassland mosaics | reading every open patch as degraded forest |
| Thar/Kutch | desert, thorn and grassland systems | treating deserts as treeless wastelands |
| Himalaya | temperate/coniferous belts, alpine ecotones and treelines | treating altitude as latitude without local qualification |
| Sundarbans/coasts | estuarine-mangrove ecotones | treating mangrove area as proof of unchanged hydrology/function |

#### Open Natural Ecosystem firewall

✅ Grasslands, savannas, scrublands, ravines and rocky outcrops can be **Open Natural
Ecosystems (ONEs)** with their own species, below-ground carbon and disturbance regimes.
They are not necessarily incomplete forests.

⚠️ In Indian records, **“wasteland”** is an administrative/revenue label, **forest** may be a
legal or canopy-measurement category, and **grassland biome** is an ecological category.
Substituting one for another can direct afforestation toward the wrong reference ecosystem.

✅ The Great Indian Bustard, blackbuck, Indian wolf, lesser florican, caracal and Indian fox
are canonical examples used by the Advanced owner to show why open habitats have
conservation value. Their mention supports the habitat argument; it does not assign a current
population trend without a dated species source.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** India’s restoration error often begins before planting—with an
> administrative land label mistaken for an ecological diagnosis.

**Must-write keywords:** Open Natural Ecosystem · biome fidelity · administrative label · legal
category · ecological category · jhum fallow · mine spoil · native reference.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** default afforestation can damage a natural open
ecosystem. **Named evidence:** Thar and Deccan grassland-scrub landscapes support specialist
fauna and substantial root/soil carbon. **Analysis:** dense tree planting changes light, water
and habitat structure. **Qualification:** genuinely degraded former forest may still require
native reforestation after site diagnosis.

### 12. Climate and land-use shifts: moving envelopes, blocked pathways

#### Causal chain

```text
warming / altered rainfall / extremes
       |
       +-> mortality and recruitment change
       +-> species ranges and seasonal timing shift
       +-> fire, pests and hydrology change
       |
       v
biome boundary pressure
       |
       +-> lag from slow dispersal/soil formation
       +-> blocked movement from farms, roads and settlements
       +-> novel communities where species respond at different rates
```

✅ IPCC AR6 WGII (2022) assesses observed changes in terrestrial and freshwater species,
communities and ecosystem boundaries consistent with climate change. This is a global/Asian
assessment; it must not be converted into a site-specific Indian treeline rate without Indian
monitoring.

✅ Land use can reinforce, hide or oppose the climatic signal. Grazing and fire can maintain an
open state; abandonment can permit woody encroachment; fragmentation can prevent species from
tracking a suitable climate; irrigation or groundwater change can create local vegetation that
does not match the regional climate envelope.

✅ FSI's **ISFR 2023**, released **21 December 2024**, reports forest plus tree cover of
**8,27,357 sq km (25.17%)**. It is an authoritative canopy-extent baseline.

⚠️ **Inference boundary:** canopy extent cannot, by itself, distinguish native forest,
plantation, agroforestry, young secondary regrowth or a mature late-successional community.
Successional quality needs composition, age/size structure, soil, hydrology, connectivity and
repeated ecological observation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Climate changes the suitability envelope, while land use decides
> whether species can move, establish and assemble into a functioning future ecosystem.

**Must-write keywords:** range shift · climate velocity · recruitment lag · fragmentation ·
novel community · woody encroachment · attribution · repeated monitoring.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** forest-cover gain is not identical to restoration.
**Named evidence:** ISFR 2023 measures forest and tree-cover extent. **Analysis:** the same
canopy class can include plantation or secondary regrowth with different ecological functions.
**Qualification:** cover data remain useful for screening trends when paired with field and
composition evidence.

### 13. A succession-informed restoration decision framework

```text
WHAT WAS / SHOULD BE HERE?
reference ecosystem + historical range + community use
        |
WHAT SURVIVED?
soil, roots, seed bank, microbes, remnant patches, hydrology
        |
WHAT STILL DRIVES DEGRADATION?
fire/grazing regime, erosion, extraction, invasive species, pollution, fragmentation
        |
CHOOSE LEAST-INTRUSIVE EFFECTIVE PATH
protect -> passive recovery -> assisted regeneration -> active reconstruction
        |
MONITOR OUTCOMES
composition + structure + function + connectivity + resilience + livelihoods
        |
ADAPT OR REVISE THE TARGET
```

| Indicator family | What to measure | Why hectares planted are insufficient |
|---|---|---|
| composition | native species, functional groups, invasive dominance | canopy can hide monoculture |
| structure | age/size classes, layers, deadwood or grass cover as appropriate | one uniform cohort is not mature structure |
| process | soil formation, nutrient cycling, hydrology, regeneration, disturbance regime | restoration is functioning, not scenery |
| landscape | connectivity, patch size, edge pressure, source populations | isolated plots may not persist |
| resilience | response and recovery after drought, flood, fire or grazing | one favourable-year survey is weak evidence |
| social | tenure, local use, conflict, stewardship and distribution of costs/benefits | exclusion can undermine long-term protection |

✅ Passive recovery is not “doing nothing”: it requires pressure removal, protection and
monitoring. Active planting is justified when dispersal, substrate, hydrology or species pools
are genuine bottlenecks.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The best restoration intervention is the minimum action that
> removes the limiting process and allows the correct native biome to recover.

**Must-write keywords:** reference ecosystem · diagnosis · biological legacies · limiting
factor · passive recovery · assisted natural regeneration · monitoring · adaptive management.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** restoration method must follow diagnosis.
**Named evidence:** jhum fallow with nearby seed sources may recover through longer fallow and
protection, while toxic mine spoil needs substrate treatment. **Analysis:** each intervention
targets a different bottleneck. **Qualification:** both must be evaluated against native
composition and livelihood safeguards, not plantation survival alone.

### Shared/application PYQ 1 — 2019 GS-I, Question 15

> **Exact official wording:** “How can the mountain ecosystem be restored from the negative
> impact of development initiatives and tourism?” **15 marks, 250 words.**

<!-- PYQ-MODEL-START:PM1 -->
**Model answer**

Mountain restoration must rebuild ecological processes rather than merely plant trees on a
damaged slope.

**First, diagnose the mountain system.** Map slope, geology, drainage, avalanche/landslide
paths, treeline and alpine ecotones, native vegetation, wildlife corridors and community use.
Project-wise clearance is insufficient where roads, hotels, hydropower and pilgrimage produce
cumulative pressure.

**Second, avoid fresh damage.** Apply carrying-capacity and cumulative-impact assessment;
exclude unstable slopes, riparian buffers and high-value habitats; regulate blasting, muck
disposal, groundwater withdrawal, traffic and construction density. Seasonal visitor caps and
public transport can reduce peak pressure.

**Third, restore by successional stage.** Stabilise exposed substrate with bioengineering,
native grasses and shrubs before introducing later-stage woody species. Protect topsoil, seed
banks and remnant patches; reconnect habitat and stream corridors. Assisted natural
regeneration is preferable where propagule sources survive, while active planting is justified
where dispersal or soil is a real bottleneck.

**Fourth, repair hydrology and waste flows.** Reopen drainage, protect springs and wetlands,
treat sewage, remove solid waste and prevent nutrient loading of mountain lakes and streams.

**Fifth, govern adaptively.** Panchayats, forest-dependent communities and tourism operators
should share monitoring and benefits. Track native recruitment, slope stability, water quality,
wildlife use and recovery after extreme events—not only planted hectares.

Restoration cannot recreate every lost state under a changing climate. Its realistic goal is a
connected, native and resilient mountain mosaic within enforceable development limits.
<!-- PYQ-MODEL-END:PM1 -->

### Shared/application PYQ 2 — 2020 GS-I, Question 5

> **Exact official wording:** “The process of desertification does not have climatic
> boundaries. Justify with examples.” **10 marks, 150 words.**

<!-- PYQ-MODEL-START:PM2 -->
**Model answer**

Desertification is land degradation in drylands, not the natural expansion of a hot desert; it
therefore crosses simple climatic boundaries.

Wind and water erosion, vegetation loss, salinisation, nutrient depletion and compaction can
develop in semi-arid, sub-humid and irrigated landscapes when use exceeds recovery. In
Rajasthan, overgrazing, groundwater stress and wind erosion intensify aridity; poor drainage can
salinise canal-irrigated soils. Chambal ravines show severe erosion outside a sand-desert
setting, while shortened jhum fallows can expose humid Northeast slopes.

Climate variability and warming amplify drought and fire, but cultivation, grazing,
deforestation, mining and faulty irrigation often determine whether stress becomes degradation.

Control must match mechanism: grazing management, soil-water conservation, native dryland
restoration, drainage and local stewardship—not blanket tree planting.
<!-- PYQ-MODEL-END:PM2 -->

### Shared/application PYQ 3 — 2023 GS-I, Question 15

> **Exact official wording:** “Identify and discuss the factors responsible for diversity of
> natural vegetation in India. Assess the significance of wildlife sanctuaries in rain forest
> regions of India.” **15 marks, 250 words.**

<!-- PYQ-MODEL-START:PM3 -->
**Model answer**

India’s vegetation diversity reflects interaction between macroclimate and local ecological
filters rather than rainfall alone.

**Climate:** latitude, temperature, monsoon amount and seasonality separate wet evergreen,
deciduous, thorn and alpine formations.
**Relief:** Himalayan altitude creates temperate-conifer-alpine belts, while Western Ghats
orography supports humid windward forests and drier rain-shadow landscapes.
**Edaphic and hydrological factors:** soil depth, salinity, drainage, groundwater and flooding
differentiate mangroves, grasslands, swamp systems and dry forests.
**Disturbance and biota:** fire, grazing, herbivory and succession maintain mosaics such as
savanna-grassland and forest gaps.
**Human history:** cultivation, logging, fragmentation, plantations and invasive species alter
composition and connectivity.

Wildlife sanctuaries in rainforest regions protect layered habitat, endemic and threatened
species, watersheds, pollination and seed-dispersal networks. In the Western Ghats and
Northeast, they can retain source populations and corridors from which disturbed patches
recover. They also support research, climate refugia and regulated livelihood/tourism benefits.

However, designation alone is insufficient. Small isolated sanctuaries face edge effects,
roads, linear infrastructure and climate-driven range shifts; wildlife also moves through
reserve forests, community lands and corridors outside boundaries. Protection must therefore
combine sanctuary enforcement with landscape connectivity, invasive/fire control, community
rights, restoration of native composition and repeated ecological monitoring.

Sanctuaries are indispensable cores, but rainforest resilience depends on the wider
forest-river-community mosaic.
<!-- PYQ-MODEL-END:PM3 -->

### Shared/application PYQ 4 — 2026 GS-I, Question 5

> **Exact official wording:** “‘Tundra regions are ecologically fragile but economically
> important.’ Examine this statement critically.” **10 marks, 150 words.**

<!-- PYQ-MODEL-START:PM4 -->
**Model answer**

Tundra fragility arises from a short growing season, permafrost, shallow active soil and slow
decomposition. Vehicle tracks, extraction, fire or thaw can disrupt drainage and persist;
warming may favour shrub expansion, changing albedo and habitat.

Yet tundra supports Indigenous cultures and livelihoods, fisheries and reindeer pastoralism,
and contains minerals and hydrocarbons. Changing sea ice may expand shipping and strategic
access, while frozen soils and wetlands store carbon and regulate water.

Opportunity is also risk: infrastructure fragments habitat, accelerates thaw and creates
hard-to-clean pollution; benefits may bypass local communities.

Policy needs exclusion zones, cumulative assessment, Indigenous participation, low-impact
design, spill readiness and long-term permafrost, hydrology and biodiversity monitoring.
Economic use is defensible only within ecological thresholds; sparse vegetation does not mean
low value.
<!-- PYQ-MODEL-END:PM4 -->

### Application Essay PYQ — 2024, Section A, Question 1

> **Exact official wording:** “Forests precede civilizations and deserts follow them.”
> **125 marks; about 1000-1200 words.**

<!-- PYQ-MODEL-START:PE1 -->
**Model essay**

The aphorism compresses a long ecological warning into one dramatic sequence. Forests appear
before civilisation because complex societies inherit soils, water cycles, biodiversity and
climatic regulation built by earlier ecosystems. Deserts “follow” when extraction destroys
those foundations. Yet the sentence must not be read literally: natural deserts existed long
before human civilisation, not every civilisation ends in desert, and not every damaged
landscape can or should become forest. Its deeper truth is that prosperity rests on ecological
processes, while development that liquidates those processes turns wealth into vulnerability.

Forests precede civilisation first in a material sense. Vegetation intercepts rain, protects
soil from raindrop impact, slows runoff and supplies litter. Roots stabilise slopes and create
pathways for infiltration; decomposers return nutrients; pollinators and seed dispersers sustain
renewal. Forested catchments, grasslands and wetlands together regulate streams and reduce the
extremes between flood and drought. Civilisations therefore do not begin from an empty map.
They occupy landscapes whose fertility and water security are products of long ecological
succession.

The phrase also points to biological capital. Tropical forests contain layered habitats and
complex food webs; dry forests and savannas support organisms adapted to seasonal stress;
montane forests connect valleys to alpine zones. Human food, medicine, fibres and knowledge
emerge from these living systems. Even when markets price timber or land, the greater value
often lies in watershed protection, soil formation, climate regulation and cultural identity.
These are enabling conditions of production, not decorative benefits added after growth.

Deserts follow when a society mistakes a stock for an endless flow. Repeated clearing,
overgrazing, poorly managed irrigation, mining and groundwater extraction can remove vegetation,
compact soil, raise salinity and accelerate erosion. Once topsoil and seed sources disappear,
secondary succession may no longer restore the earlier community. A threshold is crossed:
runoff increases, infiltration falls and sparse vegetation captures still less moisture. The
degraded state begins to reinforce itself.

History supplies many such warnings, but ecological reasoning must remain precise. The collapse
of a forest is not identical to desertification, and aridity does not arise from trees alone.
Rainfall regime, geology, winds and ocean circulation create natural deserts. The Thar is not a
failed rainforest waiting to be planted. Its thorn scrub, grassland and dunes form an arid biome
with specialised life and livelihoods. Calling every open landscape “wasteland” repeats the
same civilisational error: it values nature only when it resembles a closed forest.

This qualification changes the meaning of restoration. If the damaged site was wet evergreen
forest, native reforestation and assisted natural regeneration may be appropriate. If it was a
grassland, savanna, wetland or desert scrub, dense tree planting can consume scarce water,
shade out native species and erase habitat. Restoration must recover the reference ecosystem,
not impose one visual symbol of greenness.

Succession also challenges the desire for instant results. Bare mine spoil may need
stabilisation, microbial recovery, organic matter and pioneer cover before late-stage trees can
survive. An abandoned field with intact soil and nearby seed sources may recover largely through
protection. A wetland may require its hydrology and sediment regime to be repaired. Hectares
planted or seedlings surviving for one season cannot demonstrate restored composition,
function or resilience.

Modern ecology further rejects the idea of one permanent climax. Landscapes are mosaics shaped
by fire, floods, grazing, storms and treefall. Some disturbances maintain diversity and open
habitats; others, when intensified beyond the historical regime, push systems toward erosion,
invasion or alternative stable states. Civilisation must therefore govern disturbance rather
than imagine that nature is healthy only when motionless.

Climate change deepens the warning. Temperature and rainfall envelopes are shifting, species
respond at different rates and treelines may move where recruitment permits. Roads, farms and
settlements can block movement, leaving protected islands in climates for which they were not
designed. A forest restored to yesterday’s map without connectivity, genetic diversity or
future-climate thinking may become tomorrow’s ecological trap.

India illustrates both dependence and danger. Western Ghats and Northeast forests support
watersheds and exceptional biological diversity; central Indian deciduous landscapes connect
wildlife and livelihoods; Himalayan forests influence slope and spring systems; mangroves such
as the Sundarbans form land-sea buffers. At the same time, India’s grasslands, savannas and
scrublands are often administratively undervalued. Development can therefore degrade forests
through clearing and degrade open ecosystems through inappropriate afforestation.

Governance must begin with correct accounting. The India State of Forest Report 2023 provides
an authoritative canopy-extent baseline, but canopy cannot by itself distinguish natural
forest, plantation, agroforestry or young secondary growth. Policy should add native
composition, age structure, soil and hydrology, connectivity and recovery after disturbance.
What is measured determines what institutions learn to protect.

The economic model must change as well. Natural capital should not become a licence to assign a
price to every sacred or irreplaceable ecosystem. Rather, ecological accounting should reveal
costs that conventional accounts hide: silted reservoirs, flood exposure, lost pollination,
heat, livelihood displacement and restoration debt. The hierarchy should be avoid damage,
minimise what cannot be avoided, restore what is repairable and compensate only residual,
commensurable loss.

Communities are central to this transition. Pastoralists, forest dwellers, fishers and farmers
hold knowledge of fire, water, grazing and species change, yet conservation or development can
exclude them from decisions. Secure rights, benefit sharing and local monitoring make
restoration socially durable. Justice is not separate from ecology: a community denied secure
livelihoods may be forced into short-term extraction, while an imposed plantation may destroy
both habitat and commons.

Finally, civilisation must redefine excellence. The highest achievement is not the power to
replace every ecosystem with infrastructure, but the capacity to build without disabling the
systems that make settlement possible. This requires compact and risk-sensitive cities,
catchment protection, circular resource use, clean energy, sustainable agriculture, ecological
flows and connected conservation landscapes.

Forests precede civilisation as inherited infrastructure; deserts follow when societies consume
that inheritance without renewing its processes. But ecological decline is neither universal
nor always irreversible. Succession, restoration and stewardship show that damaged landscapes
can recover when pressure is removed and the right biome is respected. The aphorism is therefore
not a prophecy. It is a choice: civilisation may leave a desert, or it may become the custodian
of the living foundations that preceded it.
<!-- PYQ-MODEL-END:PE1 -->

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### 1. Classical climax theories and their modern use

| Lens | Central proposition | Safe modern use |
|---|---|---|
| **Clementsian monoclimax** | regional climate drives orderly convergence toward one climatic climax | useful historical baseline, too deterministic as a universal rule |
| **Tansley’s polyclimax** | climate, soil, topography, fire and biotic factors can support several persistent climaxes | improves local-factor analysis |
| **Whittaker’s climax pattern** | communities vary continuously along environmental gradients | supports ecotone and gradient thinking |
| **Gleasonian/individualistic view** | species respond individually; community composition depends on dispersal and history | explains contingency and novel assemblages |
| **Initial floristic composition** | many early and late species may arrive early; later dominance reflects longevity and growth | corrects the idea that every species waits for its fixed stage |

⚠️ These labels are explanatory lenses, not mutually exclusive laws. A high-quality answer
uses “climax” for relative persistence, then specifies scale, disturbance regime and evidence.

### 2. Priority effects, alternative states and hysteresis

Early arrival can alter resources, enemies or microclimate and thereby affect later assembly:
a **priority effect**. It may reinforce facilitation, tolerance or inhibition.

An **alternative stable state** requires more than temporary variation. Evidence should show
persistence plus feedbacks. A clear-water and turbid shallow-lake state, or a tree-grass system
maintained by different fire/herbivory feedbacks, illustrates the logic without proving that
every lake or savanna has two states.

**Hysteresis** matters for policy because pressure removal may be necessary but insufficient.
After topsoil loss, invasive dominance or hydrological change, active repair may be needed to
cross the recovery threshold.

### 3. Patch dynamics changes the unit of analysis

At patch scale, a treefall may reset light and recruitment. At landscape scale, simultaneous
young, mature and recovering patches can preserve regional structure. A disturbance can
therefore reduce local biomass while sustaining landscape heterogeneity.

Three audit questions improve answers:

1. **What is the patch size relative to organism movement?**
2. **What is disturbance return time relative to recovery time?**
3. **Are source populations and corridors still present?**

This avoids calling either all disturbance beneficial or all disturbance catastrophic.

### 4. Restoration quality: two axes, not one

```text
TEMPORAL FIDELITY
correct succession stage and process
        X
SPATIAL FIDELITY
correct native biome and landscape position
        =
CREDIBLE RESTORATION
```

The Advanced owner’s core insight is that restoration can fail on either axis. Planting
late-stage trees on hostile spoil compresses the temporal pathway; planting trees in native
grassland selects the wrong spatial target.

For Open Natural Ecosystems, below-ground biomass and soil carbon can be ecologically important
even when canopy metrics are low. Canopy-centred incentives can therefore undervalue grassland,
savanna and scrub.

### 5. Governance and evidence limits

- FSI canopy-density products are valuable for repeated large-scale screening, but cannot
  identify a seral stage or native-climax fidelity by themselves.
- MoEFCC and CAMPA-supported afforestation/restoration should be assessed against the native
  reference ecosystem and succession stage, not only planting or survival outputs.
- The Economic Survey 2025-26 confirms the current Afforestation and Green Credit Programme
  incentive channel. ⚠️ It does not establish that credited planting has already recovered
  native composition, hydrology or resilience.
- Afforestation, compensatory afforestation, plantation and restored natural forest describe
  different ecological objects.
- Long successional trajectories require permanent plots, repeat vegetation surveys, soil and
  hydrological monitoring, not one-time survival counts.
- Global IPCC evidence can establish a general climate-risk mechanism; Indian site claims need
  Indian observations and attribution.
- Restoration targets should report reference ecosystem, native recruitment, process recovery,
  connectivity, permanence and uncertainty alongside area.

### 6. Advanced Mains thesis

> Genuine restoration must respect succession’s **time axis** and the biome’s **space axis**:
> repair the limiting process at the current stage, recover the correct native ecosystem, and
> verify function and resilience through repeated monitoring.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Succession decision card

- Succession = directional community/process change through time; not any fluctuation.
- **Sere** = complete sequence; **seral stage** = one community within it.
- Classical stages: nudation → migration → ecesis → aggregation →
  competition/coaction → reaction → relative stabilisation.
- Primary: no developed soil; secondary: soil and biological legacies usually remain.
- Fire is usually secondary if soil/propagules survive.
- Mine spoil may be primary-like; diagnose before labelling.

### Seres and mechanism card

- Lithosere rock · psammosere sand · halosere saline · hydrosere fresh water · xerosere dry.
- Hydrarch classically moves from open water toward more mesic conditions.
- Xerarch begins dry; endpoint may be grassland/scrub/desert, not necessarily forest.
- Autogenic = community modifies site; allogenic = external forcing.
- Facilitation helps later species; tolerance neither requires help nor decisive suppression;
  inhibition delays later establishment.

### Climax, disturbance and resilience card

- Climax = relative persistence, not permanence.
- Climate constrains; soil, topography, species pool, dispersal, disturbance and chance modify.
- Patch dynamics: local turnover can coexist with landscape persistence.
- Disturbance regime = type + frequency + severity + season + extent.
- Resistance = small immediate change.
- Resilience = absorb/reorganise while retaining essential identity/functions.
- Recovery = observed trajectory; threshold/hysteresis may obstruct return.

### Restoration card

- Reference ecosystem first; remove continuing driver.
- Protect remnants, soil, hydrology, seed sources and connectivity.
- Choose passive recovery → assisted regeneration → active rebuilding as needed.
- Planting ≠ restoration; survival ≠ native recruitment; cover ≠ function.
- Monitor composition, structure, process, landscape, resilience and social durability.
- Correct **stage** + correct **biome** = restoration fidelity.

### Terrestrial biome card

- Rainforest: evergreen, layered, lianas/epiphytes; soil can be nutrient-poor despite lush biomass.
- Seasonal forest: dry-season leaf fall.
- Savanna: grasses + scattered trees; rainfall seasonality, fire, herbivory and soils matter.
- Desert: aridity, sparse xerophytes; desert ≠ hot only.
- Mediterranean: dry summer, wet winter, sclerophyll.
- Temperate grassland: continental, grasses, often fertile soils.
- Temperate deciduous: broadleaf seasonal leaf fall.
- Taiga: boreal conifers; tundra: treeless, short growing season/permafrost.
- Alpine tundra is altitude-controlled; Arctic tundra latitude-controlled.

### Aquatic biome card

- Lentic = standing freshwater; lotic = flowing freshwater; wetland = hydroperiod/saturation.
- Estuary/lagoon = brackish transition with tides and river inputs.
- Pelagic = water column; benthic = bottom; intertidal = alternately exposed/submerged.
- Mangrove = intertidal salt-tolerant forest/ecotone.
- Coral reef = light-sensitive marine carbonate structure built by coral colonies.
- Aquatic controls: salinity, flow, depth/light, oxygen, nutrients, substrate and hydroperiod.

### Boundary and India card

- Ecotone = transition zone, not line; edge effect is common, not universally beneficial.
- Treeline response needs temperature + snow/moisture + wind + seed/recruitment +
  grazing/fire/cutting.
- India examples: wet evergreen Western Ghats/Northeast; deciduous central India;
  Thar/Kutch desert-thorn; Himalayan temperate-alpine; Sundarbans estuary-mangrove.
- Jhum fallow = secondary succession; fallow length matters.
- ONEs: grassland, savanna, scrub, ravines and rocky outcrops can be natural ecosystems.
- “Wasteland” administrative label ≠ legal forest ≠ ecological grassland biome.

### Current evidence and PYQ card

- IPCC AR6 WGII (2022): range/biome-boundary shifts occur, but local treeline response is mixed.
- ISFR 2023, released 21 December 2024: forest + tree cover
  **8,27,357 sq km (25.17%)**; canopy extent does not prove successional quality.
- Economic Survey 2025-26: the Afforestation and Green Credit Programme is an active incentive
  channel; participation/planting is not proof of ecosystem recovery.
- Direct objective PYQ: 2021 Q20, pioneers on a surface without soil.
- Key shared routes: 2021 rainforest/savanna; 2023 rainforest soil; 2026 tundra;
