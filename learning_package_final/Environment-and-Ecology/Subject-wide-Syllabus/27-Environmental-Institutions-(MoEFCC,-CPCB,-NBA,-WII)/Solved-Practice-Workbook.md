## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Q1. A body is created by a central-government order issued under a parent environmental statute rather than by a separate enactment. Which description fits it?

A. A notified authority, whose powers are bounded by the constituting order and the parent statute.
B. A statutory board, whose composition and functions are written into the enacting Act itself.
C. A registered society, whose powers arise from its memorandum and the law of societies.
D. A tribunal, whose adjudicatory jurisdiction is conferred by the Act that establishes it.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Bodies such as the Central Ground Water Authority and the Coastal Zone Management Authorities exist because of an order made under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and their powers run only as far as that order.
- **B — Incorrect.** A statutory board such as the Central Pollution Control Board is constituted by the Act itself, not by a later executive order.
- **C — Incorrect.** A society is registered under general societies law; any public powers it exercises must be separately conferred.
- **D — Incorrect.** A tribunal decides disputes; the description given is of an administrative authority, not an adjudicatory one.

**Examiner trap:** reading "authority" in a body's name as proof that an Act created it.

### Q2. Which statement describes the position of the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change?

A. It issues consent to establish and consent to operate to industrial units across the States.
B. It frames policy, administers the environmental statutes and makes rules and notifications under them.
C. It adjudicates disputes arising from clearance decisions and pollution-consent orders.
D. It conducts the taxonomic and forest-cover surveys on which environmental policy is based.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Consent to establish and operate is granted by the State Pollution Control Board or the Union territory Pollution Control Committee.
- **B — Correct.** The Ministry administers the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, the Water Act, 1974, the Air Act, 1981, the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, and represents India in environmental conventions.
- **C — Incorrect.** Adjudication of such disputes lies with the National Green Tribunal and the courts.
- **D — Incorrect.** Survey work belongs to the Forest Survey of India, the Botanical Survey of India and the Zoological Survey of India, which report to the Ministry but are not the Ministry.

**Examiner trap:** treating the nodal ministry as the field regulator for every environmental decision.

### Q3. Under which provision was the Central Pollution Control Board constituted?

A. Section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
B. Section 16 of the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981.
C. Section 3 of the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.
D. Section 8 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Section 3(3) of the 1986 Act is the power under which authorities are constituted by notification, which is how bodies like the Central Ground Water Authority came into being.
- **B — Incorrect.** Section 16 of the 1981 Act lists the Central Board's air-pollution functions; it presupposes a Board that already exists.
- **C — Correct.** The Board was constituted under Section 3 of the Water Act, 1974, and the Air Act, 1981 later vested additional functions in the same body.
- **D — Incorrect.** Section 8 of the 2002 Act establishes the National Biodiversity Authority.

**Examiner trap:** confusing the section that creates a body with the section that lists its later functions.

### Q4. A unit in a State seeks permission to begin operating and is later inspected for effluent compliance. Which body performs both steps?

A. The Central Pollution Control Board, which grants the consent and conducts the field inspection.
B. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, acting through its regional offices.
C. The National Green Tribunal, which authorises operation and then verifies compliance.
D. The State Pollution Control Board, which grants the consent and conducts the field inspection.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** The Central Board sets standards, coordinates the State Boards and provides technical support; routine consent and inspection are not its functions in a State.
- **B — Incorrect.** Regional offices monitor clearance conditions for projects appraised at the Centre; they do not run the consent regime.
- **C — Incorrect.** The Tribunal hears disputes after a decision is taken; it does not authorise operations.
- **D — Correct.** Consent to operate, inspection and enforcement against a unit located in a State are State Board functions.

**Examiner trap:** assuming the national body must be the one that acts on the ground.

### Q5. Which of the following is a function of the Central Board under the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981?

A. Planning and executing a nationwide programme for the prevention and abatement of air pollution.
B. Issuing consent to operate to every industrial plant situated within the territory of a State.
C. Hearing appeals filed against air-quality directions issued by a State Pollution Control Board.
D. Preparing the periodic national assessment of the country's forest and tree cover.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Nationwide programme planning, advice to the Centre, coordination of State Boards, technical assistance, research, training, data compilation and standard-setting are the Central Board's listed air-pollution functions.
- **B — Incorrect.** Plant-level consent within a State belongs to that State's Board.
- **C — Incorrect.** Appeals against Board directions lie to the appellate authority or the National Green Tribunal, not to the Central Board.
- **D — Incorrect.** Forest and tree-cover assessment is the work of the Forest Survey of India.

**Examiner trap:** reading "national functions" as though they included case-by-case regulation.

### Q6. Consider the following statements about pollution-control administration in a Union territory:

1. A State Board is constituted for a Union territory in the same manner as for a State.
2. The Central Board performs the functions of a State Board for a Union territory.
3. Those functions may be delegated to a person or body that the Central Government specifies.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. Statements 1 and 2 only
B. Statements 2 and 3 only
C. Statements 1 and 3 only
D. Statements 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** It retains statement 1, which contradicts the scheme of the Water Act for Union territories.
- **B — Correct.** No State Board is constituted for a Union territory; the Central Board performs those functions and the Act permits delegation to a specified person or body, which is how Union territory Pollution Control Committees operate.
- **C — Incorrect.** Statement 3 is correct, but statement 1 is not.
- **D — Incorrect.** It treats all three as correct although the first is wrong.

**Examiner trap:** assuming every territory has its own Board because every State does.

### Q7. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act, 2024 was enacted using Article 252 of the Constitution. What follows from that route?

A. It replaced the parent Act of 1974 throughout India from the date on which assent was given.
B. It required ratification by half the State legislatures because a constitutional provision was altered.
C. It applied first to the consenting States and the Union territories, and to others on adoption.
D. It moved water pollution permanently from the State List to the Concurrent List of the Constitution.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** An Article 252 law does not extend automatically to States that have not consented.
- **B — Incorrect.** Ratification by State legislatures belongs to the constitutional-amendment procedure under Article 368, which was not used here.
- **C — Correct.** The amending Act applies in the first instance to Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan and the Union territories, and reaches another State when that State's legislature adopts it by resolution.
- **D — Incorrect.** Article 252 leaves the legislative lists untouched; it allows Parliament to legislate for consenting States without altering the entry.

**Examiner trap:** describing a consent-based central law as though it were pan-India from commencement.

### Q8. Why did the penal provisions of the Water Act, 1974 follow a different legislative route from those of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Air Act, 1981?

A. Because the Water Act is the oldest of the three and predates the general environmental legislation.
B. Because water pollution is treated as a compoundable offence under the general criminal law.
C. Because the Water Act is administered by a ministry different from the one administering the others.
D. Because water is a State List subject, so the amendment used the Article 252 consent route.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Chronology does not determine legislative competence. The 1974 Act rests on Article 252 because water is a State List subject, whereas the Air Act, 1981 was enacted under **Article 253** to implement the 1972 Stockholm Conference decisions and therefore applied throughout India without State resolutions.
- **B — Incorrect.** Compoundability is a feature of a penal provision, not a reason for choosing a legislative route.
- **C — Incorrect.** All three statutes are administered by the same environment ministry.
- **D — Correct.** Because the subject matter sits in the State List, changes to the Water Act's penal scheme were carried through the Article 252 route, while the other statutes were amended through the general decriminalisation route.

**Examiner trap:** learning the fact of decriminalisation without learning why the constitutional route differed.

### Q9. Which combination correctly describes the National Biodiversity Authority?

A. A statutory authority under Section 8 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, located at Chennai.
B. A notified authority under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, located at New Delhi.
C. An autonomous research institute under the environment ministry, located at Dehradun.
D. A registered society exercising delegated statutory powers, located at Kolkata.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** The Authority was established under Section 8 of the 2002 Act, became operational in 2003 and functions from Chennai.
- **B — Incorrect.** That description fits notified bodies such as the Coastal Zone Management Authorities, not the biodiversity regulator.
- **C — Incorrect.** Dehradun hosts the Wildlife Institute of India and the Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education, which are research institutions.
- **D — Incorrect.** The society-with-delegated-powers form describes the National Mission for Clean Ganga.

**Examiner trap:** placing the biodiversity regulator in the national capital by default.

### Q10. Which pairing of body and function under the Biological Diversity Act is correct?

A. State Biodiversity Board — preparing the People's Biodiversity Register for each local area.
B. Biodiversity Management Committee — documenting local biological resources and associated knowledge.
C. National Biodiversity Authority — issuing consent to operate to bio-processing industrial units.
D. Biodiversity Management Committee — deciding applications by foreign entities seeking resource access.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** The State Board works at the State level; register preparation is a local-committee function.
- **B — Correct.** The Biodiversity Management Committee at the local-body level documents local resources and associated traditional knowledge, principally through the People's Biodiversity Register.
- **C — Incorrect.** Industrial consent belongs to the pollution-control boards, not to the biodiversity regulator.
- **D — Incorrect.** Applications by foreign-controlled or non-resident users are decided at the national level.

**Examiner trap:** collapsing the three-tier biodiversity structure into a single regulator.

### Q11. What does a People's Biodiversity Register principally record?

A. The monetary benefits already transferred to a community under a concluded access agreement.
B. The species notified as threatened by the Union government for a particular district.
C. Local biological resources, their uses and the traditional knowledge associated with them.
D. The consent conditions attached to industrial units operating within a local body's limits.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Benefit transfers are recorded in access agreements and the accounts of the relevant authority, not in the register.
- **B — Incorrect.** Threat status is assessed through separate scientific and legal processes.
- **C — Correct.** The register is a local documentation instrument covering resources, their uses and associated knowledge, and it anchors the community's claim in benefit-sharing.
- **D — Incorrect.** Consent conditions belong to the pollution-control record of a unit.

**Examiner trap:** treating the register as a conservation notification rather than a documentation record.

### Q12. Access and benefit sharing under the Biological Diversity Act is best described as:

A. a levy on effluent discharge calculated from the pollution potential of an industrial unit.
B. a licensing system governing the export of timber and non-timber forest produce.
C. a compensation mechanism for land acquired while expanding a protected area.
D. a regulated arrangement under which users of biological resources share benefits with providers.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Effluent charges belong to the water-pollution regime, not to the biodiversity statute.
- **B — Incorrect.** Forest produce movement is governed by forest law and, for listed species, by wildlife and trade law.
- **C — Incorrect.** Compensation for acquired land is settled under land and forest-rights law.
- **D — Correct.** The Act channels access to biological resources and associated knowledge through approval or intimation, and requires that benefits reach the providers of the resource or knowledge.

**Examiner trap:** reading benefit sharing as a pollution charge because both involve payment.

### Q13. After the Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023, how does the requirement differ between an Indian company and a foreign-controlled company seeking commercial use of a biological resource?

A. The foreign-controlled user needs national approval; the Indian user gives intimation to the State Board.
B. Both users must obtain the prior approval of the national authority before any access is permitted.
C. The Indian user needs national approval; the foreign-controlled user informs the State Board instead.
D. Both users are exempt where the resource is sourced from a notified biodiversity heritage site.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** The amendment retains national approval for foreign-controlled and non-resident users while routing specified Indian commercial users through intimation to the State Biodiversity Board.
- **B — Incorrect.** It erases the distinction that the amendment deliberately created.
- **C — Incorrect.** It reverses the two requirements.
- **D — Incorrect.** Heritage-site notification protects an area; it does not by itself dissolve access obligations.

**Examiner trap:** assuming liberalisation applied equally to domestic and foreign-controlled users.

### Q14. Consider the following categories under the amended Biological Diversity Act:

1. Registered practitioners of Indian systems of medicine using biological resources in their practice.
2. Persons commercially using cultivated medicinal plants, on producing a certificate of origin.
3. Foreign nationals seeking intellectual property based on Indian biological resources.

Which of the categories given above are relieved of the requirement of prior intimation to the State Biodiversity Board?

A. Category 1 only
B. Categories 1 and 2 only
C. Categories 2 and 3 only
D. Categories 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** It omits the cultivated-medicinal-plant route, which also receives relief from prior intimation when supported by a certificate of origin.
- **B — Correct.** The amendment relieves registered AYUSH practitioners and qualifying users of cultivated medicinal plants from prior intimation under Section 7; the latter use a certificate-of-origin route involving the local committee.
- **C — Incorrect.** Foreign applicants remain within the approval framework.
- **D — Incorrect.** It extends the relief to a category that the amendment kept regulated.

**Examiner trap:** generalising "ease of doing business" into a blanket exemption.

### Q15. How are contraventions of the Biological Diversity Act dealt with after the 2023 amendment?

A. Through criminal prosecution before a magistrate, with imprisonment as the ordinary sanction.
B. Through a civil suit for damages filed by the national authority in the jurisdictional High Court.
C. Through monetary penalties determined by an adjudicating officer in an administrative inquiry.
D. Through cancellation of the offender's industrial consent by the State Pollution Control Board.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** The amendment moved the ordinary contravention away from criminal prosecution.
- **B — Incorrect.** A damages suit is not the mechanism the statute now prescribes.
- **C — Correct.** Contraventions attract monetary penalties adjudicated administratively by a designated officer, with an appellate route provided by the statute.
- **D — Incorrect.** Consent cancellation belongs to the pollution regime and to a different set of boards.

**Examiner trap:** equating decriminalisation with the removal of liability.

### Q16. Which statement about the subordinate law made after the 2023 amendment is correct?

A. The 2024 Rules shifted the national authority's headquarters to the national capital.
B. The 2025 access regulations withdrew the role of local biodiversity management committees.
C. The 2024 Rules removed the benefit-sharing obligation for commercial use of resources.
D. The 2025 regulations extended the benefit-sharing framework to digital sequence information.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** The Rules deal with procedure and adjudication machinery, not with the seat of the authority.
- **B — Incorrect.** The local committees retain their documentation and certification role.
- **C — Incorrect.** Benefit sharing is the core obligation of the statute and survives the amendment.
- **D — Correct.** The 2025 access and benefit-sharing regulations, notified on 29 April 2025, bring digital sequence information derived from Indian biological resources within the framework.

**Examiner trap:** assuming that procedural simplification repealed the substantive duty.

### Q17. Which description of the Wildlife Institute of India is accurate?

A. An autonomous research, training and advisory institute at Dehradun, established in 1982.
B. A statutory authority under the Wildlife (Protection) Act that approves tiger-reserve proposals.
C. An enforcement bureau that investigates organised trafficking in wildlife articles and derivatives.
D. A notified authority that issues clearances for projects located inside protected areas.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** The Institute was established in 1982 at Dehradun and works as an autonomous research, training and advisory body under the environment ministry.
- **B — Incorrect.** Tiger-reserve approval work belongs to the National Tiger Conservation Authority.
- **C — Incorrect.** Wildlife-crime intelligence and coordination belong to the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau.
- **D — Incorrect.** Clearances for projects affecting protected areas move through the statutory wildlife and clearance machinery, not through a research institute.

**Examiner trap:** upgrading a respected scientific institution into a regulator.

### Q18. The India State of Forest Report is produced by which body, and with what legal effect?

A. The forestry research council; it settles disputed boundaries between reserved and revenue land.
B. The Forest Survey of India; it is an assessment that informs policy without regulating land use.
C. The environment ministry; it operates as a binding land-use notification for the mapped area.
D. The National Green Tribunal; it records compliance with conditions attached to forest clearances.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Boundary settlement is a matter for forest settlement proceedings under forest law.
- **B — Correct.** The Survey conducts the periodic assessment; the report is authoritative evidence that shapes policy discussion but carries no regulatory force of its own.
- **C — Incorrect.** The Ministry issues notifications, but the assessment itself is not one.
- **D — Incorrect.** The Tribunal adjudicates; it does not compile national forest-cover data.

**Examiner trap:** converting authoritative data into a legal instrument.

### Q19. Which pairing is correct?

A. Botanical Survey of India — faunal taxonomy and animal collections, headquartered at Dehradun.
B. Zoological Survey of India — plant taxonomy and herbarium curation, headquartered at Bhopal.
C. Botanical Survey of India — plant taxonomy and floristic survey, headquartered at Kolkata.
D. Zoological Survey of India — forest-cover mapping and canopy-density classes, headquartered at Chennai.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** It gives the botanical survey an animal mandate and the wrong city.
- **B — Incorrect.** It gives the zoological survey a plant mandate and the wrong city.
- **C — Correct.** The Botanical Survey of India, founded in 1890 and headquartered at Kolkata, carries out plant taxonomy and floristic survey; the Zoological Survey of India, founded in 1916, is also headquartered at Kolkata and covers animal taxonomy.
- **D — Incorrect.** Forest-cover mapping belongs to the Forest Survey of India at Dehradun.

**Examiner trap:** memorising the two surveys as a pair without fixing which covers which kingdom.

### Q20. Which pairing of institution and principal domain is correct?

A. Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education — regulating felling permissions in State forests.
B. Indian Institute of Forest Management — conducting the national taxonomic survey of animals.
C. National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management — issuing coastal regulation zone clearances.
D. Indian Institute of Forest Management, Bhopal — forest-management education, training and capacity work.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Felling permissions are granted under State forest law by the forest department, not by a research council.
- **B — Incorrect.** Animal taxonomy is the Zoological Survey of India's mandate.
- **C — Incorrect.** Coastal clearances are appraised by the Coastal Zone Management Authorities; the Centre at Chennai supplies the science.
- **D — Correct.** The Institute at Bhopal works on forest-management education, training and capacity development, and leads the capacity-development vertical of the ministry's research platform.

**Examiner trap:** assuming that an institution with subject expertise must also hold the licensing power.

### Q21. A ministry refuses a project citing a survey institute's habitat study. Which reading of the two institutions is correct?

A. The institute supplied evidence; the decision and the accountability for it rest with the ministry.
B. The institute exercised a veto, because an adverse scientific finding legally binds the decision-maker.
C. The institute acted as a regulator, since producing evidence for a decision is a regulatory act.
D. The institute must defend the refusal in a tribunal, being the author of the operative finding.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Scientific institutions supply findings; the statutory decision, its reasons and its defence belong to the authority empowered to decide.
- **B — Incorrect.** A study informs the decision; it does not displace the decision-maker's statutory discretion.
- **C — Incorrect.** Generating evidence is not regulation; regulation requires a legal power to permit, condition or prohibit.
- **D — Incorrect.** The order under challenge is the ministry's, so the ministry defends it.

**Examiner trap:** treating scientific influence over a decision as legal authority over it.

### Q22. Under which provision, and for what purpose, was the National Tiger Conservation Authority constituted?

A. Section 38Y of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, to coordinate action against wildlife crime.
B. Section 38L of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, to oversee Project Tiger and tiger reserves.
C. Section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, to regulate land use around reserves.
D. Section 8 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, to regulate access to tiger-habitat resources.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Section 38Y relates to the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, constituted in 2007.
- **B — Correct.** The Authority was constituted in 2006 under Section 38L, inserted by the 2006 amendment, and carries statutory responsibility for Project Tiger and tiger-reserve management.
- **C — Incorrect.** That provision supports notified authorities under the general environmental statute.
- **D — Incorrect.** Section 8 establishes the biodiversity regulator, whose subject is resource access.

**Examiner trap:** mixing two adjacent sections of the same wildlife statute.

### Q23. Which body gathers and disseminates intelligence on organised wildlife crime and assists enforcement agencies?

A. The National Tiger Conservation Authority, constituted in 2006.
B. The Wildlife Institute of India, established in 1982.
C. The Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, constituted in 2007.
D. The National Board for Wildlife, chaired by the Prime Minister.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** That Authority's statutory focus is tiger conservation and reserve management.
- **B — Incorrect.** The Institute conducts research and training, including forensic science, without an enforcement mandate.
- **C — Correct.** The Bureau is the multi-disciplinary statutory body for intelligence on organised wildlife crime and for assisting enforcement agencies.
- **D — Incorrect.** The Board is an apex advisory and recommending body on wildlife policy and protected-area proposals.

**Examiner trap:** assuming the best-known wildlife body must also be the enforcement body.

### Q24. A consignment of pangolin scales is seized while moving across three States. Which institutional reading is correct?

A. The tiger authority leads, since species-protection enforcement is concentrated in that body.
B. The wildlife research institute leads, since it maintains forensic identification capability.
C. The environmental tribunal leads, since trafficking falls within its original jurisdiction.
D. The wildlife-crime bureau coordinates, since its mandate covers wildlife crime across species.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** That authority's statutory remit is tiger-specific and does not extend to trafficking generally.
- **B — Incorrect.** Forensic support is valuable, but support is not leadership of an enforcement operation.
- **C — Incorrect.** The Tribunal adjudicates civil environmental disputes; wildlife offences are prosecuted in the criminal courts.
- **D — Correct.** Cross-State organised wildlife crime is the coordination mandate of the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, working with State forest and police agencies and customs.

**Examiner trap:** picking the most famous wildlife institution instead of the one with the legal mandate.

### Q25. Consider the following statements about the National Board for Wildlife:

1. It is chaired by the Prime Minister.
2. Its Standing Committee is chaired by the Minister in charge of environment and forests.
3. Its recommendation is relevant to proposals affecting protected areas.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. Statements 1, 2 and 3
B. Statements 1 and 2 only
C. Statements 2 and 3 only
D. Statements 1 and 3 only

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** All three propositions hold: the Prime Minister chairs the Board, the environment minister chairs its Standing Committee, and its recommendation is required for de-notification of protected areas and for proposals in and around them.
- **B — Incorrect.** It drops the protected-area recommendation, which is the Board's most examined function.
- **C — Incorrect.** It drops the chairmanship, which is a frequently tested fact.
- **D — Incorrect.** It drops the Standing Committee distinction, which is where most routine business is decided.

**Examiner trap:** knowing the chairman but not that a Standing Committee handles routine proposals.

### Q26. Which statement about the National Green Tribunal is correct?

A. It frames the ambient air-quality standards that the pollution-control boards then apply.
B. It hears appeals against orders of pollution-control boards and clearance-granting authorities.
C. It grants consent to establish to industrial units proposed in ecologically sensitive areas.
D. It maintains the national inventory of contaminated sites and allocates remediation budgets.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Standard-setting is a Central Board function under the air and water statutes.
- **B — Correct.** Along with original jurisdiction over substantial environmental questions arising from the scheduled laws, the Tribunal exercises appellate jurisdiction over Board and clearance orders, and may grant relief, compensation and restitution.
- **C — Incorrect.** Consent is an executive regulatory act performed by a Board.
- **D — Incorrect.** Site inventories and remediation finance are administered under the contaminated-sites framework by executive bodies.

**Examiner trap:** blurring adjudication and regulation because both concern the same disputes.

### Q27. How do the National Green Tribunal and the Central Pollution Control Board differ in legal origin?

A. The Tribunal rests on an Act, while the Board rests on an executive order of the Union government.
B. The Board rests on an Act, while the Tribunal was established by a direction of the Supreme Court.
C. Both rest on Acts of Parliament, although on different statutes with different functions.
D. Both were created by notifications issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** The Board is statutory: it was constituted under the Water Act, 1974, not by an executive order.
- **B — Incorrect.** The Tribunal was created by the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, although judicial concern about environmental adjudication preceded it.
- **C — Correct.** Both bodies are statutory; the difference lies in the statute and in the function — adjudication in one case and pollution regulation in the other.
- **D — Incorrect.** Neither body was created by notification under the 1986 Act.

**Examiner trap:** accepting the familiar claim that the Board was created by executive order.

### Q28. A tribunal directs a State agency to complete a remediation programme within a year, and little changes. Which inference is sound?

A. The direction lapses on its own, since a tribunal order operates only for the period specified in it.
B. The agency's inaction converts the tribunal into the implementing authority for that programme.
C. The episode shows that environmental adjudication carries no legal force against State agencies.
D. Adjudication can fix a legal duty, but delivery still depends on executive capacity and follow-up.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** A time-bound direction does not extinguish itself; non-compliance invites further proceedings.
- **B — Incorrect.** A tribunal supervises compliance; it does not take over executive functions.
- **C — Incorrect.** The order remains legally binding, and non-compliance carries consequences.
- **D — Correct.** The adjudicatory forum can establish and enforce an obligation, but outcomes depend on the capacity, finance and follow-up of the executive body that must act.

**Examiner trap:** swinging between treating orders as self-executing and treating them as meaningless.

### Q29. Which statement about the Commission for Air Quality Management is accurate?

A. Within its notified area its air-quality directions prevail over conflicting State Board directions.

### Q40. Which framing of India's multi-body environmental architecture is analytically defensible?

A. Fragmentation is purely a design flaw, since one consolidated regulator would close every gap.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Specialisation gives dedicated technical focus that a single generalist regulator would find difficult to reproduce across pollution, biodiversity, wildlife crime and science.
- **B — Incorrect.** Clear drafting reduces mandate confusion, but transboundary problems still need designed mechanisms, which is why a dedicated air-quality commission was created for the capital region.
- **C — Incorrect.** A capacity claim carries weight only when attributed to a dated audit, committee report or official review.
- **D — Correct.** The defensible position holds both halves together: specialisation buys expertise, and the resulting coordination cost must be met by deliberate institutional design rather than assumed away.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish a statutory board, a notified authority and a registered society exercising delegated authority powers in India's environmental governance. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer — 149 words:**
Indian environmental bodies differ by the instrument that creates them, and that instrument fixes both their powers and their accountability channel.

A statutory board is constituted by the Act itself. The **Central Pollution Control Board** was constituted under Section 3 of the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, and the Air Act, 1981 later vested national air functions in the same body.

A notified authority is created by executive order under a parent statute. The **Central Ground Water Authority** was notified in January 1997 under Section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, so its powers run only as far as that order.

The **National Mission for Clean Ganga** is a registered society on which Authority powers were conferred by a 2016 order under the same Act.

The third form is therefore easiest to reorganise and the first hardest, which shapes how durable each mandate is.

**Why this earns marks:** three legal forms are separated, each is anchored to a named body and provision, and the closing line converts the classification into an institutional consequence.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why bodies such as the Wildlife Institute of India and the Forest Survey of India are not regulators, and why that separation matters. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer — 136 words:**
The **Wildlife Institute of India**, established at Dehradun in 1982, and the **Forest Survey of India**, which produces the periodic India State of Forest Report, are autonomous institutions whose output is evidence: population estimates, habitat assessments and forest-cover data. Neither grants a consent, refuses a clearance nor prosecutes an offence.

The separation matters in three ways. Evidence produced independently of a decision retains credibility when that decision is contested before the National Green Tribunal. Accountability stays traceable, because the deciding body — a ministry, a pollution-control board or the National Tiger Conservation Authority — cannot shelter behind a survey. Methodological limits also stay visible, since canopy-density mapping measures cover rather than ecological quality.

The firewall is not self-executing. Sustained funding and administrative distance from the bodies these institutes advise are what keep the separation real rather than formal.

**Why this earns marks:** the claim is grounded in two named institutions, three distinct reasons are given, and the final qualification identifies the condition on which the separation depends.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine whether India's specialised, multi-institution environmental architecture strengthens or weakens governance effectiveness. **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer — 249 words:**
India does not run environmental governance through a single regulator. The **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change** frames policy and administers the statutes; the **Central Pollution Control Board** sets standards while **State Boards** grant consent and inspect; the **National Biodiversity Authority**, with State Boards and local committees, regulates resource access; the **Wildlife Crime Control Bureau** handles trafficking while the **National Tiger Conservation Authority** handles tiger reserves; **WII, FSI, BSI and ZSI** generate evidence; and the **National Green Tribunal** adjudicates.

Specialisation is a genuine strength. These functions demand different competences — emission chemistry, benefit-sharing law, criminal intelligence, population ecology, judicial reasoning — and a single generalist regulator would dilute each of them. It also forces an answer to name the correct actor before proposing reform.

The cost is coordination. One project can simultaneously engage clearance, pollution consent, resource access and litigation, and transboundary pollution escapes any single State Board, which is why a dedicated air-quality commission was created for the National Capital Region and adjoining districts. The Economic Survey's own institutional diagnosis names multiplicity of clearances, weak post-clearance monitoring and wide inter-State variation.

The balance therefore turns on design. Where coordination is deliberately engineered — a commission with a defined territorial override, or a research platform with a named lead institute for each vertical — specialisation delivers. Where coordination is merely assumed, sequencing gaps appear.

The defensible conclusion is conditional. Fragmentation is not the failure and consolidation is not the remedy; designed coordination, supported by capacity at the tier that actually enforces, is.

**Why this earns marks:** the architecture is mapped by function, both sides of the argument carry named institutional evidence, a government-sourced critique is used, and the conclusion resolves the "examine" directive without a generic call to strengthen institutions.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse how the Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023 and the subordinate law made after it have reshaped the framework administered by the National Biodiversity Authority. **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer — 244 words:**
The **National Biodiversity Authority**, established under Section 8 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and working from Chennai, sits above **State Biodiversity Boards** and local **Biodiversity Management Committees**, whose People's Biodiversity Registers anchor community claims in benefit-sharing.

The 2023 amendment changed the gateway rather than the objective. Foreign-controlled and non-resident users continue to require approval of the national authority, while Indian companies without foreign control proceed by intimation to the State Board. Registered AYUSH practitioners and codified-traditional-knowledge users were relieved of prior intimation under Section 7, and commercial use of cultivated medicinal plants was routed through a certificate of origin involving the local committee. Contraventions moved from criminal prosecution to monetary penalties determined by an adjudicating officer.

Subordinate law followed. The **Biological Diversity Rules, 2024** supplied procedure and adjudication machinery, and the access and benefit-sharing regulations notified on **29 April 2025** brought **digital sequence information** within the framework — a consequential step, because much commercial value now travels as data rather than as physical material.

The reform therefore trades enforcement severity for administrative throughput while widening what counts as access. That is coherent, but two qualifications are necessary. Easier entry does not establish that benefits reach providers; that depends on whether local committees are funded, staffed and genuinely consulted. And administrative penalties deter only where adjudication is prompt and recovery actually follows.

The framework has become more usable. Whether it has become more equitable is a question that implementation evidence, not statutory text, must answer.

**Why this earns marks:** the three-tier structure is established first, each change is stated with its precise instrument and date, the analytical trade-off is named, and the qualification separates statutory design from delivered outcome.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically evaluate the shift in India's environmental compliance architecture from criminal sanction towards administrative penalty and third-party verification. **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer — 293 words:**
India's compliance architecture has moved along two tracks. On the sanction side, offences under the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, the **Air Act, 1981** and the **Indian Forest Act, 1927** were decriminalised through the general ease-of-compliance route, while the **Water Act, 1974** required the **Article 252** consent route because water is a State List subject. The constitutional difference follows from legislative competence, not from policy preference, and the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act, 2024 applies in the first instance to Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan and the Union territories. The Biological Diversity Act moved similarly, replacing prosecution with penalties set by an adjudicating officer.

On the verification side, the **Environment Audit Rules, 2025** created certified third-party environmental auditors registered through a designated agency; categorisation reform added a **Blue** class for essential environmental services; and a contaminated-sites framework notified in July 2025 provided for identification, remediation and orphan sites.

The logic is defensible. Criminal provisions that are rarely prosecuted deter weakly, whereas a penalty actually imposed can deter more reliably. State Boards face documented capacity constraints, so importing verification capacity addresses a real bottleneck without adding another regulator.

The risks are equally real. Decriminalisation lowers the expressive weight attached to environmental wrongdoing, which matters where harm is irreversible. Administrative penalties deter only if adjudication is quick and recovery follows. The Rules require random allocation and bar conflicts of interest, but the audited entity still pays its auditor; regulatory re-verification and liability for false certification determine whether those safeguards bite.

The balanced verdict is that India has outsourced verification while retaining sanction with the State. That division can work, but only if the State keeps enough inspection capacity to test the auditors themselves. Otherwise paperwork substitutes for compliance and the last-mile gap survives the reform.

**Why this earns marks:** the two tracks are separated, every instrument is named with its route or date, the constitutional reason for the divergent route is explained, and the critical evaluation identifies the specific failure mode rather than offering generalised scepticism.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** State Pollution Control Boards are described as the last mile of India's pollution governance. Discuss, with reference to the division of functions between the Central and State Boards and to recent reform measures. **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer — 294 words:**
The division of labour is statutory. The **Central Pollution Control Board**, constituted under Section 3 of the Water Act, 1974, advises the Union government, plans nationwide programmes, coordinates State Boards, supplies technical assistance and training, compiles data and lays down standards — functions repeated for air pollution in Section 16 of the Air Act, 1981. **State Boards** grant consent to establish and consent to operate, inspect premises, sample effluent and emissions, issue directions and initiate action. In a Union territory no State Board is constituted: the Central Board performs those functions and may delegate them to a body the Union government specifies, which is how Union territory Pollution Control Committees operate.

The consequence is that a national standard becomes real only at the State tier. Ambient air quality standards, effluent norms and consent conditions are all enforced by a body constituted by, funded by and answerable to a State government. Parliamentary committee reviews and audit-type assessments have repeatedly flagged staffing shortfalls, uneven technical capacity and, in specific instances, questions about independence from local pressure. The most consequential implementation gap in pollution governance is therefore a last-mile gap, not a missing institution.

Recent reform responds to that diagnosis. Uniform consent guidelines across Boards followed the water and air amendments; the **Blue** category recognised essential environmental services such as municipal waste processing, sewage treatment and compressed biogas plants; the **Environment Audit Rules, 2025** added certified third-party auditors; and single-window digital clearance and compliance monitoring has been extended.

Two qualifications are necessary. Capacity claims should be attributed to a dated review rather than asserted generally, because Boards differ widely across States. And portals and certified auditors are instruments, not outcomes: without sanctioned posts, functioning laboratories and protected tenure at the State tier, verification improves while enforcement does not.

**Why this earns marks:** the statutory split is stated precisely, including the Union territory rule, the last-mile claim is argued rather than asserted, reform measures are named with their function, and the qualification refuses both generalised criticism and reform optimism.

## RAPID REMEDIATION AND RECALL

**If you missed Q1, Q9, Q30, Q31 or Q32 — the classification block.** Re-fix one line: the *instrument of creation* decides the category. Act itself → statutory board or authority (CPCB, NBA, NTCA, WCCB, NDMA). Order under a parent Act → notified authority (CGWA, NCZMA, SCZMA). Societies law plus a conferring order → society with delegated powers (NMCG). Nothing in a body's name settles this.

**If you missed Q3, Q4, Q5 or Q6 — the Central-versus-State split.** The Central Board advises, plans, coordinates, supports, compiles and sets standards. The State Board consents, inspects and enforces. Union territories have no State Board; the Central Board covers them, with delegation permitted.

**If you missed Q7, Q8 or Q32 — the constitutional route.** Water is a State List subject, so the Water Act line of legislation runs through Article 252 and reaches other States only on adoption. Groundwater regulation, by contrast, was brought in through a notification under the 1986 Act.

**If you missed Q10, Q11, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q15 or Q16 — the biodiversity gateway.** Approval under Section 3 belongs to the national authority; intimation under Section 7 belongs to the State Board. Add the local register, practitioner and cultivated-plant relief, administrative penalties and digital sequence information.

**If you missed Q17, Q18, Q19, Q20 or Q21 — the evidence firewall.** WII, FSI, BSI, ZSI, ICFRE, IIFM and NCSCM produce findings. Ministries, boards and authorities take decisions. Influence is not authority.

**If you missed Q22, Q23, Q24 or Q35 — the wildlife set.** Section 38L and 2006 for the tiger authority; Section 38Y and 2007 for the crime bureau; the Prime Minister chairs the wildlife board with the environment minister chairing its Standing Committee.

**If you missed Q26, Q27, Q28 or Q29 — adjudication versus regulation.** The Tribunal hears and orders; the Board regulates. Both are statutory. The air-quality commission overrides State Board directions only inside its notified area.

**If you missed Q36, Q37, Q38 or Q39 — the current reform block.** Third-party audit certification; a Blue class for essential environmental services; a contaminated-sites framework; and a coordination platform with four verticals and named lead institutes. None of these creates a new regulator.

**If you missed Q2, Q25, Q33, Q34 or Q40 — the integration block.** Classify the body first, then separate science, enforcement, coordination and adjudication. Specialisation creates expertise; it does not eliminate the need for designed coordination and attributable evidence.
