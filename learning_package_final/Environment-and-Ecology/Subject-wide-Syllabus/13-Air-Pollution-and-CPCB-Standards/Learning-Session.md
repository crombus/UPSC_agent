## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

## 1. The complete air-pollution chain

### 1.1 Visual first

```text
ACTIVITY / FUEL / PROCESS
        │
        ▼
SOURCE EMISSION ── mass released per time or activity
        │          (stack, tailpipe, field fire, cooking stove, road dust)
        ▼
ATMOSPHERIC FATE ─ dilution + transport + chemistry + deposition
        │
        ▼
AMBIENT CONCENTRATION ─ mass per volume at a place and averaging time
        │
        ▼
HUMAN / ECOSYSTEM EXPOSURE ─ concentration actually contacted over time
        │
        ▼
INTERNAL DOSE ─ amount entering or retained in the body
        │
        ▼
HEALTH / CROP / ECOSYSTEM / MATERIAL OUTCOME
```

### 1.2 The five quantities UPSC commonly mixes up

| Quantity | What it measures | Typical unit/example | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emission** | Pollutant released by a source | g/s, kg/day, g/km, mg/Nm³ in stack gas | The concentration at every receptor |
| **Ambient concentration** | Pollutant present in outdoor air at a location over an averaging period | µg/m³; CO also mg/m³ in NAAQS | Personal exposure or absorbed dose |
| **Exposure** | Contact with pollution through place, time, activity and route | Time-weighted inhalation exposure | That the source causing exposure is locally dominant |
| **Dose** | Pollutant entering/retained in the body | Inhaled or absorbed amount | A guaranteed disease outcome |
| **Outcome** | Health, crop, ecosystem or material effect | Asthma exacerbation, yield loss, acidification | Which single source caused it without attribution evidence |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Effective regulation must connect **source emissions → ambient concentration → exposure → outcome**; controlling only one link can leave the others unchanged.

### 1.3 Inputs, outputs and outcomes

| Evaluation level | Air-policy examples | Frequent error |
|---|---|---|
| **Input** | Funds, staff, laboratories, monitors, buses, CRM machines | Calling allocation or procurement “cleaner air” |
| **Output** | Stations commissioned, inspections completed, roads swept, filters installed, action plans issued | Assuming implementation quality and use |
| **Intermediate outcome** | Lower source emissions; lower annual/seasonal ambient concentration | Assuming equal reduction in every neighbourhood or season |
| **Final outcome** | Lower exposure, morbidity, crop loss and ecosystem damage | Claiming health gains without exposure/epidemiological evidence |

⚠️ A monitor installed is an **output**. A defensible fall in a comparable pollutant concentration is an **intermediate outcome**. Reduced disease burden is a **final outcome** requiring additional evidence.

## 2. Pollutant taxonomy: primary, secondary, natural and anthropogenic

### 2.1 Primary and secondary are formation categories

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary pollutant** | Emitted directly from a source | CO, SO₂, NH₃, Pb/metals, primary PM, VOCs, NO from combustion |
| **Secondary pollutant** | Formed in air through chemical or physical transformation | Ground-level O₃, sulfate, nitrate, ammonium salts, secondary organic aerosol, nitric/sulfuric acids, PAN |
| **Both primary and secondary** | Has direct emissions and atmospheric formation | PM₂.₅/PM₁₀; NO₂ can be emitted and also form rapidly from oxidation of NO |
| **Precursor** | Participates in forming another pollutant | SO₂, NOx, NH₃ and VOCs form secondary PM and/or ozone |

```text
SO₂ ──oxidation──► H₂SO₄ / sulfate ─┐
NOx ─oxidation───► HNO₃ / nitrate ──┼─► secondary PM₂.₅
NH₃ ─neutralises acids───────────────┘

NO₂ + sunlight ─► NO + O
O + O₂ ─► O₃
VOCs/CO + radicals help reconvert NO to NO₂ without consuming O₃
                     └──────────────► net photochemical ozone accumulation
```

### 2.2 Source classification

- **Combustion:** vehicles, thermal power, industry, diesel generators, biomass/crop residue, cooking and tobacco.
- **Mechanical/fugitive:** road resuspension, construction dust, mining, material handling, tyre/brake wear.
- **Evaporative:** fuel storage/refuelling, solvents, paints and petrochemical processes.
- **Agricultural:** NH₃ from fertilizer/manure; smoke from residue burning.
- **Waste:** open burning, landfill fires and volatile emissions.
- **Natural:** wind-blown dust, sea salt, wildfires, volcanoes, pollen and biogenic VOCs. Natural origin does not make a pollutant harmless.

## 3. The twelve NAAQS pollutants: science, sources and effects

### 3.1 Particulate matter is a size fraction, not one chemical

- **PM₁₀:** particles with aerodynamic diameter up to 10 µm.
- **PM₂.₅:** particles with aerodynamic diameter up to 2.5 µm.
- PM₂.₅ is contained within PM₁₀ as a size fraction, but a reported PM₁₀ value is not obtained by simply adding separately reported PM₂.₅.
- **Primary PM:** dust, soot, ash, sea salt, tyre/brake wear and direct combustion particles.
- **Secondary PM:** sulfate, nitrate, ammonium and secondary organic aerosol formed from gases.
- Finer particles generally penetrate deeper into the respiratory tract; toxicity also depends on composition, surface chemistry, dose and host vulnerability.

### 3.2 Pollutant matrix

| Pollutant | Formation and major sources | Human-health significance | Ecosystem/material significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PM₂.₅ / PM₁₀** | Dust, construction, roads, combustion, industry; also secondary sulfate/nitrate/ammonium/organic aerosol | Respiratory and cardiovascular effects; PM₂.₅ reaches deeper lung regions and is linked with systemic effects | Visibility loss, soiling, radiative effects; deposited acids, nutrients and toxics alter soils/waters |
| **SO₂** | Sulfur-bearing coal/oil combustion, smelting; oxidises to sulfate | Airway irritation and asthma aggravation; contributes to PM₂.₅ | Acid deposition, foliar injury, aquatic/soil acidification, corrosion |
| **NOx / NO₂** | High-temperature combustion; NO oxidises to NO₂ | Airway inflammation; NO₂ is the NAAQS pollutant | Precursor of ozone, nitrate PM and nitric acid; acidification and nitrogen enrichment |
| **Ground-level O₃** | Secondary: NOx + VOC/CO chemistry in sunlight | Respiratory irritation, reduced lung function, asthma aggravation | Strong phytotoxicant: leaf injury, reduced photosynthesis and crop/forest productivity; damages materials |
| **CO** | Incomplete combustion: vehicles, generators, fires and indoor stoves | Binds haemoglobin and impairs oxygen delivery; high exposure is acutely dangerous | Limited direct vegetation effect at usual ambient levels; participates indirectly in atmospheric oxidation/ozone chemistry |
| **NH₃** | Fertilizer, manure/livestock, waste and some industry | Irritant at high concentrations; major precursor of ammonium PM₂.₅ | Reactive-nitrogen deposition drives eutrophication, soil change and biodiversity loss |
| **Pb** | Smelting, battery manufacture/recycling, contaminated dust and industrial combustion | Neurotoxic, especially for children; also haematological, renal and cardiovascular effects | Persistent deposition contaminates soil/water and harms organisms |
| **Benzene** | Fuel evaporation/exhaust, tobacco smoke, solvent and combustion sources | Bone-marrow toxicity; carcinogenic, associated with leukaemia | A VOC participating in atmospheric chemistry; direct ecological effects are usually less central than human toxicity |
| **Benzo(a)pyrene, particulate phase** | PAH from incomplete combustion of coal, diesel, biomass, waste and tobacco | Genotoxic/carcinogenic hazard; carried on particles | Persistent particle-bound deposition can contaminate soil/sediment and biota |
| **Arsenic** | Non-ferrous smelting, coal combustion and contaminated dust | Carcinogenic and systemic toxicity | Persistent toxic deposition affects soils, water and organisms |
| **Nickel** | Metallurgy and combustion of heavy fuel oil/coal | Respiratory toxicity; several nickel compounds are carcinogenic | Deposited nickel can be toxic to plants and aquatic/soil organisms |

### 3.3 Three high-yield distinctions

1. **NOx is not the same label as NO₂:** NOx usually means NO + NO₂ for emission chemistry; the 2009 ambient standard is listed for **NO₂**.
2. **Ground-level and stratospheric ozone are not interchangeable:** tropospheric ozone is a pollutant; stratospheric ozone shields ultraviolet radiation.
3. **Size does not identify source:** PM₂.₅ may be soot, sulfate, nitrate, ammonium, organics or metals; PM₁₀ may include coarse dust and biological material.

## 4. Smog, inversion, atmospheric transport and deposition

### 4.1 Smog types

| Feature | Classical/London smog | Photochemical/Los Angeles smog |
|---|---|---|
| Meteorology | Cool, humid, stagnant conditions | Warm, sunny, stagnant conditions |
| Traditional source mix | Coal smoke + SO₂ + particles | Traffic/industry NOx + VOCs |
| Chemistry | Sulfur oxidation, droplets and smoke; historically called **reducing** smog | Sun-driven oxidant chemistry; **oxidising** smog |
| Important products | Sulfate/acid aerosol and dense particulate haze | O₃, PAN, aldehydes and secondary organic/inorganic aerosol |
| Main control | Low-sulfur/clean fuels, combustion and PM controls | Control both NOx and VOCs, with chemistry-aware local design |

> **PYQ anchor:** `NOx + VOCs + sunlight → ground-level ozone and other oxidants` is the core formation route for photochemical smog.

### 4.2 Temperature inversion

Normally, warmer surface air can rise and mix pollutants upward. During an **inversion**, temperature increases with height across a layer, creating stable stratification and suppressing vertical mixing.

```text
NORMAL:                                  INVERSION:
cooler aloft                             warm cap aloft
     ↑ mixing                                 ─────────
surface emissions disperse              pollutants trapped below cap
```

- Clear winter nights favour shallow **radiation inversions**.
- Persistent high pressure can cause **subsidence inversions**.
- Low wind, a shallow mixing layer and high humidity can intensify episodes.
- An inversion does not create emissions; it raises concentration by reducing dispersion.

### 4.3 Transport, transformation and removal

```text
Emission
  ├─► turbulent mixing / plume rise
  ├─► horizontal advection and regional transport
  ├─► chemical ageing and gas-particle conversion
  ├─► dry deposition to surfaces
  └─► wet deposition
        ├─ rainout: incorporation within clouds
        └─ washout: scavenging below clouds
```

- Pollution measured in a city may contain **local, upwind regional and secondary** components.
- Wind changes can lower one station's concentration without reducing total emissions.
- Wet deposition may temporarily clean air while transferring contaminants to land and water.
- Long-range transport makes airsheds, not municipal borders, the scientifically relevant unit.

## 5. Indoor air pollution

### 5.1 Major indoor sources

- Solid-fuel/biomass and kerosene cooking or heating: PM, CO and combustion toxics.
- Tobacco smoke, incense, candles and mosquito coils.
- Gas cooking without adequate exhaust: NO₂ and combustion products.
- Paints, solvents, cleaners, furnishings and building materials: VOCs, including formaldehyde from some products.
- Dampness and mould; allergens and bioaerosols.
- Radon entry from soil/building materials in susceptible settings.
- Outdoor pollution infiltrating through doors, windows and ventilation systems.

### 5.2 Exposure logic

```text
Indoor concentration × time spent × breathing rate × vulnerability
                         = exposure pattern
```

Women, children, older persons, workers and people with cardiopulmonary disease may have unequal exposure even when the outdoor monitoring station reports one city AQI.

### 5.3 Control hierarchy

1. **Eliminate/substitute:** clean household energy, smoke-free spaces, low-emission materials.
2. **Capture at source:** chimneys, kitchen hoods and local exhaust.
3. **Ventilate safely:** increase air exchange when outdoor air is cleaner; filter when needed.
4. **Maintain:** service appliances, prevent dampness and replace filters.
5. **Protective behaviour:** reduce high-emission activities; portable filtration is supplementary, not source control.

### 6.2 Official averaging and compliance notes

- **Annual:** arithmetic mean of a minimum **104 measurements** at a site in a year, taken **twice a week, 24-hourly, at uniform intervals**.
- The applicable **24-hour, 8-hour or 1-hour** value is to be complied with **98% of the time in a year**.
- It may exceed the limit **2% of the time**, but **not on two consecutive days of monitoring**.
- Two consecutive monitored exceedances are adequate reason to institute regular/continuous monitoring and further investigation.

### 6.3 What NAAQS do and do not do

**They do:** set pollutant-, unit- and averaging-period-specific ambient concentration benchmarks.

**They do not:** set a tailpipe/stack mass-emission limit; report individual exposure; replace source standards; or mean that pollution below the number is risk-free.

## 8. National Air Quality Index: public communication, not a standard

### 8.1 Pollutants and method

India's National AQI uses:

**PM₁₀, PM₂.₅, NO₂, O₃, CO, SO₂, NH₃ and Pb.**

It excludes the four NAAQS pollutants having only annual standards in the 2009 table: **benzene, benzo(a)pyrene, arsenic and nickel**.

```text
Measured concentration
      │
      ├─► pollutant-specific breakpoint interpolation
      │       = pollutant sub-index
      │
      └─► AQI = maximum of available pollutant sub-indices
```

- Use 24-hour averages, except **CO and O₃ use 8-hour averages**.
- Overall AQI requires at least **three pollutants**, one necessarily **PM₂.₅ or PM₁₀**.
- At least **16 hours of data** are needed to calculate a pollutant sub-index.
- Individual sub-indices may still be disseminated when data are insufficient for an overall AQI.
- Real-time AQI uses running averages from continuous stations.

### 8.2 Exact CPCB AQI breakpoints

| AQI category | AQI | PM₁₀ 24h | PM₂.₅ 24h | NO₂ 24h | O₃ 8h | CO 8h mg/m³ | SO₂ 24h | NH₃ 24h | Pb 24h |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Good | 0–50 | 0–50 | 0–30 | 0–40 | 0–50 | 0–1.0 | 0–40 | 0–200 | 0–0.5 |
| Satisfactory | 51–100 | 51–100 | 31–60 | 41–80 | 51–100 | 1.1–2.0 | 41–80 | 201–400 | 0.6–1.0 |
| Moderate | 101–200 | 101–250 | 61–90 | 81–180 | 101–168 | 2.1–10 | 81–380 | 401–800 | 1.1–2.0 |
| Poor | 201–300 | 251–350 | 91–120 | 181–280 | 169–208 | 10.1–17 | 381–800 | 801–1200 | 2.1–3.0 |
| Very Poor | 301–400 | 351–430 | 121–250 | 281–400 | 209–748* | 17.1–34 | 801–1600 | 1201–1800 | 3.1–3.5 |
| Severe | 401–500 | 430+ | 250+ | 400+ | 748+* | 34+ | 1600+ | 1800+ | 3.5+ |

\* CPCB's table marks the high-end ozone breakpoint as a one-hour monitoring value for mathematical calculation.

### 8.3 AQI cautions

1. **Worst sub-index, not arithmetic mean:** a single pollutant can determine the published AQI.
2. **Not NAAQS compliance:** the AQI categories and breakpoints are a communication scheme; statutory assessment uses the notified standard and its averaging/compliance rule.
3. **Not a personal dosimeter:** station placement, time-activity, microenvironments and indoor infiltration matter.
4. **Not all toxics included:** benzene, BaP, arsenic and nickel do not enter the eight-pollutant AQI.
5. **Real-time caveat:** CPCB states that analyser data feed directly without scrutiny and may therefore not be for statutory purpose; calibration, power, transmission and maintenance failures can interrupt data.

## 9. Monitoring, emission inventory and source apportionment

### 9.1 Monitoring systems

| System | What it measures | Time resolution/use | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NAMP/manual ambient monitoring** | Outdoor receptor concentration using periodic sampling | Trend and compliance evidence; the NAAQS annual note uses 104 24-hour observations | Sparse temporal coverage; not every pollutant at every station |
| **CAAQMS** | Continuous ambient concentrations plus meteorology | Near-real-time AQI, episodes and high-resolution trends | Siting, calibration, downtime and raw-data scrutiny matter |
| **OCEMS/CEMS** | Source emission at stack/process | Industrial compliance and continuous source evidence | It is not an ambient monitor |
| **Personal/indoor monitor** | Microenvironment or personal exposure proxy | Exposure studies | Calibration and representativeness |
| **Satellite/model/low-cost sensor** | Column/proxy/modelled or supplementary spatial evidence | Gap filling, forecasting and screening | Must be calibrated/validated; does not automatically replace reference monitoring |

📰 **Official network status reported on 6 August 2026:** **1,646** ambient stations — **593 continuous** and **1,053 manual** — covering **603 cities**, 28 States and 7 UTs. This is a dated network count, not proof of uniform spatial representativeness.

### 9.2 Emission inventory versus source apportionment

| Feature | Emission inventory | Source apportionment |
|---|---|---|
| Core question | How much does each source emit? | How much of measured ambient pollution is attributable to source categories? |
| Starting evidence | Activity data × emission factors; surveys; source CEMS | Ambient samples, chemical fingerprints, meteorology and models |
| Typical output | Tonnes/year or gridded mass by pollutant/source | Percentage or mass contribution to receptor concentration |
| Methods | Bottom-up/top-down inventory, GIS and temporal profiles | Receptor models, dispersion models, chemical mass balance |
| Key limitation | Missing informal/fugitive activity and uncertain factors | Model/profile uncertainty; shares vary by place, season and pollutant |

CPCB's framework treats the two as complementary: build a detailed, season-aware inventory; monitor chemical composition; model dispersion; use receptor analysis where feasible; validate; then prioritise measures.

> **UPSC trap:** an emissions share is not automatically the same as an ambient concentration share, exposure share or health-burden share.

## 10. Legal and institutional architecture

### 10.1 Legal ladder

```text
AIR ACT, 1981
  ├─ ambient air-quality planning and Board functions
  ├─ air-pollution-control areas, consent and emission enforcement
  └─ CPCB / SPCB architecture

ENVIRONMENT (PROTECTION) ACT, 1986
  ├─ Central Government's umbrella powers
  ├─ binding directions
  └─ Environment (Protection) Rules: industry-specific/general standards

CAQM ACT, 2021
  └─ NCR/adjoining-area regional coordination, directions and GRAP
```

### 10.2 Air Act, 1981

- ✅ Enacted to implement decisions of the **1972 Stockholm Conference**; Parliament used the **Article 253** route.
- ✅ The Central and State Boards constituted under the Water Act perform Air Act functions.
- ✅ **Section 16:** CPCB's functions include advising the Union Government, planning a nationwide programme, coordinating State Boards, technical assistance/research/training/information, and laying down standards for air quality.
- ✅ **Section 17:** SPCBs plan State programmes, advise State Governments, inspect plants/control equipment, collect information and lay down source-emission standards in consultation with CPCB.
- ✅ **Section 19:** State Government may declare an air-pollution-control area after consulting the State Board.
- ✅ **Section 21:** prior State Board consent is required to establish or operate an industrial plant in an air-pollution-control area, subject to the Act.
- ✅ **Section 22:** no person operating an industrial plant in such an area may emit beyond State Board standards.
- ✅ **Section 31A:** Board directions can include closure/regulation of an industry and stoppage/regulation of electricity, water or other service.
- ✅ In Union Territories, the Central Board performs State Board functions and may delegate them to a specified person/body; operational **PCCs** discharge delegated pollution-control functions.

### 10.3 Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

- **Section 3:** Central Government may take measures to protect and improve the environment.
- **Section 5:** power to issue binding directions, including closure/regulation and stoppage/regulation of services.
- **Section 6 and Environment (Protection) Rules:** standards and safeguards; Schedule I contains industry-specific standards and Schedule VI general standards where a sector-specific standard is absent.

### 10.4 Institutional roles

| Institution | Core air-pollution role |
|---|---|
| **MoEFCC** | National policy, EPA rules/standards and NCAP oversight |
| **CPCB** | National technical coordination; NAAQS/AQI/monitoring guidance; coordination and directions within statutory powers |
| **SPCB** | State consent, inspections, industrial emission standards/enforcement and ambient monitoring |
| **PCC** | Corresponding delegated functions in Union Territories |
| **Urban local body/road/construction agencies** | Waste, road dust, C&D, traffic and local implementation |
| **Transport, power, agriculture and industry ministries/agencies** | Sector-specific source controls |

### 10.5 CAQM Act, 2021

- ✅ Applies to the NCR and **adjoining areas** in Haryana, Punjab, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh where a pollution source adversely affects NCR air quality.
- ✅ Section 3 gives the Commission exclusive jurisdiction over matters covered by the Act; in a conflict, CAQM's order/direction prevails over the specified governments, CPCB, relevant SPCBs/PCC and other State statutory authorities.
- ✅ Section 12 empowers CAQM to take measures, issue directions, entertain complaints, coordinate governments and authorities, plan regional programmes, identify sources, regulate/prohibit pollution-causing activities and direct closure/regulation or service restriction.
- ✅ The design addresses an **airshed coordination failure** that a single city or State Board cannot solve alone.

⚠️ Strong formal authority is necessary but cannot by itself resolve data gaps, farmer incentives, municipal capacity or inter-state political economy.

## 11. NCAP, GRAP and the current policy position

### 11.1 NCAP: structural programme

- ✅ Launched by MoEFCC in **January 2019**.
- ✅ Covers **130 non-attainment and million-plus cities in 24 States/UTs** in the 6 August 2026 official reply.
- ✅ Original target: **20–30% reduction in PM₁₀ by 2024–25** over 2017–18.
- ✅ Revised target: **up to 40% reduction in PM₁₀ or attainment of NAAQS (60 µg/m³) by 2025–26**, against the 2017–18 baseline.
- ✅ Uses national, State and city clean-air action plans; PRANA is the programme-management/review portal.

📰 **Official FY 2025–26 status, answered 6 August 2026**

| Metric | Official result |
|---|---:|
| Cities assessed | 130 |
| Cities with lower annual PM₁₀ than 2017–18 | 108 |
| Cities with reduction above 20% | 72 |
| Cities with reduction above 40% | 26 |
| Cities meeting NAAQS in FY 2025–26 | 14 |
| Cities meeting NAAQS in the 2017–18 baseline | 6 |

**Correct interpretation:** the programme produced improvement in many cities, but only 26 exceeded 40% reduction and 14 met NAAQS. Do not write that “India achieved the 40% target” as a universal outcome.

### 11.2 GRAP: episodic emergency response

The extant schedule carries the CAQM revision date **21 November 2025**.

| Stage | Delhi AQI trigger/category | Character of action |
|---|---:|---|
| **I — Poor** | 201–300 | Enforce routine dust, waste, vehicle, fuel, industry and power-plant controls |
| **II — Very Poor** | 301–400 | Intensify sweeping/sprinkling/inspection; regulate DG sets; discourage private transport and tighten entry conditions |
| **III — Severe** | 401–450 | Restrict specified dust-generating C&D work and specified older vehicle/goods-vehicle operation, subject to listed exceptions |
| **IV — Severe+** | Above 450 | Stronger truck/HGV and C&D restrictions; governments may consider additional emergency measures |

- Stages are **cumulative**.
- CAQM may invoke a stage **in advance** using IMD/IITM forecasts when the projected level is likely to persist.
- A sudden threshold breach can trigger immediate action even if not forecast.
- The trigger is Delhi AQI, but the schedule operates across the NCR through designated agencies.

> **Core distinction:** **GRAP reduces acute episode exposure; NCAP seeks multi-year structural source reduction. Neither substitutes for the other.**

## 12. Source-specific control architecture

### 12.1 Vehicles and fuels

- **BS-VI fuel and vehicle norms nationwide from 1 April 2020**; fuel and engine standards reduce PM, NOx and sulfur-related emissions relative to older norms.
- Inspection/maintenance and stronger PUC systems; control gross emitters rather than treating vehicle age as the only variable.
- Vapour-recovery systems at petrol pumps reduce refuelling VOC emissions.
- Public transport, walking/cycling, traffic flow, parking policy, electrification and fleet renewal reduce activity and/or emission intensity.
- Scrappage/testing frameworks address high-emitting end-of-life vehicles; life-cycle electricity and battery impacts still require management.

### 12.2 Industry, power plants and diesel generators

- Air Act consent and source-emission standards; EPA sector-specific/general standards.
- OCEMS/CEMS for continuous source evidence; CPCB directed 17 highly polluting categories to install OCEMS.
- Fuel switching and efficiency; enclosure/hoods for fugitives.
- **PM:** electrostatic precipitators, bag filters and process control.
- **SO₂:** low-sulfur fuel and flue-gas desulfurisation.
- **NOx:** combustion modification, low-NOx burners and catalytic/non-catalytic reduction where applicable.
- Cleaner brick-kiln designs/fuels; compliant DG sets, retrofits and reliable grid supply.

### 12.3 Agriculture and biomass

- **In-situ residue management:** retain/incorporate straw through suitable machinery and agronomy.
- **Ex-situ use:** collection and supply chains for pellets/briquettes, bioenergy, compressed biogas, ethanol, boards, feed or other suitable uses.
- Crop diversification and adjusted agronomic incentives address the short harvest-to-sowing window.
- Bio-decomposer/composting options where agronomically suitable.
- Satellite/fire monitoring and enforcement are last-mile tools, not substitutes for affordable alternatives.
- NH₃ control also requires efficient fertilizer use, manure management and covered/storage/application practices.

### 12.4 Road, construction and waste dust

- Pave/repair shoulders and roads; mechanised vacuum sweeping and scientific disposal of collected dust.
- Cover construction material and vehicles; wheel washing, barriers, on-site storage and speed control.
- Water/dust suppressants and anti-smog guns can suppress local dust but do not replace prevention.
- Enforce C&D segregation, recovery/recycling and site accountability.
- Prevent open waste burning and landfill fires; remediate legacy waste.

📰 MoEFCC's 6 August 2026 reply records the **C&D Waste Management Rules, 2025** as in force from **1 April 2026**, alongside CPCB dust-mitigation directions.

### 12.5 Indoor and household measures

- Clean cooking/heating energy and reliable access.
- Source capture and kitchen ventilation.
- Smoke-free homes/workplaces.
- Appliance maintenance and safe ventilation/filtration.
- Building moisture control and low-emission materials.

## 13. How to evaluate an air-quality policy

### 13.1 The policy test

```text
Was the important source correctly identified?
        ↓
Was an enforceable instrument matched to that source?
        ↓
Was compliance measured at the source?
        ↓
Did comparable ambient concentration fall?
        ↓
Did exposure fall across groups and places?
        ↓
Did health/ecosystem outcomes improve?
```

### 13.2 Attribution cautions

- Meteorology can improve or worsen concentration without an equal emissions change.
- A city average can improve while a roadside/industrial neighbourhood remains highly exposed.
- One year's result can be affected by unusual rainfall, wind, economic activity or station coverage.
- PM₁₀ improvement does not automatically establish an equal PM₂.₅, ozone or health improvement.
- Source shares vary by season; crop-residue burning should not be assigned one fixed annual percentage.

## 14. UPSC traps and exact corrections

| Trap | Correction |
|---|---|
| NAAQS = AQI | NAAQS are notified ambient standards; AQI communicates current air quality through sub-indices |
| AQI is the average of eight pollutants | AQI is the **maximum** available pollutant sub-index |
| All 12 NAAQS pollutants enter AQI | AQI uses eight; benzene, BaP, As and Ni are excluded |
| Ozone is emitted directly by vehicles | Ground-level ozone is predominantly secondary |
| PM₂.₅ is one chemical | It is a size fraction with many compositions and sources |
| High emissions guarantee high local concentration | Dispersion, chemistry, transport and deposition intervene |
| Low ambient concentration guarantees low exposure | Indoor sources, commuting and time-activity alter exposure |
| Source apportionment = emission inventory | One attributes ambient concentration; the other estimates mass emitted |
| GRAP is India's long-term clean-air programme | GRAP is NCR episode response; NCAP is the structural national programme |
| CAQM covers every pollution problem in India | Its statutory jurisdiction is NCR and defined adjoining areas for NCR air-pollution matters |
| WHO AQGs are Indian law | They are health-based guidelines; NAAQS are India's notified standards |
| Cloud seeding eliminates pollution | It needs suitable clouds and may only aid temporary scavenging; source emissions remain |
| Output = outcome | Equipment/action-plan counts do not prove cleaner air or better health |

## 16. Chemistry-aware control: why precursor reduction is not always linear

- Secondary PM depends on the availability of SO₂, NOx, NH₃, oxidants, humidity and temperature. A reduction in one precursor may be limited if another remains abundant.
- Ozone control can be **NOx-sensitive** or **VOC-sensitive**. In a VOC-limited urban core, reducing NOx alone can sometimes reduce ozone titration and produce a non-linear local response; policy should therefore use local chemical evidence.
- Night-time NO₃/N₂O₅ chemistry and heterogeneous reactions can form nitrate aerosol even without sunlight.
- A source policy must therefore target the **pollutant actually measured**, its precursors and the relevant season.

## 17. CAQM as airshed governance

CAQM's value lies in matching jurisdiction to the NCR airshed: it can coordinate multiple States and agencies, issue binding directions and reconcile conflicting orders. Its limitation is implementation: agricultural economics, municipal capacity, data quality and enforcement effort remain dispersed.

**Qualified verdict:** CAQM repairs a coordination failure, not every source-level incentive failure.

## 18. Measurement uncertainty and environmental justice

- Station siting can under-represent roadside, industrial, peri-urban or low-income hotspots.
- Annual means hide short episodes; AQI hides pollutants outside its set.
- Population exposure needs microenvironment, commuting and indoor evidence.
- Vulnerability is unequal: children, older persons, outdoor workers, people with disease and households using polluting fuels may experience greater harm at the same ambient concentration.

## 19. Market and regulatory innovations from the canonical owner

- **Surat particulate-matter emissions-trading scheme:** the Economic Survey 2025–26 reports 317 plants backed by CEMS and cites an experimental evaluation finding 20–30% lower particulate emissions and 11–14% lower abatement costs than the counterfactual. Use this as a specific monitored case, not a universal claim for all markets.
- **Industry categorisation:** the 6 August 2026 official reply records CPCB's 12 February 2025 classification of 419 sectors/sub-sectors into Red 125, Orange 137, Green 61, White 87 and Blue 9.
- **Jan Vishwas regulatory shift:** specified Air Act offences moved toward monetary/adjudicatory penalties. A balanced answer weighs lower criminalisation/compliance friction against deterrence where penalties are small relative to avoided control cost.

## 20. Cloud seeding: precise exam treatment

Cloud seeding introduces suitable nuclei into **seedable clouds** to encourage precipitation. The routed 2025 UPSC question's official Set-A answer is **silver iodide and potassium iodide**.

⚠️ Precipitation can scavenge some particles and soluble gases, but:

- suitable cloud microphysics is necessary;
- the effect is episodic and spatially uncertain;
- it transfers deposited material to land/water;
- it does not remove the continuing source of emissions.

Therefore, it is at most a contingent episode intervention, not an alternative to emission control.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Science spine

- Primary = emitted; secondary = formed in air; PM and NO₂ may have both pathways.
- SO₂→sulfate/acid; NOx→ozone+nitrate/acid; NH₃ neutralises acids→ammonium PM; VOCs drive photochemical oxidation.
- London smog: cool/humid, coal/SO₂/PM, reducing. Photochemical smog: sunlight+NOx+VOCs, O₃/PAN, oxidising.
- Inversion suppresses mixing; transport redistributes; dry/wet deposition removes from air but transfers pollution.
- Emission ≠ concentration ≠ exposure ≠ dose ≠ outcome.

### Standards and AQI spine

- NAAQS notified 18 November 2009; 12 pollutants.
- Exact annual PM₁₀/PM₂.₅: 60/40 µg/m³; 24-hour: 100/60.
- AQI uses eight pollutants and the maximum sub-index.
- Overall AQI: minimum three pollutants including one PM; sub-index needs 16 hours.
- Real-time AQI is not automatically statutory evidence.
- WHO AQGs are health guidance, not Indian law.

### Evidence spine

- NAMP/manual = periodic ambient evidence; CAAQMS = continuous ambient; OCEMS/CEMS = source emissions.
- Inventory estimates emissions; apportionment attributes ambient concentration.
- Shares are pollutant-, place-, method- and season-specific.

### Governance spine

- Air Act: CPCB/SPCB planning, consent, source standards and enforcement.
- EPA: umbrella Central powers and industry standards.
- PCCs discharge delegated UT functions.
- CAQM: NCR/adjoining-area airshed authority; its directions prevail in statutory conflicts covered by the Act.
- NCAP = structural city programme; GRAP = cumulative, forecast-based NCR episode response.

### Current-status spine

- Revised NCAP target: up to 40% PM₁₀ reduction or NAAQS by 2025–26.
- FY 2025–26: 108/130 improved; 72 above 20%; 26 above 40%; 14 met NAAQS.
- GRAP revision dated 21 November 2025: I 201–300; II 301–400; III 401–450; IV above 450.
- BS-VI nationwide from 1 April 2020.

### Final examiner line

> Judge clean-air policy by comparable emission, concentration, exposure and health/ecosystem outcomes—not by expenditure, equipment, action-plan counts or one day's AQI alone.
