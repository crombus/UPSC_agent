### 1. The designation firewall

**Answer-grabbing line**

> Biosphere Reserve, World Network status, Ramsar Site, protected area and World Heritage inscription are cumulative labels with different purposes, boundaries and legal consequences; none should be used as a synonym for another.

| Label | Source | Main purpose | Automatic Indian legal effect? |
|---|---|---|---|
| National Biosphere Reserve | Government of India programme | Landscape-scale conservation, sustainable development and research support | No new Wild Life (Protection) Act category |
| UNESCO WNBR | MAB Programme and 1995 Statutory Framework | International network recognition and periodic review | No transfer of sovereignty or automatic land-right restriction |
| Ramsar Site | Convention on Wetlands | Wetland of International Importance and wise-use commitment | No new Indian land category; domestic laws remain necessary |
| National Park / Wildlife Sanctuary | Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 | Direct statutory habitat, rights and activity regulation | Yes, according to the notified category |
| World Heritage Site | 1972 World Heritage Convention | Protection of Outstanding Universal Value | International obligation, but no automatic conversion into a WPA category |

**Close-option rule:** first ask **who designated it, under which instrument, for what purpose and with what boundary**.

### 2. Biosphere Reserve: concept and three functions

UNESCO describes biosphere reserves as learning places for sustainable development. They combine three mutually reinforcing functions:

| Function | Meaning | Exam use |
|---|---|---|
| **Conservation** | Protect landscapes, ecosystems, species and genetic variation | The ecological core of the model |
| **Development** | Foster socio-culturally and ecologically sustainable economic and human development | Livelihoods are designed into the model, not treated only as an external threat |
| **Logistic support** | Support research, monitoring, education and training | Produces evidence for adaptive management |

```text
CONSERVATION
     ↘
      BIOSPHERE RESERVE
     ↗                ↖
DEVELOPMENT       LOGISTIC SUPPORT
livelihoods       research • monitoring • education
```

A Biosphere Reserve is therefore not simply a very large National Park. Its distinguishing design is **zoned coexistence across a working landscape**.

### 3. Core–buffer–transition zonation

```text
TRANSITION AREA
settlements • livelihoods • sustainable production • cooperation
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  BUFFER ZONE(S)                                          │
│  research • monitoring • education • compatible use      │
│  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐  │
│  │ CORE AREA(S)                                       │  │
│  │ long-term conservation • legally constituted      │  │
│  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The diagram is conceptual. Real reserves may contain multiple cores and need not form perfect concentric rings.

| Zone | UNESCO function | Indian legal reality | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Long-term conservation of landscapes, ecosystems, species and genetic variation | Must have effective long-term protection; in India it commonly overlaps a National Park, Sanctuary or forest-law area | “Core status itself is a separate WPA category” — false |
| **Buffer** | Activities compatible with ecological objectives; research, monitoring, education and carefully managed use | Restrictions arise from underlying law, plan, tenure and administrative orders | “Buffer means unrestricted tourism/resource extraction” — false |
| **Transition** | Sustainable resource management and human development through cooperation | Settlements and livelihoods can continue; governance is negotiated and multi-actor | “Transition is an uninhabited exclusion zone” — false |

**Key distinction:** the zones describe functions and management intensity; they do not automatically impose one uniform Indian legal regime.

### 4. National Biosphere Reserve versus UNESCO World Network status

#### Two-step route

```text
INDIAN LANDSCAPE IDENTIFIED
        ↓
Government of India designates a national Biosphere Reserve
        ↓
India chooses whether to nominate it to UNESCO
        ↓
MAB Secretariat completeness check
        ↓
Advisory Committee review
        ↓
MAB International Coordinating Council decision
        ↓
WNBR inclusion + periodic review every ten years
```

- National governments nominate sites; UNESCO does not unilaterally select Indian territory.
- WNBR sites remain under the sovereign jurisdiction of the State concerned.
- Article 4 of the Statutory Framework requires appropriate zonation, participation arrangements, a management policy/plan and a designated authority or mechanism.
- Article 9 provides a **ten-year periodic review**. WNBR recognition is therefore not a once-for-all medal.

#### Dated Indian status

As recorded by PIB on **3 November 2025**, India had **18 national Biosphere Reserves** and **13 WNBR sites** after Cold Desert joined in 2025.

| WNBR members — 13 | Nationally designated but not in WNBR — 5 |
|---|---|
| Nilgiri; Gulf of Mannar; Sundarban; Nanda Devi; Nokrek; Pachmarhi; Similipal; Achanakmar–Amarkantak; Great Nicobar; Agasthyamala; Khangchendzonga; Panna; Cold Desert | Manas; Dibru–Saikhowa; Dihang–Dibang; Great Rann of Kutch; Seshachalam Hills |

- **Nilgiri** was India’s first national Biosphere Reserve (**1986**) and the first Indian site to enter WNBR (**2000**).
- **Gulf of Mannar** was India’s first marine Biosphere Reserve.

**PYQ anchor:** UNESCO’s Agasthyamala profile identifies Neyyar, Peppara and Shendurney Wildlife Sanctuaries and Kalakad Mundanthurai Tiger Reserve within the reserve—the basis of 2019 Prelims Q27.

### 5. Biosphere governance, legal status and community rights

#### Governance chain

```text
MoEFCC / Indian MAB mechanism
        ↓
State departments + reserve-level coordination
        ↓
underlying PA / forest / revenue / local-government authorities
        ↓
research institutions + communities + resource users
        ↓
zonal plan, livelihood arrangements, monitoring and review
```

1. **No stand-alone PA law:** the Biosphere Reserve label is not one of the protected-area categories created by the Wild Life (Protection) Act.
2. **Core-law overlap:** a core may already be a National Park or Sanctuary; the underlying notification supplies enforceable rights and activity rules.
3. **Buffer/transition diversity:** different parcels may be forest, revenue, community or private land. Their legal consequences follow the applicable law and tenure.
4. **Participation is structural:** UNESCO’s framework requires arrangements for public authorities, local communities and private interests to help design and carry out the reserve’s functions.
5. **No automatic extinguishment:** national or WNBR designation does not by itself cancel community or private rights. Any restriction, recognition, acquisition or relocation must come from applicable Indian law and due process.
6. **No automatic permission:** existing rights do not make every use ecologically compatible. Plans must reconcile livelihoods with conservation objectives.

**Application:** Great Rann of Kutch shows why a transition landscape must plan for pastoral and salt-production livelihoods without weakening core ecological objectives.

**Balanced verdict:** zonation can reduce the false choice between fortress conservation and unrestricted use, but only if transition-zone livelihoods, benefit sharing and conflict resolution are funded and monitored.

### 6. Ramsar Convention: scope, three pillars and wise use

The Convention on Wetlands was adopted at Ramsar, Iran, in **1971**. India became a Contracting Party on **1 February 1982**.

MoEFCC is India’s national administrative authority. The Convention has its own Secretariat, co-located with and administratively hosted by IUCN in Gland, Switzerland; it is not a UNEP convention. **World Wetlands Day is observed on 2 February.**

#### Broad wetland scope

Article 1.1 covers marsh, fen, peatland or water, whether natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt; it also includes marine water up to **six metres deep at low tide**.

That definition is wider than “lake” or “bird sanctuary”. It can include:

| Ramsar group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Marine/coastal | Estuaries, tidal flats, mangroves, seagrass beds, coral reefs and shallow marine waters |
| Inland | Rivers, floodplains, lakes, marshes, swamps, peatlands, springs and seasonal pools |
| Human-made | Reservoirs, aquaculture ponds, irrigated land, salt pans, wastewater-treatment areas and canals |

The standard Ramsar classification uses **42 wetland types**: 12 marine/coastal, 20 inland and 10 human-made. This treaty classification must not be confused with the narrower notification exclusions in India’s Wetlands Rules.

#### Three pillars

```text
1. WISE USE OF ALL WETLANDS
        +
2. DESIGNATE AND EFFECTIVELY MANAGE RAMSAR SITES
        +
3. INTERNATIONAL COOPERATION
   transboundary wetlands • shared water systems • shared species
```

#### Wise use

Ramsar defines wise use as maintaining ecological character through ecosystem approaches within sustainable development.

- It is **not** universal no-use preservation.
- It is **not** unrestricted commercial use.
- It applies to the wider wetland estate, not only listed Ramsar Sites.
- A use is “wise” only when hydrology, biodiversity, processes and services remain within acceptable ecological limits.

### 7. Ramsar criteria and wetland types

A candidate needs to meet **at least one** of nine criteria.

| Criterion | What it tests |
|---:|---|
| **1** | Representative, rare or unique natural/near-natural wetland type in a biogeographic region |
| **2** | Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered species, or threatened ecological communities |
| **3** | Species populations important for maintaining regional biological diversity |
| **4** | Support at a critical life-cycle stage or refuge during adverse conditions |
| **5** | Regular support for **20,000 or more waterbirds** |
| **6** | Regular support for **1%** of a waterbird species/subspecies population |
| **7** | Significant indigenous fish diversity, life-history stages or interactions representative of wetland values |
| **8** | Fish food source, spawning ground, nursery or migration path on which fish stocks depend |
| **9** | Regular support for **1%** of a wetland-dependent non-avian animal population |

**Close-option controls**

- Criterion 5 is an absolute waterbird number; criterion 6 is a population percentage.
- Criterion 8 concerns fish-stock dependence and life-cycle routes, not merely “fish are present”.
- Criterion 9 extends the 1% logic beyond birds.
- Designation does not require all nine criteria.

### 8. Ramsar designation, boundary, management and legal effect

#### Designation chain

```text
National Administrative Authority identifies candidate
        ↓
Ramsar Information Sheet (RIS)
criteria • ecological character • land use • threats • management
        +
precise mapped boundary
        ↓
submission to Ramsar Secretariat
        ↓
entry on List of Wetlands of International Importance
        ↓
management + monitoring + updated RIS + change reporting
```

#### Boundary rule

Article 2.1 requires each listed wetland’s boundary to be precisely described and mapped. The boundary may include adjacent riparian or coastal zones, islands and even deeper marine water lying within the wetland system where relevant.

This creates four exam distinctions:

1. **Water spread ≠ site boundary:** a Ramsar boundary can include ecologically connected land and water.
2. **Ramsar boundary ≠ PA boundary:** overlap may be partial.
3. **Designation boundary ≠ zone of influence:** domestic management may need a larger catchment-facing control area.
4. **Mapped boundary ≠ management success:** mapping is a precondition, not an outcome.

#### Legal effect

- The Convention is binding on India as a Contracting Party.
- Ramsar listing creates international commitments to conserve the listed site, maintain ecological character, report adverse change and pursue wise use.
- It does **not** automatically create a National Park, acquire private land, extinguish fishing rights or impose one universal activity ban.
- Enforceable site-level controls arise through Indian laws, notifications and management plans.

Article 2.5 permits deletion or boundary restriction only where a Party invokes **urgent national interests**; Article 4.2 calls for compensation, as far as possible, for resulting wetland-resource loss. This is an exceptional route, not ordinary rezoning.

### 9. India’s domestic wetland layer: Rules, overlap and rights

The Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 were made under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. MoEFCC’s January 2020 implementation guidelines translate the Rules into delineation, notification and management steps.

At the Union level, the **National Wetlands Committee** advises on implementation and recommends Indian wetlands for Ramsar nomination. State/UT Wetland Authorities perform the principal identification, planning, regulatory-coordination and monitoring functions, while the competent government issues the legal notification.

#### Scope and overlap

| Situation | Domestic rule |
|---|---|
| Ramsar Site | Falls within the Rules’ scope; the entire site can be delineated even where parts overlap another legal regime |
| WPA protected area / forest-law / CRZ overlap | The overlapping parcel continues under its specific statute/notification; the Wetlands Rules can govern uncovered parts and integrate the whole-site plan |
| Natural wetland partly used for irrigation, aquaculture or another human purpose | Does not become automatically exempt merely because it is partly used that way |
| Specifically constructed waterbody in an excluded class | May fall outside notification under these Rules, although another law can protect it |

#### Boundary and zone of influence

- Wetland boundary is derived principally from hydrology, inundation, hydrophytic vegetation and soil saturation.
- The **zone of influence** is the catchment-linked area where development can adversely alter wetland structure or services.
- A site notification should distinguish the wetland boundary, zone of influence, prohibited activities, regulated activities and permitted/promoted activities.

#### Prohibited and regulated uses

The Rules prohibit, among other things, conversion for non-wetland use/encroachment, new or expanded industry, specified waste handling/disposal, solid-waste dumping, discharge of untreated waste and effluent, permanent construction within the specified flood-level belt except the stated jetty exception, and poaching.

Activities such as subsistence biomass harvest, traditional practices, sustainable culture fisheries, non-motorised boating, justified desilting and temporary construction may be **regulated site by site**; they are not blanket permissions.

#### Community and tenure safeguards

- The guidelines require an account of **pre-existing rights and privileges**.
- State/UT Wetland Authorities are to consider continuation and support of traditional uses that are harmonised with ecological character.
- Private tenancy or community use is not erased by the Ramsar label.
- Equally, “traditional” cannot be used as an automatic exemption from pollution, habitat conversion or an ecologically justified site regulation.

**Governance test:** rights recognition, ecological limits and livelihood alternatives must be decided together, not in separate administrative silos.

### 10. Montreux Record, Ramsar Advisory Mission and CEPA

```text
ECOLOGICAL CHARACTER
baseline components • processes • benefits/services
        ↓
actual / ongoing / likely human-caused adverse change
        ↓
Article 3.2 reporting
        ↓
MONTREUX RECORD
priority register within the Ramsar List
        ↓
RAMSAR ADVISORY MISSION
technical advice requested by the Party
        ↓
restoration + monitoring + possible removal from Record
        ↕
CEPA
communication • capacity building • education • participation • awareness
```

| Instrument | Function | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Montreux Record** | Flags listed sites where ecological character has changed, is changing or is likely to change because of human interference | Not delisting, punishment or an honour roll |
| **Ramsar Advisory Mission (RAM)** | Voluntary expert advice, undertaken at the Contracting Party’s request, to diagnose and address site problems | Not an international enforcement raid |
| **CEPA** | Under Resolution XV.6 (2025): Communication, Capacity Building, Education, Participation and Awareness | Not merely a publicity campaign |

**Indian status checked for this repair**

- **Keoladeo National Park** — on the Montreux Record.
- **Loktak Lake** — on the Montreux Record.
- **Chilika Lake** — added in 1993 and removed on **11 November 2002** after restoration and a 2001 RAM review.

Chilika demonstrates that the Record can support a corrective cycle. It does not prove that every restoration measure or every listed site has succeeded.

### 12. Ramsar versus Biosphere Reserve versus PA versus World Heritage

| Dimension | Biosphere Reserve / WNBR | Ramsar Site | WPA protected area | World Heritage Site |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary object | People–nature landscape and sustainable-development learning | Wetland ecological character and wise use | Wildlife/habitat protection under Indian statute | Outstanding Universal Value |
| Spatial design | Core–buffer–transition | Precisely mapped wetland boundary; catchment/adjacent zones may be included | Statutory notified boundary and category-specific regime | Inscribed property plus buffer arrangements where applicable |
| Designating actor | India nationally; UNESCO MAB-ICC for WNBR | India nominates/designates through Convention process | Competent Indian government under WPA | World Heritage Committee after State nomination |
| Domestic legal force | Depends on underlying law and plan | Depends on Wetlands Rules and overlapping Indian law | Direct statutory force | International obligation; domestic law supplies land-use controls |
| Human use | Designed into transition and compatible buffer activity | Wise use; compatible use can continue | Varies by NP/WLS/other category and rights process | Depends on property, management plan and domestic law |
| Review/accountability | Ten-year periodic review | RIS updates, Article 3.2 reporting, Montreux/RAM | Statutory administration and judicial/enforcement review | Periodic reporting and state-of-conservation review |

**One-line conclusion:** BR asks how a landscape can conserve and develop; Ramsar asks how a wetland can retain ecological character; PA law asks what is legally restricted; World Heritage asks what has Outstanding Universal Value.

### 13. Ecosystem services, threats and restoration

#### Service map

| Wetland/landscape process | Service | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Floodplain and marsh storage | Moderates peaks and stores water | Effect depends on connectivity, capacity and prior saturation |
| Sediment and nutrient retention | Improves water quality | Excess loading can overwhelm the system and cause eutrophication |
| Soil–water interaction | Can support recharge | Not every wetland is a net recharge zone in every season |
| Peat, mangrove and wetland soils | Carbon storage | Drainage or fire can reverse the sink |
| Habitat and life-cycle support | Fisheries, waterbirds, pollinators and biodiversity | Requires hydrological timing and connectivity, not water presence alone |
| Biomass, fish, reeds and tourism | Livelihood and cultural value | Benefits and restrictions must be distributed fairly |
| Research and monitoring in BRs | Knowledge and adaptation learning | Data matter only if management responds |

#### Threat chain

```text
CATCHMENT CHANGE / ENCROACHMENT / POLLUTION / FLOW REGULATION
        ↓
hydroperiod, water level, sediment and nutrient balance altered
        ↓
vegetation shift • eutrophication • invasive spread • habitat fragmentation
        ↓
fish/bird breeding and migration disrupted
        ↓
flood, water-quality, livelihood and cultural services decline
```

Other pressures include unsustainable extraction, destructive fishing, roads and tourism infrastructure, peat drainage/fire, coastal salinisation and climate-driven extremes.

#### Restoration sequence

```text
1. DEFINE ecological character and reference condition
2. MAP boundary, hydrology, catchment and rights
3. STOP the active pressure: encroachment, untreated inflow, flow blockage
4. RESTORE hydrological regime and connectivity
5. CONTROL pollutants and invasive species
6. RECOVER habitat with site-appropriate native processes
7. ALIGN livelihoods, incentives and community institutions
8. MONITOR components + processes + services
9. ADAPT the plan against measured outcomes
```

**Restoration trap:** beautification, dredging or plantation is not automatically ecological restoration. The intervention must address the causal pressure and recover function without shifting damage elsewhere.

### 14. Community rights and designation versus delivery

#### Rights-and-conservation test

1. What tenure, customary use and livelihood dependence existed before designation?
2. Which instrument can lawfully regulate that use?
3. Is the activity compatible with ecological character, or can it be modified?
4. Were affected communities represented in planning and monitoring?
5. Are costs, restrictions and benefits distributed fairly?
6. Are alternative livelihoods or compensation delivered before burdens are imposed?
7. Is ecological and social performance measured after intervention?

#### Delivery chain

```text
DESIGNATION
international visibility and commitment
        ↓
LEGAL TRANSLATION
notification • boundary • authority • rights record
        ↓
PLAN
objectives • threats • zonation • permitted/regulated uses
        ↓
CAPACITY
finance • staff • hydrological data • enforcement • CEPA
        ↓
IMPLEMENTATION
pollution control • flow restoration • invasive control • livelihood action
        ↓
OUTCOME
ecological character maintained/restored + equitable, durable use
```

**Designation is necessary but not sufficient.** A rising site count is evidence of recognition; it is not evidence that sewage stopped, hydrology recovered, invasives declined or livelihoods improved.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. WNBR is a structured soft-governance network

The 1995 Statutory Framework calls the network’s arrangements a common framework while preserving national sovereignty. Its strength lies in standard-setting, peer learning, periodic review and reputational accountability. Its limitation is that enforcement still depends on national law, institutions and resources.

### B. Zonation is functional, not merely geometric

The strongest answer does not draw three circles and stop. It asks whether:

- the core is legally secure and ecologically representative;
- buffer activities reinforce rather than erode core objectives;
- the transition area has credible livelihood incentives;
- corridors and hydrology connect zones; and
- research findings alter management decisions.

### C. Ecological character is a baseline-and-change concept

Ecological character integrates **components** (water, soil, species), **processes** (flows, nutrient cycling, succession) and **benefits/services**. A species list alone is insufficient. Monitoring must retain a baseline, thresholds, spatial scale and time series.

### D. Ramsar boundary change is exceptional

Article 2.5’s urgent-national-interest route and Article 4.2 compensation duty show that a mapped Ramsar boundary is not casually adjustable. The analytical sequence is: public interest claim → alternatives → impact assessment → minimum loss → compensation → reporting and monitoring.

### E. NPCA and programme-time discipline

The 2023 Mains question uses the historical **National Wetland Conservation Programme** label. NWCP and the National Lake Conservation Plan were merged into **NPCA in 2013**. A current answer should preserve the PYQ’s wording, explain the institutional transition and assess the present framework instead of pretending the old programme still operates unchanged.

### F. Amrit Dharohar as a delivery frame

The Union Budget 2023–24 announced **Amrit Dharohar** for Ramsar-site conservation values, biodiversity, carbon stock, eco-tourism and local income. It is useful only as a policy layer; an answer must still show the site plan, authority, finance, participation and measured outcome.

The same Budget separately announced **MISHTI** for mangrove plantation along coastlines and salt-pan lands. Do not use MISHTI as the name of the Ramsar-site programme or treat every mangrove project as a Ramsar intervention.

### G. Data traps

- Never combine the stale 99-site MoEFCC landing-page figure with the later 100th/101st announcements.
- Never equate 18 national Biosphere Reserves with 13 WNBR sites.
- Never infer ecological health from a Ramsar/WNBR count.
- Never treat Montreux inclusion as loss of Ramsar status.
- Never treat Chilika’s removal as proof that every threat has permanently ended.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Status distinctions

- **National BR:** Indian landscape designation; not automatically WNBR or a WPA category.
- **WNBR:** UNESCO MAB-ICC recognition after national nomination; sovereign jurisdiction remains national.
- **Ramsar:** Wetland of International Importance; international obligation, not automatic Indian land acquisition.
- **PA:** statutory Indian category under WPA.
- **World Heritage:** Outstanding Universal Value under the 1972 Convention; domestic law still regulates land and rights.

### Biosphere Reserve recall

- Functions: **conservation + development + logistic support**.
- Zones: **core** long-term protection; **buffer** compatible activity/research; **transition** sustainable livelihoods/cooperation.
- Real geometry may be multiple-core and non-concentric.
- WNBR nomination: India → UNESCO review → MAB-ICC decision.
- Review: every **ten years** under Article 9.
- Dated India status: **18 national / 13 WNBR**, PIB **3 November 2025**.
- Latest WNBR addition: **Cold Desert, 2025**.
- **Nilgiri:** first national BR (1986) and first Indian WNBR entry (2000).
- **Gulf of Mannar:** first marine Biosphere Reserve in India.
- National-only five: Manas, Dibru–Saikhowa, Dihang–Dibang, Great Rann of Kutch, Seshachalam Hills.
- Agasthyamala PYQ set: Neyyar + Peppara + Shendurney + Kalakad Mundanthurai.

### Ramsar recall

- Convention: **1971**; India party: **1 February 1982**.
- World Wetlands Day: **2 February**.
- Governance: MoEFCC national authority; Ramsar Secretariat hosted at IUCN, Gland.
- Wetland definition is broad; shallow marine water up to **6 m at low tide** is included.
- Three pillars: wise use of all wetlands; designate/manage Ramsar Sites; international cooperation.
- Wise use: maintain ecological character through ecosystem approaches within sustainable development.
- Classification: **42 types** — 12 marine/coastal, 20 inland, 10 human-made.
- Any **one** of nine criteria can qualify a site.
- Waterbird thresholds: **20,000** under criterion 5; **1%** under criterion 6.
- Criterion 9: 1% of a wetland-dependent **non-avian** animal population.

### Boundary and legal effect

- RIS + precise map + ecological-character description.
- Boundary may include adjacent riparian/coastal zones, islands and relevant deeper water.
- Ramsar boundary, PA boundary, water spread and domestic zone of influence are not synonyms.
- Ramsar listing binds India internationally but does not itself create an NP/WLS, extinguish rights or impose one universal ban.
- Urgent national interest is the exceptional deletion/restriction route; compensation is expected as far as possible.

### Indian wetland governance

- Wetlands Rules 2017 operate under the Environment (Protection) Act.
- National Wetlands Committee advises the Union and recommends Ramsar nominations.
- State/UT Wetland Authorities identify sites, recommend notification, frame site-specific regulation, review plans and monitor enforcement.
- Overlapping PA/forest/CRZ parcels retain their own legal regime.
- Zone of influence captures catchment development capable of harming structure/services.
- Prohibitions include conversion/encroachment, industry, specified waste, untreated effluent, restricted permanent construction and poaching.
- Traditional uses may continue only where site-specifically regulated and compatible with ecological character.
- Record pre-existing rights and privileges; designation does not erase tenure.

### Accountability

- **Montreux:** adverse ecological-character watch list within the Ramsar List.
- **RAM:** voluntary technical advice requested by the Party.
- **CEPA:** Communication, Capacity Building, Education, Participation and Awareness under Resolution XV.6 (2025).
- Current Indian Montreux entries checked: **Keoladeo** and **Loktak**.
- **Chilika:** removed **11 November 2002** after restoration review.

### Dated Ramsar count

- 100th: Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary/Surha Tal, Uttar Pradesh — **5 June 2026**.
- 101st: Glaw Lake, Arunachal Pradesh — **3 August 2026**.
- Latest official addition located by **28 September 2026**: **101 sites**.

### Designation-to-delivery answer

```text
label → boundary → law → rights → plan → capacity → participation
     → pressure removal → hydrological/habitat recovery → monitoring
```

### Final traps

- BR ≠ WNBR; Ramsar ≠ PA; World Heritage ≠ domestic land category.
- Buffer ≠ transition; wise use ≠ no use; traditional use ≠ unlimited use.
- Montreux ≠ delisting; RAM ≠ enforcement; CEPA ≠ publicity alone.
- Site count ≠ management effectiveness; beautification ≠ restoration.
- A strong conclusion measures ecological character and distributive justice, not designation totals alone.
