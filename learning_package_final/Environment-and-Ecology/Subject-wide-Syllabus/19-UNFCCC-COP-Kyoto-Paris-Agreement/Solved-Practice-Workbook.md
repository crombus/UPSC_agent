## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1 — Convention objective

Which formulation most accurately states the ultimate objective of the UNFCCC?

A. Stabilising GHG concentrations to prevent dangerous interference while protecting adaptation, food security and sustainable development
B. Returning every Party to 1990 emissions through one uniform, legally binding timetable and common target
C. Requiring every Party to reach economy-wide net zero by 2050 through internationally allocated emissions quotas
D. Eliminating climate gases and ozone-depleting substances through one consolidated atmospheric-protection convention

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Article 2 uses the stabilisation formulation and expressly includes ecosystems, food production and sustainable economic development.
**Option analysis:** A reproduces the Convention’s architecture. B invents a uniform target. C is not an UNFCCC treaty obligation. D confuses climate gases with the Montreal Protocol’s controlled substances.

### MCQ 4 — Legal effect of decisions

Which statement best describes the legal character of COP, CMP and CMA decisions?

A. Every consensus decision acquires the same legal force as a ratified article of the parent treaty.
B. Governing-body decisions remain political recommendations without operational consequences under the parent treaty.
C. An ordinary CMA decision may revise Paris obligations while bypassing formal amendment and acceptance procedures.
D. Effect depends on the parent mandate and wording; a decision is not automatically a treaty amendment.

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** Treaty bodies can adopt authoritative and sometimes operationally binding rules where the treaty delegates elaboration, but a decision is not automatically a formal amendment.
**Option analysis:** A erases source hierarchy. B understates delegated rules. C bypasses consent procedures. D preserves both authority and limit.

### MCQ 7 — Treaty status

### Mains PYQ 3 — 2022 GS-III, Q17

**Official paper provenance:** `QP-CSM-22-GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III-190922.pdf`.

**Exact official wording:**
> Discuss global warming and mention its effects on the global climate. Explain the control measures to bring down the level of greenhouse gases which cause global warming, in the light of the Kyoto Protocol, 1997. (Answer in 250 words)

**Ownership:** Kyoto measures here; physical science cross-owned by Topic 17.

**Model answer (about 245 words):**

Global warming is the long-term rise in average surface temperature caused mainly by enhanced radiative forcing from anthropogenic greenhouse gases such as CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O.

**Climate effects:** warming intensifies heat extremes, shifts precipitation, increases heavy-rainfall risk, accelerates glacier and ice-sheet loss, raises sea level, warms and acidifies oceans, and alters ecosystems and agricultural risk. These effects vary regionally; no single event should be attributed to warming without event-specific evidence.

**Kyoto control architecture**

1. **Quantified commitments:** Article 3 and Annex B assigned binding emission limitation or reduction commitments to covered developed-country Parties for 2008–2012, collectively at least 5% below 1990 levels.
2. **Domestic policies:** Article 2 encouraged energy efficiency, renewable energy, sink enhancement, sustainable agriculture, transport reform and removal of market distortions.
3. **Monitoring and compliance:** national inventories, registries, expert review and assigned-amount accounting converted targets into measurable obligations.
4. **Joint Implementation:** Annex I Parties could earn ERUs through projects in another Annex I Party.
5. **Clean Development Mechanism:** projects in non-Annex I hosts generated CERs, aiding host sustainable development and Annex I compliance.
6. **Emissions trading:** eligible Annex B Parties could transfer units, supplemental to domestic action.

Kyoto’s limitation was coverage: the United States never ratified, Canada withdrew, and developing countries had no quantified caps. Project additionality and surplus units also affected integrity.

**Conclusion:** Kyoto pioneered binding carbon accounting and market mechanisms, but Paris later sought broader participation through universal NDCs.

**Why this earns marks:** it balances science, treaty measures and limitations while retaining the question’s Kyoto focus.

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain how the UNFCCC combines a common objective with differentiated commitments. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (about 145 words):**

The UNFCCC combines environmental unity with distributive equity. Article 2 gives all Parties the common objective of stabilising greenhouse-gas concentrations so as to prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference while protecting ecosystems, food production and sustainable development.

Differentiation enters through Articles 3 and 4. Article 3.1 requires action on equity and **common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities**, with developed countries taking the lead. Article 4.1 imposes general commitments on all Parties: inventories, programmes, sinks, adaptation, research and reporting. Annex I Parties carry an enhanced mitigation lead; Annex II, a subset of Annex I, additionally provides finance and facilitates technology transfer. Non-Annex I Parties, including India, retain general commitments, but Article 4.7 links their implementation to effective support and development priorities.

Thus CBDR-RC is not exemption from action. It is a calibrated allocation of leadership, support and effort within one common climate objective.

**Why this earns marks:** it links objective, principle, annexes and commitments in one argument.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish adoption, signature, ratification, entry into force, withdrawal and re-entry in climate treaties. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (about 150 words):**

These verbs mark different legal stages. **Adoption** finalises the negotiated text. **Signature** normally records preliminary endorsement and intent to consider becoming bound; it is not usually full consent. **Ratification, acceptance or approval** is the post-signature international act expressing consent after domestic procedures. **Accession** has the same legal effect without prior signature. **Entry into force** activates obligations once the treaty’s threshold or Party-specific waiting period is met.

**Withdrawal** ends Party status only after the treaty’s conditions are satisfied. Paris Article 28 permits notice after three years from entry for that Party and makes withdrawal effective one year after receipt. The second U.S. withdrawal therefore became effective on 27 January 2026, not on the 2025 notification date.

**Re-entry** is not automatic. A former Party must deposit a fresh instrument; under Paris Article 21.3, the Agreement enters into force 30 days later.

**Why this earns marks:** it defines every requested stage and uses a current official example.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks

**Question:** Compare the Kyoto Protocol’s quantified-commitment architecture with the Paris Agreement’s NDC architecture. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (about 240 words):**

Kyoto and Paris operationalise the same UNFCCC objective through different bargains between legal precision, equity and participation.

**Coverage:** Kyoto imposed quantified commitments on participating developed-country Parties listed through Annex B; non-Annex I Parties had no comparable caps. Paris requires every Party to prepare, communicate and maintain successive NDCs.

**Target formation:** Kyoto targets were internationally negotiated percentages translated into assigned amounts and AAUs for fixed commitment periods. Paris leaves substantive target design nationally determined.

**Bindingness:** Kyoto made assigned amounts and accounting compliance legally determinate. Paris binds procedure—NDC preparation, maintenance, communication, domestic pursuit and reporting—while achievement and numerical ambition remain nationally set.

**Implementation:** Kyoto used inventories, registries, expert review, compliance and CDM/JI/emissions trading. Paris combines Article 13 transparency, Article 14 Global Stocktake and Article 6 cooperation.

**Equity:** Kyoto expressed CBDR-RC through a sharper developed/developing divide. Paris retains CBDR-RC “in the light of different national circumstances” within universal participation.

**Performance limits:** Kyoto’s precision was weakened by U.S. non-ratification, Canada’s withdrawal, limited second-period participation and market-integrity concerns. Paris gained reach and durability but aggregate ambition depends on a reputational and political ratchet rather than imposed national quotas.

**Verdict:** Kyoto was binding but narrow; Paris is universal but nationally determined. Effective governance needs Kyoto-grade accounting integrity within Paris-scale participation, backed by finance and technology.

**Why this earns marks:** every comparison uses a common axis and ends with a graded verdict.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Explain how NDCs, transparency and the Global Stocktake form the Paris ambition cycle. Also locate adaptation and loss and damage within this cycle. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (about 245 words):**

Paris replaces a single negotiated emissions allocation with an iterative cycle linking national choice to international scrutiny.

Under **Article 4**, each Party prepares, communicates and maintains successive NDCs, pursues domestic measures and submits a contribution every five years. Each new NDC must represent progression and highest possible ambition, reflecting CBDR-RC and national circumstances.

Under **Article 13**, Parties report inventories and information tracking NDC implementation. Technical expert review and facilitative multilateral consideration improve transparency. Flexibility is available to developing Parties that need it in light of capacities. Reporting, however, is not proof of target achievement.

Under **Article 14**, the Global Stocktake assesses collective—not individual—progress every five years across mitigation, adaptation and means of implementation, in light of equity and best available science. Its outcome informs later nationally determined action and support. The first GST at CMA 5 produced the UAE Consensus, including transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems and 2030 renewable/efficiency calls.

**Adaptation** enters through Article 7’s global goal and adaptation communications; the GST reviews adequacy and effectiveness. **Loss and damage** under Article 8 covers residual harms through the Warsaw International Mechanism and Fund architecture; it remains distinct from adaptation, and decision 1/CP.21 excludes liability/compensation as a basis.

**Verdict:** the cycle can expose ambition and implementation gaps, but stronger NDCs still depend on domestic politics, finance, technology and capacity.

**Why this earns marks:** it explains the mechanism, not merely the three institutions.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks

**Question:** Critically evaluate Paris Agreement Article 6 as an instrument for cooperation, environmental integrity and equity. Answer in 300 words.

**Model answer (about 290 words):**

Article 6 allows voluntary cooperation to raise ambition and reduce mitigation cost, but its legitimacy depends on accounting and social integrity.

**Three pathways**

1. **Article 6.2** enables Party-led cooperative approaches using ITMOs. Authorisation, reporting, registry tracking and corresponding adjustments seek to prevent both the transferring and using Party from claiming the same outcome.
2. **Article 6.4** creates a CMA-supervised crediting mechanism. A Supervisory Body oversees methodologies and activities. Five per cent of issued A6.4ERs is forwarded to the Adaptation Fund, while at least two per cent is cancelled for overall mitigation in global emissions.
3. **Article 6.8** supports non-market cooperation involving mitigation, adaptation, finance, technology and capacity-building without tradable outcomes.

**Potential gains:** cooperation can locate lower-cost mitigation, mobilise investment, transfer technology and allow developing hosts to align projects with sustainable-development priorities. The 6.4 adaptation levy and OMGE cancellation add equity and net-mitigation elements absent from simple offsetting.

**Risks:** a corresponding adjustment prevents double counting but does not prove additionality, conservative baselines, permanence or absence of leakage. Weak authorisation, opaque registries, reversal risk and poor safeguards can create paper reductions or harm communities. Market demand may also privilege cheap credits over structural domestic decarbonisation.

**Equity:** Article 6 must remain voluntary and supplemental, not a means for wealthy buyers to defer deep domestic cuts. Host countries must avoid exporting mitigation needed for their own NDCs without fair value, technology and benefit sharing. Article 6.8 is therefore important where cooperation should not be commodified.

**Verdict:** Article 6 can improve efficiency only when robust accounting is joined by additionality, rights safeguards and transparent host-country strategy. Otherwise, it reallocates claims rather than reducing atmospheric emissions.

**Why this earns marks:** it covers all three pathways, exact levies, benefits, risks and a balanced verdict.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks

**Question:** Trace the evolution of climate governance from Bali to Belém and assess whether the regime has moved from negotiation to implementation. Answer in 300 words.

**Model answer (about 300 words):**

From Bali to Belém, climate governance evolved from designing a post-Kyoto bargain to implementing a universal but nationally determined system.

**Negotiation phase:** The **Bali Action Plan (2007)** opened a comprehensive track on mitigation, adaptation, technology and finance. The **Copenhagen Accord (2009)** supplied political signals on temperature and finance but was only “taken note of”. The **Cancun Agreements (2010)** formalised institutions including the GCF and Technology Mechanism. The **Durban Platform (2011)** mandated an outcome with legal force applicable to all Parties, producing the **Paris Agreement (2015)**. **Warsaw (2013)** separately institutionalised loss and damage.

**Rule-making phase:** Paris created the NDC cycle but left implementation rules unfinished. **Glasgow (2021)** completed major transparency and Article 6 rules and introduced the precise coal/fossil-subsidy language. **COP27 (2022)** agreed loss-and-damage funding arrangements. **CMA 5 (2023)** concluded the first GST, calling for transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, tripling renewable capacity and doubling energy-efficiency improvement rates by 2030.

**Implementation phase:** **Baku (2024)** set the NCQG: at least USD 300 billion annually by 2035 with developed Parties leading, alongside an all-actor USD 1.3 trillion scale-up call, and advanced Article 6 operation. **Belém (2025)** launched a Global Implementation Accelerator and Belém Mission to 1.5, called for tripling adaptation finance by 2035 and adopted 59 voluntary adaptation indicators.

Yet implementation remains incomplete. Goals are not disbursements; reports are not outcomes; presidency roadmaps are not negotiated decisions. India’s position—universal action with CBDR-RC, finance and technology—shows why equity determines implementability.

**Conclusion:** the centre of gravity has shifted towards implementation, but delivery, aggregate NDC adequacy and finance remain the regime’s decisive tests.

**Why this earns marks:** it traces chronology through institutional purpose and distinguishes adopted outcomes from political initiatives.
