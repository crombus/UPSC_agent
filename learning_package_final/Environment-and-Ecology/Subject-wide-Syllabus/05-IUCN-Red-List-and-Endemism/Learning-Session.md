### 1. What the Red List measures and how the nine categories fit

**Answer-grabbing line**

> The IUCN Red List is a standardised scientific estimate of a taxon’s extinction risk at a stated spatial scale and assessment date; it does not itself create a legal prohibition or conservation budget.

**Must-write keywords:** taxon; extinction risk; best available evidence; category; criterion; scale; assessment date.

#### Visual: the complete global ladder

```text
NOT YET ASSESSED                         EVALUATED
NE                              DD | LC → NT → VU → EN → CR → EW → EX
                                 ↑          └──── THREATENED ────┘
                                 └─ information inadequate

Risk order is meaningful from LC through EX.
NE and DD must not be treated as low-risk rungs.
```

| Code | Category | Exact exam meaning |
|---|---|---|
| EX | Extinct | No reasonable doubt that the last individual has died. |
| EW | Extinct in the Wild | Survives only in cultivation, captivity, or as a naturalised population well outside the past range. |
| CR | Critically Endangered | Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild. |
| EN | Endangered | Very high risk of extinction in the wild. |
| VU | Vulnerable | High risk of extinction in the wild. |
| NT | Near Threatened | Does not now qualify as CR/EN/VU, but is close to qualifying or likely to qualify soon. |
| LC | Least Concern | Evaluated and does not qualify as CR/EN/VU/NT; widespread and abundant taxa are included. |
| DD | Data Deficient | Information is inadequate for a direct or indirect extinction-risk assessment. It is not a threat category. |
| NE | Not Evaluated | The taxon has not yet been evaluated against the criteria. |

#### Three sets that UPSC repeatedly confuses

1. **Threatened = VU + EN + CR only.**
2. **Evaluated** includes DD and the categories based on sufficient information; NE alone means not yet evaluated.
3. **EW and EX are extinction categories**, not additional “threatened” categories.

**Examiner trap:** “Red-listed” can mean merely assessed and appearing in the database. It does not necessarily mean threatened.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** “Threatened” is a collective term, not a tenth category. **Evidence:** IUCN Version 3.1 groups VU, EN and CR as threatened, while DD is explicitly not a category of threat. **Analysis:** This prevents an information gap from being mistaken for safety and prevents EW/EX from being counted within the threatened subset. **Qualification:** Always state the assessment scale and date because categories can change and regional categories can differ from the global category.

**Quick recap:** category = risk result; threatened = three-category subset; DD = uncertainty; NE = no evaluation.

### 2. Assessment unit: taxon, wild population, geography and scale

**Answer-grabbing line**

> A Red List label is incomplete unless it identifies the taxon assessed, the geographic scale, the category and criteria, and the assessment date.

**Must-write keywords:** taxon; species or lower level; natural range; wild population; global; regional; national; rescue effect.

#### Visual: one taxon, different valid scales

```text
GLOBAL ASSESSMENT
entire natural range of the taxon
        │
        ├── Regional assessment: a subglobal area
        │      apply criteria to regional population
        │      then consider immigration / rescue from outside
        │
        └── National assessment: regional method at country scale

If the taxon is truly endemic to the region:
regional population = global population → no outside rescue population exists.
```

#### What may be assessed?

- The criteria apply to a **taxonomic unit at or below species level**, provided the unit is clearly specified.
- The core assessment concerns **wild populations within the natural range**; benign conservation introductions have a defined, limited treatment.
- A protected area, a state boundary or a project site is not automatically the taxonomic assessment unit.

#### Global versus regional/national

| Global assessment | Regional/national assessment |
|---|---|
| Estimates global extinction risk across the whole natural range. | Estimates extinction risk within a defined subglobal region. |
| Uses the nine global categories. | May additionally use **Regionally Extinct (RE)** and **Not Applicable (NA)**. |
| No adjustment for an outside population because the whole range is included. | Preliminary category may be adjusted for plausible immigration/rescue or dependence on declining outside populations. |
| A global LC taxon may be highly threatened in one region. | A regional LC population may belong to a globally threatened taxon. |

**RE:** no reasonable doubt that the last reproducing individual in the region has died or disappeared from the wild there.
**NA:** the taxon is ineligible for regional assessment, for example because it is not within its natural range or is merely vagrant.

**Examiner trap:** “National Red List category” is not another global category. It is a regional application whose scale must be named.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Global and national categories answer different spatial questions. **Evidence:** IUCN Regional Guidelines require assessors first to apply the criteria to the regional population and then consider extra-regional rescue. **Analysis:** A species may therefore be LC globally but CR nationally where its local population is tiny and declining. **Qualification:** For a national endemic, the national population is also the global population, so the assessment cannot rely on rescue from an external wild population.

**Quick recap:** taxon + natural range + scale + date; never quote a bare category.

### 3. Criteria A–E: five routes to a threatened category

**Answer-grabbing line**

> A category states the level of risk; Criteria A–E state the quantitative route by which that risk was demonstrated.

**Must-write keywords:** reduction; EOO; AOO; continuing decline; mature individuals; quantitative extinction probability.

#### Concept map

```text
A → POPULATION REDUCTION
B → RESTRICTED GEOGRAPHIC RANGE + qualifying subconditions
C → SMALL POPULATION + CONTINUING DECLINE
D → VERY SMALL OR, for VU D2, VERY RESTRICTED
E → QUANTITATIVE EXTINCTION-PROBABILITY ANALYSIS
```

In full: **Criterion D** is the very-small/very-restricted route, while **Criterion E** is the quantitative extinction-probability route.

#### Official threshold matrix

The figures below are from IUCN Version 3.1. They apply to **CR/EN/VU**, not to NT, LC, DD or NE.

| Criterion | CR | EN | VU | Essential qualifier |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A1 reduction | at least 90% | at least 70% | at least 50% | Last 10 years or 3 generations, whichever is longer; causes clearly reversible, understood **and** ceased |
| A2–A4 reduction | at least 80% | at least 50% | at least 30% | A2 past; A3 future; A4 past + future; causes may not have ceased, be understood or be reversible |
| B1 extent of occurrence | under 100 km² | under 5,000 km² | under 20,000 km² | Plus at least 2 of: fragmentation/few locations; continuing decline; extreme fluctuations |
| B2 area of occupancy | under 10 km² | under 500 km² | under 2,000 km² | Same two-of-three requirement |
| C population ceiling | under 250 mature | under 2,500 mature | under 10,000 mature | Plus continuing decline and C1 or C2 structure/fluctuation conditions |
| C1 decline | at least 25% in 3 years/1 generation | at least 20% in 5 years/2 generations | at least 10% in 10 years/3 generations | Whichever period is longer; future horizon capped at 100 years |
| D1 mature individuals | under 50 | under 250 | under 1,000 | No continuing-decline condition is built into D1 |
| D2 very restricted | — | — | typically AOO under 20 km² or 5 or fewer locations | Vulnerable route where a plausible threat could drive CR/EX very quickly |
| E extinction probability | at least 50% in 10 years/3 generations | at least 20% in 20 years/5 generations | at least 10% in 100 years | Quantitative analysis; “whichever longer” applies where stated |

#### Criterion B is not “small map = threatened”

For B1 or B2, the range threshold must be accompanied by at least **two** of:

1. severe fragmentation or few locations;
2. continuing decline in range, habitat, locations/subpopulations or mature individuals;
3. extreme fluctuations.

#### Criterion C and D are not interchangeable

- **C:** small population **and continuing decline**, plus the stated C1 or C2 conditions.
- **D:** very small population; VU D2 also covers an acutely restricted population exposed to a rapid plausible threat.

#### One-route rule

A taxon needs to satisfy **one complete criterion route** at the relevant threshold to enter CR, EN or VU. It does not need to satisfy A, B, C, D and E together. If several criteria are met, all qualifying criteria should be documented.

**Examiner traps**

- A species need not show a measured decline to qualify under B or D.
- EOO and AOO are different measures.
- Merely crossing a range threshold is insufficient under Criterion B.
- “Any one criterion” never means “one fragment of a criterion.”

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** High extinction risk is not reducible to low abundance. **Evidence:** Criterion B uses EOO/AOO with fragmentation, decline or fluctuation, while D can classify a very small or acutely restricted population. **Analysis:** A taxon may therefore face severe risk because one event can affect nearly its whole range even before a long decline series exists. **Qualification:** The category is valid only when the complete criterion and subcriteria are documented; a small range by itself does not automatically establish CR, EN or VU.

**Quick recap:** A decline; B range; C small + declining; D very small/restricted; E modelled probability.

### 4. Evidence, uncertainty, DD and the assessment workflow

**Answer-grabbing line**

> The Red List is evidence-based, not data-perfect: it records uncertainty, but neither DD nor a modelled estimate should be converted into false certainty.

**Must-write keywords:** best available evidence; observed; estimated; inferred; projected; suspected; uncertainty; documentation; peer review.

#### Assessment workflow

```text
1. DEFINE → taxon + natural range + scale
2. COMPILE → distribution, population, trend, habitat, threats, conservation
3. TEST → all applicable Criteria A–E
4. ASSIGN → highest category met; record criteria/subcriteria
5. REVIEW → consistency and expert/authority review
6. PUBLISH → category + rationale + date + supporting fields
7. REASSESS → repeat when evidence or status changes
```

IUCN permits different evidence qualifiers where the criterion allows them. A projection, inference or suspicion must still have a documented basis.

#### DD versus NE

| DD | NE |
|---|---|
| The taxon has been considered, but distribution/population evidence is inadequate for a risk assessment. | The taxon has not yet been evaluated against the criteria. |
| May conceal high risk. | Says nothing about risk. |
| Priority response: targeted surveys plus precaution where plausible threats/range restriction exist. | Priority response: initial assessment. |

**Examiner trap:** DD is not LC, and an old observation is not a current population estimate.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Data deficiency is a knowledge warning, not a safety certificate. **Evidence:** IUCN defines DD as inadequate information and expressly notes that later research may show a threatened classification is appropriate. **Analysis:** Ignoring DD taxa can bias conservation towards charismatic, well-surveyed groups. **Qualification:** Precaution must remain proportionate: DD does not prove that the taxon is threatened, so survey design and threat evidence must guide interim action.

**Quick recap:** uncertainty is documented; DD warrants research, not complacency.

### 5. Reassessment, uplisting, downlisting and reasons for change

**Answer-grabbing line**

> A category change is an observed change in classification; it is evidence of biological recovery or deterioration only when the reason for change is coded as genuine.

**Must-write keywords:** reassessment; genuine recent; genuine since first assessment; new information; taxonomy; error; uplisting; downlisting.

#### Change logic

```text
REASSESSMENT
   ├── SAME CATEGORY
   │      └── criteria may still change
   └── CATEGORY CHANGES
          ├── GENUINE → real status improved or deteriorated
          └── NON-GENUINE
                 ├── new/better information
                 ├── taxonomic revision
                 ├── criteria revision
                 ├── previous misinterpretation
                 └── incorrect data / other documented reason
```

#### Transfer rules

- Normal transfer from a higher-threat to a lower-threat category occurs when none of the former category’s criteria has been met for **five years or more**.
- An erroneous original classification may be corrected without delay.
- Transfer from lower to higher risk should be made without delay when the evidence supports it.

#### Genuine versus knowledge change

The 2024 IUCN Guidelines require “genuine” to be used only when the real status change alone is sufficient to cross the category threshold. Better surveys that discover previously unknown individuals are a **new-information** change unless a real increase independently crosses the threshold.

#### Indian illustration: Narcondam Hornbill

The 2020 IUCN assessment classified the Narcondam Hornbill as **VU D1+2**, down from EN. Its rationale says the population estimate was larger than previously believed and that the species **may have increased or previous estimates may have been too low**. This is exactly why “downlisted” cannot be translated automatically into “recovered.” [NH]

**Examiner trap:** reassessment year, publication year and year of genuine status change can differ.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Downlisting is not synonymous with successful conservation. **Evidence:** The 2020 Narcondam Hornbill assessment records a larger estimate but expressly leaves open whether numbers increased or earlier estimates were too low. **Analysis:** Only the genuinely biological component should be read as recovery or used in the Red List Index. **Qualification:** The taxon remains island-endemic and VU D1+2, so lower category does not remove the need for risk management.

**Quick recap:** read category + criterion + reason for change + dates.

### 6. Endemic, native, indigenous, exotic, invasive and range-restricted

**Answer-grabbing line**

> Endemism is a statement about natural distribution, whereas threatened status is a statement about extinction risk; the two may overlap but are never synonyms.

**Must-write keywords:** natural distribution; spatial reference; introduced; alien; invasive; range restriction; rarity.

| Term | Precise meaning | Close-option trap |
|---|---|---|
| Native / indigenous | Occurs naturally in the stated area without human introduction. | Native does not mean restricted to that area. |
| Endemic | Naturally found in a specified area and nowhere else; the area must be named. | Endemic to an island, mountain chain, country or continent are different claims. |
| Exotic / alien / non-native | Introduced outside natural past or present distribution by human agency. | Alien does not automatically mean harmful. |
| Invasive alien | An alien species whose introduction or spread threatens biological diversity. | Invasive is a harmful subset of alien species. |
| Range-restricted | Has a small natural distribution. | A small range may cross a border, so it need not be endemic to one country. |
| Rare | Low abundance, frequency or detectability in a stated context. | Rare is not automatically endemic or threatened. |
| Threatened | Assessed as VU, EN or CR. | It is not a distribution term. |

#### Set relationship

```text
ENDEMIC ⊂ NATIVE
INVASIVE ALIEN ⊂ ALIEN / EXOTIC

RANGE-RESTRICTED overlaps with ENDEMIC,
but neither set fully contains the other at every chosen spatial scale.
```

**Examples**

- Western hoolock gibbon is native to north-eastern India but also occurs naturally in Bangladesh and Myanmar; it is **not endemic to India**. Its IUCN assessment is EN A4acd, published in 2019. [HH]
- The Narcondam Hornbill is endemic to one Indian island. [NH]
- A species introduced to India may remain non-invasive; impact, establishment and spread matter.

**Examiner trap:** “Indian species” can mean native, resident, legally protected, recorded in India, or endemic. Demand the exact term.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Endemicity measures irreplaceability, not category. **Evidence:** The Western hoolock gibbon is EN but transboundary, while an endemic taxon may be LC if its range and population are stable. **Analysis:** Distribution and extinction risk therefore supply different decision variables. **Qualification:** Endemism must always be tied to a named area; changing the boundary can change whether the statement is true.

**Quick recap:** native = naturally present; endemic = only there; alien = introduced; invasive = harmful alien.

### 7. Centres of endemism and island/montane vulnerability

**Answer-grabbing line**

> Centres of endemism are conservation priorities because they concentrate irreplaceable evolutionary lineages, but their vulnerability arises from range geometry and threats, not from the label alone.

**Must-write keywords:** isolation; speciation; irreplaceability; single-site risk; recolonisation; sky island; upslope compression.

#### Why endemism clusters

```text
GEOGRAPHIC ISOLATION
 + environmental heterogeneity
 + long evolutionary persistence
 + limited dispersal
        ↓
LOCAL SPECIATION
        ↓
HIGH CONCENTRATION OF ENDEMIC TAXA
```

A **centre of endemism** is a scientific biogeographic description, not a statutory protected-area category.

#### Island vulnerability

- The same isolation that produces endemic species limits natural recolonisation.
- A cyclone, fire, disease, invasive predator or habitat conversion can affect a large share of the total global range at once.
- Small islands provide few refuges and short rescue distances only if a source population exists elsewhere—which a true island endemic lacks.

**Case:** Narcondam Hornbill remains restricted to one tiny island; its 2020 VU D1+2 assessment identifies introduced mammals, disease and natural disasters as plausible future threats. [NH]

#### Montane vulnerability

- High-elevation habitats can function as separated “sky islands.”
- Warming and land-use change can compress suitable climate space upslope.
- Valleys, plantations, roads or unsuitable forest can isolate neighbouring populations.
- Small climatic belts and low dispersal can make local loss hard to reverse.

**Case:** Project Nilgiri Tahr treats the tahr as an endemic of the Western Ghats’ montane shola-grassland landscape and links species recovery to habitat restoration. [TN]

#### Indian high-endemism landscapes

- Western Ghats river, forest and shola-grassland systems;
- Himalayan and north-eastern elevational/isolation mosaics;
- Eastern Ghats localised forest and hill systems;
- Andaman–Nicobar oceanic islands.

These are ecological patterns, not a claim that every species there is endemic or threatened.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Endemism compounds exposure where the whole global range is concentrated. **Evidence:** The Narcondam Hornbill’s entire natural distribution is one island, while the Nilgiri tahr depends on separated montane grassland-shola systems. **Analysis:** A local disturbance can therefore become a global species event, and recolonisation may be limited. **Qualification:** Endemism alone does not establish an IUCN category; population, trend, range and criterion subconditions still require assessment.

**Quick recap:** endemism raises irreplaceability; island and mountain geometry can magnify risk.

### 8. Status firewall: IUCN, CITES, WPA and protected areas

**Answer-grabbing line**

> Species conservation uses several complementary status systems, but each answers a different question and produces different consequences.

**Must-write keywords:** scientific assessment; trade control; domestic legal protection; place-based protection; independent processes.

| System | Core question | Character | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| IUCN Red List | How high is extinction risk at the stated scale? | Scientific/advisory assessment | It does not itself prohibit hunting, trade or development. |
| CITES Appendix | What international-trade control is needed? | Treaty-based trade regulation implemented by Parties | Appendix placement is not an IUCN category. |
| Wild Life (Protection) Act schedule | What domestic legal protection/control applies in India? | Binding Indian law | Schedule placement is not a global risk assessment. |
| National Park / Sanctuary / other PA | What legal protection applies to a defined place? | Place-based statutory protection | Presence in a PA does not determine the species’ global category. |
| Endemic/native label | Where does the species occur naturally? | Biogeographic description | It creates no automatic legal protection. |

#### Why divergence is legitimate

- A globally LC species may be rare or legally important in India.
- A globally threatened species may occur substantially outside protected areas.
- A CITES-listed species may be controlled because trade could threaten it even when its Red List category differs.
- Revisions occur on different evidence bases and timetables.

#### Current Great Indian Bustard anchor

- The current IUCN species record checked on 28 September 2026 displays **Critically Endangered**. [GIB]
- On **19 December 2025**, the Supreme Court accepted revised priority areas of **14,013 km² in Rajasthan** and **740 km² in Gujarat**, directed implementation of in-situ/ex-situ and monitoring measures, and required time-bound mitigation including undergrounding of **250 km** of critical power lines identified by WII within two years. [SC]
- These judicial directions are an **implementation response**. They do not themselves prove population recovery or a future downlisting.

**Examiner trap:** “IUCN protected,” “CITES endangered,” and “inside a PA therefore safe” are imprecise formulations.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Assessment must be translated into law and habitat action. **Evidence:** The Great Indian Bustard’s CR status identifies risk, while the Supreme Court’s 19 December 2025 judgment orders specific spatial and power-line measures. **Analysis:** Science diagnoses the problem; domestic law and adjudication create enforceable responses. **Qualification:** An order or budget is an input, not an ecological outcome, and recovery must be demonstrated through later monitoring and reassessment.

**Quick recap:** IUCN = risk; CITES = trade; WPA = Indian law; PA = place.

### 9. Red List Index: what it shows and what it cannot show

**Answer-grabbing line**

> The Red List Index tracks aggregate movement in extinction risk, not the headcount of threatened species or the abundance of every population.

**Must-write keywords:** repeated assessment; genuine change; category weight; aggregate trend; taxonomic coverage; lag.

#### Mechanism

```text
SAME SET OF SPECIES
 + repeated Red List assessments
 + category weights
 + only genuine category changes
        ↓
RED LIST INDEX TREND

1.0 → all included species LC
0.0 → all included species EX
falling line → aggregate extinction risk worsening
rising line  → aggregate extinction risk improving
```

#### What enters the index?

- Category movements caused by genuine improvement or deterioration.
- The same species set across time, with retrospective correction for taxonomy or better knowledge where required.
- Groups assessed comprehensively or through a defensible representative method at least twice.

#### Limitations

1. **Coarse sensitivity:** categories are broad; substantial decline within one category may not move the index.
2. **Coverage bias:** poorly assessed taxa and incomplete groups are under-represented.
3. **Reassessment lag:** ecological change may precede a published category movement.
4. **DD/uncertainty:** knowledge gaps complicate interpretation.
5. **Aggregation:** improvements in some species can mask deterioration in others.
6. **Scale:** a global RLI may not reflect one country or population.
7. **Not abundance:** it does not replace population indices, habitat indicators or threat monitoring.

**Examiner trap:** a falling RLI does not mean that every species is declining, and a stable RLI does not prove stable abundance.

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** The RLI is a trend indicator of aggregate extinction risk. **Evidence:** IUCN guidance calculates it from repeated category assessments and excludes changes caused only by new information or taxonomy. **Analysis:** This makes it more defensible than comparing raw threatened-species counts between editions. **Qualification:** Category thresholds are coarse and taxonomic coverage uneven, so the RLI must be read with abundance, habitat and threat indicators.

**Quick recap:** RLI = genuine category movement across a repeated species set.

### 10. Conservation prioritisation: risk informs, but does not allocate, action

**Answer-grabbing line**

> Red List category is a critical input to conservation priority, but priority and funding must also consider irreplaceability, urgency, feasibility, cost, ecological function, legal duties and distributive consequences.

**Must-write keywords:** risk; irreplaceability; urgency; feasibility; cost; data gap; ecological function; portfolio.

IUCN expressly warns that the threat category is not by itself a conservation-priority system. A priority framework should combine:

| Decision dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Extinction risk | How close is the taxon to extinction, and by which criterion? |
| Endemism/irreplaceability | Would loss in this place mean global loss? |
| Threat imminence | Is there a near-term avoidable threat or irreversible threshold? |
| Ecological role | Does the species support wider ecosystem function? |
| Feasibility and leverage | Can a realistic action materially reduce risk? |
| Cost and opportunity cost | What resources and trade-offs are involved? |
| Law and commitments | What statutory, treaty or judicial duties apply? |
| Equity and coexistence | Who bears conservation costs and who participates? |
| Knowledge deficit | Is survey investment needed before or alongside action? |
| Habitat co-benefits | Will landscape protection benefit many taxa and ecosystem services? |

#### Why category cannot equal funding

- A CR species may have no immediately feasible recovery action, while a VU endemic may be saved cheaply before deterioration.
- DD taxa may require urgent survey funding.
- Habitat action can protect several species more effectively than isolated single-species spending.
- Common species can be ecologically important and may warrant preventive action before they become threatened.

#### Assessment-to-action chain

```text
RED LIST DIAGNOSIS
      ↓
THREAT + RANGE + SCALE ANALYSIS
      ↓
LEGAL / TRADE / PLACE-BASED TOOLS
      ↓
FUNDED ACTION + COMMUNITY PARTICIPATION
      ↓
MONITORING OF POPULATION, HABITAT AND THREATS
      ↓
REASSESSMENT + RLI / RECOVERY EVIDENCE
```

**Write the paragraph**

> **Claim:** Conservation finance should be risk-informed rather than category-determined. **Evidence:** IUCN guidance states that threat category alone is insufficient for priority setting because cost, logistics and prospects of success also matter. **Analysis:** Combining risk with endemicity and actionability prevents both neglect of DD/range-restricted taxa and inefficient spending driven only by charismatic CR labels. **Qualification:** Feasibility must not become an excuse to abandon highly threatened species; a balanced portfolio should include emergency rescue, habitat prevention and knowledge generation.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Regional rescue effects

A regional population may be downlisted if regular immigration from a healthy outside population can plausibly rescue it. It may be left unchanged or, in exceptional circumstances, uplisted where the regional population is a sink and outside immigration is expected to decline. A true regional endemic has no extra-regional source population, so this adjustment is unavailable.

### B. Category notation is compressed reasoning

`EN A4acd` is not decorative coding:

- `EN` is the category;
- `A4` is the past-and-future population-reduction route;
- letters identify the accepted evidence bases.

Good answers decode the notation only as far as the source supports it; they do not invent an assessment rationale from the category alone.

### C. Red List versus Green Status

The Red List asks how high extinction risk is. The IUCN Green Status separately evaluates recovery and conservation impact. A lower extinction risk does not necessarily mean ecological function has been restored across the indigenous range, and a conservation-dependent species may remain far from full recovery.

### D. Taxonomic and survey bias

Vertebrates and vascular plants are generally better studied than many invertebrates, fungi and freshwater or marine taxa. This affects the proportions of DD/NE taxa and the apparent geography of threat. An advanced answer therefore distinguishes:

- absence of evidence from evidence of absence;
- lack of assessment from low risk;
- a sampled or comprehensively assessed group from all biodiversity.

### E. Current case-study discipline

- **Hoolock:** quote EN A4acd only with the 2019 publication/2017 assessment date; do not call it Indian-endemic. [HH]
- **Narcondam Hornbill:** quote VU D1+2 with the 2020 assessment and retain the genuine-versus-knowledge ambiguity. [NH]
- **Gooty tarantula:** quote CR B1ab(iii) with its 2008 assessment date; do not present the old assessment date as a new survey. [GT]
- **Great Indian Bustard:** separate the current displayed IUCN category from the Supreme Court’s 19 December 2025 implementation directions. [GIB; SC]

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Category rail

- Global categories: `EX, EW, CR, EN, VU, NT, LC, DD, NE`.
- Increasing assessed risk: `LC → NT → VU → EN → CR → EW → EX`.
- Threatened = `VU + EN + CR`.
- DD is evaluated but data-inadequate; NE is not yet evaluated.
- EW/EX are extinction categories, not threatened categories.

### Assessment identity

- Always write: **taxon + scale + category/criteria + assessment date**.
- Global = whole natural range.
- Regional/national = subglobal population plus possible rescue-effect adjustment.
- Regional-only additions: RE and NA.
- A national endemic’s regional population is its global population.

### Criteria A–E

- **A:** reduction; A1 thresholds 90/70/50%; A2–A4 80/50/30% for CR/EN/VU.
- **B:** EOO or AOO threshold **plus at least two** fragmentation/locations, continuing decline, extreme fluctuation conditions.
- **C:** small population + continuing decline + C1/C2.
- **D:** very small population; VU D2 also very restricted and rapidly exposed.
- **E:** quantitative extinction probability: 50%/20%/10% at the specified CR/EN/VU horizons.
- One complete route is enough; one fragment is not.

### Reassessment logic

- Uplisting/downlisting is a category movement, not automatically a biological trend.
- Genuine = real improvement/deterioration sufficient to cross the threshold.
- Non-genuine = new information, taxonomy, criteria revision, misinterpretation, incorrect data or documented other reason.
- Normal downlisting waits until the higher-category criteria have not been met for at least five years; correct an erroneous classification without delay.
- RLI counts genuine changes, not knowledge/taxonomy changes.

### Distribution vocabulary

- Endemic = native and nowhere else in the named area.
- Native/indigenous = naturally present; may occur elsewhere.
- Alien/exotic = introduced beyond natural distribution.
- Invasive alien = alien whose introduction/spread threatens biodiversity.
- Range-restricted may cross political borders.
- Endemic is not synonymous with rare, threatened or legally protected.

### Vulnerability mechanisms

- Island: single-event exposure, invasive species, disease, limited refuges, no outside recolonisation for a true endemic.
- Montane: sky-island fragmentation, narrow climate belts, upslope compression and low connectivity.
- Centres of endemism are biogeographic concentrations, not legal protected-area categories.

### Status firewall

- IUCN = extinction-risk science.
- CITES = international-trade control.
- WPA = binding Indian species law.
- Protected area = place-based legal protection.
- Category, legal schedule, trade appendix and PA occurrence can diverge.

### RLI

- Repeated assessments of the same species set.
- Only genuine category changes drive the trend.
- 1 = all included species LC; 0 = all EX.
- Limits: coarse categories, reassessment lag, DD, coverage bias, aggregation and scale.

### India anchors

- Western hoolock: EN A4acd; 2019 publication; transboundary range, so native but not Indian-endemic.
- Narcondam Hornbill: island endemic; VU D1+2 in 2020; downlisting did not by itself prove recovery.
- Gooty tarantula: CR B1ab(iii) in 2008; range-restriction example.
- Nilgiri tahr: official Indian montane-endemism and habitat-restoration example; do not attach an undated IUCN category.
- Great Indian Bustard: current IUCN record displays CR; 19 December 2025 Supreme Court directions are action, not recovery evidence.

### Final answer line

> Use the Red List to diagnose extinction risk, endemism to identify irreplaceability, law and trade instruments to create enforceable controls, and monitoring plus reassessment to test whether funded action actually reduces risk.
