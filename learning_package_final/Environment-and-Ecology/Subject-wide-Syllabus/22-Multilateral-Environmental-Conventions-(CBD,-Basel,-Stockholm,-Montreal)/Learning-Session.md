## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Learning map: four environmental objects, four regulatory designs

```text
BIODIVERSITY LOSS
    └─ CBD → conservation + sustainable use + fair benefit-sharing
             ├─ Cartagena Protocol → biosafety / living modified organisms
             └─ Nagoya Protocol → access and benefit-sharing (ABS)

TRANSBOUNDARY WASTE MOVEMENT
    └─ Basel Convention → minimisation + prior informed consent (PIC)
                          ├─ Ban Amendment → stronger Annex VII → non-Annex VII prohibition
                          └─ plastic-waste amendments → wider PIC coverage

PERSISTENT ORGANIC POLLUTANTS
    └─ Stockholm Convention → scientific screening → Annex A/B/C controls

STRATOSPHERIC OZONE DEPLETION
    └─ Vienna Convention → framework
       └─ Montreal Protocol → controlled-substance schedules + finance
          └─ Kigali Amendment → HFC phase-down for climate benefit
```

**Caption:** UPSC's first test is to match the environmental object to the correct legal mechanism.

| Regime | Environmental object | Main legal technique | Close-option trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBD, 1992 | biological diversity | national strategies, protocols, COP frameworks | not conservation alone |
| Basel, 1989 | transboundary movement and disposal of covered wastes | notification, consent and environmentally sound management | original Convention is not a total trade ban |
| Stockholm, 2001 | listed persistent organic pollutants (POPs) | elimination, restriction and release-reduction annexes | toxicity alone does not make a chemical a listed POP |
| Vienna, 1985–Montreal, 1987 | ozone layer and controlled substances | binding production/consumption schedules, reporting and finance | Montreal Protocol is not a standalone “Montreal Convention” |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Multilateral environmental law works through differentiated designs: target-setting for diffuse biodiversity loss, consent-based controls for waste trade, annex-based controls for POPs and scheduled chemical transition for ozone protection.

**Must-write keywords:** environmental object; treaty mechanism; protocol/amendment boundary; domestic translation; implementation outcome.

**How to write the paragraph:** State the distinct object → name the mechanism → show one institution or instrument → qualify that treaty adoption does not itself prove domestic compliance or ecological recovery.

### 2. Treaty-status discipline: never collapse law into outcome

```text
ADOPTION
  ↓
SIGNATURE (where applicable)
  ↓
RATIFICATION / ACCESSION / ACCEPTANCE
  ↓
ENTRY INTO FORCE FOR THE PARTY
  ↓
DOMESTIC LAW, RULES, AUTHORITIES AND BUDGET
  ↓
IMPLEMENTATION, COMPLIANCE AND MEASURED OUTCOME
```

An amendment or protocol has its **own** participation and entry-into-force status. A country may be
a party to the parent convention without being bound by every later amendment. A COP decision may
create a framework or institution without proving that money has been disbursed, national law has
changed, or outcomes have been achieved.

**Exam firewalls**

- convention ≠ protocol ≠ amendment;
- annex listing proposal ≠ scientific recommendation ≠ COP listing decision;
- global entry into force ≠ entry into force for every state;
- international obligation ≠ domestic notification;
- fund establishment ≠ contribution or benefit delivery;
- target adoption ≠ target achievement.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The legally relevant question is not merely “Was it announced?” but “Which instrument binds which party, from what date, through what domestic measure?”

### 3. CBD: the three-objective bargain

```text
                    CONVENTION ON BIOLOGICAL DIVERSITY
                                  (1992)
                                     |
              +----------------------+----------------------+
              |                      |                      |
        CONSERVATION            SUSTAINABLE USE       FAIR AND EQUITABLE
        of biodiversity         of components         BENEFIT-SHARING
        genes/species/          use compatible        from utilisation of
        ecosystems              with persistence      genetic resources
```

The CBD emerged from the 1992 Rio Earth Summit. Its three objectives are cumulative. Conservation
without sustainable-use policy can ignore livelihood dependence; access without benefit-sharing
can externalise gains from biological resources and associated knowledge.

#### 3.1 Domestic operating route

```text
CBD objectives
   ↓
National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP)
   ↓
Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (as amended) + rules/regulations
   ↓
National Biodiversity Authority → State Biodiversity Boards
   → local Biodiversity Management Committees
   ↓
conservation + sustainable use + access/benefit-sharing + local documentation
```

✅ India's updated **NBSAP 2024–2030** was launched at CBD COP16 in Cali in October 2024 as the
domestic strategy aligned with the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (KMGBF).

⚠️ The CBD does not itself replace India's wildlife, forest, pollution or land-use laws. It supplies
the biodiversity-governance framework; domestic statutes and institutions determine enforceability.

**Must-write keywords:** three objectives; NBSAP; ABS; NBA–SBB–BMC; domestic translation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The CBD is a distributive bargain as well as a conservation treaty: it links ecological protection to sustainable use and justice in the utilisation of genetic resources.

### 4. CBD protocols: Cartagena is not Nagoya

| Dimension | Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety, 2000 | Nagoya Protocol on ABS, 2010 |
|---|---|---|
| Object | living modified organisms (LMOs) resulting from modern biotechnology | genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge within its scope |
| Concern | possible adverse effects on biodiversity, also taking account of human health | fair and equitable sharing of benefits from utilisation |
| Core pathway | advance informed agreement for specified first intentional transboundary movements, risk assessment and Biosafety Clearing-House | prior informed consent and mutually agreed terms, as applicable, plus compliance and benefit-sharing |
| Do not confuse with | general approval of every biotechnology product | biosafety approval or environmental clearance |
| Related instrument | Nagoya–Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol on liability and redress | ABS Clearing-House |

```text
CARTAGENA PATH
export information → import decision → risk assessment/management → handling/documentation

NAGOYA PATH
lawful access → prior informed consent (where required) → mutually agreed terms
→ utilisation → monetary/non-monetary benefits → compliance/monitoring
```

⚠️ Protocol participation must be checked separately from CBD participation. India is a party to
both Cartagena and Nagoya, but this does not make their procedures interchangeable.

**PYQ link:** 2023 Prelims Q79 tests whether local BMCs belong to the decentralised Indian route
supporting Nagoya objectives and ABS—not whether they are central treaty secretariats.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Cartagena governs biosafety risk before or around movement and use of LMOs; Nagoya governs distributive justice in access to, and utilisation of, genetic resources.

### 5. KMGBF: goals, targets and national implementation

```text
CBD COP15 (2022)
   ↓ adopted
KUNMING–MONTREAL GLOBAL BIODIVERSITY FRAMEWORK
   ├─ 4 long-term goals for 2050
   └─ 23 action-oriented targets for 2030
          ├─ reduce threats to biodiversity
          ├─ meet people's needs through sustainable use and benefit-sharing
          └─ tools, finance and mainstreaming for implementation
```

| Goal | Exam-ready focus |
|---|---|
| A | ecosystem integrity, species extinction risk and genetic diversity |
| B | sustainable use and maintenance of nature's contributions to people |
| C | fair and equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources, DSI and associated traditional knowledge |
| D | adequate means of implementation: finance, capacity, technology and cooperation |

**High-yield targets**

- **Target 3 (“30x30”):** effective conservation and management of at least 30% of terrestrial,
  inland-water, coastal and marine areas by 2030, with representativeness, connectivity, equitable
  governance and rights safeguards. Area alone is not the full target.
- **Target 2:** restoration of at least 30% of degraded terrestrial, inland-water, coastal and
  marine ecosystems by 2030.
- **Targets 18–19:** reform harmful incentives and mobilise biodiversity finance.
- **Targets 22–23:** participation, access to justice/information and gender-responsive action.

⚠️ KMGBF is a CBD COP framework, not a new convention and not the Paris Agreement. Its global
adoption does not automatically amend Indian statutes. Parties translate it through national
targets, NBSAPs, finance, sectoral policy and reporting.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** KMGBF converts the CBD's three objectives into a 2050-goal/2030-target architecture, but success depends on nationally financed, rights-sensitive and ecologically effective implementation.

### 6. CBD COP16: Cali, resumed Rome, DSI and Article 8(j)

```text
COP16, CALI (21 Oct–1 Nov 2024; suspended early on 2 Nov)
   ├─ Decision 16/2: multilateral mechanism for benefits from use of DSI
   │                  → Cali Fund
   ├─ permanent subsidiary body on Article 8(j)
   └─ session suspended before all business concluded because quorum was lost
                         ↓
RESUMED COP16, ROME (25–27 Feb 2025)
   ├─ biodiversity-finance decisions completed
   ├─ planning, monitoring, reporting and review mechanism completed
   └─ full KMGBF indicator set agreed
```

#### 6.1 Why DSI matters

Digital sequence information allows research and commercial use of genetic information without
necessarily transferring a physical biological sample. This creates a gap for conventional ABS
models built around physical access. COP16's multilateral mechanism and **Cali Fund** respond to
that gap. The Fund was launched on **25 February 2025** during the resumed Rome session.

✅ The official CBD launch states that the Fund receives contributions from private-sector entities
making commercial use of DSI and allocates 50% of its resources to the self-identified needs of
indigenous peoples and local communities, including women and youth.

⚠️ “Fund launched” does not mean benefits have already reached every provider community. Contribution,
allocation and disbursement are separate stages.

#### 6.2 Article 8(j)

Article 8(j) concerns the knowledge, innovations and practices of indigenous peoples and local
communities relevant to biodiversity. COP16 established a permanent subsidiary body to strengthen
their participation and advice within the CBD process.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** COP16 modernised the CBD's equity pillar by addressing value derived from genetic information and by institutionalising indigenous and local-community participation, while leaving delivery dependent on contributions and national implementation.

### 7. Basel Convention: movement control, consent and environmentally sound management

```text
PROPOSED TRANSBOUNDARY MOVEMENT OF COVERED WASTE
   ↓ exporting state notifies
IMPORTING / TRANSIT STATES receive information
   ↓ written consent or objection
Movement proceeds only under Convention conditions
   ↓ tracking documents + duty of environmentally sound management
If movement is illegal or cannot be completed
   ↓ responsibility for return or alternative environmentally sound disposal
```

The Basel Convention, adopted in 1989, responds to environmental injustice created when hazardous
waste is shifted to jurisdictions with lower disposal capacity or regulatory safeguards. Its core
is broader than consent: parties must minimise hazardous-waste generation and transboundary movement,
ensure environmentally sound management, and combat illegal traffic.

#### 7.1 Prior informed consent (PIC)

PIC is a **procedure**, not permission for unrestricted trade. The exporter cannot treat silence as
universal consent; the treaty's notification and written-consent conditions matter.

#### 7.2 Ban Amendment

The Ban Amendment entered into force globally on **5 December 2019**. It strengthens the original
regime by prohibiting covered exports from **Annex VII parties** (members of the EU/OECD and
Liechtenstein) to **non-Annex VII parties** for final disposal and for specified recovery/recycling.

✅ **India status boundary:** India is a party to the Basel Convention, but the official Basel/UN
status lists checked for this session do **not** list India as having accepted the Ban Amendment.
Therefore, do not write “India is bound by the Ban Amendment” merely because the Amendment is
globally in force.

Official status page:
<https://www.basel.int/Countries/StatusofRatifications/BanAmendment/tabid/1345/Default.aspx>

#### 7.3 Plastic-waste amendments

The amendments adopted in 2019 became effective on **1 January 2021**. They brought most mixed,
contaminated or difficult-to-recycle plastic waste within Basel controls, while preserving narrower
entries for specified hazardous plastic waste and clean, sorted streams. The exam point is not
“all plastic trade is banned”; it is that classification determines whether PIC controls apply.

#### 7.4 Basel is not Rotterdam

| Basel | Rotterdam |
|---|---|
| covered wastes and their transboundary movement/disposal | listed hazardous chemicals and pesticides in international trade |
| notification/consent for waste shipment plus environmentally sound management | PIC procedure communicates import decisions for listed chemicals |
| illegal traffic and waste minimisation are central | information exchange and informed trade decision are central |

Basel, Rotterdam and Stockholm are coordinated as the **BRS conventions** and hold back-to-back COPs,
but they remain separate legal instruments with different lists and obligations.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Basel converts waste trade from a private shipment into an inter-state environmental responsibility, yet its consent model remains vulnerable to misclassification, illegal traffic and unequal enforcement capacity.

### 8. Stockholm Convention: POP identity, annexes and listing sequence

```text
POP CONCERN
   ↓ Party submits proposal
POPRC screens Annex D information
   ↓ persistence + bioaccumulation + long-range transport + adverse effects
Risk profile under Annex E
   ↓ global action warranted?
Risk-management evaluation under Annex F
   ↓ feasible controls, alternatives, socio-economic considerations
POPRC recommendation
   ↓
COP decision to list in Annex A, B and/or C
```

#### 8.1 POP characteristics

A POP persists, can bioaccumulate, can undergo long-range environmental transport and causes
significant adverse human-health or environmental effects. “Toxic” alone is insufficient.

| Annex | Function | Standard example |
|---|---|---|
| A | elimination, subject to specified exemptions | many intentionally produced POPs |
| B | restriction for specified acceptable purposes/exemptions | DDT for disease-vector control |
| C | reduce and, where feasible, ultimately eliminate releases from unintentional production | dioxins and furans |

#### 8.2 India link and status caution

✅ India ratified the Stockholm Convention on **13 January 2006**. India's Department of Chemicals
and Petrochemicals states that DDT is banned for agricultural use but restricted production/use for
vector control continues under the Convention's permitted route.

⚠️ A new POP does not bind every party merely when POPRC identifies concern. The COP must list it,
and the amendment's entry into force for a party depends on the Convention's amendment rules and
that party's legal status.

#### 8.3 Regrettable substitution

Chemical-by-chemical control may encourage replacement by a structurally similar, insufficiently
assessed substance. A stronger policy combines listing with alternatives assessment, class-based
screening where justified, product redesign and lifecycle monitoring.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Stockholm is a science-to-law pipeline: POPRC establishes the global-risk case, but only the COP's annex decision creates the treaty control, subject to party-specific legal effect.

### 9. Mercury boundary: Minamata, not Stockholm

Mercury is a toxic element, not an organic pollutant. The treaty specifically dedicated to mercury
is the **Minamata Convention on Mercury**, not the Stockholm Convention. However, mercury and POPs
share exposure pathways such as long-range transport, food-chain contamination and waste mismanagement.

**PYQ link — 2023 Prelims Q59:** artisanal/small-scale gold mining and coal combustion are major
mercury sources; the public-health proposition must be answered on mercury science, not by assigning
mercury to Stockholm.

> **Trap:** “Persistent toxic substance” does not automatically mean “persistent organic pollutant.”

### 10. Vienna–Montreal architecture

```text
SCIENTIFIC COOPERATION AND OZONE-LAYER FRAMEWORK
Vienna Convention, 1985
               ↓
BINDING CONTROL OF LISTED SUBSTANCES
Montreal Protocol, 1987
   ├─ schedules for production and consumption
   ├─ reporting, licensing and trade controls
   ├─ differentiated timetables
   ├─ periodic scientific/technical assessment
   └─ Multilateral Fund → agreed incremental costs for eligible developing countries
               ↓
Kigali Amendment, 2016
HFC phase-down in CO2-equivalent terms
```

#### 10.1 Why the Montreal Protocol is comparatively effective

| Design feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| bounded list of controlled substances | easier measurement than diffuse land-use and consumption drivers |
| binding, time-bound schedules | converts ambition into observable compliance milestones |
| differentiated schedules | recognises developing-country circumstances |
| Multilateral Fund | finances eligible incremental costs, technology transition and capacity |
| assessment and adjustment architecture | allows controls to respond to science and technology |
| trade/licensing controls | reduces leakage and supports monitoring |

⚠️ “Successful” is comparative, not absolute. It does not mean ozone recovery is complete, illegal
trade has vanished, alternatives are impact-free, or refrigeration access and energy efficiency are
irrelevant.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Montreal aligned law, technology and equity: measurable schedules created demand for transition, while predictable finance made developing-country compliance more feasible.

### 11. Kigali Amendment: ozone institution, climate-controlled substances

HFCs were widely used as substitutes for ozone-depleting substances. They contain no chlorine or
bromine and do **not** deplete stratospheric ozone, but many have high global-warming potential.
Kigali therefore uses the Montreal Protocol's institutions to phase **down**, not universally phase
out, HFC production and consumption.

#### 11.1 India: precise schedule and implementation boundary

✅ India deposited its Kigali ratification on **27 September 2021**. India belongs to the later
Article 5 schedule under Article 5(8 qua)(b) and (d):

| India-group milestone | Controlled level relative to baseline |
|---|---:|
| baseline | average HFC production/consumption in 2024–2026 + 65% of HCFC baseline |
| 2028–2031 | 100% (freeze at baseline) |
| 2032–2036 | 90% |
| 2037–2041 | 80% |
| 2042–2046 | 70% |
| 2047 onward | 15% |

Source: official Kigali Amendment text, Article 5(8 qua):
<https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/amendments/kigali-amendment-2016-amendment-montreal-protocol-agreed>

India's implementation connects the Ozone Cell/MoEFCC, the Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation
and Control) Rules, servicing-sector skills, refrigerant alternatives, equipment redesign, safety
standards and energy efficiency.

⚠️ A low-GWP refrigerant is not automatically sustainable. Leakage, flammability/toxicity,
electricity use, servicing capacity, recovery/recycling and lifecycle emissions also matter.

**PYQ link — 2023 Prelims Q20:** HFC applications include aerosol propellants, foam blowing agents
and clean fire-suppression agents, besides refrigeration and air-conditioning. HFCs are not
lubricant-making substances; refrigeration systems use separate lubricant oils. The inferred answer
is therefore **Only three**.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Kigali is adaptive treaty design: it corrects the climate cost of an ozone-safe substitution while retaining differentiated schedules, finance and reporting.

### 12. India implementation matrix

| International regime | India-facing implementation route | Boundary to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| CBD/KMGBF/Nagoya | Biological Diversity Act; NBA, SBBs and BMCs; NBSAP; ABS rules and decisions | CBD target ≠ automatically enforceable domestic rule |
| Cartagena | GEAC/RCGM and related biosafety authorities under the 1989 Rules; risk assessment, import/use regulation and Biosafety Clearing-House obligations | LMO biosafety ≠ ABS |
| Basel | Hazardous and Other Wastes rules; import/export permissions; customs and pollution-control enforcement | Basel party ≠ Ban Amendment party |
| Stockholm | chemical/pesticide controls and National Implementation Plan; DDT vector-control route | POPRC recommendation ≠ COP listing ≠ domestic control |
| Vienna–Montreal–Kigali | ODS Rules, Ozone Cell, sector plans, licensing/reporting and technology transition | HFC phase-down ≠ HFCs being ozone-depleting |

#### 12.1 Institutional map

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| CBD Secretariat, Montreal (Canada) | services the CBD and its protocols; the city name does not make it part of the ozone Montreal Protocol |
| BRS secretariat arrangements | support coordinated Basel, Rotterdam and Stockholm work; Rotterdam's secretariat functions are shared between UNEP and FAO |

| Ozone Cell, MoEFCC | coordinates India's Montreal Protocol/Kigali implementation |

> **Synthesis line:** International effectiveness is produced at the interface of treaty design and domestic administrative capacity; neither a strong treaty nor a national rule works without monitoring, finance and enforcement.

### 13. Comparative evaluation: why one design cannot simply be copied

| Question | CBD | Basel | Stockholm | Montreal/Kigali |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is the problem bounded? | diffuse across sectors and landscapes | many changing waste streams | chemical-by-chemical | comparatively bounded controlled list |
| Main compliance unit | national biodiversity action and targets | shipment and waste classification | substance and release source | production/consumption in controlled-substance terms |
| Finance/support | CBD financial mechanism and COP resource mobilisation | technical assistance/capacity | GEF-linked financial mechanism | dedicated Multilateral Fund |
| Recurring weakness | targets without adequate mainstreaming or finance | illegal traffic and misclassification | slow listing/substitution cycle | alternative safety, energy and lifecycle trade-offs |
| Adaptability example | KMGBF; DSI mechanism | plastic-waste amendments | addition of new POPs | Kigali extension to HFCs |

⚠️ Montreal's lessons—clear metrics, schedules, finance, technology assessment and reporting—are
transferable. Its exact model is not, because biodiversity loss is driven by land, production,
consumption, rights and governance across the whole economy.

### 14. UPSC traps and close-option distinctions

1. **CBD = conservation only** → false; it has three objectives.
2. **Cartagena = ABS** → false; Cartagena is biosafety, Nagoya is ABS.
3. **KMGBF = Paris Agreement** → false; it is a CBD COP15 framework.
4. **30x30 = 30% area designation only** → incomplete; effectiveness, representativeness,
   connectivity, equitable governance and rights also matter.
5. **Basel bans all hazardous-waste trade** → false; original architecture is consent-based;
   Ban Amendment has a narrower legal scope and separate party status.
6. **India is bound by the Basel Ban because it is globally in force** → false as of the official
   status check used here; India has not accepted the Amendment.
7. **Rotterdam controls waste shipments** → false; Basel does. Rotterdam applies PIC to listed
   hazardous chemicals and pesticides in trade.
8. **Any toxic chemical is a Stockholm POP** → false; POP criteria and listing procedure apply.
9. **DDT is absolutely prohibited under Stockholm** → false; Annex B allows specified vector-control use.
10. **Mercury is a Stockholm POP** → false; mercury is covered by Minamata.
11. **Vienna and Montreal are interchangeable** → false; framework convention versus operative protocol.
12. **HFCs deplete ozone** → false; Kigali controls them for climate reasons.
13. **Kigali requires immediate HFC phase-out** → false; it establishes differentiated phase-down.
14. **Montreal Convention in an aviation question = ozone treaty** → false; treaty names must be
    identified by subject, not city alone.

### 16. Answer frameworks

#### 16.1 10 marks: “Differentiate the four regimes”

1. One-line thesis: different objects require different mechanisms.
2. Four-row table: CBD / Basel / Stockholm / Vienna–Montreal.
3. One close-option trap: Nagoya–Cartagena, Basel–Rotterdam, POP–mercury, HFC–ODS.
4. Conclusion: effectiveness depends on domestic translation and finance.

#### 16.2 15 marks: “Why has Montreal performed better?”

```text
bounded substance list
      + binding schedules
      + differentiated duties
      + Multilateral Fund
      + assessment/adjustment
      = stronger compliance architecture
```

Then qualify with CBD's diffuse drivers, Basel's illegal trade and Stockholm's substitution/listing
lag. Do not reduce success to “political will.”

#### 16.3 20 marks: “Evaluate multilateral environmental governance”

Use five tests:

1. problem–instrument fit;
2. legal precision and status;
3. finance/technology equity;
4. monitoring, compliance and adaptability;
5. domestic institutions, rights and measured outcomes.

Add India examples under each regime and conclude with differentiated strengthening, not one-template
institutional copying.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Consent versus prohibition under Basel

PIC protects state sovereignty and permits legitimate recycling trade, but it can be weakened by
false declarations, ambiguous waste/product distinctions and capacity gaps. The Ban Amendment adopts
a stronger environmental-justice position for a defined Annex VII/non-Annex VII flow, yet slow and
uneven acceptance demonstrates the political economy of stricter trade controls.

### B. Stockholm and regrettable substitution

The POPRC sequence is evidence-intensive and legitimate, but a substance-by-substance method can lag
behind industrial substitution. A high-quality answer proposes alternatives assessment, class-based
controls where scientifically defensible, safer design and post-substitution monitoring.

### C. Kigali as institutional adaptation

Kigali shows that a functioning institution can be extended to a related problem. Yet HFC control
also demonstrates that environmental transitions can shift burdens: CFCs harmed ozone; some
substitutes had high GWP; some low-GWP alternatives raise safety, cost or efficiency issues. Policy
must compare full systems rather than celebrate a chemical replacement in isolation.

### D. DSI as a challenge to territorial access models

Traditional ABS assumes identifiable access to a physical resource. DSI can be copied and analysed
across borders at negligible marginal cost. The Cali mechanism therefore moves toward multilateral
benefit-sharing. The unresolved analytical issue is how to combine open science, traceability,
commercial contribution, provider-country equity and direct benefits for indigenous peoples and
local communities.

### E. Treaty-effectiveness test

```text
EFFECTIVENESS
 = precise scope
 + credible duties
 + scientific review
 + finance and technology
 + reporting/compliance
 + adaptive amendment
 + domestic enforcement
 + fair participation
 - leakage, substitution and implementation gaps
```

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Core recall

- **CBD (1992):** conservation + sustainable use + fair/equitable benefit-sharing.
- **Cartagena (2000):** biosafety and LMOs; advance informed agreement for specified movements.
- **Nagoya (2010):** access to genetic resources, prior informed consent/mutually agreed terms as
  applicable, compliance and benefit-sharing.
- **KMGBF (2022):** four 2050 goals; 23 targets for 2030; Target 3 is “30x30.”
- **COP16:** Cali 2024 adopted the DSI mechanism/Cali Fund and permanent Article 8(j) body; resumed
  Rome 2025 completed finance and monitoring decisions.
- **Basel (1989):** covered waste movement, PIC, environmentally sound management, illegal traffic.
- **Ban Amendment:** globally in force 5 December 2019; Annex VII → non-Annex VII prohibition;
  India is a Basel party but had not accepted the Ban Amendment at the status check used here.
- **Plastic amendments:** effective 1 January 2021; classification, not a universal plastic ban.
- **Rotterdam (1998):** PIC for listed hazardous chemicals and pesticides in trade.
- **Stockholm (2001):** POPs; Annex A elimination, B restriction, C unintentional releases.
- **POPRC:** Annex D screening → Annex E risk profile → Annex F management evaluation → COP decision.
- **Mercury:** Minamata Convention boundary; not a Stockholm POP.
- **Vienna (1985):** framework; **Montreal (1987):** operative controlled-substance protocol.
- **Multilateral Fund:** agreed incremental-cost support for eligible developing countries.
- **Kigali (2016):** HFC phase-down; HFCs are climate pollutants, not ozone-depleting substances.
- **India/Kigali:** ratification deposited 27 September 2021; later Article 5 schedule freezes at
  baseline in 2028 and reaches 15% of baseline from 2047.
