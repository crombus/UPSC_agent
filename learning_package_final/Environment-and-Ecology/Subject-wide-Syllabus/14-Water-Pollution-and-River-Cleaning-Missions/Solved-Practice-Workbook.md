## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1

A regulator finds an identifiable pipe carrying untreated dyeing effluent into a river, while
fertiliser is also washing from many fields during rain. Which classification is correct?

A. The pipe is a point source; the farm runoff is a non-point source.
B. Both are point sources because both ultimately reach the same river.
C. The pipe is non-point because its discharge mixes downstream; the runoff is point because farms are mapped.
D. Both are non-point because rainfall and river flow affect their measured concentrations.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct** because an identifiable outlet is the defining point-source
feature, while diffuse catchment runoff is non-point. **B** confuses a common receiving water with
a common pathway. **C** reverses the definitions: downstream mixing does not erase an outlet, and
mapping land parcels does not create one discharge point. **D** mistakes variability in transport
or concentration for source classification.

### MCQ 2

Consider the following pollutant–class pairs:

1. Suspended sediment — physical
2. Nitrate — chemical
3. Pathogenic protozoa — biological
4. Heated cooling-water discharge — exclusively biological

Which pairs are correctly matched?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 1, 2 and 3 only
C. 2, 3 and 4 only
D. 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** Sediment is a physical pollutant, nitrate a chemical nutrient
and pathogenic protozoa a biological hazard. Pair 4 is wrong: heat is conventionally classified
as physical pollution, although it causes chemical and biological consequences such as lower
oxygen solubility and species stress. **A** omits the correctly matched pathogen; **C** and **D**
include the false “exclusively biological” classification.

### MCQ 3

Which sequence best represents severe biodegradable organic pollution in a slow-moving river?

A. Organic load rises → microbial respiration accelerates → DO rises permanently → anaerobic gases disappear
B. Organic load rises → COD becomes zero → pathogens die → DO stabilises
C. Organic load rises → microbial demand/BOD rises → DO falls if replenishment is inadequate
D. Organic load rises → salinity necessarily falls → thermal stratification ends

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct:** microbes consume oxygen while decomposing biodegradable matter,
so BOD rises and DO can fall when demand exceeds reaeration and photosynthesis. **A** reverses both
the BOD and DO directions. **B** is false because organic input does not make COD zero and oxygen
indicators do not determine every pathogen. **D** invents necessary salinity and thermal effects
that do not follow from biodegradable load alone.

### MCQ 4

With reference to BOD and COD in the same wastewater sample, which statement is most accurate?

A. BOD always equals COD because both count every pollutant molecule.
B. BOD is always greater because microbes oxidise more matter than the chemical test.
C. A universal COD:BOD ratio permits direct conversion for every industry.
D. COD commonly exceeds or equals BOD, but wastewater chemistry prevents a universal ratio.

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct.** COD normally captures a broader chemically oxidisable fraction
than the biodegradable fraction measured by BOD, but inhibitory compounds, industrial chemistry
and test methods alter the relationship. **A** ignores the different analytical principles.
**B** generally reverses the expected relation. **C** is the classic trap: ratios can be locally
useful only after characterisation; they are not universal legal or scientific constants.

### MCQ 5

Which option exactly reproduces CPCB Class A Designated-Best-Use criteria?

A. Total coliform ≤50 MPN/100 mL; pH 6.5–8.5; DO ≥6 mg/L; BOD₅ at 20°C ≤2 mg/L
B. Faecal coliform ≤500 MPN/100 mL; pH 6–9; DO ≥5 mg/L; COD ≤250 mg/L
C. Total coliform ≤500 MPN/100 mL; pH 6.5–8.5; DO ≥5 mg/L; BOD₃ at 27°C ≤3 mg/L
D. Total coliform ≤5,000 MPN/100 mL; pH 6–9; DO ≥4 mg/L; BOD₅ at 20°C ≤3 mg/L

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is the exact Class A combination**, applicable to a drinking-water source
without conventional treatment but after disinfection. **C** resembles Class B but changes the
official five-day BOD method to the separate bathing-notification method. **D** is Class C.
**B** improperly mixes faecal-coliform and COD concepts with the A–E table and gives neither
Class A nor the separate notified bathing criteria.

### MCQ 6

Which use–criterion combination belongs to CPCB Class C?

A. Wildlife/fisheries; pH 6.5–8.5, DO ≥4 mg/L and free ammonia-N ≤1.2 mg/L
B. Drinking-water source after conventional treatment and disinfection; total coliform ≤5,000, DO ≥4, BOD₅ ≤3 mg/L
C. Organised outdoor bathing; total coliform ≤50, DO ≥6, BOD₅ ≤2 mg/L
D. Irrigation/cooling; faecal coliform ≤2,500 and BOD₃ ≤3 mg/L

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B correctly states Class C's use and principal thresholds** (with pH 6.0–9.0
also applicable). **A** belongs to Class D. **C** attaches Class A thresholds to Class B's use.
**D** mixes Class E's use with the separate organised-bathing notification. The question tests
both use class and parameter family; getting one half right is insufficient.

### MCQ 7

Under the notified primary criteria for organised bathing waters, consider the following:

1. Faecal coliform: 500 MPN/100 mL desirable and 2,500 maximum permissible.
2. Faecal streptococci: 100 desirable and 500 maximum permissible.
3. DO: at least 5 mg/L; BOD, three days at 27°C: at most 3 mg/L.
4. The table is identical to CPCB Class B because both use total coliform.

Which are correct?

A. 1 and 3 only
B. 2 and 4 only
C. 1, 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** Statements 1–3 reproduce G.S.R. 742(E), 25 September 2000.
Statement 4 is false: Class B's Designated-Best-Use table uses **total coliform** and BOD₅ at
20°C, while the separate notification uses faecal indicators and BOD₃ at 27°C. **A** omits the
correct faecal-streptococci criterion; **B** relies on the false equivalence; **D** includes it.

### MCQ 8

Which set belongs to CPCB Class E?

A. Total coliform, DO, BOD and pH for untreated drinking-water source
B. Faecal coliform, faecal streptococci, DO and BOD for organised bathing
C. DO, free ammonia-N and total coliform for wildlife propagation
D. pH, electrical conductivity, sodium absorption ratio and boron for irrigation/cooling

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct:** Class E concerns irrigation, industrial cooling and controlled
waste disposal, using pH 6.0–8.5, electrical conductivity up to 2,250 µmhos/cm, SAR up to 26 and
boron up to 2 mg/L. **A** describes Class A's parameter family. **B** is the separate bathing
notification. **C** partly resembles Class D but wrongly adds total coliform.

### MCQ 9

Why can a warm industrial cooling-water discharge stress aquatic life even without high BOD?

A. Warmer water can hold less dissolved oxygen while organism metabolism may increase.
B. Heat converts every dissolved salt into a heavy metal.
C. Warmer water automatically sterilises the river and eliminates algae.
D. Temperature has no relation to oxygen conditions unless faecal coliform is present.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct:** thermal pollution can reduce oxygen solubility and raise
metabolic oxygen demand, producing thermal shock and an oxygen squeeze independent of organic
BOD. **B** is chemically impossible. **C** is over-absolute; heating does not automatically
sterilise natural waters and can favour some blooms. **D** falsely makes a physical–chemical
relationship dependent on a microbiological indicator.

### MCQ 10

Which pathway most accurately describes eutrophication?

A. Sediment removal → water clarity rises → salinity increases → pathogen elimination and fish recruitment
B. Nutrient enrichment → algal growth → decomposition → oxygen depletion and food-web change
C. Metal precipitation → lower turbidity → universal potable quality
D. Thermal discharge → lower metabolism → permanent DO increase

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** Excess nitrogen/phosphorus can stimulate biomass; its decay
raises oxygen demand and may create hypoxia/anoxia, while blooms also alter light, pH and food
webs. **A** is not an eutrophication chain. **C** confuses one treatment mechanism with complete
water safety. **D** reverses common thermal effects. Eutrophication can coexist with sewage but is
specifically nutrient enrichment, not a synonym for all organic pollution.

### MCQ 11

Which statement about faecal indicators is correct?

A. Faecal coliform directly counts every virus, protozoan, helminth and antimicrobial-resistance gene in the sample.
B. A low BOD guarantees that faecal pathogens are absent.
C. Indicator organisms signal faecal contamination risk but are not a census of every pathogen.
D. Disinfection performance is independent of solids and contact conditions.

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** Indicators provide a practicable signal of faecal contamination
and associated risk without enumerating all pathogens. **A** overstates what the test measures.
**B** confuses oxygen-demanding organic matter with microbiological safety. **D** is false because
turbidity/particles, dose, contact time and organism resistance affect disinfection. Chemical,
physical and microbiological evidence must therefore be read together.

### MCQ 12

A coastal aquifer becomes more saline while a nearby biological STP remains compliant for BOD.
Which inference is best?

A. BOD compliance proves the aquifer cannot be saline.
B. The STP must be the only cause because it is the nearest plant.
C. Salinity will be removed by ordinary aerobic treatment if retention time is increased.
D. Salinity needs source/hydrogeological diagnosis; conventional biological treatment removes little dissolved salt.

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct.** Seawater intrusion, pumping, return flow, brine or geogenic
dissolution require hydrogeological and source evidence, and most dissolved salts pass through
ordinary biological treatment. **A** makes an unrelated BOD result prove salt absence. **B**
substitutes proximity for causation. **C** misunderstands biological treatment: longer aeration
does not desalinate water.

### MCQ 13

Which is the most accurate description of an “emerging contaminant”?

A. A contaminant whose detection, risk assessment or regulation is evolving, not necessarily a newly invented substance
B. Any pollutant first detected after a river authority has formally declared the receiving stretch polluted
C. Only a pollutant that produces high BOD in the standard test
D. A legally harmless substance until a universal ambient limit is notified

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct.** PFAS, pharmaceuticals, endocrine-active compounds and
microplastics may be long-used substances whose occurrence or risk management is receiving newer
attention. **B** ties the concept to an arbitrary administrative event. **C** excludes persistent
chemicals that BOD may not detect. **D** is dangerous: absence from one routine standard does not
prove safety or remove duties under broader pollution and hazardous-substance law.

### MCQ 14

With reference to membrane bioreactors (MBRs), which statement is correct?

A. They are desalination units with no biological process.
B. They combine biological treatment with membrane solids separation in wastewater treatment.
C. They are used only for vaccine production and drug delivery.
D. They guarantee removal of every dissolved salt, metal and PFAS compound.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct and matches the 2024 UPSC concept.** An MBR replaces or supplements
conventional solids separation with a membrane while retaining a biological reactor. **A**
confuses MBR with reverse osmosis/desalination. **C** repeats distractors from the PYQ. **D**
overclaims: removal depends on membrane/process characteristics and contaminant properties; an
MBR does not automatically remove all dissolved or persistent contaminants.

### MCQ 15

Why is activated carbon useful in effluent treatment?

A. It converts every dissolved organic and inorganic contaminant into harmless oxygen without producing residual media.
B. It permanently increases its adsorption capacity after saturation.
C. Its high surface area can adsorb selected organics and metals, but spent media needs management.
D. It removes pollutants only by biological digestion.

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** Surface chemistry and pore structure allow adsorption of
selected contaminants; performance depends on water chemistry and media, and capacity is finite.
**A** wrongly describes universal destruction. **B** reverses saturation behaviour; regeneration
or replacement is needed. **D** confuses adsorption with biodegradation. Capturing a pollutant on
carbon also transfers, rather than automatically destroys, its mass.

### MCQ 16

An outlet discharges 25 MLD with a pollutant concentration of 40 mg/L. What is the approximate
pollutant load?

A. 65 kg/day
B. 625 kg/day
C. 1,600 kg/day
D. 1,000 kg/day

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct:** load in kg/day equals concentration in mg/L × flow in MLD, so
40 × 25 = 1,000 kg/day. **A** adds the values rather than multiplying them. **B** multiplies flow
by itself. **C** multiplies concentration by itself. The example shows why concentration alone is
not enough: load depends simultaneously on strength and flow.

### MCQ 17

Which evidence most directly tests whether an STP reduced the pollutant load entering a river?

A. Valid influent/effluent concentrations combined with measured flows, uptime and bypass records
B. The sanctioned cost, inauguration date, rated capacity and contractor's completion certificate
C. The plant's nameplate MLD capacity alone
D. A photograph of clear-looking effluent on one day

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct** because load reduction needs concentration and flow, while uptime
and bypass establish whether the treatment operated continuously. **B** records input and ceremony,
not performance. **C** is installed capacity, not actual utilisation or compliance. **D** is a
visual snapshot that cannot diagnose dissolved pollutants, pathogens or representative operation.
Ambient upstream/downstream monitoring is additionally needed for river outcome.

### MCQ 18

An industry proposes a new outlet likely to discharge trade effluent into a stream. Which Water
Act control is most directly engaged before the discharge begins?

A. Section 16 national coordination by CPCB alone
B. Previous consent under section 25, subject to applicable exemptions and conditions
C. Class B bathing criteria treated as an automatic operating consent for every industrial outlet
D. Arth Ganga livelihood approval

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** Section 25 is the core previous-consent control for a new
outlet/discharge or relevant establishment, while notified exemptions must be read precisely.
**A** describes a CPCB function but not the project-level approval. **C** confuses ambient criteria
with legal consent. **D** is a programme pillar, not a statutory industrial permission. CTO and
ongoing compliance remain separate from merely proposing the outlet.

### MCQ 19

Which Water Act power expressly includes written directions for closure/regulation of an industry
and stoppage/regulation of electricity or water?

A. Section 21
B. Section 24
C. Section 33A
D. Section 17 only

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct:** section 33A carries the direction power, including closure,

### MCQ 20

Consider the Water Amendment Act, 2024:

1. It initially applies to Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan and Union territories.
2. Other States may adopt it through the Article 252 route.
3. It inserted section 27A on Central consent guidelines.
4. It abolished imprisonment for every Water Act contravention.

Which are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 4 only
C. 1, 3 and 4 only
D. 1, 2 and 3 only

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct.** Statements 1–3 reproduce the official Amendment Act. Statement
4 is false: section 45E retains imprisonment for failure to comply with sections 25/26 and for

### MCQ 22

Which institutional allocation is correct?

A. NMCG grants every industry's Water Act consent; ULBs set national water-quality criteria.
B. SPCBs administer major consent/enforcement functions; ULBs/utilities operate municipal sewage systems.
C. CPCB operates every city sewer; industries regulate their own receiving-water class.
D. District Ganga Committees replace SPCBs for all pollution prosecutions.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** SPCBs are statutory regulators, while municipal bodies/utilities
deliver sewage collection and treatment. **A** turns a mission agency into the consent regulator
and a local body into CPCB. **C** assigns operational sewer duties to CPCB and self-regulation to
industry. **D** overstates mission committees; coordination does not erase Water Act enforcement.
Institutional conflation is a recurring Mains weakness.

### MCQ 23

Which statement best describes responsibility when an industry sends effluent to a CETP?

A. The member industry has no further duty once it pays a common fee.
B. The CETP may lawfully accept any toxic, saline or incompatible process stream without influent characterisation.
C. The member retains segregation/pre-treatment and consent duties; the common operator must run the CETP compliantly.
D. The ULB automatically becomes liable for every member's trade effluent.

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** Common treatment divides operational roles but does not erase
member responsibility for compatible influent, pretreatment and lawful discharge, or the
operator's duty to meet conditions. **A** treats payment as immunity. **B** ignores shock loads and
process incompatibility. **D** wrongly transfers industrial responsibility to a municipal body.
Regulators must inspect both member units and the common outlet.

### MCQ 25

Which is the most defensible municipal sewage-treatment sequence?

A. Screening/grit → biological treatment/clarification → required polishing/disinfection → sludge treatment and safe destination
B. Disinfection → raw sewage bypass → river dilution → sludge ignored
C. Reverse osmosis for all sewage → untreated concentrate to river → no sludge, energy or online monitoring
D. Ghat beautification → sewer connection → consent issued by NMCG → pathogen removal assumed

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A gives the functional train**, allowing design-specific primary treatment and
tertiary polishing while retaining the essential sludge line. **B** bypasses treatment and ignores
residuals. **C** prescribes an unnecessarily universal technology and creates an unmanaged reject.
**D** reverses priorities, assigns consent to NMCG and assumes a microbiological outcome. The
correct train must be matched to influent, outlet standard and reuse.

### MCQ 26

Which practice is appropriate for faecal sludge/septage management?

A. Empty tanks manually and discharge into the nearest storm drain.
B. Mechanised emptying, licensed transport and treatment/co-treatment within verified plant capacity.
C. Mix every tanker directly into an STP regardless of inhibitory or solids load.
D. Treat septic containment as permanent storage requiring no inspection.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** Safe containment, scheduled/mechanised desludging, controlled
transport and compatible treatment form one chain. **A** is unsafe and creates a point discharge.
**C** ignores hydraulic, organic, solids and toxicity constraints; co-treatment is conditional,
not automatic. **D** neglects accumulation, leakage and eventual emptying. Transport manifests and
the final sludge destination are as important as plant capacity.

### MCQ 28

A city has a 100 MLD STP, receives 45 MLD because sewers are incomplete, bypasses during power

A. The city treats 100 MLD continuously because design capacity becomes actual utilisation after mechanical completion.

C. Sanctioned capacity and actual utilisation are equivalent once construction ends.

**Answer: D**

actual influent/load, bypass, effluent, sludge and downstream ambient conditions rather than award
success from the 100 MLD label.

### MCQ 29

Which package most closely follows a river-basin restoration approach?

A. Source control, treatment, environmental flow, tributary/wetland/riparian protection and outcome monitoring
B. Only periodic removal of floating litter and riverfront debris at the most visible urban ghats
C. Only increasing river flow to dilute unchanged discharges
D. Only building STPs without sewer connections or sludge arrangements

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct** because it integrates pollution prevention, hydrology, habitat
and evidence across the basin. **B** addresses a visible symptom but not dissolved/sewage loads.
**C** relies on dilution and leaves the source unchanged. **D** creates stranded capacity and a
secondary sludge risk. Basin restoration is broader than end-of-pipe engineering, while still
requiring that engineering where sewage and effluent are generated.

### MCQ 30

Which statement about wetlands and riparian buffers is most accurate?

A. They have unlimited assimilative capacity for untreated sewage, industrial toxics and storm-event nutrient loads.
B. They can retain sediment/nutrients and support habitat, but loading and maintenance limits remain.
C. They make industrial consent unnecessary.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** Wetlands/buffers can attenuate some pollutants and deliver

exemption. **D** falsely promises universal desalination and ignores land/residual constraints.
Nature-based solutions complement, not excuse, source control and pre-treatment.

### MCQ 31

Which chronology is correct?

A. Namami Gange → GAP-I → 2016 Authorities Order → NMCG registration
B. NMCG registration → GAP-I → National River declaration → Namami Gange
C. GAP-I launch (1985) → NMCG registration (2011) → Namami Gange (2014–15) → Authorities Order (2016)

### MCQ 36

Which sequence uses project-status verbs correctly?

A. Sanctioned → river declared clean → tendered → mechanically completed → trial testing → baseline monitoring begins
B. Commissioned → announced → under construction → compliant
C. Utilised → sanctioned → mechanically complete → awarded
D. Announced → sanctioned → constructed → trial tested → commissioned → utilised → outcome verified

**Answer: D**

commissioned yet underutilised; utilised yet non-compliant; compliant yet too small to change
ambient quality. Status language is therefore substantive evidence control, not stylistic
preference.

### MCQ 37

Which statement is the most defensible way to report river-water improvement?

A. At specified stations and periods, named indicators met a stated criterion; wider claims require wider evidence.
B. One high-flow monsoon sample at a single upstream station proves the entire river is permanently clean.
C. Completion of riverfront works proves pathogen and metal safety.
D. A compliant STP outlet proves all non-point pollution has ended.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct** because it preserves station, period, indicator and criterion.
**B** ignores dilution, seasonality and spatial variation. **C** substitutes visible infrastructure
for laboratory/ecological evidence. **D** generalises one point-source result to diffuse runoff and
other outlets. A robust claim also distinguishes concentration from load and monitors upstream as

## Solved Mains PYQs

### PYQ 6 — UPSC Mains 2024, GS-III, Q7

**Ownership:** Direct Topic 14
**Provenance:** Official local paper `03 UPSC 2024 Paper-III.pdf`; routed in
`_PYQ-ROUTING-MAINS-GS3-GS4-2024-2025.md`.
**Question:** “Industrial pollution of river water is a significant environmental issue in
India. Discuss the various mitigation measures to deal with this problem and also the
government's initiatives in this regard.”
**Marks / limit:** 10 marks / 150 words

**Model answer**

Industrial river pollution must be controlled from process to receiving water. First, industries
should substitute toxic inputs, minimise water use, segregate high-strength streams and recover
materials. Residual effluent requires equalisation, neutralisation, physico-chemical treatment,
biological treatment where compatible and advanced polishing for metals, salts or refractory
organics. Individual ETPs or compatible CETPs must operate continuously; sludge and membrane
reject cannot be shifted to drains.

The Water Act, 1974 uses SPCB consent, sampling, inspection and sections 24, 25 and 33A to prevent
unauthorised discharge and permit closure or service regulation. EPA rules provide general and
industry-specific standards. CPCB/SPCB online and laboratory monitoring, polluter liability, and
Namami Gange's industrial-effluent monitoring complement this framework.

Success requires cleaner production, flow-calibrated load monitoring and upstream–downstream
river evidence. Consent or an installed ETP is only an output; continuous compliance and reduced
river load are the outcome.

**Why this earns marks:** It follows “mitigation + government initiatives”, uses named legal and
treatment evidence, and ends with a performance qualification rather than a scheme list.

### PYQ 7 — UPSC Mains 2024, GS-III, Q15

**Ownership:** Direct Topic 14
**Provenance:** Official local paper `03 UPSC 2024 Paper-III.pdf`; routed in
`_PYQ-ROUTING-MAINS-GS3-GS4-2024-2025.md`.
**Question:** “The world is facing an acute shortage of clean and safe freshwater. What are the
alternative technologies which can solve this crisis? Briefly discuss any three such technologies
citing their key merits and demerits.”
**Marks / limit:** 15 marks / 250 words

**Model answer**

Freshwater security needs fit-for-purpose supply, not one universal technology.

**1. Desalination (reverse osmosis/thermal):** It gives a rainfall-independent source for coasts,
islands and brackish aquifers. Its constraints are energy and capital cost, intake impacts,
membrane fouling and safe brine disposal; it does not restore an overdrawn aquifer.

**2. Wastewater recycling and reuse:** Secondary treatment followed by filtration, nutrient or
membrane polishing and disinfection can supply industry, landscaping, agriculture or, with a
much stronger barrier-and-monitoring system, potable reuse. It simultaneously reduces freshwater
withdrawal and river discharge. Its weaknesses are sewer coverage, reliable power/O&M, pathogen
and emerging-contaminant control, sludge/reject management and public acceptance.

**3. Managed aquifer recharge and rainwater harvesting:** Suitable clean monsoon water can be
stored underground with lower evaporation, support baseflow and create drought reserves. It is
distributed and often cheaper than long conveyance. However, success depends on hydrogeology,
recharge-water quality, protected recharge zones and extraction discipline; contaminated recharge
can damage an aquifer.

The three are complementary: desalination expands supply, reuse closes the urban water loop and
recharge stores seasonal water. Selection should compare life-cycle energy, residuals, quality
assurance, local ecology and equitable access.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives exactly three technologies and, for each, mechanism, merits,
demerits and a portfolio verdict.

### PYQ 8 — UPSC Mains 2025, GS-III, Q13

**Ownership:** **Application only**; primary route is Economy Topic 14
(`Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture`). Topic 14 supplies
water-quality, salinity and recharge safeguards.
**Provenance:** Official local paper `UPSC Mains 2025 GS Paper 3 3.pdf`; routing ledger checked.
**Question:** “Examine the factors responsible for depleting groundwater in India. What are the
steps taken by the government to mitigate such depletion of groundwater?”
**Marks / limit:** 15 marks / 250 words

**Model answer**

Groundwater depletion is a stock–flow imbalance: abstraction persistently exceeds recharge.
Irrigation dominates demand in many regions, reinforced by water-intensive cropping, unreliable
surface supply and cheap or unmetered pumping. Urban and industrial extraction, paved recharge
zones, loss of tanks/wetlands, variable rainfall and weak aquifer-level regulation deepen the
decline. Coastal over-pumping can induce salinity, while falling tables raise energy cost and can
mobilise poor-quality water.

Government responses include Atal Bhujal Yojana's community water budgeting in stressed areas,
Jal Shakti Abhiyan–Catch the Rain, the Master Plan for Artificial Recharge, CGWA abstraction
regulation, watershed/tank restoration, micro-irrigation and crop diversification support.
Aquifer mapping and monitoring improve the evidence base.

However, recharge structures cannot compensate for uncontrolled pumping, and polluted stormwater
or sewage must not be injected into aquifers. Reform should link feeder-energy and crop incentives
to local water budgets, protect recharge zones and wetlands, meter major users, reuse treated
wastewater for suitable demand and disclose block/aquifer trends.

Thus, supply augmentation must be paired with enforceable demand management and recharge-quality
protection.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both halves, distinguishes quantity from quality and preserves
the question's primary agricultural-governance ownership.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### Original 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the relationship among DO, BOD and COD. Why can using any one of them alone
misdiagnose river pollution? (Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer**

DO is oxygen present in water; BOD is oxygen used by microbes to decompose biodegradable matter
under a specified test; COD is the oxygen equivalent of material oxidised chemically. Thus,
organic loading usually raises BOD and, when oxygen consumption exceeds reaeration and
photosynthesis, lowers DO. COD commonly exceeds BOD because it includes a wider oxidisable
fraction, but no universal ratio exists.

One indicator alone is insufficient. High DO during daylight can coexist with eutrophic pH swings;
low BOD does not exclude pathogens, metals, salts or PFAS; COD does not identify toxicity,
biodegradability or one compound. Temperature and flow also alter DO, while industrial inhibitors
can distort biological response.

A defensible diagnosis therefore combines DO/BOD/COD with flow-calibrated load, solids, nutrients,
microbiological indicators and source-specific toxics, using comparable station, season and method.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines, relates and qualifies all three indicators, then gives exact
failure modes and a monitoring solution.

### Original 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish point-source and non-point-source water pollution and explain why they
require different control instruments. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer**

A point source has an identifiable discharge location, such as an industrial pipe, municipal
outfall or CETP outlet. A non-point source is diffuse across a catchment, such as fertiliser,
pesticide, sediment or urban wash-off carried by rainfall. A storm drain may aggregate both, so
source tracing still matters.

Point sources suit command-and-control: Water Act consent, outlet standards, flow/concentration
monitoring, ETP/STP operation, inspection and closure or penalty. Non-point pollution varies with
rainfall, soil, slope and land practice; one outlet standard cannot allocate responsibility.
It needs nutrient and pesticide planning, soil cover, riparian buffers, constructed drainage,
wetland protection, incentives and event/sub-basin monitoring.

Both approaches must converge at receiving-water outcomes. Regulating pipes alone leaves diffuse
loads untouched, while catchment programmes cannot excuse an illegal outlet.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both pathways, gives India-relevant examples and links each
to the instrument that can actually control it.

### Original 3 — 15 marks

**Question:** “Treatment capacity is not treatment performance.” Discuss with reference to
municipal sewage, faecal sludge and industrial effluent. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

Installed MLD is only nameplate hydraulic capacity. Performance requires connected inflow,
continuous operation, pollutant-load removal, compliant effluent and safe residual management.

For municipal sewage, screens and grit removal are followed by primary treatment where designed,
biological treatment, clarification or membranes, polishing and disinfection. Yet incomplete
sewers, power failure, bypass and unhandled sludge can leave a large STP underused or polluting.

Faecal sludge needs safe containment, mechanised emptying, licensed transport and reception at an
FSTP or compatible STP. Co-treatment without checking solids, organic and inhibitory load can
overwhelm the plant; tanker discharge into drains creates a new point source.

Industrial effluent first requires cleaner production and stream segregation, then equalisation,
neutralisation, physico-chemical treatment, biological treatment where compatible and advanced
polishing. An ETP installed but bypassed is failure. A CETP does not erase each member's
pre-treatment and consent duties.

Assessment should compare actual influent with design load, uptime, bypass, influent/effluent
concentrations and flows, sludge/reject destination and downstream ambient quality. The 2 July
2026 Namami Gange capacity figures are therefore outputs, not automatic proof of utilisation or
river recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer covers all three waste streams, treatment logic, last-mile
failure and the exact evidence needed to convert capacity into performance.

### Original 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Examine India's legal and institutional architecture for controlling water
pollution. How do the 2024 Water Act amendment and 2026 consent guidelines alter the framework?
(Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

The Water Act, 1974 creates CPCB–SPCB pollution control. CPCB coordinates national programmes and
criteria under section 16; SPCBs plan, consent, inspect and enforce under section 17. Section 24
prohibits polluting discharges to streams/wells; sections 25–27 govern consent; sections 21 and 23
support sampling and inspection; section 33A permits closure/regulation and service directions.
PCCs perform delegated Union-territory functions. ULBs operate sewage systems, industries operate
ETPs or lawful CETP routes, and NMCG coordinates Ganga mission projects rather than replacing
regulators.

EPA sections 3, 5 and 6 provide umbrella Central powers, directions and rules, including discharge
standards and the 2016 Ganga authority architecture.

The 15 February 2024 amendment inserted section 27A for Central consent guidelines and enabled
notified section 25 exemptions. It initially applies to Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan and Union
territories; other States require Article 252 adoption. Several minor contraventions shifted to
monetary adjudication, but section 45E retains imprisonment for sections 25/26 failures.

PIB on 28 January 2026 reported consolidated permissions, CTO validity until cancellation,
90-day Red-category processing and auditor-assisted inspection. These reduce delay, but credible
monitoring, auditor independence and deterrent cancellation remain essential.

**Why this earns marks:** It maps statute, actors, remedies and both reforms while retaining their
territorial and enforcement qualifications.

### Original 5 — 20 marks

**Question:** Trace the evolution from the Ganga Action Plan to Namami Gange and critically
evaluate whether mission-mode river cleaning has overcome the earlier implementation deficit.
(Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

Ganga Action Plan Phase I, launched in June 1985 and closed on 31 March 2000, established the
sewer-interception and treatment template; Phase II expanded the effort. Its central lesson was
that plants and schemes fail without complete networks, O&M, industrial control and basin
coordination. NRCP extended central assistance to other identified rivers, while Ganga-basin work
later moved to Namami Gange.

Namami Gange, launched in 2014–15, is broader: sewage infrastructure, industrial monitoring,
surface cleaning, biodiversity, afforestation, e-flow, rural sanitation and participation. The
2016 Order created the National Ganga Council, Empowered Task Force, NMCG, State and District
Ganga Committees. HAM and One City–One Operator seek sustained O&M.

PIB on 2 July 2026 reported 524 projects sanctioned, 363 completed, and 145 of 218 sewerage
projects completed, creating/rehabilitating 4,263 MLD against 6,610 MLD sanctioned. This shows
scale and execution, not final outcome. Remaining risks are incomplete connections, under-loading,
bypass, sludge, diffuse runoff, fragmented local capacity and stretch/season variability.

Therefore mission mode has strengthened finance, coordination and monitoring, but success must be
judged by actual treatment, compliant load reduction, environmental flow and comparable river
ecology—not expenditure, installed MLD or riverfront visibility.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces chronology, identifies design improvements, uses dated
evidence and evaluates rather than celebrating project counts.

### Original 6 — 20 marks

**Question:** Design an integrated river-basin strategy for municipal, industrial and diffuse
water pollution. Include legal enforcement, ecological restoration and an input–output–outcome
monitoring framework. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

An integrated strategy begins with a basin pollutant inventory: municipal sewage/faecal sludge,
industrial outlets, farm and urban runoff, contaminated groundwater and ecological-flow deficits.

**Source control:** universal safe sanitation, scheduled desludging, cleaner industrial production,
toxic/saline stream segregation, nutrient-efficient farming, erosion control and solid-waste
interception.
**Treatment:** connected, continuously operated STPs; compatible co-treatment/FSTPs; ETPs and
CETPs with member pre-treatment; pollutant-specific polishing, disinfection and lawful sludge or
concentrate disposal.
**Law and accountability:** Water Act consent, valid sampling, inspections and section 33A
directions; EPA standards; public disclosure of flow, load, bypass and compliance. Regulators,
ULBs, industries and mission agencies must retain separate accountability.
**Ecology:** protect environmental flow, tributaries, floodplains, recharge zones, wetlands and
riparian buffers; restore habitat rather than relying on dilution or beautification.

Monitoring should follow a results chain. Inputs are finance, land and staff; outputs are connected
plants, networks and inspections; intermediate outcomes are uptime, compliant effluent and
kilograms/day load removed; environmental outcomes are sustained DO/BOD, pathogen, nutrient,
toxicant and ecological improvement at comparable upstream/downstream stations and seasons;
social outcomes are lower exposure and reliable reuse.

This framework prevents sanctioned capacity from being reported as river recovery and makes
adaptive basin management possible.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates all three source classes, law, treatment, ecology,
institutions and measurable outcomes within a single basin logic.
