## PART I — 40 ORIGINAL MCQS

### Q1. A State adds 5 GW of solar capacity during a year. Which conclusion follows without needing any further data?

A. Its nameplate solar capability rises by 5 GW; generation and dependable supply remain separate metrics.
B. Its annual solar generation has necessarily risen by 5 GWh during the same reporting year because capacity and energy use the same base unit.
C. Its evening peak is now fully served by solar because installed capacity measures availability at the time of demand.
D. Its fossil generation must have fallen by an equal amount because every renewable unit displaces one thermal unit.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** GW measures rated power capability; output, timing, curtailment and reliability require separate evidence.
- **B — Incorrect:** GW is power and GWh is energy; no fixed one-to-one conversion exists without time and utilisation.
- **C — Incorrect:** solar output normally declines around evening peak; capacity alone does not establish firm supply.
- **D — Incorrect:** dispatch, demand growth, grid constraints and storage determine actual fossil displacement.

### Q2. Which classification is accurate for India’s power-sector targets?

A. Nuclear is renewable because it does not burn coal, while large hydro is fossil because reservoirs emit methane.
B. Solar, wind and hydro are renewable; nuclear is non-fossil but is not classified as renewable.
C. Biomass is always fossil because it contains carbon, while natural gas with CCS is renewable.
D. Every non-fossil source is renewable because the two terms have the same policy meaning.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** absence of coal combustion does not make nuclear renewable; hydro remains renewable despite site-specific impacts.
- **B — Correct:** non-fossil is the wider category and includes nuclear in addition to renewable sources.
- **C — Incorrect:** sustainable biomass is renewable; CCS does not make fossil natural gas renewable.
- **D — Incorrect:** conflating the categories overstates renewable capacity and misreads NDC language.

### Q3. A 100 MW wind farm generates 262,800 MWh in a non-leap year. Its approximate capacity factor is:

A. 15 percent
B. 20 percent
C. 30 percent
D. 60 percent

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** 15 percent would yield about 131,400 MWh.
- **B — Incorrect:** 20 percent would yield about 175,200 MWh.
- **C — Correct:** 262,800 ÷ (100 × 8,760) = 0.30, or 30 percent.
- **D — Incorrect:** 60 percent would yield twice the stated generation.

### Q4. Which procurement design most directly converts variable renewable output into a specified firm service?

A. Auctioning solar modules by manufacturing capacity without an electricity-delivery obligation or hourly performance requirement
B. Building a wind farm without forecasting, evacuation capacity, balancing support or a delivery schedule
C. Reporting cumulative renewable installations while excluding availability and performance
D. Contracting a scheduled power block backed by renewable generation, storage and enforceable availability terms

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** equipment capacity is industrial capability, not a power-delivery service.
- **B — Incorrect:** missing integration arrangements makes dependable delivery less, not more, credible.
- **C — Incorrect:** an installation statistic cannot define firm supply.
- **D — Correct:** firmness is created through portfolio design, storage duration, scheduling and performance obligations.

### Q5. Why does better wind and solar forecasting help grid operation?

A. It reduces uncertainty faced by schedulers, allowing reserves and dispatch to be planned more efficiently.
B. It makes weather-dependent generators physically dispatchable in the same manner as a reservoir plant.
C. It eliminates the need for transmission because forecasts move electricity to demand centres.
D. It guarantees zero curtailment even when a transmission corridor is congested.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** improved forecasts narrow expected imbalances and help schedule balancing resources.
- **B — Incorrect:** knowledge of output does not create control over the wind or sun.
- **C — Incorrect:** information cannot substitute for physical network capacity.
- **D — Incorrect:** congestion or oversupply may still require curtailment despite accurate forecasts.

### Q6. A renewable-rich region frequently has midday surplus while a distant city has demand. Which intervention addresses the spatial mismatch most directly?

A. Increasing panel efficiency and adding more generation without strengthening the constrained network
B. Expanding transmission and coordinated power flows between the resource region and load centre
C. Reclassifying the surplus as firm power in the capacity statistics
D. Converting every rooftop system into an isolated off-grid unit

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** higher output can worsen congestion if evacuation remains constrained.
- **B — Correct:** transmission moves electricity across space and enables geographic balancing.
- **C — Incorrect:** relabelling does not deliver power.
- **D — Incorrect:** isolation can forgo diversity and balancing benefits and is not a general grid solution.

### Q7. Which example best represents demand response?

A. A coal plant storing additional fuel before the monsoon and peak-demand season
B. A solar farm clipping otherwise available output at its inverter’s rated capacity
C. A cold-storage facility shifting part of its load to a high-solar, lower-price period
D. A battery manufacturer increasing annual cell-production capacity through a new industrial line

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** fuel inventory is supply-side preparedness.
- **B — Incorrect:** inverter clipping is a generation-side technical loss.
- **C — Correct:** the consumer changes timing in response to system or price conditions.
- **D — Incorrect:** manufacturing expansion does not itself alter electricity demand timing.

### Q8. “Renewable curtailment” most accurately means:

A. solar or wind output falling because clouds or calm weather reduce the primary resource
B. a generator retiring permanently after reaching the end of its design life
C. consumers conserving electricity during a shortage without instruction from the system operator
D. available renewable generation not being accepted because of congestion, security, oversupply or commercial constraints

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** this is resource variability, not curtailment.
- **B — Incorrect:** retirement is a capacity-status change.
- **C — Incorrect:** voluntary conservation is a demand change.
- **D — Correct:** curtailment concerns usable output deliberately not dispatched or absorbed.

### Q9. Which grid service is particularly compatible with battery energy storage?

A. Batteries can provide fast frequency response and short-duration shifting through rapidly responding power electronics
B. Some battery chemistries can create new primary energy during discharge without prior charging
C. In selected networks, batteries may permanently replace every transmission corridor regardless of load location
D. With sufficient modules, batteries may provide unlimited seasonal storage without degradation or safety controls

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** batteries can respond quickly and shift energy across hours, subject to duration.
- **B — Incorrect:** batteries store previously generated energy and have round-trip losses.
- **C — Incorrect:** storage may defer some network investment but cannot universally replace transmission.
- **D — Incorrect:** duration, degradation, cost and minerals constrain battery applications.

### Q10. Pumped-storage hydropower stores energy mainly by:

A. splitting treated water into hydrogen and oxygen during low-price off-peak periods

C. compressing atmospheric air inside a conventional hydro turbine
D. retaining river sediment behind a dam and recovering its kinetic energy during floods

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** that describes electrolysis.
- **B — Correct:** electrical energy is converted to gravitational potential energy and recovered later.
- **C — Incorrect:** compressed-air storage is a different technology.
- **D — Incorrect:** sediment retention is not the storage mechanism and creates ecological problems.

### Q13. In a renewable-heavy grid, reservoir hydro may provide value beyond annual energy because it can:

A. vary output within operating limits and support balancing when water and system conditions permit

### Q14. Which condition is essential before calling biomass energy climate-beneficial?

### Q15. Why is critical-mineral policy relevant to renewable expansion?

### Q19. In water electrolysis, the immediate energy conversion is:

### Q20. Which comparison of hydrogen pathways is accurate?

### Q21. Under India’s Green Hydrogen Standard, the notified emissions threshold is:

A. Not more than 2 kg CO₂-equivalent per kg of hydrogen on the specified well-to-gate basis
B. 2 tonnes CO₂-equivalent per tonne of renewable electricity generated over the project lifetime
C. Below 20 kg CO₂-equivalent per kg hydrogen measured during final combustion or fuel-cell use
D. A direct-emissions test at the electrolyser that excludes upstream electricity, water treatment and compression

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** the unit is kg CO₂e per kg H₂ and the threshold is a maximum, not an exact value.
- **B — Incorrect:** the denominator is hydrogen, not electricity.
- **C — Incorrect:** the threshold is 2, and the boundary is production-side well-to-gate.
- **D — Incorrect:** upstream and process emissions within the notified boundary matter.

## PART III — MAINS PYQS WITH MODEL SOLUTIONS

### PYQ M1 — 2020 GS-I Q16 — 15 marks

**“India has immense potential of solar energy though there are regional variations in its development. Elaborate.” (250 words)**

**Model answer:** India’s tropical location, large landmass and modular photovoltaic technology create major solar potential, but resource quality is only the first condition of development.

Arid and semi-arid western India offers high irradiation, large contiguous sites and strong utility-scale potential, visible in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Peninsular states also combine good radiation with industrial demand. By contrast, cloudier, high-rainfall and Himalayan regions may have lower or more seasonal output and difficult terrain, though rooftop and decentralised systems remain useful. Urban areas across India possess distributed rooftop potential, while reservoirs permit carefully assessed floating solar.

Development varies because land availability, grid evacuation, DISCOM procedures, finance, state policy, demand centres and supply chains differ. Dust and heat affect module performance in dry zones; monsoon cloud affects seasonal generation; land conflict and biodiversity sensitivity constrain some apparently “vacant” sites. Rooftop growth additionally depends on roof ownership, metering and consumer finance.

Thus, India’s potential should not be read as a uniform map of projects. A regionally differentiated strategy—utility-scale parks in low-conflict high-resource zones, rooftop systems near loads, stronger transmission, storage, recycling and ecological screening—can convert physical potential into reliable and sustainable generation.

### PYQ M2 — 2020 GS-III Q16 — 15 marks

**“Describe the benefits of deriving electric energy from sunlight in contrast to the conventional energy generation. What are the initiatives offered by our Government for this purpose?” (250 words)**

**Model answer:** Solar electricity converts an indigenous, renewable flow without combustion at the point of generation. Compared with conventional fossil generation, it lowers fuel imports and operating emissions, avoids continuous fuel transport, scales from rooftop watts to utility-scale gigawatts, and can serve remote loads. Its modularity shortens construction and rooftop generation can reduce some distribution losses and consumer bills.

However, solar is variable rather than automatically firm. Large parks require land and transmission; manufacturing needs minerals and energy; cleaning may require water; and modules need end-of-life collection and recycling. Therefore the fair comparison is solar integrated with grids and storage, not a panel in isolation against a dispatchable plant.

Government action includes competitive procurement through SECI, solar parks, rooftop support including PM Surya Ghar, agricultural solarisation through PM-KUSUM, transmission expansion, renewable-purchase mechanisms and support for domestic manufacturing. Internationally, India helped establish the International Solar Alliance and promotes cross-border grid cooperation.

The next policy phase should prioritise low-conflict siting, timely grid connection, storage and demand response, consumer-friendly rooftop procedures, domestic supply-chain resilience and producer responsibility for modules. Solar’s advantage is substantial, but dependable decarbonisation arises from a complete generation–grid–storage–recycling system.

### PYQ M3 — 2021 GS-III Q6 — 10 marks

**“Explain the purpose of the Green Grid Initiative launched at World Leaders Summit of the COP26 UN Climate Change Conference in Glasgow in November, 2021. When was this idea first floated in the International Solar Alliance (ISA)?” (150 words)**

**Model answer:** The Green Grids Initiative–One Sun One World One Grid seeks interconnected regional and transnational grids capable of moving renewable electricity across geography and time zones. Solar output unavailable after sunset in one region may coexist with generation elsewhere; interconnection can therefore improve diversity, reduce curtailment and reserve requirements, and widen markets for renewable power.

The idea was first floated by India at the first Assembly of the International Solar Alliance in **October 2018**. It was jointly launched by India and the United Kingdom at the COP26 World Leaders Summit in Glasgow in November 2021.

Its promise is not automatic. Cross-border lines require harmonised grid codes, commercial settlement, cybersecurity, finance, political trust and domestic transmission/storage. The initiative should therefore complement—not substitute for—national grid strengthening, forecasting, demand response and storage. Its core purpose is to turn geographically dispersed renewable resources into a more reliable shared electricity system.

### PYQ M4 — 2022 GS-I Q7 — 10 marks

**“Examine the potential of wind energy in India and explain the reasons for their limited spatial spread.” (150 words)**

**Model answer:** India has substantial onshore wind potential in western and southern states and an emerging offshore opportunity near Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. Wind can complement solar because its seasonal and daily profile may differ, diversify rural income and provide low-operating-emission electricity without fuel imports.

Its development is spatially concentrated because viable projects require the coincidence of adequate wind speed at hub height, suitable terrain, contiguous land, road access, transmission capacity, finance and predictable clearances. Older high-resource sites may also need repowering rather than simple expansion. Offshore wind offers strong resources but needs expensive foundations, ports, subsea cables and marine/fisheries safeguards.

Ecological constraints matter: turbine placement and associated lines can affect birds, bats and sensitive grassland landscapes. Therefore limited spread does not imply wind is absent elsewhere; it reflects resource economics and infrastructure. Better resource mapping, repowering, competitive procurement, green corridors and biodiversity-sensitive siting can expand wind without treating every windy site as developable.

### PYQ M5 — 2022 GS-III Q12 — 15 marks

**“Do you think India will meet 50 percent of its energy needs from renewable energy by 2030? Justify your answer. How will the shift of subsidies from fossil fuels to renewables help achieve the above objective? Explain.” (250 words)**

**Model answer:** The question’s phrase “energy needs” must be handled carefully. India’s updated NDC refers to about 50% of cumulative installed electric-power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030, not 50% of all energy consumption from renewables. India officially announced this NDC capacity milestone as achieved five years early in July 2025, at 50.08% on 30 June 2025. By 31 August 2026, the share was 54.88%, but this does not establish the same share in generation, reliable supply or total final energy.

Progress is supported by rapid solar/wind additions, competitive procurement, rooftop and agricultural solar, transmission and storage policy. Constraints remain: variable output, land and biodiversity conflict, DISCOM finances, grid congestion, storage cost, critical-mineral dependence and continued growth in electricity demand.

Subsidy reform can help by correcting price signals, reducing incentives for inefficient fossil consumption and redirecting public finance towards grids, storage, efficiency, clean cooking/transport and worker-region transition. It can also lower investor risk for emerging technologies.

Yet abrupt withdrawal can raise inflation and energy poverty. Reform should identify producer, consumer and tax subsidies separately, protect vulnerable households through targeted transfers, and provide reliable clean alternatives before removing support.

India can exceed a non-fossil capacity-share milestone while still facing a generation and integration challenge. The objective should therefore be pursued through sequenced subsidy reform plus grid flexibility, not through headline capacity alone.

### PYQ M6 — 2025 GS-I Q6 — 10 marks

**“Explain briefly the ecological and economic benefits of solar energy generation in India with suitable examples.” (150 words)**

**Model answer:** Solar generation offers ecological gains by avoiding combustion at the point of generation, reducing conventional air pollutants and lowering lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions relative to fossil electricity. Rooftop systems use existing built surfaces, while decentralised pumps and mini-grids can replace some diesel use. Economically, solar reduces fuel-import exposure, is modular and rapidly deployable, creates installation and maintenance work, and can lower household bills under programmes such as PM Surya Ghar. Utility-scale development in Rajasthan and Gujarat demonstrates resource-based investment; Cochin International Airport illustrates institutional use.

Benefits are conditional. Large parks can fragment habitats and compete for land; modules require minerals and eventual recycling; output is variable and needs transmission, forecasting and storage. Cleaning water matters in arid zones.

Hence India should prioritise rooftops and degraded/low-conflict sites, biodiversity-sensitive project design, domestic supply chains, grid integration and producer responsibility. Solar is ecologically and economically advantageous when assessed as a lifecycle system rather than as impact-free electricity.

### PYQ M7 — 2025 GS-III Q6 — 10 marks

**“How can India achieve energy independence through clean technology by 2047? How can biotechnology can play a crucial role in this endeavour?” [sic — faithful paper wording] (150 words)**

**Model answer:** Energy independence means reducing vulnerability to imported fuels and concentrated technology supply chains, not autarky. India should combine renewable and other non-fossil generation with transmission, storage, demand response and efficiency; electrify transport and heat where practical; deploy green hydrogen in fertiliser, refining and selected steel/shipping uses; and build circular domestic supply chains for batteries, solar modules and critical minerals.

Biotechnology complements this system by converting crop residues, manure, sewage and organic waste into biogas or compressed biogas; using enzymes and microbes for advanced biofuels; improving algal and other non-food feedstocks; and producing bio-based chemicals that displace petroleum inputs.

Safeguards are essential: bioenergy must avoid food-land competition, excessive residue removal, methane leakage and local pollution. Green hydrogen awards must become certified output, while renewable capacity must become reliable generation. A diversified electricity–molecules–efficiency strategy, supported by R&D and a just transition, offers stronger independence than replacing one import dependence with another.

## PART IV — SIX ORIGINAL MAINS QUESTIONS

### O1 — 10 marks — 150-word limit

**“Installed renewable capacity is an incomplete measure of energy transition.” Explain with reference to generation and reliable supply.**

**Model answer:** Installed capacity records nameplate power capability, whereas generation records electricity actually produced over time. Reliable supply adds a third test: whether power reaches consumers when and where demand arises.

This distinction matters because solar and wind are variable. A large solar fleet can produce strongly at noon yet contribute less to an evening peak without storage or flexible demand. Generation can also be lost through transmission congestion or curtailment. Capacity factors therefore mediate the conversion of gigawatts into gigawatt-hours.

India’s 54.88% non-fossil installed-capacity share on 31 August 2026 is a major fleet-composition milestone, but it is not evidence of an identical generation share or round-the-clock decarbonisation.

Policy must therefore track generation, peak contribution, curtailment and emissions alongside capacity, while investing in forecasting, interstate transmission, demand response, batteries, pumped storage and other firming resources. The transition succeeds when clean capacity becomes dependable low-carbon service, not when nameplate megawatts merely accumulate.

### O2 — 10 marks — 150-word limit

**Why should direct electrification generally precede green hydrogen in sectoral decarbonisation? Identify important exceptions.**

**Model answer:** Direct electrification converts renewable electricity into useful work with fewer stages. Producing hydrogen requires electrolysis, followed by compression, storage or transport; reconversion to electricity adds further losses. Hence efficient motors, heat pumps and many battery vehicles normally use scarce clean electricity better than a hydrogen chain.

Hydrogen becomes valuable where a molecule or reducing agent is required, or where weight, range and high-temperature constraints make direct electricity difficult. Priority uses include replacing grey hydrogen in fertiliser and refining, hydrogen-based direct-reduced iron in suitable steel plants, and selected shipping fuels such as green ammonia or methanol. Some long-haul transport and seasonal storage may qualify after comparison with alternatives.

This hierarchy is not a ban on innovation. Technology, grid and route conditions vary. However, policy should compare full-system efficiency, infrastructure, safety and lifecycle emissions before subsidising a use. Green hydrogen is best treated as a scarce hard-to-abate solution, not a universal fuel.

**Word count:** 150

### O3 — 15 marks — 250-word limit

**Evaluate the environmental trade-offs of scaling solar, wind, hydro and bioenergy in India.**

**Model answer:** Renewable energy lowers fossil-fuel combustion and import exposure, but each source has a distinct lifecycle footprint.

Solar is modular and low-emitting in operation. Rooftops use built space, yet utility parks can convert habitats and require transmission; module manufacturing uses minerals, and end-of-life systems must recover glass, aluminium and valuable materials. Wind avoids fuel combustion and may complement solar, but roads, turbines and lines affect landscapes, birds and bats. Offshore wind adds fisheries, seabed and cable impacts.

Hydropower can provide dispatchable energy and storage, strengthening a variable-renewable grid. Its costs include displacement, altered environmental flows, sediment trapping, aquatic fragmentation and, in some reservoirs, methane. Pumped storage is valuable but remains site-specific. Biomass and biogas can convert residues and wastes into dispatchable energy and rural income. Their merit disappears where feedstock drives land-use change, food competition, excessive residue removal, methane leakage or local air pollution.

The common policy error is to treat “renewable” as “impact-free.” India needs strategic environmental assessment, cumulative-impact analysis, species-sensitivity maps, community participation and the mitigation hierarchy. Rooftops, brownfields and low-conflict sites should precede ecologically valuable grasslands. Circular-design and producer-responsibility rules should accompany manufacturing.

Thus, the choice is not renewables versus environment. It is between poorly planned projects and a diversified, spatially intelligent portfolio that reduces global emissions while protecting local ecosystems and livelihoods.

**Word count:** 215

### O4 — 15 marks — 250-word limit

**Discuss the credibility architecture required for India’s green-hydrogen market.**

**Model answer:** Hydrogen has no inherent colour; credibility rests on the production pathway and measured emissions. India’s Green Hydrogen Standard sets a maximum well-to-gate intensity of 2 kg CO₂-equivalent per kg H₂, averaged over the preceding 12 months, covering water treatment, production, purification, drying and compression.

Four layers must remain distinct. First, the label communicates a claim. Second, the standard defines eligibility and threshold. Third, a methodology attributes emissions from electricity, biomass and treatment. Fourth, the Green Hydrogen Certification Scheme uses producer data, Accredited Carbon Verifiers, technical review and certificates through a digital portal. BEE has the nodal accreditation role.

Renewable-energy banking is allowed and supports electrolyser utilisation, but accounting flexibility creates questions. Annual matching may not show that electricity was renewable in the hour and location of consumption. Nor does it prove additionality—whether hydrogen demand caused new renewable supply rather than diverted existing clean power. Unmatched grid electricity must be reflected in emissions.

Credibility also requires water-risk disclosure, biomass-sustainability rules, metering, auditable chain of custody, penalties for misreporting and interoperability with export standards. Certification should report product carbon footprint rather than offer a timeless blanket label.

India’s framework is an important market foundation. Its durable advantage will depend on transparent data and progressively stronger temporal, locational and additionality rules, while retaining workable transition pathways.

### O5 — 20 marks — 300-word limit

**India’s next renewable-energy challenge is integration rather than installation. Critically analyse.**

**Model answer:** India has moved rapidly from renewable scarcity to a power system in which non-fossil sources formed 54.88% of installed capacity on 31 August 2026. This makes “integration rather than installation” a powerful thesis, though continued capacity addition remains necessary for demand growth and the 500 GW non-fossil goal.

Integration has five dimensions. First, variable solar and wind need accurate forecasting, scheduling, reserves and flexible dispatch. Second, transmission must move power from resource-rich regions to demand centres; congestion otherwise causes curtailment. Third, demand response should shift flexible loads towards renewable-rich hours. Fourth, batteries provide fast response and intraday shifting, while pumped storage can offer longer duration where ecology and geography permit. Fifth, markets and contracts must value ramping, ancillary services and firm delivery rather than energy alone.

The challenge is also ecological and industrial. Solar parks, wind corridors and transmission lines require low-conflict siting and cumulative-impact assessment, illustrated by the Great Indian Bustard landscape. Batteries and generators intensify critical-mineral, manufacturing and recycling needs. DISCOM finances and affordable tariffs determine whether technically available flexibility is procured.

Yet installation cannot be neglected. Electrification, data centres, cooling and industrial growth will raise demand; green hydrogen adds large electricity requirements. The correct proposition is therefore integration **with** accelerated, better-sited capacity.

India should adopt resource-adequacy planning, competitive renewable-plus-storage procurement, green-energy corridors, time-responsive tariffs, storage safety/recycling standards and transparent curtailment data. Firm low-carbon portfolios may combine VRE, hydro, storage, demand response and other non-fossil resources.

The transition’s unit of success must shift from megawatts installed to clean megawatt-hours reliably delivered, with ecological and social costs internalised.

**Word count:** 255

### O6 — 20 marks — 300-word limit

**Assess the National Green Hydrogen Mission as an instrument of industrial decarbonisation and energy security.**

**Model answer:** Approved on 4 January 2023 with an initial outlay of ₹19,744 crore, the National Green Hydrogen Mission combines climate policy with industrial strategy. Its 2030 aspiration is at least 5 MMT annual production capacity, about 125 GW associated renewable addition and nearly 50 MMT annual greenhouse-gas abatement. These are targets or expected outcomes, not current achievements.

The Mission’s strongest design feature is SIGHT’s dual structure: incentives for domestic electrolyser manufacturing and for hydrogen production/procurement. Official updates report 3,000 MW per year of manufacturing capacity awarded and 862,000 tonnes per year of production capacity selected/awarded. PIB reported only about 8,000 tonnes per year commissioned by February 2026, demonstrating why awards cannot be called output.

Strategically, green hydrogen can replace imported fossil-based hydrogen and gas in fertiliser and refining, support suitable steel pathways, and create green-ammonia/methanol opportunities for shipping and trade. Domestic electrolysers, ports, pipelines, storage and standards may build new value chains.

Risks are substantial. Hydrogen is generally less energy-efficient than direct electrification; electricity demand could divert scarce renewable supply without additionality; water stress and desalination impacts are local; compression, conversion and transport are costly; and export markets may require tighter temporal and locational matching. Safety, critical minerals and skilled operation require regulation.

Policy should prioritise existing grey-hydrogen replacement and hard-to-abate sectors, link incentives to commissioned certified output, disclose electricity and water attributes, secure offtake, support R&D and recycling, and avoid indiscriminate transport subsidies.

The Mission can advance energy security, but only if domestic manufacturing and tendered capacity mature into cost-competitive, verifiably low-emission production rather than remaining an allocation-led success narrative.
