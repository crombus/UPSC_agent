> **Subject:** Environment and Ecology | **Topic:** 16 | **Level:** Basic first, optional Advanced last
> **Legally verified:** 29 September 2026
> **Core sources:** canonical Basic + Advanced owners; EIA Notification, 2006 and notified amendments; NGT Act, 2010 and Practice Rules; local official UPSC papers; cited Supreme Court judgments.

## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Central question, roadmap and answer thesis

**Central question:** How should India decide whether a project may proceed, under what safeguards, and what remedy follows when the decision or its implementation causes legally cognisable environmental harm?

```text
PROPOSED ACTIVITY
      |
      v
Is it in the EIA Schedule? -> category/conditions -> prior-EC route
      |
      v
SCREEN -> SCOPE -> STUDY -> CONSULT -> APPRAISE -> DECIDE
                    |                      |
                    v                      v
        baseline, impacts,        grant / conditions /
        alternatives, EMP        rejection with reasons
                    |
                    v
          MONITOR + COMPLY + CORRECT
                    |
          dispute / damage / appeal
                    v
     NGT: ss.14/15/16 -> s.20 principles -> relief/order
                    |
        review / SC appeal / execution / penalty
```

**Answer-grabbing line:** *EIA and the NGT are a preventive-corrective pair: EIA must preserve environmental choice before irreversible commitment, while the NGT supplies specialised adjudication, relief and restitution within defined statutory boundaries.*

**Must-write keywords:** prior EC; Schedule; Category A/B1/B2; screening; scoping; baseline; alternatives; mitigation hierarchy; EMP; public consultation; reasoned appraisal; compliance; substantial environmental question; Schedule I; restitution; precaution; polluter pays.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** EIA is decision-forming, not permission-producing. **Evidence:** paragraph 2 requires prior EC for scheduled new projects and qualifying change before construction/land preparation, except securing land. **Analysis:** the rule retains a real choice among rejection, relocation, redesign and conditions. **Qualification:** EC does not replace pollution consent, forest, wildlife, CRZ, land or rights-based approvals.

### 2. Institutional origin and legal timeline

| Date | Development | Exam significance |
|---|---|---|
| June 1972 | Stockholm Conference, recorded in the NGT Act preamble | International stimulus for domestic environmental protection |
| 1976 | Forty-second Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 48A and Article 51A(g) | State and citizen environmental duties |
| 1986 | Environment (Protection) Act | Umbrella authority under which EIA notifications operate |
| 27 January 1994 | First general EIA notification | Prior-clearance architecture begins |
| 14 September 2006 | S.O. 1533(E) superseded the 1994 notification | Current base notification, as amended |
| 2 June / 18 October 2010 | NGT Act enacted / brought into force | Specialist environmental tribunal |
| 14 March 2017 | S.O. 804(E), time-bound violation window | Later examined in ex-post-facto jurisprudence |
| 12 April 2022 | S.O. 1807(E) amended EC validity | Current differentiated validity periods |
| 29 July 2026 | *Vanashakti*, 2026 INSC 761 | Current rule-exception position on post-facto EC |

**Answer-grabbing line:** *India's environmental rule of law evolved from an executive clearance process into a constitutional, statutory and specialist-adjudicatory system; each layer has a different legal function.*

**Must-write keywords:** EPA 1986; S.O. 1533(E); delegated legislation; NGT Act; Article 21; Article 48A; Article 51A(g); instrument hierarchy.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** A current EIA answer must identify the controlling instrument. **Evidence:** the 2020 EIA text remained a draft; the 2006 notification continued, subject to notified amendments. **Analysis:** a proposal, office memorandum or platform feature cannot silently amend a notification. **Qualification:** courts may construe legality, but sector-specific amendments must still be checked for the project concerned.

### 3. EIA: concept, purpose and decision logic

**Rule/fact:** EIA is a structured ex-ante inquiry into likely environmental consequences of a proposed action before approval. Under paragraph 4, A/B categorisation reflects the spatial extent of potential impacts and effects on human health and natural and human-made resources.

| EIA function | Question it should answer | Failure if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| Problem definition | What need is the project serving? | Preferred design treated as inevitable |
| Baseline | What exists before the project? | No reliable counterfactual |
| Prediction | What changes, where, for whom and for how long? | Conditions become generic |
| Alternatives | Can need be met at another site, scale, design or time? | Avoidable harm becomes “mitigated” |
| Mitigation/EMP | Who will avoid, reduce, restore and monitor harm? | Promises are not auditable |
| Participation | What local knowledge, rights and exposure were missed? | Information and legitimacy deficit |
| Appraisal | Does evidence justify grant, conditions or refusal? | Paper completion replaces judgment |
| Monitoring | Did prediction and mitigation work? | Clearance becomes a one-time event |

**Purpose hierarchy**

```text
INFORM NEED AND ALTERNATIVES
          ↓
AVOID UNACCEPTABLE SITES / DESIGNS
          ↓
MINIMISE AND RESTORE MANAGEABLE EFFECTS
          ↓
CONDITION OR REFUSE WITH REASONS
          ↓
VERIFY OUTCOMES AND CORRECT FAILURE
```

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The quality of EIA is measured by decisions changed and harm avoided, not by pages produced.*

**Must-write keywords:** ex ante; counterfactual; receptor; significance; uncertainty; reversibility; distribution; decision space.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** EIA protects decision space. **Evidence:** prior EC is required before construction or project-led land preparation. **Analysis:** after sunk costs arise, the no-project and alternative-site options become politically and financially harder. **Qualification:** EIA should facilitate sustainable development, not create a presumption that either development or conservation always wins.

### 4. The legal gate: Schedule, category, authority and project logic

#### 4.1 Start with the Schedule

For every problem, ask in this order:

1. Is the project/activity listed?
2. What capacity, area, length or other threshold applies?
3. Is it new, expansion, modernisation or product-mix change?
4. Which column—A or B—covers it?
5. Does the General Condition alter B to A?
6. Does the Specific Condition apply?
7. Have later sector-specific amendments changed the row?
8. Which other approvals remain independently necessary?

**Paragraph 2 triggers**

- all new projects/activities listed in the Schedule;
- listed expansion/modernisation crossing the relevant threshold after change;
- specified product-mix change in an existing scheduled manufacturing unit beyond the prescribed range.

Prior EC must precede construction or preparation of land by project management, except securing land.

#### 4.2 Category and actors

| Route | Appraisal body | Decision authority | Screening? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category A | EAC | Central Government/MoEFCC | No |
| Category B1 | SEAC | SEIAA | Yes, as Category B |
| Category B2 | Applicable B2 route | SEIAA/competent route | Yes or classification under current applicable provisions; no EIA report under paragraph 7's design |

**Critical correction:** if a duly constituted SEIAA or SEAC is absent, the amended text says a Category B project is considered **at the central level as a Category B project**—not automatically converted into Category A.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *EAC/SEAC supplies expert recommendation; MoEFCC/SEIAA performs the legal act of grant or refusal.*

**Must-write keywords:** threshold; nature and location specificity; recommendation; regulatory authority; central-level Category B fallback.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** Category is a legal routing device, not a moral ranking. **Evidence:** B is divided through screening into B1 and B2. **Analysis:** location can alter risk, so the General Condition may elevate a B proposal. **Qualification:** labels and thresholds are amendment-sensitive; never quote a sector threshold without checking the current Schedule.

### 5. General Condition and Specific Condition—use only the notified meanings

#### 5.1 General Condition (Schedule text)

> Any project or activity specified in Category B will be treated as Category A if located wholly or partly within **10 km** from the boundary of:
> (i) Protected Areas notified under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972;
> (ii) critically polluted areas identified by CPCB from time to time;
> (iii) eco-sensitive areas notified under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; and
> (iv) inter-State and international boundaries.
> The notification contains a proviso allowing the 10 km inter-State-boundary requirement to be reduced or removed by agreement between the concerned States/UTs where the activity is not within 10 km of (i)–(iii).

#### 5.2 Specific Condition (Schedule text and meaning)

> If any Industrial Estate/Complex / Export processing Zones /Special Economic Zones/Biotech Parks / Leather Complex with homogeneous type of industries such as Items 4(d), 4(f), 5(e), 5(f), or those Industrial estates with pre-defined set of activities (not necessarily homogeneous), obtains prior environmental clearance, individual industries including proposed industrial housing within such estates/complexes will not be required to take prior environmental clearance, so long as the Terms and Conditions for the industrial estate/complex are complied with. Such estates/complexes must have a clearly identified management with legal responsibility for adherence throughout the life of the complex/estate.

**Do not confuse:** Schedule **Specific Condition (SC)** ≠ the project-specific conditions written into an individual EC letter.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The General Condition changes the appraisal level because of sensitive location; the Specific Condition reallocates clearance responsibility inside a qualifying pre-cleared estate.*

**Must-write keywords:** 10 km; whole or part; protected area; critically polluted area; eco-sensitive area; inter-State/international boundary; identified management; lifetime compliance.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** GC and SC solve different governance problems. **Evidence:** GC elevates a B project, while SC can avoid duplicative individual EC inside a qualifying estate. **Analysis:** one responds to sensitivity; the other to integrated estate management. **Qualification:** neither removes separate pollution, safety or other legal obligations.

### 6. The official stages and the complete technical assessment spine

#### 6.1 Two layers must be kept distinct

```text
LEGAL MAXIMUM FOUR STAGES
Screening -> Scoping -> Public Consultation -> Appraisal

TECHNICAL EIA WORK INSIDE THAT ROUTE
ToR -> baseline -> predict -> alternatives -> significance
    -> avoid/minimise/restore/offset -> EMP
    -> consultation response -> final EIA -> appraisal
```

#### 6.2 Screening

Only Category B undergoes screening. SEAC examines Form 1 and relevant material to determine whether further environmental study/EIA is needed. B1 requires an EIA report; B2 does not.

**Trap:** B2 is not shorthand for “environmentally harmless.”

#### 6.3 Scoping

EAC for Category A and SEAC for B1 determines comprehensive Terms of Reference addressing relevant concerns, using the application, suggested ToR, available information and a site visit where considered necessary. The notification gives item-8 construction/township projects a special route without ordinary scoping.

**Good ToR asks:** spatial/seasonal boundary; valued receptors; alternatives; cumulative/induced effects; climate/disaster risk; social/health/biodiversity pathways; methods; uncertainty; closure.

#### 6.4 Baseline

A baseline is the pre-project condition against which change is predicted and monitored.

| Dimension | Minimum design discipline |
|---|---|
| Air/noise/water/soil | Representative stations, season, method and detection limits |
| Ecology | Seasonal presence, habitat, connectivity, breeding/migration and ecosystem services |
| Social | Land/livelihood use, vulnerability, culture, gender and customary dependence |
| Health | Existing disease/exposure profile and sensitive groups |
| Disaster/climate | Flood, slope, cyclone, heat, drought and compound risk where relevant |

**Quality failures:** cherry-picked season; too small study area; copied data; inaccessible raw data; no uncertainty; changed project design after sampling.

#### 6.5 Impact prediction and significance

Prediction connects a project pressure to a receptor:

```text
SOURCE -> PATHWAY -> RECEPTOR -> MAGNITUDE/DURATION
       -> REVERSIBILITY -> DISTRIBUTION -> SIGNIFICANCE
```

Assess direct, indirect, induced, cumulative, accidental and closure effects. State assumptions and confidence; precaution is most relevant where harm may be serious or irreversible and certainty is incomplete.

#### 6.6 Alternatives

At minimum test:

- no-project;
- alternative site/alignment;
- scale/capacity;
- technology/process/fuel;
- layout and timing;
- demand-management or non-structural solution.

Record why each credible alternative was rejected. A token “two equipment brands” comparison is not sufficient.

#### 6.7 Mitigation hierarchy

```text
1 AVOID -> 2 MINIMISE -> 3 RESTORE/REHABILITATE
                     -> 4 OFFSET/COMPENSATE RESIDUAL IMPACT
```

Offset is last. Unique habitat, sacred/cultural landscapes, extinction risk and irreversible hydrological functions may not be commensurable.

#### 6.8 Environmental Management Plan

An EMP must turn predictions into:

| Action | Responsible person | Time | Budget | Indicator | Trigger/correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Cover construction, operation, emergency and closure. Include monitoring location/frequency, disclosure, grievance handling and adaptive correction.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The legal four-stage process is the decision shell; baseline, prediction, alternatives, mitigation and EMP are the evidence engine inside it.*

**Must-write keywords:** ToR; counterfactual; source-pathway-receptor; significance; no-project; residual impact; adaptive management.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** EIA quality is path-dependent. **Evidence:** weak scoping narrows baseline and prediction; weak alternatives pushes all harm into mitigation. **Analysis:** each error propagates into EMP and compliance. **Qualification:** assessment effort should be risk-proportionate, but proportionality requires evidence—not a label.

### 7. Public consultation: law, exemptions, access, quality and conflict

#### 7.1 Legal purpose and components

Public consultation ascertains concerns of locally affected persons and others with a plausible stake so material concerns can shape design. It ordinarily includes:

1. a site/near-site, district-wise public hearing conducted by SPCB/UTPCC; and
2. written responses from other concerned persons.

After consultation, the proponent must address material concerns and revise the EIA/EMP or submit a supplementary response.

#### 7.2 Exact exemption set in the consolidated amended text

- modernisation of irrigation projects under the stated Schedule item;
- qualifying projects within approved industrial estates/parks not disallowed by that approval;
- road/highway expansion not involving further land acquisition;
- maintenance dredging where dredged material is disposed within port limits;
- specified building/construction, area-development and township projects under items 8(a)/(b);
- all B2 projects/activities;
- national defence/security or other strategic considerations as determined by the Central Government.

An exceptional finding that free local expression is impossible may permit consultation without the hearing after the prescribed report and regulatory consideration.

#### 7.3 Access design under Appendix IV

- draft EIA plus summary in English and official State/local language;
- copies/access through specified district/local/industry/regional offices;
- one major national daily and one regional vernacular/official-language daily;
- minimum **30 days** for responses;
- alternative publicity where newspapers do not reach;
- DM/DC/Deputy Commissioner or qualifying representative presides, assisted by SPCB/UTPCC;
- videography; no quorum requirement;
- minutes read in local/vernacular language, issue-response statement in local/official language and English, and public display.

#### 7.4 Why implementation can still fail

| Problem | Why it matters | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Dense technical report | Formal disclosure without comprehension | Plain-language, local-language, map/data summary |
| Remote venue/office | Excludes affected hamlets, women, workers | Multiple access points, transport/hybrid submissions |
| Late consultation | Sunk-cost pressure closes alternatives | Engage before design is frozen |
| Proponent-funded consultant | Incentive/conflict risk | Conflict declaration, accreditation, independent audit |
| Minutes without reasoned response | Voice recorded but not used | Public issue-response matrix and committee reasons |
| Changed project after hearing | Consultation addresses old design | Re-consult material change where law requires |
| Intimidation or unequal voice | Distorts evidence and legitimacy | Safe process, neutral chair, auditable record |

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Participation is meaningful only when people can understand the proposal, speak safely and see how material concerns altered appraisal.*

**Must-write keywords:** plausible stake; district-wise hearing; written response; 30-day notice; local language; issue-response matrix; consultant conflict; reasoned treatment.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** the notification's access design is stronger than many hearings in practice. **Evidence:** it requires local-language summary, notice, access, videography and response. **Analysis:** technical opacity and late timing can still neutralise participation. **Qualification:** consultation supplies evidence and legitimacy, but does not create an automatic local veto.

### 8. Appraisal, EC decision and reason-giving

Appraisal is detailed scrutiny of the application, final EIA, consultation outcome and other validated information. The proponent may clarify. EAC/SEAC must make a categorical recommendation—grant with stipulated conditions or rejection—with reasons. The regulatory authority normally accepts the recommendation; disagreement triggers the notification's reconsideration route.

**Decision matrix**

| Finding | Proper response |
|---|---|
| Impact avoidable by another alternative | Require redesign/relocation or reject preferred option |
| Manageable with enforceable controls | Conditional EC with measurable EMP |
| Baseline/material issue unresolved | Seek further study; do not assume no impact |
| Serious/irreversible risk beyond mitigation | Refuse or defer |
| False/material concealment | Rejection/cancellation after natural justice |

**EC is not:**

- a pollution consent;
- a forest/wildlife/CRZ approval;
- proof that every prediction is correct;
- immunity from later challenge, monitoring or liability.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Reasoned appraisal is the bridge between environmental information and lawful administrative choice.*

**Must-write keywords:** detailed scrutiny; validated information; categorical recommendation; stipulated condition; reasons; natural justice.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** reason-giving is substantive. **Evidence:** *Hanuman Laxman Aroskar* required renewed expert consideration where material appraisal concerns were not adequately engaged. **Analysis:** reasons reveal whether alternatives, consultation and safeguards actually influenced the decision. **Qualification:** courts review legality and reasoning; they do not ordinarily perform the committee's scientific appraisal themselves.

### 9. After EC: expansion, validity, transfer, amendment, monitoring and compliance

#### 9.1 Expansion/modernisation/product mix

Applications increasing production beyond approved capacity, increasing mining lease area/production, modernising beyond Schedule thresholds or changing product mix go to EAC/SEAC, which determines due diligence—including whether EIA and public consultation are needed—before appraisal. A proponent cannot self-certify that a material change is “only an amendment.”

#### 9.2 Current validity under S.O. 1807(E), 12 April 2022

| Project class | Base validity |
|---|---:|
| River-valley | 13 years |
| Nuclear power / nuclear-fuel processing | 15 years |
| Other non-mining projects | 10 years |
| Mining | Approved mining-plan life, subject initially to 30 years; conditional extensions can reach the earlier of lease/life/50 years with five-year safeguard review beyond 30 |

Limited extensions: river-valley up to 2 years; nuclear up to 5; other non-mining up to 1, on timely application and regulatory process. “Validity” is the defined period to start production or complete construction; it is not the operating life of every condition.

#### 9.3 Transfer

During validity, EC may be transferred to another entitled legal person on application by the transferor or the transferee with written no-objection from the transferor. It remains on the same terms and validity; no EAC/SEAC reference is necessary under paragraph 11.

#### 9.4 Amendment

Treat amendment as a legal classification question:

- Does the change cross paragraph 2 or paragraph 7(ii)?
- Does it alter impact area, capacity, product mix, technology, pollution load or public-consultation basis?
- Does current sector-specific law require committee appraisal or additional study?

No material project change is valid merely because the proponent calls it an amendment.

#### 9.5 Monitoring and compliance

Paragraph 10 requires publicity of EC and conditions. Project management submits half-yearly compliance reports on **1 June and 1 December**; the reports are public documents and the latest is displayed by the authority. Good monitoring compares baseline/prediction with observed outcome, not merely expenditure or installed equipment.

```text
CONDITION -> INDICATOR -> DATA -> PUBLIC REPORT
       -> DEVIATION? -> CORRECT / ENFORCE / MODIFY / PROSECUTE
```

**Answer-grabbing line:** *An EC is a continuing bundle of enforceable conditions, not a one-day administrative event.*

**Must-write keywords:** threshold-crossing change; validity; same terms; material amendment; half-yearly; public document; adaptive correction.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** post-clearance monitoring closes the EIA feedback loop. **Evidence:** paragraph 10 mandates recurring public compliance reports. **Analysis:** outcome data tests both prediction and mitigation. **Qualification:** self-reporting is useful but needs risk-based inspection, independent verification and enforceable correction.

### 10. Ex-post-facto and violation jurisprudence—current position

#### 10.1 Why retrospective EC is controversial

Assessment after construction cannot fully recover:

- the no-project choice;
- genuine site/alignment alternatives;
- pre-disturbance baseline;
- timely local participation;
- prevention of irreversible loss.

#### 10.2 Case line

| Case/instrument | Sourced proposition |
|---|---|
| *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2017) | Mining without required EC attracted illegality and compensation consequences |
| S.O. 804(E), 14 March 2017 | Narrow, time-bound violation window |
| *Alembic Pharmaceuticals v. Rohit Prajapati* (2020) | Ex-post-facto EC described as alien to environmental jurisprudence under the prior-EC regime |
| *Electrosteel Steels* (2021) | Fact-specific observations suggested exceptional regularisation; later corrected in 2026 on interpretation of the 2006 regime |
| *Pahwa Plastics* / *D. Swamy* | Addressed consequences in the context of 2017 notification/2021 OM |
| 2021 Office Memorandum | Perpetual administrative violation route; later quashed prospectively |
| *Vanashakti v. Union of India*, 2026 INSC 761 | Current controlling rule-exception synthesis |

#### 10.3 Exact current status after *Vanashakti* (29 July 2026)

1. Prior EC under the 2006 notification is mandatory; that notification itself does not accommodate post-facto EC.
2. A narrowly tailored, time-bound amnesty may be created only through valid delegated legislation under section 3 EPA where constitutionally sustainable—not by a perpetual office memorandum.
3. The 2017 notification was upheld as such a narrow, time-bound measure.
4. The 2021 OM was quashed **prospectively**.
5. ECs already granted under the 2017 notification/2021 OM remain valid unless challenged on merits.
6. Pending applications may reach lawful conclusion; certain matters dismissed only because of the earlier stay/judgment may be reconsidered.
7. **No fresh application** under the 2017 notification or 2021 OM may be entertained.
8. Government is restrained from future administrative orders granting ex-post EC for projects begun contrary to prior EC; a valid notification and the Supreme Court's Article 142 power remain legally distinct exceptions.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Current law makes prior EC the rule, rejects perpetual executive regularisation, and recognises only narrowly lawful exceptional mechanisms without reopening the 2017/2021 windows.*

**Must-write keywords:** prior mandatory; prevention; delegated legislation; office memorandum; prospective quashing; no fresh applications; Article 142.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** the 2026 judgment is neither an absolute timeless ban nor routine permission. **Evidence:** it upheld the closed 2017 window, prospectively quashed the 2021 OM and barred fresh applications. **Analysis:** legal form, time-boundedness, classification and public interest distinguish exception from business-as-usual amnesty. **Qualification:** an existing clearance remains individually challengeable on merits.

### 11. Beyond project EIA: strategic, cumulative, carrying-capacity, social, health and biodiversity lenses

| Lens | Unit of analysis | What project EIA can miss |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic environmental assessment | Policy, plan, programme | Whether the development pathway itself is sound |
| Cumulative impact assessment | Cluster, basin, corridor, airshed; past/present/foreseeable pressures | Combined load and threshold crossing |
| Carrying-capacity assessment | Ecological/social system | Maximum use compatible with acceptable condition |
| Social impact assessment | People, institutions, rights, culture, livelihoods | Distribution, displacement and non-market loss |
| Health impact assessment | Exposure pathway and population | Vulnerable groups, chronic/combined exposure |
| Biodiversity assessment | Genes/species/habitat/connectivity/ecosystem services | Seasonality, fragmentation and irreplaceability |

**Recurring limits**

- project boundary smaller than impact boundary;
- one-season or stale baseline;
- consultant conflict and copied reports;
- alternatives treated as formality;
- induced development and supply-chain effects omitted;
- climate, disaster and closure risk weakly integrated;
- offsets assumed commensurable;
- conditions not linked to finance, indicators or enforcement;
- approval data fragmented across authorities.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Project EIA asks whether one proposal is acceptable; strategic and cumulative tools ask whether the wider development pattern remains within ecological and social limits.*

**Must-write keywords:** tiering; landscape; combined load; threshold; distribution; exposure; connectivity; ecosystem services.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** tiered assessment avoids project-by-project fragmentation. **Evidence:** mining clusters, river basins, mountain tourism and transport corridors generate interacting pressures. **Analysis:** a project EMP cannot repair an already exceeded regional carrying capacity. **Qualification:** broader assessments supplement—not replace—project EC and separate rights/clearance duties.

### 12. NGT establishment, composition and benches

#### 12.1 Establishment and purpose

Section 3 authorises the Central Government to establish the National Green Tribunal. It came into force on 18 October 2010. The preamble emphasises effective and expeditious disposal of environmental protection, forest/natural-resource, legal-right and relief/compensation matters involving multidisciplinary issues.

#### 12.2 Composition

Section 4:

- full-time Chairperson;
- 10–20 full-time Judicial Members;
- 10–20 full-time Expert Members;
- specialist assistance may be invited in a case;
- a hearing bench must have equal numbers of Judicial and Expert Members.

#### 12.3 Benches

| Institutional location | Place |
|---|---|
| Principal Bench | New Delhi |
| Central Zone | Bhopal |
| Eastern Zone | Kolkata |
| Southern Zone | Chennai |
| Western Zone | Pune |

Circuit procedure permits sitting elsewhere under the rules; it does not create a permanent bench in every State.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The NGT's distinctive design places legal and scientific expertise inside the adjudicating bench rather than leaving science as an occasional external input.*

**Must-write keywords:** specialised statutory tribunal; multidisciplinary; judicial-expert parity; ordinary place of sitting; circuit procedure.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** composition responds to environmental complexity. **Evidence:** section 4 mandates Judicial and Expert Members and parity on hearing benches. **Analysis:** technical questions are considered within adjudication. **Qualification:** expertise does not relax natural justice or statutory jurisdiction.

### 13. NGT sections 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20 and 22

| Section | Core function | Limitation / distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **14** | Original civil jurisdiction over a substantial environmental question, including legal environmental rights, arising from Schedule I enactments | 6 months from first cause of action + maximum 60 days |
| **15** | Relief/compensation to victims; restitution of property; restitution of environment; additional to PLI Act relief | 5 years from cause + maximum 60 days |
| **16** | Appeal against enumerated statutory orders/directions/determinations, including EC grant/refusal | 30 days from communication + maximum 60 days |
| **18** | Form and broad standing: injured person, owner, legal representative, agent, any aggrieved person/representative body/organisation, governments and listed authorities | Broad standing does not erase sections 14–16 |
| **19** | Natural justice; own procedure; not bound by CPC/Evidence Act; specified civil-court powers, review, interim orders and cease-desist | Flexibility remains subject to fair hearing |
| **20** | Apply sustainable development, precautionary principle and polluter pays | Guides order/award within jurisdiction |
| **22** | Appeal from NGT to Supreme Court on section 100 CPC grounds | 90 days from communication; SC may condone for sufficient cause |

**Section 18(3):** endeavour to dispose finally within six months after hearing opportunity. This is an endeavour clause, not automatic invalidation after six months.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *NGT forum selection requires a five-part test: cause of action, Schedule I nexus, original versus appellate door, relief sought and limitation clock.*

**Must-write keywords:** substantial question; first arose; enumerated appeal; broad standing; natural justice; statutory principles; section 100 CPC grounds.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** environmental urgency does not create unbounded jurisdiction. **Evidence:** sections 14 and 16 use Schedule/enumeration gates and distinct clocks. **Analysis:** precise pleading protects both access and legal certainty. **Qualification:** continuing harm can raise fact-specific cause-of-action questions; never assert an automatic limitation reset.

### 14. Schedule I, standing, remedies and jurisdiction boundaries

#### 14.1 Printed Schedule I

1. Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974
2. Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977
3. Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980—now titled the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980
4. Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981
5. Environment (Protection) Act, 1986
6. Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991
7. Biological Diversity Act, 2002

**Current-status caution:** the Water Cess Act has been repealed, although it remains in the printed Schedule I text. The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and Indian Forest Act, 1927 are **not** listed.

#### 14.2 Standing and relief

Broad standing under section 18 supports affected persons and representative organisations. Relief can include:

- victim compensation;
- damaged-property restitution;
- environmental restitution;
- interim stay/injunction;
- cease-and-desist order;
- modification/quashing of appealable decisions where law permits;
- costs and directions necessary to effectuate orders.

#### 14.3 Boundaries

- A wildlife issue may reach NGT when the legal dispute also arises under a Schedule I enactment; the Wildlife Act alone is not a Schedule I source.
- Criminal prosecution under environmental laws is distinct from NGT's civil/appellate role.
- Constitutional writ jurisdiction of High Courts/Supreme Court remains.
- Section 20 principles cannot manufacture missing jurisdiction.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *NGT standing is broad, but subject-matter jurisdiction is statute-linked.*

**Must-write keywords:** printed Schedule; repealed enactment; renamed enactment; representative body; restitution; statutory nexus; constitutional review.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** access and jurisdiction must be separated. **Evidence:** section 18 permits representative organisations, while section 14 still requires Schedule I linkage. **Analysis:** broad standing solves who may approach, not what the Tribunal may decide. **Qualification:** mixed disputes require identifying the precise statutory source of each relief.

### 15. Original vs appellate jurisdiction; review, appeal, execution and “contempt”

#### 15.1 Forum fork

```text
ONGOING/HARM-BASED CIVIL DISPUTE?
 -> s.14 (+ s.15 remedy), 6 months / 5 years as applicable

SPECIFIED ORDER/DIRECTION/EC CHALLENGED?
 -> s.16 appeal, 30 + 60 days

ERROR IN NGT'S OWN ORDER?
 -> s.19(4)(f) review + Rule 22, ordinarily 30 days

CHALLENGE TO NGT ORDER?
 -> s.22 Supreme Court appeal, 90 days

ORDER NOT OBEYED?
 -> s.25 execution / recovery; s.26 prosecution where ingredients proved
```

#### 15.2 Review

Section 19(4)(f) gives review power. Rule 22 requires filing within **30 days from receipt of the order copy**, ordinarily before the same place of sitting; one review only; sworn affidavit required.

#### 15.3 Appeal

Section 22 appeal goes to the Supreme Court, not routinely to a High Court appellate bench, though constitutional judicial review remains conceptually distinct.

#### 15.4 Execution

Section 25 makes an NGT award/order/decision executable as a civil-court decree. NGT may transmit it to a civil court. Unpaid directed amounts may be recovered as arrears of land revenue/public demand, without prejudice to prosecution.

#### 15.5 Penalty is not a loose synonym for contempt

Section 26 creates a specific penal offence for failure to comply; offences are non-cognisable. In *Dr. I.S. Tomar/Umesh Pratap Singh* (23 May 2025), the Supreme Court treated section 26 strictly: personal liability required responsibility/power to comply, and individual punishment could not stand without the necessary finding; the municipal corporation's continuing monetary direction was not otherwise disturbed.

NGT is not exercising the Supreme Court's Article 129 or High Courts' Article 215 constitutional contempt jurisdiction merely because non-compliance is alleged. Use **review**, **appeal**, **execution**, **section 26 prosecution** and **constitutional contempt** as separate labels.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *An NGT order is corrected by review/appeal, implemented by section 25 and penalised under section 26 only on proved statutory ingredients—not by calling every dispute “contempt.”*

**Must-write keywords:** Rule 22; 30 days; section 100 CPC; decree; land revenue; strict construction; responsibility; wilful default.

**How to write the paragraph:** **Claim:** enforcement has civil and penal tracks. **Evidence:** section 25 supplies execution, while section 26 creates an offence. **Analysis:** separating them protects effective restoration and fair personal liability. **Qualification:** constitutional courts retain their own contempt powers under the Constitution.

### 19. Full solved PYQs

#### PYQ 1 — Prelims 2018 Q4

**Question:** How is the National Green Tribunal (NGT) different from the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)?

1. The NGT has been established by an Act whereas the CPCB has been created by executive order of the Government.
2. The NGT provides environmental justice and helps reduce the burden of litigation in the higher courts whereas the CPCB promotes cleanliness of streams and wells, and aims to improve the quality of air in the country.

A. 1 only
B. 2 only
C. Both 1 and 2
D. Neither 1 nor 2

**INFERRED ANSWER — NOT OFFICIALLY VERIFIED LOCALLY: B.** CPCB is also statutory under the Water Act, 1974; statement 1 is false. Statement 2 correctly contrasts adjudication and regulatory pollution control.

#### PYQ 2 — Mains 2018 GS-II Q6

**Question:** “Policy contradictions among various competing sectors and stakeholders have resulted in inadequate ‘protection and prevention of degradation’ to environment.” Comment with relevant illustrations. *(10 marks, 150 words)*

**Model answer:**

Environmental degradation reflects not absence of law but incoherence between sectoral decisions. Mining or infrastructure agencies may value output and connectivity, while EIA, forest, wildlife, water and community-rights authorities examine fragments of the same landscape. POSCO illustrated the danger of appraising a smaller project phase while wider linked impacts remained material. Niyamgiri showed that mineral policy could not ignore forest, livelihood and religious rights recognised through the Gram Sabha route. At Mopa, the Supreme Court required expert scrutiny because appraisal must engage material environmental concerns, not merely complete procedure.

The cure is integrated spatial planning, cumulative-impact and carrying-capacity studies, shared baseline data, early alternatives analysis, and reasoned reconciliation of public-hearing concerns. Independent post-clearance monitoring must connect approval conditions to outcomes. Yet coordination should not become dilution: each specialised law protects a distinct interest. A coherent “whole-project, whole-landscape” decision is therefore better than either clearance silos or a single weak window.

**Why this earns marks:** Three bounded examples, causal analysis, reform and qualification.

#### PYQ 3 — Mains 2019 GS-I Q15

**Question:** How can the mountain ecosystem be restored from the negative impact of development initiatives and tourism? *(15 marks, 250 words)*

**Model answer:**

Mountain ecosystems combine steep slopes, young geology, concentrated biodiversity and narrow settlement corridors; damage therefore travels through landslides, altered drainage, waste, habitat fragmentation and livelihood loss. Restoration must begin by avoiding fresh stress, not by planting after construction.

First, landscape-level carrying-capacity and cumulative-impact studies should precede roads, hydropower, hotels and town expansion; project EIAs must test no-project, alignment and scale alternatives. Second, hazard zonation should exclude unstable slopes, floodplains, recharge areas and wildlife corridors, while construction follows slope stabilisation, muck-disposal and drainage plans. Third, tourism should shift from unlimited footfall to destination-specific caps, public transport, parking controls, eco-sensitive zoning and user fees earmarked for waste and sanitation. The NGT's Rohtang interventions illustrate the regulatory logic of controlling vehicular pressure in a fragile destination.

Fourth, native vegetation, springs, wetlands and riparian buffers should be restored through catchment treatment, assisted natural regeneration and invasive-species control rather than monoculture planting. Fifth, solid waste, sewage and plastic must be managed by local bodies under enforceable service standards. Gram Sabhas, forest-right holders, women and pastoral communities should co-design measures because they hold seasonal ecological knowledge and bear opportunity costs.

Monitoring should use slope movement, water quality, spring discharge, habitat connectivity and waste leakage—not expenditure alone. Restoration succeeds when development stays within ecological and disaster-risk limits; engineering repair cannot indefinitely compensate for excessive siting and scale.

**Why this earns marks:** Process-to-remedy linkage, named regulatory illustration, community dimension and measurable conclusion.

A. 1 only

#### PYQ 5 — Mains 2020 GS-III Q7

**Question:** How does the draft Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) Notification, 2020 differ from the existing EIA Notification, 2006? *(10 marks, 150 words)*

**Model answer:**

The 2020 instrument was a **draft**, not the replacement law; the EIA Notification, 2006 continued to govern. Compared with 2006, the draft proposed a classification and procedure, wider B2/exemption space for activities, shorter public-hearing notice, and annual rather than half-yearly compliance reporting. It also proposed longer validity periods and a formal “violation” route for projects begun without prior clearance, supported by appraisal, remediation and resource-augmentation plans.

These changes promised quicker, risk-differentiated approvals and a route to bring violators under regulation. Critics, however, argued that reduced participation, broader exemptions and post-facto processing weaken EIA's preventive logic: information received after construction cannot inform the original siting or no-project choice.

The legal-status distinction is decisive. Draft provisions never became the governing notification merely because they were published for comment. Later amendments to the 2006 regime must be cited independently, and current ex-post-facto law is controlled by the Supreme Court's 2026 *Vanashakti* ruling.

**Why this earns marks:** Direct comparison, legal-status discipline and current qualification.

#### PYQ 6 — Mains 2022 GS-II Q1

**Question:** “The most significant achievement of modern law in India is the constitutionalization of environmental problems by the Supreme Court.” Discuss this statement with the help of relevant case laws. *(10 marks, 150 words)*

**Model answer:**

The Supreme Court transformed environmental protection from statutory control into a constitutional obligation. It read a healthy environment into Article 21, reinforced by State duty under Article 48A and citizen duty under Article 51A(g). In *M.C. Mehta* (Oleum Gas Leak), absolute liability strengthened remedies for hazardous industry. *Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum* treated precaution, polluter pays and sustainable development as principles. *M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath* applied the public-trust doctrine to natural resources, while *A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu* linked precaution to scientific uncertainty and institutional expertise.

This constitutionalisation widened standing, enabled continuing mandamus and made environmental reasoning relevant to executive approvals. Yet courts cannot replace baseline science, public consultation or enforcement. The NGT Act, 2010 institutionalised specialist adjudication, but jurisdiction remains statutory. The achievement is therefore constitutional accountability combined with—rather than substituted for—competent regulators, credible EIA and legislatively bounded tribunals.

**Why this earns marks:** Constitutional text, four cases, doctrines and institutional balance.

#### PYQ 7 — Mains 2024 GS-III Q8

**Question:** What role do environmental NGOs and activists play in influencing Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) outcomes for major projects in India? Cite four examples with all important details. *(10 marks, 150 words)*

**Model answer:**

NGOs and affected citizens improve EIA by exposing missing baseline data, livelihood effects and appraisal failures through hearings, independent evidence and litigation.

1. **Mopa airport—*Hanuman Laxman Aroskar* (2019):** citizen challenges led the Supreme Court to require renewed expert review; this was procedural correction, not permanent cancellation.
2. **Niyamgiri—*Orissa Mining Corporation* (2013):** Dongria Kondh mobilisation made religious, forest and livelihood claims decision-relevant through Gram Sabhas; it was primarily a forest-rights route.
3. **POSCO—*Prafulla Samantray* (NGT, 2012):** litigation exposed appraisal of a 4-MTPA phase within a proposed 12-MTPA project; the EC was remitted/suspended for fuller review.
4. **Kudremukh—*Godavarman/K.M. Chinnappa* (2002):** conservation litigation secured precautionary limits, closure and reclamation in a national park.

Thus civil society supplies information and accountability, but cannot replace transparent expert appraisal. Its strongest contribution is forcing the State to confront the whole project and give reasons.

**Why this earns marks:** Four precise examples with legal-route boundaries and a direct verdict.

#### PYQ 8 — Mains 2025 GS-III Q17

**Question:** Mineral resources are fundamental to the country's economy and these are exploited by mining. Why is mining considered an environmental hazard? Explain the remedial measures required to reduce the environmental hazard due to mining. *(15 marks, 250 words)*

**Model answer:**

Mining concentrates environmental risk across the entire life cycle. Vegetation and topsoil removal fragment habitat and accelerate erosion; drilling, blasting, crushing and haulage generate dust, vibration and noise. Overburden and tailings can cause sedimentation, instability and chemical drainage; dewatering changes aquifers and stream flow. Roads and worker settlements induce further land-use change. Abandoned pits, fires and unstable dumps convert short extraction into long liability, while Adivasi and forest-dependent communities may bear displacement and livelihood losses.

Remedy should follow the mitigation hierarchy. **Avoid:** strategic mineral planning, no-go screening for irreplaceable habitats, cumulative-impact/carrying-capacity studies and genuine site alternatives. **Minimise:** rigorous prior EC, hydrogeological and biodiversity baselines, dust suppression, enclosed transport, water balance, controlled blasting, safe tailings design and emergency plans. **Restore progressively:** conserve topsoil, stabilise/backfill dumps, rehabilitate with native ecological communities and begin reclamation during extraction. **Govern closure:** a funded, enforceable mine-closure plan, measurable completion criteria and post-closure water/geotechnical monitoring must prevent orphan liabilities.

Public consultation, Forest Rights Act due diligence and accessible grievance redress make distributional impacts visible. Independent inspections and public half-yearly compliance reports should trigger corrective action; NGT relief and restitution remain available for qualifying statutory harm. Kudremukh demonstrates why precaution may require limiting extraction in a protected landscape.

Minerals are economically necessary, but clearance is only permission subject to conditions—not evidence that restoration has occurred.

**Why this earns marks:** Full hazard chain, hierarchy-based remedies, rights, closure, enforcement and qualification.

### 20. Six original Mains questions and model answers

#### ORIGINAL 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish screening, scoping and appraisal. Why does collapsing them weaken EIA?

**Model answer:** Screening applies only to Category B and determines whether a proposal is B1, requiring EIA, or B2, following the applicable reduced route. Scoping follows for Category A/B1: EAC or SEAC frames project- and site-specific Terms of Reference defining the evidence required. Appraisal is the later scrutiny of the application, final EIA, consultation record and validated information, ending in a reasoned recommendation to grant with conditions or reject.

The stages answer different questions: **how much study, what must be studied, and what decision follows from the evidence**. Merging them lets an early classification prejudge merits, permits generic ToR, and turns appraisal into document checking. *Hanuman Laxman Aroskar* shows why expert appraisal must engage material concerns rather than merely complete procedure.

However, procedural separation should not cause avoidable delay. Digital data, concurrent lawful approvals and clear timelines can improve speed while preserving each stage's distinct decision function.

**Why this earns marks:** Complete distinction, causal analysis, case and balanced reform.

#### ORIGINAL 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain how sections 14, 15 and 16 require different forum-selection questions.

**Model answer:** Section 14 asks whether a **civil dispute** involves a substantial environmental question arising from a Schedule I enactment; its ordinary limitation is six months plus at most sixty days. Section 15 asks whether victims, property or the environment require compensation or restitution; its clock is five years plus sixty days. Section 16 asks whether the impugned order is one of the enumerated appealable decisions—such as grant or refusal of EC—and allows thirty days plus sixty days.

Thus one incident may generate different remedies, but a pleading must identify the door, relief and limitation rather than invoke “environment.” Section 18 supplies standing, while section 20 guides the Tribunal only after jurisdiction exists. *Techi Tagi Tara* confirms that an institution's appointment dispute does not fit sections 14–15.

Forum selection combines cause of action, Schedule I nexus, impugned order, remedy and clock. Environmental importance cannot cure jurisdictional defects.

**Why this earns marks:** Section-specific comparison, authority and direct forum test.

#### ORIGINAL 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** The legal design of public consultation is stronger than its frequent implementation. Critically examine.

**Model answer:** Paragraph 7 gives consultation a substantive purpose: ascertain concerns of locally affected persons and others with a plausible stake so material issues shape project design. It ordinarily combines a district-wise site/near-site hearing with written responses. Appendix IV requires a major national and regional vernacular/official-language daily, at least thirty days' notice, English and local-language summary, access to the draft EIA, videography, same-day agreed minutes and public display. The proponent must address material concerns in the final EIA/EMP.

Implementation can still be weak. A technically dense report may be formally available but unusable; remote offices, inconvenient venue, incomplete local translation or missing raw data restrict informed participation. Because the proponent commissions the EIA, consultant incentives can favour a preferred design. Hearings late in project planning create sunk-cost pressure, while minutes may record objections without showing how appraisal resolved them. Exemptions, though lawful, also remove local evidence from specified cases.

Reform should require searchable raw baseline data, plain-language and local-language disclosure, independent conflict declarations and audits, accessible hybrid submission, reasoned issue-response matrices, protection against intimidation and committee reasons on every material concern. Consultation should begin while alternatives remain open.

Yet a hearing cannot become a local veto over every project; appraisal must reconcile evidence, rights, wider public interest and statutory mandates. Quality participation improves legitimacy because it changes reasons and conditions—not because attendance alone determines the result.

**Why this earns marks:** Legal detail, implementation critique, reform and qualification.

#### ORIGINAL 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Why must project EIA be supplemented by strategic, cumulative, carrying-capacity, social, health and biodiversity assessment?

**Model answer:** Project EIA is strongest at a defined site and proposal, but many environmental decisions are made earlier or operate at larger scales. **Strategic environmental assessment** tests policies, plans and programmes before individual projects lock in a corridor or energy pathway. **Cumulative assessment** measures combined past, present and reasonably foreseeable pressures that separate EIAs can understate. **Carrying-capacity analysis** asks whether an airshed, basin, coast, mountain destination or city can absorb total use without unacceptable ecological or social change.

Specialist lenses correct distributional blind spots. Social assessment identifies displacement, livelihoods, culture, gender and community institutions; health assessment traces exposure pathways and vulnerable groups; biodiversity assessment examines seasonal species use, habitat connectivity, ecosystem services and irreplaceability rather than a one-season species list.

These tools matter because project boundaries can fragment river basins, mining clusters, wildlife corridors and induced development. They improve alternatives: the answer may be a different policy, regional cap or no-go area, not merely a taller stack or compensatory plantation.

However, additional studies can become paperwork unless they share quality-controlled data, define decision thresholds and bind appraisal. Nor should one assessment substitute for Forest Rights Act, wildlife, forest, land or pollution-control duties. India needs tiered assessment: strategic choices first, regional cumulative/carrying-capacity limits second, and project EIA/EMP last, followed by public monitoring.

**Why this earns marks:** Every lens is defined, linked to a gap and placed in a workable hierarchy.

#### ORIGINAL 5 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** State the current legal position on prior and ex-post-facto EC after *Vanashakti* (2026). Evaluate the implications.

**Model answer:** The EIA Notification, 2006 makes prior EC mandatory for scheduled new projects, threshold-crossing expansion/modernisation and specified product-mix change before construction or land preparation, except securing land. *Common Cause* and *Alembic Pharmaceuticals* treated retrospective clearance as contrary to this preventive design. Later decisions, including *Pahwa Plastics*, addressed consequences under the violation instruments, creating doctrinal tension.

In *Vanashakti v. Union of India*, 2026 INSC 761 (29 July 2026), the Supreme Court restored a rule-exception structure. It held that the 2006 regime does not itself accommodate post-facto EC. The 2017 notification survived because it was a narrow, time-bound delegated-legislation amnesty under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act. The perpetual 2021 Office Memorandum was an impermissible administrative alteration and was quashed prospectively. Existing ECs remain open to merits challenge; pending applications may conclude; no fresh applications under either instrument are allowed. Future executive office orders cannot regularise new violations, although a valid narrowly tailored notification—and Article 142 in an appropriate case—remain legally distinct possibilities.

Regulators must detect violation early, stop treating penalty as permission, and link any lawful exceptional scheme to restoration, proportionality and public interest. Proponents face strong incentives to secure EC before irreversible commitment; finance and contracts should condition disbursement on legal clearance.

The judgment avoids both routine amnesty and indiscriminate demolition. Its success now depends on monitoring, transparent enforcement and refusal to convert exceptional delegated legislation into a recurring business model.

**Why this earns marks:** Operative directions, doctrinal reconciliation and stakeholder implications.

#### ORIGINAL 6 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess whether the NGT's statutory design is adequate to deliver environmental justice.

**Model answer:** The NGT Act creates a specialised preventive-remedial forum. A full-time Chairperson, Judicial and Expert Members, equal representation on hearing benches and flexible procedure address the scientific-legal character of environmental disputes. Section 14 supplies original civil jurisdiction over substantial questions arising from Schedule I laws; section 16 supplies enumerated appeals, including EC decisions. Sections 15 and 17 enable compensation and restitution, section 19 provides review/interim/cease-and-desist powers, and section 20 mandates sustainable development, precaution and polluter pays. Section 18's broad standing and *Ankita Sinha*'s recognition of suo motu power improve access.

Design nevertheless has boundaries. Schedule I omits the Wildlife (Protection) Act and Indian Forest Act; *Techi Tagi Tara* confirms that principles cannot manufacture jurisdiction. Short section 14/16 clocks can burden dispersed communities. Technical quality depends on members, transparent experts and natural justice. Orders also require executive implementation: section 25 enables decree-like execution and revenue recovery, while section 26 is penal and must be strictly applied. In *I.S. Tomar/Umesh Pratap Singh* (2025), the Supreme Court rejected personal penalties without proven responsibility/wilful default.

Reform should maintain full bench strength, circuit/digital access, legal aid, open scientific data, reasoned compensation methods and time-bound agency compliance dashboards. Schedule coverage should be reviewed legislatively rather than stretched judicially. High Courts and the Supreme Court retain constitutional review; section 22 provides statutory appeal.

Thus the architecture is strong but not self-executing. Environmental justice requires expert adjudication plus credible regulators, enforceable restoration and fair participation.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates architecture, access, boundaries, enforcement and reform with precise sections/cases.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### EIA core

- EIA = ex-ante decision support: predict, compare alternatives, avoid/mitigate, decide, monitor.
- Paragraph 2: scheduled new projects; threshold-crossing expansion/modernisation; specified product-mix change; prior to construction/land preparation except securing land.
- Category A: EAC recommends → MoEFCC decides.
- Category B: screening → B1/B2; SEAC recommends → SEIAA decides.
- No SEIAA/SEAC: central-level consideration **as Category B**.
- Legal stages: screening → scoping → public consultation → appraisal.
- Technical spine: baseline → prediction → alternatives → mitigation hierarchy → EMP.
- GC: Category B treated A within 10 km of protected area, critically polluted area, notified eco-sensitive area, inter-State/international boundary; use proviso exactly.
- SC: qualifying prior-EC industrial estate/complex may cover individual units; identified management retains legal responsibility.

### Consultation and appraisal

- Consultation = hearing + written responses; affected persons + others with plausible stake.
- Exemptions: irrigation modernisation; qualifying industrial estates; road expansion without more land; maintenance dredging within port; item 8; B2; defence/security/centrally determined strategic.
- Appendix IV: national + vernacular/official-language daily; 30 days; local-language summary; draft access; videography; minutes/display.
- Quality tests: usable information, early timing, safe access, conflict disclosure, issue-response reasons.
- Appraisal = substantive scrutiny; EAC/SEAC categorical recommendation; regulator decides.

### EC life cycle

- Validity (2022): river-valley 13; nuclear 15; other non-mining 10; mining plan/life with 30-year base and conditional route to 50.
- Transfer: entitled legal person, application/no-objection, same conditions and validity, no committee reference.
- Material amendment may trigger paragraph 2/7(ii); label cannot defeat substance.
- Compliance reports: 1 June and 1 December; public documents.
- EC ≠ consent/forest/wildlife/CRZ approval ≠ proof of achieved outcomes.

### Ex-post current law

- Prior EC mandatory under 2006 regime.
- *Alembic* supports preventive logic.
- *Vanashakti* 2026: 2017 narrow notification valid; 2021 OM prospectively quashed; past ECs merits-challengeable; pending applications conclude; no fresh applications; no future administrative regularisation; valid delegated legislation/Article 142 remain distinct.

### NGT rapid law

- Act in force: 18 October 2010.
- Composition: Chairperson + 10–20 Judicial + 10–20 Expert; parity on hearing bench.
- Benches: Delhi; Bhopal, Kolkata, Chennai, Pune.
- s.14 original civil: 6 months + 60 days.
- s.15 compensation/restitution: 5 years + 60 days.
- s.16 enumerated appeal incl. EC: 30 days + 60 days.
- s.18 broad standing; six-month disposal endeavour.
- s.19 natural justice; not bound CPC/Evidence Act; review/interim/cease-desist.
- s.20 sustainable development + precaution + polluter pays.
- s.22 Supreme Court: 90 days, section 100 CPC grounds.
- Rule 22 review: ordinarily 30 days from receipt.
- s.25 execution/decree/revenue recovery; s.26 penal non-compliance ≠ loose “contempt.”
- Schedule I printed seven; Wildlife Act and Indian Forest Act absent; Water Cess Act repealed; forest Act renamed.

- *Vellore*: statutory environmental principles.
- *Techi Tagi*: jurisdiction boundary.
- *Mopa/Hanuman*: reasoned appraisal.
- *Alembic*: prior-EC logic.

### Final qualified conclusion

*The strongest environmental regime is not the one that grants the fewest or fastest clearances. It is the one that makes alternatives real before commitment, converts conditions into public performance data, and supplies accessible, expert and enforceable remedies when prevention fails.*
