## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1

Which statement best preserves the four distinct meanings commonly attached to “forest” in India?

A. Forest type is ecological, forest cover is a canopy measurement, recorded forest area is a
government-record category, and an FRA right concerns recognised tenure or use.
B. Forest type and forest cover are interchangeable, while recorded forest area alone determines
all customary rights.
C. Forest cover is a legal notification, whereas Reserved Forest and Protected Forest are canopy
density classes.
D. An FRA title converts the land into a new Champion–Seth type, vests exclusive private
ownership and removes every other approval, record and conservation process by operation of law.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** A separates ecological classification, remote-sensing measurement, legal record
and rights. B wrongly equates type with cover and makes records conclusive of rights. C reverses
measurement and legal categories. D is false because a title recognises a right; it neither
changes vegetation type nor displaces every other law. Within the legal ledger, Reserved Forest
uses “prohibited unless permitted,” while Protected Forest uses “permitted unless prohibited.”

### MCQ 2

Across most tropical parts of India, which pair most directly controls the transition from wet
evergreen through deciduous to thorn formations?

A. Longitude and magnetic declination measured at the relevant district headquarters
B. Available moisture and the length/intensity of the dry season
C. Administrative forest status and district boundary
D. Canopy class and title-distribution rate recorded for the same reporting year

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Moisture balance and seasonal water stress drive the broad wet-to-dry ecological
sequence. Longitude can correlate with climate but is not the operative mechanism in A. C and D
are administrative or measurement variables, not primary ecological controls. Soil, fire and
topography modify the sequence but do not displace the central climate-water relation.

### MCQ 3

Consider the following pairs:

1. Littoral and Swamp Forests — tidal, coastal or freshwater waterlogging
2. Subtropical Pine Forests — a canopy-density class used by FSI
3. Himalayan Dry Temperate Forests — inner/rain-shadow temperate conditions
4. Moist Alpine Scrub — vegetation above the tree line under relatively moist alpine conditions

How many pairs are correctly matched?

A. Only one
B. Only two
C. Only three
D. All four

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Pairs 1, 3 and 4 correctly describe Champion–Seth groups. Pair 2 is wrong:
Subtropical Pine is an ecological forest-type group, whereas FSI density classes are VDF, MDF and
open forest. The question tests classification level, not whether pine stands can also be mapped
into a canopy class.

### MCQ 4

Which one of the following is the most accurate relationship between mangroves and the
Champion–Seth “Littoral and Swamp Forests” group?

A. Every littoral or swamp forest is a mangrove because all waterlogged forests are saline,
tidal and exposed to daily seawater exchange.
B. Mangrove is an FSI legal category, while littoral forest is a tenure right.
C. Mangroves are alpine scrub that has descended to sea level through succession.
D. Mangroves are salt-tolerant intertidal forests within a broader group that also includes
non-mangrove littoral and freshwater swamp formations.

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** D gives the correct nested relationship. A ignores freshwater and other coastal
formations. B confuses ecological and legal vocabularies. C is ecologically untenable: alpine
scrub and mangroves arise under radically different temperature, salinity and inundation regimes.

### MCQ 5

In a wet monsoonal region, a forest contains both evergreen and deciduous canopy elements and
occupies a transition between wet evergreen and moist deciduous formations. It is best classified
as:

A. Tropical Semi-Evergreen Forest
B. Tropical Thorn Forest
C. Himalayan Dry Temperate Forest
D. Dry Alpine Scrub

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Semi-evergreen forests are transitional wet-region formations containing both
evergreen and deciduous elements. Thorn forest reflects severe tropical moisture deficit. Dry
temperate and dry alpine groups belong to colder Himalayan elevation/rain-shadow settings, not a
wet tropical transition.

### MCQ 6

Which distinction between Tropical Dry Evergreen Forest and Tropical Thorn Forest is sound?

A. Dry evergreen is necessarily denser than 70%, while thorn is always below 10% canopy.
B. Dry evergreen is an evergreen ecological formation under seasonal dryness; thorn is a sparse,

**Answer: B**

Champion–Seth groups; “dry” does not make their floristics and structure identical.

### MCQ 7

Which sequence most plausibly represents increasing elevation in a Himalayan landscape, subject
to local variation in aspect and moisture?

A. Dry alpine scrub → tropical wet evergreen → littoral swamp → thorn
B. Moist alpine scrub → subtropical pine → mangrove → subalpine
C. Subtropical broadleaf/pine → temperate forest → subalpine forest → alpine scrub
D. Tropical thorn → mangrove → moist deciduous → dry alpine scrub

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** C follows the broad lower-to-upper mountain sequence. A, B and D insert coastal
or tropical lowland formations into an elevational ladder. The qualifier matters: altitude is not
the only control, because aspect, humidity and rain shadow determine whether a belt is wet, moist
or dry.

### MCQ 9

### MCQ 17

Which restoration sequence best follows an ecological hierarchy?

### MCQ 18

### MCQ 19

A road leaves the total forest area statistically unchanged but divides one continuous habitat
into several isolated patches. Which impact is most directly implicated?

A. Automatic conversion of all patches into Protected Forest
B. Guaranteed increase in interior habitat
C. Fragmentation, edge effects and reduced ecological connectivity
D. Recognition of CFR rights without a claim process

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Fragmentation can damage ecological function without an immediate fall in gross
area. A and D are legal consequences that do not follow from road construction. B is the reverse
of the likely pattern: division usually raises edge-to-interior ratios and can interrupt movement,
gene flow and fire/water processes.

### Routed PYQ 4 — 2020 Mains GS-I, Q17

**Ownership:** Direct — forest resources and climate change
**Routing provenance:** `_PYQ-ROUTING-MAINS-GS1-GS2-ESSAY-2018-2023.md`
**Local official-paper provenance:** `books\more_previous_papers\Gen_St_P1.pdf`
**Key status:** Not applicable; UPSC publishes no official model answer.

**Question:** Examine the status of forest resources of India and its resultant impact on climate
change. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer:**

India’s forest resource must be read through both extent and quality. ISFR 2023 reports
7,15,342.61 sq km of forest cover and 1,12,014.34 sq km of tree cover, together 25.17% of
geographical area. Yet FSI’s canopy metric includes natural forests, orchards, bamboo, palm and
plantations irrespective of ownership or legal status. It therefore cannot alone establish
native-forest condition, fragmentation, regeneration or tenure.

Forests affect climate through several mechanisms. Biomass and soils store carbon, while growing
vegetation can remove atmospheric carbon. Conversion, severe degradation and fire release stored
carbon and reduce future sequestration. Fragmentation and simplified plantations may retain
canopy yet weaken ecological resilience. Forested catchments, litter and roots also influence
infiltration, erosion, evapotranspiration and local microclimate, making healthy forests important
for adaptation as well as mitigation.

Climate change in turn intensifies heat, drought, fire risk, pests and species-range shifts,
creating a feedback: stressed forests store less carbon and become more disturbance-prone.

Policy should therefore protect intact natural forests first; control diversion, fragmentation,
fire and invasive species; restore hydrology and connectivity; use assisted natural regeneration
and diverse native planting; and distinguish plantations from natural forests in monitoring.
Recognised CFR institutions can support stewardship, but titles require capacity and ecological
measurement.

Thus, India’s canopy total is significant, but climate benefit depends on forest quality,
permanence and rights-secure management, not area statistics alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses dated official data, explains two-way forest–climate mechanisms,
qualifies the cover metric and gives a hierarchy rather than a planting-only prescription.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish forest type, forest cover, recorded forest area, tree cover and Trees
Outside Forests. Why does this distinction matter for environmental policy?
**Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer:**

Forest type is an ecological category defined by climate, soil, altitude, floristics and
disturbance. Forest cover is FSI’s physical measure: land
of at least one hectare with at least 10% tree canopy, irrespective of ownership, legal status or
natural/planted origin. Recorded forest area is land entered as forest in government records and
may or may not carry canopy.

Tree cover comprises single trees and patches below one hectare outside recorded forest area that
are not mapped as forest cover. Trees Outside Forests is broader: all trees outside recorded
forest area, irrespective of patch size.

The distinctions prevent policy errors. A plantation can increase forest cover without restoring
native biodiversity; a degraded Reserved Forest can remain recorded forest despite sparse canopy;
and TOF growth cannot prove tenure security. Sound policy therefore reports area, legal status,
origin, composition, fragmentation and rights separately before claiming conservation success.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all five categories, gives two counter-intuitive examples and
shows their policy consequence within the word limit.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the eligibility tests and principal right families under the Forest Rights
Act, 2006.
**Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer:**

The FRA corrects historical non-recognition of customary forest tenure. A forest-dwelling
Scheduled Tribe must belong to an ST scheduled in the relevant State/area, primarily reside in
and depend on forest land for bona fide livelihood, and satisfy the pre-13 December 2005
occupation condition. An Other Traditional Forest Dweller must additionally prove such primary
residence and dependence for three generations—75 years—before that date.

Rights include habitation and self-cultivation land under actual occupation, capped at four
hectares; community nistar, grazing, fishing, water and pastoral access; ownership, collection,
use and disposal of minor forest produce including bamboo; habitat rights of PVTGs and
pre-agricultural communities; conversion of specified pattas, forest villages and settlements;
and Community Forest Resource authority to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage customary
forest.

Titles are heritable but non-transferable. Hunting is excluded. Rights therefore combine tenure,
livelihood, community governance and conservation duty rather than amounting to unrestricted
private ownership.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates FDST and OTFD tests, states exact date/duration/ceiling and
classifies the rights with a limiting qualification.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks

**Question:** Explain how climate, topography, soil and disturbance generate India’s major
Champion–Seth forest groups. Suggest an ecologically defensible restoration strategy.
**Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer:**

Champion–Seth classifies Indian vegetation into 16 major groups, from tropical wet evergreen to
dry alpine scrub. Its first control is climatic water balance. Perennially humid regions favour
dense evergreen structure; a moderate dry season produces semi-evergreen and moist deciduous
formations; stronger seasonal stress favours dry deciduous and, under severe deficit, thorn
vegetation.

Topography modifies this gradient. Altitude lowers temperature and shortens the growing season,
creating subtropical, temperate, subalpine and alpine belts. Aspect and exposure alter solar
receipt, wind and moisture, while inner-Himalayan rain shadows produce dry temperate and dry
alpine formations beside wetter outer slopes.

Edaphic controls can override regional rainfall. Tidal salinity produces mangroves within the
littoral-and-swamp group; freshwater waterlogging, shallow rock, soil depth and drainage create
different local communities. Fire, grazing, lopping, invasive species and past land use then
reshape structure and regeneration. Hence a present open patch may reflect degradation rather
than a climatic “thorn” type.

Restoration should follow a hierarchy: avoid loss of intact natural forest; protect corridors,
soil and hydrology; remove the active pressure; assist natural regeneration where seed sources
and ecological memory survive; use diverse, site-matched native species where recruitment is
inadequate; and restore fire/water regimes and connectivity.

Monitoring must separate natural forest, plantation, canopy density and legal status, and measure
composition, regeneration, fragmentation and community outcomes. Dense planting or a Miyawaki
mini-forest may suit a small urban site but cannot substitute for an old natural ecosystem.

**Why this earns marks:** It links every control to a mechanism, explains close options and turns
the diagnosis into a sequenced restoration framework.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Examine the FRA claims process. How do its evidence and appeal rules seek to prevent
the exclusion of communities with oral or customary tenure?
**Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer:**

The FRA uses a bottom-up process because customary tenure is often absent from formal land
records. The Gram Sabha receives claims and elects a Forest Rights Committee. The Committee
acknowledges claims, visits sites, receives witnesses and records, maps individual and customary
CFR boundaries, and presents findings. The Gram Sabha passes a reasoned resolution and forwards
it to the Sub-Divisional Level Committee (SDLC). The SDLC examines resolutions, reconciles maps
and records, resolves disputes and hears first petitions. The District Level Committee (DLC)
finally approves the record, issues titles and directs correction of revenue/forest records.

Rule 13 deliberately creates an evidentiary basket: gazetteers, census and settlement material,
maps, satellite imagery, authorised identity records, houses and improvements, court records,
reputable studies of custom, princely-State records, wells, sacred places, burial grounds,
genealogy and written statements of elders. CFR evidence includes grazing, MFP and fishing areas,
water systems, sacred groves and traditional agriculture. More than one item is considered;
officials cannot insist on one documentary form, and imagery supplements rather than replaces
other evidence.

An aggrieved person may petition the SDLC within 60 days of a Gram Sabha resolution and the DLC
within 60 days of the SDLC decision. Notice, hearing and written reasons are required. Rejection
cannot rest merely on a technical defect; incomplete claims should be remanded.

The design is inclusive, but results depend on awareness, fair verification, functioning Gram
Sabhas and faithful compliance by field authorities.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces the entire process, names evidence and remedies, and separates
progressive design from implementation risk.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks

**Question:** “Conservation relocation is lawful only after rights recognition, scientific
necessity and informed consent.” Analyse with reference to Critical Wildlife Habitat, Critical
Tiger Habitat and the FRA–WPA interface.
**Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer:**

Critical Wildlife Habitat (CWH) and Core/Critical Tiger Habitat (CTH) are distinct. CWH is defined
by FRA Section 2(b) for National Parks and Sanctuaries and is notified by the Central Government
after a scientific, objective and open expert process including local and Tribal Affairs
representation. CTH is a tiger-reserve core category under Wild Life (Protection) Act Section
38V, identified scientifically and notified by the State Government in consultation with an
expert committee.

Neither notification by itself authorises displacement. FRA Section 4(5) bars eviction until
recognition and verification are complete. For CWH, Section 4(2) cumulatively requires completed
rights recognition; a State finding that rights-holder presence causes irreversible damage and
threatens the species/habitat; conclusion that reasonable coexistence options are unavailable; a
secure-livelihood resettlement package; written free informed Gram Sabha consent; and completed
land and facilities before movement. The vacated habitat cannot later be diverted to another use.

NTCA’s protocol applies comparable Section 38V safeguards to CTH: recognition/determination and
acquisition or settlement of rights, consent-based assessment of irreversible tiger-habitat
damage, no reasonable coexistence option, mutually agreed terms, informed consent of Gram Sabhas
and affected persons, and completed facilities. It expressly treats CWH and CTH as different
legal expressions.

PESA strengthens Gram Sabha authority in Scheduled Areas, while the FRA supplies the
rights-recognition process. Conservation credibility therefore depends on sequence: establish
rights and evidence first, test coexistence, disclose a viable package, obtain uncoerced consent,
then monitor rehabilitation and ecological outcomes.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes the two categories, states the cumulative safeguards
and gives a rights-and-conservation sequencing test rather than taking an absolutist position.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks

**Question:** Evaluate the implementation of the Forest Rights Act using the distinction between
claims, titles, effective rights and outcomes. How can CFR governance support forest restoration
without romanticising community management?
**Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer:**

MoTA’s report dated 30 June 2026 records 54,01,561 FRA claims, 25,42,359 titles and 43,55,591
disposed claims. These figures show administrative scale, not a single success rate: disposal is
not identical to approval, community titles do not all reveal CFR quality, and a distributed title
does not prove boundary demarcation, record mutation or exercise of the right.

Implementation should therefore be assessed as a chain. First, were eligible FDST/OTFD,
community, PVTG and pastoral claims invited and verified with the plural evidence allowed by Rule
13? Second, were adverse decisions reasoned, communicated and appealable rather than technical
rejections? Third, were approved rights entered in revenue and forest records, mapped and
protected against conflicting concessions? Fourth, could holders access MFP markets,
grazing, water and decision-making institutions? Only then should livelihood, conflict and
ecological outcomes be measured.

CFR can align tenure security with restoration because Section 3(1)(i) grants
protect-regenerate-conserve-manage authority and Section 5 imposes wildlife, biodiversity,
catchment and habitat duties. Gram Sabha committees can prepare conservation plans and regulate
access. However, neither community identity nor title guarantees good ecology. Drought, invasive
species, altered fire, elite capture, boundary disputes and weak technical support may persist.

Policy should complete CFR recognition, correct records, fund participatory mapping and
ecological monitoring, secure transparent benefit-sharing, and link native assisted regeneration,
fire/water management and connectivity to locally enforceable plans. The proper verdict is
conditional: rights are an enabling institution whose performance must be verified through both
distributional and ecological indicators.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses current official data without inventing a rejection rate,
constructs an implementation ladder and balances CFR potential with measurable limitations.
