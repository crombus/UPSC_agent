> **Practice contract:** exactly **40 distinct original MCQs**, four options each, full
> explanations, strict A → B → C → D rotation repeated ten times; every relevant direct, shared
> and application PYQ identified in the audited 2018–2026 routing; and six original Mains
> questions: **2 × 10 marks, 2 × 15 marks and 2 × 20 marks**.
> **Legal/current cut-off:** 29 September 2026 (IST).
> **Controlling current notifications:** SWM S.O. 388(E); PWM G.S.R. 73(E);
> E-Waste G.S.R. 164(E).

> 2024–2025 answers use local official UPSC Set-A keys; the 2026 Set-A key is explicitly

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1

Which sequence most accurately represents the statutory waste hierarchy?

A. Prevention → reduction → reuse → recycling → recovery → disposal
B. Reduction → prevention → recovery → reuse → recycling → disposal
C. Reuse → collection → incineration → recycling → landfill → prevention
D. Collection → transportation → recovery → reduction → disposal → reuse

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct** and preserves the hierarchy in both the 2016 and 2026 SWM
definitions: prevention is most preferred and landfill disposal least preferred. **B** wrongly
places reduction before prevention and recovery before reuse/recycling. **C** substitutes
operational stages for the hierarchy and elevates incineration. **D** begins after waste has
already arisen and reverses higher-value options.

---

### MCQ 2

Which intervention ordinarily retains the greatest embedded product value?

A. Shredding a repairable appliance for metal recovery
B. Repairing and reusing the appliance for its intended function
C. Co-processing the appliance's plastic fraction in a cement kiln
D. Disposing non-recyclable residues in a secured facility

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct:** repair and reuse preserve the assembled product, components,
manufacturing energy and material value. **A** recovers some material but destroys functional
value. **C** is energy/mineral recovery for a suitable residual fraction, lower in the hierarchy.
**D** may be necessary for irreducible residue but retains the least value.

### MCQ 3

A housing complex puts food waste, recyclable paper and used sanitary pads into one vehicle after
residents separated them correctly. What is the best diagnosis?

A. Source segregation alone proves compliant waste management.
B. Mixing is acceptable because all three streams remain municipal waste.
C. Intermixing during collection defeats the segregation chain and increases contamination risk.
D. Only the sanitary pads become non-compliant; food and paper remain unaffected.

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** Segregation must be preserved through collection and transport
to the appropriate processor. **A** confuses an initial action with a complete chain. **B** ignores
stream-specific processing and worker exposure. **D** is too narrow: wet waste contaminates paper,
paper degrades compost quality and sanitary waste exposes handlers.

### MCQ 4

Match the activity with its correct meaning:

1. Collection — taking or aggregating waste from generators
2. Processing — scientific handling for reuse, recycling or transformation
3. Recycling — transforming segregated waste into new material/product
4. Disposal — final safe destination for authorised residual fractions

Which are correctly matched?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1, 3 and 4 only
D. 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct.** All four preserve distinct lifecycle stages. **A** omits
recycling and disposal; **B** omits collection and disposal; **C** wrongly omits processing.
Collection is not processing, processing is broader than recycling, and lawful disposal is not
open dumping.

### MCQ 5

After the Solid Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2020, the 2016 Rules applied to which of the
following?

1. Census towns
2. Villages with population more than 3,000
3. Airports and railway-controlled areas
4. Radioactive waste generated in those areas

Select the correct answer.

A. 1, 2 and 3 only
B. 1 and 4 only
C. 2, 3 and 4 only
D. 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct.** The 2020 amendment added villages above 3,000, while census
towns, airports and railway areas were already within coverage. Statement 4 is false: radioactive
waste remained under separate law. **B** includes the exclusion and misses two covered areas.
**C** includes radioactive waste. **D** fails for the same reason.

### MCQ 6

Under the SWM Rules, 2016, which duty belonged to an ordinary waste generator?

A. Issue EPR compliance certificates to registered plastic producers through the centralised portal.
B. Segregate waste into biodegradable, non-biodegradable and domestic-hazardous streams.
C. Authorise, inspect and monitor a common regional sanitary-landfill facility.
D. Fix conversion factors for recovered end products.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** The 2016 generator duty used three streams and required lawful
handover. **A** is not a generator function. **C** belongs to government/regulatory
implementation. **D** is a specialised regulator/portal function found in EPR systems, not an
ordinary household duty.

### MCQ 7

Which establishment met the 2016 definition of a bulk waste generator solely by quantity?

A. Any gated community or institution with floor area above 5,000 sq m
B. Any institution consuming at least 40,000 litres of water each day
C. A listed establishment generating more than 100 kg of waste per day
D. Every commercial establishment, irrespective of waste generation

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **C is correct.** The 2016 definition used average waste generation exceeding
100 kg/day for listed establishments. **A** confuses the separate 5,000-sq-m on-site-processing
duty with the bulk definition. **B** is a 2026 criterion, not 2016. **D** removes the quantitative
threshold.

### MCQ 8

Which action was inconsistent with the 2016 local-body landfill duty?

A. Sending segregated wet waste to biomethanation
B. Sending recyclable dry waste to an MRF/recycler
C. Biomining a feasible legacy dumpsite
D. Landfilling mixed waste after the infrastructure deadline

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** **D is correct** because local bodies were to stop landfilling mixed waste after
the implementation timeline. **A** and **B** follow stream-specific processing. **C** was an
express route for old dumps where feasible. Landfill was reserved for specified inert/residual
fractions.

Which notification–change pair is correctly matched?

### MCQ 10

Which statement correctly states the present legal status of municipal solid-waste rules?

A. The 2016 Rules remain current and the 2026 text is only a draft pending public comments.
B. The 2026 Rules superseded the 2016 Rules and took effect on 1 April 2026.
C. The 2026 Rules apply only to newly registered rural generators and exclude existing urban bodies.
D. The 2016 and 2026 Rules operate concurrently for the same post-April 2026 duties.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct** under S.O. 388(E), 27 January 2026. **A** ignores the final
notification. **C** understates the urban-and-rural application. **D** overlooks supersession,
subject only to the saving for things already done or omitted.

### MCQ 12

An establishment is a current bulk waste generator if it satisfies at least one prescribed
criterion. Which option reproduces the rule?

A. 5,000 sq m; 20,000 litres/day; or 50 kg/day

C. 10,000 sq m; 40,000 litres/day; or 100 kg/week

### MCQ 13

Which statement about an EBWGR certificate under the SWM Rules, 2026 is correct?

A. A local body issues it after registered processing; it remains valid for three years.
B. Any waste collector may issue it upon collection, without processor proof or expiry.
C. It replaces source segregation and annual returns for every existing bulk waste generator.
D. It is an e-waste certificate bought from a registered recycler of recovered metals.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct.** Wet-waste certificates additionally require the processor's
reported receipt/processing. **B** removes the authorised issuer, processing evidence and
validity. **C** converts a compliance route into an exemption from segregation. **D** confuses
EBWGR with e-waste EPR.

### MCQ 14

Under the SWM Rules, 2026, which material may ordinarily be sent to sanitary landfill?

A. Source-segregated wet waste awaiting composting or biomethanation
B. Non-recyclable, non-energy-recoverable residual/inert waste
C. Recyclable dry paper
D. Unprocessed construction and demolition debris from city projects

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **B is correct.** **A** belongs in wet-waste processing. **C** should be
recovered/recycled. **D** is expressly barred and belongs under the separate C&D framework. The
rule uses landfill as a residual destination, not a substitute for processing.

### MCQ 16

Which routing is legally correct?

A. Used EV battery → E-Waste Management Rules; discarded laptop casing → Battery Waste Management Rules
B. Hospital sharps → SWM Rules; discarded monitor → Bio-Medical Waste Rules
C. Demolition concrete → PWM Rules; e-recycling residue → C&D Rules
D. Waste battery → Battery Rules; e-recycling hazardous residue → Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules

### MCQ 17

Which plastic-packaging category is correctly matched?

A. Category I — rigid plastic packaging
B. Category II — plastic combined with at least one non-plastic layer
C. Category III — compostable-plastic commodities
D. Category V — all multilayer plastic packaging

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** **A is correct.** **B** describes Category III. **C** belongs to Category IV.
**D** is wrong because Category V covers biodegradable-plastic packaging/commodities, while
plastic-only multilayers fall in Category II.

A. Category I

**Explanation:** **B is correct:** Category II includes flexible multilayer packaging made from

non-plastic layer. **D** concerns compostable plastic packaging/commodities.

Which entity must register on the current centralised plastic EPR portal?

### MCQ 25

Which statement best preserves the plastic end-of-life hierarchy?

A. Non-recyclable plastic alone should enter prescribed road, energy, oil or co-processing routes.
B. Collected plastic should undergo incineration or co-processing before any recycling decision.

## Solved Mains PYQs

### PYQ 11 — UPSC Mains 2018, GS-III, Q6

**Ownership:** Direct Topic 15
**Provenance:** Official local paper `GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III.pdf`; routed in
`_PYQ-ROUTING-MAINS-GS3-GS4-2018-2023.md`.
**Question:** “What are the impediments in disposing the huge quantities of discarded solid
wastes which are continuously being generated? How do we remove safely the toxic wastes that have
been accumulating in our habitable environment?”
**Marks / limit:** 10 marks / 150 words

**Model answer**

The disposal deficit begins upstream: rising throughput, weak prevention and source
segregation create wet–dry–hazardous mixtures that contaminate recyclables and overload local
bodies. Collection gaps, scarce land, poor cost recovery, fragmented institutions, under-used
processing, legacy dumps and unsafe informal handling deepen it. Technology chosen without
feedstock control merely transfers pollution into ash, rejects or leachate.

The response should follow the hierarchy. Reduce and reuse first; segregate wet, recyclable,
sanitary and hazardous fractions; integrate waste pickers into protected collection and sorting;
compost/biomethanate organics; recycle clean dry material; and send only suitable residuals to
engineered landfill. Toxic waste needs an inventory, risk-based containment, labelled collection,
authorised transport, specialised recovery/treatment and secured TSDF disposal. Batteries,
biomedical, e-waste and hazardous residues must follow their separate rules.

Legacy contaminated sites require assessment, remediation, groundwater/leachate monitoring and
polluter-pays finance. Safe removal means chain of custody and worker protection, not
shifting waste beyond city limits.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both impediments and safe toxic-waste removal through one
hierarchy, while preserving rule firewalls and residue accountability.

### PYQ 12 — UPSC Mains 2026, GS-III, Q17

**Ownership:** Direct Topic 15
**Provenance:** Official local paper
`QP-CSM-26-010926-GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III.pdf`; routed in `_PYQ-GS3-2026.md`.
**Question:** “What are the challenges to solid waste management in India? Discuss the
governmental policy framework on solid waste management. Discuss the success/failure cases of
Delhi and Indore cities highlighting the salient feature of their solid waste management
initiatives.”
**Marks / limit:** 15 marks / 250 words

**Model answer**

India's waste challenge is a broken material chain: high generation, weak prevention and source
segregation, mixed transport, inadequate local finance and processing, unsafe informal work,
legacy dumps, poor residue tracking and confusion among municipal, plastic, e-waste, biomedical
and hazardous streams.

The Environment (Protection) Act supports stream-specific rules. The Solid Waste Management
Rules, 2026 superseded the 2016 Rules from 1 April 2026. They extend urban–rural coverage, require
wet, dry, sanitary and special-care segregation, define bulk generators through 20,000 sq m,
40,000 litres/day or 100 kg/day criteria, introduce portal registration and EBWGR certificates,
preserve segregation through transport and restrict landfill to non-recyclable,
non-energy-recoverable residues/inerts. Wet and C&D waste cannot be landfilled; wrongly routed
waste attracts a fee above collection, transport and processing cost. Plastic and e-waste EPR
remain under separate rules.

**Delhi** represents legacy correction. Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla require biomining, leachate
and fire control and verified destinations for recovered fractions. MCD's dashboard still showed
continuing operations in September 2026: progress is real, but remediation is not completed merely
because a deadline was announced.

**Indore** represents prevention-first management: disciplined door-to-door segregated
collection, enforcement, citizen participation and stream-specific processing. PIB's 19 February
2022 record of its 550-TPD source-segregated wet-waste Bio-CNG plant illustrates wet-waste
valorisation.

The transferable lesson is to combine Indore's segregation-first flow control with Delhi's
auditable legacy remediation, worker integration and residual-only landfill.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers all three clauses, uses current law and dated city evidence,
and distinguishes design success from remediation in progress.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### Original 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish collection, processing, recycling, recovery and disposal. Why is
registration or an EPR certificate not by itself proof of environmental performance?
(Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer**

Collection is taking or aggregation of waste; transportation moves it while preserving the
stream. Processing is scientific handling of segregated waste for reuse, recycling
or transformation. Recycling converts material into a new product or secondary raw material.
Recovery reclaims useful material or, lower in the hierarchy, energy. Disposal is the final safe
destination for authorised residual or inert fractions.

These stages are not interchangeable. A collector may send waste to an unsafe destination; a
processor may recover metal but dump hazardous residue; energy recovery may destroy
recyclable material.

Registration proves that an entity entered the regulatory system. An EPR certificate records
eligible compliance quantity under portal rules. Environmental performance additionally requires
weighment, chain of custody, capacity, conversion factor or process yield, recovered-output
evidence, emissions compliance, lawful residue destination and physical audit.

Thus, documentary compliance becomes credible only when it
matches verified material flow and reduced harm.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines every stage and directly answers the proof-versus-outcome
clause.

### Original 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Compare the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 with the current Solid Waste
Management Rules, 2026. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer**

The 2016 Rules created the baseline: urban-area coverage, three-stream segregation
into biodegradable, non-biodegradable and domestic-hazardous waste, a >100 kg/day bulk-generator
definition, ULB-led door-to-door collection, waste-picker integration, decentralised wet-waste
processing, MRFs and residual-only sanitary landfill. Amendments changed recyclable-bin colour to
blue in 2019 and added villages above 3,000 and MoHUA terminology in 2020.

S.O. 388(E) superseded that framework from 1 April 2026. The Rules cover urban and rural
local bodies, require four streams—wet, dry, sanitary and special-care—and define a bulk generator
through any of three tests: 20,000 sq m, 40,000 litres/day or 100 kg/day. They add centralised
registration, public returns and EBWGR certificates, strengthen registered-facility routing and
impose a landfill user fee above processing cost for wrongly routed waste. Wet and C&D waste are
barred from landfill.

The continuity is segregation-to-residual-disposal; the shift is digital, rural and
certificate-backed accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It preserves legal chronology and compares mechanisms rather than merely
listing dates.

### Original 3 — 15 marks

**Question:** Examine whether the plastic-packaging EPR certificate system can deliver a circular
economy without stronger verification. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

Plastic EPR shifts lifecycle cost upstream to producers, importers and brand owners. Schedule II
sets category-wise collection/recycling, reuse and recycled-content obligations, while registered
plastic waste processors generate certificates for verified processing. Trading lets specialised
recyclers serve many producers, reduces the need for each brand to retrieve its own package and
can reward secondary-material markets.

However, a certificate is only as credible as the material record behind it. Risks include false
input invoices, double counting, certificates above installed throughput, category
misclassification, overstated recycling yield and dumping of low-value residues. Collection,
registration and certificate transfer can therefore coexist with little circular use.

Verification should combine GST-linked purchase/output records, calibrated weighment, category
and composition checks, installed-capacity and energy consistency, process conversion factors,
sale of recovered material, residue destination and surprise physical audits. The portal's lower
reconciled figure and the 30–100% environmental-compensation price band help, but cannot replace
site inspection.

Policy must also protect hierarchy. Reuse and recycled-content obligations should precede
end-of-life energy routes; non-recyclable fractions alone should reach co-processing, roads or
waste-to-oil. Informal collectors should receive recognition, safe aggregation contracts and a
share of EPR value.

Thus, EPR is a sound accountability instrument, not circularity itself. It succeeds when verified
certificates correspond to durable design, reuse, real material recycling and safe residual
management.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates benefits and fraud/implementation risk, then proposes a
complete verification and inclusion framework.

### Original 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Explain the role of manufacturers, producers, refurbishers and recyclers under the
E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022. Add the relevance of EPR targets and RoHS.
(Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

The Rules organise e-waste from design to final recovery. A **manufacturer** registers, manages
manufacturing e-waste and files returns. A **producer**—own-brand seller, rebrander or importer of
new/used listed EEE—registers and remains responsible for the EPR target. A **refurbisher**
repairs/assembles used EEE for the same intended use, sends process waste to a registered recycler
and can generate a refurbishing certificate. A **recycler** uses CPCB-compliant facilities,
accounts for collected/dismantled/recycled material, sends unrecycled fractions to other
registered recyclers and hazardous residue to an authorised TSDF.

For FY 2026–27, the normal target is 70% of EEE placed on the market in Y–X, where X is average
product life. Recent sellers use 20% of sales two years earlier; used-EEE imports carry 100%
end-of-life liability if not re-exported. Producers buy recycler-generated certificates. A
refurbishing certificate only defers liability; 75% later returns, and final recycling still
extinguishes EPR.

RoHS acts upstream: homogeneous material may contain at most 0.1% lead, mercury, hexavalent
chromium, PBB and PBDE, and 0.01% cadmium, subject to schedules. Producers self-declare, maintain
technical records and face CPCB sampling/recall.

Together, RoHS reduces hazardous input, refurbishment extends life and verified recycling recovers
materials. Registration or certificates without mass balance and residue control remain
insufficient.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates actors and connects upstream design, life extension
and end-of-life accountability.

### Original 5 — 20 marks

**Question:** Design an integrated city material-flow system for solid, plastic and e-waste that
also delivers worker safety and a just transition. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

An integrated city should begin with prevention and a material-flow inventory: wet, dry,
sanitary/special-care, plastic packaging and listed e-waste, mapped by source, quantity and
destination.

**At source:** enforce four-stream SWM segregation; separate C&D, biomedical, batteries and
e-waste; promote refill, repair and procurement with recycled content. Bulk generators should
register, process wet waste or meet EBWGR lawfully and disclose returns.

**Collection and sorting:** use compartmentalised tracked vehicles, ward-level drop-off points and
registered MRFs. Waste-picker groups should receive identity cards, service contracts, fair
weighment/prices, PPE, vaccination/health surveillance, insurance and grievance channels.

**Processing:** send clean wet waste to composting/biomethanation; dry material to authorised
recyclers; plastic through category-wise EPR; e-waste to registered refurbishers/recyclers; and
hazardous residues to TSDFs. Only non-recyclable, non-energy-recoverable residue should reach
sanitary landfill. Legacy dumps need audited biomining, leachate/fire control and destination
proof.

**Digital accountability:** link generator, vehicle, MRF, processor and certificate data. Audit
input/output mass balance, installed capacity, recovered-product sales, emissions and residue.
Registration and certificates must never substitute for physical verification.

**Just transition:** retain informal collection knowledge while ending unsafe burning, acid
leaching and unprotected dismantling through training, cooperative upgrading, formal aggregation
roles and access to EPR revenue.

The system should report avoided waste, segregated capture, verified material recovery, residual
share, worker injuries/exposure and neighbourhood outcomes—not merely plant capacity.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates hierarchy, infrastructure, separate laws, data and worker
justice into one operational design.

### Original 6 — 20 marks

**Question:** “Bans and EPR targets are necessary but insufficient for a circular waste economy.”
Discuss with reference to plastic and e-waste governance in India. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer**

India uses complementary controls. The plastic rules prohibit identified low-utility,
high-littering SUP items, prescribe thickness/labels and impose category-wise EPR, reuse,
recycling and recycled-content duties. The e-waste rules impose producer recycling targets,
RoHS, registered recycling and refurbishment certificates.

These measures are necessary. A targeted ban removes products for which collection/recycling is
structurally weak. EPR internalises end-of-life cost, creates demand for authorised processing and
shifts liability beyond taxpayers/ULBs. RoHS reduces hazardous material before waste arises, while
refurbishment preserves product value.

They are insufficient for five reasons. First, circularity begins with durable, repairable and
refillable design, not only end-of-life collection. Second, mixed waste destroys material quality.
Third, certificates can become paper compliance without input–output mass balance, physical audit
and lawful residue destinations. Fourth, energy recovery can displace recycling if the hierarchy
is ignored. Fifth, formal EPR may exclude waste pickers and informal collectors who provide
collection reach.

Reform should combine eco-design and repair rights, standardised source segregation, deposit/return
and reuse systems, recycled-content markets, interoperable traceability, independent audits,
strong sanctions for false certificates, and worker cooperatives linked to registered processors.
Legacy dumps and orphaned/historical e-waste need separate stock-remediation finance.

Therefore bans remove selected leakage and EPR assigns responsibility, but circularity is achieved
only when products last longer, materials return as verified secondary inputs, residues are safe
and workers share the transition's value.

**Why this earns marks:** It recognises regulatory gains, identifies structural limits and gives a
balanced circular-economy reform agenda.
