# Jainism — Solved Practice Workbook

> **Coverage inventory:** 33 MCQs; 10 primary-owned solved 2018–2026 PYQs plus 1 expressly supporting Cārvāka-owned PYQ; 8 original solved Mains models (2 × 10, 3 × 15, 3 × 20). Additional originals probe omniscience and karma's distinct causal stages.
> **Why 33, not a quota:** Items 1–24 cover substance and modes, forms of knowledge, the seven predications, qualified standpoint and bondage; 25–32 reach karma-types, stoppage/shedding, spiritual stages and embodied omniscience; 33 targets the 2026 third-versus-fourth-predication distinction. ### MCQ ANSWER KEY AND EXPLANATIONS

#### MCQ 1 — solution

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the double ascription and the joint three-jewel path formula are both standard, and together they explain why the text functions as a shared systematic grammar.
- **B:** Incorrect: it drops the double ascription that names the text's cross-sectarian standing while keeping the false restriction of its authority.
- **C:** Incorrect: statements 1 and 3 are sound, but including 2 contradicts them by denying the very cross-sectarian acceptance that makes the text quotable for both traditions.
- **D:** Incorrect: it retains statement 2, which confines the text to a single tradition, whereas the *Tattvārthasūtra* is precisely the rare work treated as authoritative across both.

**Examiner trap 1:** Shared textual authority is not doctrinal uniformity; the same *sūtra* text can anchor both traditions while canon, monastic practice and several details continue to diverge.

#### MCQ 2 — solution

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: liberation ends rebirth, while merely suppressed passions are not identical to eradicated bondage.
- **B:** Correct: distinguishes venerated teacher from cosmic governor and joins individual karmic conditioning to genuine instruction.
- **C:** Incorrect: denying creation does not make a liberated teacher a continuing distributor of karmic reward.
- **D:** Incorrect: moral teaching is not the efficient regulator of others' karmic particles; passion and activity are causally relevant.

**Examiner trap 2:** Omniscience and devotion do not entail providence; identify the karmic mechanism before attributing causal agency to a liberated teacher.

#### MCQ 3 — solution

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: reducing change to appearance is the static-monist error that Jainism explicitly rejects, since it cannot explain why the bracelet actually became a ring.
- **B:** Incorrect: a pure series of productions is closer to momentariness and leaves recognition of the same gold across the change unexplained.
- **C:** Correct: the real is defined by origination, decay and persistence together, which is what allows permanence to be predicated of substance and change of mode.
- **D:** Incorrect: making cessation definitional inverts the doctrine, because persistence is precisely the term that blocks a purely momentary reading.

**Examiner trap 3:** The three terms are joint, not alternative; a script that offers "permanence with some change" has already lost the definition, because change is constitutive rather than conceded.

#### MCQ 4 — solution

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: collapsing the two removes the distinction that lets Jainism assert permanence and change of one thing in different respects.
- **B:** Incorrect: treating the substance as a mere inference concedes the Buddhist nominalist case that Jainism answers by insisting that changes require something whose changes they are.
- **C:** Incorrect: it exactly reverses the two terms, which is the most frequent examinable confusion in this part of the syllabus.
- **D:** Correct: qualities belong to the substance as abiding characteristics, whereas modes are its changing states, which is why consciousness is a quality of the soul while delusion is a mode.

**Examiner trap 4:** Consciousness is a *guṇa* of the soul; pleasure, pain, delusion and liberated omniscience are *paryāyas* — the same soul, different states.

#### MCQ 5 — solution

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: time is admitted as a substance but is not an extended substance, which is why the tradition speaks of five extended substances plus time.
- **B:** Incorrect: matter is extended and aggregative, and it is additionally the only substance that possesses form.
- **C:** Incorrect: the medium of rest is an extended substance co-extensive with world-space, on a par with the medium of motion.
- **D:** Incorrect: space is an extended substance, articulated as world-space and the space beyond the world, and it accommodates the other substances.

**Examiner trap 5:** Six substances but five extended substances; writing "six *astikāyas*" converts a precise doctrine into an avoidable factual error.

#### MCQ 6 — solution

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: conduct is treated under right conduct and the vows; the substances in question are cosmological conditions, not moral orientations.
- **B:** Correct: they are enabling conditions of movement and rest, comparable to water for a swimming fish, and they are co-extensive with world-space.
- **C:** Incorrect: no Jaina text assigns them cosmic order and disorder; they enable motion and rest without themselves pushing or halting anything.
- **D:** Incorrect: merit and demerit are *puṇya* and *pāpa*, which appear in the ninefold list of categories, not in the sixfold list of substances.

**Examiner trap 6:** The same two Sanskrit words carry a moral sense elsewhere in Indian philosophy; in Jaina ontology they are strictly media, and the shift of sense is deliberately examined.

#### MCQ 7 — solution

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: this is the Nyāya account of a self that knows through conjunction with the internal organ; in Jainism consciousness is intrinsic.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the Advaita position; Jainism holds that souls are numerically many and remain distinct even after liberation.
- **C:** Correct: souls are innumerable, beginningless and intrinsically endowed with the infinite tetrad, which is obscured rather than absent in bondage.
- **D:** Incorrect: this is the Buddhist no-self account, which Jainism rejects because bondage and liberation require an enduring knower.

**Examiner trap 7:** Intrinsic omniscience is not empirical omniscience; the soul's infinite knowledge is present as capacity and obscured in fact, which is exactly what action-matter is introduced to explain.

#### MCQ 8 — solution

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: accommodation is the function of space; matter occupies room rather than providing it.
- **B:** Incorrect: matter is extended and aggregative, built from indivisible atoms combining into aggregates, so indefinite division without residue is not the Jaina view.
- **C:** Incorrect: every substance has modes; origination and decay belong to the definition of the real as such, not to matter alone.
- **D:** Correct: matter is the sole substance with form, which is why sound is classed as a material modification and why karmic matter can adhere at all.

**Examiner trap 8:** In Jainism sound is a modification of matter, not a quality of ether; importing the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika treatment of sound here is a silent cross-school error.

#### MCQ 9 — solution

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: ordinary perception is *sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa*, direct for practical purposes but mediated by organ, mind and karmic limitation.
- **B:** Incorrect: verbal knowledge is derived from sense-and-mind cognition rather than constituting it, so the dependence runs the other way.
- **C:** Incorrect: this is the Nyāya expectation; Jainism measures directness by absence of mediation, not by sensory clarity.
- **D:** Incorrect: ordinary perception is valid for practical dealings, and its wrongness arises only under wrong orientation, not from the senses as such.

**Examiner trap 9:** The Jaina and Nyāya senses of *pratyakṣa* differ; using the word without the qualifier "conventional" imports a rival classification into a Jaina answer.

#### MCQ 10 — solution

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: it pairs one genuinely mediated kind with one strictly direct kind, so the classification is half right and therefore wrong.
- **B:** Correct: sense-and-mind cognition and verbal or scriptural knowledge are mediated, the second being derived from the first through signs and teaching.
- **C:** Incorrect: both members are strictly direct, since the soul knows without the mediation of sense or mind, even though both remain limited.
- **D:** Incorrect: both are direct, and omniscience is the complete case of directness rather than an instance of mediation.

**Examiner trap 10:** Verbal or scriptural knowledge is not blind faith; it is a mediated cognition dependent on prior cognition and communication, and Jainism grades it accordingly.

#### MCQ 11 — solution

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: it inverts the first pair and places omniscience before knowledge of others' mental modes, breaking the graded removal of obscuration.
- **B:** Incorrect: it inserts clairvoyance between the two mediated kinds, separating verbal knowledge from the sense-and-mind cognition it derives from.
- **C:** Correct: the canonical ascending order runs sense-and-mind cognition, verbal knowledge, clairvoyance, knowledge of others' mental modes, and omniscience.
- **D:** Incorrect: it places the two direct but limited kinds before the mediated kinds, reversing the logic of progressive unveiling.

**Examiner trap 11:** The order is not a ranking of usefulness but a curve of karmic unveiling; each step marks how much knowledge-obscuring action-matter has been removed.

#### MCQ 12 — solution

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: unqualified pre-existence is the Sāṃkhya position, and it makes production add nothing at all.
- **B:** Incorrect: unqualified fresh production is the Nyāya position, and it leaves unexplained why clay yields a jar rather than anything whatever.
- **C:** Incorrect: dependence without a substrate is the Buddhist framing, which Jainism rejects because it dissolves the persisting bearer of change.
- **D:** Correct: the effect is continuous with the cause as enduring substance and novel as fresh mode, which is many-sidedness applied to causal identity.

**Examiner trap 12:** Calling Jaina causation *satkāryavāda* is a common slip; the whole point of the Jaina label is the added respect-index that Sāṃkhya does not use.

#### MCQ 13 — solution

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the thesis is that the real is *ananta-dharmātmakaṃ vastu*, so finite judgement necessarily captures an aspect rather than the whole.
- **B:** Incorrect: equal truth for all judgements is relativism, whereas Jainism grades judgements by whether they respect their own conditions.
- **C:** Incorrect: unknowability contradicts Jaina realism, which holds that aspects are objectively in the thing and are genuinely reached under standpoints.
- **D:** Incorrect: identity of respect is exactly what the *syāt* operator excludes; without different indices there would be a plain contradiction.

**Examiner trap 13:** Many-sidedness is ontology before it is logic; a script that introduces it as tolerance or open-mindedness has lost the derivation that the doctrine of judgement depends on.

#### MCQ 14 — solution

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: reducing many-sidedness to a debating convention removes the ontological claim on which the whole system rests.
- **B:** Correct: the three occupy distinct levels, and the sevenfold predication is the formal schema in which the third operates.
- **C:** Incorrect: it misassigns every level and turns standpoint theory into ethics, which no Jaina exposition supports.
- **D:** Incorrect: identifying the metaphysical thesis with the mode of assertion destroys the derivation from object to knower to sentence.

**Examiner trap 14:** The examiner is testing levels, not vocabulary; naming all three terms without assigning each its level scores as recall rather than understanding.

#### MCQ 15 — solution

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the generic standpoint is second, and it narrows what the first standpoint leaves deliberately mixed.
- **B:** Incorrect: the present-state standpoint is fourth and forms the hinge between substance-directed and mode-directed views.
- **C:** Correct: the mixed practical or purposive standpoint opens the list, blending general and particular, often through intention.
- **D:** Incorrect: the function-in-act standpoint is seventh and the strictest, so placing it first inverts the entire funnel.

**Examiner trap 15:** The list moves from loosest to strictest; reciting it out of order destroys the analytical point that partiality tightens as language becomes more literal.

#### MCQ 16 — solution

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: etymological differentiation belongs to *samabhirūḍha*, while the practical standpoint concerns ordinary dealings with particulars.
- **B:** Incorrect: class-character belongs to the generic standpoint, while the verbal standpoint attends to the expression actually used.
- **C:** Incorrect: the present-moment restriction belongs to *ṛjusūtra*; the generic standpoint deliberately abstracts from present particularity.
- **D:** Correct: on the function-in-act standpoint a person counts as a singer only while singing is actually occurring.

**Examiner trap 16:** The last three standpoints are all verbal, so they are easily swapped; distinguish attention to the word, to its derivation, and to the performance it names.

#### MCQ 17 — solution

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the operator states the respect in which the predication holds, which is why it blocks absolutisation without weakening assertion.
- **B:** Incorrect: an appearance-reality split is alien to Jaina realism, in which aspects are objectively in the thing.

#### VERIFIED PREVIOUS-YEAR QUESTIONS — TEN COMPLETE SOLUTIONS

#### PYQ 1 · 2018 · Q6(c) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** How do the Jaina philosophers explain 'bondage'? What, according to them, is the distinction between 'liberated soul' and 'bound soul'? What do the Jainas think about the condition of the 'liberated soul'? Discuss.

**Demand decoded.** Three explicit demands under a "Discuss" directive: the mechanism of bondage, the contrast between two classes of soul, and the positive condition of the liberated soul. Answer all three in order; the third is the one most scripts omit.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

Jaina bondage is a real material event, not a moral metaphor. The soul is intrinsically endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and power, yet in embodied life these stand obscured. That obscuration requires a binding factor, and Jainism identifies it as action-matter (*karma-pudgala*), a subtle form of matter drawn to the soul.

The mechanism runs through five causes: wrong vision, non-restraint, carelessness, passions and activity. Activity of body, speech and mind opens the channel of influx (*āsrava*) and determines the type and quantity of karma bound; passion supplies the adhesion and determines its duration and intensity. Bondage therefore has two faces: psychic bondage (*bhāvabandha*), the soul's own passionate modification, and material bondage (*dravyabandha*), the actual adhesion of karmic particles. The relation is beginningless (*anādi*) but not endless.

The bound soul (*baddha-jīva*) is thus an embodied, karmically conditioned knower whose native capacities are veiled and whose body, status, lifespan and felt experience are determined by the non-destructive karmas. The liberated soul (*mukta-jīva*) is one from which stoppage (*saṃvara*) has excluded fresh influx and shedding (*nirjarā*) has removed the accumulated deposit, so that nothing foreign remains.

On its condition, the Jainas are precise. The liberated soul is a perfected soul (*siddha*): disembodied, activity-free, beyond rebirth, and manifesting the infinite tetrad in full. Crucially it retains numerical individuality; there is no merger into a universal consciousness as in Advaita, and no dissolution as in accounts of no-self. Being unweighted by karmic matter it rises to the summit of the world (*siddha-śilā*).

The account is internally coherent, though the interaction of formless soul and subtle matter remains its contested joint.

#### PYQ 2 · 2019 · Q6(a) · 20 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** How is reality defined by the Jainas? How is this theory of reality reflected in their view on judgements? Discuss.

**Demand decoded.** The hinge word is "reflected". The examiner wants a derivation, not two separate essays. Define reality first; then show that the theory of judgement follows from that definition rather than accompanying it.

**Model answer (expanded for objection and reply; 20 marks).**

The Jainas define the real (*sat*) as that which possesses origination (*utpāda*), decay (*vyaya*) and persistence (*dhrauvya*) together. The definition is chosen to save two facts that rival definitions sacrifice. Pure permanence cannot explain that a bracelet became a ring; pure flux cannot explain why this is recognised as the same gold. Indexing permanence to substance (*dravya*) and change to mode (*paryāya*), with quality (*guṇa*) as the abiding characteristic, secures both.

From this follows many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*): the real is *ananta-dharmātmakaṃ vastu*, a thing constituted by innumerable aspects. Its ontology is pluralist — six substances, of which five are extended, and innumerable souls — and its causation is correspondingly qualified, the effect being continuous with the cause as substance and novel as mode.

The reflection in judgement is strict rather than analogical. Ordinary cognition is *kṣāyopaśamika*, arising from the partial destruction-cum-subsidence of knowledge-obscuring karma; it is therefore necessarily partial. A judgement by a non-omniscient knower captures an aspect, not the whole. That yields standpoint theory (*nayavāda*), and the tradition classifies seven standpoints, running from the mixed practical view through the generic, the particular and the present-state view to the three increasingly literal verbal standpoints. A standpoint that denies the remaining aspects ceases to be a standpoint and becomes its perversion (*nayābhāsa*), which is the technical form of one-sidedness (*ekāntavāda*).

Because judgement is standpoint-bound, assertion must carry the operator *syāt*: conditional predication (*syādvāda*). Combining affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under that operator, taken singly, pairwise and jointly, yields exactly seven predications (*saptabhaṅgī*) — three plus three plus one. Opposed predications are indexed to substance, place, time and mode, so existence is asserted in own-form and non-existence in other-form; contradiction would require identity of respect, which the operator excludes.

Thus the theory of judgement is not an ornament on the theory of reality but its epistemic consequence: because the object exceeds any single finite judgement, and because the knower is karmically partial, unconditioned assertion is available only to omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*).

The derivation is powerful, though Śaṃkara's residual objection presses its reflexive cost: if the proposition "every finite judgement is conditioned" is itself only conditionally true, how can it refute an absolutist? The Jaina can distinguish a **general discipline for finite predication**—state the relevant respects—from a finite assertion claiming complete knowledge of every object; requiring that discipline to apply to itself expresses consistency, not self-exemption. Yet the reply needs a non-arbitrary rule for selecting relevant respects, since indiscriminate qualification could immunise falsehood as readily as protect insight. Thus many-sided reality explains why judgements must be qualified, not why every proposed standpoint is sound.

#### PYQ 3 · 2020 · Q5(a) · 10 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** Examine the concept of Karma according to Jainism. How does it bear upon their conception of Liberation?

**Demand decoded.** "Examine" asks for accurate componential analysis, and the second sentence demands the causal link. A definition without the bearing scores half.

**Model answer (10 marks).**

For Jainism, action and moral consequence is not an unseen quality of the self, as in Nyāya *adṛṣṭa*, nor a ritual potency as in Mīmāṃsā *apūrva*, nor a mental impression as in Buddhist *vāsanā*. It is subtle matter (*karma-pudgala*) that flows toward the soul and adheres to it. Psychical action-matter (*bhāva-karma*), the soul's passionate modification, attracts material action-matter (*dravya-karma*).

Adhesion is analysed under four parameters: activity fixes the type and quantity bound, while passion fixes the duration and intensity. Eight karma-types divide into four destructive, which obscure knowledge, perception, right orientation and power, and four non-destructive, which determine felt experience, body, status and lifespan.

The bearing on liberation (*mokṣa*) is direct. Since bondage is a real deposit, liberation is subtraction and not acquisition: stoppage (*saṃvara*) closes the channel and shedding (*nirjarā*) removes the accumulation. Destroying the destructive karmas yields omniscience while still embodied; exhausting the non-destructive karmas yields the perfected soul.

Liberation therefore reveals what the soul always was, rather than conferring anything new.

#### PYQ 4 · 2021 · Q7(c) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** Explain the Jaina view of seven-fold (sapta-bhaṅgī) 'Naya'.

**Demand decoded.** The printed wording fuses two distinct doctrines — the sevenfold predication and standpoint theory. A safe answer explains **both** and expressly distinguishes them, rather than assuming the fusion is doctrinally correct.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

The printed phrase joins two doctrines that Jaina authors keep apart, so both must be explained. Standpoint theory (*nayavāda*) concerns perspectives; the sevenfold predication (*saptabhaṅgī*) concerns propositional forms.

Standpoint theory holds that every ordinary judgement expresses a *naya*, a partial but legitimate view of a many-sided real. The classical seven are: the mixed practical or purposive standpoint (*naigama*); the generic standpoint (*saṃgraha*); the practical particular standpoint (*vyavahāra*); the present-state standpoint (*ṛjusūtra*); the verbal standpoint (*śabda*); the etymological standpoint (*samabhirūḍha*); and the function-in-act standpoint (*evambhūta*), on which a person counts as a singer only while singing. The list narrows from loosest to strictest; the first three are substance-directed, the last three mode-directed and verbal, with the present-state view as the hinge.

The sevenfold predication is generated differently. Three primitives — affirmation, negation and inexpressibility — taken singly, in pairs and jointly yield exactly seven forms: *syād asti*; *syān nāsti*; *syād asti ca nāsti ca*; *syād avaktavyam*; *syād asti ca avaktavyam*; *syān nāsti ca avaktavyam*; and *syād asti ca nāsti ca avaktavyam*. Each is governed by the operator *syāt*, which states the respect and does not mean "perhaps".

The two are related but not identical: standpoints supply the respects; predications express them in disciplined sentential form. Contradiction is avoided because opposed predications are indexed to different substance, place, time or mode. Inexpressibility marks a limit of simultaneous unqualified expression, not a third truth-value.

The doctrine is therefore a theory of conditioned assertion rather than of doubt, though critics from Śaṃkara onward hold that permanent qualification dilutes assertoric force.

#### PYQ 5 · 2022 · Q5(d) · 10 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** How does Jaina view of Karma bear upon their soteriology? Critically discuss.

**Demand decoded.** Same subject-matter as 2020 Q5(a), but the directive adds "critically": the answer must end with an evaluation, not a description.

**Model answer (10 marks).**

Because Jaina action-matter is literally material, soteriology becomes a physics of removal rather than a programme of moral improvement. Activity opens the influx channel and fixes what and how much is bound; passion fixes how long and how strongly it fruits. Hence passion-free activity produces only momentary bondage (*īryāpathika*), whereas passion-coloured activity produces durable bondage (*sāmparāyika*).

Two remedies follow, because bondage has two problems. Stoppage (*saṃvara*), operated through the vows, restraint, carefulness, the virtues, contemplations and endurance of afflictions, blocks fresh influx. Shedding (*nirjarā*), operated chiefly through austerity, exhausts what is already bound. Merit does not substitute for either, since even good karma keeps the soul in the cycle of rebirth.

Critically, the doctrine gains real explanatory precision: it grounds an ethics of intention in a causal mechanism, and it explains graded spiritual progress. Its cost is equally real. Nyāya presses that material karma cannot intelligibly bind a formless consciousness, and a Cārvāka denies the whole apparatus as unverifiable.

The account is therefore coherent within Jaina ontology, not demonstrable outside it.

#### PYQ 6 · 2022 · Q6(b) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** 'The doctrine of Relativism of Jain Philosophy cannot be logically sustained without postulating 'Absolutism'.' Critically examine this view and give reasons in the favour of your answer.

**Demand decoded.** A stated view must be engaged, not paraphrased. Locate the equivocation, give the Jaina reply, then take a reasoned position and state what survives.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

The quoted view rests on an equivocation between two senses of "absolute". Jaina non-absolutism (*anekāntavāda*) denies **one-sided exclusivism** by finite knowers; it does not deny that comprehensive knowledge is possible. Once the two are separated, the alleged self-contradiction dissolves.

The charge runs: the doctrine claims universal scope; universal scope appears absolute; therefore the doctrine covertly relies on what it attacks. The Jaina reply is threefold. First, the doctrine is itself asserted *syāt*, so it obeys its own rule; reflexive qualification exhibits the discipline it teaches rather than exempting itself from it. Second, the thesis is not that all judgements are equally true but that truth is aspect-conditioned, so judgements remain gradable by whether they respect their own conditions. Third, within the system omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) is the internal case of complete knowledge, available when knowledge-obscuring karma is destroyed; ordinary discourse remains standpoint-bound because ordinary cognition is *kṣāyopaśamika*.

It should also be said that "relativism" mislabels the position. Jainism is ontologically realist: the aspects are objectively in the thing, and only finite access to them is partial. Relativism relativises truth; non-absolutism relativises exclusive claims.

Critically, the reply succeeds against the charge as printed but does not leave the field untouched. The unconditioned status has been relocated rather than abolished: omniscience now carries the completeness that finite discourse is denied. A critic may say this concedes the substance of the objection at a higher level.

My position is therefore qualified agreement with the Jaina: the doctrine is logically sustainable and not self-refuting, but it is not standpoint-free, and honesty requires saying that its consistency is purchased by admitting one privileged cognitive state.

#### PYQ 7 · 2023 · Q5(a) · 10 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** "All human knowledge is empirical and therefore relative." Critically examine Jaina theory of sevenfold judgement (saptabhaṅgīnaya) in the light of above statement.

**Demand decoded.** The quoted proposition is an empiricist thesis. The task is to test whether the sevenfold judgement is an instance of it, and to show precisely where Jainism agrees and where it refuses.

**Model answer (10 marks).**

Jainism agrees with half of the statement and rejects the other half. It agrees that human knowledge is limited: ordinary cognition is *kṣāyopaśamika*, partial because knowledge-obscuring karma is only partly removed, and ordinary perception is only conventionally direct.

It refuses the inference to relativism. The sevenfold judgement — the seven forms generated by affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under the operator *syāt* — records the **respects** in which a predication holds, using the indices of substance, place, time and mode. Each form is a definite assertion, not a probability estimate; *syāt* does not mean "perhaps".

Critically, the difference from empiricist relativism is ontological. For the empiricist, relativity attaches to knowledge because there is no access beyond experience. For Jainism, relativity attaches to finite judgement while the aspects remain objectively in the object, and omniscience remains internally possible.

Hence the statement describes a Jaina premise, not a Jaina conclusion: partiality of knowing does not entail relativity of truth.

#### PYQ 8 · 2024 · Q6(c) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** What is the distinction between Bhāvabandha and Dravyabandha, according to the Jainas? Discuss.

**Demand decoded.** A single precise distinction, discussed. The answer must state both terms exactly, show their causal order, and explain why Jainism refuses to collapse them.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

Jaina bondage has two forms which are distinct in nature yet inseparable in operation.

**Psychic bondage (bhāvabandha)** is a modification of the soul itself. Through passion, attachment, aversion, delusion and wrong disposition the soul undergoes an affective-volitional deformation. This is a state of the conscious substance, describable in the vocabulary of moral psychology.

**Material bondage (dravyabandha)** is the actual adhesion of karmic particles. Because matter alone possesses form, and because the embodied soul has space-points and is co-extensive with its body, subtle matter can be attracted and fixed. This is an event in the ontology of substances, not a description of an attitude.

The causal order is fixed: psychical action-matter (*bhāva-karma*) attracts material action-matter (*dravya-karma*). Inner deformation precedes and shapes outer adhesion. This is why the four parameters divide as they do — activity determines the type and quantity of matter bound, whereas passion, which is a psychic condition, determines duration and intensity. A monk's careful walking binds momentarily; an angry act binds deeply.

Jainism refuses to collapse the pair because each carries something the other cannot. Retain only psychic bondage and karma becomes a metaphor, leaving the obscuration of an intrinsically omniscient soul unexplained. Retain only material bondage and the system becomes crude physicalism in which intention is irrelevant to moral consequence.

The distinction also organises the path: stoppage works primarily on the psychic side by removing the causes of influx, while shedding works on the material deposit already formed.

Critically, the pairing preserves both agency and ontology, though the interaction of a formless soul with subtle matter — the very point Nyāya attacks — remains the doctrine's unresolved joint.

#### PYQ 9 · 2025 · Q7(c) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Question, as printed:** Is Jaina philosophy pluralistic and realistic? Critically discuss.

**Demand decoded.** Two labels, both to be tested. "Critically" requires that each be affirmed with the correct qualifier rather than simply endorsed.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

Jaina philosophy is strongly pluralistic and realistic, but its realism must be described as qualified.

It is pluralistic in three independent ways. Ontologically it admits six substances — soul, matter, medium of motion, medium of rest, space and time — of which five are extended. Psychologically it admits innumerable souls, each beginningless and numerically distinct. Soteriologically it holds that liberation produces many perfected souls at the summit of the world, with no merger into any absolute. Monistic reduction is refused at every level.

It is realistic in that substances, qualities and modes are objectively real; the external world is not appearance; and knowledge aims at real objects. Aspects are in the thing, not projected onto it, which is why many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) is an ontological thesis before it is a logical one.

The qualifier is epistemic. Because finite cognition is *kṣāyopaśamika* and therefore partial, judgement is standpoint-indexed and assertion carries the operator *syāt*. This yields the decisive formula: qualification of **knowledge** does not entail unreality of the **object**. Ontological realism and epistemic non-absolutism are separable, and Jainism holds both.

Critically, two costs deserve statement. First, Rāmānuja objects that a real substance bearing opposed real attributes is unintelligible, and a Dharmakīrti-style Buddhist presses that a permanent-yet-changing soul fails the test of causal efficacy; the Jaina reply, that the opposition is between aspects of substance and mode, persuades only those who already grant aspect-realism. Second, the pluralism is metaphysically expensive, requiring material action-matter and intrinsically omniscient souls that a Cārvāka will reject as unverifiable.

The defensible verdict is therefore that Jainism is genuinely pluralistic and genuinely realistic, but its realism is qualified realism rather than naive realism.

#### PYQ 10 · 2020 · Q7(c) · 15 marks · Cārvāka (supporting routed question)

> **Question, as printed:** Explain the differences of conception of Reality between Cārvāka and Jainism.
> **Routing note:** primary ownership rests with Cārvāka; it is solved here because the Jaina half of the contrast is owned by this topic and is the half most often written badly.

**Demand decoded.** "Explain the differences" is comparative and expository. Use fixed axes so that the contrast is systematic rather than anecdotal.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

Cārvāka and Jainism differ on the definition of the real, on its inventory and on the reach of knowledge.

**Definition.** Cārvāka recognises as real only what is materially constituted and, in principle, perceptible. Jainism defines the real as that which possesses origination, decay and persistence together, so permanence and change are equally real in one thing, indexed to substance and mode.

**Inventory.** Cārvāka admits four elements — earth, water, fire and air — and characteristically rejects ether. Jainism admits six substances, five of them extended, including a conscious substance and two cosmological media that Cārvāka has no use for. Where Cārvāka has one kind of stuff, Jainism has an irreducibly plural ontology.

**Consciousness.** For Cārvāka, awareness emerges from the organised body, so the living body qualified by consciousness is the self. For Jainism, consciousness is the defining quality of an independent soul, and there are innumerable such souls, intrinsically endowed with infinite knowledge that karma obscures.

**Moral consequence and liberation.** Cārvāka rejects action-consequence, rebirth and liberation. Jainism makes action-matter literally material, so bondage is a real deposit and liberation is its removal.

**Knowledge.** Cārvāka restricts valid knowledge to perception and refuses inference independent standing. Jainism accepts inference and testimony as indirect, treats ordinary perception as only conventionally direct, and grades knowledge up to omniscience.

The deepest difference is methodological. Cārvāka's ontology contracts to fit one means of knowledge; Jainism's theory of knowledge expands to fit a many-sided reality. One shrinks the world to secure certainty; the other qualifies assertion to preserve the world's complexity.

#### ORIGINAL SOLVED MAINS PRACTICE — SIX EXISTING MODELS; TWO FURTHER MODELS AFTER 2026 PYQ

#### Original 1 · 10 marks

> **Question:** Distinguish between many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*), standpoint theory (*nayavāda*) and conditional predication (*syādvāda*). Why is the order of derivation philosophically significant?

**Model answer (10 marks).**

The three doctrines occupy different levels and are derived in a fixed order.

Many-sidedness is **metaphysical**: the real object is *ananta-dharmātmakaṃ vastu*, constituted by innumerable aspects, so no single unqualified proposition exhausts it. Standpoint theory is **epistemic**: because finite cognition is partial, every ordinary judgement expresses a *naya*, one legitimate perspective among many; a standpoint that denies the rest becomes its perversion. Conditional predication is **logical-linguistic**: assertion must carry *syāt*, stating the respect in which it holds, and the sevenfold predication is its formal schema.

The order matters for two reasons. First, it makes the logic non-arbitrary. If many-sidedness came last, the *syāt* operator would look like a debating trick; derived first, it makes qualification obligatory. Second, it protects realism. The aspects are in the object; only access is partial. Reversing the order would make Jainism a theory about language or attitude rather than about the world.

Hence a script that names the three terms but not their levels has recited without explaining.

#### Original 2 · 10 marks

> **Question:** "In Jainism the moral quality of an act is fixed by passion rather than by activity." Examine this claim with reference to the parameters of bondage.

**Model answer (10 marks).**

The claim is accurate if stated with care. Karmic bondage is analysed under four parameters. Activity of body, speech and mind determines the **type** (*prakṛti*) of karma bound and the **quantity** (*pradeśa*) of matter bound. Passion determines the **duration** (*sthiti*) and the **intensity** (*anubhāga*) of its fruition.

Since duration and intensity are the parameters that actually decide how heavily an act weighs on the future of the soul, the moral seriousness of an act tracks passion rather than mere movement. The doctrinal expression of this is the contrast between momentary bondage from passion-free activity and durable bondage from passion-coloured activity: a monk's careful walking binds only momentarily, while an angry act binds deeply.

The claim must nevertheless be qualified. Activity is not morally irrelevant, since it fixes what is bound at all, and total cessation of activity belongs only to the fourteenth stage.

The precise formulation is therefore that activity determines the karmic content while passion determines its karmic weight.

#### Original 3 · 15 marks

> **Question:** Examine the Jaina claim that ordinary sense-perception is only conventionally direct. What does this reveal about the relation between Jaina epistemology and Jaina soteriology?

**Model answer (15 marks).**

Jainism divides valid knowledge into direct (*pratyakṣa*) and indirect (*parokṣa*), but measures directness by absence of mediation rather than by sensory vividness. Ordinary sense-perception depends on sense-organs, the mind, signs and karmic limitation. It is therefore only **conventional direct knowledge** (*sāṃvyavahārika pratyakṣa*) and strictly belongs with mediated cognition. What is direct in the full sense is knowledge the soul has by itself: limited clairvoyance (*avadhi*), knowledge of others' mental modes (*manaḥparyāya*) and omniscience (*kevala*).

The claim is defensible on its own terms. It preserves ordinary life, since perception remains valid for practical dealings; it distinguishes practical adequacy from metaphysical finality; and it avoids the Nyāya assumption that sense-contact is the paradigm of directness.

What it reveals is that Jaina epistemology is not a free-standing theory. The five kinds of knowledge are stations on a single curve of karmic unveiling: *mati* and *śruta* are mediated, the remaining three progressively direct, and omniscience is the state in which knowledge-obscuring karma has been destroyed. Knowledge is therefore graded by **purity**, not by method.

Two consequences follow. First, conditional predication becomes obligatory rather than optional, because a *kṣāyopaśamika* knower cannot make an unconditioned claim. Second, wrong cognition is orientation-relative: *kumati*, *kuśruta* and *vibhaṅga* are cognitions held under wrong vision, so error is a spiritual as well as a logical category.

Critically, this integration is the system's strength and its exposure. It gives Jainism a unified account of knowing and liberation, but it makes the epistemology depend on an ontology of soul and karmic matter that an empiricist critic will refuse at the outset.

#### Original 4 · 15 marks

> **Question:** "Jaina non-violence is a metaphysical consequence, not a sentiment." Critically discuss with reference to the doctrine of the soul and the mechanics of bondage.

**Model answer (15 marks).**

The claim is well founded. Jaina non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) is derived, not asserted.

The derivation begins in ontology. Souls are innumerable and graded from one-sense immobile beings — earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied — to five-sense mobile beings. Every one of them is a conscious substance intrinsically endowed with the infinite tetrad and currently obscured. Injury therefore falls on a knower, not on a thing.

It continues in the mechanics of bondage. Injury is characteristically produced by passion, and passion fixes the duration and intensity of karmic adhesion. Violence is thus not merely wrong by a rule; it is causally the most efficient way to deepen bondage. Non-violence, conversely, is an operation of stoppage: it closes the channel through which the heaviest karma enters.

The doctrine is graded rather than utopian. Ascetics take the great vows; householders take the lesser vows; and carefulness in movement, speech, alms-seeking, handling objects and bodily functions extends the principle into ordinary conduct. Extreme care toward one-sense beings is an implication of the ontology, not an excess of feeling.

Critically, two points deserve statement. First, the derivation is only as strong as its premises: a critic who denies that plants or elemental bodies house souls will accept the ethics while rejecting its scope. Second, the account is intention-sensitive rather than consequence-sensitive, which is a philosophical choice with costs of its own.

Nevertheless, the central contention holds: Jaina non-violence follows from plural souls and karmic mechanics, and treating it as sentiment removes precisely the argument that makes it distinctive among Indian ethical positions.

#### Original 5 · 20 marks

> **Question:** Analyse the Jaina fourteen stages of spiritual development. How do the two ladders explain spiritual relapse, and what does this show about the relation between insight and conduct in Jainism?

**Model answer (20 marks).**

The fourteen stages (*guṇasthāna*) map the soul's movement from false vision to final liberation, and they are graded by karmic condition rather than by external observance.

Stages one to three describe wrong, falling and mixed orientation. Stage four, right vision without restraint, is the decisive breakthrough: right vision (*samyag-darśana*) becomes stable enough to orient the path even though conduct has not yet followed. Stage five introduces partial restraint through lay vows. Stages six and seven mark full restraint with and then without negligence. Stages eight to ten describe progressive purification of the passions, from unprecedented purification through advanced purification to the condition in which only subtle greed remains. Stages eleven and twelve mark the crucial difference between suppression and destruction of delusion. Stage thirteen is the embodied omniscient being, and stage fourteen the momentary activity-free condition immediately preceding the perfected state.

The two ladders explain relapse without treating it as mere failure. On the ladder of suppression (*upaśama-śreṇi*), delusion is covered rather than removed; such a soul may reach the eleventh stage, but because suppressed delusion retains its potency the fall is necessary, not accidental. On the ladder of annihilation (*kṣapaka-śreṇi*), delusion is destroyed, so the twelfth stage is stable and the ascent continues to omniscience.

Three philosophical consequences follow. First, appearance and condition come apart: two souls may look identical at the tenth stage and have opposite futures, which is why Jainism refuses to identify spiritual attainment with observable conduct alone. Second, insight and conduct are interdependent rather than alternative. Right vision at stage four is genuine yet insufficient; conduct without right vision has no orientation; and the ladder records the joint operation of the three jewels. Third, omniscience is decoupled from disembodiment: destroying the destructive karmas yields omniscience at stage thirteen, while the non-destructive karmas sustain body, status, lifespan and felt experience until the end.

Critically, an Advaita-style objection holds that a fourteen-stage ladder mechanises liberation, substituting a karmic audit for insight. The Jaina reply is that insight which does not destroy karma has not yet done its work, and that the ladder simply records the difference between a covered and a destroyed defilement.

The scheme is therefore best evaluated as a psychology of purification with an unusually honest account of relapse, and its taxonomic weight is the price of that honesty.

#### Original 6 · 20 marks

> **Question:** "Jainism preserves the reality of the world at the cost of the simplicity of logic; Advaita preserves the simplicity of logic at the cost of the reality of the world." Critically examine this comparison.

**Model answer (20 marks).**

The comparison is illuminating but requires correction on both sides.

On the Jaina side it is broadly accurate. Jainism refuses to treat either permanence or change as appearance. The real is defined as originating, decaying and persisting together, so both are secured — but the price is that predication must be indexed. Assertion carries the operator *syāt*; opposed predications are separated by substance, place, time and mode; and the exhaustive schema of qualified assertion contains seven forms rather than a simple affirmation and denial. Ontological economy is sacrificed too: six substances, innumerable souls and material action-matter are admitted because each performs explanatory work. The world is saved, and logic becomes intricate.

On the Advaita side the statement needs care. Advaita does not abandon the world; it grades it. The world is empirically real and only ultimately *mithyā*, and sublation, not denial, is the operative notion. What Advaita gains is a single ultimate reality with a straightforward account of contradiction: opposed characterisations belong to different levels, and the lower is finally cancelled. So the accurate formulation is that Advaita preserves logical simplicity by admitting **levels of reality**, whereas Jainism preserves ontological plurality by admitting **respects of predication**.

That reformulation locates the real difference. Both schools face the problem of apparently opposed predicates. Advaita separates them **vertically**, by rank, with the lower cancelled at realisation. Jainism separates them **horizontally**, by aspect, with nothing cancelled at all. Vertical separation buys a simple logic and requires a doctrine of appearance; horizontal separation buys an unqualified realism and requires a doctrine of conditional assertion.

Critically, each pays where the other saves. Śaṃkara charges that Jaina qualification erodes assertoric force and that a doctrine which qualifies even itself cannot claim universal scope. The Jaina replies that reflexive qualification is obedience rather than exemption, and counter-charges that treating the world as ultimately *mithyā* purchases simplicity by demoting the very reality it set out to explain. Neither charge is answerable from a neutral standpoint, because each presupposes what the other denies.

The defensible verdict is that the quoted comparison identifies a genuine trade-off but mislabels the Advaita side. Jainism trades logical simplicity for unqualified realism; Advaita trades ontological plurality, not the world itself, for a single unsublated real.

### 2026 PRIMARY-OWNED VERIFIED PYQ — additional complete solution

#### 2026 · Q7(b) · 15 marks · Jainism

> **Verified question:** Explain the meaning of seven steps of judgement (Saptabhanginaya) and its application in Jaina's Syadvada for advocating a philosophy of non-absolutism (Anekantavada).

**Demand decoded:** Explain *all seven* judgements, show how qualified assertion implements many-sided reality, and defend non-absolutism without turning it into self-defeating scepticism.

**Model answer.** Many-sided reality (*anekāntavāda*) is the ontological thesis that an object possesses an enduring substance (*dravya*) and mutable modes (*paryāya*). Standpoint (*naya*) selects an aspect, while conditional predication (*syādvāda*) explicitly restricts the scope of what that standpoint asserts. "In a certain respect" (*syāt*) does not mean "perhaps": it binds a predicate to a determinate respect, place and time. Sevenfold judgement (*saptabhaṅgī*) systematically combines affirmation, negation and inexpressibility: (1) in some respect it is (*syād asti*); (2) in some respect it is not (*syān nāsti*); (3) in some respects it is and is not (*syād asti ca nāsti ca*); (4) in some respect it is inexpressible (*syād avaktavyaḥ*); (5) in some respects it is and is inexpressible; (6) in some respects it is not and is inexpressible; (7) in some respects it is, is not and is inexpressible. The last four arise because opposed qualifications cannot be asserted together in one *unqualified simultaneous utterance*, not because reality is wholly unknowable.

Consider a clay pot: it exists here and now as a pot, does not exist as a cloth or at a time before its manufacture, and combines the two predications under explicitly different qualifications. Likewise a substance persists while a mode begins or ends. This is neither the contradiction "the same pot both exists and does not exist in precisely the same respect" nor the relativist claim that any sentence is true. The seven forms exhaust combinations of three predicative possibilities, not seven ontologically independent objects.

The strongest objection, associated with Vedāntic critics, asks whether a conditional thesis can itself claim universal validity; if even *anekāntavāda* is only conditionally true, how can it correct a dogmatist? The Jaina reply distinguishes the universally applicable **method of specifying respects** from an unqualified assertion that one partial predicate exhausts the whole; still, it owes a principled account of which standpoints are legitimate. Qualified non-absolutism thus protects determinate, corrigible knowledge, not arbitrary equivalence of incompatible claims.

**Outstanding-answer check:** Enumerate the seven without collapsing (3) into contradiction or (4) into ignorance; connect ontology → standpoint → conditional speech; include a worked pot example, a reflexivity objection and a limited defence.

### ADDITIONAL ORIGINAL SOLVED MAINS — uncovered syllabus demands

#### Original 7 · 20 marks — kevala, partial standpoints and liberation

**Question:** Critically examine whether Jaina claims of omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) make conditional judgement (*syādvāda*) dispensable. Relate the answer to the distinction between partial knowledge and the liberated soul.

**Model answer.** Jaina epistemology separates limited standpoint-knowledge (*naya*) from complete direct knowledge (*kevala-jñāna*). A pot can be predicated as existent in its own substance, place, time and mode and non-existent in another respect; conditioned speech (*syāt*) prevents any one finite perspective from pretending to exhaust a many-sided reality (*anekānta*). By contrast, the omniscient perfected knower (*kevalin*) apprehends all aspects of each substance and all its modes directly, unobscured by knowledge-obscuring karmic matter. This does not imply that the *world* is contradictory or that everyday speakers may dispense with conditions: complete cognition and selective linguistic reporting are different achievements.

The *Tattvārtha-sūtra* links removal of karmic obscurations to perfect knowledge. A liberated soul (*siddha*) retains individual identity and perfected knowledge, unlike Advaita's absorption of individuality. The conditional method is therefore primarily a discipline for finite judgements; if an omniscient knower chooses to speak in a limited sentence, that sentence still needs its respect specified to avoid communicating a partial truth as an absolute one.

The strongest challenge is epistemic: how can finite hearers verify a claim that any *kevalin* knows every mode without already trusting testimony or possessing such cognition? A Jaina may cite the staged removal of obscuring karma and the coherence of partial knowledge's progressive correction, but a progression from improved accuracy to literally unrestricted knowledge is not demonstrative. Further, if even the theory of standpoints itself is conditional, its claim to govern every judgement needs a reflexive defence. The distinction between complete apprehension and context-bounded assertion addresses a formal tension; whether perfect apprehension is actually attained remains contested.

**Outstanding-answer check:** Separate ontology, epistemic access and speech; identify karmic obscuration, complete knowledge and reflexive challenge, with a restricted verdict.

#### Original 8 · 15 marks — real karma and causal mechanism

**Question:** Distinguish influx (*āsrava*), bondage (*bandha*), stoppage (*saṃvara*) and shedding (*nirjarā*). Is the postulate of subtle karmic matter explanatory or an additional metaphysical burden? Evaluate.

**Model answer.** In the *Tattvārtha-sūtra*'s path, activity of body, speech and mind opens the route for influx (*āsrava*) of subtle karmic matter (*pudgala*). Passion (*kaṣāya*) binds that matter to the conscious soul (*bandha*) and affects its duration and intensity. Stoppage (*saṃvara*) blocks fresh influx through restraint and discipline; shedding (*nirjarā*) expels previously accumulated matter through austerity and purification. One cannot equate stopping a leak with emptying an already filled reservoir: both are needed before liberation (*mokṣa*). Psychic bondage (*bhāvabandha*) concerns passionate modification; material bondage (*dravyabandha*) is the postulated adhesion of matter.

The theory explains why habitual choices constrain later experience without declaring the soul intrinsically impure. It also connects the vows of non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) to a causal discipline, rather than reducing them to sentiment. Yet a conscious, non-material self binding imperceptible particles is not a transparent mechanical relation; describing attachment as "adhesion" risks renaming the interaction without explaining how unlike substances affect each other. The Jaina invokes the soul's bodily extension (*pradeśa*) and the role of passions as mediators, but an opponent can ask how these are known independently of the desired soteriology. The model's strength is a fine distinction between stopping new karma and eliminating old; its cost is a real but hard-to-verify soul–matter bridge.

**Outstanding-answer check:** Preserve all four causal stages and both senses of bondage; give one explanatory gain, one soul–matter objection and a qualified response.
