#### In plain words

Somebody, somewhere, has to be able to say "this is now settled" — otherwise every dispute reopens forever and collective life dissolves into rival commands. That capacity to settle finally is what the word *sovereignty* names. Notice at once what this does **not** mean. A gunman can make you hand over your wallet; he cannot make the transfer *lawful*, and nobody treats his order as a reason. A government can issue commands today and be dismissed tomorrow; its authority is real but derived. Sovereignty is the claim standing behind both: the claim to be the last word in the political community.

The moment you state the claim, you can see why four thinkers fight over it. Final authority is exactly the thing that liberty fears, that plural associations resist, that federal constitutions split up, that morality may condemn and that international life constrains. So the syllabus item is not "define sovereignty"; it is "adjudicate a dispute about how final authority may be located, structured and limited".

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The core statement.** Sovereignty means the **supreme, final and authoritative power within a political community**. A state is distinguished from other associations because it claims a power of ultimate decision: to issue binding rules, adjudicate disputes, maintain order and represent the community externally.

⚠️ **The supporting argument.** Without some form of final authority, collective life would fragment into competing commands; every unresolved dispute would generate a rival claimant, and the community would have no procedure for closure.

⚠️ **What the concept presupposes.** Three things: an organised political community; a relation of ruler and ruled; and a distinction between **ordinary** authority and the **highest** authority. Remove any of the three and the word loses its work.

✅ **The controlling distinction.** Sovereignty is **not identical with mere force**. A bandit may coerce, but sovereignty implies recognised or effective final authority within a political order. This single distinction is what allows the whole clause to be a *philosophical* dispute rather than a description of who has the biggest army.

⚠️ **Where the constitutional example fits.** A constitution may distribute functions among legislature, executive and judiciary and still embody a structure for final decision. Distribution of function is not, by itself, the absence of finality — and noticing that is what stops the beginner's objection below from being decisive.

⚠️ **Why the concept becomes controversial.** Because the need for final authority clashes with **liberty, plurality, federalism, morality and global interdependence** — and because each printed thinker writes from inside a different one of those pressures. Bodin writes amid civil war and seeks unity; Austin seeks juristic clarity; Laski fears absolutism; Kautilya ties power to welfare and strategic necessity. The controversy is therefore not merely semantic; it is a dispute about order, freedom and statecraft.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Bodin** ✅ — sovereignty is the **absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth**; the first systematic modern statement, framed against religious civil war.
- **Austin** ✅ — the sovereign is a **determinate human superior**, habitually obeyed by the bulk of a society and habitually obeying no one; the sharpest legal formulation.
- **Laski** ✅ — the state is **one association among many**; absolute sovereignty is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic.
- **Kautilya** ✅ — sovereignty is centred on the ruler (*svāmī*) but the state is an organism of **seven limbs (saptāṅga)** oriented to **security and welfare (yogakṣema)**.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Sovereignty is a claim about **finality, not about force**: the question the paper actually sets is never whether a political order needs a last word, but whether that last word must be single, legally unlimited and morally self-justifying.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**final and authoritative power** · **not reducible to force** · **ordinary against highest authority** · **the five pressure points** · **monist–pluralist spectrum** · **located, structured, limited** · **finality, not omnipotence**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Sovereignty names the capacity for final decision within a political community, and is therefore distinct from the mere ability to coerce. **Named evidence.** Bodin defines it as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth, and Austin as the determinate human superior habitually obeyed by the bulk of society — two very different formulations, but both are answers to the question "who or what decides last?", not to the question "who is strongest?". **Analysis.** That is why a bandit is excluded by definition while a constitutionally limited legislature is not: the criterion is recognised or effective **finality of decision**, and a constitution that distributes functions may still contain a structure of finality. **Qualification.** The concept nevertheless becomes contested the moment it is stated, because finality is precisely what liberty, associational plurality, federal division, moral judgement and international interdependence each resist — which is why the syllabus prints four rival names rather than a single definition.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ✅.** In modern constitutional democracies power is so dispersed — between houses, levels, courts and electorates — that speaking of one "supreme" authority is misleading.
- **Reply ⚠️.** The objection weakens **monistic** theories, but not the broader inquiry. The concept remains useful if one distinguishes the legal, political and external dimensions instead of assuming one undivided centre; the question then becomes *which* dimension the stem is testing.
- **Residual limit ❓.** The dispute is genuine and survives the reply. Whether a distributed constitutional order still contains a single point of finality, or merely a set of procedures, is exactly what Kelsen, Hart and Laski disagree about later in this session. Never assert that the objection has been answered; state which dimension survives it.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Opening with a definition of the **state** instead of a definition of **sovereignty**, and then never returning to finality. The state is the bearer; sovereignty is the claim.
2. Treating all four printed thinkers as saying the same thing about "supremacy". The paper repeatedly asks for exact differences, and 2019 Q3(a) makes that demand explicit.
3. Answering the general question "is sovereignty good?" instead of the dimensional question the stem sets. Fix the axis — internal/external, legal/political, monist/pluralist — before writing a single verdict.

#### Mini recap

Finality, not force. Three presuppositions, one controlling distinction, five pressure points. The four printed names are four answers to *where*, *how* and *how far* — and the axis must be named before any of them is graded.

#### In plain words

Sovereignty looks in two directions at once. Facing inward it says: inside this territory, my decision is the last one. Facing outward it says: no other state gets to make that decision for me. Those are different claims and they can come apart — a government may be perfectly obeyed at home while being unable to resist a stronger neighbour, and a state may be recognised by everyone abroad while unable to collect taxes in half its own districts.

Then there is the classical list of five adjectives that textbooks attach to sovereignty. Learn them, but learn each with the sentence that limits it — because every one of the five, stated flat, is false, and an examiner who has read Laski will notice.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Internal sovereignty** is supreme authority over persons and associations within the state. It is central to all four printed thinkers.
✅ **External sovereignty** is independence from external control in the society of states. It is central to Kautilya's diplomacy and to the modern globalisation debate.
⚠️ **Why they must not be collapsed:** a state may control internal life yet be constrained externally by stronger powers; equally, it may be externally recognised yet internally fragile.
✅ **Kautilya's use:** the circle-of-states (*maṇḍala*) theory assumes that external sovereignty is always **strategic and relational** rather than merely declaratory — a point that pays directly in the 2025 part.

**The five classical attributes, each with its required qualification** ✅

| Attribute | Exam-safe meaning | Required qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Absoluteness** | No superior human law-maker legally commands the sovereign within the political order | Legal non-subordination does not prove moral infallibility or practical omnipotence |
| **Permanence / continuity** | Governments and office-holders change while sovereignty continues with the state | Permanence means continuity while the state remains independent, not literal eternity |
| **Universality** | Sovereign jurisdiction extends in principle across persons and associations within the territory | Immunities and autonomous spheres may exist by constitutional or legal recognition |
| **Inalienability** | Sovereignty cannot be transferred away without the old state losing that sovereign status | Delegation, devolution or treaty commitment is not automatically alienation |
| **Indivisibility** | The classical theory requires one final legal source | Governmental competences may be distributed even where monists insist legal finality remains one |

✅ **Bodin–Austin placement.** Bodin supplies the defining pair **absolute and perpetual**; Austin gives the sharpest determinate-superior formulation; the five-part list is a standard **consolidation** of their monist tradition, not five claims uniquely invented by either.

⚠️ **The continuity bridge you will need in Session 12.** Austin's habit theory struggles to explain succession and the persistence of old law — exactly the *permanence* attribute. Kelsen's hierarchy of norms and Hart's rules later repair that problem **without restoring a personal absolute sovereign**.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Bodin** ✅ — *absolute and perpetual*: the two adjectives that generate the whole classical list.
- **Austin** ✅ — determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable, inalienable: the sharpened legal restatement.
- **Kautilya** ✅ — the circle of states (*maṇḍala*) as the working theory of the **external** face.
- **Laski** ✅ — the standing objection that universality and indivisibility misdescribe a society of real associations.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The classical attributes are defensible only in their qualified form: sovereignty is **absolute without being morally infallible, permanent without being eternal, universal without abolishing recognised immunity, inalienable although competences may be delegated, and indivisible in legal source although divisible in competence**.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**internal against external** · **absoluteness** · **permanence and continuity** · **universality** · **inalienability** · **indivisibility** · **delegation is not alienation** · **competence against finality** · **relational external sovereignty**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The classical attributes of sovereignty survive criticism only when each is stated with its own limiting clause. **Named evidence.** Bodin supplies the defining pair — *absolute and perpetual* — and Austin sharpens the list into determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable authority; but Bodin himself concedes that the sovereign is bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm. **Analysis.** The concession shows what the adjectives were ever meant to do: *absolute* denies a superior **law-maker**, not a superior **norm**; *inalienable* denies the giving away of the sovereign status, not the delegation of competences; *indivisible* asserts one final legal source, not the impossibility of splitting governmental functions. **Qualification.** So stated, the list is compatible with federal division and treaty commitment; stated flat, it is refuted by the first federation or treaty a critic names — which is precisely how the objections in Sessions 10 and 14 get their force.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** Global interdependence has made external sovereignty obsolete; states no longer decide anything of consequence alone.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Globalisation changes the **mode** of external sovereignty but does not abolish the question of who makes final decisions on war, treaty, economy or political membership. The issue is **transformed** sovereignty, not vanished sovereignty.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The reply concedes something real: where material dependence is severe, legal independence may survive while effective autonomy declines. Session 19 turns that concession into the structure of the 2020 Q4(b) answer instead of hiding it.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Reciting the five attributes without their qualifications. Every unqualified attribute is refutable by a first-year example, and the examiner will supply it.
2. Omitting **permanence** and **universality** from the classical set, which happens whenever a candidate remembers only Austin's shorter list.
3. Treating external sovereignty as a mere declaration of independence. For Kautilya it is **positional and relational** — which is exactly why the 2025 quoted line is answerable.

#### Mini recap

Two faces that can come apart; five attributes that are only defensible qualified. Absolute ≠ infallible, permanent ≠ eternal, universal ≠ immunity-free, inalienable ≠ undelegatable, indivisible ≠ competence-undividable.

*Caption: two questions, two answerers, one divergence. The final box is the sentence that converts this session into marks in 2021 Q1(c) and 2023 Q2(a).*

#### In plain words

Ask "who may make law here?" and you get a legal answer — a named body with a recognised competence. Ask "who decides what that body will actually do?" and you often get a different answer entirely: a party high command, a mass movement, a financial constraint, an army, a church. Both questions are real. The mistake is to think that answering one has answered the other.

Austin built his theory around the first question and made it beautifully precise. Laski attacked from the second and showed how much the first leaves out. Neither is simply wrong; the failure is to collapse them.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Legal sovereignty** is the authority recognised by law as competent to make law. It is Austin's preferred angle.
✅ **Political sovereignty** is the actual power behind legal institutions — the force that can compel or shape the legal sovereign. It belongs to democratic and sociological analysis and is directly relevant to Laski.
✅ **The core statement.** Legal sovereignty names the **juristic source of valid law**; political sovereignty names the **real locus of social power**.
⚠️ **The argument.** The two often diverge. A constitution may vest law-making power in one body, while parties, public opinion, organised interests, military power, religion or economic classes determine what that body can actually do.
⚠️ **Placement of the two thinkers.** Austin's theory is strongest exactly where law is analytically separated from politics; Laski's critique begins exactly where that separation obscures the lived distribution of power.

⚠️ **Why this distinction is the hinge of the whole clause.** Almost every objection to monism in Sessions 10, 11, 14 and 15 is a *political-sovereignty* objection aimed at a *legal-sovereignty* thesis. Naming the distinction before you deploy the objection is what separates a philosophical answer from a list.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Austin** ✅ — sovereignty as a legal relation of command, obedience and sanction: the purest statement of the first question.
- **Laski** ✅ — allegiance is earned by what institutions do, not owed because of legal title: the purest statement of the second.
- **Bodin** ⚠️ — mixes the two deliberately, because he is theorising law, office **and** state-building at once.
- **Kautilya** ⚠️ — integrates power, welfare and diplomacy, and so does not sit cleanly on either side of the modern distinction.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Legal and political sovereignty answer two different questions — **who may make law** and **what decides what the law-maker does** — and most objections to monistic sovereignty are political answers offered against a legal thesis, which is why the distinction must be stated before the objection is used.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**juristic source of valid law** · **real locus of social power** · **divergence** · **validity against efficacy** · **Austin's angle** · **Laski's angle** · **do not let one question swallow the other**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The most common failure in sovereignty answers is a category error: answering a question about legal competence with evidence about political power. **Named evidence.** Austin defines sovereignty as the relation between a determinate human superior and habitually obedient subjects, which is a claim about the **source of legal validity**; Laski replies that the state must compete for allegiance and cannot claim unlimited obedience by title alone, which is a claim about the **distribution of real power**. **Analysis.** The two claims can both be true at once, because a constitution may vest law-making in one body while parties, organised interests or economic classes decide what that body is able to do — so the divergence is a feature of political life, not a contradiction between the theorists. **Qualification.** The balanced verdict must therefore refuse both reductions: Austin overstates the sufficiency of legal validity, pluralists overstate the sufficiency of political efficacy, and a good answer keeps both questions open rather than declaring one of them the real one.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** If political sovereignty always overrides legal sovereignty, jurisprudence becomes pointless — the law is simply a record of who won.
- **Reply ⚠️.** The reply is not to abolish jurisprudence but to recognise that **legal validity and political efficacy are different questions**. Austin overstates the first; pluralists overstate the second; a balanced answer keeps both.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The reply does not tell us how much efficacy a legal order may lose before its validity claim becomes empty. Kelsen concedes exactly this by making by-and-large effectiveness a *condition* of validity — a concession Session 12 states rather than hides.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Confusing legal and political sovereignty, then attacking Austin with evidence he never claimed to explain. This is trap 7 of the owner's standing list.
2. Treating "political sovereignty" as a synonym for popular sovereignty. Political sovereignty is a **sociological** category; popular sovereignty is a **legitimacy** claim, and Session 5 separates them.
3. Writing the divergence as if it were a defect of the constitution. It is a general feature of political life and is precisely why the distinction is analytically useful.

#### Mini recap

Two questions, not one. Austin owns the first, Laski attacks from the second, Bodin mixes them and Kautilya integrates them. Never let one question swallow the other.

```text
   THE FOUR COMBINATIONS
   +--------------------+-------------------------+------------------------+
   |                    | OBEYED IN PRACTICE      | NOT OBEYED             |

   |                    | order                   | but no control         |

   |                    | without legal warrant   | pretender              |
   +--------------------+-------------------------+------------------------+

   WHERE EACH THINKER SITS
   AUSTIN -> habitual obedience gives his theory a DE FACTO EDGE
   BODIN  -> perpetuity attaches to the OFFICE, not the magistrate

```

*Caption: four cells, two diagnoses. The bottom rail is the highest-value line on the page: it shows that Bodin and Austin diverge on the office/obedience axis, not only on limits.*

#### In plain words

A person may have every legal right to rule and command nobody. Another may command everybody and have no legal right at all. Political history is full of both, and the vocabulary for the difference is *de jure* against *de facto*. A second, related split runs between the office that formally holds authority and the people who actually exercise it — a crown against a cabinet, a ceremonial head against a party leadership.

These are not decorative distinctions. They are how you avoid saying something false about Austin, who leans on obedience, and about Bodin, who leans on office.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **De jure sovereignty** is authority lawful in title and formal right — the useful category for constitutional analysis.
✅ **De facto sovereignty** is authority actually obeyed in practice regardless of legal title — the useful category for revolution, coups and unstable orders.
✅ **The core statement.** One may possess the lawful title to rule without actual control, or actual control without lawful title.
⚠️ **Why the distinction matters.** Obedience can attach to success, fear, legitimacy or legality in different combinations; the categories keep those grounds apart.
⚠️ **Thinker placement, and this is the mark-bearing sentence.** Austin's emphasis on **habitual obedience** gives his theory a *de facto* edge, whereas Bodin is more concerned with the **permanent office-character** of sovereign authority.

✅ **Titular sovereignty** is authority vested formally in an office or symbol — a crown, a ceremonial headship, a constitutional title.
✅ **Actual sovereignty** is authority effectively exercised by those who really govern — a cabinet, a party leadership, a ruling coalition, a strategic bureaucracy.
✅ **The core statement.** The formal bearer of sovereignty may differ from its effective wielder.
⚠️ **What it explains.** Constitutional monarchies, parliamentary government and many republics where nominal and real authority diverge. It also prevents the careless identification of "the state" with whoever appears at the top ceremonially.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Austin** ✅ — habitual obedience by the bulk of society: the criterion that gives the theory its factual edge.
- **Bodin** ✅ — perpetuity attaches to the **office**, not to a temporary magistrate or delegated official.
- **Kautilya** ⚠️ — a ruler without treasury (*kośa*) or ministers (*amātya*) may possess title but not effective sovereignty: the *saptāṅga* version of the same point.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Austin's criterion of habitual obedience gives his sovereign a **de facto edge**, whereas Bodin's perpetuity attaches to the **office** rather than the office-holder — so the two founders of the monist tradition already diverge on where finality is anchored, before any critic arrives.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**de jure** · **de facto** · **titular** · **actual** · **lawful title without control** · **control without title** · **office against magistrate** · **the ceremonial identification error**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The de jure/de facto and titular/actual pairs are not textbook ornaments; they mark the exact point at which Bodin and Austin part company. **Named evidence.** Bodin's second defining adjective is *perpetual*, and he explains it as authority attaching to the sovereign **office** rather than to a temporary magistrate; Austin's second defining condition is **habitual obedience by the bulk** of a given society, which is a claim about observed compliance. **Analysis.** Anchoring finality in an office makes sovereignty survive the death of the holder without any factual question arising, whereas anchoring it in obedience makes the theory hostage to whether compliance has in fact formed — which is why the succession problem bites Austin and not Bodin. **Qualification.** The divergence should not be overstated into an opposition: Bodin's office is still supreme over positive law and Austin's superior is still determinate, so the two remain monists — they simply locate the durability of the claim in different places.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** *De facto* power alone should count, because politics is about effectiveness and legal title is decoration.
- **Reply ⚠️.** That is too reductionist. Political order usually seeks legality **as well as** effectiveness; hence de jure and de facto status are analytically separable and normatively important.
- **Objection ❓.** Such distinctions dilute the concept of sovereignty beyond usefulness.
- **Reply ⚠️.** On the contrary, they **rescue** the concept from oversimplification: the examination repeatedly rewards answers that distinguish legal title, actual power, office and obedience.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** Neither reply tells us how long an effective but untitled authority must last before it becomes lawful, which is exactly the point at which Kelsen's efficacy condition, in Session 12, becomes unavoidable.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Using *de facto* as a synonym for *illegitimate*. It is a description of obedience, not a moral verdict.
2. Identifying "the state" with the ceremonial head of state. The titular/actual pair exists precisely to prevent that.
3. Writing that Austin and Bodin agree because both are monists. They diverge on the office/obedience anchor, and saying so is a cheap, high-value differentiation in 2019- and 2022-type comparison stems.

#### Mini recap

Four cells and two anchors. Title against obedience; office against wielder. Austin leans factual, Bodin leans institutional — and that single observation is worth a paragraph in any comparison answer.

#### In plain words

"Sovereignty belongs to the people" is one of the most repeated sentences in political thought, and one of the least examined. It answers a **legitimacy** question — where does the right to rule come from? — and it is not the same as the **self-determination** question of which community counts as a people entitled to its own state. Keeping the two apart is worth marks, because they generate different problems: the first has to explain how a shifting population can be represented without being reduced to today's majority; the second has to explain what happens when the boundaries of nation, people and state do not coincide.

Rousseau is the bridge. He puts sovereignty in the general will and refuses to hand it over to any government. That is exactly what Austin cannot accommodate.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Popular sovereignty.** The people in their corporate capacity are the source of legitimate public authority; government is their agent. **Main problem:** "the people" must be institutionally represented without being reduced to a temporary majority or to the incumbent government.
⚠️ **National sovereignty.** A national community claims collective self-determination and territorial political independence. **Main problem:** nation, people and state may not coincide, and the claim can conflict with minorities or rival national identities.
✅ **The Rousseau bridge.** Rousseau locates sovereignty in the **general will**, understood as a claim about the **common good** rather than the numerical sum of private preferences. Sovereignty remains with the people and is **not identical** with whichever government temporarily exercises power.
⚠️ **The controlling distinction.** Popular sovereignty answers the internal legitimacy question *from whom does authority arise?*; national sovereignty primarily answers the collective self-determination question *which people may govern itself?* **Neither expression makes every majority decision morally sovereign.**
✅ **Source control.** O. P. Gauba's *An Introduction to Political Theory*, searchable local PDF pp. 189–193, directly supports popular sovereignty and the general will; the *Cambridge Dictionary* self-determination entry, searchable local PDF p. 858, supports the strict territorial-independence sense used for the bounded national-sovereignty bridge.

⚠️ **The consequence for Austin, which is the whole reason this session exists.** Austin requires a **determinate** human superior. The people are indeterminate, changing and institutionally mediated. So either the sovereign is not the people, or the sovereign is not determinate — Austin cannot keep both, and this is the sharpest form of the democracy objection that Session 11 and the 2021 part both turn on.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Rousseau** ✅ — the **general will** as a claim about the common good; sovereignty stays with the people, government is agent not principal. Used here strictly as the popular-sovereignty counterpoint to Austin; the contract theory itself belongs to **Individual and State**.
- **Austin** ✅ — the determinate human superior: the requirement that popular sovereignty defeats.
- **Laski** ⚠️ — allegiance earned from below rather than claimed from above: the pluralist cousin of the popular-sovereignty intuition, though Laski disperses authority among associations rather than concentrating it in a unified people.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Popular sovereignty and Austinian sovereignty cannot both be right in the same breath: Austin's sovereign must be **determinate**, whereas "the people" are indeterminate, changing and institutionally mediated — so the democratic claim does not merely limit Austin's sovereign, it dissolves the very determinacy his definition requires.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**corporate capacity of the people** · **government as agent** · **general will** · **common good, not aggregated preference** · **collective self-determination** · **nation ≠ people ≠ state** · **no majority is morally sovereign** · **indeterminacy against determinacy**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Popular sovereignty is not a softer version of Austin's doctrine but its direct competitor, because the two disagree about the one property Austin cannot surrender. **Named evidence.** Austin defines the sovereign as a **determinate human superior** habitually obeyed by the bulk of society; Rousseau locates sovereignty in the **general will**, a standing claim about the common good that remains with the people and is never transferred to the government that exercises power. **Analysis.** If the sovereign is the people, then the sovereign is a changing, institutionally mediated body whose will has to be constructed through representation — precisely the indeterminacy Austin's definition excludes; conversely, if determinacy is preserved by naming a legislature as sovereign, then the people have been demoted from sovereign to electorate. **Qualification.** This does not license the opposite excess: neither popular nor national sovereignty makes every majority decision morally sovereign, and the general will is defined by reference to the common good rather than by counting heads — so the argument limits Austin without handing victory to majoritarianism.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** Parliament can simply be treated as the determinate superior in a democracy, which restores Austin.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Even then, a parliament is constitutionally constrained, electorally dependent and politically shaped by parties and opinion; democratic sovereignty is therefore more complex than Austin's legal monism allows.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The reply establishes that Austin's model fits democracy badly; it does not establish that "the people" is a coherent sovereign agent. That second problem is genuine and unresolved, and the honest answer names it rather than pretending popular sovereignty is unproblematic.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Equating popular with national sovereignty. One locates the **source** of public authority; the other concerns **collective self-determination and independence**. This is trap 17 of the owner's standing list.
2. Turning the general will into "whatever the majority votes for". Rousseau's term is defined by the **common good**, not by aggregation, and the collapse destroys the argument against Austin.
3. Importing the social contract, the state of nature or political obligation. Those belong to **Individual and State**; here Rousseau is a bridge, not a syllabus thinker.

#### Mini recap

Two questions, one bridge, one consequence. Source against self-determination; general will against aggregation; and Austin's determinacy dissolved by the very body democratic theory calls sovereign.

*Caption: crisis → move → definition → the two adjectives → the pre-eminent mark. The bottom box is what separates a Bodin answer from a Hobbes answer.*

#### In plain words

Imagine a country where a church, a nobility, a set of town corporations and a king each insist that their word is final. There is no procedure left for ending an argument, and the argument is being settled by killing people. That was Bodin's France. His response was not to praise kings; it was to identify what a political community must possess if it is to stop tearing itself apart: one authority whose law-making word is last.

He then packed that requirement into two adjectives. **Absolute** — no human superior inside the commonwealth commands it. **Perpetual** — it belongs to the office and outlives the person. Everything else in Bodin follows from those two words, including the limits that Session 7 supplies.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Historical context and purpose.** Bodin formulates sovereignty in the context of religious civil wars and the breakdown of political unity in sixteenth-century France.
⚠️ **The argument.** His doctrine responds to **disorder**: if rival churches, estates and feudal powers all claim final authority, the commonwealth disintegrates. He therefore seeks a stable centre of command capable of maintaining order above faction.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Social peace requires a final law-giving authority not reducible to shifting religious or feudal claims.
✅ **The controlling distinction.** Bodin is **not merely praising despotism**; he is theorising state unity under conditions of civil conflict.

✅ **Core doctrine.** Sovereignty is the **absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth**, and the argument runs in three numbered steps:
1. **Absolute** means not subject to any human superior within the commonwealth. ✅
2. **Perpetual** means enduring and attached to **office**, not to a temporary magistrate or delegated official. ✅
3. The sovereign's **pre-eminent mark** is the power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without needing consent from a superior. ✅

⚠️ **Presupposition.** Law requires a source that is itself not legally derivative from another earthly authority.
✅ **The distinction that prevents the standard error.** "Absolute" in Bodin does not mean arbitrary in every sense; it means **supreme in the hierarchy of positive law**.
⚠️ **The worked illustration.** A magistrate may issue commands, but only the sovereign can legislate in the final sense — which is exactly what "perpetual" is doing: separating the office from the officer.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Bodin, *Six Books of the Commonwealth*** ✅ — the definition, the two adjectives and the law-making mark.
- **The French wars of religion** ✅ — the context that explains why unity, not liberty, is the organising value.
- **Hobbes** ⚠️ — the comparative foil: a genuinely security-first absolutism against which Bodin's higher-law limits become visible. Hobbes's own contract theory belongs to **Individual and State**.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Bodin's two adjectives do two different jobs: **absolute** removes any human superior inside the commonwealth, while **perpetual** detaches authority from the person and fixes it in the office — and it is the second, not the first, that turns a strong ruler into a *state*.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth** · **the wars of religion** · **unity above faction** · **office, not magistrate** · **make and unmake law without consent from a superior** · **supreme in the hierarchy of positive law** · **not lawless**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Bodin's importance is not that he made rulers stronger but that he made authority **impersonal**. **Named evidence.** He defines sovereignty as the *absolute and perpetual* power of a commonwealth, glossing "perpetual" as authority attaching to the sovereign office rather than to a temporary magistrate or delegated official, and identifying its pre-eminent mark as the power to make and unmake law for all subjects without a superior's consent. **Analysis.** Perpetuity is doing the state-building work: a power that dies with its holder is a personal ascendancy, whereas a power that survives the holder is an institution, and only an institution can supply the continuous procedure of closure that a society torn by confessional civil war actually lacked. **Qualification.** The gain is bought at a price he never fully discharges: an authority defined as having no human superior is, by construction, hard to restrain from within, and the higher-law limits he adds are weaker than modern constitutional checks — a tension the 2022 part asks you to press.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** Because the theory emerges from crisis, it merely sacralises centralised monarchy.
- **Reply ⚠️.** It certainly strengthens monarchy, but its lasting importance lies in giving the modern state a conceptual language of **permanent, impersonal authority** — a language later used by republics and democracies that Bodin never contemplated.
- **Objection ❓.** If sovereignty is absolute, it negates all rights and liberties.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Bodin allows that sovereignty is above ordinary positive law while still bound by higher normative constraints such as divine and natural law; his absolutism is **juristic, not caprice**.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The reply postpones rather than removes the difficulty, because the sovereign is also the judge of what those higher constraints require. Session 7 states that residual problem instead of concealing it.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Writing Bodin as Hobbes. Bodin allows higher-law restraints; the Hobbesian sovereign does not sit under the same structure of limits. This is trap 8 of the owner's standing list.
2. Explaining "perpetual" as "eternal". It means attached to the **office** and enduring across office-holders, not everlasting.
3. Turning the historical context into the answer. Two lines of civil-war background are worth marks; two paragraphs of French history are not.

#### Mini recap

Crisis, move, definition, two adjectives, one pre-eminent mark. Absolute removes the human superior; perpetual creates the office. Neither makes Bodin lawless.

#### In plain words

If two authorities in one country are each genuinely final, then when they disagree there is no way to decide who wins — which is the situation Bodin was trying to escape. So sovereignty cannot be shared. But that does not make the sovereign lawless. Bodin's sovereign stands above the **ordinary** law of the land and beneath three things: God's law, the moral order accessible to reason, and the constitutive laws that make the office exist at all.

The Latin tag is worth learning because it states the whole position in two words: *legibus solutus*, freed from the laws — but only from the ordinary positive ones.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Indivisibility.** For Bodin, sovereignty is indivisible and undivided.
⚠️ **The argument.** Shared supremacy is a contradiction: if two authorities are each final, conflict becomes insoluble, and divided sovereignty would reintroduce the very civil disorder Bodin wants to overcome.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Political order requires a final point of decision.
✅ **The distinction.** Division of **governmental functions** is possible; division of **sovereign title** is not, in Bodin's logic. Advisory estates or subordinate courts may exist, but the ultimate power of law remains one.

✅ **The limits.** Bodin's sovereign is above positive law but not above all law:

| Bound by | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Divine law** ✅ | The sovereign cannot validly command what offends divine order |
| **Natural law** ✅ | Justice and basic right reason remain normative limits |
| **Fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*)** ✅ | Certain constitutive laws of the realm structure the sovereign office |

⚠️ **The argument.** Bodin distinguishes the sovereign's relation to **ordinary civil law** from the relation to **higher normative order** — which is why he can combine absolutist language with claims about limits without simple self-contradiction.
✅ **The formula.** He is ***legibus solutus*** with respect to ordinary positive law, **not** with respect to divine or natural obligations.
⚠️ **The worked case.** Arbitrary confiscation of property is commonly discussed as incompatible with Bodin's own limiting framework.

✅ **Why Bodin matters.** He is the first great systematic theorist of modern sovereignty. He shifts political thought from medieval plural and feudal claims toward a unified concept of state authority, and supplies a vocabulary — **absolute, perpetual, undivided** — that shapes later debates, including Austin's monism and Laski's critique. ⚠️ Many later writers either radicalise his absolutism or try to limit it.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **The *leges imperii*** ✅ — the fundamental laws of the realm: the least-remembered and most rewarding of the three limits.
- ***Legibus solutus*** ✅ — the technical formula; quote it and immediately gloss the restriction to ordinary positive law.
- **Austin** ✅ — the radicalisation: a legal sovereign that is *formally* unlimited, with the higher-law restraints removed from the definition.
- **Laski** ✅ — the attack: indivisibility misdescribes a society of genuinely plural associations.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Bodin's sovereign is ***legibus solutus* only of ordinary positive law**: bound still by divine law, by natural law and by the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*) — which is exactly why he is the founder of the modern doctrine and not its most extreme exponent.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**indivisible and undivided** · **shared finality means insoluble conflict** · **functions divisible, title not** · **divine law** · **natural law** · **leges imperii** · **legibus solutus of ordinary law only** · **founder, not extremist**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Bodin's absolutism and his doctrine of limits are not an inconsistency but a single two-level structure. **Named evidence.** He holds the sovereign to be *legibus solutus* — freed from the laws — while simultaneously binding that sovereign by divine law, by natural law and by the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*), and he treats arbitrary confiscation of property as falling outside legitimate sovereign action. **Analysis.** The consistency comes from the level at which each claim operates: the freedom is from **ordinary positive law**, which the sovereign makes and can therefore not be subject to without circularity, whereas the constraints are **higher normative order and constitutive law**, which the sovereign does not make and therefore can be subject to. **Qualification.** The structure is coherent but weak in enforcement, since no institution is empowered to hold the sovereign to those higher norms; the honest verdict is that Bodin's limits are conceptually real and practically thin, which is precisely the concession the 2022 stem is testing.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ✅.** Constitutional government proves that power *can* be divided, so indivisibility is empirically refuted.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Bodin would answer that administrative distribution is not equivalent to sharing the final law-making power. ⚠️ Critics, however, argue that federal and constitutional systems weaken his rigid indivisibility thesis — and that argument, not the reply, is what Session 10 develops against Austin.
- **Objection ✅.** The three limits are too vague to restrain actual power.
- **Reply ⚠️.** True, they are weaker than modern constitutional checks; yet they matter conceptually because they show Bodin is **not simply Hobbes before Hobbes**. His sovereign is supreme, but not morally empty.
- **Residual limit ✅.** Modern equality, justice and liberty remain in tension with the doctrine. Bodin can support public order and impartial law by centralising authority, but his theory offers weak guarantees against the concentration of power. The balanced formula is: **historically foundational, normatively problematic**.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Making Bodin lawless. His sovereign is above ordinary positive law but bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm. This is trap 3 of the owner's standing list.
2. Treating "indivisible" as a claim that functions cannot be distributed. He allows advisory estates and subordinate courts; he refuses a divided **title**.
3. Answering the 2022 compatibility question with a flat yes or no. The demand is *critically discuss*, so the answer must name the criterion — equality, justice, liberty — apply it, and grade the result.

#### Mini recap

One title, many functions. Three limits, one Latin formula, one honest concession. Foundational in architecture, problematic in guarantees.

#### In plain words

Austin is not asking whether laws are good. He is asking what makes something a **law** at all, and he wants an answer as clean as a definition in geometry. His answer: a law is an order issued by whoever is at the top of a chain of obedience, with a penalty attached. That relocates the whole question of sovereignty into jurisprudence — sovereignty is now the thing that makes legal validity possible.

The definition of the sovereign has three parts, and all three matter. It must be a **specific, identifiable** person or body. It must be **habitually obeyed** by most people. And it must **habitually obey no one else**. Drop any one part and the definition stops doing its work.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The project.** Austin's theory belongs to **analytical jurisprudence**; its aim is to define law and sovereignty with conceptual precision.
✅ **The argument.** He wants to separate what law **is** from what law **ought to be**. To do so he identifies the source of law in a sovereign whose commands are backed by sanctions; sovereignty thus becomes the **juristic foundation of legal validity**.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Jurisprudence requires a determinate origin of binding command.
✅ **Distinction.** Austin is explaining **positive law**, not moral legitimacy.

✅ **The definition.** A sovereign is a **determinate human superior** who receives habitual obedience from the bulk of society and habitually obeys no one else. The three linked elements are:
1. **Determinate human superior** — the sovereign must be identifiable, not vague or mystical. ✅
2. **Habitual obedience by the bulk of society** — sovereignty rests on regular compliance, not occasional force alone. ✅
3. **Habitual obedience to no one** — the sovereign is legally independent and supreme. ✅

⚠️ **Presupposition.** Every independent political society must have such an apex.
✅ **The correction that must appear in every Austin answer.** Austin's sovereign is **not "the state" in the abstract**; it is a determinate person or body within the political community. ⚠️ A legislature or monarch fits the model better than diffuse democratic opinion.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Austin, *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*** ✅ — the analytical project and the three-part definition.
- **The is/ought separation** ✅ — the methodological commitment that makes the theory positivist and explains why moral limits sit outside the definition rather than inside it.
- **Bodin** ⚠️ — the comparison: Bodin keeps higher-law limits inside the account; Austin moves them outside it.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Austin's sovereign is a **determinate person or body, not "the state" in the abstract** — and every serious criticism of him, from federalism to popular sovereignty, is an attack on that one word *determinate*.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**analytical jurisprudence** · **is against ought** · **determinate human superior** · **habitual obedience by the bulk** · **obeys no one** · **juristic foundation of legal validity** · **not the state in the abstract**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Austin's theory is best read as an answer to a question about **legal validity**, not about political goodness, and its whole architecture follows from that choice. **Named evidence.** He defines the sovereign as a *determinate human superior* who receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society and habitually obeys no other like superior, and he treats a rule as law when it is a command of that superior backed by sanction. **Analysis.** Each element is doing analytic work: *determinate* supplies an identifiable origin for the chain of validity, *habitual obedience by the bulk* distinguishes an established legal order from occasional successful force, and *obeying no one* closes the chain so that validity terminates rather than regressing. **Qualification.** The precision is real but purchased narrowly: because moral legitimacy has been deliberately excluded from the definition, the theory can tell us that a command is valid law while remaining silent on whether it deserves obedience — a silence Austin accepts and Laski regards as the doctrine's most dangerous feature.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ✅.** The theory reduces law to coercion.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Austin **intentionally** privileges enforceability and source; the objection is a description of his method, not a refutation of it. ⚠️ Critics nevertheless argue that this leaves out important dimensions of law such as authority, procedure and acceptance — the very dimensions Hart later supplies.
- **Objection ✅.** Modern constitutional democracies do not contain one such determinate superior.
- **Reply ⚠️.** That is one of the central criticisms and Austin cannot fully absorb it. His model works best for relatively centralised legal orders and less well for constitutional, federal and democratic systems where authority is distributed and reciprocal.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The Austinian may relocate finality — to the constitution-making authority, or to the electorate — but Session 10 shows that relocation is often strained, and Sessions 12 and 13 show that the better repairs abandon the personal sovereign altogether.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Calling Austin's sovereign "the state". It is a determinate person or body. This is trap 1 of the owner's standing list and the fastest way to lose the first mark.
2. Presenting Austin as a defender of tyranny. He is describing legal validity; the normative silence is a methodological choice, and criticising it requires saying why that choice fails.
3. Forgetting the third element. "Habitually obeys no one" is what makes the chain terminate; candidates who drop it cannot answer the international-law criticism in Session 10.

#### Mini recap

Analytical project, three-part definition, one juristic function. Determinate, habitually obeyed, obeying nobody. Not the state — a person or a body.

#### In plain words

Austin's account of a law is deliberately simple: somebody with authority tells you to do something, and if you do not, something bad happens to you. Command plus sanction. That formula works beautifully for criminal law, where both halves are visible, and it works badly for the enormous part of law that simply tells you *how* to do something — how to make a valid will, a valid contract, a valid marriage.

The five attributes are not a list Austin chose; they are what his definition forces. If finality can be split, limited from above, or located nowhere in particular, then the chain of legal validity has no end — and the whole point of the theory was to give it one.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Law as command backed by sanction.** For Austin, positive law is the **command of the sovereign backed by sanction**.
✅ **The argument.** *Command* implies an expression of desire by a superior; *sanction* implies the evil or pain attached to non-compliance. Law is therefore a **species of imperative** issued within a structure of superiority and obedience.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** The essential feature of legal obligation is enforceable command.
✅ **The distinction.** Austin separates positive law from **morality, custom and divine law** as distinct normative domains.
⚠️ **Where it fits and where it does not.** Penal law fits Austin especially well because command and sanction are explicit; enabling, permissive and procedural rules fit badly.

✅ **The five attributes of Austinian sovereignty:**

| Attribute | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Determinate** | The sovereign must be identifiable |
| **Absolute** | No legal limits bind the sovereign from above |
| **Indivisible** | Final authority cannot be partitioned |
| **Illimitable** | No superior legal authority restricts it |
| **Inalienable** | Sovereignty cannot be transferred without ceasing to be sovereignty |

⚠️ **Why the list is forced.** If the sovereign were divided, limited by another legal superior, or indeterminate, the chain of command would be broken and the legal system would lack a final source.
⚠️ **The honest concession.** Austin's indivisibility is **stricter than empirical political reality**; it is a logical feature of his conceptual scheme rather than an observation about actual states. Saying this in the answer pre-empts the objection and earns the mark for critical control.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **The command–sanction formula** ✅ — Austin's definition of positive law.
- **Penal law** ⚠️ — the paradigm case that makes the formula plausible.
- **Enabling and procedural rules** ✅ — the counter-case that later jurisprudence turns into a decisive objection; Hart develops it in Session 13.
- **The five attributes** ✅ — determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable, inalienable.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Austin's five attributes are not a description of states but a set of **logical requirements of his own definition**: each one exists to stop the chain of legal validity from breaking, which is why refuting them empirically leaves his conceptual scheme untouched and refuting them *internally*, as Kelsen and Hart do, does not.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**command backed by sanction** · **species of imperative** · **superiority and obedience** · **determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable, inalienable** · **the chain of validity must terminate** · **stricter than political reality** · **power-conferring rules**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The five attributes of Austinian sovereignty are entailments of the command theory rather than independent observations, and reading them that way changes how they must be criticised. **Named evidence.** Austin defines positive law as the command of a sovereign backed by sanction, and characterises that sovereign as determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable. **Analysis.** Each attribute closes a possible gap in the chain of validity: indeterminacy would leave the origin unlocatable, divisibility would leave two origins in conflict, limitation from above would push validity to a further source, and alienability would make the origin transferable and therefore not final — so the attributes are what the definition needs in order to work. **Qualification.** This is why the strongest criticisms are the internal ones: pointing to a federation shows that no actual state matches the scheme, whereas showing that much of law confers powers rather than issuing commands attacks the definition itself, and only the second forces a reconstruction.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ✅.** Not all laws are commands; many are enabling, permissive or procedural.
- **Reply ⚠️.** This criticism is **powerful and largely successful**. Later jurisprudence shows that legal systems include power-conferring rules, constitutional rules and secondary rules not reducible to orders backed by threats.
- **Objection ✅.** Constitutional amendment procedures, judicial review and federal arrangements prove divisible authority.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Austinian theory tries to **relocate** finality somewhere in the system; critics argue this relocation is often strained and artificial.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** Austin has no resource for explaining why a defective will is not "punished" but simply void. That is not a marginal example: it exposes that his single model of law cannot cover facilities, and it is the doorway to Session 13.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Reciting D-A-I-I-I without saying **why** the list is forced. The reason is the mark; the list is the packaging.
2. Treating the nullity of a defective legal act as a "sanction". It is not; nullity is the absence of a legal effect, and saying otherwise concedes Hart's point while pretending to resist it.
3. Attacking indivisibility only with federal examples. Austin has a reply to those; the internal objection from power-conferring rules is the one he cannot answer.

#### Mini recap

Command plus sanction; five attributes forced by the definition; the best fit is penal law and the worst fit is the law of facilities. Concede the strictness — then use it.

*Caption: three criticisms, three Austinian relocations, three costs. The boxed pattern is what turns a list into an argument.*

#### In plain words

The three classic objections all work the same way. Each points at something everybody calls law and asks Austin to show the determinate superior behind it. In a federation, the answer is "which one?" In international law, the answer is "nobody". In customary law, the answer is "the custom got there first".

Austin can reply to each, but only by moving the sovereign somewhere else — to the constitution-makers, out of the category of law altogether, or backwards into a fiction of tacit adoption. Noticing that the reply is always a relocation, and that each relocation has a price, is what makes this session an argument rather than a list.

#### The doctrine, exactly

**(a) Federalism** ✅
- **The criticism.** Federal systems challenge Austin's idea of one undivided sovereign.
- **The argument ⚠️.** In federations, powers are constitutionally distributed between union and states; neither level can easily be described as the sole determinate superior in all matters.
- **Austinian reply ❓.** The constitution-making authority is sovereign.
- **Counter ⚠️.** That move often **shifts rather than solves** the problem, because the constitution itself may embody shared or entrenched authority.

**(b) International law** ✅
- **Austin's own position.** International law is **not truly law in the strict sense**, because there is no world sovereign.
- **The argument ✅.** Since states are not habitually obedient to a determinate global superior, international law lacks the Austinian foundation of command and sanction.
- **Criticism ✅.** This ignores the normative and practical force of treaty, custom and international institutions.
- **Verdict ⚠️.** Austin's state-centric lens is too narrow; legal order can exist through **reciprocity, consent, institutions and accepted practice** even without a world sovereign.

**(c) Customary law** ✅
- **The criticism.** Customary law sits uneasily within command theory.
- **The argument ⚠️.** Customs often arise from social practice **before** formal command; Austin tries to explain them as **tacitly adopted** by the sovereign.
- **Counter ✅.** That explanation is artificial, because many customs operate independently of explicit sovereign will.
- **The deeper point ⚠️.** Obedience and recognition may **precede** sovereign enactment rather than derive from it — which anticipates Hart's rule of recognition in Session 13.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Federal constitutional distribution** ✅ — the standard structural counter-example to indivisibility.
- **Treaty, custom and international institutions** ✅ — the sources of normativity Austin's definition excludes.
- **Tacit adoption** ⚠️ — Austin's own device for custom, and the reason the reply is judged artificial.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Each of the three structural criticisms locates a place where law exists but a determinate superior does not, and Austin's reply in every case is a **relocation of the sovereign** — to the constitution-makers, out of the category of law, or backwards into tacit adoption — with each relocation costing precisely the determinacy the theory was built to supply.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**constitutionally distributed competence** · **shifts rather than solves** · **no world sovereign** · **reciprocity, consent, institutions, accepted practice** · **tacit adoption** · **recognition precedes enactment** · **relocation has a price**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The federalism, international-law and custom objections are not three separate complaints but one objection applied three times: law is found where Austin's determinate superior is not. **Named evidence.** In a federation, competences are constitutionally distributed so that neither level is the sole superior in all matters; international law binds through treaty, custom and institutional practice with no world sovereign; and customary law arises from social practice before any command, which Austin can accommodate only by the device of tacit adoption. **Analysis.** Austin's replies share a single shape — relocate finality to the constitution-making authority, deny that international law is law at all, or backdate a sovereign will onto pre-existing custom — and each purchases consistency by weakening determinacy, which was the property the whole theory existed to secure. **Qualification.** The objections nevertheless do not show that legal validity needs no ultimate criterion; they show that the criterion cannot be a **person**, which is exactly the conclusion Kelsen and Hart draw while remaining positivists.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** Austin can simply define international law out of the category of law and preserve the theory.
- **Reply ⚠️.** He can, and he does — but at the cost of a definition that excludes a body of norms that states themselves treat as binding, argue about in legal terms and enforce through reciprocity and institutions. Conceptual purity is preserved and explanatory reach is lost.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The criticism does not settle what international law *is*; it settles only that the Austinian account of it is inadequate. The positive theory belongs to **International Relations** and is routed, not developed.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Listing five criticisms with no reply. On *critically examine* stems the objection–reply–residual layer **is** the answer; a bare list scores as description.
2. Presenting the international-law criticism as Austin's oversight. It is his explicit position, argued from his own definition, and treating it as carelessness misses the point.
3. Confusing the custom objection with the popular-sovereignty objection. Custom attacks the **direction** of authority; popular sovereignty attacks its **determinacy**, and Session 11 handles the second.

#### Mini recap

One objection applied three times. Federalism attacks indivisibility, international law attacks scope, custom attacks direction. Every reply relocates the sovereign, and every relocation costs determinacy.

#### In plain words

Is Austin compatible with democracy? The honest answer has two levels and the paper rewards both. At the level of pure legal form, yes — you can always point at a legislature. At the level of what democracy actually is, no — because a democratic authority is elected, accountable, rights-bound and revisable, and Austin's sovereign is none of those things by definition.

Then there is a phrase that trips up almost everyone: *parliamentary sovereignty*. It is a claim about one legal system, not a description of every country with a parliament. India has parliamentary government under a supreme written Constitution — which is a different thing entirely.

#### The doctrine, exactly

**Popular sovereignty and democracy** ✅
- **The criticism.** Austin's model fits uneasily with democratic theories in which the people are sovereign.
- **The argument ⚠️.** "The people" are often indeterminate, changing and institutionally mediated. Austin requires a **determinate** superior, so his theory struggles to accommodate representative democracy, constitutionalism and public accountability.

**Is Austin compatible with democracy?** ⚠️ Only partially, and in a strict sense he sits uneasily with it. Use the two-level structure:
- **Yes, in a narrow legal sense** ✅ — one may identify a law-making body.
- **No, in a deeper constitutional-political sense** ⚠️ — democracy requires accountability, rights and limited government, which undermine Austin's absoluteness.
- **The reason ⚠️.** Democracy disperses authority through elections, rights, representation, opposition and constitutional limitation; it makes sovereignty **mediated, divided in function and politically answerable**, whereas Austin's sovereign is legally supreme, indivisible and not habitually obedient to another.

**Parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy** — the jurisdiction control:

#### In plain words

Austin says a rule is law because a powerful person ordered it. Kelsen says that cannot be right, because a *fact* about who is powerful can never by itself generate an *obligation*. So he asks a different question: not "who commanded this?" but "what authorised this?" And the answer is always another rule, higher up. The tax order is authorised by the statute; the statute by the constitution; the constitution by — what?

Kelsen's answer is deliberately austere. At the top there is no super-ruler. There is a norm we **presuppose** in order to be able to read the whole chain as law at all. Call it the basic norm. Once you accept it, the sovereign has vanished as a person and survives only as a name for the highest law-making competence in the system.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **Why the Austinian model needs repair.** Austin locates law's validity in the will of a determinate human superior, which produces three problems he cannot solve from within his own system:
1. **The succession problem.** When one sovereign dies and another takes office, why is the new sovereign's first command law, before any habit of obedience has formed? Habit cannot explain the continuity of legal authority across persons.
2. **The persistence problem.** Why does a statute enacted centuries ago, by a sovereign long dead and never habitually obeyed by anyone now living, remain valid law?
3. **The power-conferring problem.** Much of law does not command anybody. Rules about how to make a valid will, contract or marriage confer facilities; on the command model, a defective will is not a "sanction" for disobedience — it is simply a nullity.

⚠️ Each problem points the same way: legal authority is carried by **rules**, not by the habits or the person of a commander.

✅ **Kelsen's central claim.** The validity of a legal norm is never derived from a fact of power but always from **another, higher norm**. Law is a **hierarchy (pyramid) of norms**: an administrative order is valid because a statute authorises it; the statute is valid because the constitution authorises its enactment; the constitution is valid because it is presupposed to be valid.
✅ **The basic norm (*Grundnorm*)** stands at the apex — not itself enacted by anyone, but **presupposed** by jurists as the condition that makes it possible to interpret the whole chain as a system of valid norms rather than as a sequence of effective threats.

⚠️ **The reconstructed argument, in five steps:**
1. a norm's validity cannot be derived from a mere fact, because "is" does not yield "ought";
2. therefore validity must be derived from a further norm;
3. an infinite regress of authorising norms would leave nothing valid;
4. therefore the chain must terminate in a norm that is presupposed rather than derived — the *Grundnorm*;
5. law is accordingly a self-referring hierarchy of validity, distinct from both morality and brute power.

⚠️ **Presupposition.** A "pure" theory of law is possible: legal science can describe validity while excluding morality, sociology, politics and psychology.
⚠️ **The effect on sovereignty.** Kelsen **dissolves** the Austinian sovereign. There is no determinate human superior standing above the law; the "sovereign" is simply the personification of the legal order's highest norm-creating competence. Sovereignty becomes an attribute of the **normative order**, not of a person or body.

✅/❌ **Bibliographic precision.** The standard reference is ***General Theory of Law and State*, first published in English in 1945** (translated by Anders Wedberg). ❌ **Do not write 1961 for Kelsen.** 1961 is the year of **Hart's** *The Concept of Law*. The two dates sit adjacent in the Austin → Kelsen → Hart sequence and are the most commonly swapped pair in this topic. ⚠️ If unsure in the examination hall, name the works and the sequence without years rather than guessing.

#### Named evidence you can cite

- **Kelsen, *General Theory of Law and State*, first English edition 1945** ✅ — cited by title and year only; no page, chapter, edition or verbatim wording is asserted.
- **The is/ought gap** ✅ — the premise that makes the whole pyramid necessary.
- **Austin's three internal failures** ✅ — succession, persistence, power-conferring rules: the agenda Kelsen is answering.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Kelsen does not limit the sovereign; he **dissolves** him — once validity is shown to descend from norm to norm and to terminate in a presupposed basic norm, supremacy becomes a property of the legal order's highest competence rather than of any determinate human superior.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**hierarchy of norms** · **basic norm (Grundnorm)** · **presupposed, not enacted** · **is does not yield ought** · **regress must terminate** · **purity of legal science** · **personification of the highest competence** · **succession and persistence problems** · **1945, not 1961**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** Kelsen's repair of Austin is not a softening of sovereignty but its relocation from a person to a normative order. **Named evidence.** In *General Theory of Law and State*, first published in English in **1945**, Kelsen argues that a norm's validity derives only from a higher norm, so that an administrative order rests on a statute, the statute on the constitution, and the constitution on a **presupposed basic norm (*Grundnorm*)** that is not itself enacted by anyone. **Analysis.** The move is forced by the is/ought gap: if no fact about who is obeyed can generate an obligation, then habitual obedience cannot be the source of validity, and the chain of authorisation must terminate in something presupposed rather than observed — at which point the determinate human superior has no work left to do and survives only as a name for the system's highest law-making competence. **Qualification.** The gain is bought at a cost Kelsen concedes: the basic norm is a postulate rather than a demonstrated truth, and revolution forces him to treat by-and-large effectiveness as a condition of validity, which quietly readmits the fact of power his theory was designed to exclude.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ⚠️.** The *Grundnorm* is a fiction — a presupposition invented to stop a regress, doing exactly the work Austin's habitual obedience did, only less honestly.
- **Reply ✅.** Kelsen does not claim the basic norm is a **fact**; he claims it is a **transcendental-logical condition** of treating anything as valid law at all, comparable to a postulate in a formal science. The theory therefore remains positivist — the basic norm confers validity, not moral obligation.
- **Objection ⚠️.** The theory cannot explain revolution, since a successful coup replaces the constitution without any authorising norm.
- **Reply ⚠️.** When a new order becomes **by and large effective**, jurists presuppose a new basic norm; efficacy is thus a *condition* of validity even though it is not its *source*.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** Critics reply that this quietly readmits the fact of power the theory was designed to exclude. That residual problem is genuine and should be **conceded** in the answer rather than argued away.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. ❌ Dating *General Theory of Law and State* to 1961. That is Hart. This is trap 11 of the owner's standing list, and it is the single most punished factual error in the clause.
2. Calling the *Grundnorm* a moral standard. It confers **legal validity only**; keeping it morally inert is exactly why Kelsen remains a positivist. This is trap 13.
3. Filing Kelsen alongside Laski as an external critic. Kelsen is an **internal** repair inside legal positivism; putting him in the wrong column destroys the two-stage structure that earns marks on 20-mark stems.

#### Mini recap

Three unsolved problems, one pyramid, one presupposed apex. Validity descends from norms, never from facts; the sovereign becomes a competence, not a person; and the date is **1945**.

*Caption: three defects, three cures, one social-fact apex, four strikes. The internal/external distinction is the single highest-yield idea on this page.*

#### In plain words

Hart's objection to Austin begins with an image: a gunman says "your money or your life", and you hand over the money. You were **obliged**. But you had no **obligation**. Austin's theory, built on habitual obedience and threats, cannot mark that difference — and if it cannot, it has not explained law at all.

His replacement is structural. Some rules tell you what to do; others tell you how rules are made, changed, identified and applied. A community with only the first kind cannot say authoritatively what its rules are, cannot change them deliberately, and cannot settle disputes. Add the second kind and you have a legal system. At the top sits not a person but a **practice**: what the officials of the system actually treat as the test of law.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The rejection.** Hart, in ***The Concept of Law* (1961)**, rejects the command-and-sanction model as too narrow — it is built on the criminal-law paradigm, "the gunman situation writ large", and cannot account for power-conferring law. He replaces it with law as a **union of primary and secondary rules**.

| Rule type | Function | What it answers | Defect it cures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary rules** ✅ | impose duties directly on conduct — do not steal, do not assault, pay tax | what must be done? | — |
| **Secondary: rule of recognition** ✅ | identifies what counts as valid law in this system | which rules are law? | **uncertainty** in a regime of primary rules alone |
| **Secondary: rules of change** ✅ | authorise creation, amendment and repeal of primary rules | how is law altered? | **static character** of custom |
| **Secondary: rules of adjudication** ✅ | confer authority to determine breach and remedy | who decides, and how? | **inefficiency** of diffuse enforcement |

⚠️ **The reconstructed argument, in five steps:**
1. a society with only primary rules suffers uncertainty, stasis and inefficiency;
2. these three defects are cured by three corresponding secondary rules;
3. the union of primary rules with secondary rules is what distinguishes a legal system from a set of social habits;
4. the ultimate criterion of validity — the **rule of recognition** — is not itself valid or invalid: it **exists as a social fact**, in the convergent practice of officials who treat it as the common public standard from an **internal point of view**;
5. therefore law rests neither on a commander's will (Austin) nor on a presupposed norm (Kelsen) but on an **accepted official practice**.

✅ **The internal and external points of view.** An external observer records regularities of behaviour; a participant taking the **internal point of view** treats the rule as a *reason* for conduct and as a ground for criticising deviation. Austin's "habit of obedience" captures only the external aspect and therefore cannot distinguish a legal obligation from a gunman's threat, or a rule from a habit.

⚠️ **Hart's critique of Austin, in four strikes:**

| Austin's claim | Hart's objection |
|---|---|
| Law is a command backed by sanction | ignores power-conferring rules; **nullity is not a sanction** |
| The sovereign is habitually obeyed | habit cannot explain continuity on succession or the persistence of old statutes |
| The sovereign obeys no one | in a modern legal order the law-maker is itself constituted and limited by rules of change |
| Obligation is being obliged by threat | conflates **being obliged** (the gunman) with **having an obligation** (the internal point of view) |

⚠️ **Hart against Kelsen.** Hart accepts the need for an ultimate criterion of validity but denies that it is a presupposed norm. The rule of recognition is a matter of **social fact** — what the officials of a given system actually treat as law — not a logical postulate standing outside the system.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Legal theory is descriptive-analytical sociology of a special kind: validity can be explained without moral evaluation, but not without reference to shared official practice.

⚠️ **Indian application, with the legal-status caution.** The **Constitution of India** is a written, supreme instrument containing rules of change (the amendment procedure) and rules of adjudication (the judicial hierarchy and writ jurisdiction) — a textbook fit for Hart's secondary rules and for Kelsen's norm-hierarchy alike. ⚠️ Indian federalism, judicial review and the amendment procedure together show that legal authority in India is **competence-distributed and rule-governed**, which is precisely what Austin's determinate illimitable superior cannot accommodate. ❌ Do not describe any Indian institution, office or period as "the sovereign" in Austin's sense, and do not attribute a *Grundnorm* to any named document as though Kelsen had said so.

> **Claim.** Hart's reconstruction shows that the ultimate authority in a legal system is a **practice**, not a person and not a postulate. **Named evidence.** In *The Concept of Law* (**1961**) he argues that a regime of primary duty-imposing rules suffers uncertainty, stasis and inefficiency, each cured by a corresponding secondary rule — recognition, change and adjudication — and that the rule of recognition itself is neither valid nor invalid but exists as the convergent practice of officials who accept it from the internal point of view. **Analysis.** This settles the question Austin could not: the new sovereign's first command is law because the rule of recognition already identifies the office, and the centuries-old statute persists for the same reason, so continuity is carried by an accepted criterion rather than by a habit that must be freshly formed. **Qualification.** The account is not unassailable: Dworkin objects that in hard cases judges rely on principles that no pedigree test can identify, Hartian positivists reply that judges exercise bounded discretion at the penumbra of settled rules, and the dispute is best reported as live rather than closed.

- **Objection ⚠️.** A rule of recognition grounded in official practice cannot condemn a legally impeccable but morally monstrous regime.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Hart's position is that separating legal validity from moral merit is what makes moral criticism of valid law **possible and clear-eyed**, rather than smuggling the verdict into the definition of law.

#### In plain words

Look at how people actually live. They belong to families, congregations, unions, professional bodies, universities, local communities — and they obey those bodies for reasons that have nothing to do with the state's permission. Laski's argument starts there. If real obligation is generated in many places, then the claim that all authority flows from a single sovereign will is not just morally alarming; it is **false as a description of society**.

Two guardrails. First, he is not an anarchist: the state still coordinates and still coerces, and he never proposes abolishing it. Second, his own position moved: the early Laski called sovereignty a legal fiction, the mature Laski accepted its importance while denying its omnipotence. Quoting one phase as if it were the whole man is a standard error.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The starting-point.** Laski rejects the monistic doctrine that the state possesses an absolute, indivisible and unlimited sovereignty.
✅ **The argument.** Human beings live in multiple associations — family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession, locality — and these bodies command **real loyalty**. Therefore the state cannot plausibly claim that all forms of obligation derive from it alone.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Social life is plural, layered and morally prior to the state's totalising claim.
✅ **The controlling distinction.** Laski is a **pluralist, not an anarchist**. He does not deny the need for the state; he denies that the state is the sole and morally unlimited source of all authority.
⚠️ **The illustration.** Workers may obey trade unions, believers may obey churches and citizens may follow local bodies for reasons not reducible to state command.

✅ **The state as one association among many.** Unlike monists, Laski refuses to derive all rights and institutions from the state. Associations are not merely permissions granted by sovereignty; they are **real sites of personality, cooperation and authority**.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Society precedes the state's monopolistic claim in moral richness.
✅ **The qualification.** "One association among many" does **not** mean all associations are equal in function; the state still has coordinating and coercive capacities.

✅ **The supported pluralist lineage.** Laski is the printed thinker and must remain central; the following names locate the school rather than replace his argument.

#### In plain words

Laski's rejection is not one argument repeated three times; it is three arguments of three different kinds. One is moral: nothing human deserves unlimited obedience. One is political: an unlimited authority is an invitation to tyranny. One is factual: societies do not actually work that way. An answer that offers only the moral point has offered a third of the case.

From that follows his positive proposal — authority should be shared, and the state should have to **earn** allegiance by what it does rather than claim it by what it is. And then the honest concession: in emergencies, in defence, in redistribution, somebody has to be able to act for the whole. Pluralism is a magnificent critique and an incomplete replacement.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The threefold rejection.** Laski rejects absolute sovereignty because it is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic:
1. **Philosophically false** ✅ — no human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience.
2. **Politically dangerous** ✅ — absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty.
3. **Sociologically unrealistic** ✅ — real societies distribute power among many groups and institutions.

⚠️ **Presupposition.** Liberty and moral personality require dispersed authority.
⚠️ **Target.** He attacks both the legal abstraction of Austin and the absolutist impulse inherited from Bodin.

✅ **Sovereignty must be divided and shared.** For Laski, sovereignty in the monistic sense should give way to a **distributed structure of authority**.
⚠️ **The argument.** Modern society is functionally differentiated: economic, cultural, educational, local and religious life cannot be adequately governed by a single centralised will, so power should be shared through institutions, representation and autonomy.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Freedom depends on institutional pluralism.
✅ **The distinction that must be written.** Laski's division of authority is **not mere administrative decentralisation**; it is a deeper challenge to the idea of one final and unlimited sovereign will.

✅ **The state must compete for allegiance.** Allegiance is not automatically owed because the state declares itself supreme. Citizens obey when institutions protect rights, secure welfare and enable meaningful life.
⚠️ **The consequence.** Authority is **functional and moral, not mystical**: a state that fails in justice, liberty and welfare cannot claim unlimited obedience merely by legal title.

✅ **Why monistic sovereignty is dangerous.** If the state is treated as the sole final authority, dissenting associations, minority rights, local autonomies and moral conscience can all be overridden in the name of public supremacy.
✅ **The double character of the critique.** It is both empirical and normative: monism **misdescribes** society and **licenses** domination.

⚠️ **The assessment, both halves.** Laski powerfully exposes the limits of monism but may understate the coordinating necessity of the state. His strength lies in showing that law, obedience and authority operate through a social field of associations; his weakness is that emergencies, defence and redistribution often require a stronger integrating public authority than pure pluralism acknowledges.
⚠️ **The balanced conclusion.** Laski is strongest as a **critic of absolute sovereignty** and as a **defender of liberty through social plurality**, not as a complete replacement for all concepts of final authority.

#### In plain words

Kautilya does not begin with a definition of authority; he begins with an inventory of what a working state is made of. Seven things: a ruler, ministers, a populated territory, a fortified defence, a treasury, an armed coercive capacity and an ally. Take any one away and the state limps.

That inventory is itself an argument. If sovereignty were purely the ruler's will, the list would be unnecessary. Because the list is necessary, sovereignty turns out to be a property of the **whole organism** — with the ruler as its head rather than its sole substance. A king with an empty treasury and no ministers has a title and not much else.

#### The doctrine, exactly

✅ **The framework.** Kautilya's theory of sovereignty in the *Arthaśāstra* is embedded in a comprehensive science of statecraft, order, welfare, punishment and interstate strategy.
✅ **The argument.** Unlike Austin's narrow jurisprudence, Kautilya treats sovereignty as a **practical art of rulership** involving administration, treasury, coercion, alliances, intelligence, territory and public welfare. His sovereign is active, strategic and institutionally situated.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Political order is fragile and must be sustained through prudent organisation of the state.
⚠️ **The placement.** Kautilya is neither a simple absolutist nor a liberal constitutionalist; he is a **realist thinker of rule constrained by dharma and public welfare**.

✅ **The seven-limbed (*saptāṅga*) state.** The state consists of seven interdependent limbs (*saptāṅga* / *prakṛtis*):

| Limb | Meaning | Function in sovereignty |
|---|---|---|
| **Svāmī** ✅ | The ruler or king | Headship, direction, decision |
| **Amātya** ✅ | Ministers and officials | Counsel, administration, execution |
| **Janapada** ✅ | Territory together with its people | Productive social base |
| **Durga** ✅ | Fortified capital and defence structure | Security and protection |
| **Kośa** ✅ | Treasury | Material basis of rule |
| **Daṇḍa** ✅ | Force, army, coercive power | Enforcement, punishment, defence |
| **Mitra** ✅ | Ally | External support in the interstate order |

⚠️ **The argument.** The theory presents the state as an **organism**. The ruler is the head, but he cannot function without ministers, treasury, territory, force and allies; sovereignty is therefore not merely personal but **systemic**.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Stable rule depends on interdependence among institutions and the material bases of power.
✅ **The comparison that earns the mark.** This differs from Austin: Austin seeks the **legal source of command**; Kautilya explains the **operational anatomy of rule**.
⚠️ **The illustration.** A ruler without treasury (*kośa*) or ministers (*amātya*) may possess title but not effective sovereignty.

✅ **The ruler (*svāmī*), exactly.** The *svāmī* occupies the highest place, yet Kautilyan sovereignty is **not Austinian unlimitedness**.
⚠️ **The argument.** The ruler must govern through counsel, prudence, discipline and regard for the well-being of subjects. Authority is real, but its legitimacy and success are judged by order and welfare.
⚠️ **Presupposition.** Rulership is a **duty-bearing office**, not merely a licence to command.
✅ **The distinction.** The king is central, but the state is **not identical with the king's private will**.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ❓.** Because he centres the king, Kautilya is just a premodern authoritarian.
- **Reply ⚠️.** The king is central, but the state is not reducible to royal whim: the seven limbs, the welfare obligations and the strategic constraints all show a more **institutional and purposive** theory.
- **Objection ❓.** If sovereignty is distributed among limbs, the king ceases to be sovereign.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Kautilya preserves royal primacy while recognising that effective sovereignty is **organismic and conditional** on administrative and strategic capacities.
- **Objection ✅.** A monarch with coercive power can always ignore duty.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Kautilya is too realistic to deny abuse; his response is institutional vigilance, ministerial competence, discipline and reason of state tied to long-term stability — **misrule weakens sovereignty itself**.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** These are prudential rather than enforceable checks. Nothing in the scheme empowers any limb to remove a ruler who ignores them, and a candid answer says so instead of dressing prudence as constitutionalism.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Equating Kautilya with Austinian absolutism. The ruler is strong but bound by dharma, welfare, prudence and institutional interdependence. This is trap 4 of the owner's standing list.
2. Listing the limbs without the argument. Seven Sanskrit words score as vocabulary; the interdependence claim scores as philosophy.
3. Translating *janapada* as "territory" alone. It is territory **together with its people** — the productive social base — and the omission removes the welfare link that Session 18 needs.

#### Mini recap

Seven limbs, one head, one systemic claim. Austin asks what makes a command valid; Kautilya asks what makes rule possible. Title without treasury is not sovereignty.

#### In plain words

The lazy answer to "is sovereignty still relevant?" is "no, globalisation ended it". The lazy answer is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that is easy to demonstrate. States still decide who may enter, who may be a citizen, whether to go to war, what the tax code says and whether to leave a treaty. What has changed is not that decisions vanished but that they are increasingly taken **through** commitments, delegations and shared procedures.

The trick is to distinguish four different things that get lumped together. Promising to be bound is not the same as handing power to somebody else; letting an institution do a job is not the same as sharing the decision; sharing the decision is not the same as being too poor to refuse. Each has a different consequence for sovereignty, and separating them is the answer.

#### The doctrine, exactly

⚠️ **The thesis.** Globalisation **modifies** sovereignty but does not abolish it.
⚠️ **The argument.** External economic, legal and strategic interdependence constrains states; yet states still claim final authority over territory, law, security and political membership. The issue is **pooled, negotiated and networked sovereignty** rather than the disappearance of sovereignty.

✅/⚠️ **The four mechanisms of constraint and reconfiguration:**

| Mechanism | What happens | Sovereignty consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Treaty commitment** ✅/⚠️ | A state accepts binding rules through its recognised treaty-making capacity | Self-limitation can constrain future action without creating a global Austinian sovereign |
| **Delegation** ⚠️ | A state authorises an institution to perform a defined adjudicative, administrative or monitoring function | Exercise of a competence moves outward while the legal source and limits of the delegation remain treaty-based |
| **Pooling** ⚠️ | States exercise a defined competence jointly through common voting or bargaining rules | Finality becomes shared for the agreed field, not universally transferred |
| **Material dependence / conditionality** ⚠️ | Finance, markets, technology or security asymmetry narrows feasible choices | Legal independence may survive while effective autonomy declines |

Modified, not abolished. Four mechanisms, four different consequences, four thinkers re-deployed. Legal form persists; effective autonomy varies; and the honest verdict names both.

#### In plain words

By this point the whole dispute can be laid on one grid. Read down a column and you have a thinker; read across a row and you have a debate. That is what an examiner means by "compare": fixed axes, both thinkers run down each, then a judgement.

✅ **The concluding line for 2019-type answers.** Austin's sovereign is legally supreme because no superior commands it; Kautilya's ruler is politically central but normatively and functionally bound by dharma, welfare, ministers, treasury, force and allies.

✅ **Bodin against Austin, in four rows:** historical role — founder of modern sovereignty against most systematic legal analyst; definition — absolute and perpetual power of the commonwealth against determinate human superior habitually obeyed; limits — divine and natural law and the *leges imperii* against no legal superior with moral limits outside the definition; orientation — state-building amid civil war against jurisprudential analysis of positive law.

✅ **Bodin and Austin against Laski.** Where the monists insist on one final superior, Laski insists that real society is composed of many autonomous centres of authority; where they privilege order and finality, he privileges liberty and associational plurality. ✅ **Monist reply:** without a final sovereign, conflict between associations becomes insoluble. ⚠️ **Pluralist counter-reply:** treating one authority as legally final does not eliminate conflict; it often merely suppresses legitimate diversity.

✅ **The criticisms of absolute sovereignty, with their standard replies:**

| Criticism | Against whom primarily | Core point | Typical reply |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Democracy** ✅ | Austin, Bodin | Popular rule and accountability are difficult to reconcile with unlimited sovereign power | One may retain legal finality while limiting actual government ⚠️ |

| **International law** ✅ | Austin | The absence of a world sovereign does not mean the absence of normativity | Austin preserves conceptual purity but narrows law excessively ⚠️ |
| **Customary law** ✅ | Austin | Law can arise from social practice | Austin's tacit-adoption reply is often judged artificial ⚠️ |
| **Moral limits** ✅ | Austin | Pure legal supremacy ignores justice | Austin separates analysis from morality; critics say the separation is incomplete ⚠️ |

⚠️ **The reply on behalf of sovereignty.** Even severe critics of absolutism rarely deny that political order needs some principle of final decision. The real dispute is not whether order needs authority, but **whether that authority must be conceived as undivided, legally unlimited and morally self-justifying**. Bodin and Austin answer yes or nearly yes; Laski answers no; Kautilya offers a duty-bound and strategic version. ⚠️ The modern debate is therefore better framed as **how sovereignty is structured and limited**, not simply whether it exists.

⚠️ **The four-way structure.** Bodin and Austin ask *who* is sovereign. Kelsen and Hart deny that this is the right question and ask *what makes a norm valid*. Laski denies that any single answer is possible. Kautilya asks what the holder of authority is **for**. An answer that runs this structure — personal sovereign → normative order → official practice → plural allegiance, with Kautilya's duty-bound variant — is doing philosophy rather than summarising a textbook.
⚠️ **Convergence with divergence.** Kelsen, Hart and Laski each dissolve the *personal* absolute sovereign, but they disagree entirely about what replaces it — a norm-hierarchy, a social practice, or a plurality of associations. Do not merge them into a single "modern critique of Austin".

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**fixed comparison axes** · **who / what makes valid / no single answer / what it is for** · **norm-hierarchy, official practice, plural associations** · **do not merge the three** · **structured and limited, not existent or absent** · **graded verdict** · **residual problem**

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH — claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification

> **Claim.** The four printed thinkers are not four answers to one question but four different questions about final authority, and organising an answer that way converts comparison into philosophy. **Named evidence.** Bodin locates sovereignty in the commonwealth's supreme law-giving power and Austin in a determinate human superior; Kelsen, in *General Theory of Law and State* (**1945**), and Hart, in *The Concept of Law* (**1961**), both refuse the personal question and ask instead what confers validity; Laski holds that authority is socially plural and cannot be reduced to a single unlimited will; and Kautilya's *svāmī* is head of a seven-limbed state oriented to security and welfare. **Analysis.** Read as answers to one question, the four positions look like a disagreement about degree; read as answers to *who*, *what makes valid*, *whether any single answer exists* and *what authority is for*, they become a structured debate in which each position exposes a blind spot in the others. **Qualification.** The structure must not be flattened into agreement: Kelsen, Hart and Laski all dissolve the personal sovereign but replace him with a norm-hierarchy, an official practice and a plurality of associations respectively, and treating those as one critique loses precisely the discrimination the paper rewards.

#### Objection → reply → residual limit

- **Objection ✅.** Without a final sovereign, conflict between associations becomes insoluble.
- **Reply ⚠️.** Treating one authority as legally final does not eliminate conflict; it often merely suppresses legitimate diversity — and modern constitutional orders require a **final procedure**, not an unanswerable will.
- **Residual limit ⚠️.** The pluralist reply still owes an account of who decides in a genuine emergency, and the monist reply still owes an account of why legal finality should command moral obedience. Neither debt is discharged; a graded verdict names both and then decides.

#### Examiner traps on this session

1. Comparing thinkers by biography or chronology instead of by fixed axes. The corpus-driven audit is explicit: fix axes — source, location, divisibility, legal and moral limits, organs of state and modern relevance.
2. Treating all four printed thinkers as saying the same thing about supremacy. The paper repeatedly asks for exact differences.
3. Turning examples into fabricated historical detail. Keep examples generic unless the question itself supplies the frame.

#### Mini recap

One grid, four questions, three dissolutions that must not be merged. The concept of supreme legal authority survives; the personal sovereign does not; and the verdict must be graded, not declared.

#### MCQ 1. What the concept of sovereignty actually asserts

A bandit can compel obedience and a government can issue commands. On the account developed in this package, what distinguishes the claim of sovereignty from both?

A. It asserts that the holder of power possesses greater coercive capacity than any rival inside the territory.

B. It asserts that the holder of power has acquired office through a procedure that the community regards as lawful.

C. It asserts a recognised or effective power of **final** decision, not merely an ability to compel compliance.

D. It asserts that the holder of power is morally entitled to the obedience of every person within the territory.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: superior force is neither necessary nor sufficient, since the canonical statement expressly separates sovereignty from mere force and locates it in recognised or effective finality.
- **B:** Incorrect: lawful acquisition is the *de jure* question of Session 4; a *de facto* sovereign obeyed without lawful title still raises the question of finality.
- **C:** Correct: the owner defines sovereignty as the supreme, final and authoritative power within a political community, and distinguishes it from the bandit's coercion precisely by finality.
- **D:** Incorrect: moral entitlement is what Laski denies to any human institution; the concept can be stated without it, and Austin deliberately excludes it.

**Examiner trap 1:** Opening a sovereignty answer with a definition of the *state* rather than of *sovereignty* wastes the first paragraph, because the state is the bearer of the claim while sovereignty is the claim itself.

#### MCQ 2. The classical attributes, stated so that they survive criticism

Which statement gives the five classical attributes of sovereignty in the qualified form the paper rewards?

A. Absolute of any human law-maker within, permanent in the state rather than the officer, universal subject to recognised immunity, inalienable though delegable, indivisible in legal source.

B. Absolute in every moral and practical respect, permanent for all future time, universal without exception, inalienable and undelegable, indivisible in both source and competence.

C. Absolute against foreign powers only, permanent while the ruling party lasts, universal over citizens alone, inalienable except by treaty, indivisible except in federations.

D. Absolute over ordinary citizens, permanent while obedience continues, universal within the capital, inalienable unless a court permits, indivisible unless the constitution says otherwise.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: each of the five is stated with the limiting clause the owner attaches — legal non-subordination, continuity of the state, recognised immunities, delegation without alienation, and one final legal source.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the unqualified version that every first-year counter-example refutes; the owner explicitly warns that legal non-subordination does not prove moral infallibility or practical omnipotence.
- **C:** Incorrect: absoluteness is an *internal* claim about the absence of a superior human law-maker, and permanence attaches to the state rather than to any government or party.
- **D:** Incorrect: universality extends across persons and associations within the territory rather than a capital, and inalienability is not a matter of judicial permission.

**Examiner trap 2:** Reciting the five attributes without their qualifications converts a defensible doctrine into a refutable slogan, and omitting permanence and universality — the two most frequently dropped — leaves the classical set incomplete.

#### MCQ 3. Legal against political sovereignty

Why does the owner insist that most objections to monistic sovereignty must be labelled before they are deployed?

A. Because objections drawn from history are weaker than objections drawn from logic, and the examiner grades the two differently.

B. Because Austin himself accepted political sovereignty and only later writers separated it from the legal question he was answering.

C. Because political sovereignty is a normative claim while legal sovereignty is descriptive, so the two cannot be compared at all.

D. Because most such objections answer the question of real social power, while the thesis attacked is a claim about competence to make law.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the distinction is not between historical and logical objections; both kinds can be aimed at either the legal or the political question.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner places Austin firmly on the legal side and treats political sovereignty as the angle from which Laski attacks, not as something Austin conceded.
- **C:** Incorrect: legal sovereignty identifies the juristic source of valid law and political sovereignty the real locus of social power; both are descriptive, and both are comparable.
- **D:** Correct: legal sovereignty names competence to make law and political sovereignty names the power that shapes the law-maker, so an unlabelled objection commits a category error.

**Examiner trap 3:** Treating "political sovereignty" as a synonym for popular sovereignty confuses a sociological description of where power lies with a normative claim about where authority originates.

#### MCQ 4. Lawful title, actual obedience, and the divergence it exposes

Which statement correctly identifies where Bodin and Austin anchor the durability of sovereign authority?

A. Both anchor it in habitual obedience, since neither can explain continuity without appealing to the settled compliance of subjects.

B. Bodin anchors it in the perpetual office rather than the magistrate, whereas Austin's habitual-obedience criterion gives his theory a de facto edge.

C. Bodin anchors it in divine sanction of the monarch, whereas Austin anchors it in the constitutional text that confers legislative competence.

D. Both anchor it in the office, since Austin's determinate superior is defined as an institution rather than as any identifiable person or body.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Bodin's second defining adjective is *perpetual*, which the owner glosses as authority attaching to office rather than to a temporary magistrate, so obedience is not his anchor.
- **B:** Correct: the owner states exactly this contrast — Austin's emphasis on habitual obedience has a de facto edge, while Bodin is concerned with the permanent office-character of authority.
- **C:** Incorrect: divine law is a *limit* on Bodin's sovereign rather than the ground of durability, and Austin's sovereign is not defined by a constitutional text.
- **D:** Incorrect: Austin's sovereign is expressly a determinate person or body, which is why the succession problem bites him and not Bodin.

**Examiner trap 4:** Writing that Bodin and Austin agree simply because both are monists throws away a cheap, high-value differentiation that comparison stems reward directly.

#### MCQ 5. Popular sovereignty, national sovereignty and the general will

Which statement handles the two forms and the Rousseau bridge with the precision the owner requires?

A. Popular sovereignty means majority rule, national sovereignty means independence, and Rousseau's general will is the numerical sum of individual preferences.

B. Popular sovereignty and national sovereignty are interchangeable expressions, and Rousseau uses the general will to justify whatever the assembled citizens decide.

C. Popular sovereignty means the government represents the nation, national sovereignty means the nation obeys the government, and Rousseau merges the two.

D. Popular sovereignty locates the source of public authority in the people, national sovereignty concerns collective self-determination, and the general will names the common good.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner explicitly defines the general will as a claim about the common good rather than the numerical sum of private preferences, and popular sovereignty is not identical with majority rule.
- **B:** Incorrect: the two forms answer different questions — from whom authority arises, and which people may govern itself — and the owner lists their conflation as a standing trap.
- **C:** Incorrect: this inverts the doctrine, since popular sovereignty makes government the agent of the people rather than their representative superior.
- **D:** Correct: this reproduces the owner's controlling distinction and its gloss on the general will, and it preserves the warning that no majority decision is thereby morally sovereign.

**Examiner trap 5:** Collapsing the general will into "whatever the majority votes for" destroys the argument against Austin, because the force of the popular-sovereignty objection lies in the people's indeterminacy, not in their counting power.

#### MCQ 6. Bodin's definition and its pre-eminent mark

What, on Bodin's account, is the pre-eminent mark by which sovereignty is identified?

A. The power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without needing the consent of any superior.

B. The power to command the armed forces of the commonwealth and to declare war on any neighbouring realm.

C. The power to appoint magistrates and to remove them at pleasure without stating a reason to the estates.

D. The power to interpret divine and natural law authoritatively for every subject of the commonwealth.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner identifies the sovereign's pre-eminent mark as the power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without needing consent from a superior.
- **B:** Incorrect: military command is an ordinary attribute of rule and does not distinguish the sovereign from a powerful magistrate or general.
- **C:** Incorrect: appointing and removing magistrates is precisely the sort of delegated function Bodin allows without conceding any division of sovereign title.
- **D:** Incorrect: divine and natural law constrain Bodin's sovereign rather than falling within his interpretive gift; making him their authoritative interpreter would erase the limits.

**Examiner trap 6:** Explaining "perpetual" as "eternal" misses its actual work, which is to detach authority from the office-holder and fix it in the office so that a personal ascendancy becomes a state.

#### MCQ 7. What limits Bodin's sovereign

Which statement gives Bodin's position on limits without either exaggerating or erasing them?

A. The sovereign is bound by ordinary positive law and by the estates, but not by divine law, which belongs to the church alone.

B. The sovereign is bound by nothing whatever, since the power to make law entails the power to unmake every constraint upon it.

C. The sovereign is freed from ordinary positive law yet bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm.

D. The sovereign is bound only by the fundamental laws of the realm, divine and natural law having no place in his account.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: this reverses the structure, since being *legibus solutus* means freedom from ordinary positive law while divine law remains a genuine normative limit.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the Hobbesian caricature the owner warns against; Bodin's absolutism is juristic rather than caprice, and he retains higher-law constraints.
- **C:** Correct: the owner lists exactly three limits — divine law, natural law and the *leges imperii* — and glosses *legibus solutus* as freedom from ordinary positive law only.
- **D:** Incorrect: the fundamental laws are one of three limits, and dropping the other two removes the moral content that distinguishes Bodin from pure absolutism.

**Examiner trap 7:** Making Bodin lawless removes the very feature that makes him answerable to the 2022 compatibility question, since the interesting tension is between an authority that is supreme in positive law and one that is nonetheless normatively bound.

#### MCQ 8. Bodin's indivisibility thesis

What exactly does Bodin's indivisibility claim forbid, and what does it permit?

A. It forbids the delegation of any executive function and permits only advisory bodies with no power to issue binding instructions.

B. It forbids the division of sovereign title and permits the distribution of governmental functions among estates, courts and magistrates.

C. It forbids federal arrangements of every kind and permits unitary states to establish provincial councils with limited competence.

D. It forbids consultation with subordinate authorities and permits the sovereign to legislate only through a single unified assembly.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Bodin allows magistrates to issue commands; what he denies them is the final legislative power, so the prohibition is not on delegated function.
- **B:** Correct: the owner states that division of governmental functions is possible while division of sovereign title is not, citing advisory estates and subordinate courts as permitted.
- **C:** Incorrect: federal systems are a later critical counter-example to the thesis rather than the content of the thesis itself.
- **D:** Incorrect: consultation is compatible with indivisibility, and Bodin's argument concerns the location of finality rather than the procedure of legislating.

**Examiner trap 8:** Answering the constitutional-division objection by simply repeating that sovereignty is indivisible loses the mark; the reply must distinguish administrative distribution from the sharing of final law-making power, and then concede that critics regard the distinction as strained.

#### MCQ 9. Austin's definition of the sovereign

Which formulation states Austin's definition with the precision the paper rewards?

A. A determinate human superior, receiving habitual obedience from the bulk of a society, and habitually obeying no other like superior.

B. The state considered in the abstract, exercising the totality of public powers, and answerable to the electorate at regular intervals.

C. Whichever institution possesses the constitutional competence to amend the fundamental law of the political community it governs.

D. The person or body that commands the greatest coercive force and can therefore secure compliance from the population it rules.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: this reproduces all three linked elements — determinacy, habitual obedience by the bulk, and obedience to no one — which together close the chain of legal validity.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner's first standing trap is exactly this, since Austin's sovereign is a determinate person or body rather than the state in the abstract.
- **C:** Incorrect: locating sovereignty in an amending competence is the *relocation* Austinians attempt under pressure from federalism, not Austin's own definition.
- **D:** Incorrect: this reduces the definition to force and abandons the distinction from mere coercion that the concept requires from the outset.

**Examiner trap 9:** Dropping the third element — that the sovereign habitually obeys no one — makes the international-law criticism unanswerable, because the criticism turns precisely on the absence of any determinate global superior.

#### MCQ 10. Command, sanction and the law of facilities

Which criticism of the command theory does the owner treat as the most damaging to Austin's definition itself?

A. That penal law is only a small part of any developed legal system, so the paradigm case is statistically unrepresentative.

B. That international law binds states through treaty and custom despite the absence of any determinate global superior.

C. That federal constitutions distribute competences so that no single level of government is superior in all matters.

D. That much of law confers facilities rather than issuing orders, so that a defective will is a nullity and not a sanction.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: relative frequency is not the objection; the difficulty is conceptual, since power-conferring rules cannot be redescribed as commands at all.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is a strong external criticism of the theory's scope, but Austin can answer it by denying that international law is law in the strict sense.
- **C:** Incorrect: federalism attacks indivisibility empirically, and Austin replies by relocating finality to the constitution-making authority.
- **D:** Correct: the owner treats power-conferring rules as an internal defect Austin cannot solve, since nullity is the absence of legal effect rather than an evil attached to disobedience.

**Examiner trap 10:** Describing the nullity of a defective legal act as a "sanction" concedes Hart's point while appearing to resist it, because a sanction is an evil imposed for disobedience whereas nullity is simply the failure of an attempted legal act.

#### MCQ 11. Why the five Austinian attributes are forced

On the reading developed here, why does Austin's definition require determinacy, absoluteness, indivisibility, illimitability and inalienability?

A. Because each attribute was independently observed in the centralised European legal orders that Austin had studied in detail.

B. Because each closes a possible gap through which the chain of legal validity would fail to terminate in a final source.

C. Because each was inherited directly from Bodin, whose definition Austin restated in more precise analytical language.

D. Because each is required by the moral claim that a legal system must make on the obedience of the subjects it governs.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner concedes that Austinian indivisibility is stricter than empirical political reality and is a logical feature of the scheme, not an observation.
- **B:** Correct: indeterminacy would leave the origin unlocatable, divisibility would create rival origins, limitation would push validity to a further source, and alienability would make finality transferable.
- **C:** Incorrect: Bodin supplies absolute and perpetual and retains higher-law limits, so Austin's illimitable legal sovereign is a radicalisation rather than a restatement.
- **D:** Incorrect: Austin's project separates what law is from what law ought to be, so no moral claim enters the definition of the attributes.

**Examiner trap 11:** Listing the five attributes without stating why the list is forced scores as vocabulary rather than argument, and it leaves the candidate unable to explain why empirical counter-examples do not by themselves refute the scheme.

#### MCQ 12. The pattern shared by the three structural criticisms

Federalism, international law and customary law are usually presented as three separate objections to Austin. What single pattern do they share?

A. Each shows that Austin underestimated the practical importance of moral limits on the exercise of legal authority.

B. Each shows that Austin's theory applies well to monarchies but poorly to any political order with an elected legislature.

C. Each locates law where no determinate superior stands, and each Austinian reply relocates the sovereign at a cost to determinacy.

D. Each depends on historical examples drawn from periods after Austin wrote, so none of them can fairly be pressed against him.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: moral limits form a separate criticism in the owner's table, and the three structural objections turn on the absence of a determinate superior rather than on morality.
- **B:** Incorrect: federalism and custom arise in monarchies too, and the democracy objection is treated separately in the owner's scheme.
- **C:** Correct: the constitution-making authority, the denial that international law is law and the device of tacit adoption are three relocations, each weakening determinacy.
- **D:** Incorrect: customary law and federal arrangements long predate Austin, and Maine's counter-example is drawn from a contemporaneous political order.

**Examiner trap 12:** Presenting Austin's denial that international law is truly law as an oversight misreads him, since it is his explicit and argued position; the criticism must attack the definition's narrowness, not his attention.

#### MCQ 13. Austin and democracy

Which answer structure does the owner require for the compatibility question?

A. A single negative verdict, since democracy disperses authority and therefore cannot contain a legally supreme law-making body at all.

B. A single affirmative verdict, since every democracy contains a legislature that can be identified as the determinate human superior.

C. A historical verdict, since Austin wrote before mass democracy and cannot be assessed against institutions he never observed.

D. A two-level verdict: compatible in the narrow legal sense of naming a law-maker, incompatible in the constitutional-political sense.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner concedes that in a narrow legal sense one may identify a law-making body, so a flat negative overstates the case.
- **B:** Incorrect: this ignores that democracy makes final authority derivative, revisable and answerable, which is what Austin's illimitability forbids.
- **C:** Incorrect: the papers ask the question directly and the owner answers it doctrinally, so a refusal to judge does not meet the demand.
- **D:** Correct: the owner's balanced structure is yes in the narrow legal sense and no in the deeper constitutional-political sense, with the reason for the split stated.

**Examiner trap 13:** Answering the 2021 stem with a flat yes or no forfeits the structural mark, because the demand is a graded judgement in which the level at which each verdict holds must be named.

#### MCQ 14. Parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy

Which statement handles the jurisdiction control correctly?

A. In the United Kingdom Parliament is the supreme legal authority, while in India Parliament exercises competences conferred by a supreme written Constitution.

B. In the United Kingdom the courts may strike down primary legislation, while in India Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution at will.

C. Both the United Kingdom and India practise parliamentary sovereignty, because both have parliamentary government and a legislature that makes law.

D. In India the judiciary is sovereign because judicial review permits it to invalidate legislation enacted by the elected legislature.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner's table states precisely this contrast, with courts generally unable to overrule UK primary legislation and Indian competences derived from the Constitution.
- **B:** Incorrect: this inverts both halves, since UK courts generally cannot overrule primary legislation and the Indian amending power is limited by the basic-structure doctrine.
- **C:** Incorrect: parliamentary sovereignty is jurisdiction-specific and is not a synonym for every parliamentary form of government.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner expressly cautions that India's judiciary is not sovereign merely because it reviews legislation.

**Examiner trap 14:** Using *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) as proof that a philosophical theory is correct misclassifies it; it is a dated judicial decision illustrating a legally limited amending competence, not evidence for or against Austin, Kelsen or Hart.

#### MCQ 15. Kelsen's hierarchy of norms

Which statement gives Kelsen's central argument and its effect on sovereignty?

A. Validity derives from the sovereign's command, and the basic norm records the moral principles that a legal order must satisfy.

B. Validity derives from a higher norm, terminating in a presupposed basic norm, so the sovereign becomes the order's highest competence.

C. Validity derives from the convergent practice of officials, and the basic norm is the social fact of their acceptance of that practice.

D. Validity derives from the effectiveness of the legal order, and the basic norm is an empirical generalisation about observed obedience.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Kelsen's founding premise is that "is" does not yield "ought", so no fact of command generates validity, and the basic norm is expressly morally inert.
- **B:** Correct: the owner sets out the pyramid from administrative order to statute to constitution to presupposed *Grundnorm*, with sovereignty becoming an attribute of the normative order.
- **C:** Incorrect: convergent official practice is Hart's rule of recognition, which Hart offers precisely against Kelsen's presupposed norm.
- **D:** Incorrect: efficacy is a *condition* of validity in Kelsen's treatment of revolution, never its source, and the basic norm is presupposed rather than generalised.

**Examiner trap 15:** Filing Kelsen alongside Laski as an external critic destroys the two-stage structure that twenty-mark stems reward, since Kelsen repairs Austin from inside legal positivism while Laski attacks from outside it.

#### MCQ 16. Kelsen, revolution and the residual problem

A successful revolution replaces a constitution without any authorising norm. What is Kelsen's reply, and what problem remains?

A. He denies that revolutions occur within legal science, and the residual problem is that his theory cannot describe political change at all.

B. He treats the new constitution as valid because the revolutionaries obtain habitual obedience, and the residual problem is circularity with Austin.

C. He argues that the old basic norm survives all changes of regime, and the residual problem is that valid law would then never change.

D. He holds that jurists presuppose a new basic norm once the order is by and large effective, and efficacy thereby re-enters the theory.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Kelsen offers a positive account of revolutionary change rather than excluding it, and the owner records that account explicitly.
- **B:** Incorrect: habitual obedience is Austin's criterion; Kelsen's condition is the by-and-large effectiveness of the order, and he does not adopt Austin's formulation.
- **C:** Incorrect: the survival of the old basic norm would make the new order invalid, which is the opposite of Kelsen's position.
- **D:** Correct: the owner states that a new basic norm is presupposed when the order becomes by and large effective, and concedes that this readmits the fact of power.

**Examiner trap 16:** Defending the basic norm as though it were a demonstrated truth misstates Kelsen's own claim, which is that it is a transcendental-logical condition of treating anything as valid law — a postulate, not a fact.

#### MCQ 17. Hart's three defects and three secondary rules

Which pairing of defect and cure is correct on Hart's account?

A. Uncertainty is cured by rules of change; the static character is cured by rules of adjudication; inefficiency is cured by the rule of recognition.

B. Uncertainty is cured by rules of adjudication; the static character is cured by the rule of recognition; inefficiency is cured by rules of change.

C. Uncertainty is cured by the rule of recognition; the static character is cured by rules of change; inefficiency is cured by rules of adjudication.

D. Uncertainty is cured by primary rules; the static character is cured by sanctions; inefficiency is cured by the sovereign's habitual command.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: rules of change authorise creation, amendment and repeal, which cures stasis rather than uncertainty about which rules count as law.
- **B:** Incorrect: rules of adjudication confer authority to determine breach and remedy, which cures inefficiency rather than uncertainty.
- **C:** Correct: the owner's table pairs recognition with uncertainty, change with the static character of custom, and adjudication with the inefficiency of diffuse enforcement.
- **D:** Incorrect: primary rules are the regime whose defects are being cured, and sanctions and command belong to the Austinian model Hart rejects.

**Examiner trap 17:** Treating Hart's rule theory as a restatement of Austin misses the rejection at its core, since Hart denies that nullity is a sanction, that habit explains succession, and that being obliged is having an obligation.

#### MCQ 18. Being obliged and having an obligation

Why does Hart regard this distinction as fatal to Austin's account of legal obligation?

A. Because habitual obedience records only external regularity and so cannot separate a legal duty from submission to a gunman's threat.

B. Because it shows that sanctions are morally objectionable, so any theory built upon them cannot justify the state's coercive power.

C. Because it proves that most citizens obey the law from fear, so habitual obedience is an inaccurate description of their conduct.

D. Because it establishes that only judges, and not ordinary subjects, can be said to have obligations under a developed legal system.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the internal point of view treats a rule as a reason for conduct and a ground for criticising deviation, which external regularity cannot capture.
- **B:** Incorrect: Hart's objection is analytical rather than moral, and he insists that legal validity be explained without moral evaluation.
- **C:** Incorrect: the distinction concerns what obligation *is*, not the empirical motives from which people in fact comply.
- **D:** Incorrect: the internal point of view is available to participants generally, and the rule of recognition is described through officials without confining obligation to them.

**Examiner trap 18:** Developing Dworkin's principles-and-hard-cases objection inside a sovereignty answer breaches ownership; name it as the next objection to Hart and route it, because the doctrine belongs to Individual and State.

#### MCQ 19. Laski's central thesis

Which statement gives Laski's position without overstating or understating it?

A. The state should be abolished because every association is equally competent to govern the affairs of its own members.

B. The state should be preserved unchanged because plural associations derive whatever authority they possess from its permission.

C. The state is one association among many, uniquely important yet not the sole and morally unlimited source of all authority.

D. The state is one association among many, exactly equal in function and authority to churches, unions and professional bodies.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner records that Laski is a pluralist and not an anarchist, and that he does not deny the need for the state.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the monist position Laski attacks, since he refuses to treat associations as permissions granted by sovereignty.
- **C:** Correct: this reproduces the owner's formulation, including the concession that the state retains coordinating and coercive capacities.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner expressly warns that "one association among many" does not mean all associations are equal in function.

**Examiner trap 19:** Quoting the early Laski's attack on sovereignty as a legal fiction as though it were his settled view ignores the phase caution: his mature position conceded sovereignty's importance while denying omnipotence.

#### MCQ 20. Why Laski rejects absolute sovereignty

His rejection rests on three grounds. What is the structural feature that makes the argument strong?

A. The three grounds are three restatements of a single moral premise, which gives the argument unusual rhetorical force.

B. The three grounds are of three different kinds — moral, political and empirical — so answering one leaves the others standing.

C. The three grounds are arranged in order of increasing strength, so the final ground alone is sufficient to defeat monism.

D. The three grounds are drawn from three different thinkers, so the argument carries the authority of an entire school.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: philosophical falsity, political danger and sociological unrealism are distinct claims rather than restatements of one premise.
- **B:** Correct: their logical independence is what forces the monist to answer each separately, and conceding the empirical ground already abandons indivisibility.
- **C:** Incorrect: the owner presents the three as independent rather than as a ranked series culminating in a decisive ground.
- **D:** Incorrect: Figgis, Cole and MacIver locate the school and supply support, but the three grounds are Laski's own and are not divided among them.

**Examiner trap 20:** Answering the 2018 stem on whether Laski's view is a satisfactory position without naming the criterion of satisfactoriness produces an unanchored verdict; name the test, apply it, and concede the coordination debt.

#### MCQ 21. Kautilya's seven-limbed state

What is the philosophical point of the *saptāṅga* doctrine?

A. Sovereignty is systemic rather than merely personal, since effective rule depends on ministers, territory, defence, treasury, force and allies.

B. Sovereignty is divided among seven office-holders, each of whom exercises final authority within a separately defined sphere of competence.

C. Sovereignty is administrative rather than political, since the list enumerates departments of government rather than sources of authority.

D. Sovereignty is contractual, since the seven limbs represent the parties whose agreement establishes and sustains the authority of the ruler.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner presents the state as an organism in which the ruler is head but cannot function without the other limbs, making sovereignty systemic.
- **B:** Incorrect: royal primacy is preserved; the limbs are constituents of state capacity rather than seven co-ordinate holders of final authority.
- **C:** Incorrect: the doctrine is an argument about what makes rule possible, and the owner contrasts it with Austin's search for the legal source of command.
- **D:** Incorrect: nothing in the *saptāṅga* scheme is contractual, and reading consent into it imports a modern framework the owner warns against.

**Examiner trap 21:** Translating *janapada* as territory alone drops its people, and with them the productive social base that connects the seven limbs to the welfare argument.

#### MCQ 22. Calibrated coercion

What does *daṇḍanīti* assert about the relation between force and sovereignty?

A. That coercion is an emergency power, to be reserved for occasions when ordinary administration and counsel have already failed.

B. That coercion should be maximised, since the ruler's security depends on the certainty that disobedience will be severely punished.

C. That coercion should be minimised, since the ruler's legitimacy depends chiefly on welfare provision rather than on enforcement.

D. That coercion is constitutive of rule and must be calibrated, since its absence brings disorder and its excess breeds resentment.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: *daṇḍa* is one of the seven constituents of the state, which makes coercive capacity a permanent structural feature rather than an exceptional one.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner states that excessive *daṇḍa* breeds resentment and instability, so maximisation is self-defeating on Kautilya's own terms.
- **C:** Incorrect: the owner is explicit that without *daṇḍa* society falls into disorder, so minimisation is equally rejected.
- **D:** Correct: the art of sovereignty lies in calibrated punishment, and the *nīti* half of the term names the policy governing the use of force.

**Examiner trap 22:** Reading modern proportionality doctrine into *daṇḍanīti* manufactures a theory of rights; the restraint is prudential and stability-based, resting on the ruler's interest rather than on enforceable claims held by subjects.

#### MCQ 23. The circle of states and the printed maxim

The 2025 paper prints "There is no permanent friend or permanent enemy." How should the candidate handle it?

A. As a verified quotation from the *Arthaśāstra*, cited with the translator's name and used as direct textual evidence for the answer.

B. As the examiner's framing, answered through the verified *maṇḍala* and *ṣāḍguṇya* doctrines that make alignment positional and policy responsive.

C. As a maxim of modern realist international relations, which should be traced to its twentieth-century sources before Kautilya is discussed.

D. As a mistranslation, which the candidate should correct before proceeding to expound Kautilya's actual view of interstate relations.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner records that the exact wording has not been verified in the local *Arthaśāstra* synopsis or the checked source passages, so calling it a quotation invents a fact.
- **B:** Correct: the owner instructs that the line be treated as prompt wording and answered through the circle of states and the six-fold policy.
- **C:** Incorrect: the stem asks for Kautilya's view of sovereignty, so relocating the discussion into modern realism answers a different question.
- **D:** Incorrect: nothing in the source trail establishes a mistranslation, and asserting one would itself be an unsupported claim.

**Examiner trap 23:** Reducing Kautilya to foreign policy loses most of the owned corpus, because the seven limbs, calibrated coercion and the duty to security and welfare carry four of the five Kautilya parts.

#### MCQ 24. Sovereignty under globalisation

Which set of consequences correctly matches the four mechanisms the owner distinguishes?

A. Treaty transfers sovereignty, delegation abolishes it, pooling suspends it, and material dependence restores it once the asymmetry ends.

B. Treaty suspends sovereignty, delegation shares it, pooling transfers it, and material dependence has no consequence for it whatever.

C. Treaty is self-limitation, delegation moves the exercise while the source stays treaty-based, pooling shares finality in a field, dependence narrows autonomy.

D. Treaty creates a global sovereign, delegation confirms it, pooling extends it, and material dependence demonstrates that legal independence is fictional.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: treaty commitment is an exercise of a state's own recognised capacity and constrains future action without transferring sovereignty at all.
- **B:** Incorrect: material dependence has the most serious practical consequence, since legal independence may survive while effective autonomy declines.
- **C:** Correct: this reproduces the owner's four-row table exactly, preserving the difference between legal source, exercise, shared finality and practical constraint.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner cautions that not every international organisation is supranational and that no supranational institution is automatically sovereign in Austin's sense.

**Examiner trap 24:** Writing that sovereignty simply disappears under globalisation abandons the analytical grid; the required phrasing is reconfigured, constrained, pooled or negotiated, and the four mechanisms must be kept apart.

#### MCQ 25. Remedial — the provenance pair that the paper punishes

Which attribution is correct?

A. Kelsen, *The Concept of Law*, 1945; Hart, *General Theory of Law and State*, 1961.

B. Kelsen, *General Theory of Law and State*, 1961; Hart, *The Concept of Law*, 1945.

C. Kelsen, *The Concept of Law*, 1961; Hart, *General Theory of Law and State*, 1945.

D. Kelsen, *General Theory of Law and State*, first English edition 1945; Hart, *The Concept of Law*, 1961.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the titles are swapped, since *The Concept of Law* is Hart's work and *General Theory of Law and State* is Kelsen's.
- **B:** Incorrect: both the titles and the years are swapped, producing the exact error the owner marks as most frequently committed.
- **C:** Incorrect: the titles are swapped and the years are attached to the wrong authors, compounding two separate mistakes in one line.
- **D:** Correct: Kelsen's *General Theory of Law and State* appeared in English in 1945 and Hart's *The Concept of Law* in 1961, cited by title and year only.

**Examiner trap 25:** Guessing a year rather than naming the works in sequence converts an avoidable risk into a factual error; where the date is uncertain, write "Austin, then Kelsen, then Hart" and omit the years entirely.

#### MCQ 26. Remedial — three dissolutions that must not be merged

Kelsen, Hart and Laski each dissolve the personal absolute sovereign. What replaces him in each case?

A. A norm-hierarchy for all three, differing only in whether the apex is presupposed, socially accepted or democratically authorised.

B. A norm-hierarchy for Kelsen, an accepted official practice for Hart, and a plurality of associations for Laski.

C. An accepted official practice for Kelsen, a plurality of associations for Hart, and a norm-hierarchy for Laski.

D. A plurality of associations for all three, differing only in whether the associations are legal, social or economic in character.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Laski offers no norm-hierarchy at all, and Hart's rule of recognition is a social fact rather than a norm standing above the system.
- **B:** Correct: the owner states this three-way divergence and warns explicitly against merging the three into one modern critique of Austin.
- **C:** Incorrect: the replacements are attached to the wrong thinkers, reversing the very distinction the owner requires candidates to preserve.
- **D:** Incorrect: only Laski replaces the sovereign with plural associations; Kelsen and Hart remain legal positivists offering intra-systemic accounts of validity.

**Examiner trap 26:** Presenting Kelsen and Hart as allies of Laski loses the ordering that marks philosophical control, since a twenty-mark answer should run the internal critique first and the external critique second.

#### MCQ 27. Remedial — the Indian illustration, handled safely

Which use of Indian material is permitted by the owner's cautions?

A. India illustrates competence-distributed authority under constitutional supremacy, with judicial review and a limited amending power.

B. India illustrates Austinian sovereignty, since Parliament is habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and obeys no like superior.

C. India illustrates Kelsen's theory, since the Preamble of the Constitution functions as the basic norm of the Indian legal order.

D. India illustrates Laski's pluralism, since federal states possess a share of sovereignty equal to that of the Union government.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner permits exactly this description — rule-governed, competence-distributed authority that Austin's illimitable superior cannot accommodate.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner forbids describing any Indian institution, office or period as "the sovereign" in Austin's sense.
- **C:** Incorrect: the owner forbids attributing a *Grundnorm* to any named document as though Kelsen had said so.
- **D:** Incorrect: this overstates the constitutional position and imports a philosophical conclusion that the federal arrangement does not establish.

**Examiner trap 27:** Calling India a system of parliamentary sovereignty confuses parliamentary *government* with the United Kingdom's jurisdictional doctrine, and adding that the judiciary is therefore sovereign compounds the error.

#### MCQ 28. Remedial — what globalisation does and does not do

Which formulation is both accurate and usable in a fifteen-mark answer?

A. Sovereignty has been abolished, since no state can now act without the approval of international institutions and financial markets.

B. Sovereignty is unaffected, since legal independence is unchanged and every constraint is voluntarily accepted by the state concerned.

C. Sovereignty is reconfigured: legal form persists, exercise may be delegated or pooled, and material dependence can reduce effective autonomy.

D. Sovereignty is transferred to supranational bodies, which now satisfy Austin's definition of a determinate and illimitable superior.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: states continue to claim final authority over territory, law, security and political membership, so abolition overstates the evidence.
- **B:** Incorrect: this ignores conditionality, under which legal independence survives while the range of feasible choices narrows sharply.
- **C:** Correct: this preserves the owner's distinction between legal persistence, delegated exercise, pooled finality and practical constraint.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner cautions that even a supranational institution is not automatically sovereign in Austin's sense.

#### MCQ 29. Remedial — Bodin against Hobbes

- **C:** Incorrect: the estates are advisory in Bodin's scheme, and the description of Hobbes here is not supported by this owner.

#### MCQ 31. Remedial — Kautilya beyond strategy

#### Solved PYQ 1 · 2018 · Q1(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** Discuss Kautilya's concept of sovereignty in the light of 'Danda-neety'.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Discuss" — exposition plus one adjudicated tension, not a description of punishment |
| Core issue | What calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*) reveals about the nature of Kautilyan sovereignty |
| Scope boundary | Ten marks: lead with calibration; use the seven limbs and welfare as support only |
| Thinker thesis | Sovereignty is the capacity to maintain order through **disciplined** force, not arbitrary cruelty |
| Non-negotiables | *Daṇḍa* as one of the seven limbs; the two-sided failure of too little and too much; welfare as the complement |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ For Kautilya, sovereignty is measured by the ruler's capacity to calibrate force, not by his freedom to use it; *daṇḍanīti* is therefore a doctrine of proportion rather than of severity.

**Where coercion sits.** ✅ Coercive force (*daṇḍa*) is one of the seven constituents of the state (*saptāṅga*), alongside the ruler (*svāmī*), ministers (*amātya*), territory with its people (*janapada*), fortified defence (*durga*), treasury (*kośa*) and ally (*mitra*). ⚠️ Placing it among the constituents makes the capacity to punish a permanent structural feature of rule rather than an emergency power.

**The calibration argument.** ✅ Without *daṇḍa*, society falls into disorder; with excessive *daṇḍa*, the ruler breeds resentment and instability. ⚠️ The art of sovereignty therefore lies in ordered, proportionate enforcement — *daṇḍa* is the force and *nīti* the policy that governs its use.

**What this shows about sovereignty.** ⚠️ The ruler is head of an organism rather than an unlimited master: authority is real, but its legitimacy and durability are judged by order and by security and welfare (*yogakṣema*). ✅ Fear without welfare is self-defeating; welfare without order is impossible.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Kautilyan sovereignty is strong precisely because it is duty-bound. ❓ The restraint is nonetheless prudential rather than rights-based: it binds the ruler's interest, not his entitlement, and an accurate answer concedes that difference rather than reading modern constitutionalism into the text.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the commonest failure here is a paragraph on ancient punishment. The stem says *in the light of daṇḍanīti*, which means the doctrine of calibrated coercion must be the lens through which sovereignty is characterised.

**Exam-length execution — 10 marks, about 150 to 220 words:** thesis two lines; placement three; calibration three; welfare link three; verdict two.

- **Structure:** thesis → *daṇḍa* as a constituent → two-sided failure → welfare complement → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** *daṇḍanīti* as the science of punishment and disciplined enforcement.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs by name; disorder without force and resentment beyond it.
- **Distinction:** ordered coercion against blind terror.
- **Criticism:** prudential restraint is not a rights-based limit, conceded in the verdict.

#### Solved PYQ 2 · 2018 · Q2(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** Critically evaluate Laski's view on sovereignty as a satisfactory position in political theory.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Critically evaluate … as a satisfactory position" — the criterion of satisfactoriness must be named first |
| Core issue | Whether pluralism succeeds as a **positive** theory, not merely as a critique |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: exposition, two objections with replies, one residual problem, conditional verdict |
| Thinker thesis | The state is one association among many; sovereignty cannot be absolute |
| Non-negotiables | Three grounds of three kinds; the phase caution; the coordination debt |

##### Independent model answer

**Naming the test.** ⚠️ A position is satisfactory in political theory if it describes political life accurately, protects the values it claims to protect, and supplies an account of the function it displaces. Laski passes the first two tests decisively and the third only partly.

**The position.** ✅ Laski rejects the monistic doctrine that the state possesses absolute, indivisible and unlimited sovereignty. Individuals belong simultaneously to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; the state is therefore **one association among many**, uniquely important but not the sole source of obligation. ✅ Allegiance must be earned by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life, not claimed by legal title.

**Why absolutism is rejected — three grounds of three kinds.** ✅ Philosophically, no human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience; politically, absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty; sociologically, real societies distribute power among many groups. ⚠️ Their independence is the argument's strength: answering one leaves the others standing.

**First objection and reply.** ✅ Without a final sovereign, conflict between associations becomes insoluble. ⚠️ Laski replies that legal finality does not eliminate conflict but often suppresses legitimate diversity, and that modern orders need a final **procedure**, not an unanswerable will.

**Second objection and reply.** ✅ Shared sovereignty is conceptually incoherent. ⚠️ His real claim is that the monistic concept itself misdescribes overlapping authority.

**Residual problem.** ⚠️ Neither reply supplies a decision procedure for emergencies, defence or redistribution; MacIver's residual coordinating role concedes exactly this.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Satisfactory as a critique of absolutism and as a defence of liberty through social plurality; **not** satisfactory as a complete replacement for the concept of final authority. ✅ Note also the phase caution: "Laski abolishes sovereignty" is as inaccurate as "Laski accepts Austin".

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** "satisfactory" is an evaluative word with no fixed content, so the criterion must be supplied by the candidate in the first two lines. Scripts that omit it produce a verdict with nothing behind it.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** test two lines; position four; three grounds three; two objection–reply pairs four; residual two; verdict three.

- **Structure:** name the test → position → three grounds → two objections with replies → residual → conditional verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pluralism; earned allegiance; the state as one association among many.
- **Evidence:** the association list; the three grounds; MacIver's coordination role.
- **Distinction:** pluralist against anarchist; conceptual challenge against administrative decentralisation.
- **Criticism:** the coordination debt, stated as an unpaid debt rather than argued away.

#### Solved PYQ 3 · 2019 · Q3(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Discuss how far does Austin's concept of sovereignty go along with Kautilya's concept of sovereignty.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "How far … go along with" — a **degree** judgement on fixed comparison axes |
| Core issue | Whether two theories answering different questions can be said to agree at all |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: five axes run in parallel, then an adjudication; not two mini-essays |
| Thinker thesis | Austin analyses the **form** of authority; Kautilya prescribes its **function** |
| Non-negotiables | Determinate human superior; *saptāṅga*, *daṇḍanīti*, *yogakṣema*; the asymmetry verdict |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The two converge on a single point — that a political order requires an identifiable apex of direction — and diverge on everything that follows, because they are answering different questions: Austin asks what makes a command legally valid, Kautilya asks what makes rule possible.

**Axis one — nature of the theory.** ✅ Austin's is analytical jurisprudence, separating what law **is** from what it ought to be. Kautilya's is statecraft: administration, treasury, coercion, alliance, intelligence and welfare.

**Axis two — locus of sovereignty.** ✅ Austin locates it in a **determinate human superior**, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one. Kautilya locates it in the ruler (*svāmī*) as head of a seven-limbed state (*saptāṅga*): ministers, territory with its people, fortified defence, treasury, coercive force and ally. ⚠️ Austin's sovereign is a person or body; Kautilya's is a **system with a head**.

**Axis three — limits.** ✅ Austin's sovereign is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable, with moral limits placed outside the definition. Kautilya's ruler is bound by dharma, prudence, counsel and the duty to security and welfare (*yogakṣema*); a ruler without treasury or ministers holds title without effective sovereignty.

**Axis four — law and order.** ✅ Austin: law is command backed by sanction. Kautilya: order is maintained through calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*), counsel, treasury and administration — too little force brings disorder, too much brings resentment.

**Axis five — the external dimension.** ✅ Austin's is underdeveloped, extending only to the denial that international law is truly law. Kautilya's is highly developed through the circle of states (*maṇḍala*) and the six-fold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*).

**Adjudication.** ⚠️ They "go along" only at the thinnest level of shared structure — an apex of direction — and part company on source, limits, purpose and external relations. ⚠️ The asymmetry is the answer: **Austin analyses the form of authority; Kautilya prescribes its function**, so Austin's sovereign is legally supreme because no superior commands it, while Kautilya's ruler is politically central but functionally bound by dharma, welfare, ministers, treasury, force and allies.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** comparison stems are lost by writing two sequential summaries. Fix the axes in advance and run both thinkers down each one; the adjudication then writes itself.

**Exam-length execution — 20 marks, about 330 to 400 words:** thesis three lines; five axes at four lines each; adjudication four.

- **Structure:** thesis → five parallel axes → asymmetry adjudication.
- **Doctrine:** determinate human superior; *saptāṅga*; *daṇḍanīti*; *yogakṣema*; *maṇḍala* and *ṣāḍguṇya*.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs by name; the five Austinian attributes; the international-law denial.
- **Distinction:** legal source of command against operational anatomy of rule.
- **Criticism:** the anachronism control — Kautilya predates the modern doctrine, so agreement must be graded, never assumed.

#### Solved PYQ 4 · 2020 · Q1(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the importance of Bodin's theory of sovereignty.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Explain the importance" — internal logic and historical significance, with light criticism only |
| Core issue | What Bodin **added** that political thought did not previously possess |
| Scope boundary | Ten marks: definition, the state-building move, the limits, one qualification |
| Thinker thesis | He made authority impersonal and gave the modern state its vocabulary |
| Non-negotiables | Absolute and perpetual; the law-making mark; the three limits; not Hobbes |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Bodin's importance is not that he made rulers stronger but that he made authority **impersonal**, supplying modern politics with a language of permanent, undivided public power.

**The definition.** ✅ Sovereignty is the **absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth**: *absolute* means subject to no human superior within the commonwealth; *perpetual* means attached to the office rather than to a temporary magistrate. ✅ Its pre-eminent mark is the power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without the consent of any superior.

**Why this mattered.** ⚠️ Writing amid the French wars of religion, when rival churches, estates and feudal powers each claimed finality, Bodin identified what a community must possess to end its disputes: one authority whose law-making word is last. ⚠️ Perpetuity does the state-building work — a power that survives its holder is an institution, and only an institution supplies a continuous procedure of closure.

**The limits, which must not be omitted.** ✅ He is *legibus solutus* only of ordinary positive law, remaining bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*). ✅ This is why he is **not** simply Hobbes before Hobbes.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Historically foundational, normatively problematic: he gives later thought its vocabulary — absolute, perpetual, undivided — which Austin radicalises and Laski attacks, but offers weak guarantees against the concentration of power.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** "importance" invites a history lesson. Resist it: two lines of civil-war context are worth marks, two paragraphs are not, and the marks lie in what the two adjectives accomplish.

**Exam-length execution — 10 marks, about 150 to 220 words:** thesis two lines; definition three; significance four; limits three; verdict two.

- **Structure:** thesis → definition → why it mattered → the three limits → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** absolute and perpetual power; the pre-eminent law-making mark.
- **Evidence:** the wars of religion; *legibus solutus*; the *leges imperii*.
- **Distinction:** office against magistrate; supremacy in positive law against lawlessness.
- **Criticism:** weak protection against concentration, conceded in the verdict.

#### Solved PYQ 5 · 2020 · Q4(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** State and explain the relevance of the doctrine of sovereignty in times of globalization.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "State and explain the relevance" — a thesis, then a mechanism-by-mechanism explanation |
| Core issue | Whether interdependence abolishes, or merely reconfigures, final authority |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: four mechanisms distinguished, four thinkers redeployed, one concession |
| Thinker thesis | Constraint is not extinction; the mode changes, the question does not |
| Non-negotiables | Treaty, delegation, pooling, dependence kept apart; the supranational caution |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Globalisation **modifies** sovereignty without abolishing it: states still claim final authority over territory, law, security and political membership, so the doctrine remains relevant as an account of how that authority is exercised, delegated, pooled and constrained.

**Mechanism one — treaty commitment.** ✅ A state accepts binding rules through its own recognised treaty-making capacity. ⚠️ Self-limitation constrains future action without creating a global Austinian sovereign; indeed it **presupposes** the capacity it limits.

**Mechanism two — delegation.** ⚠️ A state authorises an institution to perform a defined adjudicative, administrative or monitoring function. The **exercise** of a competence moves outward while its legal source and limits remain treaty-based.

**Mechanism three — pooling.** ⚠️ States exercise a defined competence jointly through common voting or bargaining rules. Finality becomes **shared for the agreed field**, not universally transferred.

**Mechanism four — material dependence.** ⚠️ Finance, markets, technology or security asymmetry narrows the range of feasible choices. Here legal independence may survive while **effective autonomy declines** — the mechanism that does the most practical damage and involves no legal transfer at all.

**The four thinkers redeployed.** ⚠️ For **Bodin**, external pressure would not negate the need for a final internal authority. For **Austin**, globalisation complicates the idea of an uncommanded superior. For **Laski**, interdependence strengthens the critique of absolute state supremacy. For **Kautilya**, the strategic-relational reading of external sovereignty, through the circle of states, remains directly serviceable.

**Caution and verdict.** ⚠️ Not every international organisation is supranational, and no supranational institution is automatically sovereign in Austin's sense; ask which competence was conferred, by what instrument, with what exit rule, and whether dependence makes formal consent substantively unequal. ⚠️ **Sovereignty is illimitable in legal form, limited in political fact and negotiated in international practice** — which is precisely why the doctrine remains relevant rather than obsolete.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the whole answer turns on refusing to treat "globalisation" as one thing. Four mechanisms with four different consequences is the structure; the slogan "sovereignty has ended" is what the examiner is testing against.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** thesis two lines; four mechanisms at three lines each; redeployment four; caution and verdict four.

- **Structure:** thesis → four mechanisms → four thinkers → caution → degree verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pooled, negotiated and networked sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** treaty capacity; delegated function; joint voting rules; conditionality.
- **Distinction:** legal source against exercise; shared finality against transfer; legal independence against effective autonomy.
- **Criticism:** the conditionality concession, stated as the strongest case against the thesis.

#### Solved PYQ 6 · 2021 · Q1(c) · 10 marks

**Question:** Is Austin's theory of sovereignty compatible with democracy? Discuss.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Is … ? Discuss" — a graded verdict with the level of each judgement named |
| Core issue | Whether determinacy and illimitability survive democratic accountability |
| Scope boundary | Ten marks: the two-level structure, one Indian illustration, one caution |
| Thinker thesis | Compatible in legal form, incompatible in constitutional substance |
| Non-negotiables | Determinate human superior; the indeterminacy of "the people"; jurisdiction control |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Only partially, and the answer must be given at two levels: compatible in the narrow legal sense that a law-making body can always be named, incompatible in the deeper constitutional-political sense.

**The theory.** ✅ Austin's sovereign is a **determinate human superior**, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one, and is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**Where compatibility holds.** ✅ Every democracy contains an identifiable legislature, so at the level of legal form the Austinian can point to a determinate law-maker.

**Where it fails.** ⚠️ Democracy disperses authority through elections, rights, representation, opposition and constitutional limitation; it makes final authority **derivative, revisable and answerable**, whereas Austin's is legally supreme and obeys no one. ⚠️ Popular sovereignty sharpens the difficulty: "the people" are indeterminate, changing and institutionally mediated, so either the sovereign is not the people or the sovereign is not determinate.

**Illustration, handled carefully.** ✅ In India, Parliament exercises amendment power conferred by Article 368 of a supreme written Constitution, and *Kesavananda Bharati* (Supreme Court judgment, **1973**) holds that this power does not extend to altering the basic structure. ⚠️ This is a dated judicial decision illustrating a legally limited amending competence; it is not philosophical proof, and no Indian institution should be called "the Austinian sovereign".

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The **function** of final legal authority survives in a democracy; Austin's **account** of it does not.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** a flat yes or no forfeits the structural mark. Name the level at which each verdict holds, and the examiner sees a graded judgement rather than an opinion.

**Exam-length execution — 10 marks, about 150 to 220 words:** thesis two lines; theory two; compatibility one; failure four; illustration three; verdict two.

- **Structure:** two-level thesis → the theory → holds here → fails there → illustration → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** determinate human superior; illimitability; popular sovereignty as the counter-thesis.
- **Evidence:** Article 368; *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973).
- **Distinction:** legal form against constitutional substance; function against account.
- **Criticism:** the legal-status caution attached to the Indian illustration.

#### Solved PYQ 7 · 2021 · Q3(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** Discuss Kautilya's contribution regarding the concept of sovereignty. Is it applicable in a democratic form of government? Explain.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | Two-part stem: "Discuss … contribution" then "Is it applicable … ? Explain" |
| Core issue | Which elements are form-bound and which are principles of statecraft |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: contribution in four moves, applicability by selective appropriation |
| Thinker thesis | Strong but duty-bound authority; principles transferable, monarchical form not |
| Non-negotiables | *Saptāṅga*, *daṇḍanīti*, *maṇḍala*/*ṣāḍguṇya*, *yogakṣema*; the selectivity rule |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Kautilya's contribution is a theory of authority that is strong **because** duty-bound; its monarchical form is untransferable, but its principles of statecraft are directly applicable to democratic government.

**Contribution one — sovereignty as systemic.** ✅ The state is an organism of seven limbs (*saptāṅga*): ruler (*svāmī*), ministers (*amātya*), territory with its people (*janapada*), fortified defence (*durga*), treasury (*kośa*), coercive force (*daṇḍa*) and ally (*mitra*). ⚠️ A ruler without treasury or ministers holds title without effective sovereignty, so authority is a property of the whole organism.

**Contribution two — calibrated coercion.** ✅ *Daṇḍanīti* makes the capacity to punish constitutive of rule and its **proportion** the test of good rule: too little brings disorder, too much resentment.

**Contribution three — external sovereignty.** ✅ The circle of states (*maṇḍala*) and the six-fold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*) — peace, war, marching, remaining poised, seeking shelter, dual policy — make alignment positional and policy responsive.

**Contribution four — welfare as legitimacy.** ✅ Security and welfare (*yogakṣema*) tie the ruler's welfare to the people's; durability depends on prosperity, security and justice in administration. ⚠️ This is what moves Kautilya beyond pure force theory.

**Applicability — the selectivity rule.** ⚠️ What is not transferable is the monarchical form. What is transferable is institutional interdependence, prudent and proportionate use of force, treasury discipline, strategic diplomacy and welfare-centred governance. ✅ Democratic applicability lies in principles of statecraft, not in reproducing kingship.

**Objection and reply.** ❓ A democratic order cannot borrow from monarchical realism without undermining liberty. ⚠️ The reply is **selective appropriation**: retain strategic realism, administrative discipline and welfare orientation while rejecting monarchical absolutism.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Applicable in principle, not in form — and the caveat matters: prudential statecraft is not consent-based legitimacy, so welfare in Kautilya is a condition of durable rule rather than a right held by subjects.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** two-part stems are lost by answering only the first part well. Allocate roughly two-thirds to the contribution and one-third to a genuinely argued applicability verdict.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** thesis two lines; four contributions at three lines each; applicability four; objection–reply two; verdict two.

- **Structure:** thesis → four contributions → selectivity rule → objection and reply → qualified verdict.
- **Doctrine:** *saptāṅga*, *daṇḍanīti*, *maṇḍala*, *ṣāḍguṇya*, *yogakṣema*.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs and six measures by name; title without treasury.
- **Distinction:** form against principle; prudential duty against consent-based right.
- **Criticism:** the caveat that welfare is a condition of rule rather than an enforceable claim.

#### Solved PYQ 8 · 2022 · Q2(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** What arguments does Bodin present to contend that sovereignty must be absolute, perpetual and undivided? Is Bodin's conception of sovereignty compatible with the social and political ideals of equality, justice and liberty? Critically discuss.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | Two-part stem ending in "Critically discuss" — arguments first, then a criterion-based compatibility verdict |
| Core issue | Whether an authority defined as having no human superior can secure three modern ideals |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: three arguments, three ideals tested separately, one graded verdict |
| Thinker thesis | Historically foundational, normatively problematic |
| Non-negotiables | Each adjective argued, not asserted; the three limits; the ideals used as a **test**, not expounded |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Bodin's three arguments are internally coherent, but compatibility with equality, justice and liberty is **conditional at best**: his theory can deliver impartial law and public order while offering weak guarantees against the concentration of power.

**Argument for absoluteness.** ✅ Law requires a source not itself legally derivative from another earthly authority; the sovereign's pre-eminent mark is the power to make and unmake law for all subjects without a superior's consent. ⚠️ *Absolute* therefore denies a superior **law-maker**, not a superior **norm**.

**Argument for perpetuity.** ✅ Sovereignty attaches to the office rather than to a temporary magistrate or delegated official. ⚠️ A power that dies with its holder is a personal ascendancy; only one that survives its holder is a state capable of continuous closure.

**Argument for undividedness.** ⚠️ If two authorities are each final, conflict becomes insoluble and the civil disorder of the wars of religion returns. ✅ Division of governmental **functions** is permitted — advisory estates, subordinate courts — but division of sovereign **title** is not.

**The limits that qualify all three.** ✅ He is *legibus solutus* only of ordinary positive law, and remains bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*); arbitrary confiscation of property is discussed as incompatible with his own framework.

**Testing against equality.** ⚠️ A single impartial law-giver can dissolve feudal and confessional privilege, which serves equality before the law; but nothing in the theory requires equal civic standing, and the sovereign is the judge of his own compliance.

**Testing against justice.** ⚠️ Divine and natural law supply a normative standard, so the sovereign is not morally empty; ✅ yet no institution is empowered to enforce that standard against him, so the limits are conceptually real and practically thin.

**Testing against liberty.** ⚠️ Order is a condition of liberty, and Bodin secures it; but an authority with no human superior within the commonwealth gives liberty no institutional guarantee, which is exactly Laski's objection that absolute sovereignty is politically dangerous.

**Graded verdict.** ⚠️ **Historically foundational, normatively problematic.** Bodin makes the modern state possible and modern constitutional protection necessary; the compatibility is partial, conditional and unenforced.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the second half is where scripts collapse into "both are important". Test the three ideals **separately**, and let each test yield a different partial result; the verdict is then earned rather than announced.

**Exam-length execution — 20 marks, about 330 to 400 words:** thesis three lines; three arguments at three lines each; limits three; three tests at three lines each; verdict three.

- **Structure:** thesis → three arguments → the limits → three separate tests → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** absolute, perpetual, undivided; *legibus solutus*; *leges imperii*.
- **Evidence:** the law-making mark; advisory estates and subordinate courts; arbitrary confiscation.
- **Distinction:** superior law-maker against superior norm; functions against title.
- **Criticism:** unenforceable higher-law limits, conceded as the theory's permanent vulnerability.

#### Solved PYQ 9 · 2023 · Q2(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Elucidate why the absolute nature of sovereignty was rejected by Laski.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Elucidate why" — the internal logic of the rejection, expounded rather than merely listed |
| Core issue | The three independent grounds and what each rules out |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: three grounds developed, targets named, positive proposal, one residual |
| Thinker thesis | Absolutism is false, dangerous and unrealistic — three claims of three kinds |
| Non-negotiables | Pluralist not anarchist; not decentralisation; the coordination debt |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Laski rejects absolute sovereignty on three logically independent grounds — philosophical, political and sociological — and the independence is what makes the rejection difficult to answer.

**Ground one — philosophically false.** ✅ No human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience. ⚠️ Obligation is generated wherever human purposes are pursued in common, so a claim that all obligation derives from one will is not merely severe but mistaken about the source of obligation itself.

**Ground two — politically dangerous.** ✅ Absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty. ⚠️ If the state is the sole final authority, dissenting associations, minority rights, local autonomies and moral conscience can all be overridden in the name of public supremacy; pluralism is therefore a doctrine of **liberty** as much as of sociology.

**Ground three — sociologically unrealistic.** ✅ Real societies distribute power among many groups and institutions. Individuals belong to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; associations are not permissions granted by sovereignty but **real sites of personality, cooperation and authority**.

**The two targets.** ⚠️ He attacks both the legal abstraction of Austin — the determinate superior whose command exhausts law — and the absolutist impulse inherited from Bodin.

**The positive proposal.** ✅ Sovereignty in the monistic sense should give way to a distributed structure of authority: modern society is functionally differentiated, so economic, cultural, educational, local and religious life cannot be governed by one centralised will. ✅ The state must **compete for allegiance** by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life; authority is functional and moral, not mystical.

**Two controls.** ✅ Laski is a **pluralist, not an anarchist**: the state retains coordinating and coercive capacities. ✅ His claim is **not administrative decentralisation** but a challenge to the idea that one final unlimited will exists at all. ⚠️ Note also the phase caution — early Laski called sovereignty a legal fiction; the mature Laski conceded its importance while denying omnipotence.

**Objection, reply, residual.** ✅ Objection: without a final sovereign, conflict between associations is insoluble. ⚠️ Reply: legal finality does not eliminate conflict but often suppresses legitimate diversity, and what is needed is a final **procedure**, not an unanswerable will. ⚠️ Residual: emergencies, defence and redistribution still require an integrating authority, and MacIver's residual coordinating role concedes it.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Laski's corrective against absolutism is indispensable; a polity that can never speak with one voice cannot discharge the coordinating task that made sovereignty necessary in the first place.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the stem says *why*, so the three grounds must be **developed** rather than listed, and the mark for critical control comes from conceding the coordination debt in the verdict.

**Exam-length execution — 20 marks, about 330 to 400 words:** thesis two lines; three grounds at four lines each; targets two; proposal four; controls three; objection–reply–residual three; verdict two.

- **Structure:** thesis → three grounds → targets → positive proposal → two controls → objection, reply, residual → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pluralism; distributed authority; earned allegiance.

#### Solved PYQ 10 · 2024 · Q4(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** What insights does the Arthasastra offer with regard to the concept of sovereignty? Does it have any relevance in the modern times? Critically discuss.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | Two-part stem ending in "Critically discuss" — insights, then a graded relevance judgement |
| Core issue | Whether an ancient science of statecraft can inform a modern constitutional order |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: four insights, then relevance argued through themes rather than transposition |
| Thinker thesis | It refuses the false choice between moralism and realism |
| Non-negotiables | All four insights, not just strategy; the anachronism control in the verdict |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The *Arthaśāstra*'s central insight is that sovereignty is **systemic, coercive, strategic and welfare-conditioned at once**, and its modern relevance lies in that refusal of the false choice between moralism and realism.

**Insight one — the state as organism.** ✅ Seven interdependent limbs (*saptāṅga*): ruler (*svāmī*), ministers (*amātya*), territory with its people (*janapada*), fortified defence (*durga*), treasury (*kośa*), coercive force (*daṇḍa*), ally (*mitra*). ⚠️ Sovereignty is therefore not merely personal; a ruler without treasury or ministers holds title without effective authority.

**Insight two — coercion as constitutive and calibrated.** ✅ *Daṇḍanīti* makes enforcement a permanent structural feature of rule while making its proportion the test of good rule.

**Insight three — external sovereignty as relational.** ✅ The circle of states (*maṇḍala*) and the six-fold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*) treat alignment as positional and policy as responsive to relative power and interest.

**Insight four — welfare as the ground of durability.** ✅ Security and welfare (*yogakṣema*) tie the ruler's welfare to the people's; treasury, territory and administration are conditions of stable rule, not merely instruments of extraction.

**Relevance — argued through themes.** ⚠️ Modern relevance lies in institutional capacity, security, diplomacy and public welfare taken together: sovereignty involves administration, intelligence, fiscal strength, external balancing and legitimacy through welfare. ⚠️ The transferable elements are principles of statecraft; the untransferable element is the monarchical form.

**Objection and reply.** ❓ Ancient categories cannot speak to modern constitutional states. ⚠️ Direct transposition is indeed impossible, but conceptual adaptation is fruitful, and relevance is shown through themes rather than by pretending ancient monarchy equals modern democracy.

**Critical verdict.** ⚠️ Genuinely relevant, but with two limits stated: prudential statecraft is not consent-based legitimacy, and welfare here is a condition of durable rule rather than a right held by subjects. ❓ The realism is elastic enough to license much, which is why the welfare and dharma constraints must be read as integral rather than decorative.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** most scripts give the circle of states and stop. Four insights, in the order organism → coercion → strategy → welfare, cover the doctrine and set up a relevance judgement that is argued rather than asserted.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** thesis two lines; four insights at three lines each; relevance four; objection–reply two; verdict three.

- **Structure:** thesis → four insights → thematic relevance → objection and reply → critical verdict.
- **Doctrine:** *saptāṅga*, *daṇḍanīti*, *maṇḍala*, *ṣāḍguṇya*, *yogakṣema*.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs and six measures by name; treasury as a condition of rule.
- **Distinction:** conceptual adaptation against direct transposition.
- **Criticism:** elastic realism and the absence of consent-based legitimacy, both conceded.

#### Solved PYQ 11 · 2025 · Q2(c) · 15 marks

**Question:** 'There is no permanent friend or permanent enemy.' Discuss this statement in the light of Kautilya's view on Sovereignty.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | Quoted-line comment — locate the line in its doctrine before judging it |
| Core issue | Whether the conditionality of alliance follows from Kautilya's model |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: provenance control first, then *maṇḍala* and *ṣāḍguṇya*, then the internal counterweight |
| Thinker thesis | Alignment is positional, so permanence is conceptually unavailable |
| Non-negotiables | The line is prompt wording, not verified authorship; strategy balanced by welfare |

##### Independent model answer

**Provenance first.** ⚠️ The printed line is the **examiner's framing**. Its exact wording has not been verified in the local *Arthaśāstra* synopsis or in the checked Shamasastry source passages, so it should not be presented as a Kautilyan quotation. What can be shown is that the verified doctrines make alliances conditional and policy responsive.

**Thesis.** ⚠️ On Kautilya's model, friendship and enmity are **positional rather than personal**, so the conditionality the line asserts follows from the structure of interstate relations and needs no quotation to establish it.

**The circle of states.** ✅ The *maṇḍala* framework locates friends and enemies **relationally** within a competitive and unstable states-system, in which position rather than sentiment fixes alignment.

**The six-fold policy.** ✅ *Ṣāḍguṇya* supplies the gears: **sandhi** (peace or treaty), **vigraha** (hostility or war), **yāna** (marching or expedition), **āsana** (remaining poised), **saṃśraya** (seeking shelter or alliance) and **dvaidhībhāva** (dual policy). ⚠️ Policy therefore changes with circumstance, relative power and interest — a thesis of **strategic flexibility**, not a verbatim maxim.

**Why permanence is unavailable.** ⚠️ If alignment is defined by relative position, then a state that gains or loses power changes position, and a change of position changes which measure is prudent. Conditionality falls out of the model rather than being asserted about it.

**The internal counterweight.** ✅ External flexibility is not amorality. Sovereignty is also bound internally by the seven limbs (*saptāṅga*), by calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*) and by the duty to security and welfare (*yogakṣema*). ⚠️ Kautilya is realistic but not normless.

**Objection and reply.** ✅ Objection: such realism is cynical. ⚠️ Reply: strategic flexibility operates alongside the ruler's internal duty toward order and welfare, and misrule weakens sovereignty itself.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The statement is a fair **interpretation** of Kautilya's external sovereignty and a poor summary of his theory as a whole: it captures the *maṇḍala* logic exactly and omits the welfare-bound, organismic account of internal authority that gives that logic its purpose.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** quoted-line stems are lost by treating the quotation as self-explanatory. Locate it in the doctrine, state what it does and does not assert, and only then judge it.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** provenance two lines; thesis two; *maṇḍala* two; six measures four; permanence three; counterweight three; objection–reply two; verdict two.

- **Structure:** provenance control → thesis → circle of states → six measures → why permanence is unavailable → internal counterweight → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** *maṇḍala*; *ṣāḍguṇya*; *saptāṅga*; *yogakṣema*.
- **Evidence:** the six measures by name; the relational definition of friend and enemy.
- **Distinction:** positional against personal alignment; interpretation against authorship.
- **Criticism:** the line's incompleteness as a summary of the whole theory, stated in the verdict.

### ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE WITH MODEL SOLUTIONS

> Six original questions in the style of the paper — two each at 10, 15 and 20 marks — covering the parts of the clause that the 2018–2025 corpus has not yet examined directly: the legal/political distinction, the de jure and de facto pairs, the classical attributes and their qualifications, the internal repairs of Austin by Kelsen and Hart, the pluralist lineage behind Laski, and the four-way structure of the whole dispute. Every model answer is independent learner practice and is never an official key.

#### Original 1 · 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish legal from political sovereignty, and explain why the distinction matters for evaluating Austin.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ The two answer different questions — who is **competent to make law**, and what power **shapes or compels** the law-maker — and the distinction matters because most objections to Austin are answers to the second offered against a thesis about the first.

**Legal sovereignty.** ✅ It names the authority recognised by law as competent to make law: the juristic source of validity, and Austin's preferred angle. His sovereign is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one.

**Political sovereignty.** ✅ It names the actual power behind legal institutions — the force that can compel or shape the legal sovereign. Parties, public opinion, organised interests, military power, religion and economic classes belong here, and this is the standpoint from which Laski attacks.

**Why they diverge.** ⚠️ A constitution may vest law-making power in one body while something else determines what that body can actually do; the divergence is a general feature of political life rather than a constitutional defect.

**Why it matters for Austin.** ⚠️ An unlabelled objection commits a category error. Showing that a legislature is politically constrained does not refute a claim about legal competence; showing that power-conferring rules cannot be redescribed as commands does.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Austin overstates the sufficiency of legal validity and pluralists overstate the sufficiency of political efficacy; a balanced answer keeps both questions open rather than declaring one of them the real one.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** two definitions earn little; two definitions plus the diagnosis of a category error earn the question.

- **Structure:** thesis → legal → political → divergence → consequence for Austin → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** juristic source of valid law against the real locus of social power.
- **Evidence:** the determinate human superior; the list of political forces.
- **Distinction:** validity against efficacy.
- **Criticism:** the symmetrical overstatement of both camps, named in the verdict.

#### Original 2 · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the five classical attributes of sovereignty, and show why each requires a qualification.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ The five attributes are defensible only in qualified form; stated flat, each is refuted by the first counter-example a critic supplies.

**Absoluteness.** ✅ No superior human law-maker legally commands the sovereign within the political order. ⚠️ Qualification: legal non-subordination does not prove moral infallibility or practical omnipotence.

**Permanence or continuity.** ✅ Governments and office-holders change while sovereignty continues with the state. ⚠️ Qualification: permanence means continuity while the state remains independent, not literal eternity.

**Universality.** ✅ Sovereign jurisdiction extends in principle across persons and associations within the territory. ⚠️ Qualification: immunities and autonomous spheres may exist by constitutional or legal recognition.

**Inalienability.** ✅ Sovereignty cannot be transferred away without the old state losing that status. ⚠️ Qualification: delegation, devolution or treaty commitment is not automatically alienation.

**Indivisibility.** ✅ The classical theory requires one final legal source. ⚠️ Qualification: governmental competences may be distributed even where monists insist legal finality remains one.

**Placement and verdict.** ✅ Bodin supplies the defining pair *absolute and perpetual*; Austin supplies the sharpest determinate-superior formulation; the five-part list is a **consolidation** of that monist tradition rather than five claims uniquely invented by either. ⚠️ Qualified in this way, the list survives federal division and treaty commitment; unqualified, it does not.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the qualification column is the answer. A candidate who writes only the five definitions has written a glossary.

- **Structure:** thesis → five attributes each with its qualification → placement → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** the classical monist taxonomy of legal sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** Bodin's defining pair; Austin's sharpened list.
- **Distinction:** legal non-subordination against moral infallibility; delegation against alienation.
- **Criticism:** the vulnerability of the unqualified list, stated as the reason for qualifying it.

#### Original 3 · 15 marks

**Question:** Examine the claim that Kelsen and Hart refute Austin more effectively than Laski does.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The claim is largely correct but must be graded: Kelsen and Hart defeat Austin's **definition**, whereas Laski defeats its **application** — and only the first is a refutation in the strict sense.

**Austin's position.** ✅ Law is the command of a determinate human superior backed by sanction; sovereignty is determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**Laski's external attack.** ✅ The state is one association among many; absolute sovereignty is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic. ⚠️ This shows that no actual society matches the Austinian picture, but it does not show that the picture is internally incoherent, and Austin can reply that he is analysing legal validity rather than describing social power.

**Kelsen's internal repair.** ✅ Validity derives from a higher norm, never from a fact, terminating in a presupposed basic norm (*Grundnorm*) — *General Theory of Law and State*, first English edition **1945**. ⚠️ This dissolves the personal sovereign from within positivism and answers the succession and persistence problems that habit cannot explain.

**Hart's internal repair.** ✅ Law is a union of primary and secondary rules, with a rule of recognition existing as convergent official practice viewed from the internal point of view — *The Concept of Law*, **1961**. ⚠️ Nullity is not a sanction, and being obliged is not having an obligation; both strikes attack the definition rather than its fit.

**Adjudication.** ⚠️ Refutation of a definition is stronger than refutation of a description, so the claim holds. ⚠️ But Laski's attack survives the repairs: neither Kelsen nor Hart addresses the danger of unlimited authority or the plurality of allegiance, so the three are complementary rather than competitive.

**Control.** ⚠️ They must not be merged: Kelsen replaces the sovereign with a norm-hierarchy, Hart with an official practice, Laski with plural associations.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Kelsen and Hart refute Austin **as a theory of law**; Laski refutes him **as a theory of political authority** — and a complete answer runs the internal critique first and the external critique second.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** "more effectively" demands a criterion of effectiveness. Supplying one — definition against application — converts an opinion into an argument.

- **Structure:** graded thesis → Austin → external attack → two internal repairs → adjudication → control → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** command theory; hierarchy of norms; primary and secondary rules; pluralism.
- **Evidence:** Kelsen 1945; Hart 1961; the nullity strike; the three grounds of rejection.
- **Distinction:** internal against external critique; definition against application.
- **Criticism:** the warning against merging three incompatible replacements.

#### Original 4 · 15 marks

**Question:** Assess the significance of the pluralist lineage — Figgis, Cole and MacIver — for Laski's rejection of monistic sovereignty.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The lineage strengthens Laski's rejection empirically and supplies the concession his own account most needs, but it must locate the school rather than replace the printed thinker.

**Laski's position.** ✅ The state is one association among many. Individuals belong to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; associations are not permissions granted by sovereignty.

**Figgis.** ✅ Churches and other real associations expose the danger of an absolute state allied with economic concentration. ⚠️ His significance is as the group-personality and anti-absorption precursor: he shows that associations have purposes of their own that the state did not create.

**Cole.** ✅ A forerunner of pluralism associated with functional or guild representation and socially organised group life. ⚠️ Significance: he converts plural loyalty into a proposal about representation. The boundary matters — the answer must not become a theory of guild socialism.

**MacIver.** ✅ Law and constitution, not a mysterious sovereign will, ground legitimate public force; the state **coordinates** associations without creating all their purposes. ⚠️ Significance: this is both the strongest sociological supplement to Laski and the honest statement of what pluralism still owes to monism.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Figgis supplies the moral urgency, Cole the institutional imagination and MacIver the residual coordination role. ⚠️ The last is decisive: because someone must settle conflicts **between** associations, MacIver's coordination claim measures exactly how much of the monist case survives.

**Control and verdict.** ✅ Laski is the printed thinker and the marks follow him; the three names locate the school. ⚠️ The lineage therefore makes the rejection of monism harder to dismiss as one writer's preference, while making explicit that pluralism limits sovereignty rather than abolishing the function it names.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the risk here is a biography parade. Give each name **one** supported contribution and **one** boundary, then assess the lineage as a whole.

- **Structure:** thesis → Laski's position → three names with contribution and boundary → assessment → control and verdict.
- **Doctrine:** associational autonomy; group personality; coordination without absolutism.
- **Evidence:** the association list; MacIver's law-and-constitution argument.
- **Distinction:** locating a school against replacing the printed thinker.
- **Criticism:** the residual coordination role, treated as the measure of what monism retains.

#### Original 5 · 20 marks

**Question:** "The concept of supreme legal authority survives every criticism examined; the image of a determinate personal sovereign does not." Critically examine this verdict with reference to Bodin, Austin, Laski and Kautilya.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The verdict is sound but requires two amendments: the surviving concept is **final procedure** rather than final will, and Kautilya shows that a duty-bound personal head can survive where an illimitable one cannot.

**What the image asserts.** ✅ Bodin's sovereign is the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth; Austin's is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk and obeying no one, whose authority is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**The internal criticisms.** ✅ Austin cannot explain succession, persistence or power-conferring rules. ⚠️ Kelsen (*General Theory of Law and State*, English edition **1945**) replaces the commander with a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm; Hart (*The Concept of Law*, **1961**) replaces him with a union of primary and secondary rules resting on a rule of recognition accepted from the internal point of view. ⚠️ Both remain positivists: the image dissolves, the concept of ultimate validity does not.

**The external criticisms.** ✅ Federalism distributes competence; international law binds without a world sovereign; custom precedes command; democracy makes authority derivative and answerable. ✅ Laski adds that absolutism is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic. ⚠️ Each attacks the personal image; none shows that a legal order can dispense with an ultimate criterion of validity.

**What Bodin retains.** ⚠️ Bodin survives better than Austin because his sovereign is already limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm, and because perpetuity locates authority in an **office**. His weakness is enforcement, not conception.

**What Kautilya adds.** ⚠️ The seven-limbed state makes sovereignty systemic; calibrated coercion makes force proportionate; security and welfare make legitimacy conditional on outcomes. ⚠️ A head bound by dharma, counsel, treasury and welfare is not the illimitable superior the criticisms destroy, which is why Kautilya is the least damaged of the four.

**The amendments.** ⚠️ First, what survives is best described as a **final procedure** for closure rather than a final will, since Hart's rule of recognition and a constitutional amending procedure both perform closure without personal supremacy. ⚠️ Second, the three dissolutions must not be merged: a norm-hierarchy, an official practice and a plurality of associations are rivals, not allies.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The quoted verdict holds. The image of a determinate personal sovereign fails on succession, on facilities, on federal competence, on international normativity and on democratic accountability; the concept of supreme legal authority survives, reconstituted as procedure, and Kautilya's duty-bound headship survives alongside it because it never claimed illimitability in the first place.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** examining a quoted verdict requires the candidate to **amend** rather than merely endorse. Two precise amendments are worth more than three paragraphs of agreement.

- **Structure:** graded thesis → the image → internal criticisms → external criticisms → what Bodin retains → what Kautilya adds → two amendments → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** the five attributes; hierarchy of norms; rule of recognition; pluralism; *saptāṅga* and *yogakṣema*.
- **Evidence:** Kelsen 1945; Hart 1961; the *leges imperii*; the seven limbs.
- **Distinction:** image against concept; final will against final procedure.
- **Criticism:** the warning against merging the three dissolutions, and the enforcement gap in Bodin.

#### Original 6 · 20 marks

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that the four printed thinkers on sovereignty are answering four different questions rather than giving four answers to one.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The claim is largely true and analytically productive, but it must be qualified: the four questions overlap at one point — every position must say what makes closure possible — and it is that overlap which makes comparison legitimate.

**The four questions.** ⚠️ Bodin and Austin ask **who** is sovereign. Kelsen and Hart deny that this is the right question and ask **what makes a norm valid**. Laski denies that any single answer is possible and asks **how allegiance is actually distributed**. Kautilya asks what the holder of authority is **for**.

**Bodin and Austin — the "who" question.** ✅ Bodin answers: the commonwealth's supreme law-giving authority, absolute and perpetual, limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm. ✅ Austin answers: a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society, whose authority is illimitable within the legal order.

**Kelsen and Hart — the validity question.** ⚠️ Because "is" does not yield "ought", validity cannot rest on the fact of obedience; Kelsen derives it from a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm (**1945**), and Hart from a rule of recognition existing as convergent official practice (**1961**).

**Laski — the allegiance question.** ✅ The state is one association among many and must compete for allegiance by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life; monistic sovereignty misdescribes society and licenses domination.

**Kautilya — the purpose question.** ✅ The ruler heads a seven-limbed state, governs through calibrated coercion, and is measured by security and welfare; authority is justified by what it delivers.

**Why the reframing is productive.** ⚠️ Read as four answers to one question, the positions look like a disagreement of degree about how much authority is too much. Read as four questions, each exposes a blind spot in the others: Austin cannot see facilities, Kelsen and Hart cannot see danger, Laski cannot see emergency closure, and Kautilya cannot see consent.

**The qualification.** ⚠️ The questions are not wholly independent. Every position must explain how a political community reaches closure, and each does: Bodin through an undivided title, Austin through a determinate superior, Kelsen through a presupposed apex, Hart through an accepted criterion, Laski through a final procedure, Kautilya through a duty-bound head. ⚠️ Comparison is therefore legitimate on that axis and illegitimate on axes the thinkers do not share.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The claim should be accepted with that amendment: four questions, one shared problem. The corresponding examination discipline is to **fix the axes** — source, location, divisibility, legal and moral limits, organs of state and modern relevance — and to refuse comparison by biography or by chronology.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** a "critically examine" stem asking about a methodological claim is won by amending the claim precisely, not by illustrating it at length.

- **Structure:** qualified thesis → the four questions → each answered → why the reframing helps → the shared-problem qualification → verdict with the axis discipline.
- **Doctrine:** all four printed positions plus the two internal repairs.
- **Evidence:** the *leges imperii*; the determinate superior; Kelsen 1945 and Hart 1961; the seven limbs.
- **Distinction:** four questions against four answers; shared closure problem against unshared axes.
- **Criticism:** the blind-spot audit, which prevents the reframing from becoming an excuse for not comparing.

- **The contending positions ⚠️.** One view locates ultimate authority in **the people who found the order** — the power that makes a constitution; the rival view locates it in **the legal institutions that operate within the order** — the powers a constitution makes.
- **Why it stays optional ✅.** The held corpus asks classical sovereignty and does not put this later constitutional-theory distinction directly in a stem.

- **The one place it earns marks ⚠️.** Where a stem asks whether shared authority is *coherent* rather than whether it is *desirable*, this framing separates the conceptual question from the normative one.

### A3. Sovereignty under interdependence ⚠️

- **The contending positions ⚠️.** "Globalisation weakens sovereignty" against the rival view that sovereignty **changes form** rather than disappearing.
- **Why it stays optional ✅.** It is useful for modern-relevance answers, but the classical thinker grid stays primary and Session 19 already supplies the four-mechanism analysis the 2020 part needs.

- **The contending positions ❓.** A Schmitt-style emphasis on **decision in crisis** against a constitutionalist insistence on **norm-governed emergency power**.
- **Why it stays optional ✅.** It is a high-value evaluative add-on but is not required by the corpus owned here.
- **The one place it earns marks ⚠️.** It gives the sharpest possible form of the coordination debt named in Session 15 — the pluralist owes an account of who decides when the ordinary procedures cannot run.

- **Why this is the best available upgrade ⚠️.** It converts the residual limit of Sessions 15 and 20 from a concession into an argument, and it is the single most reusable sentence in this block.

| **Hart** | Law is a **union of primary and secondary rules**, with a **rule of recognition** existing as convergent official practice viewed from the internal point of view ✅ |

**Statement bank — crisp openers.** ✅ Bodin transforms sovereignty into the permanent and supreme law-giving power of the commonwealth. ✅ Austin sharpens sovereignty into a legal relation of command, obedience and sanction. ✅ Laski dissolves monistic sovereignty into a plural social field of overlapping loyalties. ⚠️ Kautilya embeds sovereignty in an organismic state whose headship is inseparable from welfare and strategy. ⚠️ The central question is not whether authority exists, but whether it can be absolute, indivisible and morally unconditioned. ⚠️ Modern constitutionalism limits sovereignty without eliminating the need for final public authority. ⚠️ Globalisation challenges the isolation of sovereignty, not the existence of sovereign decision.

### C. The five classical attributes — never write them unqualified

| Attribute | Claim | Qualification that saves it |
|---|---|---|
| Absoluteness ✅ | no superior human law-maker within | not moral infallibility or practical omnipotence |
| Permanence ✅ | continues with the state, not the office-holder | continuity while independent, not eternity |
| Universality ✅ | reaches persons and associations in the territory | recognised immunities may exist |
| Inalienability ✅ | cannot be given away without ceasing to be sovereign | delegation and treaty commitment are not alienation |
| Indivisibility ✅ | one final legal source | competences may still be distributed |

✅ **Placement:** Bodin supplies *absolute and perpetual*; Austin supplies the determinate-superior sharpening; the five-part list is a **consolidation** of the monist tradition.

### D. Bodin in nine lines

1. Context: French wars of religion; rival churches, estates and feudal powers each claim finality. ✅
2. Definition: the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth. ✅
3. *Absolute* = no human superior within. ✅
4. *Perpetual* = attached to the **office**, not the magistrate — this is the state-building move. ✅
5. Pre-eminent mark: making and unmaking law for all subjects without a superior's consent. ✅
6. Indivisible: functions divisible, **title** not. ✅
7. Three limits: divine law, natural law, the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*). ✅
8. Formula: *legibus solutus* of **ordinary positive law only**. ✅
9. Verdict: historically foundational, normatively problematic; the limits are conceptually real and practically unenforced. ⚠️

### E. Austin built, then criticised

**Built.** ✅ Analytical jurisprudence → determinate human superior, habitual obedience by the bulk, obedience to no one → law as command backed by sanction → five attributes (determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable, inalienable), each closing a gap in the chain of validity. ⚠️ Indivisibility is a **logical** feature of the scheme, stricter than political reality.

**Criticised — group them, never list them.** ✅

| Group | Criticism | Austinian move | The cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural | Federalism distributes competence | relocate finality to the constitution-making authority | shifts rather than solves |
| Structural | International law binds with no world sovereign | deny that it is law in the strict sense | conceptual purity, lost reach |
| Structural | Custom precedes command | tacit adoption by the sovereign | judged artificial |
| Political | Democracy makes authority derivative | name a legislature as the superior | authority becomes revisable and answerable |
| Historical | Maine on Ranjit Singh | exceptional cases do not defeat a theory | the mismatch is wider than one case |
| Internal | Power-conferring rules; nullity is not a sanction | none available | forces reconstruction |

⚠️ **Democracy verdict:** yes in the narrow legal sense, no in the constitutional-political sense. ✅ **Jurisdiction control:** the United Kingdom centres Parliament; India has parliamentary government under **constitutional supremacy**, with Article 368 and *Kesavananda Bharati* (Supreme Court judgment, **1973**) limiting the amending power through the basic-structure doctrine. ⚠️ A judgment is not philosophical proof.

### F. The two internal repairs — Kelsen and Hart

| | **Kelsen** ✅ | **Hart** ✅ |
|---|---|---|
| Work and year | *General Theory of Law and State*, first English edition **1945** | *The Concept of Law*, **1961** |
| Diagnosis | validity cannot rest on a fact; "is" does not yield "ought" | the command model is the gunman situation writ large |
| Structure | hierarchy of norms: order → statute → constitution → presupposed apex | primary rules plus three secondary rules |
| Apex | **basic norm (*Grundnorm*)**, presupposed, not enacted | **rule of recognition**, a social fact in official practice |
| Key distinction | validity against efficacy | **being obliged** against **having an obligation** |
| Effect on sovereignty | sovereignty is an attribute of the **normative order** | ultimate authority is an **accepted practice** |
| Residual problem | efficacy re-enters through revolution | Dworkin's hard cases remain unresolved |

⚠️ **Hart's three defects and cures:** uncertainty → rule of recognition; static character → rules of change; inefficiency → rules of adjudication.
❌ **Never swap the dates.** Kelsen 1945, Hart 1961. If unsure, name the works in sequence without years.

### G. Laski and the supported pluralist lineage

- **Thesis ✅.** The state is one association among many — uniquely important, not the sole and morally unlimited source of authority. Associations are real sites of personality, cooperation and authority, not permissions granted by sovereignty.
- **Three grounds, three kinds ✅.** Philosophically false (no institution deserves unlimited moral obedience); politically dangerous (legitimises tyranny, suppresses liberty); sociologically unrealistic (power is in fact distributed).
- **Positive proposal ✅.** Authority divided and shared because society is functionally differentiated; the state must **compete for allegiance** by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life. Authority is functional and moral, not mystical.
- **Lineage ✅.** **Figgis** — real associations and the danger of an absolute state allied with economic concentration. **Cole** — functional or guild representation. **MacIver** — law and constitution, not a mysterious sovereign will, ground legitimate public force; the state **coordinates** without creating all purposes.
- **Two controls ✅.** Pluralist, **not** anarchist. A challenge to the concept of one unlimited will, **not** administrative decentralisation.
- **Phase caution ✅.** Early Laski: sovereignty is a legal fiction. Mature Laski: important, not omnipotent.
- **Verdict ⚠️.** Decisive as critique, incomplete as replacement; the **coordination debt** is unpaid.
