#### MCQ 1. What the concept of sovereignty actually asserts

A bandit can compel obedience and a government can issue commands. On the account developed in this package, what distinguishes the claim of sovereignty from both?

A. It asserts that the holder of power possesses greater coercive capacity than any rival inside the territory.

B. It asserts that the holder of power has acquired office through a procedure that the community regards as lawful.

C. It asserts a recognised or effective power of **final** decision, not merely an ability to compel compliance.

D. It asserts that the holder of power is morally entitled to the obedience of every person within the territory.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: superior force is neither necessary nor sufficient, since the canonical statement expressly separates sovereignty from mere force and locates it in recognised or effective finality.
- **B:** Incorrect: lawful acquisition is the *de jure* question of Session 4; a *de facto* sovereign obeyed without lawful title still raises the question of finality.
- **C:** Correct: the owner defines sovereignty as the supreme, final and authoritative power within a political community, and distinguishes it from the bandit's coercion precisely by finality.
- **D:** Incorrect: moral entitlement is what Laski denies to any human institution; the concept can be stated without it, and Austin deliberately excludes it.

**Examiner trap 1:** Opening a sovereignty answer with a definition of the *state* rather than of *sovereignty* wastes the first paragraph, because the state is the bearer of the claim while sovereignty is the claim itself.

#### MCQ 2. The classical attributes, stated so that they survive criticism

Which statement gives the five classical attributes of sovereignty in the qualified form the paper rewards?

A. Absolute of any human law-maker within, permanent in the state rather than the officer, universal subject to recognised immunity, inalienable though delegable, indivisible in legal source.

B. Absolute in every moral and practical respect, permanent for all future time, universal without exception, inalienable and undelegable, indivisible in both source and competence.

C. Absolute against foreign powers only, permanent while the ruling party lasts, universal over citizens alone, inalienable except by treaty, indivisible except in federations.

D. Absolute over ordinary citizens, permanent while obedience continues, universal within the capital, inalienable unless a court permits, indivisible unless the constitution says otherwise.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: each of the five is stated with the limiting clause the owner attaches — legal non-subordination, continuity of the state, recognised immunities, delegation without alienation, and one final legal source.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the unqualified version that every first-year counter-example refutes; the owner explicitly warns that legal non-subordination does not prove moral infallibility or practical omnipotence.
- **C:** Incorrect: absoluteness is an *internal* claim about the absence of a superior human law-maker, and permanence attaches to the state rather than to any government or party.
- **D:** Incorrect: universality extends across persons and associations within the territory rather than a capital, and inalienability is not a matter of judicial permission.

**Examiner trap 2:** Reciting the five attributes without their qualifications converts a defensible doctrine into a refutable slogan, and omitting permanence and universality — the two most frequently dropped — leaves the classical set incomplete.

#### MCQ 3. Legal against political sovereignty

Why does the owner insist that most objections to monistic sovereignty must be labelled before they are deployed?

A. Because objections drawn from history are weaker than objections drawn from logic, and the examiner grades the two differently.

B. Because Austin himself accepted political sovereignty and only later writers separated it from the legal question he was answering.

C. Because political sovereignty is a normative claim while legal sovereignty is descriptive, so the two cannot be compared at all.

D. Because most such objections answer the question of real social power, while the thesis attacked is a claim about competence to make law.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the distinction is not between historical and logical objections; both kinds can be aimed at either the legal or the political question.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner places Austin firmly on the legal side and treats political sovereignty as the angle from which Laski attacks, not as something Austin conceded.
- **C:** Incorrect: legal sovereignty identifies the juristic source of valid law and political sovereignty the real locus of social power; both are descriptive, and both are comparable.
- **D:** Correct: legal sovereignty names competence to make law and political sovereignty names the power that shapes the law-maker, so an unlabelled objection commits a category error.

**Examiner trap 3:** Treating "political sovereignty" as a synonym for popular sovereignty confuses a sociological description of where power lies with a normative claim about where authority originates.

#### MCQ 4. Lawful title, actual obedience, and the divergence it exposes

Which statement correctly identifies where Bodin and Austin anchor the durability of sovereign authority?

A. Both anchor it in habitual obedience, since neither can explain continuity without appealing to the settled compliance of subjects.

B. Bodin anchors it in the perpetual office rather than the magistrate, whereas Austin's habitual-obedience criterion gives his theory a de facto edge.

C. Bodin anchors it in divine sanction of the monarch, whereas Austin anchors it in the constitutional text that confers legislative competence.

D. Both anchor it in the office, since Austin's determinate superior is defined as an institution rather than as any identifiable person or body.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Bodin's second defining adjective is *perpetual*, which the owner glosses as authority attaching to office rather than to a temporary magistrate, so obedience is not his anchor.
- **B:** Correct: the owner states exactly this contrast — Austin's emphasis on habitual obedience has a de facto edge, while Bodin is concerned with the permanent office-character of authority.
- **C:** Incorrect: divine law is a *limit* on Bodin's sovereign rather than the ground of durability, and Austin's sovereign is not defined by a constitutional text.
- **D:** Incorrect: Austin's sovereign is expressly a determinate person or body, which is why the succession problem bites him and not Bodin.

**Examiner trap 4:** Writing that Bodin and Austin agree simply because both are monists throws away a cheap, high-value differentiation that comparison stems reward directly.

#### MCQ 5. Popular sovereignty, national sovereignty and the general will

Which statement handles the two forms and the Rousseau bridge with the precision the owner requires?

A. Popular sovereignty means majority rule, national sovereignty means independence, and Rousseau's general will is the numerical sum of individual preferences.

B. Popular sovereignty and national sovereignty are interchangeable expressions, and Rousseau uses the general will to justify whatever the assembled citizens decide.

C. Popular sovereignty means the government represents the nation, national sovereignty means the nation obeys the government, and Rousseau merges the two.

D. Popular sovereignty locates the source of public authority in the people, national sovereignty concerns collective self-determination, and the general will names the common good.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner explicitly defines the general will as a claim about the common good rather than the numerical sum of private preferences, and popular sovereignty is not identical with majority rule.
- **B:** Incorrect: the two forms answer different questions — from whom authority arises, and which people may govern itself — and the owner lists their conflation as a standing trap.
- **C:** Incorrect: this inverts the doctrine, since popular sovereignty makes government the agent of the people rather than their representative superior.
- **D:** Correct: this reproduces the owner's controlling distinction and its gloss on the general will, and it preserves the warning that no majority decision is thereby morally sovereign.

**Examiner trap 5:** Collapsing the general will into "whatever the majority votes for" destroys the argument against Austin, because the force of the popular-sovereignty objection lies in the people's indeterminacy, not in their counting power.

#### MCQ 6. Bodin's definition and its pre-eminent mark

What, on Bodin's account, is the pre-eminent mark by which sovereignty is identified?

A. The power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without needing the consent of any superior.

B. The power to command the armed forces of the commonwealth and to declare war on any neighbouring realm.

C. The power to appoint magistrates and to remove them at pleasure without stating a reason to the estates.

D. The power to interpret divine and natural law authoritatively for every subject of the commonwealth.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner identifies the sovereign's pre-eminent mark as the power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without needing consent from a superior.
- **B:** Incorrect: military command is an ordinary attribute of rule and does not distinguish the sovereign from a powerful magistrate or general.
- **C:** Incorrect: appointing and removing magistrates is precisely the sort of delegated function Bodin allows without conceding any division of sovereign title.
- **D:** Incorrect: divine and natural law constrain Bodin's sovereign rather than falling within his interpretive gift; making him their authoritative interpreter would erase the limits.

**Examiner trap 6:** Explaining "perpetual" as "eternal" misses its actual work, which is to detach authority from the office-holder and fix it in the office so that a personal ascendancy becomes a state.

#### MCQ 7. What limits Bodin's sovereign

Which statement gives Bodin's position on limits without either exaggerating or erasing them?

A. The sovereign is bound by ordinary positive law and by the estates, but not by divine law, which belongs to the church alone.

B. The sovereign is bound by nothing whatever, since the power to make law entails the power to unmake every constraint upon it.

C. The sovereign is freed from ordinary positive law yet bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm.

D. The sovereign is bound only by the fundamental laws of the realm, divine and natural law having no place in his account.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: this reverses the structure, since being *legibus solutus* means freedom from ordinary positive law while divine law remains a genuine normative limit.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the Hobbesian caricature the owner warns against; Bodin's absolutism is juristic rather than caprice, and he retains higher-law constraints.
- **C:** Correct: the owner lists exactly three limits — divine law, natural law and the *leges imperii* — and glosses *legibus solutus* as freedom from ordinary positive law only.
- **D:** Incorrect: the fundamental laws are one of three limits, and dropping the other two removes the moral content that distinguishes Bodin from pure absolutism.

**Examiner trap 7:** Making Bodin lawless removes the very feature that makes him answerable to the 2022 compatibility question, since the interesting tension is between an authority that is supreme in positive law and one that is nonetheless normatively bound.

#### MCQ 8. Bodin's indivisibility thesis

What exactly does Bodin's indivisibility claim forbid, and what does it permit?

A. It forbids the delegation of any executive function and permits only advisory bodies with no power to issue binding instructions.

B. It forbids the division of sovereign title and permits the distribution of governmental functions among estates, courts and magistrates.

C. It forbids federal arrangements of every kind and permits unitary states to establish provincial councils with limited competence.

D. It forbids consultation with subordinate authorities and permits the sovereign to legislate only through a single unified assembly.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Bodin allows magistrates to issue commands; what he denies them is the final legislative power, so the prohibition is not on delegated function.
- **B:** Correct: the owner states that division of governmental functions is possible while division of sovereign title is not, citing advisory estates and subordinate courts as permitted.
- **C:** Incorrect: federal systems are a later critical counter-example to the thesis rather than the content of the thesis itself.
- **D:** Incorrect: consultation is compatible with indivisibility, and Bodin's argument concerns the location of finality rather than the procedure of legislating.

**Examiner trap 8:** Answering the constitutional-division objection by simply repeating that sovereignty is indivisible loses the mark; the reply must distinguish administrative distribution from the sharing of final law-making power, and then concede that critics regard the distinction as strained.

#### MCQ 9. Austin's definition of the sovereign

Which formulation states Austin's definition with the precision the paper rewards?

A. A determinate human superior, receiving habitual obedience from the bulk of a society, and habitually obeying no other like superior.

B. The state considered in the abstract, exercising the totality of public powers, and answerable to the electorate at regular intervals.

C. Whichever institution possesses the constitutional competence to amend the fundamental law of the political community it governs.

D. The person or body that commands the greatest coercive force and can therefore secure compliance from the population it rules.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: this reproduces all three linked elements — determinacy, habitual obedience by the bulk, and obedience to no one — which together close the chain of legal validity.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner's first standing trap is exactly this, since Austin's sovereign is a determinate person or body rather than the state in the abstract.
- **C:** Incorrect: locating sovereignty in an amending competence is the *relocation* Austinians attempt under pressure from federalism, not Austin's own definition.
- **D:** Incorrect: this reduces the definition to force and abandons the distinction from mere coercion that the concept requires from the outset.

**Examiner trap 9:** Dropping the third element — that the sovereign habitually obeys no one — makes the international-law criticism unanswerable, because the criticism turns precisely on the absence of any determinate global superior.

#### MCQ 10. Command, sanction and the law of facilities

Which criticism of the command theory does the owner treat as the most damaging to Austin's definition itself?

A. That penal law is only a small part of any developed legal system, so the paradigm case is statistically unrepresentative.

B. That international law binds states through treaty and custom despite the absence of any determinate global superior.

C. That federal constitutions distribute competences so that no single level of government is superior in all matters.

D. That much of law confers facilities rather than issuing orders, so that a defective will is a nullity and not a sanction.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: relative frequency is not the objection; the difficulty is conceptual, since power-conferring rules cannot be redescribed as commands at all.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is a strong external criticism of the theory's scope, but Austin can answer it by denying that international law is law in the strict sense.
- **C:** Incorrect: federalism attacks indivisibility empirically, and Austin replies by relocating finality to the constitution-making authority.
- **D:** Correct: the owner treats power-conferring rules as an internal defect Austin cannot solve, since nullity is the absence of legal effect rather than an evil attached to disobedience.

**Examiner trap 10:** Describing the nullity of a defective legal act as a "sanction" concedes Hart's point while appearing to resist it, because a sanction is an evil imposed for disobedience whereas nullity is simply the failure of an attempted legal act.

#### MCQ 11. Why the five Austinian attributes are forced

On the reading developed here, why does Austin's definition require determinacy, absoluteness, indivisibility, illimitability and inalienability?

A. Because each attribute was independently observed in the centralised European legal orders that Austin had studied in detail.

B. Because each closes a possible gap through which the chain of legal validity would fail to terminate in a final source.

C. Because each was inherited directly from Bodin, whose definition Austin restated in more precise analytical language.

D. Because each is required by the moral claim that a legal system must make on the obedience of the subjects it governs.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner concedes that Austinian indivisibility is stricter than empirical political reality and is a logical feature of the scheme, not an observation.
- **B:** Correct: indeterminacy would leave the origin unlocatable, divisibility would create rival origins, limitation would push validity to a further source, and alienability would make finality transferable.
- **C:** Incorrect: Bodin supplies absolute and perpetual and retains higher-law limits, so Austin's illimitable legal sovereign is a radicalisation rather than a restatement.
- **D:** Incorrect: Austin's project separates what law is from what law ought to be, so no moral claim enters the definition of the attributes.

**Examiner trap 11:** Listing the five attributes without stating why the list is forced scores as vocabulary rather than argument, and it leaves the candidate unable to explain why empirical counter-examples do not by themselves refute the scheme.

#### MCQ 12. The pattern shared by the three structural criticisms

Federalism, international law and customary law are usually presented as three separate objections to Austin. What single pattern do they share?

A. Each shows that Austin underestimated the practical importance of moral limits on the exercise of legal authority.

B. Each shows that Austin's theory applies well to monarchies but poorly to any political order with an elected legislature.

C. Each locates law where no determinate superior stands, and each Austinian reply relocates the sovereign at a cost to determinacy.

D. Each depends on historical examples drawn from periods after Austin wrote, so none of them can fairly be pressed against him.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: moral limits form a separate criticism in the owner's table, and the three structural objections turn on the absence of a determinate superior rather than on morality.
- **B:** Incorrect: federalism and custom arise in monarchies too, and the democracy objection is treated separately in the owner's scheme.
- **C:** Correct: the constitution-making authority, the denial that international law is law and the device of tacit adoption are three relocations, each weakening determinacy.
- **D:** Incorrect: customary law and federal arrangements long predate Austin, and Maine's counter-example is drawn from a contemporaneous political order.

**Examiner trap 12:** Presenting Austin's denial that international law is truly law as an oversight misreads him, since it is his explicit and argued position; the criticism must attack the definition's narrowness, not his attention.

#### MCQ 13. Austin and democracy

Which answer structure does the owner require for the compatibility question?

A. A single negative verdict, since democracy disperses authority and therefore cannot contain a legally supreme law-making body at all.

B. A single affirmative verdict, since every democracy contains a legislature that can be identified as the determinate human superior.

C. A historical verdict, since Austin wrote before mass democracy and cannot be assessed against institutions he never observed.

D. A two-level verdict: compatible in the narrow legal sense of naming a law-maker, incompatible in the constitutional-political sense.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner concedes that in a narrow legal sense one may identify a law-making body, so a flat negative overstates the case.
- **B:** Incorrect: this ignores that democracy makes final authority derivative, revisable and answerable, which is what Austin's illimitability forbids.
- **C:** Incorrect: the papers ask the question directly and the owner answers it doctrinally, so a refusal to judge does not meet the demand.
- **D:** Correct: the owner's balanced structure is yes in the narrow legal sense and no in the deeper constitutional-political sense, with the reason for the split stated.

**Examiner trap 13:** Answering the 2021 stem with a flat yes or no forfeits the structural mark, because the demand is a graded judgement in which the level at which each verdict holds must be named.

#### MCQ 14. Parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy

Which statement handles the jurisdiction control correctly?

A. In the United Kingdom Parliament is the supreme legal authority, while in India Parliament exercises competences conferred by a supreme written Constitution.

B. In the United Kingdom the courts may strike down primary legislation, while in India Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution at will.

C. Both the United Kingdom and India practise parliamentary sovereignty, because both have parliamentary government and a legislature that makes law.

D. In India the judiciary is sovereign because judicial review permits it to invalidate legislation enacted by the elected legislature.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner's table states precisely this contrast, with courts generally unable to overrule UK primary legislation and Indian competences derived from the Constitution.
- **B:** Incorrect: this inverts both halves, since UK courts generally cannot overrule primary legislation and the Indian amending power is limited by the basic-structure doctrine.
- **C:** Incorrect: parliamentary sovereignty is jurisdiction-specific and is not a synonym for every parliamentary form of government.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner expressly cautions that India's judiciary is not sovereign merely because it reviews legislation.

**Examiner trap 14:** Using *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) as proof that a philosophical theory is correct misclassifies it; it is a dated judicial decision illustrating a legally limited amending competence, not evidence for or against Austin, Kelsen or Hart.

#### MCQ 15. Kelsen's hierarchy of norms

Which statement gives Kelsen's central argument and its effect on sovereignty?

A. Validity derives from the sovereign's command, and the basic norm records the moral principles that a legal order must satisfy.

B. Validity derives from a higher norm, terminating in a presupposed basic norm, so the sovereign becomes the order's highest competence.

C. Validity derives from the convergent practice of officials, and the basic norm is the social fact of their acceptance of that practice.

D. Validity derives from the effectiveness of the legal order, and the basic norm is an empirical generalisation about observed obedience.

**Answer: B.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Kelsen's founding premise is that "is" does not yield "ought", so no fact of command generates validity, and the basic norm is expressly morally inert.
- **B:** Correct: the owner sets out the pyramid from administrative order to statute to constitution to presupposed *Grundnorm*, with sovereignty becoming an attribute of the normative order.
- **C:** Incorrect: convergent official practice is Hart's rule of recognition, which Hart offers precisely against Kelsen's presupposed norm.
- **D:** Incorrect: efficacy is a *condition* of validity in Kelsen's treatment of revolution, never its source, and the basic norm is presupposed rather than generalised.

**Examiner trap 15:** Filing Kelsen alongside Laski as an external critic destroys the two-stage structure that twenty-mark stems reward, since Kelsen repairs Austin from inside legal positivism while Laski attacks from outside it.

#### MCQ 16. Kelsen, revolution and the residual problem

A successful revolution replaces a constitution without any authorising norm. What is Kelsen's reply, and what problem remains?

A. He denies that revolutions occur within legal science, and the residual problem is that his theory cannot describe political change at all.

B. He treats the new constitution as valid because the revolutionaries obtain habitual obedience, and the residual problem is circularity with Austin.

C. He argues that the old basic norm survives all changes of regime, and the residual problem is that valid law would then never change.

D. He holds that jurists presuppose a new basic norm once the order is by and large effective, and efficacy thereby re-enters the theory.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: Kelsen offers a positive account of revolutionary change rather than excluding it, and the owner records that account explicitly.
- **B:** Incorrect: habitual obedience is Austin's criterion; Kelsen's condition is the by-and-large effectiveness of the order, and he does not adopt Austin's formulation.
- **C:** Incorrect: the survival of the old basic norm would make the new order invalid, which is the opposite of Kelsen's position.
- **D:** Correct: the owner states that a new basic norm is presupposed when the order becomes by and large effective, and concedes that this readmits the fact of power.

**Examiner trap 16:** Defending the basic norm as though it were a demonstrated truth misstates Kelsen's own claim, which is that it is a transcendental-logical condition of treating anything as valid law — a postulate, not a fact.

#### MCQ 17. Hart's three defects and three secondary rules

Which pairing of defect and cure is correct on Hart's account?

A. Uncertainty is cured by rules of change; the static character is cured by rules of adjudication; inefficiency is cured by the rule of recognition.

B. Uncertainty is cured by rules of adjudication; the static character is cured by the rule of recognition; inefficiency is cured by rules of change.

C. Uncertainty is cured by the rule of recognition; the static character is cured by rules of change; inefficiency is cured by rules of adjudication.

D. Uncertainty is cured by primary rules; the static character is cured by sanctions; inefficiency is cured by the sovereign's habitual command.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: rules of change authorise creation, amendment and repeal, which cures stasis rather than uncertainty about which rules count as law.
- **B:** Incorrect: rules of adjudication confer authority to determine breach and remedy, which cures inefficiency rather than uncertainty.
- **C:** Correct: the owner's table pairs recognition with uncertainty, change with the static character of custom, and adjudication with the inefficiency of diffuse enforcement.
- **D:** Incorrect: primary rules are the regime whose defects are being cured, and sanctions and command belong to the Austinian model Hart rejects.

**Examiner trap 17:** Treating Hart's rule theory as a restatement of Austin misses the rejection at its core, since Hart denies that nullity is a sanction, that habit explains succession, and that being obliged is having an obligation.

#### MCQ 18. Being obliged and having an obligation

Why does Hart regard this distinction as fatal to Austin's account of legal obligation?

A. Because habitual obedience records only external regularity and so cannot separate a legal duty from submission to a gunman's threat.

B. Because it shows that sanctions are morally objectionable, so any theory built upon them cannot justify the state's coercive power.

C. Because it proves that most citizens obey the law from fear, so habitual obedience is an inaccurate description of their conduct.

D. Because it establishes that only judges, and not ordinary subjects, can be said to have obligations under a developed legal system.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the internal point of view treats a rule as a reason for conduct and a ground for criticising deviation, which external regularity cannot capture.
- **B:** Incorrect: Hart's objection is analytical rather than moral, and he insists that legal validity be explained without moral evaluation.
- **C:** Incorrect: the distinction concerns what obligation *is*, not the empirical motives from which people in fact comply.
- **D:** Incorrect: the internal point of view is available to participants generally, and the rule of recognition is described through officials without confining obligation to them.

**Examiner trap 18:** Developing Dworkin's principles-and-hard-cases objection inside a sovereignty answer breaches ownership; name it as the next objection to Hart and route it, because the doctrine belongs to Individual and State.

#### MCQ 19. Laski's central thesis

Which statement gives Laski's position without overstating or understating it?

A. The state should be abolished because every association is equally competent to govern the affairs of its own members.

B. The state should be preserved unchanged because plural associations derive whatever authority they possess from its permission.

C. The state is one association among many, uniquely important yet not the sole and morally unlimited source of all authority.

D. The state is one association among many, exactly equal in function and authority to churches, unions and professional bodies.

**Answer: C.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: the owner records that Laski is a pluralist and not an anarchist, and that he does not deny the need for the state.
- **B:** Incorrect: this is the monist position Laski attacks, since he refuses to treat associations as permissions granted by sovereignty.
- **C:** Correct: this reproduces the owner's formulation, including the concession that the state retains coordinating and coercive capacities.
- **D:** Incorrect: the owner expressly warns that "one association among many" does not mean all associations are equal in function.

**Examiner trap 19:** Quoting the early Laski's attack on sovereignty as a legal fiction as though it were his settled view ignores the phase caution: his mature position conceded sovereignty's importance while denying omnipotence.

#### MCQ 20. Why Laski rejects absolute sovereignty

His rejection rests on three grounds. What is the structural feature that makes the argument strong?

A. The three grounds are three restatements of a single moral premise, which gives the argument unusual rhetorical force.

B. The three grounds are of three different kinds — moral, political and empirical — so answering one leaves the others standing.

**Answer: B.**

- **A:** Incorrect: philosophical falsity, political danger and sociological unrealism are distinct claims rather than restatements of one premise.
- **B:** Correct: their logical independence is what forces the monist to answer each separately, and conceding the empirical ground already abandons indivisibility.
- **C:** Incorrect: the owner presents the three as independent rather than as a ranked series culminating in a decisive ground.
- **D:** Incorrect: Figgis, Cole and MacIver locate the school and supply support, but the three grounds are Laski's own and are not divided among them.

#### MCQ 21. Kautilya's seven-limbed state

What is the philosophical point of the *saptāṅga* doctrine?

A. Sovereignty is systemic rather than merely personal, since effective rule depends on ministers, territory, defence, treasury, force and allies.

B. Sovereignty is divided among seven office-holders, each of whom exercises final authority within a separately defined sphere of competence.

C. Sovereignty is administrative rather than political, since the list enumerates departments of government rather than sources of authority.

D. Sovereignty is contractual, since the seven limbs represent the parties whose agreement establishes and sustains the authority of the ruler.

**Answer: A.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Correct: the owner presents the state as an organism in which the ruler is head but cannot function without the other limbs, making sovereignty systemic.
- **B:** Incorrect: royal primacy is preserved; the limbs are constituents of state capacity rather than seven co-ordinate holders of final authority.
- **C:** Incorrect: the doctrine is an argument about what makes rule possible, and the owner contrasts it with Austin's search for the legal source of command.
- **D:** Incorrect: nothing in the *saptāṅga* scheme is contractual, and reading consent into it imports a modern framework the owner warns against.

#### MCQ 22. Calibrated coercion

What does *daṇḍanīti* assert about the relation between force and sovereignty?

A. That coercion is an emergency power, to be reserved for occasions when ordinary administration and counsel have already failed.

B. That coercion should be maximised, since the ruler's security depends on the certainty that disobedience will be severely punished.

C. That coercion should be minimised, since the ruler's legitimacy depends chiefly on welfare provision rather than on enforcement.

D. That coercion is constitutive of rule and must be calibrated, since its absence brings disorder and its excess breeds resentment.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**
- **A:** Incorrect: *daṇḍa* is one of the seven constituents of the state, which makes coercive capacity a permanent structural feature rather than an exceptional one.
- **B:** Incorrect: the owner states that excessive *daṇḍa* breeds resentment and instability, so maximisation is self-defeating on Kautilya's own terms.
- **C:** Incorrect: the owner is explicit that without *daṇḍa* society falls into disorder, so minimisation is equally rejected.
- **D:** Correct: the art of sovereignty lies in calibrated punishment, and the *nīti* half of the term names the policy governing the use of force.

**Examiner trap 22:** Reading modern proportionality doctrine into *daṇḍanīti* manufactures a theory of rights; the restraint is prudential and stability-based, resting on the ruler's interest rather than on enforceable claims held by subjects.

#### MCQ 24. Sovereignty under globalisation

#### Solved PYQ 1 · 2018 · Q1(d) · 10 marks

**Question:** Discuss Kautilya's concept of sovereignty in the light of 'Danda-neety'.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Discuss" — exposition plus one adjudicated tension, not a description of punishment |
| Core issue | What calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*) reveals about the nature of Kautilyan sovereignty |
| Scope boundary | Ten marks: lead with calibration; use the seven limbs and welfare as support only |
| Thinker thesis | Sovereignty is the capacity to maintain order through **disciplined** force, not arbitrary cruelty |
| Non-negotiables | *Daṇḍa* as one of the seven limbs; the two-sided failure of too little and too much; welfare as the complement |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ For Kautilya, sovereignty is measured by the ruler's capacity to calibrate force, not by his freedom to use it; *daṇḍanīti* is therefore a doctrine of proportion rather than of severity.

**Where coercion sits.** ✅ Coercive force (*daṇḍa*) is one of the seven constituents of the state (*saptāṅga*), alongside the ruler (*svāmī*), ministers (*amātya*), territory with its people (*janapada*), fortified defence (*durga*), treasury (*kośa*) and ally (*mitra*). ⚠️ Placing it among the constituents makes the capacity to punish a permanent structural feature of rule rather than an emergency power.

**The calibration argument.** ✅ Without *daṇḍa*, society falls into disorder; with excessive *daṇḍa*, the ruler breeds resentment and instability. ⚠️ The art of sovereignty therefore lies in ordered, proportionate enforcement — *daṇḍa* is the force and *nīti* the policy that governs its use.

**What this shows about sovereignty.** ⚠️ The ruler is head of an organism rather than an unlimited master: authority is real, but its legitimacy and durability are judged by order and by security and welfare (*yogakṣema*). ✅ Fear without welfare is self-defeating; welfare without order is impossible.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Kautilyan sovereignty is strong precisely because it is duty-bound. ❓ The restraint is nonetheless prudential rather than rights-based: it binds the ruler's interest, not his entitlement, and an accurate answer concedes that difference rather than reading modern constitutionalism into the text.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the commonest failure here is a paragraph on ancient punishment. The stem says *in the light of daṇḍanīti*, which means the doctrine of calibrated coercion must be the lens through which sovereignty is characterised.

**Exam-length execution — 10 marks, about 150 to 220 words:** thesis two lines; placement three; calibration three; welfare link three; verdict two.

- **Structure:** thesis → *daṇḍa* as a constituent → two-sided failure → welfare complement → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** *daṇḍanīti* as the science of punishment and disciplined enforcement.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs by name; disorder without force and resentment beyond it.
- **Distinction:** ordered coercion against blind terror.
- **Criticism:** prudential restraint is not a rights-based limit, conceded in the verdict.

#### Solved PYQ 2 · 2018 · Q2(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** Critically evaluate Laski's view on sovereignty as a satisfactory position in political theory.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Critically evaluate … as a satisfactory position" — the criterion of satisfactoriness must be named first |
| Core issue | Whether pluralism succeeds as a **positive** theory, not merely as a critique |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: exposition, two objections with replies, one residual problem, conditional verdict |
| Thinker thesis | The state is one association among many; sovereignty cannot be absolute |
| Non-negotiables | Three grounds of three kinds; the phase caution; the coordination debt |

##### Independent model answer

**Naming the test.** ⚠️ A position is satisfactory in political theory if it describes political life accurately, protects the values it claims to protect, and supplies an account of the function it displaces. Laski passes the first two tests decisively and the third only partly.

**The position.** ✅ Laski rejects the monistic doctrine that the state possesses absolute, indivisible and unlimited sovereignty. Individuals belong simultaneously to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; the state is therefore **one association among many**, uniquely important but not the sole source of obligation. ✅ Allegiance must be earned by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life, not claimed by legal title.

**Why absolutism is rejected — three grounds of three kinds.** ✅ Philosophically, no human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience; politically, absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty; sociologically, real societies distribute power among many groups. ⚠️ Their independence is the argument's strength: answering one leaves the others standing.

**First objection and reply.** ✅ Without a final sovereign, conflict between associations becomes insoluble. ⚠️ Laski replies that legal finality does not eliminate conflict but often suppresses legitimate diversity, and that modern orders need a final **procedure**, not an unanswerable will.

**Second objection and reply.** ✅ Shared sovereignty is conceptually incoherent. ⚠️ His real claim is that the monistic concept itself misdescribes overlapping authority.

**Residual problem.** ⚠️ Neither reply supplies a decision procedure for emergencies, defence or redistribution; MacIver's residual coordinating role concedes exactly this.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Satisfactory as a critique of absolutism and as a defence of liberty through social plurality; **not** satisfactory as a complete replacement for the concept of final authority. ✅ Note also the phase caution: "Laski abolishes sovereignty" is as inaccurate as "Laski accepts Austin".

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** "satisfactory" is an evaluative word with no fixed content, so the criterion must be supplied by the candidate in the first two lines. Scripts that omit it produce a verdict with nothing behind it.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** test two lines; position four; three grounds three; two objection–reply pairs four; residual two; verdict three.

- **Structure:** name the test → position → three grounds → two objections with replies → residual → conditional verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pluralism; earned allegiance; the state as one association among many.
- **Evidence:** the association list; the three grounds; MacIver's coordination role.
- **Distinction:** pluralist against anarchist; conceptual challenge against administrative decentralisation.
- **Criticism:** the coordination debt, stated as an unpaid debt rather than argued away.

#### Solved PYQ 3 · 2019 · Q3(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Discuss how far does Austin's concept of sovereignty go along with Kautilya's concept of sovereignty.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "How far … go along with" — a **degree** judgement on fixed comparison axes |
| Core issue | Whether two theories answering different questions can be said to agree at all |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: five axes run in parallel, then an adjudication; not two mini-essays |
| Thinker thesis | Austin analyses the **form** of authority; Kautilya prescribes its **function** |
| Non-negotiables | Determinate human superior; *saptāṅga*, *daṇḍanīti*, *yogakṣema*; the asymmetry verdict |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The two converge on a single point — that a political order requires an identifiable apex of direction — and diverge on everything that follows, because they are answering different questions: Austin asks what makes a command legally valid, Kautilya asks what makes rule possible.

**Axis one — nature of the theory.** ✅ Austin's is analytical jurisprudence, separating what law **is** from what it ought to be. Kautilya's is statecraft: administration, treasury, coercion, alliance, intelligence and welfare.

**Axis two — locus of sovereignty.** ✅ Austin locates it in a **determinate human superior**, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one. Kautilya locates it in the ruler (*svāmī*) as head of a seven-limbed state (*saptāṅga*): ministers, territory with its people, fortified defence, treasury, coercive force and ally. ⚠️ Austin's sovereign is a person or body; Kautilya's is a **system with a head**.

**Axis three — limits.** ✅ Austin's sovereign is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable, with moral limits placed outside the definition. Kautilya's ruler is bound by dharma, prudence, counsel and the duty to security and welfare (*yogakṣema*); a ruler without treasury or ministers holds title without effective sovereignty.

**Axis four — law and order.** ✅ Austin: law is command backed by sanction. Kautilya: order is maintained through calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*), counsel, treasury and administration — too little force brings disorder, too much brings resentment.

**Axis five — the external dimension.** ✅ Austin's is underdeveloped, extending only to the denial that international law is truly law. Kautilya's is highly developed through the circle of states (*maṇḍala*) and the six-fold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*).

**Adjudication.** ⚠️ They "go along" only at the thinnest level of shared structure — an apex of direction — and part company on source, limits, purpose and external relations. ⚠️ The asymmetry is the answer: **Austin analyses the form of authority; Kautilya prescribes its function**, so Austin's sovereign is legally supreme because no superior commands it, while Kautilya's ruler is politically central but functionally bound by dharma, welfare, ministers, treasury, force and allies.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** comparison stems are lost by writing two sequential summaries. Fix the axes in advance and run both thinkers down each one; the adjudication then writes itself.

**Exam-length execution — 20 marks, about 330 to 400 words:** thesis three lines; five axes at four lines each; adjudication four.

- **Structure:** thesis → five parallel axes → asymmetry adjudication.
- **Doctrine:** determinate human superior; *saptāṅga*; *daṇḍanīti*; *yogakṣema*; *maṇḍala* and *ṣāḍguṇya*.
- **Evidence:** the seven limbs by name; the five Austinian attributes; the international-law denial.
- **Distinction:** legal source of command against operational anatomy of rule.
- **Criticism:** the anachronism control — Kautilya predates the modern doctrine, so agreement must be graded, never assumed.

**Question:** Explain the importance of Bodin's theory of sovereignty.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Explain the importance" — internal logic and historical significance, with light criticism only |
| Core issue | What Bodin **added** that political thought did not previously possess |
| Scope boundary | Ten marks: definition, the state-building move, the limits, one qualification |
| Thinker thesis | He made authority impersonal and gave the modern state its vocabulary |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Bodin's importance is not that he made rulers stronger but that he made authority **impersonal**, supplying modern politics with a language of permanent, undivided public power.

**The definition.** ✅ Sovereignty is the **absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth**: *absolute* means subject to no human superior within the commonwealth; *perpetual* means attached to the office rather than to a temporary magistrate. ✅ Its pre-eminent mark is the power to make and unmake law for all subjects in general without the consent of any superior.

**Why this mattered.** ⚠️ Writing amid the French wars of religion, when rival churches, estates and feudal powers each claimed finality, Bodin identified what a community must possess to end its disputes: one authority whose law-making word is last. ⚠️ Perpetuity does the state-building work — a power that survives its holder is an institution, and only an institution supplies a continuous procedure of closure.

- **Structure:** thesis → definition → why it mattered → the three limits → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** absolute and perpetual power; the pre-eminent law-making mark.

#### Solved PYQ 5 · 2020 · Q4(b) · 15 marks

**Question:** State and explain the relevance of the doctrine of sovereignty in times of globalization.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "State and explain the relevance" — a thesis, then a mechanism-by-mechanism explanation |
| Core issue | Whether interdependence abolishes, or merely reconfigures, final authority |
| Scope boundary | Fifteen marks: four mechanisms distinguished, four thinkers redeployed, one concession |
| Thinker thesis | Constraint is not extinction; the mode changes, the question does not |
| Non-negotiables | Treaty, delegation, pooling, dependence kept apart; the supranational caution |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Globalisation **modifies** sovereignty without abolishing it: states still claim final authority over territory, law, security and political membership, so the doctrine remains relevant as an account of how that authority is exercised, delegated, pooled and constrained.

**Mechanism one — treaty commitment.** ✅ A state accepts binding rules through its own recognised treaty-making capacity. ⚠️ Self-limitation constrains future action without creating a global Austinian sovereign; indeed it **presupposes** the capacity it limits.

**Mechanism two — delegation.** ⚠️ A state authorises an institution to perform a defined adjudicative, administrative or monitoring function. The **exercise** of a competence moves outward while its legal source and limits remain treaty-based.

**Mechanism three — pooling.** ⚠️ States exercise a defined competence jointly through common voting or bargaining rules. Finality becomes **shared for the agreed field**, not universally transferred.

**Mechanism four — material dependence.** ⚠️ Finance, markets, technology or security asymmetry narrows the range of feasible choices. Here legal independence may survive while **effective autonomy declines** — the mechanism that does the most practical damage and involves no legal transfer at all.

**The four thinkers redeployed.** ⚠️ For **Bodin**, external pressure would not negate the need for a final internal authority. For **Austin**, globalisation complicates the idea of an uncommanded superior. For **Laski**, interdependence strengthens the critique of absolute state supremacy. For **Kautilya**, the strategic-relational reading of external sovereignty, through the circle of states, remains directly serviceable.

**Caution and verdict.** ⚠️ Not every international organisation is supranational, and no supranational institution is automatically sovereign in Austin's sense; ask which competence was conferred, by what instrument, with what exit rule, and whether dependence makes formal consent substantively unequal. ⚠️ **Sovereignty is illimitable in legal form, limited in political fact and negotiated in international practice** — which is precisely why the doctrine remains relevant rather than obsolete.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the whole answer turns on refusing to treat "globalisation" as one thing. Four mechanisms with four different consequences is the structure; the slogan "sovereignty has ended" is what the examiner is testing against.

**Exam-length execution — 15 marks, about 250 to 330 words:** thesis two lines; four mechanisms at three lines each; redeployment four; caution and verdict four.

- **Structure:** thesis → four mechanisms → four thinkers → caution → degree verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pooled, negotiated and networked sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** treaty capacity; delegated function; joint voting rules; conditionality.
- **Distinction:** legal source against exercise; shared finality against transfer; legal independence against effective autonomy.
- **Criticism:** the conditionality concession, stated as the strongest case against the thesis.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Is … ? Discuss" — a graded verdict with the level of each judgement named |

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Only partially, and the answer must be given at two levels: compatible in the narrow legal sense that a law-making body can always be named, incompatible in the deeper constitutional-political sense.

**The theory.** ✅ Austin's sovereign is a **determinate human superior**, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one, and is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**Where compatibility holds.** ✅ Every democracy contains an identifiable legislature, so at the level of legal form the Austinian can point to a determinate law-maker.

**Where it fails.** ⚠️ Democracy disperses authority through elections, rights, representation, opposition and constitutional limitation; it makes final authority **derivative, revisable and answerable**, whereas Austin's is legally supreme and obeys no one. ⚠️ Popular sovereignty sharpens the difficulty: "the people" are indeterminate, changing and institutionally mediated, so either the sovereign is not the people or the sovereign is not determinate.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | Two-part stem ending in "Critically discuss" — arguments first, then a criterion-based compatibility verdict |
| Core issue | Whether an authority defined as having no human superior can secure three modern ideals |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: three arguments, three ideals tested separately, one graded verdict |
| Thinker thesis | Historically foundational, normatively problematic |
| Non-negotiables | Each adjective argued, not asserted; the three limits; the ideals used as a **test**, not expounded |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Bodin's three arguments are internally coherent, but compatibility with equality, justice and liberty is **conditional at best**: his theory can deliver impartial law and public order while offering weak guarantees against the concentration of power.

**Argument for absoluteness.** ✅ Law requires a source not itself legally derivative from another earthly authority; the sovereign's pre-eminent mark is the power to make and unmake law for all subjects without a superior's consent. ⚠️ *Absolute* therefore denies a superior **law-maker**, not a superior **norm**.

**Argument for perpetuity.** ✅ Sovereignty attaches to the office rather than to a temporary magistrate or delegated official. ⚠️ A power that dies with its holder is a personal ascendancy; only one that survives its holder is a state capable of continuous closure.

**Argument for undividedness.** ⚠️ If two authorities are each final, conflict becomes insoluble and the civil disorder of the wars of religion returns. ✅ Division of governmental **functions** is permitted — advisory estates, subordinate courts — but division of sovereign **title** is not.

**The limits that qualify all three.** ✅ He is *legibus solutus* only of ordinary positive law, and remains bound by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm (*leges imperii*); arbitrary confiscation of property is discussed as incompatible with his own framework.

**Testing against equality.** ⚠️ A single impartial law-giver can dissolve feudal and confessional privilege, which serves equality before the law; but nothing in the theory requires equal civic standing, and the sovereign is the judge of his own compliance.

**Testing against justice.** ⚠️ Divine and natural law supply a normative standard, so the sovereign is not morally empty; ✅ yet no institution is empowered to enforce that standard against him, so the limits are conceptually real and practically thin.

**Testing against liberty.** ⚠️ Order is a condition of liberty, and Bodin secures it; but an authority with no human superior within the commonwealth gives liberty no institutional guarantee, which is exactly Laski's objection that absolute sovereignty is politically dangerous.

**Graded verdict.** ⚠️ **Historically foundational, normatively problematic.** Bodin makes the modern state possible and modern constitutional protection necessary; the compatibility is partial, conditional and unenforced.

- **Structure:** thesis → three arguments → the limits → three separate tests → graded verdict.
- **Doctrine:** absolute, perpetual, undivided; *legibus solutus*; *leges imperii*.
- **Evidence:** the law-making mark; advisory estates and subordinate courts; arbitrary confiscation.
- **Distinction:** superior law-maker against superior norm; functions against title.
- **Criticism:** unenforceable higher-law limits, conceded as the theory's permanent vulnerability.

#### Solved PYQ 9 · 2023 · Q2(a) · 20 marks

**Question:** Elucidate why the absolute nature of sovereignty was rejected by Laski.

##### Demand decoding

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Directive | "Elucidate why" — the internal logic of the rejection, expounded rather than merely listed |
| Core issue | The three independent grounds and what each rules out |
| Scope boundary | Twenty marks: three grounds developed, targets named, positive proposal, one residual |
| Thinker thesis | Absolutism is false, dangerous and unrealistic — three claims of three kinds |
| Non-negotiables | Pluralist not anarchist; not decentralisation; the coordination debt |

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ Laski rejects absolute sovereignty on three logically independent grounds — philosophical, political and sociological — and the independence is what makes the rejection difficult to answer.

**Ground one — philosophically false.** ✅ No human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience. ⚠️ Obligation is generated wherever human purposes are pursued in common, so a claim that all obligation derives from one will is not merely severe but mistaken about the source of obligation itself.

**Ground two — politically dangerous.** ✅ Absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty. ⚠️ If the state is the sole final authority, dissenting associations, minority rights, local autonomies and moral conscience can all be overridden in the name of public supremacy; pluralism is therefore a doctrine of **liberty** as much as of sociology.

**Ground three — sociologically unrealistic.** ✅ Real societies distribute power among many groups and institutions. Individuals belong to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; associations are not permissions granted by sovereignty but **real sites of personality, cooperation and authority**.

**The two targets.** ⚠️ He attacks both the legal abstraction of Austin — the determinate superior whose command exhausts law — and the absolutist impulse inherited from Bodin.

**The positive proposal.** ✅ Sovereignty in the monistic sense should give way to a distributed structure of authority: modern society is functionally differentiated, so economic, cultural, educational, local and religious life cannot be governed by one centralised will. ✅ The state must **compete for allegiance** by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life; authority is functional and moral, not mystical.

**Two controls.** ✅ Laski is a **pluralist, not an anarchist**: the state retains coordinating and coercive capacities. ✅ His claim is **not administrative decentralisation** but a challenge to the idea that one final unlimited will exists at all. ⚠️ Note also the phase caution — early Laski called sovereignty a legal fiction; the mature Laski conceded its importance while denying omnipotence.

**Objection, reply, residual.** ✅ Objection: without a final sovereign, conflict between associations is insoluble. ⚠️ Reply: legal finality does not eliminate conflict but often suppresses legitimate diversity, and what is needed is a final **procedure**, not an unanswerable will. ⚠️ Residual: emergencies, defence and redistribution still require an integrating authority, and MacIver's residual coordinating role concedes it.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Laski's corrective against absolutism is indispensable; a polity that can never speak with one voice cannot discharge the coordinating task that made sovereignty necessary in the first place.

**Answer-writing focus:** the stem says *why*, so the three grounds must be **developed** rather than listed, and the mark for critical control comes from conceding the coordination debt in the verdict.

- **Structure:** thesis → three grounds → targets → positive proposal → two controls → objection, reply, residual → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** pluralism; distributed authority; earned allegiance.
- **Evidence:** the association list; the two targets; MacIver's coordination role.
- **Distinction:** pluralist against anarchist; conceptual challenge against decentralisation.
- **Criticism:** the unpaid coordination debt, named in the verdict.

- **Criticism:** the line's incompleteness as a summary of the whole theory, stated in the verdict.

### ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE WITH MODEL SOLUTIONS

> Six original questions in the style of the paper — two each at 10, 15 and 20 marks — covering the parts of the clause that the 2018–2025 corpus has not yet examined directly: the legal/political distinction, the de jure and de facto pairs, the classical attributes and their qualifications, the internal repairs of Austin by Kelsen and Hart, the pluralist lineage behind Laski, and the four-way structure of the whole dispute. Every model answer is independent learner practice and is never an official key.

#### Original 1 · 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish legal from political sovereignty, and explain why the distinction matters for evaluating Austin.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ The two answer different questions — who is **competent to make law**, and what power **shapes or compels** the law-maker — and the distinction matters because most objections to Austin are answers to the second offered against a thesis about the first.

**Legal sovereignty.** ✅ It names the authority recognised by law as competent to make law: the juristic source of validity, and Austin's preferred angle. His sovereign is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one.

**Political sovereignty.** ✅ It names the actual power behind legal institutions — the force that can compel or shape the legal sovereign. Parties, public opinion, organised interests, military power, religion and economic classes belong here, and this is the standpoint from which Laski attacks.

**Why they diverge.** ⚠️ A constitution may vest law-making power in one body while something else determines what that body can actually do; the divergence is a general feature of political life rather than a constitutional defect.

**Why it matters for Austin.** ⚠️ An unlabelled objection commits a category error. Showing that a legislature is politically constrained does not refute a claim about legal competence; showing that power-conferring rules cannot be redescribed as commands does.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Austin overstates the sufficiency of legal validity and pluralists overstate the sufficiency of political efficacy; a balanced answer keeps both questions open rather than declaring one of them the real one.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** two definitions earn little; two definitions plus the diagnosis of a category error earn the question.

- **Structure:** thesis → legal → political → divergence → consequence for Austin → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** juristic source of valid law against the real locus of social power.
- **Evidence:** the determinate human superior; the list of political forces.
- **Distinction:** validity against efficacy.
- **Criticism:** the symmetrical overstatement of both camps, named in the verdict.

#### Original 2 · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain the five classical attributes of sovereignty, and show why each requires a qualification.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ✅ The five attributes are defensible only in qualified form; stated flat, each is refuted by the first counter-example a critic supplies.

**Absoluteness.** ✅ No superior human law-maker legally commands the sovereign within the political order. ⚠️ Qualification: legal non-subordination does not prove moral infallibility or practical omnipotence.

**Permanence or continuity.** ✅ Governments and office-holders change while sovereignty continues with the state. ⚠️ Qualification: permanence means continuity while the state remains independent, not literal eternity.

**Universality.** ✅ Sovereign jurisdiction extends in principle across persons and associations within the territory. ⚠️ Qualification: immunities and autonomous spheres may exist by constitutional or legal recognition.

**Inalienability.** ✅ Sovereignty cannot be transferred away without the old state losing that status. ⚠️ Qualification: delegation, devolution or treaty commitment is not automatically alienation.

**Indivisibility.** ✅ The classical theory requires one final legal source. ⚠️ Qualification: governmental competences may be distributed even where monists insist legal finality remains one.

**Placement and verdict.** ✅ Bodin supplies the defining pair *absolute and perpetual*; Austin supplies the sharpest determinate-superior formulation; the five-part list is a **consolidation** of that monist tradition rather than five claims uniquely invented by either. ⚠️ Qualified in this way, the list survives federal division and treaty commitment; unqualified, it does not.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the qualification column is the answer. A candidate who writes only the five definitions has written a glossary.

- **Structure:** thesis → five attributes each with its qualification → placement → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** the classical monist taxonomy of legal sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** Bodin's defining pair; Austin's sharpened list.
- **Distinction:** legal non-subordination against moral infallibility; delegation against alienation.
- **Criticism:** the vulnerability of the unqualified list, stated as the reason for qualifying it.

#### Original 3 · 15 marks

**Question:** Examine the claim that Kelsen and Hart refute Austin more effectively than Laski does.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The claim is largely correct but must be graded: Kelsen and Hart defeat Austin's **definition**, whereas Laski defeats its **application** — and only the first is a refutation in the strict sense.

**Austin's position.** ✅ Law is the command of a determinate human superior backed by sanction; sovereignty is determinate, absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**Laski's external attack.** ✅ The state is one association among many; absolute sovereignty is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic. ⚠️ This shows that no actual society matches the Austinian picture, but it does not show that the picture is internally incoherent, and Austin can reply that he is analysing legal validity rather than describing social power.

**Kelsen's internal repair.** ✅ Validity derives from a higher norm, never from a fact, terminating in a presupposed basic norm (*Grundnorm*) — *General Theory of Law and State*, first English edition **1945**. ⚠️ This dissolves the personal sovereign from within positivism and answers the succession and persistence problems that habit cannot explain.

**Hart's internal repair.** ✅ Law is a union of primary and secondary rules, with a rule of recognition existing as convergent official practice viewed from the internal point of view — *The Concept of Law*, **1961**. ⚠️ Nullity is not a sanction, and being obliged is not having an obligation; both strikes attack the definition rather than its fit.

**Adjudication.** ⚠️ Refutation of a definition is stronger than refutation of a description, so the claim holds. ⚠️ But Laski's attack survives the repairs: neither Kelsen nor Hart addresses the danger of unlimited authority or the plurality of allegiance, so the three are complementary rather than competitive.

**Control.** ⚠️ They must not be merged: Kelsen replaces the sovereign with a norm-hierarchy, Hart with an official practice, Laski with plural associations.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ Kelsen and Hart refute Austin **as a theory of law**; Laski refutes him **as a theory of political authority** — and a complete answer runs the internal critique first and the external critique second.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** "more effectively" demands a criterion of effectiveness. Supplying one — definition against application — converts an opinion into an argument.

- **Structure:** graded thesis → Austin → external attack → two internal repairs → adjudication → control → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** command theory; hierarchy of norms; primary and secondary rules; pluralism.
- **Evidence:** Kelsen 1945; Hart 1961; the nullity strike; the three grounds of rejection.
- **Distinction:** internal against external critique; definition against application.
- **Criticism:** the warning against merging three incompatible replacements.

#### Original 4 · 15 marks

**Question:** Assess the significance of the pluralist lineage — Figgis, Cole and MacIver — for Laski's rejection of monistic sovereignty.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The lineage strengthens Laski's rejection empirically and supplies the concession his own account most needs, but it must locate the school rather than replace the printed thinker.

**Laski's position.** ✅ The state is one association among many. Individuals belong to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; associations are not permissions granted by sovereignty.

**Figgis.** ✅ Churches and other real associations expose the danger of an absolute state allied with economic concentration. ⚠️ His significance is as the group-personality and anti-absorption precursor: he shows that associations have purposes of their own that the state did not create.

**Cole.** ✅ A forerunner of pluralism associated with functional or guild representation and socially organised group life. ⚠️ Significance: he converts plural loyalty into a proposal about representation. The boundary matters — the answer must not become a theory of guild socialism.

**MacIver.** ✅ Law and constitution, not a mysterious sovereign will, ground legitimate public force; the state **coordinates** associations without creating all their purposes. ⚠️ Significance: this is both the strongest sociological supplement to Laski and the honest statement of what pluralism still owes to monism.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Figgis supplies the moral urgency, Cole the institutional imagination and MacIver the residual coordination role. ⚠️ The last is decisive: because someone must settle conflicts **between** associations, MacIver's coordination claim measures exactly how much of the monist case survives.

**Control and verdict.** ✅ Laski is the printed thinker and the marks follow him; the three names locate the school. ⚠️ The lineage therefore makes the rejection of monism harder to dismiss as one writer's preference, while making explicit that pluralism limits sovereignty rather than abolishing the function it names.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** the risk here is a biography parade. Give each name **one** supported contribution and **one** boundary, then assess the lineage as a whole.

- **Structure:** thesis → Laski's position → three names with contribution and boundary → assessment → control and verdict.
- **Doctrine:** associational autonomy; group personality; coordination without absolutism.
- **Evidence:** the association list; MacIver's law-and-constitution argument.
- **Distinction:** locating a school against replacing the printed thinker.
- **Criticism:** the residual coordination role, treated as the measure of what monism retains.

#### Original 5 · 20 marks

**Question:** "The concept of supreme legal authority survives every criticism examined; the image of a determinate personal sovereign does not." Critically examine this verdict with reference to Bodin, Austin, Laski and Kautilya.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The verdict is sound but requires two amendments: the surviving concept is **final procedure** rather than final will, and Kautilya shows that a duty-bound personal head can survive where an illimitable one cannot.

**What the image asserts.** ✅ Bodin's sovereign is the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth; Austin's is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk and obeying no one, whose authority is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable.

**The internal criticisms.** ✅ Austin cannot explain succession, persistence or power-conferring rules. ⚠️ Kelsen (*General Theory of Law and State*, English edition **1945**) replaces the commander with a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm; Hart (*The Concept of Law*, **1961**) replaces him with a union of primary and secondary rules resting on a rule of recognition accepted from the internal point of view. ⚠️ Both remain positivists: the image dissolves, the concept of ultimate validity does not.

**The external criticisms.** ✅ Federalism distributes competence; international law binds without a world sovereign; custom precedes command; democracy makes authority derivative and answerable. ✅ Laski adds that absolutism is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic. ⚠️ Each attacks the personal image; none shows that a legal order can dispense with an ultimate criterion of validity.

**What Bodin retains.** ⚠️ Bodin survives better than Austin because his sovereign is already limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm, and because perpetuity locates authority in an **office**. His weakness is enforcement, not conception.

**What Kautilya adds.** ⚠️ The seven-limbed state makes sovereignty systemic; calibrated coercion makes force proportionate; security and welfare make legitimacy conditional on outcomes. ⚠️ A head bound by dharma, counsel, treasury and welfare is not the illimitable superior the criticisms destroy, which is why Kautilya is the least damaged of the four.

**The amendments.** ⚠️ First, what survives is best described as a **final procedure** for closure rather than a final will, since Hart's rule of recognition and a constitutional amending procedure both perform closure without personal supremacy. ⚠️ Second, the three dissolutions must not be merged: a norm-hierarchy, an official practice and a plurality of associations are rivals, not allies.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The quoted verdict holds. The image of a determinate personal sovereign fails on succession, on facilities, on federal competence, on international normativity and on democratic accountability; the concept of supreme legal authority survives, reconstituted as procedure, and Kautilya's duty-bound headship survives alongside it because it never claimed illimitability in the first place.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** examining a quoted verdict requires the candidate to **amend** rather than merely endorse. Two precise amendments are worth more than three paragraphs of agreement.

- **Structure:** graded thesis → the image → internal criticisms → external criticisms → what Bodin retains → what Kautilya adds → two amendments → verdict.
- **Doctrine:** the five attributes; hierarchy of norms; rule of recognition; pluralism; *saptāṅga* and *yogakṣema*.
- **Evidence:** Kelsen 1945; Hart 1961; the *leges imperii*; the seven limbs.
- **Distinction:** image against concept; final will against final procedure.
- **Criticism:** the warning against merging the three dissolutions, and the enforcement gap in Bodin.

#### Original 6 · 20 marks

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that the four printed thinkers on sovereignty are answering four different questions rather than giving four answers to one.

##### Independent model answer

**Thesis.** ⚠️ The claim is largely true and analytically productive, but it must be qualified: the four questions overlap at one point — every position must say what makes closure possible — and it is that overlap which makes comparison legitimate.

**The four questions.** ⚠️ Bodin and Austin ask **who** is sovereign. Kelsen and Hart deny that this is the right question and ask **what makes a norm valid**. Laski denies that any single answer is possible and asks **how allegiance is actually distributed**. Kautilya asks what the holder of authority is **for**.

**Bodin and Austin — the "who" question.** ✅ Bodin answers: the commonwealth's supreme law-giving authority, absolute and perpetual, limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm. ✅ Austin answers: a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society, whose authority is illimitable within the legal order.

**Kelsen and Hart — the validity question.** ⚠️ Because "is" does not yield "ought", validity cannot rest on the fact of obedience; Kelsen derives it from a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm (**1945**), and Hart from a rule of recognition existing as convergent official practice (**1961**).

**Laski — the allegiance question.** ✅ The state is one association among many and must compete for allegiance by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life; monistic sovereignty misdescribes society and licenses domination.

**Kautilya — the purpose question.** ✅ The ruler heads a seven-limbed state, governs through calibrated coercion, and is measured by security and welfare; authority is justified by what it delivers.

**Why the reframing is productive.** ⚠️ Read as four answers to one question, the positions look like a disagreement of degree about how much authority is too much. Read as four questions, each exposes a blind spot in the others: Austin cannot see facilities, Kelsen and Hart cannot see danger, Laski cannot see emergency closure, and Kautilya cannot see consent.

**The qualification.** ⚠️ The questions are not wholly independent. Every position must explain how a political community reaches closure, and each does: Bodin through an undivided title, Austin through a determinate superior, Kelsen through a presupposed apex, Hart through an accepted criterion, Laski through a final procedure, Kautilya through a duty-bound head. ⚠️ Comparison is therefore legitimate on that axis and illegitimate on axes the thinkers do not share.

**Verdict.** ⚠️ The claim should be accepted with that amendment: four questions, one shared problem. The corresponding examination discipline is to **fix the axes** — source, location, divisibility, legal and moral limits, organs of state and modern relevance — and to refuse comparison by biography or by chronology.

##### Why this earns marks

**Answer-writing focus:** a "critically examine" stem asking about a methodological claim is won by amending the claim precisely, not by illustrating it at length.

- **Structure:** qualified thesis → the four questions → each answered → why the reframing helps → the shared-problem qualification → verdict with the axis discipline.
- **Doctrine:** all four printed positions plus the two internal repairs.
- **Evidence:** the *leges imperii*; the determinate superior; Kelsen 1945 and Hart 1961; the seven limbs.
- **Distinction:** four questions against four answers; shared closure problem against unshared axes.
- **Criticism:** the blind-spot audit, which prevents the reframing from becoming an excuse for not comparing.
