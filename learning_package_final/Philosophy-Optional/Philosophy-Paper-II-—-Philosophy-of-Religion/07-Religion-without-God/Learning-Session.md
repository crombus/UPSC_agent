### SESSION 1 — THE NECESSITY QUESTION, AND THE DEFINITION THAT DECIDES IT

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** "Religion without God" asks whether a personal creator is *necessary* for something to count as a religion, or whether sacred order, disciplined practice, community and transformation can be enough on their own.

**Technical definition.** The clause tests a **necessity claim**. A *substantive* definition identifies religion by the kind of object it affirms (God, gods, sacred beings); a *functional* definition identifies it by what it does (orientation to ultimacy, practice, community, meaning, liberation); a *multidimensional* definition identifies it by a cluster of dimensions; a *family-resemblance* definition denies that any single feature is shared by all.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> A personal creator is sufficient for many religions but not necessary for religion as such: what cannot be removed without conceptual cost is not the deity but the sacred order, the disciplined practice and the transformation they organise.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**substantive definition · functional definition · multidimensional definition · family resemblance · Ninian Smart · ultimate concern · necessity claim · question-begging**

**How to use them:** the first three lines of every answer in this clause must name the definition you are using. If you adopt the substantive definition you have conceded the question; if you adopt an unrestricted functional definition you have emptied it. Announce the middle position and then keep to it — inconsistency here is the commonest cause of a mediocre mark.

#### VISUAL — THE FOUR DEFINITIONS, AND WHERE EACH ONE BREAKS

```text
  ┌──────────────────────── "WHAT IS A RELIGION?" ────────────────────────┐
  │                                                                        │
  │  SUBSTANTIVE            FUNCTIONAL           MULTIDIMENSIONAL          │
  │  by its OBJECT          by what it DOES      by a CLUSTER (Smart)      │
  │  God / gods /           ultimacy, practice,  doctrinal · mythic ·      │
  │  sacred beings          community, meaning,  ethical · ritual ·        │
  │                         liberation           experiential · social ·   │
  │                                              material                  │
  │        │                      │                      │                 │
  │        ▼                      ▼                      ▼                 │
  │  TOO NARROW             TOO BROAD             THE WORKABLE MIDDLE      │
  │  excludes Buddhism      admits nationalism,   creator-belief is NOT    │
  │  by stipulation         art, political cause  on the essential list    │
  │  = QUESTION-BEGGING     = EMPTY CATEGORY                               │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        FAMILY RESEMBLANCE: no single feature is shared by every member;
        "religion" names a family of historically varied practices,
        not one essence.  <-- this is what licenses the cluster test.
```

*Read the top row for the three live definitions, the bottom row for the failure each incurs. The whole clause is decided in the gap between "too narrow" and "too broad".*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT]** The printed clause owns the necessity question, not the existence question. Nothing in it turns on whether God exists; everything turns on whether *religion* can be constituted without one. That is why an answer that spends its length arguing against theism has answered a different paper.

**[FACT] Smart's dimensions.** Ninian Smart's multidimensional analysis lists the doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social and material dimensions of religion. **Belief in a creator God is not on the essential list.** This is not a rhetorical convenience: it is a comparative finding drawn from traditions that plainly possess the seven dimensions while lacking a maker of the world.

**[FACT] The functional definition.** On the functional account, religion is a way of life orienting a person toward ultimate meaning, liberation and the transcendent or absolute — and none of those three requires a personal God. Hence "religion without God" is coherent from the start: the **sacred or ultimate is not necessarily a deity.**

**[INFERENCE] Why the family-resemblance point matters.** "Religion" names a family of historically varied practices rather than one essence. Once that is granted, the demand for a single necessary feature is itself suspect, and the burden shifts to the theist to show why *this* feature, rather than ritual or community or soteriology, is the indispensable one.

**Objection.** The substantive definition is not arbitrary: it is the definition every major theistic tradition uses of itself, and it protects the category from dissolving into ethics or sentiment.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** Conceded that it protects the category — that is its real strength and it must be granted before it is answered. But a tradition's account of *itself* is not automatically a cross-cultural definition of the genus. If creator-belief is made necessary **by definition**, Buddhism is excluded by stipulation rather than by evidence, and a definition that decides a comparative question before looking at the comparanda is question-begging.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Whether religion without God is possible is settled by which definition of religion the answer adopts, so the definition must be stated and defended first.
> **Named evidence:** Ninian Smart's multidimensional analysis places doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social and material dimensions on the essential list and leaves creator-belief off it; John Hick's comparative treatment admits Theravāda Buddhism although it lacks worship of a creator.
> **Analysis:** A substantive definition therefore decides the question in advance, while an unrestricted functional definition decides it too cheaply by admitting any strong commitment; only a cluster definition tests the traditions rather than the vocabulary.
> **Qualification:** The cluster still has to be policed, because a definition broad enough to include nationalism explains nothing — which is why the sacred-order and soteriological conditions are added in the next paragraph.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not begin by listing godless schools. Begin by stating the definition of religion you will use; the schools are evidence *for* a definition, not a substitute for one.
2. ⚠️ Do not adopt a definition narrow enough to exclude Buddhism and then cite Buddhism as a counterexample. That is the commonest internal contradiction in this clause.
3. ⚠️ A multidimensional definition must not become so broad that every ideology counts as religion; the breadth has to be policed inside the same answer that introduces it.

#### MINI RECAP

Four definitions — substantive, functional, multidimensional, family-resemblance. Two failures — too narrow (question-begging) and too broad (empty). One habit — name your definition in sentence one and never drift from it.

### SESSION 2 — NECESSARY, SUFFICIENT AND OVER-BROAD: THE WORKING CLUSTER

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** The cluster test asks which features of religion are genuinely indispensable, which are merely widespread, and which are so common that they cannot mark religion off from anything else.

**Technical definition.** No single feature below is uncontroversially **necessary** for every religion; a defensible examination definition therefore uses a **sufficient cluster** rather than a single necessary condition.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Religion is a socially embodied and disciplined orientation to an ultimate or sacred order, expressed through doctrine, ritual, ethical formation, experience, community and a transformative goal; belief in a personal creator is one historically powerful way of organising that orientation, not a condition of it.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**necessary condition · sufficient cluster · sacred order · ultimate concern · soteriology · disciplined practice · over-breadth · discriminator**

**How to use them:** the examiner is buying the word **discriminator**. Say which feature does the discriminating work in your definition — the owner's answer is *diagnosis-and-liberation* joined to *disciplined relation to a sacred order* — and then show that it admits Buddhism and excludes a football crowd.

#### VISUAL — THE SEVEN CANDIDATE FEATURES, GRADED

| Candidate feature | Necessary? | Why it is not sufficient on its own |
|---|---|---|
| Personal creator God | **No** — Buddhism, Jainism and classical Mīmāṃsā are countercases | It defines theism, not religion as such |
| Sacred or ultimate concern | Strong candidate, but very broad | Nations, art or political causes can become ultimate concerns |
| Ritual and disciplined practice | Widespread, but not universal in one form | Secular ceremonies also have ritual |
| Ethical orientation | Common | Ethical philosophies are not automatically religions |
| Community and tradition | Common | Clubs and nations also organise community |
| Diagnosis and liberation/salvation | **Strong discriminator** | Some traditions stress worship or belonging more than liberation |
| Myth, doctrine and experience | Family features | Each may be thin or absent in a particular strand |

```text
   THE WORKING SUFFICIENT CLUSTER
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  a SOCIALLY EMBODIED and DISCIPLINED orientation                    │
   │  to an ULTIMATE / SACRED ORDER,                                     │
   │  expressed through some combination of                              │
   │      doctrine · ritual · ethical formation · experience · community │
   │  and a TRANSFORMATIVE or SOTERIOLOGICAL goal.                       │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ADMITS: Buddhism · Jainism · Mīmāṃsā · Sāṃkhya-Yoga
        EXCLUDES: any strong commitment as such (nationalism, ideology, fandom)
        AND EXCLUDES: Cārvāka — no sacred order, no soteriology, no path
```

*Read the table for the grading of each candidate; read the box for the cluster you will actually write. The cluster is a sufficient condition, not a list of necessary ones — say so explicitly.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT]** The table's first row is the whole clause in miniature. A personal creator God is not necessary, because three established Indian traditions are countercases; and it is not what makes something a religion, because affirming it is precisely what makes something *theistic*. Necessity and definition are being conflated by the position under test.

**[FACT] The over-breadth problem is real and must be conceded.** Ultimate concern is a strong candidate but a very broad one: nations, art and political causes can all become ultimate concerns. Ritual is widespread but secular ceremonies have it. Ethical orientation is common but ethical philosophies are not religions. Community is common but so are clubs. Every single functional feature, taken alone, over-generates.

**[FACT] The discriminator.** What does the discriminating work is the pairing of a **sacred or ultimate order** with a **diagnosis-and-liberation structure**. That pairing is present in Buddhism (suffering and its cessation), Jainism (bondage by karmic matter and release into the perfected state), Mīmāṃsā (duty, merit and the promised fruit) and Sāṃkhya (entanglement and isolation), and absent from every secular analogue.

**[INFERENCE] Why the cluster is stated as sufficient, not necessary.** Listing necessary conditions invites a counterexample for each. Stating a sufficient cluster shifts the argument onto ground you can defend: you are not claiming that every religion has all of these, only that a tradition possessing this combination is a religion whatever it says about a creator.

**Objection.** A cluster with no necessary condition cannot exclude anything at all: any sufficiently determined movement could be described as possessing "some combination" of the listed features.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The cluster is not a menu. Two elements are load-bearing and must both be present — a **disciplined relation to an ultimate or sacred order** and a **transformative or soteriological practice**. A political movement may have ritual, community, ethics and doctrine and still fail both, because its ultimate is not treated as sacred and it offers no release from a diagnosed predicament.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** The definition that survives both objections requires a disciplined relation to a sacred order together with a soteriological practice.
> **Named evidence:** Buddhism supplies the diagnosis of suffering and the path to its cessation; Jainism supplies bondage by karmic matter and release into the perfected state (*siddha*); Mīmāṃsā supplies injunction, merit and a promised fruit; classical Sāṃkhya supplies entanglement and isolation (*kaivalya*).
> **Analysis:** All four therefore satisfy the cluster while denying a creator, which shows the exclusion of a creator does not remove what makes a tradition religious.
> **Qualification:** Because the same test excludes Cārvāka, which has neither a sacred order nor a path, the cluster is not merely permissive — it does real classificatory work, and that is what answers the over-breadth charge.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not present the cluster as a list of necessary conditions. It is a sufficient combination, and saying so pre-empts the counterexample hunt.
2. ⚠️ Do not answer the nationalism objection by abandoning functionalism. Add the sacred-order and soteriological conditions instead.
3. ⚠️ Do not omit the concession that every functional feature over-generates on its own. Making the concession is what earns the right to the repair.

#### MINI RECAP

Seven candidates, one strong discriminator — diagnosis and liberation. One cluster: sacred order plus disciplined transformative practice. Two things it must do — admit Buddhism, exclude both nationalism and Cārvāka.

### SESSION 3 — WHICH RELIGIOUS FUNCTIONS ACTUALLY REQUIRE A DEITY

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Instead of asking whether religion needs God, this session asks the sharper question: which *particular jobs* inside religious life can only be done by a personal deity?

**Technical definition.** The functions of religion are separable. God is necessary for some **specifically theistic goods** — petition, grace, covenant, propositional revelation from a divine speaker — and not for the generic functions of worship, ritual efficacy, moral motivation or liberation.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> God is a prerequisite for *some* religious goods — petition, grace and covenant — but not for religion as such; the theist's strongest argument is therefore devotional rather than definitional, and it deserves to be met on that ground rather than dismissed.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**petitionary prayer · worship and veneration · propositional revelation · grace and providence · ritual efficacy · moral motivation · soteriology · separability**

**How to use them:** run the function list as a **ledger**, marking each function "requires a responsive person" or "does not". The concession in the first column is what makes the denials in the second column credible.

#### VISUAL — THE FUNCTION LEDGER

| Religious function | Personal deity required? | The non-theistic form it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Petitionary prayer | **Yes, normally** — a responsive addressee is built into petition | meditation, aspiration or recitation, which are not petition in the same sense |
| Worship / veneration | No | reverence for teaching (*Dharma*), liberated exemplars, scripture or sacred value |
| Propositional revelation from God | **Yes, by definition** | Mīmāṃsā has authorless scriptural authority, not creator-revelation |
| Grace and providence | **Yes**, in their personal-agency sense | self-effort, karmic law, disciplined path, communal support perform other functions |
| Ritual efficacy | No | Mīmāṃsā's unseen ritual potency (*apūrva*); Buddhist and Jain discipline |
| Moral motivation | No | compassion, non-injury (*ahiṃsā*), karma, vows, insight, human flourishing |
| Liberation / salvation | No, as a generic function | *nirvāṇa*, Jain liberation, Sāṃkhya–Yoga isolation (*kaivalya*) |

```text
   THE SEPARABILITY RESULT
   petition ──────────► needs a hearer who can choose      ✔ theistic good
   revelation ────────► needs a speaker                     ✔ theistic good
   grace ─────────────► needs a giver                       ✔ theistic good
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   worship ───────────► needs an object worthy of reverence ✘ not a person
   efficacy ──────────► needs a law or a potency            ✘ not a person
   motivation ────────► needs a reason and a discipline     ✘ not a person
   liberation ────────► needs a diagnosis and a path        ✘ not a person
   ======================================================================
   WHAT MĪMĀṂSĀ PROVES: authority, efficacy and desert are each SEPARABLE
   and can each be secured otherwise.
```

*Read the table as a ledger of concessions and denials, and the box as the single result it delivers: the functions God performs are separable from one another and from God.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] Petition is the honest concession.** A responsive addressee who can hear, choose and grant is built into the very act of petition. Meditation, aspiration and recitation are religiously serious, but they are not petition in the same sense. An answer that denies this looks unargued; an answer that concedes it looks candid, and buys credit for everything else it says.

**[FACT] Worship does not follow petition.** Reverence can be directed to teaching, to liberated exemplars, to scripture or to sacred value. The Jain veneration of a passionless perfected being (*vītarāga*) is the sharpest case: nothing is asked of the object and nothing is granted by it, yet the practice is unmistakably religious.

**[FACT] Revelation and grace stay on the theistic side.** Propositional revelation *from God* requires a divine speaker by definition; grace and providence in their personal-agency sense require an agent who can give. Mīmāṃsā does not dispute this — it removes the need by making the Veda authorless, which is a different move from claiming a non-personal revealer.

**[FACT] Efficacy, motivation and liberation cross over cleanly.** Ritual efficacy is secured by Mīmāṃsā's unseen potency (*apūrva*) and by Buddhist and Jain discipline; moral motivation by compassion, non-injury, karma, vows, insight and human flourishing; liberation by *nirvāṇa*, Jain release and Sāṃkhya–Yoga isolation.

**Objection.** If the theistic goods are genuine goods, a religion that lacks them is thereby a worse religion, and the necessity claim survives in a weakened evaluative form.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The evaluative point is fair and should be granted: non-theistic religion has a thinner personal structure, and there is a real cost in devotional texture. But "worse in one respect" is not "not a religion", and the printed stems ask about possibility and prerequisite, not about comparative richness.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** The necessity claim should be tested function by function rather than in the abstract.
> **Named evidence:** Petition requires a responsive addressee and propositional revelation requires a speaker, whereas Mīmāṃsā secures scriptural authority through authorlessness (*apauruṣeya*) and ritual efficacy through unseen potency (*apūrva*), and Buddhism secures liberation through the eightfold path.
> **Analysis:** The functions God performs — authority, efficacy and desert — are therefore separable from one another and can each be discharged otherwise, so the necessity claim holds only for the specifically relational goods.
> **Qualification:** That concession is not a retreat: it identifies exactly where the theist's real case lies, and an answer that meets the devotional argument rather than the definitional one is answering the strongest version of the objection.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not claim that God is required for nothing at all. Petition, grace and covenant are genuine theistic goods, and conceding them strengthens the rest of the answer.
2. ⚠️ Do not treat "worship" and "prayer" as a single function. Veneration survives the removal of a hearer; petition does not.
3. ⚠️ Do not say that non-theistic religions "replace" grace with self-effort. Say that they do not require the function, which is a different and stronger claim.

#### MINI RECAP

Seven functions, three of them genuinely theistic — petition, revelation, grace. Four of them separable — worship, efficacy, motivation, liberation. The result: what God does is separable, and Mīmāṃsā proves it in a single system.

### SESSION 4 — FOUR NEGATIVE POSITIONS: NON-THEISM, ATHEISM, AGNOSTICISM, INDIFFERENCE

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Four different things can be meant by saying that someone "does not believe in God", and they have four different consequences for whether a religion is possible.

**Technical definition.** **Non-theism** is the absence of a creator from a tradition's diagnosis and path; **atheism** is the positive denial that God exists; **agnosticism** is suspension of judgement, either because the matter is not known (weak) or because it is unknowable (strong); **indifference** is the absence of the question rather than of the answer.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Non-theism is a claim about what a path requires, atheism a claim about what exists, agnosticism a claim about what can be known, and indifference no claim at all: only the first is compatible with a fully constituted religion, and confusing them is what makes a strong case sound like a weak one.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**non-theism · atheism · weak agnosticism · strong agnosticism · indifference · practical atheism · anti-theism · creator-denial**

**How to use them:** decide, for each tradition you cite, which of the four positions it occupies, and say so. Buddhism is non-theistic; Cārvāka is atheistic and more; Huxley is agnostic; the merely unreligious are indifferent. That single sorting sentence signals control of the clause.

**[FACT] The firewall.** Non-theistic is not identical with atheistic. Rejecting a creator does not entail denying every deity, sacred being, devotional act or transcendent value. Theravāda Buddhism is the canonical illustration: it permits cosmological gods (*devas*) while denying both their creatorhood and their salvific ultimacy.

**[FACT] Agnosticism is epistemic.** Its claim is about knowledge, not about existence. **T. H. Huxley coined the word in 1869.** The weak form says the matter is not known; the strong form says it is unknowable. An agnostic may continue to value religion's ethical and experiential dimensions while withholding assent on God — which is why agnosticism supports a **minimal or non-theistic religiosity** rather than a rejection of religion.

**[FACT] Indifference is not a philosophical position.** It is the absence of the question. It appears in the list only so that it can be kept out of the answer; treating a life lived without reference to God as a *theory* about God is a category error.

**[INFERENCE] Why sorting matters for marks.** The four positions have different burdens. The non-theist must show that a path can be complete; the atheist must defend a denial; the agnostic must explain how suspension coexists with practice. An answer that uses the words interchangeably has quietly taken on all three burdens and discharged none.

**Objection.** The distinction is verbal: a tradition that assigns a creator no role has effectively denied one, so non-theism collapses into atheism after all.
**Reply.** **[FACT/INFERENCE]** It does not collapse, and the evidence is doctrinal. Buddhism explicitly retains impermanent divine beings inside the round of rebirth and later develops full devotional practice toward Buddhas and bodhisattvas; Jainism venerates liberated perfected beings. A denial of *creatorhood and salvific ultimacy* is compatible with all of that, whereas a denial of *existence* is not.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** The four negative positions must be separated before any tradition is classified.
> **Named evidence:** Theravāda Buddhism permits cosmological gods while denying their creatorhood and salvific ultimacy; Huxley, who coined "agnostic" in 1869, claims only that God's existence is unknown or unknowable; Cārvāka denies the whole supersensible order rather than a creator alone.
> **Analysis:** The three positions therefore differ in what they remove — a role, a knowledge claim and an entire ontological domain — and only the first leaves a religion standing.
> **Qualification:** The distinction is doctrinal rather than diplomatic, so it cannot be dismissed as politeness; it is what allows non-theistic religions to retain devotion without incoherence.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not call Buddhism simply "atheistic". "Non-theistic" is more precise, and the precision is worth marks in the first paragraph.
2. ⚠️ Do not say that Buddhism denies every deity. It denies creatorhood and salvific ultimacy, which is a narrower and stronger claim.
3. ⚠️ Do not merge agnosticism with atheism or with indifference. Suspension of judgement is an epistemic stance with reasons behind it.

#### MINI RECAP

Four positions — path, existence, knowledge, absence of question. One firewall — non-theistic ≠ atheistic. One coinage to date correctly — Huxley, 1869. One rule — religion survives non-theism, not the loss of every sacred order.

### SESSION 5 — BUDDHISM I: THE DIAGNOSIS THAT NEEDS NO CREATOR

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Buddhism explains why life goes wrong and how it can be put right without any appeal to a being who made the world.

**Technical definition.** Early Buddhism does not make a creator God part of its diagnosis or its path. **Dependent origination** (*pratītyasamutpāda*) and **karma** explain conditioned arising, while gods and *Brahmā* figures remain impermanent beings inside the round of rebirth (*saṃsāra*) rather than creators or final saviours.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Buddhism does not refute a creator; it renders one idle — suffering arises dependently from craving and ignorance, and removing the conditions removes the suffering, so a maker adds no explanatory and no practical step to the sequence.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) · karma · Four Noble Truths · suffering (*duḥkha*) · craving (*tṛṣṇā*) · undeclared questions (*avyākata*) · *deva* · *Brahmā***

**How to use them:** the phrase that earns the mark is **"explanatorily idle"**. Do not write that Buddhism disproves God; write that the creator hypothesis contributes no necessary step to the causal-soteriological sequence, and then show the sequence.

#### VISUAL — THE CAUSAL-SOTERIOLOGICAL SEQUENCE, WITH THE VACANT SLOT

```text
   │  no step of the     │     │  in the cosmology, but they are  │
```

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The structure of the argument.** Suffering arises dependently from craving and ignorance; removing the conditions removes the suffering. A creator adds no explanatory and no practical step to this causal-soteriological sequence. The claim is one of **redundancy**, not of refutation, and the difference is examinable: the printed stems ask whether religion is possible without God, not whether God has been disproved.

**[FACT] The presupposition, stated honestly.** The Buddhist case presupposes that **dependent causation is explanatorily sufficient for the religious problem Buddhism addresses**. It does not claim to have shown that dependent causation explains everything whatever. Stating the presupposition is what distinguishes an exposition from a slogan.

**[FACT] The undeclared questions, at their real scope.** The Buddha's refusal to centre certain speculative questions (*avyākata*) is not a blanket claim that no divine beings exist. It concerns specific issues whose settlement does not advance the path. The **poisoned-arrow parable** prioritises removal of suffering over metaphysical delay; use it as a practical illustration of urgency, not as a proof of atheism.

**[FACT] The gods are inside the system, not above it.** Where cosmological gods are accepted, they remain impermanent beings within the round of rebirth. The *Brahmajāla Sutta* (DN 1) and the *Aggañña Sutta* (DN 27) are the standard text anchors for the non-creator position, and they work by relocating the would-be creator inside the conditioned order rather than by denying that he exists.

**Objection.** Karma and the moral order function exactly as providence functions: they guarantee that deeds meet their deserts, so a hidden divine administration has merely been renamed.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The reply is the same one that answers the hidden-God objection generally. Causal-moral regularity is impersonal: nothing selects, forgives, rewards out of love, or answers. An omnipotent allocator would add features — intention, discretion, responsiveness — that the Buddhist account not only lacks but positively excludes.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Buddhism is a religion without God because its diagnosis and its cure are complete without one.
> **Named evidence:** The Four Noble Truths identify suffering, its origin in craving and ignorance, its cessation and the path; dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) supplies the causal machinery; the *Brahmajāla* (DN 1) and *Aggañña* (DN 27) Suttas place *Brahmā* figures inside the conditioned order.
> **Analysis:** The creator hypothesis therefore contributes no step to either the explanation or the therapy, which is a claim of redundancy rather than of refutation and is correspondingly easier to defend.
> **Qualification:** The argument presupposes that dependent causation suffices for the specific problem Buddhism addresses, and that presupposition — not a demonstration of God's non-existence — is what the theist must contest.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not present the undeclared questions as the whole Buddhist case. They have a restricted scope, and over-reading them turns a strong position into an evasion.
2. ⚠️ Do not use the poisoned-arrow parable as proof of atheism. It illustrates urgency and the priority of the cure.
3. ⚠️ Do not write that Buddhism "disproves" a creator. Write that it makes one explanatorily idle, and then show the sequence that does the work.

#### MINI RECAP

Four truths, one causal engine — dependent origination. One vacancy — no step needs a maker. One scope control — the undeclared questions are narrow. Two text anchors — DN 1 and DN 27.

### SESSION 6 — BUDDHISM II: A COMPLETE RELIGION, AND THE DEVOTIONAL COMPLICATION

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Having shown that Buddhism needs no creator, this session shows that what remains is not a philosophy but a full religion — and then deals honestly with the fact that Buddhists worship.

**Technical definition.** Buddhism possesses the **Four Noble Truths**, the **Noble Eightfold Path**, moral discipline (*śīla*), meditation, the **community** (*Saṅgha*) and a soteriology (**nirvāṇa**, liberation from suffering). Teaching (*Dharma*), community and liberation organise doctrine, practice and value **without functioning as one-for-one substitutes for a personal creator**.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Buddhism is a strong actual counterexample to the claim that a personal creator is necessary — provided religion is defined multidimensionally rather than stipulated as God-relation — because it supplies a complete diagnosis, a disciplined path, an ethic, a community and a final goal.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**Noble Eightfold Path · moral discipline (*śīla*) · meditation · community (*Saṅgha*) · *nirvāṇa* · teaching (*Dharma*) · bodhisattva · devotional Mahāyāna**

**How to use them:** run the cluster from Session 2 against Buddhism item by item — doctrine, ritual, ethics, experience, community, soteriology — and tick each one. That is the 20-mark structure for the 2024 stem, and it converts a list of names into an argument.

#### VISUAL — THE CLUSTER TEST APPLIED TO BUDDHISM

| Cluster element | What Buddhism supplies | Does it need a creator? |
|---|---|---|
| Doctrine | Four Noble Truths; dependent origination; impermanence and non-self | No |
| Ritual and practice | meditation; recitation; monastic observance; later devotional (*pūjā*) forms | No |
| Ethical formation | moral discipline (*śīla*); right speech, action and livelihood | No |
| Experience | staged meditative attainment; insight | No |
| Community | the *Saṅgha*, with rule, ordination and transmission | No |
| Soteriological goal | *nirvāṇa* — liberation from suffering | No |
| Sacred order | *Dharma* as the order that the path discloses and conforms to | No |

```text
   THE DEVOTIONAL COMPLICATION — HOW TO HANDLE IT
   Later MAHĀYĀNA devotion to Buddhas and bodhisattvas is real, widespread and
   religiously serious. Two temptations, both wrong:
     ✘  "so Buddhism became theistic"        (function mistaken for ontology)
     ✘  "so it must be a later corruption"   (an evidential claim you cannot support)
   THE CORRECT LINE:
     ✔  Buddhas and bodhisattvas may perform religious FUNCTIONS — refuge,

        disarms the objection instead of conceding it.
```

*Read the table as the cluster test being discharged row by row, and the box for the one complication that every good answer on this stem must meet.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] What Buddhism has.** It has the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, ethics (*śīla*), meditation, the *Saṅgha* and a soteriology in *nirvāṇa* — liberation from suffering (*duḥkha*). Every element of the working cluster is present, and none of them is borrowed from theism.

**[FACT] What organises it.** Teaching (*Dharma*), the community (*Saṅgha*) and liberation (*nirvāṇa*) organise doctrine, practice and value. The owner's qualification is essential and must be reproduced: they do **not** function as one-for-one substitutes for a personal creator. Saying that *Dharma* "takes the place of God" is precisely the sentence the hidden-God objection needs.

**[FACT] The devotional development.** Later Mahāyāna devotion to Buddhas and bodhisattvas confirms that non-theistic does not mean devotion-free. The reply to the theist is not to deny the devotion but to deny the inference: performing a religious function is not the same as occupying the creator role.

**[INFERENCE] The 20-mark shape.** For the 2024 stem — *state and evaluate* — the statement must be complete before the evaluation begins. State the non-creator diagnosis (Session 5) and the complete religious structure (this session); then evaluate by conceding the devotional complication and the petition cost, and close with a graded verdict. Evaluating a caricature is the standing fault on "state and evaluate" stems.

**Objection.** If Buddhas can be prayed to, sacrificed to and relied on for assistance, the tradition has readmitted through practice precisely what it excluded in doctrine, and the counterexample is compromised.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The doctrinal exclusion was specific: no maker of the world, no indispensable saviour, no omnipotent allocator. Devotional practice adds none of those. What it does show is that religion needs an **object of reverence**, and that a non-theistic tradition supplies one — which supports the cluster definition rather than undermining it.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Buddhism is not merely compatible with the definition of religion; it satisfies every element of it.
> **Named evidence:** The Four Noble Truths supply doctrine, the Eightfold Path supplies disciplined practice, moral discipline (*śīla*) supplies ethical formation, meditation supplies experience, the *Saṅgha* supplies community, and *nirvāṇa* supplies the soteriological goal.
> **Analysis:** Since the cluster is discharged without a creator at any point, the necessity claim fails against an actual and historically continuous tradition rather than against a hypothetical one.
> **Qualification:** Later devotional Mahāyāna shows that this is not a religion stripped of reverence, but the reverence is directed to awakened exemplars rather than to a maker, so the counterexample survives with its devotional texture intact.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not say that *Dharma*, *Saṅgha* and *nirvāṇa* "replace" God one for one. Say what work each does and what work it does not do.
2. ⚠️ Do not treat devotional Mahāyāna as either an embarrassment or a refutation. It is evidence that non-theism is compatible with devotion.
3. ⚠️ On a "state and evaluate" stem, do not begin evaluating before the statement is complete. Half the marks are in the exposition.

#### MINI RECAP

Seven cluster elements, all discharged. One qualification — not one-for-one substitutes. One complication met head-on — devotional Mahāyāna performs functions without installing a creator.

### SESSION 7 — JAINISM: CREATOR-DENIAL, AND AN ACHIEVED RATHER THAN A GOVERNING DIVINITY

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Jainism rejects a creator outright, yet keeps liberated perfected beings whom it venerates — which makes it the sharpest test of whether "worship" requires a God.

**Technical definition.** Jainism accepts **perfected liberated beings** but **no creator**. Souls (*jīva*) are eternal and innumerable; bondage is by **karmic matter**; release is achieved through vows and asceticism into the state of the perfected (*siddha*).

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The Jain liberated being is an *achieved* perfection, not a *governing* one: it is venerated as an exemplar of what a soul may become, and precisely because it neither creates nor grants, it shows that veneration is separable from petition.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**soul (*jīva*) · non-soul (*ajīva*) · karmic matter · vows · ford-maker (*tīrthaṅkara*) · perfected being (*siddha*) · passionless (*vītarāga*) · motive argument · embodiment argument**

**How to use them:** the two arguments — **motive** and **embodiment** — are the examinable core. Name them as arguments, not as opinions, and note that the same argument-family recurs in Kumārila's *Ślokavārttika* against a bodiless agent acting on matter.

#### VISUAL — THE TWO ARGUMENTS, AND THE STATUS OF THE LIBERATED BEING

```text
   THE JAIN CASE AGAINST A CREATOR
   ┌────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┐
   │  1. THE MOTIVE ARGUMENT        │  2. THE EMBODIMENT ARGUMENT       │
   │  A perfect being lacks any     │  Action on matter requires a body │
   │  unfulfilled desire.           │  or instrument.                   │
   │  Creation would answer a need. │  A body is itself made and        │
   │  A perfect being has no need.  │  limited.                         │
   │  ∴ no motive to create.        │  ∴ an embodied creator is not     │
   │                                │    the unlimited creator claimed. │
   └────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘
             SAME ARGUMENT FAMILY: Kumārila's Ślokavārttika
             against a bodiless agent acting upon matter.

   WHAT REPLACES THE CREATOR                WHAT IS VENERATED
   ┌───────────────────────────┐            ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │ eternal souls (jīva)      │            │ tīrthaṅkara — the        │
   │ + non-soul (ajīva)        │            │ ford-maker who TEACHES   │
   │ + KARMIC MATTER that      │            │ the path                 │
   │   binds and is shed       │  ────────► │ siddha — the perfected   │
   │ + VOWS and asceticism     │            │ soul, passionless        │

                                            └──────────────────────────┘
   THE DECISIVE LINE: an ACHIEVED divinity, never a GOVERNING one.
```

*Read the top pair as the two arguments; read the bottom pair for the machinery that replaces a creator and the object that survives as worshipful.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The doctrine.** Jainism accepts perfected liberated beings but no creator. The Jain argument presses both pressures at once: **a perfect being lacks the motive to create**, and **an embodied creator would be limited** by the very embodiment that action on matter requires. These are arguments with premises, and they should be written as such.

**[FACT] The replacement machinery.** Order and moral consequence are carried by **karmic matter** binding the soul (*jīva*), by the vows that arrest the inflow, and by asceticism that sheds what is already bound. Liberation is the soul's own achievement. Nothing in the sequence requires a supervising intelligence.

**[FACT] The status of the venerated object.** The ford-maker (*tīrthaṅkara*) teaches the path; the perfected being (*siddha*) has completed it. Neither creates, governs, hears or grants. This is exactly why Jainism is the sharpest case in the clause: it retains full-blooded veneration while removing every relational function that veneration is usually assumed to require.

**[INFERENCE] Why the theist's best objection lands here.** A theist will say that Jain veneration of a passionless liberated being is "really commemoration, not worship", and therefore that Jainism is a philosophy with ceremonies. The reply is that commemoration of a human exemplar does not carry vows, ritual calendars, temple practice, ascetic discipline and a soteriological goal; a category that excludes this is tracking theism rather than religion.

**Objection.** Jain liberated beings are simply a plurality of gods, so Jainism is polytheistic rather than non-theistic and cannot serve as a counterexample.
**Reply.** **[FACT/INFERENCE]** The objection mistakes perfection for divinity in the relevant sense. A *siddha* has no world-making function, no providential function, no capacity to alter another soul's karma and no responsiveness. What is denied in this clause is a **creator**, and the Jain position denies one explicitly and argues for the denial.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Jainism rejects a creator by argument rather than by silence, and remains a complete religion afterwards.
> **Named evidence:** The motive argument holds that a perfect being has no unfulfilled desire that creating could satisfy; the embodiment argument holds that acting on matter requires a body, which is itself limited; the same argument-family appears in Kumārila's *Ślokavārttika*.
> **Analysis:** Because bondage by karmic matter, the vows and ascetic discipline carry the whole moral-causal and soteriological load, the removal of a creator leaves the religious structure intact.
> **Qualification:** The liberated perfected being (*siddha*) must be described as an achieved and not a governing perfection, since treating it as a substitute deity would concede exactly the hidden-God objection the Jain case is best placed to defeat.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not conflate Jain liberated beings with a creator God. The difference between an achieved and a governing perfection is the whole point.
2. ⚠️ Do not state the Jain position as a bare denial. Give the motive argument and the embodiment argument, with their premises.
3. ⚠️ Do not concede that Jain veneration is "merely commemoration". Answer by pointing to vows, ritual, asceticism and the soteriological goal.

#### MINI RECAP

Two arguments — motive and embodiment. One replacement machinery — souls, karmic matter, vows, asceticism. One decisive formula — achieved divinity, never governing divinity.

### SESSION 8 — MĪMĀṂSĀ I: AUTHORLESS SCRIPTURE, INJUNCTION AND INTRINSIC VALIDITY

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Classical Mīmāṃsā keeps scripture, ritual, duty and merit — and removes the author of the scripture, so that no divine speaker is needed to make it authoritative.

**Technical definition.** The Veda is **authorless** (*apauruṣeya*) — hence eternal, hence not dependent on any speaker's reliability. Its injunctions (*codanā*) are **self-validating** (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*), and dharma is defined by injunction alone.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Mīmāṃsā removes God not by denying him but by making him unnecessary: an authorless scripture needs no speaker whose trustworthiness must first be established, so the entire argument from the reliability of a divine author has nothing left to work on.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**How to use them:** the sentence that separates a top script is **"*apauruṣeya* is not revelation"**. Write it, explain why, and the Mīmāṃsā counterexample becomes unanswerable at the definitional level.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The strongest ritual counterexample.** Classical Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā is a **ritual and scriptural religious order in which no creator God performs the main explanatory work**. It keeps scripture, ritual, duty, merit and a soteriological promise, and relocates authority and efficacy elsewhere.

**[FACT] The definition of dharma.** Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 defines dharma as *codanā-lakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ* — that which is **characterised by injunction** and is beneficial. The epistemic consequence is the reason for citing it: dharma is known **only** from injunction, not from perception, inference or any deity's command. Quoting the sūtra without that consequence earns nothing.

**[FACT] Why this removes God, part one.** No author is needed for the Veda, so Udayana's arguments from the fact that it is a sentence (*vākyāt*), from its being believed (*pratyayataḥ*) and from its being scripture (*śruteḥ*) have no purchase. The theistic proof-strategy is not refuted point by point; its premise is withdrawn.

**[FACT] The deities are not agents.** Deities named in the mantras function as **elements within the ritual structure** — the dative to which the oblation is offered — not as beings who hear, choose and grant. This is the point at which a careless script surrenders the counterexample by describing Vedic religion as worship of gods who answer.

**[FACT] Kumārila argues the negative case explicitly.** The *Ślokavārttika* contains sustained arguments against a creator God, including the **motive problem** and the **impossibility of a bodiless agent acting on matter** — the same argument-family as the Jain arguments of Session 7.

**Objection.** How can a beginningless, authorless text have determinate meaning at all? Meaning is conventional, and conventions are instituted by speakers.
**Reply.** **[FACT/INFERENCE]** Mīmāṃsā answers that meaning is carried by the **eternal relation of word and referent** (*śabdārtha-sambandha-nityatva*), which Nyāya denies as conventional. The dispute is genuine and should be stated as a dispute, not decided by assertion; but it shows the school has an answer rather than an oversight.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Mīmāṃsā secures scriptural authority without a divine speaker.
> **Named evidence:** The Veda is authorless (*apauruṣeya*) and its injunctions are intrinsically valid (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*); Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 defines dharma as what is characterised by injunction and is beneficial; the deities named in mantras are the dative of the offering rather than agents who respond.
> **Analysis:** Every function a divine author would have discharged — guaranteeing reliability, validating authority and fixing meaning — is therefore reassigned inside the system, so the theistic argument loses its premise rather than its conclusion.
> **Qualification:** The meaning problem remains a live dispute with Nyāya, and the honest form of the claim is that Mīmāṃsā has a determinate answer to it, not that the answer is uncontested.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not describe the Mīmāṃsā Veda as "revelation". It is **authorless**, therefore not the speech of any God, and the word "revelation" reinstates the speaker the school has removed.
2. ⚠️ Do not present the mantra-deities as recipients who answer. They are structural elements of the rite.
3. ⚠️ Do not quote *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 as decoration. Quote it for the epistemic consequence that dharma is knowable only from injunction.

#### MINI RECAP

Three jobs an author would do — reliability, validation, meaning. Three devices that retire them — authorlessness, intrinsic validity, eternal word-referent relation. One sūtra, quoted for its consequence: 1.1.2.

### SESSION 9 — MĪMĀṂSĀ II: UNSEEN POTENCY, THE TWO SCHOOLS, AND THE NYĀYA OBJECTIONS

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** If a ritual performed today produces a result years later or after death, something must carry the connection — and Mīmāṃsā says that something is a potency generated by the act, not a god keeping accounts.

**Technical definition.** **Unseen potency** (*apūrva*, "the not-before") is a **postulate** (*arthāpatti*) introduced to explain the delayed fruit of an injunction. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa treats it as a **power** (*śakti*) residing in the act or agent; Prabhākara Miśra reframes the matter around the **ought-to-be-done** (*niyoga*/*kārya*) disclosed by the injunction.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Mīmāṃsā shows that the functions God performs — authority, efficacy and desert — are separable and can each be secured otherwise: authority by an authorless Veda, efficacy and desert by a postulated potency that operates as law rather than as administration.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**unseen potency (*apūrva*) · postulation (*arthāpatti*) · power (*śakti*) · ought-to-be-done (*niyoga*/*kārya*) · Kumārila Bhaṭṭa · Prabhākara Miśra · superintendent of karma (*karmādhyakṣa*) · law-like versus deliberative**

**How to use them:** state the five steps of the argument, then the two-school split, then the Nyāya objection and its reply. A named potency without the perished-cause problem behind it reads as vocabulary rather than argument.

#### VISUAL — THE FIVE-STEP ARGUMENT, THE SPLIT, AND THE THREE OBJECTIONS

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The argument.** A Vedic injunction promises a result that does not follow immediately upon the act; the act is momentary and perishes; a perished cause cannot produce a later effect; therefore the act must generate an unseen potency that persists until the result matures. That potency is *apūrva* — "the not-before", something new that did not previously exist.

**[FACT] Its epistemic status.** It is a **postulate** (*arthāpatti*), not a perceived entity. Mīmāṃsā's own epistemology licenses postulation to explain an otherwise inexplicable fact. Treating it as something observed is a listed trap, and treating it as an arbitrary invention ignores that the school has a sanctioned method for introducing it.

**[FACT] The two schools, and why the split matters.** Kumārila treats the potency as a **power** (*śakti*) residing in the act or the agent and linking act to fruit. Prabhākara reframes the whole matter around **the ought-to-be-done** (*niyoga*/*kārya*) — the sheer imperative disclosed by the injunction, which motivates action without needing a promised fruit as its primary content. Prabhākara's ethics is therefore closer to a **deontology of the injunction**; Kumārila's to a **consequential mechanism of desert**.

**[FACT] Why this removes God, part two.** No dispenser of karmic fruit is needed, since the potency does that work — so the Nyāya argument that a superintendent of karma (*karmādhyakṣa*) is required is answered directly rather than ignored.

**[FACT] The three objections and replies** are set out in the visual and must be reproduced in a 15-mark answer. The third is the one candidates omit: the charge that this is administration rather than religion is answered by the cluster from Session 2 — sacred order, soteriological promise, disciplined practice, community.

**❓ Honest qualification.** Later Mīmāṃsā authors drift toward theism. The atheistic reading defended here is of the **classical** system, and saying so protects the answer from an easy correction.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Mīmāṃsā answers the desert argument that is usually thought to require a divine administrator.
> **Named evidence:** The unseen potency (*apūrva*) is postulated because a perished momentary act cannot produce a fruit that matures much later, often after death; Kumārila treats it as a power in the act, Prabhākara recentres the analysis on the ought-to-be-done; Nyāya replies that only an intelligent superintendent can allocate fruits.
> **Analysis:** The Mīmāṃsā answer is that the relation is law-like rather than deliberative, so the Nyāya objection presupposes what it needs to prove — that determinate outcomes require a selecting mind.
> **Qualification:** The atheistic reading holds of the classical system, since later authors of the school drift toward theism, and the argument should be advanced with that restriction stated.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not treat *apūrva* as an observed entity. It is a postulate (*arthāpatti*) introduced to explain a delayed fruit.
2. ⚠️ Do not merge Kumārila's power (*śakti*) version with Prabhākara's *niyoga*/*kārya* version. The split has a real ethical payoff.
3. ⚠️ Do not leave the Nyāya allocation objection unanswered. The reply is that law-like relations produce determinate outcomes without deliberation.

#### MINI RECAP

Five steps to a postulate. Two schools — power versus imperative, desert versus duty. Three objections answered — allocation, meaning, bureaucracy. One honest limit — the classical system only.

### SESSION 10 — SĀṂKHYA AND YOGA: A COSMOS WITHOUT A MAKER

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Classical Sāṃkhya explains the whole evolving world from unconscious nature working in the presence of conscious selves, and seeks release by discrimination rather than by rescue.

**Technical definition.** Classical Sāṃkhya explains the cosmos through **primordial nature** (*prakṛti*) evolving through its constituent strands (*guṇa*) in proximity to plural conscious selves (*puruṣa*), with liberation as **isolation** (*kaivalya*) attained through discriminative knowledge (*viveka-khyāti*). Yoga retains a **special self** (*Īśvara*) who is **not straightforwardly a creator** in the classical Sāṃkhya sense.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Sāṃkhya shows that a complete cosmology and a complete soteriology can be built from an unconscious principle and a plurality of conscious selves, so that the demand for a maker is a demand for an extra term the system never needed.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**primordial nature (*prakṛti*) · self (*puruṣa*) · strands (*guṇa*) · isolation (*kaivalya*) · discriminative knowledge (*viveka-khyāti*) · special self (*Īśvara*) · non-conscious purposiveness · milk-and-calf analogy**

**How to use them:** keep Sāṃkhya and Yoga in separate sentences. The single most damaging error here is importing Yoga's *Īśvara* into classical Sāṃkhya, which destroys the counterexample you have just offered.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The doctrine.** Classical Sāṃkhya explains the cosmos through nature (*prakṛti*) for the sake of the selves (*puruṣa*), without a creator. Unconscious nature evolves through the strands (*guṇa*) in proximity to the conscious self, and the goal is isolation (*kaivalya*) reached by discriminative knowledge.

**[FACT] The presupposition.** Impersonal law or dual principles can explain order and liberation. That is a substantive philosophical commitment, not a neutral observation, and an answer that states it as a presupposition rather than smuggling it in reads as controlled.

**[FACT] The Yoga distinction.** Yoga's special self (*Īśvara*) is **not straightforwardly a creator** in the classical Sāṃkhya sense. It is a self distinguished by freedom from affliction, action and their residues, not a maker of the world; and importing it into Sāṃkhya converts a non-theistic system into a theistic one by editorial accident.

**[FACT] The canonical examples.** Karmic matter binding the soul is Jain; the Vedic injunction is Mīmāṃsā; for Sāṃkhya the standard illustration is **milk seeming to nourish the calf** — an analogy used in the discussions for non-conscious purposiveness, where an end is served without anything deliberating about it.

**[FACT] The Nyāya objection and the reply.** Nyāya argues that unconscious principles cannot organise ends. The non-theistic schools reply that **observed regularity need not be modelled on artefact production**. That reply is the general form of the answer to design arguments inside this clause, and it also serves Buddhism and Mīmāṃsā.

**Objection.** Sāṃkhya's nature acts "for the sake of" the self, which is teleological language; teleology without a mind is either incoherent or a disguised creator.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The reply is the milk analogy generalised: purposive-looking regularity is observed in cases where no deliberation occurs, so the inference from apparent purpose to a deliberating agent is not compulsory. The Sāṃkhya position is that this is the primitive fact about nature; the theist is free to deny it, but must then argue for the artefact model rather than assume it.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Classical Sāṃkhya supplies a complete non-theistic cosmology and soteriology.
> **Named evidence:** Nature (*prakṛti*) evolves through its three strands in proximity to plural conscious selves (*puruṣa*); the predicament is misidentification; the release is isolation (*kaivalya*) attained by discriminative knowledge; Yoga adds a special self (*Īśvara*) who is not a creator in the Sāṃkhya sense.
> **Analysis:** Because both the world-process and the release are accounted for without a maker, the demand for one is a demand for an additional term with no explanatory work to do.
> **Qualification:** Nyāya's objection that unconscious principles cannot organise ends is genuine, and the reply — that observed regularity need not be modelled on artefact production — is a rejection of the design model rather than a refutation of it.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not import Yoga's *Īśvara* into classical Sāṃkhya. Yoga's special self is not straightforwardly a creator, and Sāṃkhya has none at all.
2. ⚠️ Do not describe isolation (*kaivalya*) as the destruction of nature. It is the self's release from entanglement with it.
3. ⚠️ Do not leave the Nyāya design pressure unanswered; the artefact-model reply is what converts exposition into evaluation.

#### MINI RECAP

Two principles, one evolution, one release. One distinction to protect — Yoga's special self is not Sāṃkhya's creator, because Sāṃkhya has none. One reply to design — regularity is not artefact production.

### SESSION 11 — CĀRVĀKA I: PERCEPTION ALONE, AND THE ATTACK ON CONCOMITANCE

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Cārvāka holds that the only reliable way of knowing anything is seeing it, and then argues that reasoning from what we see to what we cannot see never delivers certainty.

**Technical definition.** Cārvāka (Lokāyata) holds that **perception** (*pratyakṣa*) is the only valid means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*). Consequently nothing supersensible can be known. Reality is the **four gross elements** (*bhūtacatuṣṭaya*: earth, water, fire, air; ether is rejected as imperceptible), and the human ends reduce to pleasure (*kāma*) and wealth (*artha*).

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The Cārvāka critique is epistemological before it is anything else: it is an argument about what a means of knowledge must deliver, and the hedonism that the tradition is remembered for is a consequence of that argument, not its substance.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**perception (*pratyakṣa*) · means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*) · inference (*anumāna*) · concomitance (*vyāpti*) · mark (*hetu*) · inferred (*sādhya*) · conditioning factor (*upādhi*) · deviation (*vyabhicāra*) · four elements (*bhūtacatuṣṭaya*)**

**How to use them:** the examinable core is the **concomitance problem**, and it has four moves — not by perception, not by inference, not by testimony, and the residual conditioning-factor problem. Give all four or the argument has not been given.

#### VISUAL — THE FOUR CLOSED ROUTES TO CONCOMITANCE

```text
   WHY ONLY PERCEPTION?
   1  a means of knowledge must yield CERTAINTY about its object
   2  perception is self-evident and immediately certain
   3  every other candidate — inference, comparison, testimony, postulation —
      is either reducible to perception or depends on a general rule that
      perception cannot certify
   4  ∴ perception alone is a pramāṇa, and knowledge does not extend
      beyond the perceptible

   THE ATTACK ON INFERENCE
   inference (anumāna) depends on VYĀPTI — an INVARIABLE, UNCONDITIONAL
   concomitance between the mark (hetu: smoke) and the inferred (sādhya: fire).
   CĀRVĀKA'S QUESTION: by what means is vyāpti ITSELF known?
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ (1) NOT BY PERCEPTION  — perception grasps only particular            │
   │     co-presences, here and now. Vyāpti is UNIVERSAL, covering         │
   │     unobserved past, future and remote cases. No perception reaches   │
   │     those.                                                            │
   │ (2) NOT BY INFERENCE   — that inference needs its own vyāpti:         │
   │     INFINITE REGRESS or CIRCULARITY.                                  │
   │ (3) NOT BY TESTIMONY   — testimony's authority would itself have to   │
   │     be established, and for the Cārvāka it is not a pramāṇa at all.   │
   │ (4) THE UPĀDHI PROBLEM — even repeated observation cannot rule out    │
   │     an unnoticed CONDITIONING FACTOR making the concomitance hold     │
   │     only contingently. Standard example: fire is accompanied by       │
   │     smoke ONLY WHEN THE FUEL IS WET. Unless every possible upādhi     │
   │     is excluded — and that exclusion is itself unverifiable — the     │
   │     concomitance remains merely PROBABLE: vyabhicāra stays possible.  │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   CONCLUSION: inference cannot deliver certainty. Where it works in daily
   life it works by luck or habit; and in the supersensible domain — God,
   soul, karma, afterlife — no perceptual check is EVER available, so there
   inference is worthless.
```

*Read the top block for why perception alone, the boxed block for the four closed routes, and the closing lines for the domain-restricted conclusion that is the school's real thesis.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The ontology.** Reality is the four gross elements — earth, water, fire and air. **Ether is rejected because it is imperceptible**, which is not an arbitrary omission but a direct consequence of the epistemology. The four human ends reduce to pleasure (*kāma*) and wealth (*artha*), because the other two require a supersensible order to be intelligible.

**[FACT] Why only perception.** The argument runs in four steps, given in the visual. Its force is that it does not merely assert a preference for the senses: it sets a **certainty condition** on what counts as a means of knowledge and then argues that only perception meets it.

**[FACT] The concomitance problem is the examinable core.** Inference depends on an invariable, unconditional concomitance between the mark and the inferred. The Cārvāka asks how that concomitance is itself known, and closes the three obvious routes in turn. This is the passage a 10-mark answer on the 2025 stem must contain.

**[FACT] The conditioning-factor residue.** Even repeated observation cannot exclude an unnoticed conditioning factor (*upādhi*) that makes the concomitance hold only contingently — the standard example being that fire is accompanied by smoke only when the fuel is wet. Unless every possible conditioning factor is excluded, and that exclusion is itself unverifiable, deviation (*vyabhicāra*) remains possible and the concomitance remains merely probable.

**[INFERENCE] The shape of the conclusion.** Notice that the conclusion is domain-restricted in its own statement: inference "works by luck or habit" in daily life, and is worthless precisely where no perceptual check is available. That restriction is what makes the moderate reading of the school available in Session 12.

**Objection.** The certainty condition is too strong. No source of knowledge, including perception, delivers indubitable certainty, so the argument proves too much.
**Reply.** **[FACT/INFERENCE]** This is the **parity argument**, and it is one of the standard replies: perception itself faces illusion, dream and defective organs, and is corrected only by further cognition. The Cārvāka must either weaken the certainty condition — in which case inference re-enters — or accept that perception fails its own test. That dilemma is the cleanest single criticism of the strong thesis.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** The Cārvāka critique of belief in suprasensible entities is an argument about the means of knowledge, not a set of slogans about pleasure.
> **Named evidence:** Perception (*pratyakṣa*) is held to be the only means of knowledge; inference depends on an invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*) between mark and inferred; that concomitance cannot be reached by perception, since it is universal, nor by inference, on pain of regress or circularity, nor by testimony; and an unnoticed conditioning factor (*upādhi*), such as wet fuel, can never be conclusively excluded.
> **Analysis:** The conclusion is therefore that inference yields probability rather than certainty, and that in the supersensible domain, where no perceptual check exists, it yields nothing at all.
> **Qualification:** The certainty condition on which the argument rests is contestable, since perception is itself corrigible, and the parity objection is the point at which the strong version of the thesis begins to fail.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not state the Cārvāka position on inference without the concomitance problem. The argument is the four closed routes, not the conclusion.
2. ⚠️ Do not omit the conditioning factor. Repeated observation without the exclusion problem is not the Cārvāka case.
3. ⚠️ Do not make hedonism the substance of a 2025-style answer. The exam wants the epistemology; the ethics is a consequence.

#### MINI RECAP

One certainty condition, four closed routes, one residue. The conclusion is domain-restricted in its own wording. The parity objection is where the strong thesis first cracks.

### SESSION 12 — CĀRVĀKA II: THE APPLICATION TO RELIGION, THE REPLIES, AND THE LIMITING-CASE VERDICT

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Once nothing beyond the senses can be known, everything religion trades in goes: God, soul, karma, heaven, rebirth, ritual merit, liberation and the authority of scripture.

**Technical definition.** The epistemology is applied to remove God, the immaterial soul, unseen merit (*adṛṣṭa*), heaven and hell, rebirth, ritual potency (*apūrva*), liberation and Vedic authority. Consciousness becomes an **emergent property of the body** (*dehātmavāda*), and order is explained by things acting according to **their own nature** (*svabhāva-vāda*).

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The emergence thesis and its analogy.** Consciousness is an emergent property of the body. The standard analogy is the intoxicating power (*madaśakti*) that arises when fermenting ingredients are combined although present in none of them singly. Because there is no separate conscious substance, there is no survival, no transmigration and no liberation — the soteriological apparatus is removed in one step.

**[FACT] Order without design.** Things behave according to their own nature (*svabhāva-vāda*): the sharpness of a thorn and the colour of a peacock require no maker. This is the Cārvāka counterpart of the Sāṃkhya reply to design, and it is stronger because it declines the supersensible altogether.

Treat them as **reported Cārvāka positions**, not as verified primary text, and never present the popular hedonist couplet as anything more than a doxographic quotation.

**[FACT] The five replies must all appear in an evaluation.** Self-refutation, the pragmatic argument, Nyāya's positive defence of concomitance, parity, and the reported moderate strand. Leaving the self-refutation charge unanswered forfeits the evaluation marks; the two rejoinders are the dialectical reading and the Purandara strand.

**[FACT] The verdict formula.** "Cārvāka's critique succeeds exactly as far as its epistemology does. **[INFERENCE] Why the classification matters so much here.** Candidates reach for Cārvāka as the most obvious "godless Indian school" and thereby lose the clause, because absence of God is not by itself the criterion of anything. Placing Cārvāka correctly is the fastest available demonstration that you know what the criterion is.

**Objection.** If the moderate reading is adopted, Cārvāka is no longer radical: it becomes ordinary empiricism, and the school's distinctive place in the clause disappears.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The moderate reading is *more* damaging, not less. Ordinary empiricism plus a domain restriction is exactly what closes every inference-based natural theology, and it does so without the self-refutation that makes the strong thesis easy to dismiss. The radicalism migrates from the epistemology to its consequences.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Cārvāka marks the boundary of this clause rather than supplying an instance of it.
> **Named evidence:** Consciousness is treated as an emergent property of the body (*dehātmavāda*), illustrated by the intoxicating power arising from combined ingredients; order is explained by own-nature (*svabhāva-vāda*); the school has no soteriology, no sacred, no community of practice and no ultimate concern.
> **Analysis:** Buddhism, Jainism and Mīmāṃsā therefore show that religion survives the loss of God, while Cārvāka shows that it does not survive the closure of the route to every supersensible object.
> **Qualification:** On the reported moderate strand associated with Purandara the school admits ordinary inference and denies it only for what lies beyond the senses; the evidence for that strand is fragmentary and reported by opponents, so it must be flagged as a reported reading rather than the settled position.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not call Cārvāka a "religion without God". It has no soteriology, no sacred and no practice; it is the limiting case.
2. ❓ Do not quote the Bṛhaspati verses or the hedonist couplet as primary text. They survive only in opponents' doxographies.
3. ⚠️ Do not leave the self-refutation objection standing. Give the dialectical reading and the reported Purandara strand, with the evidence flagged.

#### MINI RECAP

Five applications, five replies, one verdict formula. The strong thesis is self-refuting; the moderate thesis is unrefuted and fatal to natural theology. And Cārvāka is the limit, not the example.

### SESSION 13 — WESTERN CRITIQUES OF THEISTIC RELIGION: FEUERBACH, MARX, FREUD, COMTE

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** A family of nineteenth- and twentieth-century critics argued that religion is not about God at all — it is about human beings, and it can be explained by what humans want, fear or suffer.

**Technical definition.** These are **explanatory critiques** of theistic religion: Feuerbach's **projection** thesis, Marx's account of religion as consolation masking exploitation, Freud's account of it as **illusion** and wish-fulfilment, and Comte's proposal of a positive **religion of humanity** to occupy the vacated place.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The projection critiques do not prove that God does not exist; they claim that belief in God can be fully explained without him — and it is that explanatory claim, not an atheistic conclusion, which they must be assessed for.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**projection · "theology is anthropology" · "the opium of the people" · illusion · wish-fulfilment · cosmic father-projection · religion of humanity · secular humanism**

**How to use them:** cite each by work, never by an invented year, and mark each as a **genetic** explanation. Then say what a genetic explanation can and cannot establish — that is the analytical move the examiner is buying.

#### VISUAL — FOUR CRITIQUES, ONE STRUCTURE, ONE LIMIT

|---|---|---|---|
| **Feuerbach** | "Theology is anthropology" — God is the **projection** of humanity's own idealised essence | human self-alienation | *The Essence of Christianity* |
| **Marx** | religion is "the **opium of the people**", a consoling illusion masking economic exploitation; it dissolves with a just society | material conditions | Introduction to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*, 1843–44 |
| **Freud** | religion is an **illusion**, a wish-fulfilment and cosmic father-projection | infantile helplessness and desire | Freud's writings on religion as illusion |

```text
   1  identify a HUMAN need, fear, want or condition
   2  show that belief in God would be produced by it ANYWAY
```

*Read the table for the four positions and their named sources, and the box for the single evaluative move that must accompany all four.*

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] Feuerbach.** "Theology is anthropology" — God is the projection of humanity's own idealised essence. The predicates traditionally assigned to God — love, wisdom, power — are human predicates, magnified and then set over against their owners. The critique is therefore an account of **self-alienation** as much as of belief.

**[FACT] Marx.** Religion is "the opium of the people": a consoling illusion masking economic exploitation, which will dissolve with a just society. If the fuller sentence is quoted it is markedly more sympathetic than the slogan, and the source is the Introduction to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1843–44).

**[FACT] Freud.** Religion is an illusion — a wish-fulfilment and a cosmic father-projection. "Illusion" in his technical use marks a belief held because it is wished for, which is a claim about its motivation rather than a direct verdict on its truth.

**[FACT] Comte.** These critiques lead toward secular humanism and a "religion of humanity": devotion to human values without a deity. Comte is the bridge from critique to construction, and he is the reason this session belongs in a clause about religion *without* God rather than in a clause about arguments against theism.

**[INFERENCE] The evaluative move.** All four share one structure and one limit. They are **genetic** explanations: they explain why the belief arises. Origin does not settle truth — a true belief may also have a psychological or economic cause. What the critiques do establish is narrower and still important: the belief is not self-authenticating, so the move from widespread belief to a real object is blocked.

**Objection.** If the projection thesis is right, then Comte's religion of humanity is equally a projection, and merely relocating devotion from God to humanity is no gain.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The reply available to Comte is that a projection becomes pathological only when its human origin is concealed; a religion of humanity is transparent about its object, so the self-alienation Feuerbach diagnoses is precisely what it removes. Whether the result is still *religion* is the question this clause then hands to Sessions 16 and 17.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** The nineteenth-century critiques explain theistic belief without appealing to its object.
> **Named evidence:** Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* treats God as the projected essence of humanity; Marx's 1843–44 Introduction calls religion the opium of the people; Freud treats it as illusion and cosmic father-projection; Comte proposes a religion of humanity in the space that is left.
> **Analysis:** Each therefore offers a genetic account, and a genetic account blocks the inference from belief to object without establishing that the object is absent.
> **Qualification:** Origin does not settle truth, so these critiques belong in this clause as evidence that religious functions are detachable from theism, not as arguments that no deity exists.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not present these as proofs of atheism. They are explanatory critiques, and their limit is the genetic fallacy.
2. ⚠️ Do not quote "opium of the people" as though it were a sneer. The fuller sentence is more sympathetic, and quoting it accurately signals control.
3. ⚠️ Do not let secular humanism crowd out the Indian cases. Comte belongs here as a bridge, and the Humanism owner develops him further.

#### MINI RECAP

Four critics, one structure — identify a need, derive the belief, dispense with the object. One limit — origin does not settle truth. One destination — Comte's religion of humanity, and the modern debate of Sessions 16 and 17.

### SESSION 14 — NIETZSCHE: DIAGNOSIS, GENEALOGY, AND WHO OWNS THE QUESTION

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Nietzsche's famous declaration is a claim about a culture, not a claim about a cosmos: the framework that made belief credible has collapsed, and the values that leaned on it are now unsupported.

**Technical definition.** "God is dead" diagnoses the collapse of the **Christian-metaphysical horizon** and the resulting danger of **nihilism**; genealogy exposes ascetic and slave-moral valuations; the response demanded is **revaluation of all values** and self-creation (the *Übermensch*).

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Nietzsche is not an argument for atheism but a diagnosis of what happens after theism: he describes a culture whose values were underwritten by a framework it no longer believes, and demands that the values be remade rather than inherited.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**"God is dead" · Christian-metaphysical horizon · nihilism · genealogy · resentment (*ressentiment*) · slave morality · revaluation of all values · *Übermensch* · post-theism**

His critique targets **religion and its morality together**, which is exactly why the printed 2025 stem belongs to the neighbouring owner.

**[FACT] The argument.** Four steps: modern truthfulness and science erode traditional belief; inherited values depended on that framework; their collapse creates nihilism; overcoming requires revaluation and self-creation. Written as four steps it is an argument; written as a slogan it is decoration.

**[FACT] The distinction that must be made.** The phrase is a **cultural diagnosis**, not a simple syllogistic proof that God does not exist. Presenting it as an empirical event, or as a bare atheist slogan, is a listed trap and is the single commonest error in Nietzsche paragraphs.

**[FACT] The canonical example.** *Ressentiment* reverses aristocratic value terms and converts weakness into moral superiority. One worked example of the genealogical method is worth more than a list of Nietzschean vocabulary.

**[FACT] Objection and reply.** Revaluation risks elitism and arbitrary power. The Nietzschean reply appeals to creative self-overcoming, though a universal normative standard remains difficult. State the difficulty; do not resolve it by assertion.

**[INFERENCE] The comparative payoff for this clause.** Nietzsche is mourning a horizon that Buddhism and Jainism never possessed. Keeping the two in separate paragraphs is what prevents an answer from treating "religion without God" as a modern Western predicament.

**Objection.** If values are genealogically conditioned, Nietzsche's own valuation of life-enhancement is equally conditioned, and the critique undercuts itself.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The Nietzschean answer is that he does not claim a view from nowhere: the revaluation is offered as an act of creation whose warrant is its capacity to affirm life, not its derivation from an external standard. The self-reference is acknowledged rather than escaped, and whether that is a defence or a concession is exactly the contested point.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Named evidence:** *The Gay Science*, section 125, presents the declaration through the madman; the genealogical method exposes *ressentiment* as a reversal of aristocratic valuation; the demand that follows is a revaluation of all values.
> **Analysis:** The claim is therefore about the credibility and consequences of a framework, so it establishes a cultural predicament rather than a metaphysical conclusion.
> **Qualification:** That predicament is specific to a culture that once possessed the theistic horizon, which is why Buddhist and Jain non-theism cannot be assimilated to it and must be argued separately.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not present "God is dead" as an empirical event or a bare atheist slogan. It is a cultural diagnosis with a four-step argument behind it.
2. ⚠️ Do not import the full genealogical dossier here. The printed stem coupling religion with morality is owned by the Religion and Morality clause.
3. ⚠️ Do not use Nietzsche as the general spokesman for religion without God. Post-theism and non-theism are different conditions.

#### MINI RECAP

### SESSION 15 — AGNOSTICISM: HUXLEY, THE TWO STRENGTHS, AND THE PRINTED DOUBLE DELIVERABLE

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Agnosticism is not a weak atheism. It is a claim about what can be known, and it leaves a surprising amount of religious life available.

**Technical definition.** Agnosticism is the claim that the existence of God is **unknown or unknowable**, so judgement should be suspended. **Weak agnosticism** says the matter is *not known*; **strong agnosticism** says it is *unknowable*. It is distinct from atheism, which *denies* God.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> For the agnostic, religion and God come apart: the ethical and experiential dimensions of religious life can be affirmed while assent on the existence of God is withheld, which supports a minimal or non-theistic religiosity rather than a rejection of religion.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**agnosticism · T. H. Huxley (1869) · weak agnosticism · strong agnosticism · suspension of judgement · practical faith · the Unknowable · graded religiosity**

**How to use them:** the printed 2023 stem has **two** deliverables — *what is agnosticism* and *how do agnostics conceptualise the relation between religion and God*. Answer both, in that order, and signpost the transition.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The definition and the coinage.** Agnosticism is the claim that the existence of God is unknown or unknowable, so judgement should be suspended. The word was coined by **T. H. Huxley in 1869**. It is distinct from atheism, which denies God's existence.

**[FACT] The subdivision.** Weak agnosticism ("not known") differs from strong agnosticism ("unknowable"). The difference is not verbal: the weak form is hostage to future evidence, the strong form is a claim about the limits of human knowledge and is therefore itself a substantial epistemological thesis needing defence.

**[FACT] The relation between religion and God.** An agnostic may still value religion's ethical and experiential dimensions while withholding assent on God — supporting a non-theistic or minimal religiosity. The **presupposition** must be stated: religious commitment can be **graded** and need not include metaphysical certainty.

**[FACT] Adjacent positions.** Kant's practical faith and Spencer's "Unknowable" are agnostic-adjacent. Naming them shows that suspension of theoretical judgement has a philosophical pedigree and is not merely indecision.

**[FACT] The canonical example.** A person may participate in Buddhist ethical and meditative practice while withholding creator claims. This example does double duty: it illustrates graded religiosity and it links the modern Western position back to the Indian material that carries the weight of this clause.

**[FACT] Objection and reply.** The objection is that this reduces religion to ethics or aesthetics. The reply is that community, ritual, transformation and orientation to ultimacy exceed ordinary morality.

**Objection.** Strong agnosticism is self-undermining: to know that something is unknowable is already to know a great deal about it.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The strong agnostic replies that the claim is about the *reach of our faculties*, not about the object — an argument of the same family as Kant's restriction of theoretical knowledge to possible experience. That reply is available, but it converts strong agnosticism into a positive epistemology that must then be defended, which is why the weak form is the safer one to attribute in an answer unless the stem asks otherwise.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Agnosticism separates the question of God from the practice of religion instead of abolishing both.
> **Named evidence:** T. H. Huxley coined the term in 1869 for the view that God's existence is unknown or unknowable; the weak form claims only that it is not known while the strong form claims that it is unknowable; Kant's practical faith and Spencer's Unknowable are adjacent positions.
> **Analysis:** Because the suspension is epistemic, the ethical, experiential, ritual and communal dimensions of religion remain available, which is why agnosticism supports a minimal or non-theistic religiosity rather than a rejection of religion.
> **Qualification:** The reduction objection — that what survives is ethics or aesthetics — is met by pointing to community, ritual, transformation and orientation to ultimacy, though the resulting religiosity is admittedly thinner than a fully theistic one.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Distinguish agnosticism from atheism and from indifference. Suspension, denial and absence of the question are three different things.
2. ⚠️ Do not answer only the first half of the 2023 stem. The relation between religion and God is a separate printed deliverable.
3. ⚠️ Do not attribute strong agnosticism where weak agnosticism will do. The strong form is a substantial epistemological claim that needs its own defence.

#### MINI RECAP

One definition, one coinage (1869), two strengths. Two deliverables in the printed stem. One presupposition — commitment can be graded. One canonical case — practice without creator claims.

### SESSION 16 — RELIGIOUS NATURALISM: FROM DEWEY'S "THE RELIGIOUS" TO DWORKIN'S RELIGIOUS ATHEISM

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Religious naturalists say that awe, reverence, ultimacy and commitment can be directed at nature and at value, without a supernatural person anywhere in the account.

**Technical definition.** **Thesis:** ultimacy, reverence and the religious response are located **within nature**; there is no supernatural person, but nature itself sustains genuinely religious attitudes of awe, gratitude, humility and commitment.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Religious naturalism asks the sharper modern form of the question: not whether a classical tradition can do without a creator, but whether someone who accepts a fully naturalistic ontology can still be religious — and it answers by relocating ultimacy rather than abolishing it.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**religious naturalism · "religion" versus "the religious" · ideal ends · the ideal–actual relation · religious atheism · objective intrinsic meaning · the sublime universe · value realism**

**How to use them:** attach each thesis to its named work and year. This is the part of the clause where undated name-dropping is most tempting and most visible.

#### VISUAL — THE NATURALIST BENCH

### SESSION 17 — RELIGIOUS FICTIONALISM AND NON-REALISM, AND THE INDIAN GRADED-TRUTH CONTROL

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Fictionalists keep going to the temple or church without asserting that the claims are true — they participate in the practice as one enters a story, seriously but without belief.

**Technical definition.** **Thesis:** religious sentences are (or may be) **literally false or unwarranted**, yet participation in religious practice — taking the claims in a *fictive*, non-assertoric spirit — is rationally permissible and even valuable.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Fictionalism is defined not by a verdict on truth but by a refusal to assert: it grants that religious claims have truth-conditions, declines to affirm them, and continues in the practice — which is why it is distinct from error theory, from non-cognitivism, and from Advaita's graded truth.

**How to use them:** the discriminations are the marks. Write the three-way distinction (fictionalism / error theory / non-cognitivism) and then the Indian control, and the paragraph is complete.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The thesis.** Religious sentences are, or may be, literally false or unwarranted, yet participation in religious practice — taking the claims in a fictive, non-assertoric spirit — is rationally permissible and even valuable.

**[FACT] The named positions.** Don Cupitt's non-realism in *Taking Leave of God* (1980) treats God not as an objective being but as the personification of the religious ideal — a "Sea of Faith" theology. Robin Le Poidevin's *Arguing for Atheism* (1996) develops explicit religious fictionalism. Andrew Eshleman writes on the fictionalist's participation, and Howard Wettstein on the significance of religious experience without metaphysical commitment.

**[FACT] The distinctions that matter.** Fictionalism is **not error theory**: the error theorist says the claims are false and we should stop, while the fictionalist says they are not to be *asserted* but can be *accepted* within the practice. Fictionalism is **not non-cognitivism**: the non-cognitivist says religious sentences never had truth-conditions, while the fictionalist grants that they do and declines to assert them.

**[FACT] Objections and replies.** Three objections — insincerity, motivation and the internal objection — with the replies set out in the visual. The motivational reply rests on an empirical claim about the sustained efficacy of narrative and ritual that remains contested, and saying so is more honest than asserting it.

**[FACT] The Indian comparison, which must not be omitted.** Advaita already contains a two-level apparatus doing structurally similar work without fictionalism: worship of the qualified absolute is genuinely efficacious at the everyday level and is **sublated**, not exposed as fiction, at the ultimate level — and **sublation is not falsification**. Buddhism's two truths permit full participation in conventional religious practice while denying ultimate substantial reference.

**[INFERENCE] The verdict formula.** Naturalism and fictionalism both show that the *functions* of religion — reverence, community, ritual, orientation to ultimacy, self-transformation — are detachable from theistic belief. Whether what survives is still *religion* depends on the definition adopted at the start, which is why this clause always returns to the substantive-versus-functional question.

**Objection.** If sublation is not falsification, then Advaita is simply a two-tier realism, and comparing it with fictionalism is a category mistake that flatters the Indian material.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** The comparison is not an identification. What is being compared is the *problem* both address — how to participate wholeheartedly in a practice whose object one does not affirm at the highest level of analysis — and the observation is that the Indian device solves the sincerity problem while the Western one inherits it. That is a comparison of adequacy, and it is precisely the kind of controlled comparison this clause rewards.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Fictionalism is a distinct position defined by the refusal to assert, not by a verdict on truth or meaning.
> **Named evidence:** Cupitt's *Taking Leave of God* (1980) treats God as the personification of the religious ideal; Le Poidevin's *Arguing for Atheism* (1996) develops explicit religious fictionalism; the error theorist by contrast abandons the discourse and the non-cognitivist denies it ever had truth-conditions.
> **Analysis:** The sincerity objection therefore bites hardest here, because the fictionalist participates in acts — prayer above all — whose intelligibility seems to presuppose the belief she withholds.
> **Qualification:** Advaita's everyday level is not fictionalism, since sublation is not falsification, and the comparative point is that Indian systems offer graded truth where Western fictionalism offers pretence.

#### UPSC TRAPS

1. ⚠️ Do not merge religious naturalism, fictionalism and error theory. Assertion, abandonment and meaning are three different dividing lines.
2. ⚠️ Do not present Advaita's everyday level as fictionalism. Sublation is not falsification, and the two-level device preserves sincerity.
3. ⚠️ Do not assert that fictionalist practice sustains motivation. The claim is empirical and contested; say so.

#### MINI RECAP

Three positions, one dividing line — assertion. Two dated anchors — Cupitt 1980, Le Poidevin 1996. Three objections, three replies. One comparison — graded truth against pretence.

#### MCQ 1. The definition that decides the whole clause

Why is it a mark-earning move to deny that Carvaka is itself an instance of religion without God?

A. Because its texts survive only in the works of opponents, and a tradition without its own surviving scripture cannot properly be counted as a religion.

B. Because it denies rebirth, and any system without rebirth falls outside the recognised Indian category of religion altogether and at once.

C. Because it is a school of philosophy rather than a tradition, and philosophy is never counted as religion within the classical Indian doxographic context.

D. Because it has no soteriology, sacred order, community of practice or ultimate concern, and so shows what non-theistic religions actually retain.

**Answer: D.**

**Option explanations:**

- **A:** Incorrect: the transmission problem is a source-discipline point governing how the verses may be quoted, and it is not a criterion of religious status at all.
- **B:** Incorrect: rebirth is not the criterion in play here, and adopting it would wrongly expel non-Indian non-theistic positions by applying a purely local test to them.
- **C:** Incorrect: Samkhya and Mimamsa are also philosophical schools yet function as religious systems in this clause, so the philosophy label does not settle the classification question.
- **D:** Correct: the contrast is the point, since Buddhism, Jainism and Mimamsa survive the loss of God while Carvaka closes the epistemic route to any supersensible object and ends in no religion at all.

**Examiner trap 21:** The verdict formula is that the critique succeeds exactly as far as its epistemology does: the strong thesis is self-refuting and unliveable, the moderate thesis is unrefuted and fatal to inference-based natural theology.

#### MCQ 22. Nietzsche's diagnosis, and who owns the question

C. It is a cultural diagnosis of a collapsed value horizon, and the printed question coupling religion with morality belongs to the Religion and Morality owner.

#### MCQ 23. Dewey and Dworkin on religion within nature

Which pairing of thinker and thesis in religious naturalism is accurate as the owner states it?

C. Dewey identifies God with the natural universe taken as a single substance, while Dworkin identifies God with humanity's own idealised and projected essence.

**Answer: A.**

- **A:** Correct: Dewey proposes using the word God for the active relation between the ideal and the actual, and Dworkin makes value realism rather than theism the core of what is religious about religion.

#### MCQ 24. Fictionalism, error theory and non-cognitivism

Which set of distinctions among the modern non-theistic positions is correct?

A. All three are versions of religious naturalism, differing only in how much reverence each of them recommends towards the natural world as it stands.

C. Non-cognitivism and fictionalism agree that religious sentences lack truth-conditions and differ only over which of the inherited rituals ought to be retained.

**Answer: B.**

- **A:** Incorrect: naturalism asserts its claims about ultimacy within nature and therefore stands apart from all three, and merging the four is expressly listed among the clause's traps.

#### MCQ 27. Remedial — “the Mimamsa Veda is divine revelation”

### Verified previous-year questions — 7 complete solutions

Every question below is reproduced in its **exact printed wording** from the audited Philosophy of Religion previous-year ledger, 2018–2025, in which this clause owns seven question-parts and is absent as a primary owner only in 2022. **The model answers are independent learner practice and are never official UPSC keys.**

**Timing skeleton.** 10 marks — 1 minute to decode, 1 to outline, 6 to write, 1 to check. 15 marks — 1 / 2 / 10 / 2. 20 marks — 2 / 3 / 13 / 2. **Standing penalties in this clause:** an answer with no stated definition of religion; "atheistic" used where "non-theistic" is meant; Cārvāka offered as an instance rather than as the limit; the Mīmāṃsā Veda called revelation; and any invented year for Smart, Feuerbach, Freud or Comte.

#### PYQ 1 — 2018, Q5(c), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Can you justify religion without God? Support your answer.

**Demand decoding:** "Justify" requires a defended position with the criterion stated, not a catalogue of godless schools. Announce the definition of religion first; the whole answer follows from it.

**Model solution:**
Yes — provided religion is defined not substantively, by the kind of object affirmed, but multidimensionally. Ninian Smart's dimensions are doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social and material; belief in a creator is not among them.

Buddhism satisfies every dimension. Dependent origination and karma explain conditioned arising; the Four Noble Truths diagnose; the Eightfold Path disciplines; the *Saṅgha* sustains; *nirvāṇa* completes. Jainism rejects a creator on the motive and embodiment arguments while retaining vows and the perfected *siddha*. Mīmāṃsā is sharper still: the Veda is authorless (*apauruṣeya*), so no divine speaker is required, and unseen potency (*apūrva*) links act to delayed fruit without a divine dispenser.

The justification must be bounded. A definition broad enough to admit any strong commitment is empty, so the sacred-order and soteriological conditions are added — which is exactly why Cārvāka, having closed the route to every supersensible object, is the limiting case rather than an instance.

Religion without God is therefore justified; religion without a sacred order is not.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Opens with a defended criterion instead of a list of schools.
- Uses three Indian traditions with their distinct mechanisms, not their names.
- Polices the over-breadth of its own definition inside the answer.
- Classifies Cārvāka correctly as the limit, which is the clause's signature discrimination.
- Closes with a decided verdict in the exact form the directive requested.

#### PYQ 2 — 2019, Q6(c), 15 marks

**Exact verified question:** How would a religious person deny the possibility of a religion without God? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** The stem asks you to argue the theist's case first, sympathetically and in its strongest form, and only then assess it. Half the marks are in the construction.

**Model solution:**
A religious theist would deny the possibility on five grounds. **Definitional:** religion is a living relation between finite persons and a personal transcendent, so "religion without God" is a category error rather than an unusual species. **Devotional:** worship requires a recipient; praise, petition and surrender are relational acts, and Jain veneration of a passionless perfected being would then be commemoration rather than worship. **Moral:** unconditional obligation requires a commander whose will binds. **Cosmic-order:** purposive order and the exact allotment of karmic fruit require intelligence, which is why Nyāya posits *Īśvara* as creator and superintendent of karma (*karmādhyakṣa*). **Soteriological:** forgiveness, grace and covenant require a responsive agent who can bestow them.

The denial nevertheless assumes its conclusion at the first step. Smart's dimensions show that doctrine, ritual, ethics, experience, narrative and community exist without a creator. Buddhism supplies a complete diagnosis of suffering, dependent origination, the path, the *Saṅgha* and *nirvāṇa*. Mīmāṃsā answers the third and fourth arguments directly: the authorless Veda secures authority without a speaker, and unseen potency (*apūrva*) secures desert without a dispenser, the relation being law-like rather than deliberative. Jainism's *siddha* is an achieved, not a governing, divinity.

The theist may reply that *Dharma*, *nirvāṇa* and *apūrva* are hidden substitutes; but functional similarity does not establish ontological identity. The strongest surviving point is therefore devotional, not definitional: petition and grace genuinely require a responsive God, and non-theistic religions do without them at a real cost in devotional structure.

Religion without God is possible; religion without a sacred order is not.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Obeys the unusual directive by giving the theist's five arguments priority and philosophical force.
- Names Nyāya's superintendent of karma and then answers it with the correct Mīmāṃsā mechanism.
- Distinguishes achieved from governing divinity in the Jain case.
- Refuses the hidden-God inference explicitly rather than ignoring it.
- Concedes the devotional cost, which is what a graded verdict requires.

#### PYQ 3 — 2020, Q6(c), 15 marks

**Exact verified question:** Critically examine the concept of God as prerequisite for a religion.

**Demand decoding:** "Prerequisite" is a necessity claim. Give the substantive definition its strongest defence, produce counterinstances, then state the limited functions for which a personal God really is indispensable.

**Model solution:**
To call God a prerequisite is to claim that nothing counts as religion unless it includes belief in a divine being, usually a personal creator, moral governor and saviour.

The claim has genuine force. A substantive definition captures prayer, worship, revelation, covenant and grace precisely, and it guards against an over-broad functionalism under which nationalism or ideology becomes a religion merely by supplying meaning and community. The theist adds that worship requires one who is worshipped, unconditional obligation a supreme commander, and karmic order an intelligent administrator.

A prerequisite is nevertheless a necessary condition, and established traditions defeat it. Buddhism has doctrine, ethical discipline, meditation, monastic community and liberation without a creator; where gods occur they are impermanent beings within rebirth, neither makers nor indispensable saviours. Jainism rejects creation while affirming eternal souls, karmic matter, vows and the perfected *siddha*. Mīmāṃsā is the sharpest counterexample: dharma is known only from injunction — Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 — the Veda is authorless rather than revealed, and unseen potency (*apūrva*) explains the delayed fruit without a dispenser. Classical Sāṃkhya derives evolution from nature in proximity to plural selves and seeks isolation (*kaivalya*); Yoga's *Īśvara* is a special self and must not be read back into Sāṃkhya.

Modern positions extend the case while complicating it: Dewey's "the religious", Dworkin's religious atheism and religious fictionalism all detach religious functions from theistic belief, though critics reasonably ask whether what survives is religion or only spirituality.

God is therefore indispensable for petition, covenant, propositional revelation and grace — but not for religion as such. The concept of God is one powerful way of integrating religious functions, not their universal prerequisite.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Treats "prerequisite" as a necessity claim rather than describing non-theism generally.
- Defends the substantive definition before dismantling it.
- Uses four Indian counterexamples with doctrine-specific mechanisms, and protects the Sāṃkhya–Yoga distinction.
- Integrates the modern positions without equating them with classical soteriology.
- Ends with a bounded, function-by-function necessity verdict.

#### PYQ 4 — 2021, Q5(d), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Is religious life possible without the belief in God? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** A possibility claim is established by one actual case done in depth, not by four cases mentioned. "Religious life" shifts the emphasis from doctrine to lived practice.

**Model solution:**
It is possible, and the proof is actual rather than hypothetical.

Religious life consists in disciplined orientation to a sacred order, ritual and ethical formation, community and a transformative goal. Buddhism supplies all four without a creator: the Four Noble Truths diagnose the predicament, the Eightfold Path prescribes conduct, meditation and understanding, moral discipline (*śīla*) shapes daily action, the *Saṅgha* transmits and sustains, and *nirvāṇa* is the goal. Where gods appear they are impermanent beings within rebirth, not saviours.

Mīmāṃsā shows the same for a ritual rather than a meditative life: injunction defines duty, and unseen potency (*apūrva*) connects the act to its fruit without a divine dispenser. Jain vows and asceticism organise a whole life around release.

Two limits should be conceded. Petition and grace do require a responsive God, so the personal texture of non-theistic religious life is different and arguably thinner. And religious life is not possible where every supersensible order has been closed off — which is why Cārvāka has an ethic but no religious life.

Belief in God is therefore sufficient for religious life in many traditions, but not necessary for it.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Answers the possibility question with one tradition treated in depth.
- Reads "religious life" as practice and answers on that register.
- Adds a second and third case briefly to show the result is not idiosyncratic.
- Concedes the devotional limit instead of overclaiming.
- Uses the Cārvāka boundary to show what "religious life" positively requires.

#### PYQ 5 — 2023, Q5(e), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** What is Agnosticism? How do agnostics conceptualize the relation between religion and God? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** Two printed deliverables — a definition and a positional account. Define, sub-divide into weak and strong, then give the relation. Answering only the first half forfeits half the marks.

**Model solution:**
Agnosticism is the claim that the existence of God is unknown or unknowable, so that judgement should be suspended. The word was coined by T. H. Huxley in 1869. It is distinct from atheism, which denies God, and from indifference, which does not raise the question. **Weak agnosticism** holds that the matter is not known and remains open to evidence; **strong agnosticism** holds that it is unknowable in principle, which is itself a substantial epistemological thesis. Kant's practical faith and Spencer's "Unknowable" are adjacent positions.

On the relation between religion and God, agnostics treat the two as separable. Religious commitment can be graded, and need not include metaphysical certainty. An agnostic may therefore affirm religion's ethical dimension, its experiential and contemplative dimension, and its ritual and communal life, while withholding assent on the existence of God — a minimal or non-theistic religiosity. A person may, for instance, participate fully in Buddhist ethical and meditative practice while withholding creator claims.

The objection is that this reduces religion to ethics or aesthetics. The reply is that community, ritual, transformation and orientation to ultimacy exceed ordinary morality — although what remains is admittedly thinner than a fully theistic religious life.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Answers both printed deliverables, in the printed order, with a visible transition.
- Dates the coinage correctly and distinguishes agnosticism from atheism and indifference.
- Sub-divides into weak and strong and notes the cost of the strong form.
- States the presupposition — graded commitment — instead of assuming it.
- Meets the reduction objection and concedes what it should.

#### PYQ 6 — 2024, Q7(a), 20 marks

**Exact verified question:** State and evaluate Buddhism as a religion without God.

**Demand decoding:** Two halves. State the doctrine fully before assessing it; do not evaluate a caricature. The evaluation must include the devotional complication and a graded verdict.

**Model solution:**
**Statement.** Buddhism is non-theistic rather than atheistic: a creator is absent from its diagnosis and its path, though impermanent gods and later devotional practice are not denied. Dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) and karma explain conditioned arising; gods and *Brahmā* figures remain beings inside rebirth rather than creators or final saviours, as the *Brahmajāla* (DN 1) and *Aggañña* (DN 27) Suttas indicate. The Buddha's undeclared questions (*avyākata*) concern specific speculative issues that the path does not require, and must not be presented as the whole case.

What remains is a complete religion on a multidimensional definition. Doctrine: the Four Noble Truths, impermanence and non-self. Practice: the Noble Eightfold Path, meditation and monastic observance. Ethics: moral discipline (*śīla*). Community: the *Saṅgha*, with rule, ordination and transmission. Goal: *nirvāṇa*, liberation from suffering. Sacred order: *Dharma*, the order the path discloses. Teaching, community and liberation organise all of this without functioning as one-for-one substitutes for a personal creator.

**Evaluation.** The argument is one of redundancy, not refutation: a creator adds no explanatory or practical step to the causal-soteriological sequence. Its presupposition — that dependent causation suffices for the problem Buddhism addresses — is where a theist should press.

Three objections deserve answers. That karma is providence renamed: causal-moral regularity is impersonal, with nothing that selects, forgives or answers. That devotional Mahāyāna makes Buddhism theistic: Buddhas and bodhisattvas may perform religious functions without becoming an omnipotent creator, which shows that non-theistic does not mean devotion-free. That *Dharma* is God renamed: functional similarity does not establish ontological identity.

The cost must be conceded. Petition, covenant and grace require a responsive God, and Buddhism does without them; its devotional structure is therefore differently shaped.

**Verdict.** Buddhism is a strong actual counterexample to the claim that a personal creator is necessary — provided religion is defined multidimensionally rather than stipulated as God-relation, in which case the counterexample would have been excluded before it was examined.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Completes the statement before beginning the evaluation, as the directive demands.
- Runs the multidimensional cluster explicitly, element by element.
- Restricts the undeclared questions to their real scope and cites the text anchors.
- Answers the three standing objections rather than the easiest one.
- Concedes the devotional cost and closes with a conditional, graded verdict.

#### PYQ 7 — 2025, Q5(a), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Discuss Cārvāka’s critique of the belief in the existence of suprasensible entities.

**Demand decoding:** The directive buys the **argument** of the critique, not its conclusions. Give the epistemology — the concomitance problem — not the hedonist slogans.

**Model solution:**
The critique is epistemological. Cārvāka holds that perception (*pratyakṣa*) alone is a means of knowledge, because a means of knowledge must yield certainty and every other candidate depends on a general rule that perception cannot certify.

The argument turns on inference. Inference depends on invariable, unconditional concomitance (*vyāpti*) between mark and inferred. How is that concomitance known? Not by perception, which grasps only particular co-presences while concomitance is universal. Not by inference, which would need its own concomitance, generating regress or circularity. Not by testimony, which is not a means of knowledge at all here. And even repeated observation cannot exclude an unnoticed conditioning factor (*upādhi*) — fire is accompanied by smoke only when the fuel is wet — so deviation remains possible and the concomitance remains merely probable.

Applied to religion: God is never perceived; consciousness is emergent from the body (*dehātmavāda*), illustrated by the intoxicating power arising from combined ingredients; karma and unseen merit are imperceptible postulates; order needs no maker, since things act by their own nature (*svabhāva-vāda*).

Two replies are required. The charge of self-refutation is met by reading the denial dialectically, and by the reported Purandara strand admitting ordinary inference while denying it for what lies beyond the senses. On that moderate reading the critique is unrefuted and fatal to every inference-based natural theology.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Gives the argument rather than the conclusions, as the directive requires.
- States all four moves of the concomitance problem, including the conditioning-factor residue.
- Applies the epistemology to the specific religious postulates.
- Supplies both replies to self-refutation and flags the reported strand as reported.
- Ends with the verdict formula tying the critique's success to its epistemology.

### Original solved Mains practice — 6 models

The following are original, independently prepared practice questions and model answers, not previous-year questions or official solutions.

#### Original 1 · 10 marks

**Question:** "Non-theistic is not identical with atheistic." Examine the significance of this distinction for the possibility of religion without God.

**Model solution:**
The distinction decides the clause. Atheism denies that God exists; non-theism reports that a creator is absent from a tradition's diagnosis and path. The first is a claim about what exists, the second about what a path requires.

Its significance is that non-theism leaves standing everything a religion needs. Theravāda Buddhism permits cosmological gods while denying their creatorhood and salvific ultimacy; later Mahāyāna develops full devotion to Buddhas and bodhisattvas; Jainism venerates the perfected *siddha*. None of that would be available to a tradition committed to denying every deity and every sacred being.

The distinction also redistributes the burden of proof. The non-theist need only show that a complete religious structure exists without a creator, which is a claim about actual traditions. The atheist must additionally defend a universal negative.

The objection is that a creator assigned no role has effectively been denied. The reply is doctrinal: what Buddhism denies is creatorhood and final salvific power, not existence, and the surviving devotional practice is evidence that the narrower denial is the real one.

Loose use of "atheistic" therefore concedes the theist's framing in the first line and makes a strong case sound like a weak one.

**Why this earns marks:** States the distinction precisely; shows what it preserves; identifies the shift in burden of proof; answers the collapse objection with doctrinal evidence; ends with the examination consequence.

#### Original 2 · 10 marks

**Question:** Explain why classical Mīmāṃsā is often regarded as the sharpest counterexample to the claim that religion requires God.

**Model solution:**
Because it removes God from a tradition that keeps everything else religion is usually thought to need: scripture, ritual, duty, merit and a promised fruit.

Three functions a deity normally performs are reassigned. Authority: the Veda is authorless (*apauruṣeya*), so no speaker's reliability needs establishing, and the theistic arguments from the text's status lose their premise. Duty: Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 defines dharma as what is characterised by injunction and is beneficial, so dharma is known from injunction alone, not from any deity's command. Desert: unseen potency (*apūrva*) is postulated to connect a perished momentary act with a much later fruit, answering the Nyāya demand for a superintendent of karma.

The deities named in the mantras are elements within the ritual structure — the dative of the offering — rather than agents who hear and grant.

Two qualifications are owed. Nyāya objects that an unconscious potency cannot select the right fruit for the right agent; Mīmāṃsā replies that the relation is law-like rather than deliberative, which the objector must then argue against. And later authors of the school drift toward theism, so the atheistic reading is of the classical system.

Mīmāṃsā thus shows that the functions God performs are separable, which is a stronger result than any single tradition's silence about a creator.

**Why this earns marks:** Identifies the three reassigned functions; quotes the sūtra for its epistemic consequence; states the postulate's status correctly; answers Nyāya; restricts the claim to the classical system.

#### Original 3 · 15 marks

**Question:** "Functional definitions of religion purchase the possibility of religion without God at the price of admitting anything." Critically discuss.

**Model solution:**
The charge is serious and half true, and the correct answer concedes the half before repairing it.

It is true that every functional feature over-generates on its own. Ultimate concern is a strong candidate but nations, art and political causes can become ultimate concerns. Ritual is widespread but secular ceremonies have it. Ethical orientation is common but ethical philosophies are not religions. Community is common but so are clubs. A definition assembled from these alone would indeed admit almost anything with sufficient intensity.

It is false that this is the only functional definition available. The working repair is a **cluster with two load-bearing conditions**: a disciplined and socially embodied relation to an ultimate or sacred order, together with a transformative or soteriological practice. Both must be present. A political movement may have ritual, doctrine, ethics and community and still fail both, because its ultimate is not treated as sacred and it offers no release from a diagnosed predicament.

The repair earns its keep by doing real classificatory work in both directions. It admits Buddhism, whose diagnosis of suffering and path to its cessation satisfy the soteriological condition exactly; Jainism, with bondage by karmic matter and release into the perfected state; Mīmāṃsā, with injunction, merit and a promised fruit; and Sāṃkhya, with entanglement and isolation. And it **excludes Cārvāka**, which denies the whole supersensible order and offers no path — an exclusion that shows the criterion is not merely permissive.

The alternative to functionalism is worse. A substantive definition centred on a personal creator settles a comparative question by stipulation, excluding Buddhism before examining it; and it cannot explain why traditions that lack a creator nonetheless possess doctrine, ritual, ethics, experience, narrative and institution in the pattern Smart identified.

A residual concession remains. Civil religion and secular spirituality sit uncomfortably close to the boundary, and the cluster classifies them only after asking whether a transformative discipline is present. That is a real cost of any functional account, but it is the cost of a working definition rather than of an empty one.

The charge therefore fails against the cluster while succeeding against unrestricted functionalism, which is precisely why the definition must be stated with its restrictions in the opening paragraph.

**Why this earns marks:** Concedes the true half explicitly; states the two load-bearing conditions; demonstrates classification in both directions; shows the substantive alternative is worse; ends with a residual concession rather than a clean sweep.

#### Original 4 · 15 marks

**Question:** Assess the claim that dharma, *nirvāṇa* and *apūrva* are simply God under other names.

**Model solution:**
This is the hidden-God objection, and it is the most persistent challenge to the whole clause. It infers, from the fact that a non-theistic concept performs a function God performs, that the concept is a disguised deity.

The inference is invalid, and the reply must say why rather than merely deny it. Functional similarity does not establish ontological identity. An impersonal law is not an omniscient person. Liberation reached by insight is not grace bestowed by an agent. A potency postulated to connect act and fruit does not select, forgive, respond or love. Each of these differences is not a matter of degree but of kind.

Take the three concepts in turn. *Dharma* in Buddhism is the order the path discloses and conforms to; it issues no commands, hears no petitions and grants no pardons. *Nirvāṇa* is the cessation of the conditions of suffering; it is a goal, not an agent, and it plays no cosmological role — treating it as a first cause reimports precisely the structure Buddhism dispenses with. *Apūrva* is explicitly a postulate introduced to bridge a perished act and a later fruit, and the Mīmāṃsā reply to Nyāya is that the connection is law-like rather than deliberative.

The objector has a comeback worth stating. If these concepts are doing the work God did, the tradition has acknowledged that the work must be done, so the theist's diagnosis of what religion requires was correct even if his ontology was not. That is a fair point and should be conceded: religion does require a sacred order, an account of moral consequence and a goal. What it does not require is that these be unified in a single responsive person.

Two disciplinary consequences follow for an answer. First, avoid writing that *Dharma* "takes the place of" God; describe instead what work it does and what work it does not do. Second, notice that the objection is strongest exactly where the theistic goods are relational — petition and grace — and weakest where they are structural.

The claim therefore fails as stated, while identifying correctly the functions any religion must discharge somehow.

**Why this earns marks:** Names the objection and its inference; refutes the inference with three kind-differences; treats the three concepts separately; concedes the objector's strongest comeback; converts the analysis into two writing rules.

#### Original 5 · 20 marks

**Question:** "Religion can survive the loss of God, but not the loss of a sacred order." Critically examine with reference to both Indian and modern Western positions.

**Model solution:**
The statement is defensible, and examining it requires a criterion, two bodies of evidence and a boundary case.

**The criterion.** Religion is a socially embodied and disciplined orientation to an ultimate or sacred order, expressed through some combination of doctrine, ritual, ethical formation, experience and community, and directed at a transformative or soteriological goal. Two conditions are load-bearing: the sacred order and the transformative practice. Creator-belief is one historically powerful way of organising the orientation, not a condition of it — and a definition that made it a condition would settle a comparative question by stipulation.

**The Indian evidence: survival without God.** Buddhism diagnoses suffering, explains its arising through dependent origination and karma, prescribes the Eightfold Path, sustains a community and completes in *nirvāṇa*. Jainism argues against a creator from motive and embodiment while retaining eternal souls, karmic matter, vows and the perfected *siddha* — an achieved rather than a governing divinity. Mīmāṃsā keeps scripture, ritual, duty and merit while making the Veda authorless and postulating unseen potency (*apūrva*) to connect act and fruit; Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2 makes dharma knowable from injunction alone. Classical Sāṃkhya derives the world-process from nature in proximity to plural selves and seeks isolation; Yoga's special self is not a creator and must not be read back into Sāṃkhya.

In every case what survives the removal of God is precisely a sacred order plus a path.

**The modern Western evidence: the thinner case.** Religious naturalism relocates ultimacy inside nature. Dewey's *A Common Faith* (1934) distinguishes "religion" from "the religious" and redefines God as the active relation between the ideal and the actual; Dworkin's *Religion without God* (2013) defends religious atheism on objective life-meaning and cosmic sublimity; Goodenough's *The Sacred Depths of Nature* (1998) develops reverence for natural depth. Religious fictionalism goes further, declining to assert the claims at all: Cupitt's non-realism (1980) and Le Poidevin's fictionalism (1996) retain narrative, liturgy and formation without metaphysical assent.

These are genuinely religious on the criterion where a community and a transformative practice accompany the philosophy; where they do not, "religious" survives chiefly as an adjective for a quality of experience. Fictionalism additionally faces the sincerity objection, since prayer seems directed to a believed hearer. Advaita's two levels supply the instructive contrast: worship of the qualified absolute is efficacious at the everyday level and is **sublated**, not falsified, at the ultimate level — graded truth rather than pretence.

**The boundary case.** Cārvāka closes the epistemic route to every supersensible object: perception alone is a means of knowledge, concomitance cannot be established, consciousness is emergent, and order follows from own-nature. It therefore has no soteriology, no sacred, no community of practice and no ultimate concern. What results is not a godless religion but no religion at all — which is the strongest single confirmation of the statement under examination.

**The qualification.** "Survive" understates a real cost. Petition, covenant and grace require a responsive God, and traditions without them have a differently shaped devotional life. And the sacred-order condition needs policing at the modern boundary, since civil religion and secular spirituality display ritual and ultimate concern without a transformative discipline.

The statement therefore holds: God is separable from religion, a sacred order is not.

**Why this earns marks:** States a criterion before assembling evidence; runs Indian and Western bodies of evidence separately and comparably; uses Cārvāka as the confirming boundary; names the defeater — the devotional cost — that qualifies the verdict; ends by deciding rather than summarising.

#### Original 6 · 20 marks

**Question:** Compare the classical Indian non-theistic traditions with modern religious naturalism and fictionalism as answers to the question whether religion requires God.

**Model solution:**
Both answer "no", but they arrive from opposite directions, and the comparison is decided by what each has to build and what each already possessed.

**What is removed, and what replaces it.** Buddhism removes creator and permanent self, replacing them with dependent origination, *Dharma*, *Saṅgha* and *nirvāṇa*. Jainism removes only the creator, replacing it with karmic matter, vows and the perfected *siddha*. Mīmāṃsā removes both the author of the Veda and the dispenser of fruit, replacing them with authorless authority (*apauruṣeya*) and unseen potency (*apūrva*). Sāṃkhya removes the creator, replacing it with nature's evolution in proximity to plural selves and release as isolation. Religious naturalism removes supernatural ontology, replacing it with nature as the locus of ultimacy. Fictionalism removes assertion itself, replacing it with participation in a non-assertoric spirit.

**The structural difference.** The Indian systems are **positive** systems: they possess doctrines of causation, moral consequence, community and a final goal that were never derived from theism and therefore lose nothing when theism is withdrawn. Buddhism does not retain Christian forms after withdrawing belief; it has its own diagnosis, its own causal account and its own soteriology. The modern positions are **reconstructive**: they must build or re-motivate ultimacy after inherited metaphysics has weakened. That is why they are thinner, and the thinness is structural rather than accidental.

**The sincerity test.** Fictionalism's characteristic liability is sincerity: worship seems directed to a believed hearer, and make-believe worship risks being either dishonest or hollow. The fictionalist replies that transparent imaginative immersion is a familiar and non-deceptive human capacity, and that ritual can express commitment rather than report ontology; whether motivation survives over time is an unresolved empirical and existential issue. The comparative point that earns marks is that **Indian systems offer graded truth where Western fictionalism offers pretence**. Advaita treats worship of the qualified absolute as efficacious at the everyday level and sublated, not falsified, at the ultimate level; Buddhism's two truths permit serious conventional practice while denying ultimate substantial reference. Sublation is not falsification, and that difference preserves the practitioner's sincerity.

**Where the modern positions are stronger.** They address a question the classical traditions never faced: whether someone committed to a naturalistic ontology can be religious at all. Dewey's separation of "religion" from "the religious", Dworkin's religious atheism grounded in objective life-meaning and cosmic sublimity, and Goodenough's reverence for natural depth are answers to that specific modern predicament, and the Indian systems have nothing to say to it directly.

**The shared result, and the shared limit.** Both families show that the *functions* of religion — reverence, community, ritual, orientation to ultimacy, self-transformation — are detachable from theistic belief. Both leave untouched the specifically relational goods: petition, covenant and grace still require a responsive God. And whether what survives counts as religion depends on the definition adopted at the start, which is why this clause always returns to the substantive-versus-functional question.

The classical traditions therefore give the stronger evidence that religion without God is actual; the modern positions give the sharper test of whether it remains possible under a naturalistic ontology.

**Why this earns marks:** Uses a removal-and-replacement bench for six routes; identifies the positive/reconstructive asymmetry as the structural difference; runs the sincerity test with the Indian graded-truth control; concedes where the modern positions are stronger; closes on the shared result and the shared limit.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

*Eighteen compressed retrieval cards, one per taught session, in teaching order. These are revision notes, not a second exposition: definitions, discriminations, named evidence, traps and answer routes only.*

### 1. The necessity question, and the definition that decides it

- **The question owned:** whether a personal creator is *necessary* for religion — not whether God exists.
- **Four definitions:** substantive (by object) · functional (by what it does) · multidimensional (Smart's cluster) · family resemblance (no shared essence).
- **Two failures:** too narrow ⇒ excludes Buddhism by stipulation, question-begging · too broad ⇒ admits nationalism, empty category.
- **Smart's seven dimensions:** doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social, material. Creator-belief is **not** on the list.
- **Opening habit:** name the definition in sentence one and never drift from it.

### 2. The criteria table, and the working cluster

- **Seven candidates graded:** creator God (**not necessary**) · sacred/ultimate concern (broad) · ritual (not universal in one form) · ethics (common) · community (common) · **diagnosis-and-liberation (strong discriminator)** · myth/doctrine/experience (family features).
- **The working sufficient cluster:** a socially embodied, disciplined orientation to an ultimate/sacred order, through doctrine, ritual, ethical formation, experience and community, with a transformative or soteriological goal.
- **Two load-bearing conditions:** sacred order **and** soteriological practice. Both, not either.
- **What it must do:** admit Buddhism, Jainism, Mīmāṃsā, Sāṃkhya; exclude nationalism; exclude Cārvāka.
- **Trap:** presenting the cluster as necessary conditions instead of a sufficient combination.

### 3. Which functions require a deity

- **Require a person:** petitionary prayer (a responsive addressee is built in) · propositional revelation from God (a speaker by definition) · grace and providence (in their personal-agency sense).
- **Do not:** worship/veneration · ritual efficacy (*apūrva*; Buddhist and Jain discipline) · moral motivation (compassion, non-injury, karma, vows, insight) · liberation (*nirvāṇa*, Jain release, *kaivalya*).
- **The separability result:** authority, efficacy and desert are separable and can each be secured otherwise — Mīmāṃsā proves it inside one system.
- **Answer move:** concede petition, grace and covenant; the concession buys credit for every denial that follows.
- **Trap:** treating "prayer" and "worship" as one function.

### 4. The four negative positions

- **Non-theism:** about the **path** — a creator is absent from diagnosis and prescription.
- **Atheism:** about **existence** — the positive denial that God exists.
- **Agnosticism:** about **knowledge** — weak ("not known") and strong ("unknowable").
- **Indifference:** the question does not arise. Adjacent: anti-theism; practical atheism.
- **The firewall:** non-theistic ≠ atheistic. Theravāda Buddhism permits cosmological gods while denying creatorhood and salvific ultimacy.
- **Trap:** "Buddhism is atheistic" in line one concedes the theist's framing.

### 5. Buddhism — the diagnosis without a creator

- **Engine:** dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) + karma explain conditioned arising and cessation.
- **The claim is redundancy, not refutation:** a creator adds no explanatory and no practical step.
- **Presupposition to state:** dependent causation is explanatorily sufficient *for the problem Buddhism addresses*.
- **Scope control:** the undeclared questions (*avyākata*) concern specific speculative issues; they are not the whole case. Poisoned-arrow parable = urgency, not proof of atheism.
- **Text anchors:** *Brahmajāla Sutta* DN 1 · *Aggañña Sutta* DN 27. Gods and *Brahmā* figures are impermanent beings inside rebirth.
- **Objection/reply:** karma is providence renamed ⇒ causal-moral regularity is impersonal; nothing selects, forgives or answers.

### 6. Buddhism — the complete religion, and devotion

- **Cluster discharged:** doctrine (Four Noble Truths) · practice (Eightfold Path, meditation) · ethics (*śīla*) · experience · community (*Saṅgha*) · goal (*nirvāṇa*) · sacred order (*Dharma*).
- **The compulsory qualification:** *Dharma*, *Saṅgha* and *nirvāṇa* organise doctrine, practice and value **without functioning as one-for-one substitutes** for a creator.
- **Devotional Mahāyāna:** Buddhas and bodhisattvas may perform religious functions without becoming an omnipotent creator ⇒ non-theistic does not mean devotion-free.
- **2024 stem shape:** complete the *statement* before the *evaluation*; do not evaluate a caricature.
- **Verdict:** a strong actual counterexample — *provided* religion is defined multidimensionally.

### 7. Jainism — creator-denial and achieved divinity

- **Two arguments:** the **motive** argument (a perfect being has no unfulfilled desire creation could satisfy) and the **embodiment** argument (acting on matter requires a body, which is itself limited).
- **Same argument-family:** Kumārila's *Ślokavārttika* against a bodiless agent acting on matter.
- **Replacement machinery:** eternal souls (*jīva*) and non-soul (*ajīva*) · karmic matter binding and shed · vows and asceticism · liberation as the soul's own achievement.
- **The decisive line:** the ford-maker (*tīrthaṅkara*) teaches and the perfected being (*siddha*) has completed — an **achieved** divinity, never a **governing** one.
- **Theist's best shot here:** worship of a passionless being is "commemoration" ⇒ answer with vows, ritual, asceticism, soteriology.

### 8. Mīmāṃsā — authorless scripture and injunction

- **Three jobs an author would do, all retired:** reliability ⇒ **authorless** (*apauruṣeya*) · validation ⇒ **intrinsic validity** (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*) · meaning ⇒ the **eternal word-referent relation** (Nyāya denies this; note the dispute).
- **The definition:** Jaimini, *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* **1.1.2** — *codanā-lakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ*: dharma is what is characterised by injunction and is beneficial ⇒ known **only** from injunction.
- **Effect on theistic proofs:** Udayana's arguments from the Veda's status lose their premise rather than their conclusion.
- **Mantra-deities:** elements within the ritual structure — the dative of the offering — not agents who hear, choose and grant.
- **Trap:** calling the Veda "revelation" reinstates the speaker the school removed.

### 9. Mīmāṃsā — unseen potency and the two schools

- **Five steps:** injunction promises a result → result is delayed → act is momentary and perishes → a perished cause cannot produce a later effect → therefore an unseen potency (*apūrva*, "the not-before") persists.
- **Status:** a **postulate** (*arthāpatti*), licensed by the school's own epistemology — never a perceived entity.
- **The split:** Kumārila — a **power** (*śakti*) in act or agent ⇒ consequential mechanism of **desert**. Prabhākara — **the ought-to-be-done** (*niyoga*/*kārya*) ⇒ a **deontology of the injunction**.
- **Three objections/replies:** allocation (Nyāya's *karmādhyakṣa*) ⇒ law-like, not deliberative, and the objection begs the question · meaning ⇒ eternal word-referent relation · "bureaucracy not religion" ⇒ sacred order, soteriological promise, discipline, community.
- **❓ Honest limit:** later Mīmāṃsā authors drift toward theism; the atheistic reading is of the **classical** system.

### 10. Sāṃkhya and Yoga — a cosmos without a maker

- **Two principles:** nature (*prakṛti*), one, unconscious, active, with three strands (*guṇa*) · selves (*puruṣa*), plural, conscious, inactive.
- **Process:** proximity → evolution of intellect, ego-sense, mind, senses, elements → misidentification → discriminative knowledge (*viveka-khyāti*) → **isolation** (*kaivalya*).
- **Yoga's special self (*Īśvara*)** is **not straightforwardly a creator** in the classical Sāṃkhya sense — never read it back into Sāṃkhya.
- **Illustration:** milk seeming to nourish the calf — non-conscious purposiveness.
- **Nyāya pressure and reply:** unconscious principles cannot organise ends ⇒ observed regularity need not be modelled on artefact production.

### 11. Cārvāka — perception alone, and concomitance

- **Ontology:** the four gross elements (*bhūtacatuṣṭaya*: earth, water, fire, air); **ether rejected as imperceptible**; human ends reduce to pleasure (*kāma*) and wealth (*artha*).
- **Why only perception:** a means of knowledge must yield certainty → perception is immediately certain → every other candidate depends on a rule perception cannot certify.
- **The four closed routes to concomitance (*vyāpti*):** not by perception (it is universal) · not by inference (regress/circularity) · not by testimony (not a means of knowledge here) · and the **conditioning factor** (*upādhi*) can never be excluded — fire with smoke only when the fuel is wet ⇒ deviation (*vyabhicāra*) stays possible.
- **Conclusion, already domain-restricted:** inference works by luck or habit in daily life and is worthless where no perceptual check exists.
- **Parity objection:** perception errs too — illusion, dream, defective organs.

### 12. Cārvāka — application, replies, and the limit

- **Application:** God unperceived · consciousness emergent (*dehātmavāda*), the intoxicating-power (*madaśakti*) analogy ⇒ no survival, transmigration or liberation · karma and unseen merit (*adṛṣṭa*) are props for ritual · Vedic authority denied · order by own-nature (*svabhāva-vāda*).
- **❓ Attribution:** the Bṛhaspati verses and the hedonist couplet survive only in opponents' doxographies, chiefly Mādhava's *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha*. Report as doxographic; never as primary text.
- **Five replies:** self-refutation · pragmatic · Nyāya's defence (repeated uncontradicted co-observation, non-observation of a counter-instance, hypothetical reasoning) · parity · **the reported Purandara strand** admitting ordinary (*laukika*) inference and denying it for the beyond-the-senses (*atīndriya*).
- **Two rejoinders to self-refutation:** the dialectical (*prasaṅga*) reading; the moderate Purandara thesis.
- **Verdict formula:** the critique succeeds exactly as far as its epistemology does — strong thesis self-refuting and unliveable; **moderate thesis unrefuted and fatal to every inference-based natural theology, including Udayana's**.
- **Classification:** ❌ **not** a religion without God — the limiting case that shows non-theistic religions retain an accepted supersensible order.

### 13. Western critiques of theistic religion

- **Feuerbach**, *The Essence of Christianity* — "theology is anthropology"; God is the **projection** of humanity's idealised essence.
- **Marx**, Introduction to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (**1843–44**) — religion is "the **opium of the people**", consolation masking exploitation, dissolving with a just society.
- **Freud** — religion as **illusion**, wish-fulfilment, cosmic father-projection.
- **Comte** — the **religion of humanity**: devotion to human values without a deity; the bridge to secular humanism.
- **Shared structure:** identify a human need → derive the belief → dispense with the object.
- **Shared limit:** these are **genetic** explanations. Origin does not settle truth. What they do establish: belief is not self-authenticating.

### 14. Nietzsche and post-theistic value

- **The claim:** "God is dead" diagnoses the collapse of the **Christian-metaphysical horizon** and the danger of **nihilism** — a cultural diagnosis, not a proof of non-existence.
- **Four steps:** truthfulness and science erode belief → inherited values depended on the framework → collapse creates nihilism → overcoming requires **revaluation** and self-creation.
- **Presupposition:** values have genealogies tied to forms of life and can be assessed as life-enhancing or life-denying.
- **Canonical example:** *ressentiment* reverses aristocratic value terms and converts weakness into moral superiority.
- **Objection/reply:** revaluation risks elitism and arbitrary power ⇒ creative self-overcoming, though a universal standard remains difficult.
- **Ownership gate:** the printed 2025 stem coupling religion **with morality** is owned by **Religion and Morality**. This clause keeps Nietzsche as critique of theistic religion + bridge to post-theism.
- **Comparative point:** a horizon **lost** is not a horizon **never had** — Buddhism and Jainism never possessed it.

### 15. Agnosticism

- **Definition:** God's existence is **unknown or unknowable** ⇒ suspend judgement. Distinct from atheism (denial) and indifference (no question).
- **Coinage:** **T. H. Huxley, 1869.**
- **Subdivision:** weak = "not known" (open to evidence) · strong = "unknowable" (a substantial epistemological thesis needing its own defence).
- **Adjacent:** Kant's practical faith; Spencer's "Unknowable".
- **The relation:** religion and God are **separable**; ethical, experiential, ritual and communal dimensions remain available while assent is withheld ⇒ minimal or non-theistic religiosity.
- **Presupposition:** religious commitment can be **graded** and need not include metaphysical certainty.
- **Objection/reply:** reduces religion to ethics/aesthetics ⇒ community, ritual, transformation and orientation to ultimacy exceed ordinary morality.
- **Trap:** answering only the first of the two printed deliverables.

### 16. Religious naturalism

- **Thesis:** ultimacy, reverence and the religious response are located **within nature**; no supernatural person, but genuine attitudes of awe, gratitude, humility and commitment.
- **Dewey**, *A Common Faith* (**1934**): "religion" (institution with supernatural claims) vs "**the religious**" (a quality of experience unifying the self toward inclusive ideal ends); "God" = the **active relation between the ideal and the actual**, not a being.
- **Dworkin**, *Religion without God* (**2013**): "**religious atheism**" — objective intrinsic meaning of human life + the sublimity and intrinsic beauty of the universe; **value realism**, not theism, is the core.
- **Also:** Julian Huxley (evolutionary humanism) · Goodenough, *The Sacred Depths of Nature* (**1998**) · Crosby and Rue on nature as ground of ultimacy · Spinoza (*Deus sive Natura*) as ancestor.
- **Objection/reply:** ethics and aesthetics in religious vocabulary ⇒ ultimacy, reverence, community and transformation are present, and demanding a supernatural person restates theism.
- **Bounded caution:** civil religion and secular spirituality are **boundary tests, not owners**; without a transformative discipline the category becomes too broad.

### 17. Religious fictionalism and non-realism

- **Thesis:** religious sentences are (or may be) literally false or unwarranted, yet participation in a **non-assertoric**, fictive spirit is rationally permissible and valuable.
- **Named:** Cupitt, *Taking Leave of God* (**1980**) — non-realism, God as personification of the religious ideal, "Sea of Faith" · Le Poidevin, *Arguing for Atheism* (**1996**) — explicit fictionalism · Eshleman on participation · Wettstein on significance without metaphysical commitment.
- **Three-way discrimination:** **error theory** = false, so stop · **non-cognitivism** = never had truth-conditions · **fictionalism** = grants truth-conditions, declines to assert, continues in the practice.
- **Three objections:** insincerity/self-deception · motivational hollowing · the internal objection (believers reject the description).
- **Indian control — do not omit:** Advaita's everyday level (*vyāvahārika*) is efficacious and is **sublated**, not falsified, at the ultimate level (*pāramārthika*); Buddhism's two truths (*saṃvṛti*/*paramārtha*) permit serious conventional practice. **Sublation is not falsification.**
- **The comparative sentence:** Indian systems offer **graded truth** where Western fictionalism offers **pretence**.

### 18. The theist's denial, the bench and the verdict

- **Five theistic arguments:** definitional (category error) · devotional (worship needs a recipient) · moral (obligation needs a commander) · cosmic order (Nyāya's *Īśvara* as superintendent of karma) · soteriological (grace needs a giver).
- **Five rebuttals:** Smart's dimensions and the stipulation charge · the *siddha* as achieved not governing · non-theistic moral motivation · Mīmāṃsā's authorless Veda + *apūrva*, law-like not deliberative · Buddhism's complete diagnosis-and-path.
- **The assessment that scores:** the strongest surviving theistic point is **worship/grace**, not the definition; petition and grace genuinely require a responsive God, at a real cost in devotional structure.
- **Six-route bench:** Buddhism (yes) · Jainism (yes) · Mīmāṃsā (strong under ritual/functional definitions) · Sāṃkhya–Yoga (yes) · modern naturalism/fictionalism (only on a functional definition) · **Cārvāka (no — the limit)**.
- **Four standing criticisms:** definition · hidden-God · motivation · nihilism — with the replies in Session 18.
- **Architectures:** 10 = define → four-step doctrine → nearest confusion → one objection → verdict. 15 = frame → one Indian case in depth → example → objection and reply → comparison. 20 = criteria → full Buddhist/Jain/Mīmāṃsā case → creator critique → devotional complication → Western comparison → graded conclusion.
- **Closing formula:** *religion without God is possible; religion without a sacred order is not.*

#### ASCII MASTER FLOW — PANEL 14/26: Carvaka I - perception alone, and the four-element ontology

```ascii-master
WHY ONLY PERCEPTION (pratyaksa)?
 1 a means of knowledge (pramana) must yield CERTAINTY about its object
 2 perception is self-evident and immediately certain
 3 every other candidate - inference, comparison, testimony, postulation - is either reducible to
   perception or DEPENDS ON A GENERAL RULE that perception cannot certify
 4 .: perception alone is a pramana, and knowledge does not extend beyond the perceptible
THE ONTOLOGY THAT FOLLOWS
 reality = the FOUR GROSS ELEMENTS (bhutacatustaya): earth, water, fire, air
 ETHER IS REJECTED because it is IMPERCEPTIBLE - not an omission but a consequence
 the human ends (purusarthas) reduce to KAMA and ARTHA, because the other two require a
 supersensible order to be intelligible
THE CERTAINTY CONDITION IS THE HINGE - and it is where the school is most vulnerable:
 PARITY OBJECTION: perception itself faces illusion, dream and defective organs, and is corrected
 only by further cognition. So either the certainty condition is weakened - and inference
 re-enters - or perception fails its own test.
NOTE THE SHAPE OF THE CONCLUSION: it is ALREADY DOMAIN-RESTRICTED in its own statement -
 inference "works by luck or habit" in daily life and is worthless where no perceptual check
 exists. That restriction is what makes the moderate reading available in Panel 17.
```

#### ASCII MASTER FLOW — PANEL 15/26: Carvaka II - the attack on inference, in full

```ascii-master
INFERENCE (anumana) depends on VYAPTI - an INVARIABLE, UNCONDITIONAL concomitance between the
 mark (hetu, e.g. smoke) and the inferred (sadhya, e.g. fire).
CARVAKA'S QUESTION: by what means is VYAPTI ITSELF known?
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | (1) NOT BY PERCEPTION - perception grasps only PARTICULAR co-presences, here and now.       |
 |     Vyapti is a UNIVERSAL covering all cases, including unobserved past, future and remote  |
 |     ones, and no perception reaches those.                                                  |
 | (2) NOT BY INFERENCE - establishing this vyapti by another inference requires that          |
 |     inference's own vyapti: INFINITE REGRESS or CIRCULARITY.                                |
 | (3) NOT BY TESTIMONY - testimony's authority would itself have to be established, and for   |
 |     the Carvaka it is not a pramana at all.                                                 |
 | (4) THE UPADHI PROBLEM - even repeated observation cannot rule out an unnoticed CONDITIONING|
 |     FACTOR (upadhi) that makes the concomitance hold only contingently. Standard example:   |
 |     fire is accompanied by smoke ONLY WHEN THE FUEL IS WET. Unless every possible upadhi is |
 |     excluded - and that exclusion is itself unverifiable - the concomitance remains merely  |
 |     PROBABLE: deviation (vyabhicara) stays possible.                                        |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
CONCLUSION: inference cannot deliver CERTAINTY. Where it works in daily life it works by luck or
 habit; and precisely in the supersensible domain - God, soul, karma, afterlife - no perceptual
 check is EVER available, so there inference is worthless.
TRAP: "Carvaka rejects inference" states a CONCLUSION and omits the ARGUMENT the 2025 directive
 asked for. Give all four moves, including the upadhi residue.
```
