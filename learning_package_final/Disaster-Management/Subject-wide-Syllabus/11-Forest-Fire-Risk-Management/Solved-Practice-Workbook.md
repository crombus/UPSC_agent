## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1. Which formulation best states the fire triangle?

A. Combustion requires fuel, oxygen and sufficient heat or ignition.
B. Spread requires only wind, slope and a satellite hotspot.
C. Smoke, ash and burned area are the three causes of ignition.
D. Fuel, rainfall and population together guarantee a forest fire.

**Answer: A**

- **A:** Correct: these are the three essential combustion elements.
- **B:** Incorrect: these shape detection or behaviour but omit the combustion requirements.
- **C:** Incorrect: these are products or measurements, not the triangle.
- **D:** Incorrect: none of the three guarantees ignition and sustained combustion.

### MCQ 2. Which set most directly governs fire behaviour after ignition?

A. District boundaries, alert subscribers and compensation rules.
B. Fuel condition, weather and topography.
C. Forest title, census year and satellite owner.
D. Only the source of ignition.

**Answer: B**

- **A:** Incorrect: governance variables matter for management, not flame behaviour itself.
- **B:** Correct: fuel, weather and terrain shape spread, intensity and direction.
- **C:** Incorrect: these do not constitute the physical behaviour triad.
- **D:** Incorrect: identical ignitions can behave differently under different conditions.

### MCQ 3. Which classification is correct?

A. Surface fire burns only tree canopies; crown fire burns peat.
B. Ground fire is any flame observed by satellite.
C. Surface burns litter, ground burns organic soil, and crown burns canopy.
D. Spot fire is a legal category for all deliberate ignition.

**Answer: C**

- **A:** Incorrect: surface and crown layers are reversed and peat is ground fuel.
- **B:** Incorrect: ground fire is defined by burning layer, not detection method.
- **C:** Correct: each type is linked to the layer consumed.
- **D:** Incorrect: spotting is a fire-behaviour process, not an intent category.

### MCQ 4. Why can spotting defeat a maintained fire line?

A. Fire lines raise fuel moisture beyond the line.
B. Ground fire always becomes crown fire at a line.
C. A danger rating legally suspends all firefighting.
D. Wind-borne embers can ignite receptive fuel beyond the break.

**Answer: D**

- **A:** Incorrect: a line does not necessarily change moisture.
- **B:** Incorrect: transition depends on fuels and conditions.
- **C:** Incorrect: a rating informs decisions but does not impose one universal legal result.
- **D:** Correct: spot fires can cross a gap without the flame front crossing it.

### MCQ 5. Which is an ignition source rather than a spread condition?

A. Lightning striking dry fuel.
B. Low relative humidity.
C. A continuous litter bed.
D. A steep upslope alignment.

**Answer: A**

- **A:** Correct: lightning can supply the initiating heat.
- **B:** Incorrect: humidity affects fuel moisture and behaviour.
- **C:** Incorrect: continuity affects spread.
- **D:** Incorrect: slope affects preheating and rate of spread.

### MCQ 6. A small escaped campfire becomes a fast uphill fire. Which explanation is best?

A. The satellite alert created the fire.
B. Ignition met dry fuel, wind and slope.
C. Every uphill fire is a crown fire.
D. Exposure determined the direction of flame spread.

**Answer: B**

- **A:** Incorrect: monitoring observes rather than creates combustion.
- **B:** Correct: it separates ignition from the factors governing behaviour.
- **C:** Incorrect: the burning layer still must be identified.
- **D:** Incorrect: exposure affects consequence, not flame direction.

### MCQ 7. Why does fire commonly spread faster upslope?

A. Gravity pulls flames downhill.
B. Oxygen is absent below the ignition point.
C. Hot gases preheated fuel upslope.
D. Satellite pixels become smaller on steep slopes.

**Answer: C**

- **A:** Incorrect: the key mechanism is heat transfer and preheating.
- **B:** Incorrect: atmospheric oxygen remains available.
- **C:** Correct: upslope fuel receives greater convective/radiant preheating.
- **D:** Incorrect: sensor resolution does not drive physical spread.

### MCQ 8. Which statement correctly separates fire danger from ignition?

A. A danger class confirms that a fire has started.
B. Ignition proves the eventual burned area.
C. A hotspot is identical to a danger forecast.
D. Danger estimates potential; ignition starts combustion.

**Answer: D**

- **A:** Incorrect: danger is prospective.
- **B:** Incorrect: behaviour and suppression determine final extent.
- **C:** Incorrect: one is forecast potential and the other a thermal detection.
- **D:** Correct: the concepts answer different questions.

### APPLICATION PYQ 1 — 2020 GS-III Q18 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording:** “Discuss the recent measures initiated in disaster management by the Government of India departing from the earlier reactive approach.”

**Primary owner:** Disaster Management Topic 02. Forest fire is used as a hazard-specific application.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\Gen_St_P3.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 235 words):**

India's disaster governance has increasingly moved from post-event relief toward risk identification, mitigation, preparedness and resilient recovery, though implementation remains uneven.

The Disaster Management Act architecture, national/state/district plans and Sendai-aligned policy place risk reduction before impact. Hazard mapping, early warning, capacity building, mitigation funds and continuity planning illustrate the change. In forest fire management, FSI's satellite-based danger rating, MODIS/SNPP-VIIRS near-real-time alerts, Large Forest Fire Monitoring and Van Agni create anticipatory information. The 2018 National Action Plan on Forest Fires and the continuing FPM Scheme support prevention, State capacity and community participation.

This is proactive when prone-area analysis positions patrols and crews; communities co-design safe seasonal practices; fire lines/fuel treatment are maintained; WUI exposure is controlled; and alerts activate field verification, incident command and evacuation. Recovery should assess burn severity, erosion, water, regeneration and livelihoods rather than stop at extinguishment.

However, capability must not be confused with outcome. A hotspot does not prove a fire, an alert does not ensure a crew can reach it, and funds or training counts do not show containment or ecological recovery. State capacity, access, inter-agency command and routine community partnership remain variable.

Thus the policy direction is proactive, but success should be judged by reduced avoidable spread, injury, recurrence and recovery time—not by the number of plans, portals or alerts.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the shift, uses forest-fire evidence across the cycle and critically separates institutional inputs from outcomes.

### APPLICATION PYQ 2 — 2024 GS-III Q17 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording:** “What is disaster resilience? How is it determined? Describe various elements of a resilience framework. Also mention the global targests of Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015-2030)”

**Primary owner:** Disaster Management Topic 01. “targests” is retained from the official local English extraction.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\03 UPSC 2024 Paper-III.md`.

**Model answer (about 245 words):**

Disaster resilience is the ability of exposed systems and communities to resist, absorb, adapt to, transform and recover from hazards while preserving essential functions. It is determined by hazard and exposure, social/physical/ecological vulnerability, institutional and financial capacity, redundancy, learning and recovery speed.

A resilience framework should include: risk and vulnerability assessment; prevention and land-use control; structural and ecosystem-based mitigation; multi-hazard early warning; inclusive preparedness and drills; robust health, communications and lifelines; coordinated response; social protection and finance; and recovery that Builds Back Better. Indicators must test outcomes such as avoided loss, continuity, restoration time and reduced recurrence.

Forest fire illustrates the framework. FSI danger rating and satellite alerts strengthen risk knowledge and warning; State crews and district escalation provide response; community partnership manages recurrent ignition and fuels; WUI planning reduces exposure; smoke protection preserves health; and burn-severity, watershed and livelihood work support recovery. Yet an alert or FPM allocation alone does not prove resilience.

Sendai's seven global targets seek reductions in mortality, affected people, economic loss and damage/disruption to critical infrastructure and basic services; more national/local DRR strategies; stronger international cooperation for developing countries; and wider access to multi-hazard early warning and risk information. Target E had a 2020 horizon; the others use 2030.

Hence resilience is a measured system property, not the existence of one technology or plan.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines and determines resilience, lists framework elements and all seven Sendai target themes, then applies them critically.

### SHARED PYQ 1 — 2026 GS-III Q18 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording (obvious OCR punctuation normalised):** “Community participation is the cornerstone of effective disaster management”. Analyse this statement with suitable examples from India. Also discuss the challenges to community participation and measures to strengthen it.

**Primary owner:** Disaster Management Topic 03. The punctuation is retained from the official local English extraction; forest-fire participation is a direct shared example.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\QP-CSM-26-010926-GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 240 words):**

Community participation is foundational because local people are the first observers, affected rights-holders and immediate responders; their knowledge and trust determine whether warnings become action. It is not, however, a substitute for State capacity.

In forest-fire management, residents and forest-dependent groups can identify recurrent ignition sites, report smoke early, maintain agreed fire lines, support evacuation and explain seasonal NTFP, pasture or farming practices. Joint Forest Fire Management, the National Action Plan on Forest Fires and the FPM Scheme recognise awareness, training and partnership. Comparable Indian examples include cyclone shelters managed with local volunteers and community-based flood/landslide warning.

Participation faces unequal tenure and benefit-sharing, distrust of forest authorities, unpaid or unsafe labour expectations, gender/caste/tribal exclusion, seasonal migration, inaccessible messages, weak training/equipment and token consultation after plans are fixed. Punitive restrictions without livelihood alternatives may drive burning underground rather than prevent it.

Strengthening measures include legally and administratively defined local roles; paid, insured and trained crews; representation of women and marginal groups; co-designed calendars for permitted resource use; local-language redundant alerts; grievance and feedback systems; school/SHG/PRI involvement; joint drills; transparent use of FPM support; and integration of community observations with FSI and professional incident command.

The source's critique is decisive: authorities cannot exclude communities from routine resource governance yet expect support only in crisis. Participation becomes a cornerstone when it redistributes voice, resources and accountability throughout prevention, response and recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses why participation works, gives Indian examples, addresses structural barriers and proposes institutional remedies without romanticising volunteerism.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish surface, ground and crown fires and explain why the classification matters for response.

**Model answer (about 140 words):**

A **surface fire** burns litter, grasses, shrubs and low debris. It may spread rapidly through fine fuel and can climb “ladder fuels” into the canopy. A **ground fire** smoulders in duff, peat, roots or organic soil; it can remain hidden and re-emerge after visible flame is gone. A **crown fire** spreads through tree or shrub canopies, often supported by surface fire and wind, and may be too intense for direct attack.

Classification changes response. Surface fire may permit fire-line or fuel-break use and direct containment where conditions are safe. Ground fire requires heat detection, deep mop-up and prolonged patrol. Crown fire prioritises evacuation, indirect control and protection of crews and settlements. Spotting can breach any line.

The categories can coexist or transition, so they are not fixed labels from one satellite image. Current fuel, weather, terrain and field verification must determine tactics.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three, links each to a response implication and adds transition/verification limits.

### ORIGINAL 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why is a satellite forest-fire alert not equivalent to successful suppression?

**Model answer (about 135 words):**

A satellite alert is an information output. FSI processes MODIS and SNPP-VIIRS thermal detections and disseminates near-real-time alerts, helping State authorities locate possible fire quickly. Large-fire tracking and danger ratings further support prioritisation.

Suppression is a field outcome. Authorities must verify the hotspot, identify fuel, weather, terrain and exposed values, mobilise trained crews and equipment, secure communications and safe access, choose an appropriate containment method, protect residents and complete mop-up. Cloud, satellite pass timing, resolution and false or ambiguous heat sources can affect detection; steep terrain, wind, poor access and staffing can delay control.

Therefore, performance should be measured from detection to verification, arrival, containment, re-ignition and avoided harm. Alert count or portal availability proves capability, not extinguishment or ecological recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** It follows the full alert-to-action chain and identifies technological and operational limits.

### ORIGINAL 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Examine the role of forest-dependent communities in preventing and managing forest fires in India.

**Model answer (about 225 words):**

Forest-dependent communities influence fire risk because daily livelihood practices—NTFP collection, pasture use, shifting cultivation and access—interact with ignition and fuel. They also possess fine-grained knowledge of paths, water, recurrent fire sites and vulnerable households.

Their preventive role includes co-designing safe seasonal-use rules, maintaining agreed fire lines, reducing hazardous fuel around assets, reporting smoke and discouraging harmful ignition. During response, trained local teams can provide early verification, communication, evacuation support and logistical knowledge. In recovery, they can monitor erosion, invasive species, regeneration and livelihood loss.

Indian policy recognises this role through Joint Forest Fire Management ideas, the 2018 National Action Plan on Forest Fires and community/training support under the FPM Scheme. Yet the local source identifies a structural contradiction: communities are often excluded from routine forest-resource decisions but expected to assist during crisis.

Challenges include insecure or contested rights, distrust, benefit-sharing failures, unpaid dangerous labour, gender/tribal exclusion, inadequate equipment/insurance and top-down bans that ignore livelihood needs. Participation may also be captured by local elites.

Reform should give representative institutions defined roles, remuneration, insurance, training and protective equipment; align resource-use calendars and incentives; provide grievance channels and transparent funds; integrate local reports with FSI alerts; and retain professional incident command.

Community partnership is therefore prevention infrastructure, but the State remains accountable for safety, technology and equitable governance.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers all cycle stages, names policy anchors, diagnoses power/incentive barriers and avoids transferring State duty.

### ORIGINAL 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate India's forest-fire monitoring architecture and identify the reforms needed to convert alerts into reduced loss.

**Model answer (about 235 words):**

India has a layered national information architecture. FSI's Fire Danger Rating uses weather, fuel-related indices, terrain, forest type and archives for pre-fire potential. MODIS and finer-resolution SNPP-VIIRS hotspots support automated near-real-time detection; Large Forest Fire Monitoring tracks candidate events; Van Agni 3.0 displays alerts, prone areas and related layers.

This architecture improves spatial coverage, prioritisation and escalation. FSI states alerts may be produced at least six times in 24 hours and sent to State forest officials/subscribers. Its 2004–2021 detection analysis also identifies extremely and very highly fire-prone areas for targeted resources.

However, four gaps prevent automatic loss reduction. First, hotspot, fire event, burned area and severity are different measures, affected by sensors and field verification. Second, alerts may not reach the correct beat staff or community in usable form. Third, steep terrain, weak access, crews, water, equipment and command can delay suppression. Fourth, containment says little about smoke, erosion, ecological recovery or recurrence.

Reforms should integrate verified local contacts and escalation rules; publish alert-to-verification/response metrics; pre-position trained and protected crews; maintain access, water and equipment; add community reporting; map WUI exposure; standardise post-fire severity and watershed assessment; and compare consistent datasets.

Thus India's detection layer is a major asset, but its success must be judged at the field and recovery end of the chain.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately describes FSI products, evaluates their value and limits, and proposes outcome-linked reforms.

### ORIGINAL 5 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** “Effective forest-fire policy must balance prevention, suppression and ecological fire management.” Critically discuss.

**Model answer (about 275 words):**

Forest fire is both a biophysical process and a governance problem. Policy that treats every fire as identical may either under-react to destructive events or suppress ecological processes indiscriminately.

**Prevention** should address ignition and hazardous fuel: co-designed resource-use practices, patrols, maintained fire lines/fuel breaks, safe tourism and farm burning, enforcement against harmful acts, and WUI land-use control. The FPM Scheme and National Action Plan provide a framework, while FSI danger ratings allow seasonal targeting.

**Suppression** protects life and assets when fire is uncontrolled. Near-real-time alerts must connect to field verification, trained crews, incident command, safe access, water/equipment, evacuation and mop-up. Crown fire, spotting and hidden ground fire require different tactics. Yet aggressive suppression without ecological context can be costly, dangerous and, in some fire-adapted systems, permit fuel accumulation.

**Ecological management** asks what fire regime—frequency, season, intensity and severity—the ecosystem can tolerate or require. Scientifically justified prescribed burning may reduce selected fuels or meet ecological objectives, but it is not a licence for open burning. It needs legal authorisation, suitable weather, trained crews, smoke management, escape plans, monitoring and community consent. Blanket litter removal, species removal or post-fire plantation can damage nutrients, habitat and natural regeneration.

The balance is place-specific. Dense WUI and critical watersheds justify strong prevention; remote adapted ecosystems may use carefully governed fire; extreme weather can require life-first suppression. After containment, burn severity, erosion, water, invasive species and livelihoods must guide recovery.

Policy success is therefore not hectares treated or fires extinguished alone, but fewer avoidable losses, safe crews, appropriate ecological condition and lower recurrence.

**Why this earns marks:** It critically weighs all three approaches, identifies conditions and safeguards, and ends with outcome criteria.

### ORIGINAL 6 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Design a forest-fire risk-management framework for an Indian State with high fire-proneness and expanding wildland–urban interface.

**Model answer (about 285 words):**

The State should treat forest fire as a seasonal risk system linking ignition, fuel, weather, terrain, settlements, health and operational capacity.

**Risk intelligence:** Combine FSI's long-term fire-proneness classes, Fire Danger Rating, MODIS/SNPP-VIIRS alerts and large-fire tracking with State data on fuels, access, water, past severity, WUI assets and vulnerable people. Keep hotspot, event, burned area and damage metrics separate.

**Prevention:** Zone or condition new WUI construction; create defensible space around critical facilities; maintain ecologically suitable fire lines and access; regulate high-risk seasonal activities; co-design NTFP, pasture and agricultural-burning practices; and use prescribed fire only under authorised scientific protocols.

**Preparedness and response:** Establish one incident-command structure linking forest, district, fire, police, health, utilities and community teams. Maintain trained, paid and insured local crews, protective equipment, water points, communications, lookout/escape/safety arrangements and evacuation shelters. Define alert verification and escalation times, protect hospitals and power/telecom, and issue smoke-health guidance.

**Recovery:** Map burn severity, stabilise erosion-prone slopes and water sources, monitor invasives and re-ignition, favour natural/native regeneration where suitable, restore livelihoods and revise risk maps and fuel plans.

**Governance:** Use the National Action Plan and FPM support but publish State-level staffing, maintenance, response and expenditure evidence. Track detection-to-verification, arrival, containment, injuries, WUI loss, smoke exposure, repeat fire, watershed condition and livelihood recovery.

Trade-offs require transparency: clearance may harm ecology; closure may restrict livelihoods; suppression may build future fuel; and prescribed fire carries escape/smoke risk. Representative community institutions and independent review can make these choices legitimate.

**Why this earns marks:** It provides a State-scale system from data to recovery, addresses WUI and community equity, and uses measurable outcomes.
