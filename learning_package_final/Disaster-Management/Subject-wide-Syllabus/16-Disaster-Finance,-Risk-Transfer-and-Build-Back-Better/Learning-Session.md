#### 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **National Disaster Response Fund (the fund, not the force)** | Centrally managed fund under **s. 46** of the DM Act for emergency response/relief/rehabilitation expenses; used when State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) capacity is insufficient; the National Calamity Contingency Fund (NCCF, 11th Finance Commission) was merged into it (PDF p. 15). |

#### 3. Risk/problem mechanism

1. **Fund-escalation logic**: relief needs first draw on the State's own
   SDRF; only when State-level funds are insufficient does Central
   assistance flow via the National Disaster Response Fund (PDF p. 15) —
   a two-tier, need-triggered escalation, not automatic Central funding.
2. **2019 Standing Committee on Finance findings** (Chair: Dr. M.
   Veerappa Moily, "Central Assistance for Disaster Management and
   Relief"): recommended enhancing SDRF/NDRF assistance scales to cover
   major expenditure heads (government buildings, transmission
   stations); operationalising a separate Disaster Mitigation Fund;
   earmarking 10% of centrally sponsored scheme allocations for
   permanent restoration; and a 15% annual SDRF corpus increase for
   2020-25 given the funding gap between State demand and Central
   release (PDF p. 15) — ⚠️ document-period recommendations requiring
   current-status verification.
3. **CDRI's financing logic**: standardised risk data and design
   standards are prerequisites for both budget reserve funds and
   catastrophe bonds to be priced/structured correctly — financing
   depends on the risk-assessment thematic area functioning first (PDF
   p. 64).

#### 4. Disaster-management cycle application

- ✅ **Pre-disaster**: mitigation investment (recommended Disaster
  Mitigation Fund) and risk-transfer instruments (insurance, catastrophe
  bonds) reduce or shift future financial exposure before an event
  occurs.
- ✅ **Response**: SDRF/NDRF finance immediate relief.
- ✅ **Recovery**: Build Back Better principle applied "not only for the
  structural design of the infrastructure but also in terms of
  management systems around it" (PDF p. 64) — financing reconstruction
  that reduces future risk, not merely restores the prior state.

#### 1. Advanced proposition

**Thesis:** ⚠️ India's disaster-finance architecture was, until 2021,
structured almost entirely around *ex-post* relief funding (SDRF/NDRF,

advanced argument is therefore no longer "India has no mitigation fund";
it is that **India has priced mitigation at one-fifth of response, and
capped it there by design**. **Boundary:** ❌ this topic does not cover

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Disaster Finance, Risk Transfer and Build Back Better: FINANCE TIMING STATUTORY FUNDS RISK LAYERS AND TRIGGERS MAP

1. **Ex-ante finance:** Ex-ante disaster finance is arranged before an event for prevention, mitigation, preparedness, contingent response or pre-agreed recovery; timing alone does not make an instrument risk reducing.
2. **Ex-post finance:** Ex-post finance is mobilised after impact through budget reallocation, supplementary grants, borrowing, relief funds, appeals or other measures; it can be necessary but may be slow and fiscally disruptive.
3. **Four-fund statutory grid:** The Disaster Management Act separates the National Disaster Response Fund under section 46, National Disaster Mitigation Fund under section 47, and State response and mitigation funds under section 48; fund and force must never be confused.
4. **Response versus mitigation funds:** Response funds meet eligible post-event response and relief needs, whereas mitigation funds finance ex-ante risk reduction; a response payment is not proof that future risk was reduced.
5. **Budgetary and contingency reserves:** Annual budget provisions and contingency reserves retain risk on the public balance sheet but improve liquidity; adequacy depends on rules, replenishment, accessibility and the scale and timing of loss.
6. **Pre-arranged finance:** Pre-arranged finance fixes eligibility, trigger, amount or drawdown procedure before a disaster so funds can arrive faster; speed does not ensure good targeting, sufficient volume or risk reduction.
7. **Risk layering:** Risk layering matches frequent lower-severity losses, less frequent medium losses and rare severe losses with different combinations of retention, reserves, contingent credit, insurance and external support.
8. **Sovereign subnational household layers:** The Union, States, local bodies, utilities, firms and households face different fiscal capacities and loss types; transferring sovereign risk does not automatically protect uninsured households or municipal services.
9. **Indemnity insurance:** Indemnity insurance pays for verified covered loss subject to policy terms, exclusions, deductibles and limits; loss assessment supports alignment but may delay settlement.
10. **Parametric insurance:** Parametric insurance pays when a pre-agreed measurable index crosses a threshold, regardless of exact realised loss; it can be rapid but creates basis risk.
11. **Basis risk:** Basis risk is the mismatch between a parametric payout and actual loss, including no or low payout despite severe local harm or a payout where losses are limited.
12. **Risk pools:** A risk pool combines diversified participants or exposures and common financing arrangements to spread volatility; correlation, governance, pricing and entry rules determine whether diversification is real.
13. **Catastrophe bonds:** A catastrophe bond transfers a defined layer of catastrophe risk to capital-market investors through specified triggers and loss of principal or interest; it should be used conceptually unless a dated official deployment is verified.
14. **Moral hazard and adverse selection:** Moral hazard is changed behaviour after protection reduces the perceived cost of loss, while adverse selection arises when higher-risk participants are more likely to seek cover; design, pricing and safeguards address different problems.
15. **Fiscal protection:** Fiscal protection aims to preserve timely government financing and essential public services after shocks through risk assessment, layered instruments, transparent rules and debt or budget safeguards.
16. **Damage loss and needs assessment:** Transparent damage, economic-loss and recovery-needs assessments serve different purposes and baselines; none should be substituted for another merely because each produces a monetary estimate.
17. **Build Back Better:** Build Back Better uses recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction to reduce future risk through safer location, standards, services, institutions and livelihoods rather than recreating pre-disaster vulnerability.
18. **BBB safeguards:** Build Back Better requires participation, tenure and livelihood safeguards, environmental assessment, accessibility, maintenance finance and avoidance of rushed rebuilding in hazardous locations.
19. **Incentive and affordability balance:** Deductibles, co-financing, risk-based signals and mitigation conditions can reduce moral hazard, but unaffordable pricing can exclude high-risk low-income households and local bodies.
20. **Finance-outcome firewall:** A fund corpus, allocation rule, policy, premium, bond, pool, payout, sanction or reconstruction budget proves finance or transfer only; coverage, timeliness, equity, safer rebuilding and reduced fiscal loss require separate evidence.

- Do not confuse NDRF-the-fund with NDRF-the-force.
