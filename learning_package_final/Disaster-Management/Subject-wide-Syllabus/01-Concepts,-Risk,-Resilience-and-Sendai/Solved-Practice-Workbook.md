## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Q1. A severe cyclone remains offshore and causes no significant disruption on land. Which conclusion is conceptually correct?

A. A hazard occurred, but intensity alone cannot make it a disaster without material exposure and serious disruption.
B. A disaster occurred because physical intensity determines disaster status even when exposed land systems remain undisrupted.
C. No hazard occurred because damaging potential is recognised only when an official assessment records losses.
D. Offshore location necessarily makes risk negligible for navigation, livelihoods and coastal ecosystems.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Hazard denotes damaging potential; disaster requires serious disruption arising through interaction with exposure, vulnerability and capacity.
- **B — Incorrect.** Intensity alone does not establish a disaster; the social and functional consequences matter.
- **C — Incorrect.** A hazard can exist before loss occurs and does not depend on an official damage assessment.
- **D — Incorrect.** Offshore location does not necessarily make risk to navigation, livelihoods or coastal ecosystems negligible.

**Examiner trap:** equating physical magnitude with disaster status.

### Q2. Which pair is correctly distinguished?

A. Exposure describes susceptibility to harm; vulnerability identifies people or assets located within the hazard zone.
B. Exposure places people or assets in harm’s way; vulnerability makes those exposed elements susceptible to damage.
C. Exposure describes recovery capacity; vulnerability estimates the probability and physical intensity of the hazardous event.
D. Exposure measures expected monetary loss; vulnerability denotes residual risk transferred through insurance or contingency finance.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** It reverses the two concepts.
- **B — Correct.** Exposure concerns presence in a hazard-prone location; vulnerability concerns conditions that increase susceptibility.
- **C — Incorrect.** Ability to recover belongs to capacity or resilience, and hazard probability is not vulnerability.
- **D — Incorrect.** Expected loss concerns risk, while insurance and contingency finance are residual-risk instruments.

**Examiner trap:** treating “being there” and “being susceptible” as synonyms.

### Q3. Consider the following statements about capacity:

1. It includes strengths, attributes and resources usable for reducing disaster risk.
2. It is exhausted by the formal existence of equipment and written plans.
3. It may include institutions, skills, finance, leadership and social networks.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. Only statement 1
B. Only statements 2 and 3
C. Only statements 1 and 3
D. All three statements

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Statement 3 is also correct.
- **B — Incorrect.** Statement 2 is false because nominal assets are not capacity unless they are usable.
- **C — Correct.** Statements 1 and 3 capture the multidimensional and operational character of capacity.
- **D — Incorrect.** A plan or equipment inventory alone does not prove functioning capacity.

**Examiner trap:** confusing sanctioned or listed capacity with deployable capacity.

### Q4. Why should the expression “risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity” be used cautiously?

A. It excludes exposure and vulnerability whenever hazard probability is expressed qualitatively rather than numerically.
B. It applies only to technological hazards with quantified, consistent records, not natural or biological hazards.
C. It assumes that additional capacity always removes residual, compound and cascading risks from the system.
D. It can only organise causation, while precise estimation requires defined, event-specific data and measurable relationships.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Qualitative probability does not make exposure or vulnerability irrelevant to risk analysis.
- **B — Incorrect.** The heuristic can organise analysis across natural, biological and technological hazard families.
- **C — Incorrect.** Added capacity may reduce risk but does not always remove residual, compound or cascading risk.
- **D — Correct.** The expression organises causal factors; precise estimation needs defined data and measurable event-specific relationships.

**Examiner trap:** presenting a conceptual relation as an exact calculator.

### Q5. Which is the best example of a socio-natural hazard?

A. Urban flooding intensified by blocked drainage, wetland loss and construction over natural channels.
B. A meteorite entering the atmosphere above an uninhabited ocean, without human alteration of the process.
C. A factory explosion caused solely by defective storage, weak maintenance and unsafe operating procedures.
D. A volcanic eruption contained within a monitored exclusion zone established around the crater.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** A natural rainfall process is amplified by human alteration of drainage, land cover and exposure.
- **B — Incorrect.** This is a natural hazard without the human amplification described by “socio-natural.”
- **C — Incorrect.** This is an anthropogenic or technological hazard.
- **D — Incorrect.** This remains a natural hazard; controlled exposure does not make it socio-natural.

**Examiner trap:** assuming every event with human losses is anthropogenic.

### Q6. Which planning implication most clearly distinguishes slow-onset from rapid-onset risk?

A. Slow-onset processes need only minimal preparedness because gradual change provides sufficient time for spontaneous adjustment.
B. Slow-onset risk cannot rely on short warning windows alone, whereas rapid-onset risk often requires warning and evacuation.
C. Rapid onset necessarily identifies natural hazards, while slow onset identifies hazards produced through human activity or technological failure.
D. Rapid-onset events favour response planning, whereas advance mitigation is useful mainly for gradual and predictable processes.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** The canonical source expressly requires slow-onset risks to be reflected in preparedness.
- **B — Correct.** Slow-onset risk needs sustained monitoring and adaptation, while rapid onset often demands short-lead warning and evacuation.
- **C — Incorrect.** Onset speed and hazard origin are separate classifications.
- **D — Incorrect.** Advance mitigation is important for rapid-onset hazards as well as gradual processes.

**Examiner trap:** treating gradual onset as low importance.

### Q7. A municipal authority prohibits new construction in an active floodway. This is primarily:

A. compensatory risk management because the rule may reduce future relief and insurance expenditure.
B. corrective risk management because the authority addresses an established flood hazard already present within its jurisdiction.
C. prospective risk management because it acts only on proposed construction before new exposure is created.
D. response because a public authority takes an enforceable decision concerning an identified hazard.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Compensatory management addresses residual risk through preparedness, response, recovery or finance.
- **B — Incorrect.** An existing hazard does not make the measure corrective; the rule governs proposed, not embedded, exposure.
- **C — Correct.** The restriction acts on proposed construction and prevents future development from creating new exposure.
- **D — Incorrect.** An enforceable public decision is not automatically response, which concerns immediate action around impact.

**Examiner trap:** classifying by who acts rather than what risk is addressed.

### Q8. Which measure is the clearest example of corrective disaster risk management?

A. Purchasing insurance to finance losses remaining after feasible structural and non-structural risk reduction.
B. Reserving an undeveloped coastal buffer within the mapped surge zone to prevent future construction and exposure.
C. Pre-positioning relief stocks and trained emergency teams before the annual cyclone season begins.
D. Retrofitting an existing hospital whose structure falls below the applicable seismic safety standard.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Insurance is compensatory or residual-risk management.
- **B — Incorrect.** Preventing new coastal exposure is prospective management.
- **C — Incorrect.** Preparedness manages residual risk and is compensatory.
- **D — Correct.** Retrofitting reduces risk already embedded in an existing critical facility.

**Examiner trap:** confusing all pre-disaster action with prospective management.

### Q9. Which set falls primarily within compensatory disaster risk management?

A. Preparedness, response, recovery, contingency finance and insurance for residual risk.
B. No-build zoning, prospective highway redesign, setback regulation and risk-sensitive approval of proposed development.
C. Bridge retrofitting, relocation of exposed households, slope stabilisation and correction of unsafe construction.
D. Hazard mapping, risk registers, published atlases and declarations that all documented risk has been eliminated.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** UNDRR places preparedness, response, recovery and financial instruments under compensatory or residual-risk management.
- **B — Incorrect.** These are prospective measures that prevent proposed development from creating new risk.
- **C — Incorrect.** These are corrective measures addressing risk embedded in existing settlements and infrastructure.
- **D — Incorrect.** Mapping, registers and atlases inform action but do not eliminate documented risk.

**Examiner trap:** reducing compensatory management to post-disaster cash compensation.

### Q10. Which statement correctly distinguishes prevention from mitigation?

A. Prevention uses only structural works; mitigation instead relies on regulatory and behavioural measures.
B. Prevention avoids risk where feasible; mitigation cannot remove all residual risk and instead lessens adverse impacts.
C. Prevention begins after impact, whereas mitigation operates during rehabilitation, reconstruction and long-term recovery.
D. Prevention transfers financial loss, whereas mitigation accepts residual consequences without further risk-reduction intervention.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Both prevention and mitigation may use structural, regulatory and behavioural approaches.
- **B — Correct.** Prevention seeks feasible avoidance; mitigation lessens impacts where some risk remains.
- **C — Incorrect.** Both are principally pre-disaster risk-reduction functions rather than post-impact recovery stages.
- **D — Incorrect.** Financial transfer and risk acceptance are distinct treatments, not prevention and mitigation.

**Examiner trap:** using prevention and mitigation interchangeably.

### Q11. Which activity belongs most directly to preparedness rather than response?

A. Conducting coordinated urban search, rescue and casualty evacuation immediately after occupied buildings collapse.
B. Providing emergency shelter, food, water and medical relief throughout the immediate humanitarian aftermath.
C. Rehearsing accessible evacuation and testing warning protocols before the cyclone season.
D. Reconstructing damaged housing, lifeline infrastructure and public facilities to a safer standard.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Search and rescue is response.
- **B — Incorrect.** Immediate relief is part of response/crisis management.
- **C — Correct.** Plans, drills, warning procedures and trained capacity are preparedness.
- **D — Incorrect.** Safer reconstruction belongs to recovery and Build Back Better.

**Examiner trap:** calling every action connected with an emergency “response.”

### Q12. Which sequence best represents the post-impact progression without conflating its stages?

A. Damage assessment → relief → prevention → evacuation → mitigation → exposure mapping → recovery
B. Forecast → hazard occurrence → vulnerability creation → relief → preparedness → rehabilitation → mitigation
C. Reconstruction → response → relief → recovery → damage assessment → rehabilitation → preparedness
D. Response and relief → rehabilitation → reconstruction → recovery with Build Back Better

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** It places pre-disaster prevention, evacuation and mitigation after impact and relief.
- **B — Incorrect.** It treats vulnerability creation and preparedness as post-impact stages and scrambles rehabilitation.
- **C — Incorrect.** It begins with reconstruction before response and places damage assessment after recovery.
- **D — Correct.** It moves from urgent life-saving and assistance to restoration, rebuilding and long-term recovery.

**Examiner trap:** treating rehabilitation, reconstruction and recovery as identical.

### Q13. Extensive disaster risk is best described as:

A. Frequent, relatively low-severity local losses that accumulate through recurring floods, landslides, storms or drought over time.
B. Infrequent catastrophic losses generated exclusively when major hazards meet concentrated exposure and very high vulnerability.
C. Residual financial losses shared among insurers, governments, firms and exposed households after mitigation.
D. Cascading disruption transmitted across interdependent sectors after failure of a critical service.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** This follows UNDRR’s definition and highlights cumulative local loss.
- **B — Incorrect.** Infrequent catastrophic loss with concentrated exposure and vulnerability describes intensive risk.
- **C — Incorrect.** Sharing residual financial loss describes risk transfer rather than extensive risk.
- **D — Incorrect.** Failure transmitted across interdependent sectors describes systemic propagation.

**Examiner trap:** overlooking the aggregate burden of recurrent small events.

### Q14. Intensive disaster risk is most closely associated with:

A. Recurrent local losses with low individual severity but a substantial cumulative burden over time.
B. High-severity, low-frequency disasters where major hazards meet concentrated exposure and vulnerability.
C. Slow-onset processes whose duration, rather than severity, frequency or exposure, determines risk intensity.
D. Residual losses managed through contingency funds, insurance, preparedness and emergency-response arrangements.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** That is extensive risk.
- **B — Correct.** UNDRR links intensive risk to major hazards and concentrated exposure/vulnerability.
- **C — Incorrect.** Onset duration and intensive/extensive risk are different axes.
- **D — Incorrect.** Residual-risk financing is compensatory management, not a risk-frequency category.

**Examiner trap:** equating “intensive” with long duration rather than severity-frequency pattern.

### Q15. Which situation most clearly illustrates systemic risk?

A. A household loses one uninsured appliance during waterlogging that causes no wider service disruption.
B. A village experiences repeated, minor crop losses without disruption to connected markets, services or infrastructure.
C. Flooded substations disable electricity, interrupting water, hospitals, telecommunications and digital payments across a city.
D. A proposed industrial zone is shifted away from a mapped chemical-risk corridor before construction begins.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** This is an individual loss, not cross-system propagation.
- **B — Incorrect.** This better illustrates extensive, recurrent risk.
- **C — Correct.** Interdependent infrastructure transmits failure across sectors.
- **D — Incorrect.** This is prospective risk management.

**Examiner trap:** using “systemic” merely as a synonym for severe.

### Q16. Which statement best captures disaster risk reduction?

A. It centres on specialist-force response and emergency relief after serious disruption reaches exposed communities.
B. It covers only engineering structures that lower physical hazard intensity, while governance remains outside its scope.
C. It is completed when national and local disaster-management plans are formally approved and published.
D. DRR cannot be confined to response because it prevents new risk, reduces existing risk and manages residual risk.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Specialist response and relief are components of DRR, not its organising centre or full scope.
- **B — Incorrect.** DRR also includes governance, ecosystem, social, financial and preparedness measures.
- **C — Incorrect.** Approved plans are inputs; implementation and demonstrated outcomes remain necessary.
- **D — Correct.** DRR extends beyond response by integrating prospective, corrective and compensatory risk management.

**Examiner trap:** reducing DRR to relief or infrastructure.

### Q17. Which formulation of resilience is most complete?

A. The ability to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover while restoring essential functions.
B. The speed, cost efficiency and physical volume of rebuilding assets to their previous condition.
C. The number, equipment, mobility and command structure of emergency responders available to national authorities.
D. The permanent absence of all hazards, exposure, vulnerability and disruption within a community’s administrative boundary.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** It captures continuity, recovery, adaptation and transformation.
- **B — Incorrect.** Rebuilding the previous unsafe condition can recreate risk.
- **C — Incorrect.** Responder numbers, equipment, mobility and command are capacity inputs, not the whole resilience concept.
- **D — Incorrect.** Resilience concerns system performance despite risk, not the absence of hazards, exposure or disruption.

**Examiner trap:** defining resilience only as “bouncing back.”

### Q18. In a resilience framework, redundancy primarily means:

A. removing all duplication from essential systems to minimise expenditure and simplify operational control.
B. maintaining substitutes, backups or alternate pathways when a component fails.
C. relying on one highly specialised agency as the central provider for each essential service.
D. restoring services only after the complete damage assessment is final and reconstruction resources are sanctioned.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Some duplication is deliberate because it prevents single-point failure.
- **B — Correct.** Alternate power, routes, suppliers or communication channels preserve function.
- **C — Incorrect.** Concentration can create a single point of failure.
- **D — Incorrect.** Essential services often require immediate continuity or staged restoration.

**Examiner trap:** treating efficiency and resilience as identical design goals.

### Q19. Which indicator most directly tests resilience as an outcome rather than an input?

A. A multi-sector resilience strategy has been drafted, consulted upon and formally approved.
B. Allocated funds and administrative sanctions necessarily establish the resilience of identified critical infrastructure.
C. Essential health services remain available and recover within a measured time after disruption.
D. A city has joined an international resilience platform, filed a baseline profile and completed registration.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** A strategy is a governance input.
- **B — Incorrect.** Allocation and sanction do not necessarily prove expenditure, completion or resilient performance.
- **C — Correct.** Continuity and restoration time measure system performance.
- **D — Incorrect.** Membership can enable support but does not demonstrate reduced loss or continuity.

**Examiner trap:** substituting institutional activity for verified outcomes.

### Q20. Build Back Better is best understood as:

A. Restoring damaged assets exactly as they existed before the hazardous event.
B. Prioritising visible reconstruction projects over livelihoods, ecosystems, inclusion and continuity of basic services.
C. Beginning risk reduction only after physical reconstruction and restoration of public services are completed.
D. Using recovery and reconstruction to reduce risk while restoring or improving essential functions.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Identical rebuilding may reproduce unsafe siting or design.
- **B — Incorrect.** BBB includes livelihoods, services, inclusion and systems.
- **C — Incorrect.** Risk reduction must be integrated into recovery decisions.
- **D — Correct.** This reflects Sendai Priority 4 and the recovery definition.

**Examiner trap:** confusing faster rebuilding with safer recovery.

### Q21. Which statement about the Sendai Framework is correct?

A. It is a voluntary, non-binding framework adopted on 18 March 2015 for the 2015–2030 period.
B. It is a binding multilateral treaty whose compliance sanctions are administered directly by UNDRR.
C. It became operational only after States enacted uniform domestic disaster laws and reporting mechanisms.
D. It governs emergency response to major natural hazards rather than recovery or risk prevention.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** The adoption date, period and legal character are verified by UNDRR.
- **B — Incorrect.** UNDRR supports implementation and monitoring; Sendai is not an enforceable treaty.
- **C — Incorrect.** The framework did not depend on uniform domestic laws or reporting mechanisms becoming operational.
- **D — Incorrect.** It addresses prevention, reduction, preparedness, response and recovery across multiple hazard types.

**Examiner trap:** inventing legal force from UN adoption.

### Q22. Sendai Priority 1 requires disaster risk management to begin with:

A. Negotiating post-disaster reconstruction contracts before assessing hazards, exposure, vulnerability or capacity.
B. Understanding hazard, exposure, vulnerability, capacity and environmental dimensions of disaster risk.
C. Securing international financial transfers before undertaking national, local or sector-specific risk assessment.
D. Building preparedness, community resilience and response capacity solely through deployment of specialist forces.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Reconstruction belongs mainly to recovery and Priority 4; it does not precede risk understanding.
- **B — Correct.** Risk knowledge is the basis for assessment and all later interventions.
- **C — Incorrect.** Cooperation can support action but cannot replace risk understanding.
- **D — Incorrect.** Preparedness is broader than force deployment.

**Examiner trap:** beginning with solutions before diagnosing risk.

### Q23. Match the Sendai priorities with their central emphasis:

1. Priority 1 — Understanding risk
2. Priority 2 — Strengthening risk governance
3. Priority 3 — Investing in DRR for resilience
4. Priority 4 — Preparedness, effective response and Build Back Better

Which of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

A. Pairs 1 and 2 only
B. Pairs 2, 3 and 4 only
C. All four pairs
D. Pairs 1 and 4 only

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Priorities 3 and 4 are also correctly matched.
- **B — Incorrect.** Priority 1 is also correctly matched.
- **C — Correct.** All four formulations reproduce the official priority structure.
- **D — Incorrect.** It omits the correctly matched governance and investment priorities.

**Examiner trap:** remembering the count but scrambling the sequence.

### Q24. Which action most directly expresses Sendai Priority 4?

A. Mapping socioeconomic vulnerability and exposed assets before approving a major infrastructure project.
B. Creating a statutory coordination mechanism that assigns risk-governance duties across institutions.
C. Financing seismic retrofits for an existing hospital before the next damaging earthquake.
D. Acting anticipatorily and using reconstruction to improve future safety and restore essential services.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** This primarily supports Priority 1.
- **B — Incorrect.** This primarily supports Priority 2.
- **C — Incorrect.** This primarily supports Priority 3 and corrective risk management.
- **D — Correct.** Preparedness, effective response and BBB are the core of Priority 4.

**Examiner trap:** placing every resilience measure under Priority 3.

### Q25. Which group correctly represents Sendai Targets A–D?

A. Mortality; affected people; economic loss relative to global GDP; critical infrastructure and services.
B. National and local strategies; international cooperation; multi-hazard warning systems and accessible risk information.
C. Risk knowledge; risk governance; resilience investment; preparedness, response and Build Back Better.
D. Risk avoidance; existing-risk reduction; residual-risk preparedness; financial transfer and social protection.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** These are the four loss/outcome targets.
- **B — Incorrect.** These elements belong to Targets E–G, with national and local strategies combined in Target E.
- **C — Incorrect.** These are the four priorities, not targets.
- **D — Incorrect.** These are risk-treatment choices.

**Examiner trap:** confusing priorities with targets.

### Q26. Which statement correctly describes Sendai Targets E–G?

A. They measure mortality, affected people, economic loss and disruption of critical infrastructure and services.
B. They concern DRR strategies, international cooperation, multi-hazard warning systems and accessible risk information.
C. They all share a 2020 deadline and record completed national implementation against uniform standards.
D. They establish enforceable national quotas supported by international compliance, review and sanction mechanisms.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Mortality, affected people, economic loss and critical-service disruption belong to Targets A–D.
- **B — Correct.** E–G are chiefly means-of-implementation or capacity targets.
- **C — Incorrect.** Only Target E carried the 2020 deadline; F and G run to 2030.
- **D — Incorrect.** Sendai is voluntary and its targets are global.

**Examiner trap:** treating all seven targets as identical in type and deadline.

### Q27. Which Sendai target alone carried a 2020 deadline?

A. Only Target A on mortality.
B. Only Target C on direct economic loss.
C. Only Target E on national and local DRR strategies.
D. Only Target G on multi-hazard early warning and risk information.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Target A uses the 2030 horizon and a decadal-average comparison.
- **B — Incorrect.** Target C is due by 2030.
- **C — Correct.** Target E sought a substantial increase in countries with national and local DRR strategies by 2020.
- **D — Incorrect.** Target G is due by 2030.

**Examiner trap:** assigning the 2020 deadline to all means targets.

### Q28. Which interpretation of Target E is defensible?

A. Its deadline proves every country had compliant national and local strategies by 2020.
B. Missing its deadline created a legal breach enforceable through an international court.
C. Publishing a national strategy necessarily proves that mortality and economic losses subsequently declined.
D. It created a measurable strategy milestone, but achievement requires evidence and Sendai remains non-binding.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** A deadline is not evidence of universal achievement.
- **B — Incorrect.** Sendai creates no such judicial enforcement mechanism.
- **C — Incorrect.** Strategy adoption is an input, not proof of loss reduction.
- **D — Correct.** It preserves both the monitoring value and legal-status boundary.

**Examiner trap:** converting deadline into achievement or legal compulsion.

### Q29. Which statement accurately describes Sendai Target C?

A. It seeks to reduce direct disaster economic loss relative to global GDP by 2030.
B. It seeks to eliminate all insured and uninsured disaster losses worldwide before the Target E deadline.
C. It measures public expenditure devoted to emergency response, relief distribution and temporary shelter.
D. It assigns identical national disaster-loss ceilings to high-income, middle-income and low-income economies.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** This is the target’s verified substance.
- **B — Incorrect.** The framework seeks reduction rather than elimination, and Target E’s deadline does not govern Target C.
- **C — Incorrect.** Direct economic loss is broader than emergency response, relief and temporary-shelter expenditure.
- **D — Incorrect.** Target C is global and does not assign identical ceilings across differently sized economies.

**Examiner trap:** replacing “reduce loss relative to global GDP” with “eliminate loss.”

### Q30. Target D links resilience most directly to:

A. The number of developing countries receiving sustained, adequate and timely international cooperation for DRR implementation.
B. Damage to critical infrastructure and disruption of basic services, including health and education.
C. The physical frequency and intensity of all natural, biological and technological hazards recorded worldwide.
D. The value of insurance premiums, relief transfers and compensation paid after each declared disaster.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** International cooperation is Target F.
- **B — Correct.** Target D focuses on critical infrastructure and basic-service continuity.
- **C — Incorrect.** Sendai does not set a target to reduce the physical frequency of every hazard.
- **D — Incorrect.** Insurance may manage residual risk but is not Target D’s measure.

**Examiner trap:** omitting basic-service disruption and remembering infrastructure damage alone.

### Q31. Which comparison between Hyogo and Sendai is the most accurate?

A. Hyogo imposed binding international duties, while Sendai replaced them with voluntary national action plans.
B. Sendai discarded Hyogo preparedness and reorganised international action solely around preventing hazardous events from occurring.
C. Sendai retained Hyogo’s logic while adding four priorities, seven targets and an explicit Build Back Better principle.
D. Hyogo governed 2015–2030, while Sendai was the predecessor framework operating from 2005 to 2015.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Hyogo-to-Sendai was not a transition from binding international duties to voluntary national plans.
- **B — Incorrect.** Sendai retained preparedness and focuses on reducing disaster risk rather than preventing hazardous events from occurring.
- **C — Correct.** It captures continuity and the major structural developments.
- **D — Incorrect.** The periods are reversed.

**Examiner trap:** portraying Sendai as a complete rejection of Hyogo.

### Q32. Which statement best reflects Sendai’s responsibility principle?

A. UNDRR replaces national governments as the primary implementation authority and directly enforces compliance.
B. Private insurers solely bear responsibility for financing and managing residual disaster risk across society.
C. Local authorities manage disaster risk independently from national institutions, sector policies and development planning.
D. Stakeholder participation cannot transfer primary responsibility for disaster risk away from the State.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** UNDRR supports implementation and monitoring but does not replace governments or enforce compliance directly.
- **B — Incorrect.** Residual-risk management includes public, private, community and household responsibilities.
- **C — Incorrect.** Sendai calls for vertical and horizontal coordination with development policy.
- **D — Correct.** Stakeholders share roles, but participation does not transfer the State’s primary responsibility.

**Examiner trap:** confusing shared responsibility with diluted State responsibility.

### Q33. Which sequence gives defensible Indian anchors for a before-and-after-Sendai answer?

A. DM Act 2005 and National Policy 2009; then NDMP 2016/2019 and ten-point agenda.
B. NDMP 2019 and MCR2030 before Sendai; the Disaster Management Act and National Policy after it.
C. MCR2030 and EW4All before Hyogo; the National Policy and Disaster Management Act after Sendai.
D. EW4All before the Disaster Management Act; Hyogo’s adoption after the NDMP revision in 2019.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** It preserves chronology and distinguishes domestic frameworks from later alignment.
- **B — Incorrect.** It places the 2019 plan and 2020 initiative before Sendai and pre-2015 Indian instruments after it.
- **C — Incorrect.** MCR2030 and EW4All post-date Hyogo, while both Indian instruments pre-date Sendai.
- **D — Incorrect.** EW4All dates from 2022, the Act from 2005, and Hyogo preceded the 2019 NDMP revision.

**Examiner trap:** treating framework alignment as if institutions did not exist before 2015.

### Q34. Which statement about India’s NDMP is most defensible?

A. Every documented limitation of the 2016 first edition necessarily remains unchanged in the 2019 revision.
B. NDMP began in 2016 and was revised in 2019, so continuing-gap claims need textual comparison.
C. NDMP is a binding international instrument whose implementation and monitoring are administered directly by UNDRR.
D. NDMP publication establishes uniform local implementation, completed projects and independently verified reductions in disaster losses.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** A document-period critique cannot be silently carried forward.
- **B — Correct.** It states the verified chronology and preserves the evidence boundary.
- **C — Incorrect.** The NDMP is a domestic plan, not an international treaty.
- **D — Incorrect.** Publication is an institutional input, not an outcome.

**Examiner trap:** freezing a critique after the underlying document has been revised.

### Q35. What is the safest use of the 2023 Sendai Midterm Review Political Declaration?

A. As conclusive proof that all Sendai targets had been achieved globally by midterm.
B. As a binding amendment that created sanctions for national implementation failures.
C. As a dated record of renewed commitment and review, without treating it as country-specific outcome proof.
D. As evidence that the Sendai implementation period formally ended during 2023.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** The declaration does not establish universal target achievement.
- **B — Incorrect.** It did not convert Sendai into a binding enforcement regime.
- **C — Correct.** UNGA resolution 77/289 is a valid current-status anchor with clear evidentiary limits.
- **D — Incorrect.** Sendai continues to 2030.

**Examiner trap:** treating a review declaration as an outcome scoreboard.

### Q36. Which sequence correctly gives the four Early Warnings for All pillars?

A. Relief finance → reconstruction contracts → insurance coverage → international cooperation
B. National strategy → local strategy → mortality reduction → direct economic loss
C. Prevention → structural mitigation → rehabilitation → physical reconstruction
D. Risk knowledge → observation/forecasting → dissemination/communication → preparedness/response

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** These are not the EW4All end-to-end pillars.
- **B — Incorrect.** These mix Sendai targets.
- **C — Incorrect.** These are disaster-cycle activities.
- **D — Correct.** WMO lists these four linked pillars; WMO and UNDRR co-lead the initiative.

**Examiner trap:** equating a forecast with an end-to-end warning system.

### Q37. What is the correct order of the MCR2030 resilience roadmap?

A. Cities Know Better → Cities Plan Better → Cities Implement Better.
B. Cities Respond Better → Cities Rebuild Better → Cities Insure Better.
C. Cities Invest Better → Cities Forecast Better → Cities Recover Better.
D. Cities Register Better → Cities Report Better → Cities Graduate Better.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** The official roadmap progresses from knowledge to planning to implementation.
- **B — Incorrect.** These are not the named stages and would narrow resilience to crisis action.
- **C — Incorrect.** These are useful activities but not the official stage names.
- **D — Incorrect.** Registration and reporting are not substitutes for the roadmap’s substantive progression.

**Examiner trap:** remembering “three stages” but inventing their names.

### Q38. Which statement correctly describes MCR2030?

A. It is a treaty requiring participating cities to attain a uniform resilience score by a fixed deadline.
B. It is a UNDRR initiative supporting local resilience through a flexible three-stage roadmap and partnerships.
C. It is restricted only to national capitals and major metropolitan regions, excluding smaller municipalities and local governments.
D. It certifies that each registered city has completed the roadmap and achieved measurable disaster resilience outcomes.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** MCR2030 is an initiative, not a treaty imposing a uniform score or fixed compliance deadline.
- **B — Correct.** This matches the official MCR2030 description.
- **C — Incorrect.** The official roadmap is intended for municipalities of different sizes.
- **D — Incorrect.** Registration is entry to a support pathway, not certification of roadmap completion or resilience outcomes.

**Examiner trap:** treating programme membership as certification of resilience.

### Q39. Which inference is valid?

A. A warning was issued; therefore all exposed people received, understood and acted upon it in time.
B. Funds were allocated; therefore the project was completed and disaster losses subsequently declined.
C. A notified strategy cannot establish implementation or outcomes without separate evidence.
D. MCR2030 membership proves that a city’s critical services cannot experience cascading failure.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Issuance, dissemination, receipt, comprehension and timely action are separate stages.
- **B — Incorrect.** Allocation, release, expenditure, completion and reduced losses are distinct evidentiary stages.
- **C — Correct.** Strategy notification by itself cannot prove implementation or outcomes; each needs separate evidence.
- **D — Incorrect.** Membership offers a support pathway but does not prove protection from cascading failure.

**Examiner trap:** jumping from institutional input to social outcome.

### Q40. A city faces recurrent waterlogging and a low-probability catastrophic flood. It updates drainage, protects wetlands, retrofits hospitals, runs inclusive evacuation drills, maintains backup power and buys insurance. Which assessment is best?

A. The package is exclusively compensatory because insurance and evacuation preparedness dominate the other interventions.
B. The package addresses only extensive risk because recurrent waterlogging is the municipality’s most frequent impact.
C. The package removes systemic and residual risk by combining ecosystem, infrastructure and preparedness measures.
D. It combines all three management modes and multiple risk patterns but leaves some residual risk.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Insurance and drills are compensatory, but ecosystem protection and retrofitting add prospective and corrective action.
- **B — Incorrect.** Recurrent waterlogging engages extensive risk, while the catastrophic-flood scenario also engages intensive risk.
- **C — Incorrect.** Ecosystem, infrastructure and preparedness measures reduce but do not remove systemic or residual risk.
- **D — Correct.** It integrates risk modes, risk patterns and resilience elements while recognising that residual risk remains.

**Examiner trap:** forcing a multi-layered strategy into one category.

### PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-III Q18 — DIRECT

**Wording status:** **OFFICIAL LOCAL PAPER — TRANSPARENT OCR NORMALISATION.** The English OCR was readable but split word-by-word and rendered “DRR” once as “DER”; spacing, acronym and punctuation are normalised without changing meaning.

> “Describe various measures taken in India for Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) before and after signing ‘Sendai Framework for DRR (2015–2030)’. How is this framework different from ‘Hyogo Framework for Action, 2005’?”
> **15 marks · 250 words**

**Model answer — 237 words**

Disaster risk reduction seeks to prevent new risk, reduce existing risk and manage residual risk. India’s measures span both sides of the 2015 Sendai transition.

**Before Sendai**, the Disaster Management Act, 2005 created a national–state–district institutional architecture, while the National Policy on Disaster Management, 2009 emphasised prevention, mitigation, preparedness and capacity development. Risk assessment, early warning, safer construction and community preparedness were increasingly added to the earlier relief-centred approach.

**After Sendai**, the National Disaster Management Plan, 2016 translated the four Sendai priorities into national planning; it was revised in 2019. The Prime Minister’s ten-point DRR agenda, announced at AMCDRR in New Delhi in November 2016, linked development sectors, local capacity, technology and international cooperation with Sendai priorities. These measures indicate policy alignment, although adoption alone does not prove uniform implementation or reduced losses.

**Hyogo–Sendai comparison:** Hyogo 2005–2015 had five priorities: institutional priority, risk assessment and warning, knowledge and safety culture, reduction of underlying risk, and preparedness. Sendai 2015–2030 reorganises action around understanding risk, risk governance, investment in resilience, and preparedness with Build Back Better. It also adds seven global targets covering mortality, affected people, economic loss, critical infrastructure and services, strategies, cooperation, and multi-hazard warning/risk information.

Thus, Sendai retains Hyogo’s preventive logic but makes risk governance, investment, measurable targets and safer recovery more explicit. It remains voluntary and non-binding; India must therefore connect plans with finance, local execution and outcome monitoring.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both chronological and comparative limbs, names Indian evidence, gives the framework architecture and preserves the implementation caveat.

### PYQ 2 — 2019 GS-III Q8 — DIRECT

**Wording status:** **OFFICIAL LOCAL PAPER — TRANSPARENT OCR NORMALISATION.** Line breaks and punctuation are normalised; wording is otherwise preserved.

> “Vulnerability is an essential element for defining disaster impacts and its threat to people. How and in what ways can vulnerability to disasters be characterized? Discuss different types of vulnerability with reference to disasters.”
> **10 marks · 150 words**

**Model answer — 141 words**

Vulnerability comprises conditions that increase the susceptibility of people, assets or systems to hazard impacts. It explains why the same hazard produces unequal losses.

It may be characterised by:

- **Physical vulnerability:** unsafe siting, weak buildings and fragile lifelines; for example, non-engineered housing in a seismic zone.
- **Social vulnerability:** age, disability, health, gendered constraints, marginalisation and weak networks that impede warning or evacuation.
- **Economic vulnerability:** poverty, insecure livelihoods, low savings, credit and insurance, which reduce coping and recovery ability.
- **Environmental vulnerability:** degraded wetlands, forests, slopes or drainage that remove natural protection.
- **Attitudinal vulnerability:** distrust, fatalism or resistance to preparedness, often shaped by previous institutional experience.

These dimensions frequently compound. Vulnerability must not be confused with exposure: exposure identifies who or what is in harm’s way, while vulnerability identifies susceptibility. Impact therefore depends on hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity, not hazard intensity alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the term, answers “how,” covers all five source-owned types, uses examples and closes with the decisive distinction.

### PYQ 3 — 2024 GS-III Q17 — DIRECT

**Wording status:** **OFFICIAL LOCAL PAPER — TRANSPARENT OCR NORMALISATION.** The local OCR reads “targests”; this obvious OCR error is normalised to “targets.”

> “What is disaster resilience? How is it determined? Describe various elements of a resilience framework. Also mention the global targets of Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030).”
> **15 marks · 250 words**

**Model answer — 232 words**

Disaster resilience is the ability of a hazard-exposed system or community to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover efficiently while preserving or restoring essential functions. It is not merely rebuilding the pre-disaster condition.

**Determination:** First establish the risk baseline—hazards, exposure, physical/social/economic/environmental vulnerability and usable capacity. Then test performance: continuity of critical services during stress, warning-to-action capability, institutional coordination, fiscal and household coping capacity, speed and equity of restoration, and whether recovery reduces future risk.

**Framework elements** include:

1. **Risk knowledge** through updated, accessible assessment.
2. **Robustness** of infrastructure, institutions and ecosystems.
3. **Redundancy** through alternate power, routes, communications and supplies.
4. **Resourcefulness** to mobilise information, finance, personnel and leadership.
5. **Inclusive preparedness**—warnings, drills, shelters and evacuation usable by high-risk groups.
6. **Rapid, equitable recovery** of services and livelihoods.
7. **Adaptive and transformative capacity** so development and reconstruction do not recreate exposure or vulnerability.

The Sendai Framework’s global targets are: **A** mortality; **B** affected people; **C** direct economic loss relative to global GDP; **D** critical-infrastructure damage and disruption of basic services; **E** national and local DRR strategies; **F** international cooperation to developing countries; and **G** multi-hazard early warning plus risk information and assessments. Target E had a 2020 deadline; the others use 2030.

Sendai is voluntary and non-binding. Therefore, strategies, investments or warning platforms are inputs; resilience must be established through verified continuity, recovery and loss outcomes.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers all four limbs, names seven targets compactly and distinguishes framework inputs from resilience outcomes.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish hazard, exposure, vulnerability, capacity, risk and disaster. Explain why the distinctions matter for public policy. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer — 148 words**

A **hazard** is a potentially damaging process or activity. **Exposure** is the presence of people and assets where that hazard may occur. **Vulnerability** comprises conditions that increase their susceptibility, while **capacity** denotes usable strengths and resources for anticipation, coping and recovery. **Risk** is the potential loss arising from their interaction; a **disaster** is serious realised disruption that exceeds or severely tests coping ability.

The distinctions identify different policy levers. Flood probability may be difficult to eliminate, but exposure can be limited through zoning; vulnerability can be reduced through safer housing and livelihood security; capacity can improve through warnings, shelters and trained local institutions. Conversely, a hazard map without enforcement leaves exposure unchanged, and equipment without trained operators is only nominal capacity.

Therefore, public policy should diagnose the relevant component before prescribing action. Hazard-centred responses alone naturalise socially produced losses; risk-informed development combines prevention, mitigation, preparedness and residual-risk management.

**Why this earns marks:** Six terms are distinguished, each is converted into a policy lever, and the conclusion rejects hazard determinism.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Differentiate prospective, corrective and compensatory disaster risk management with suitable examples. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer — 141 words**

UNDRR distinguishes disaster risk management by the risk addressed.

**Prospective management** prevents new or increased risk. Risk-sensitive land-use planning that keeps future housing outside a floodway avoids creating exposure.

**Corrective management** reduces risk already embedded in society. Retrofitting an existing hospital, strengthening an old bridge or relocating assets from an unstable slope tackles present vulnerability and exposure.

**Compensatory management**, also called residual-risk management, manages risk that cannot be effectively eliminated. Preparedness, response, recovery, contingency funds, insurance, reinsurance and safety nets belong here.

The categories are complementary. A coastal city may prohibit new development in a buffer, retrofit existing critical facilities and still maintain shelters and insurance because residual risk remains. Compensatory management should not be reduced to post-disaster compensation; nor should every pre-disaster measure be called prospective. A complete strategy prevents tomorrow’s risk, corrects accumulated risk and prepares for what remains.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three official categories, supplies matched examples and demonstrates their complementarity.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Disaster resilience is a system property, not the sum of emergency assets. Discuss and suggest a framework for measuring it. **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer — 216 words**

Disaster resilience is the ability to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover while preserving or restoring essential functions. Hence, an inventory of teams, shelters or equipment measures inputs, not the performance of an interconnected system.

A resilience framework should assess:

1. **Risk knowledge:** current hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity assessments available to decision-makers and communities.
2. **Robustness:** whether hospitals, bridges, housing and ecosystems withstand expected stress.
3. **Redundancy:** alternate power, water, communications, suppliers and access routes that prevent single-point failure.
4. **Resourcefulness:** the ability of institutions and communities to mobilise information, finance, personnel and leadership.
5. **Inclusive preparedness:** whether warnings are received, understood and acted upon by persons facing disability, age, poverty or livelihood constraints.
6. **Continuity and recovery:** downtime of essential services, restoration speed, livelihood recovery and distribution of recovery benefits.
7. **Adaptation:** whether post-disaster decisions reduce future exposure and vulnerability through safer siting, standards and ecosystem protection.

Measurement should combine baseline indicators with drills, stress tests and post-event data. For example, an allocated hospital-retrofit budget is not equivalent to completed work, and a warning issued is not equivalent to evacuation.

Thus, resilience is credible only when capacities operate together before, during and after disruption. A dashboard of service continuity, inclusion, recovery time and risk reduction is more defensible than an unsupported single score.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly evaluates the proposition and converts resilience into measurable dimensions while preserving the input–outcome distinction.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the guiding logic of the Sendai Framework’s four priorities and seven targets. Why should the framework not be treated as a binding or self-executing instrument? **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer — 213 words**

The Sendai Framework 2015–2030, adopted on 18 March 2015, seeks substantial reduction of disaster risk and losses. Its four priorities form a causal chain.

**First**, understand risk through hazard, exposure, vulnerability, capacity and environmental knowledge. **Second**, convert knowledge into risk governance through mandates, coordination and participation. **Third**, invest publicly and privately in structural and non-structural risk reduction for resilience. **Fourth**, strengthen preparedness for effective response and use recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction to Build Back Better.

Its seven global targets translate this logic into monitoring categories: **A** mortality, **B** affected people, **C** direct economic loss relative to global GDP, **D** critical-infrastructure damage and basic-service disruption, **E** national and local DRR strategies, **F** international cooperation to developing countries, and **G** multi-hazard warning plus risk information. A–D chiefly concern loss outcomes; E–G concern means of implementation. Target E alone had a 2020 deadline.

However, Sendai is voluntary and non-binding. UNDRR supports terminology, implementation and monitoring but does not impose treaty sanctions. Global targets are not automatically enforceable country quotas. Further, a strategy, investment or warning platform proves an input, not reduced mortality or loss.

Therefore, Sendai is best used as a common risk-governance architecture. Its effectiveness depends on domestic law, finance, local capacity, disaggregated data and implementation that connects knowledge to verified outcomes.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains rather than merely lists the architecture and directly answers the legal-force and implementation limbs.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse how development choices create intensive, extensive and systemic disaster risk. How can risk-informed development and Build Back Better interrupt this process? **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer — 272 words**

Disaster risk is often co-produced by development. Land use, construction, drainage, ecosystem conversion and infrastructure concentration can increase exposure and vulnerability even when the underlying hazard is unchanged.

**Extensive risk** accumulates through frequent, lower-severity local events. Encroached drains, degraded wetlands and insecure housing can turn seasonal rain into repeated waterlogging, livelihood loss and disease burdens. Individually small events may remain under-recorded while eroding household capacity.

**Intensive risk** arises where a major hazard meets concentrated exposure and high vulnerability. Dense construction in a floodplain or non-engineered buildings in a seismic zone can produce catastrophic, less frequent loss.

**Systemic risk** emerges from interdependence. Flooded electricity substations may disable water pumping, hospitals, telecommunications, transport and digital payments. The problem is not only hazard magnitude but cascading network failure.

Risk-informed development interrupts the process prospectively by screening projects, protecting buffers, enforcing safe siting and standards, preserving ecosystems and avoiding concentration of critical functions. Corrective management then retrofits unsafe assets, removes bottlenecks and builds redundancy. Compensatory measures—warnings, drills, emergency finance, insurance, response and recovery—manage residual risk.

Build Back Better is the decisive feedback intervention. Recovery should restore livelihoods and essential services while changing unsafe location, design and governance. Rebuilding the same road alignment, drainage obstruction or fragile lifeline merely recreates risk.

Yet trade-offs remain: relocation can damage livelihoods; redundancy costs money; rapid reconstruction can weaken participation and oversight. Measures therefore require local consultation, social protection, transparent finance and outcome indicators such as service downtime, inclusive warning action and recurrence of losses.

Thus, resilience is not resistance to nature alone. It is the capacity of development and recovery systems to stop producing vulnerability and cascading failure.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses all three risk patterns, links them causally to development, and supplies prospective, corrective, compensatory and BBB solutions with qualifications.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine the strengths and accountability limits of the Sendai Framework. Illustrate how EW4All and MCR2030 translate its priorities into operational pathways. **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer — 280 words**

The Sendai Framework’s principal strength is a common, all-cycle language for disaster risk. Its four priorities connect risk knowledge, governance, investment and preparedness with Build Back Better. Seven targets cover both losses—mortality, affected people, economic loss, infrastructure and services—and means of implementation—strategies, cooperation and multi-hazard warning. This architecture moves policy beyond relief and makes risk creation, resilience and recovery visible.

Its accountability is nevertheless limited. Sendai is voluntary and non-binding; UNDRR cannot enforce performance through treaty sanctions. Global targets do not automatically become national quotas. Targets A–D are outcome measures influenced by hazard occurrence, exposure and development conditions, while E–G more directly record policy capacity. Even these require standardised, disaggregated national data. A passed deadline, published strategy or installed platform does not establish implementation or reduced loss. The 2023 Midterm Review Political Declaration, adopted as UNGA resolution 77/289, renewed political commitment rather than creating an enforcement mechanism.

**Early Warnings for All** operationalises Target G. WMO states the end-2027 aim of protecting everyone through four linked pillars: risk knowledge; detection, observation and forecasting; dissemination and communication; and preparedness and response. Its value is the end-to-end chain; its limit is that issuance does not prove last-mile receipt or action.

**MCR2030**, launched by UNDRR on 28 October 2020, translates urban resilience into “Cities Know Better, Plan Better, Implement Better.” It supplies tools, technical expertise, partnerships, city learning and national–local links. Yet registration or stage placement is an enabling input, not certified resilience.

Sendai therefore provides strong normative and monitoring architecture but weak coercive accountability. Its success depends on domestic law, financed local implementation, inclusive capacity and evidence linking institutional inputs to continuity, recovery and reduced losses.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances strengths and limits, uses dated official anchors and evaluates both initiatives without equating participation with outcomes.
