## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Begin with the correct risk grammar

```text
CONDITIONING FACTORS                 TRIGGER
geology · slope · weathering        intense/prolonged rain
drainage · land cover               earthquake/vibration
old failures · cutting/loading      snow loading/warming
              \                     /
               \                   /
                HAZARD OCCURRENCE
                       ↓
EXPOSURE × VULNERABILITY ÷ CAPACITY → DISASTER RISK
```

| Term | Exact meaning in this topic |
|---|---|
| **Hazard** | Potentially damaging landslide, avalanche or glacial-lake outburst process |
| **Conditioning factor** | Relatively persistent property that makes failure more or less likely |
| **Trigger** | Shorter-duration change that initiates failure at a particular time |
| **Susceptibility** | Relative spatial likelihood of failure; principally answers **where** |
| **Hazard assessment** | Adds probability/frequency, magnitude or time window; asks **what, where and how likely/when** |
| **Exposure** | People, roads, settlements, utilities and assets in the path |
| **Vulnerability** | Their propensity to suffer harm |
| **Capacity** | Ability to anticipate, withstand, evacuate, respond and recover |

A steep, fractured, road-cut slope is conditioned for failure; exceptional rainfall may be its trigger. A mapped high-susceptibility slope without exposed assets can present less societal risk than a moderately susceptible slope above a dense transport corridor.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Mountain risk is not the trigger alone; it is a conditioned hazard intersecting exposed and vulnerable systems with limited coping capacity.

**Must-write keywords:** conditioning factor · trigger · susceptibility · hazard · exposure · vulnerability · capacity · residual risk.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* rainfall does not by itself explain landslide disaster. *Evidence:* GSI susceptibility work combines terrain and past-failure information, while rainfall-threshold forecasting adds a dynamic trigger. *Analysis:* mapping “where” and forecasting “when” answer different questions. *Qualification:* neither proves who is exposed, whether a warning is received or whether loss is reduced.

### 2. Landslide, rockfall, debris flow, avalanche and GLOF are not synonyms

| Process | Moving material and mechanism | Main management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Landslide** | gravity-driven movement of rock, debris or earth down a slope | investigate slope, failure surface, drainage and runout |
| **Rockfall** | detached rock falls, bounces or rolls from a steep face | protect/avoid fall and bounce paths |
| **Debris flow** | rapid, water-rich mixture of soil, rock and organic matter, often channelised | map source, channel and deposition fan |
| **Snow avalanche** | unstable snow/ice mass moves rapidly downslope, often entraining debris | snowpack-weather warning, path/runout zoning and route control |
| **GLOF** | sudden release of impounded glacial-lake water after dam failure, overtopping or displacement | monitor lake-dam system and downstream flood corridor |
| **Cloudburst** | extremely intense, localised rainfall event | treat as a meteorological trigger that may cause flash flood/debris flow/slide |

The local canonical source defines a landslide as movement of rock, debris or earth down a slope and a GLOF as sudden release from a naturally dammed glacial lake. Shared mountain location does not make the processes identical.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Correct management begins by naming what moves—rock, debris, snow or stored lake water—because each process has a different warning and protection chain.

**Must-write keywords:** mass wasting · rockfall · debris flow · snowpack · runout · dam breach · outburst flood · compound event.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* a cloudburst is not a landslide. *Evidence:* the local source treats it as highly localised intense rainfall and notes that it can trigger flash floods and landslides. *Analysis:* conflating trigger and hazard misdirects monitoring. *Qualification:* a single emergency may still contain all three processes.

### 3. Landslide mechanism: resistance is progressively weakened

```text
WEAK / FRACTURED MATERIAL + STEEP OR MODIFIED SLOPE
                         ↓
water infiltration → pore-water pressure ↑ → effective resistance ↓
toe erosion/cutting → support ↓       loading/vibration → driving stress ↑
                         ↓
              SHEAR STRESS > SHEAR STRENGTH
                         ↓
fall / topple / slide / spread / flow / complex movement
```

**Conditioning factors** include lithology and structure, weathering, slope angle and shape, drainage, land cover, toe condition, old failures, excavation, loading and spoil disposal. **Triggers** include intense or prolonged rainfall, earthquakes, rapid snowmelt, freeze-thaw, river erosion and construction vibration. Road cutting can be a long-term conditioning change and an immediate trigger if excavation removes support.

Heavy rain is prominent in the local source, but “rain caused it” is incomplete unless the answer explains why that slope was susceptible.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A trigger supplies the final disturbance; conditioning factors determine whether the slope was already close to failure.

**Must-write keywords:** shear strength · pore-water pressure · toe support · drainage · loading · failure surface · runout.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* unsafe road formation can create risk before the monsoon. *Evidence:* the canonical source names road cutting, altered slope angle and construction debris. *Analysis:* these reduce support, obstruct drainage or add load, so later rainfall becomes more effective. *Qualification:* causation remains site-specific and needs geotechnical investigation.

### 4. Susceptibility, hazard and risk maps serve different decisions

```text
INVENTORY + GEOLOGY + SLOPE + DRAINAGE + LAND USE
                         ↓
SUSCEPTIBILITY MAP: where failure is relatively more likely
                         ↓ + trigger probability / magnitude / time
HAZARD MAP: expected event likelihood and intensity
                         ↓ + exposure + vulnerability + capacity
RISK MAP: expected human, service and economic consequences
```

Zonation becomes disaster mitigation only when translated into decisions: avoidance or conditional development; site investigation; route alignment; drainage and slope treatment; settlement control; monitoring; evacuation routes; shelters; and protection of lifelines. Macro-scale mapping cannot replace detailed project investigation.

GSI's official Bhusanket role page identifies landslide susceptibility planning, hazard/risk research, early-warning development, SOPs and information dissemination. The canonical source records national susceptibility mapping at 1:50,000 over about 4.3 lakh sq km in parts of 19 States/UTs; retain that figure as a dated official-programme status, not a claim that every site is safe to build. **Bhukosh** serves mapped geoscience information, **Bhusanket** the forecasting interface and **Bhooskhalan** the mobile dissemination/crowdsourcing channel in the canonical current account.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A zonation map mitigates disaster only when its colours change land-use, engineering, warning and evacuation decisions.

**Must-write keywords:** inventory · susceptibility · temporal probability · magnitude · risk map · scale · land-use control · enforcement.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* national mapping is an input, not an outcome. *Evidence:* GSI supervises NLSM and develops forecasting, while local authorities control actual construction and evacuation. *Analysis:* the information-to-action chain crosses institutions. *Qualification:* coarse maps cannot certify individual slopes.

### 5. Himalayan and Western-Ghat landslide risk must be differentiated

| Dimension | Himalayan region | Western Ghats/Nilgiris |
|---|---|---|
| Geological setting | young, tectonically active, highly fractured and high-relief terrain | older terrain, but steep, deeply weathered and locally structurally weak slopes |
| Major triggers | intense/prolonged rain, earthquakes, snowmelt/freeze-thaw, toe erosion | intense monsoon rain, saturation, stream/toe erosion |
| Human pressure | road widening, hydropower, tunnelling, tourism, slope loading and spoil | quarrying/mining, road cutting, deforestation, plantation/settlement change |
| Risk pattern | long valleys and narrow corridors allow cascading blockage/outburst and access isolation | dense settlements and transport on steep rain-soaked slopes create localised high consequence |
| Management emphasis | seismic-sensitive siting, cascade analysis, muck/drainage control, alternative access | drainage, quarry/land-use regulation, slope treatment and local warning |

The local source's broad zonation is document-period evidence and contains over-generalised “safe area” language. It estimated at least 15% of India's land area as affected and associated mining/subsidence-linked failures with Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Goa and Kerala. These are source-period descriptors, not a current district safety map; no place should be called safe without current site evidence.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Both regions combine steep slopes and intense rain, but Himalayan tectonic youth and seismicity differ from the Western Ghats’ deeply weathered, monsoon-saturated and heavily modified slopes.

**Must-write keywords:** tectonic youth · weathered regolith · monsoon saturation · seismicity · quarrying · corridor exposure · site-specific.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* one national recipe is insufficient. *Evidence:* the 2021 GS-I PYQ explicitly asks differentiation. *Analysis:* different conditioning factors change design, monitoring and enforcement priorities. *Qualification:* intra-regional variation prevents treating either mountain chain as uniform.

### 6. Avalanche risk requires path-and-runout zoning, not a weather alert alone

```text
SNOWPACK CONDITION                  RELEASE TRIGGER
weak layer · slab · wind loading   new load · warming · rain · vibration
                 \                 /
                  RELEASE ZONE
                       ↓
                   TRACK/PATH
                       ↓
                   RUNOUT ZONE
                       ↓
       settlement / road / troops / tourists exposed
```

Avalanche susceptibility depends on terrain, snowpack structure and weather. Slopes around 30°–45° are commonly associated with slab-avalanche release, but this is a typical range, not an absolute legal threshold. **Zoning** maps release areas, tracks and runout, then guides avoidance, seasonal occupancy, road closure, deflection structures and rescue planning. **Forecasting** estimates current danger from snow and meteorological observations.

DGRE, a DRDO laboratory, is the specialist Indian avalanche-warning institution. Its dated bulletins demonstrate a warning service; they do not guarantee safety at every slope because local snow and weather can change rapidly.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Avalanche governance joins static path/runout zoning with dynamic snowpack-weather assessment and pre-authorised movement controls.

**Must-write keywords:** weak layer · slab · release zone · track · runout · DGRE · danger bulletin · route closure.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* an avalanche bulletin cannot substitute for zoning. *Evidence:* DGRE bulletins communicate changing danger, while settlement/road planning needs known release and runout paths. *Analysis:* one manages time, the other space. *Qualification:* both retain local uncertainty.

### 7. GLOF risk begins with a lake–dam–trigger–downstream system

```text
GLACIER RETREAT / LAKE EXPANSION
              ↓
LAKE + MORAINE / ICE / BEDROCK OR OTHER NATURAL DAM
              ↓
TRIGGER: slope/ice fall · overtopping wave · dam degradation
         earthquake · blocked/drained outlet change
              ↓
SUDDEN RELEASE OF WATER + SEDIMENT + ICE
              ↓
EROSION / DEBRIS BULKING / BRIDGE-DAM-SETTLEMENT FAILURE DOWNSTREAM
```

A large or expanding lake is a **screening signal**, not proof of imminent outburst. Risk assessment must examine dam material and geometry, freeboard, seepage, lake growth, surrounding slope/ice instability, possible displacement waves, downstream channel and exposed assets. The local source also lists black-carbon surface heating as a possible melt-related factor; use it as a general mechanism, not event-specific attribution.

ISRO's official 22 April 2024 note used 1984–2023 satellite imagery to identify expanding Himalayan glacial lakes. Satellite monitoring is indispensable for remote terrain, but cloud cover, revisit intervals, sub-surface weakness and sudden mass entry limit certainty. Field verification, instruments and downstream planning remain necessary.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** GLOF monitoring must move from “lake seen from space” to a lake–dam–trigger–runout–community risk chain.

**Must-write keywords:** moraine-dammed · ice-dammed · freeboard · seepage · displacement wave · breach hydrograph · downstream cascade.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* lake expansion is not the same as forecasted failure. *Evidence:* ISRO provides dated remote-sensing evidence of expansion, while NDMA measures also require hazard mapping and evacuation planning. *Analysis:* size informs screening but not the entire breach probability. *Qualification:* a dynamic inventory must be updated and field-checked.

### 8. GLOF monitoring and mitigation form a layered portfolio

| Layer | Measures | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Screening | satellite inventory, change detection, lake prioritisation | imminent failure |
| Site assessment | dam/slope surveys, bathymetry, seepage and outlet checks | zero residual risk |
| Monitoring | water level, weather, movement/seismic indicators where suitable | warning will reach everyone |
| Structural mitigation | controlled lowering/outlet works, channel or protective works after expert appraisal | universal suitability |
| Preparedness | inundation/runout maps, safe sites, evacuation routes, drills, redundant communication | successful evacuation without testing |
| Response/recovery | search and rescue, medical care, access restoration, debris management, resilient rebuilding | restored livelihoods merely from road reopening |

NDMA's GLOF Guidelines date to October 2020. The MHA's 4 February 2025 parliamentary reply records the National GLOF Risk Mitigation Programme at ₹150 crore—₹135 crore from NDMF and ₹15 crore State contribution—for Arunachal Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim and Uttarakhand. The official 195-high-risk-lake count cited on 4 December 2024 was explicitly dynamic; it must remain date-labelled.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** GLOF risk reduction needs remote sensing, field diagnosis, safe engineering and downstream preparedness; no single sensor or lake count completes the chain.

**Must-write keywords:** dynamic inventory · lake prioritisation · controlled lowering · inundation map · last-mile warning · NGRMP · residual risk.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* funding approval is not protection delivered. *Evidence:* NGRMP has a dated outlay and four-State scope. *Analysis:* impact depends on site selection, installation, maintenance, drills and local receipt. *Qualification:* allocation, release, expenditure and measured risk reduction are separate evidence rungs.

### 9. Cloudbursts and compound mountain emergencies

The local source uses roughly 10 cm or more rain in an hour over about 20–30 sq km as its cloudburst benchmark. It also stresses the phenomenon's small spatial and temporal scale, making precise prediction difficult. Treat those figures as the source's meteorological description, not a universal substitute for a current IMD warning product.

```text
local intense rainfall
       ├─→ rapid runoff / flash flood
       ├─→ saturation / debris flow / landslide
       └─→ river blockage → temporary lake → breach flood
```

The local source lists the Great Musi flood (1908), Mumbai (2005), Jammu and Kashmir (2010), and Uttarakhand (1998 and 2013) as cloudburst examples, describing 2013 as a multi-cloudburst event. Event attribution should remain source-bound; do not turn every intense-rain disaster into a proven cloudburst. It also cites Copenhagen's stormwater master-plan approach as a comparative urban example; transfer to Himalayan valleys requires terrain-specific adaptation. Its statement that NDMA had no cloudburst-specific guideline is a document-period claim, not asserted here as a verified 2026 status.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Because cloudbursts are small and sudden, resilience depends less on perfect prediction than on drainage, safe siting, local observation and rapid protective action.

**Must-write keywords:** localised extreme rainfall · orographic lift · nowcasting limit · flash flood · debris flow · compound event.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* a rainfall alert does not specify the final hazard. *Evidence:* cloudbursts can trigger flash flood and slope failure. *Analysis:* evacuation zones must follow both drainage paths and unstable slopes. *Qualification:* the event must be meteorologically verified before applying the label.

### 10. Mountain development can reduce isolation while creating new risk

| Development need | Risk-creation pathway | Risk-informed correction |
|---|---|---|
| roads and tunnels | toe cutting, drainage interception, vibration, spoil loading | geotechnical alignment, staged excavation, drainage, spoil plan and monitoring |
| hydropower | workers/assets in narrow valleys; excavation and cascade exposure | cumulative basin assessment, safe siting/design, emergency action and downstream warning |
| tourism and urban growth | seasonal exposure, slope loading, waste/drain blockage | carrying-capacity controls, serviced safe zones and evacuation planning |
| livelihoods and farming | vegetation/soil change may affect stability | community-consented, locally suitable land management rather than blanket bans |

The canonical material alleges that tourism, roads and hydropower can alter the Himalayan radiative balance and accelerate melt. That proposition should not be universalised without project-specific evidence. The stronger, defensible claim is that poorly sited or executed development increases slope disturbance, exposure and cascading consequences.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The policy choice is not development versus no development, but development that prices slope, cryosphere and downstream cascade risk before construction.

**Must-write keywords:** risk-informed development · carrying capacity · cumulative impact · muck disposal · drainage interception · corridor redundancy · livelihood transition.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* mountain infrastructure can both build resilience and amplify hazard. *Evidence:* roads provide evacuation access but unsafe cutting and spoil destabilise slopes. *Analysis:* design and maintenance determine the direction of effect. *Qualification:* blanket prohibitions can harm connectivity and livelihoods.

### 11. Authority must be assigned by function

| Function | Principal actor | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| landslide geoscience, susceptibility and forecast development | **GSI / NLFC** | forecast capability is district- and status-specific, not national certainty |
| avalanche observation and warning | **DGRE, DRDO** | warning does not itself order civilian evacuation everywhere |
| glacial-lake remote sensing | **ISRO/NRSC** and collaborating agencies | imagery is monitoring input, not breach prediction |
| national policy/guidelines and mitigation programme | **NDMA/MHA** | guideline/programme is not proof of local execution |
| weather/rainfall information | **IMD** | rainfall forecast is not a slope-failure guarantee |
| local land use, preparedness and evacuation | **State/SDMA, district/DDMA and local administration** | must use competent technical advice and lawful powers |
| project safety | road/hydropower/local project authority and operator | cannot transfer all risk to the district response system |

NDRF/SDRF, fire, police, health, armed-forces support and community responders may act during a major emergency under the incident command structure, but scientific agencies, civil authorities and operators retain different mandates.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Mountain safety fails when the agency that observes risk is assumed also to possess land-use, closure, evacuation and recovery authority.

**Must-write keywords:** GSI/NLFC · DGRE · ISRO/NRSC · NDMA/MHA · IMD · SDMA/DDMA · operator duty · incident command.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* warning must have a decision owner. *Evidence:* GSI may issue a landslide forecast, but district authorities control local protective action. *Analysis:* pre-agreed thresholds prevent delay and blame-shifting. *Qualification:* legal competence and local SOPs must be verified rather than presumed.

### 12. Apply the complete disaster-management cycle

| Stage | Landslide/avalanche/GLOF actions |
|---|---|
| **Prevention/avoidance** | prohibit or redirect unsafe occupation and critical assets from high-risk paths where feasible |
| **Mitigation** | drainage, slope stabilisation, bioengineering, snow barriers/deflection, lake/outlet measures after expert review |
| **Preparedness** | monitoring, warnings, route-closure/evacuation triggers, drills, shelters, medical/logistics staging and alternative access |
| **Response** | incident command, cordon, search and rescue, triage, safe access, secondary-hazard watch and verified public information |
| **Relief** | food, water, shelter, health, communication and assistance without excluding remote or undocumented households |
| **Recovery** | restore access and services, stabilise residual hazards, support livelihoods and reconstruct to safer siting/design |

Response teams must avoid becoming secondary victims: unstable slopes, renewed snow release, damaged dams, blocked rivers and rainfall can produce repeat events. Recovery should not rebuild the same road, settlement or utility in the same failure/runout path without reassessment.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A successful rescue closes the response phase; resilience begins only when recovery removes the conditions that recreated the loss.

**Must-write keywords:** avoidance · structural/non-structural · route closure · secondary hazard · safe return · rehabilitation · Build Back Better.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* rapid road reopening is not a complete recovery metric. *Evidence:* the corridor may remain under an unstable slope or downstream of a lake. *Analysis:* restoration without reassessment recreates exposure. *Qualification:* essential access may require temporary controlled operation before permanent realignment.

### 13. Early warning is a chain, not a sensor

```text
OBSERVE → ANALYSE → FORECAST/WARNING → AUTHORISED DECISION
   → DISSEMINATE → RECEIVE/UNDERSTAND → ACT → REACH SAFETY
   → REVIEW FALSE ALARMS, MISSES, LEAD TIME AND LOSS
```

| Evidence claim | What may honestly be said |
|---|---|
| map exists | susceptibility information is available at its stated scale/date |
| portal/app exists | a dissemination channel exists |
| experimental forecast | method is being tested; no service guarantee follows |
| operational forecast | a defined service exists for the stated place/period |
| warning issued | message was generated, not necessarily received or acted upon |
| evacuation completed | exposed people moved; safety and inclusion still need review |
| lower loss | requires comparative outcome evidence, not an alert count |

GSI's official page records experimental rainfall-threshold forecasting work. Dated PIB releases report expansion, but operational and experimental district counts have changed across releases; quote a count only with its date. DGRE bulletins warn of rapidly changing local conditions. GLOF warnings face sparse high-altitude observations, telemetry/power/communication fragility, uncertain breach timing and short downstream lead time.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Warning effectiveness is measured at the last mile—understood action and reduced harm—not at the sensor or bulletin.

**Must-write keywords:** end-to-end warning · lead time · false alarm · missed event · redundancy · last mile · accessible message · outcome.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* “India has landslide early warning” is too broad. *Evidence:* GSI distinguishes susceptibility work and experimental regional rainfall-threshold forecasting. *Analysis:* coverage and status vary by district. *Qualification:* use a dated service claim and do not infer accuracy or loss reduction.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Probabilistic thresholds and non-stationarity

Rainfall thresholds are probabilistic, not deterministic. Changing rainfall extremes, slope modification and incomplete inventories can make historical relationships less reliable. Use ensembles, local calibration, uncertainty ranges and post-event updating; never promise exact prediction. Cloudburst prediction is difficult because of its small space–time scale, but that is not the same claim as the absence of reliable short-term earthquake prediction.

### B. Cascading-hazard corridor governance

Mountain roads, rivers, dams, settlements and communication lines occupy the same narrow corridors. An earthquake or rain-triggered slide can block a river; impoundment failure can then produce an outburst. Plans should model sequences, alternative access and secondary hazards rather than separate departmental scenarios.

### C. Cumulative project-risk screening

Project-by-project clearance can miss the combined effects of multiple roads, tunnels, quarries, hydropower sites, spoil dumps and settlements within one basin. ⚠️ A defensible advanced answer calls for cumulative basin/corridor assessment while recognising connectivity and livelihood needs.

### D. Avalanche control trade-offs

Explosive release or structural defence may protect selected corridors, but needs specialist control, exclusion zones and ecological/operational appraisal. Forecast-based closure reduces exposure but can isolate communities and disrupt livelihoods; redundancy and pre-positioning are therefore part of mitigation.

### E. GLOF engineering and moral hazard

Controlled lowering or outlet engineering can reduce risk at selected lakes but may create construction risk, ecological disturbance and false confidence. Independent design review, downstream preparedness and maintenance remain necessary after structural works.

### F. Equity and community knowledge

Seasonal workers, pilgrims, tourists, remote villages and people with limited mobility may receive warnings differently. Local observation can improve inventories and message design, but cannot replace geotechnical, snow or glacial expertise. Participation must influence siting, routes and drills—not merely validate a completed plan.

The local source recommends terrace farming in place of jhum cultivation for slope protection. ⚠️ That cannot be a blanket technical instruction: jhum is embedded in community tenure and livelihoods, so any transition needs ecological evidence, consent and viable alternatives.

### G. Accountability dashboard

Track: mapped area and scale; sites investigated; unsafe permissions prevented; drains/works maintained; forecast status and lead time; message reach; closure/evacuation compliance; inclusion; secondary responder injury; restoration time; livelihood recovery; and recurrence after rebuilding. Activity counts must not be presented as resilience outcomes.

The local source mentions landslide insurance and compensation only as a general recommendation. Do not invent a current dedicated insurance product or treat post-loss compensation as ex-ante mitigation finance.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Risk grammar

- condition = persistent predisposition; trigger = initiating change.
- susceptibility answers **where**; hazard adds probability/intensity/time.
- risk combines hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity.
- high susceptibility is not automatically high societal risk.

### Hazard distinctions

- landslide: rock/debris/earth downslope under gravity.
- rockfall: fall/bounce/roll of detached rock.
- debris flow: rapid water-rich debris mixture.
- avalanche: unstable snow/ice mass; release–track–runout.
- GLOF: sudden release from naturally dammed glacial lake.
- cloudburst: local intense rainfall; may trigger flood/slide.
- source-period cases: Great Musi 1908, Mumbai 2005, J&K 2010, Uttarakhand 1998/2013.

### Landslide engine

- conditioning: geology, structure, weathering, slope, drainage, land cover, toe, old failure, cutting/loading.
- triggers: intense/prolonged rain, earthquake, snowmelt, freeze-thaw, erosion, excavation/vibration.
- water raises pore pressure; toe cutting reduces support; loading raises driving stress.
- classify by material + movement: fall, topple, slide, spread, flow, complex.

### Geography and zonation

- Himalaya: young, fractured, seismic, high relief and corridor development.
- Western Ghats/Nilgiris: steep, weathered, monsoon-saturated, quarry/road/land-use pressure.
- susceptibility map ≠ hazard forecast ≠ risk map.
- mapping must change siting, codes, drainage, monitoring and evacuation.

### Avalanche

- snowpack weak layer + slope + weather/trigger.
- typical release slopes around 30°–45°; not an absolute threshold.
- zone release area, track and runout.
- DGRE warning is distinct from GSI landslide forecast and ISRO lake monitoring.

### GLOF

- lake + natural dam + trigger + breach/overtopping + downstream runout.
- expansion screens concern; does not prove imminent failure.
- satellite change detection → field assessment → instruments → EWS → evacuation.
- structural lowering/outlet works are site-specific and retain residual risk.
- October 2020 NDMA guidelines; NGRMP dated official status must retain date.

### Complete management cycle

- avoid unsafe siting; regulate cutting, loading, quarrying and spoil.
- drainage, retaining/protective works and bioengineering need site design/maintenance.
- warnings require thresholds, authority, redundant communication, drills and safe destination.
- response includes secondary-hazard control and responder safety.
- recovery reassesses slope/runout and builds back safer; reopening alone is not recovery.

### Institutional map and evidence firewall

- GSI/NLFC: landslide geoscience, mapping and forecast development.
- Bhukosh: mapped data; Bhusanket: forecast portal; Bhooskhalan: mobile dissemination/crowdsourcing.
- DGRE: avalanche assessment/warnings.
- ISRO/NRSC: remote-sensing inputs for lake/terrain monitoring.
- NDMA/MHA: national guidance/programme; State/district/local bodies implement protective action.
- map/app/bulletin/programme = input or output; reduced harm needs outcome evidence.
- operational and experimental services, dynamic lake lists and funding stages must remain dated.

### Qualified conclusion

Mountain resilience requires hazard-specific science but integrated governance: control the development choices that condition failure, connect monitoring to authorised last-mile action, plan for cascades and rebuild away from repeatedly exposed paths.
