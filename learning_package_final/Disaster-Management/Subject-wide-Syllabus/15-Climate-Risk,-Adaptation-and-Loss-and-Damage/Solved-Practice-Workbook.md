## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1. Which statement best defines Climate disaster risk equation?

A. Climate-related disaster risk arises from hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity; a stronger climate signal does not by itself determine loss.
B. Trend attribution, event attribution and disaster-loss attribution ask whether climate changed a trend, altered a particular event, and why that event produced losses.
C. Residual risk remains after feasible mitigation, adaptation and DRR; it still requires preparedness, protection, finance and recovery.
D. Non-economic loss includes life, health, culture, identity, territory, biodiversity and social cohesion that markets may not represent adequately.

**Answer: A**

- **A:** Correct: This is the precise meaning of **Climate disaster risk equation**.
- **B:** Incorrect: This describes **Attribution boundary**, not the concept asked.
- **C:** Incorrect: This describes **Residual risk**, not the concept asked.
- **D:** Incorrect: This describes **Non-economic loss and damage**, not the concept asked.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish hazard, exposure, vulnerability and climate-change attribution. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (149 words):**

Hazard is a potentially damaging event or trend, such as heat or extreme rainfall. Exposure is the people, livelihoods, ecosystems and assets located where it may occur. Vulnerability is their susceptibility, shaped by housing, health, poverty, dependence and service access. Capacity is the ability to anticipate, cope and recover.

Climate attribution is a separate evidence question. Trend attribution asks whether human influence changed a long-term hazard pattern; event attribution asks whether it changed a particular event's probability or intensity; disaster-loss attribution asks why the event produced observed harm. The last necessarily includes exposure and vulnerability.

Thus a stronger climate signal does not mechanically determine disaster loss. Risk can fall through land-use control, warning, resilient services and social protection even when hazard cannot be eliminated. Conversely, rising exposure can increase losses without proof that climate change caused the individual event. A precise answer states the attribution type, evidence and confidence.

### ORIGINAL 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Differentiate mitigation, adaptation, disaster risk reduction and loss and damage. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer (140 words):**

Climate mitigation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions or enhances sinks to limit future climate change. Adaptation adjusts ecological, social or economic systems to actual or expected climate impacts. Disaster risk reduction prevents new and reduces existing disaster risk through measures such as safe land use, codes and warning; it covers climate-related and non-climatic hazards.

Loss and damage concerns adverse climate impacts not fully avoided through mitigation and adaptation. The UNFCCC grammar is avert, minimise and address: mitigation helps avert; adaptation and DRR minimise; finance, technical assistance, recovery and protection address realised or unavoidable residual harm.

The categories overlap but are not substitutes. A mangrove may support adaptation and DRR while storing carbon, whereas earthquake codes are DRR without climate adaptation. Relief for any disaster is not automatically loss-and-damage finance. A complete policy combines all four while preserving causal, institutional and funding boundaries.

### ORIGINAL 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain incremental and transformational adaptation, maladaptation and limits to adaptation. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (220 words):**

Incremental adaptation reduces climate risk while retaining the system's basic character: heat plans, efficient irrigation, stronger standards or improved warnings. It is appropriate where adjustments can keep risk acceptable.

Transformational adaptation changes fundamental attributes when incremental measures become insufficient—for example, changing land use, livelihoods, settlement location or governance. It may be necessary near adaptation limits, but its scale creates rights and distributional risks.

Maladaptation occurs when an intervention increases emissions, vulnerability, inequality or future lock-in for some group, place or period. A seawall may protect one frontage while worsening erosion elsewhere; intensive groundwater-based drought adaptation may deepen long-term scarcity; relocation may reduce physical exposure but destroy livelihoods and social networks.

A soft adaptation limit can be overcome through finance, technology, knowledge or institutions. A hard limit cannot be avoided through adaptation within the system considered, such as irreversible loss of a place-based ecosystem or territory. Both are context-specific and require evidence.

Policy should use adaptation pathways: monitor risk, set trigger points, stage reversible measures and preserve options. Every intervention needs lifecycle, emissions, equity and downstream-risk tests, local decision power and grievance remedy. Residual risk should be covered by preparedness, social protection and risk finance. Therefore transformation is not automatically superior to incremental action; the correct mix depends on thresholds, feasibility, consent and whether it reduces rather than redistributes risk.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer uses the requested directive, named institutions/examples, causal analysis, equity and a qualified verdict.

### ORIGINAL 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Analyse economic and non-economic loss and damage through the avoid–minimise–address framework. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer (219 words):**

Loss and damage becomes visible after distinguishing three policy jobs. **Avert** reduces future climate change through mitigation. **Minimise** uses adaptation, DRR, early action and ecosystem protection to reduce foreseeable harm. **Address** supports realised or unavoidable residual impacts through recovery, finance, technical assistance and protection.

Economic loss includes damaged homes, infrastructure, crops, production and income. Estimates need a stated baseline, price year, direct/indirect boundary and method for avoided loss. Non-economic loss includes life, health, biodiversity, culture, identity, territory and social cohesion. These harms are real even when no adequate market price exists; community evidence and health, cultural and ecological indicators should complement monetary estimates.

The international architecture includes the Warsaw Mechanism's knowledge, coordination and action/support functions, the Santiago Network for technical assistance and the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage. Establishment or operationalisation does not prove access, approval or disbursement. Delivery must be tested by speed, grant/debt terms, transaction costs, local participation and reach to affected people.

The framework also prevents false substitution. Compensation cannot restore every cultural or ecological loss, and spending on realised damage should not crowd out adaptation. A just system finances prevention and residual harm together, protects displaced people and states attribution and uncertainty honestly. The objective is not to monetise everything, but to recognise, reduce and remedy harm through appropriate instruments.

### ORIGINAL 6 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Critically evaluate whether current loss-and-damage institutions can address climate justice without causal and status overclaiming. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer (273 words):**

Loss-and-damage institutions respond to a justice problem: populations with lower historical responsibility and adaptive capacity often suffer larger harm from equivalent hazards. The Warsaw International Mechanism builds knowledge, coordination and action/support; the Santiago Network catalyses technical assistance; and the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage, established at COP27 and operationalised at COP28, creates a dedicated finance channel.

Their value is institutional recognition of residual economic and non-economic harm, including slow-onset impacts, displacement, health, culture, biodiversity and territory. They can improve assessment, technical capacity and access to support beyond ordinary humanitarian relief.

However, status must not be confused with delivery. A fund being operationalised does not prove capital is adequate, access is simple, a project is approved or money reaches affected households. Technical-assistance requests, provider matching and completed assistance are separate stages. Pledges, deposits, commitments, approvals and disbursements are also different. High transaction costs, debt-creating finance, weak local voice and poor data can reproduce injustice.

Causal discipline matters too. Climate change may alter a hazard's probability or intensity, but disaster losses also reflect exposure, vulnerability and governance. Transparent attribution and loss assessment are needed without using uncertainty to deny obvious protection needs. Non-economic losses cannot be fully converted into compensation, and displaced persons are not automatically refugees; domestic rights and services remain essential.

A just architecture should provide additional, predictable and accessible support; prioritise grants and vulnerable groups; fund anticipatory adaptation alongside residual harm; recognise non-economic loss; enable direct/local access; publish delivery data; and provide grievance and review. Current institutions are necessary advances, but climate justice will be demonstrated only by timely, equitable outcomes, not by COP decisions or institutional labels.
