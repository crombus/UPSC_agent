## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### MCQ 1. Which statement best distinguishes an industrial accident from a chemical emergency?

A. Industrial accident is wider; chemical emergency involves hazardous chemicals.
B. Every industrial accident is biological, while every chemical emergency is nuclear.
C. A chemical emergency exists only after court-determined compensation.
D. Industrial accidents occur only inside factories and never during transport.

**Answer: A**

- **A:** Correct: industrial events include several hazard types, while chemical emergency identifies the agent pathway.
- **B:** Incorrect: the categories have distinct mechanisms.
- **C:** Incorrect: emergency status does not await litigation.
- **D:** Incorrect: industrial hazards can arise during transport, storage and waste handling.

### MCQ 2. What does CBRN denote?

A. One uniform medical protocol for all hazardous materials.
B. Chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear hazards.
C. Only deliberate military use of toxic agents.
D. A replacement for industrial-safety law.

**Answer: B**

- **A:** Incorrect: detection, PPE, decontamination and care differ by agent.
- **B:** Correct: the acronym groups four distinct hazard families.
- **C:** Incorrect: civilian accidents and natural occurrences can also create CBRN consequences.
- **D:** Incorrect: it is a category, not a statute.

### MCQ 3. Which statement correctly separates nuclear and radiological emergencies?

A. Every lost radioactive source is a nuclear reactor accident.
B. A nuclear emergency cannot create radioactive contamination.
C. Radiological may involve a source; nuclear involves chain or facility events.
D. The terms are interchangeable whenever AERB is consulted.

**Answer: C**

- **A:** Incorrect: source incidents need not involve reactors.
- **B:** Incorrect: nuclear events may have radiological consequences.
- **C:** Correct: it identifies the defining context while recognising overlap in consequences.
- **D:** Incorrect: institutional involvement does not erase the distinction.

### MCQ 4. Which statement about radiation is accurate?

A. External exposure always leaves radioactive material on the person.
B. Contamination can never cause internal exposure.
C. A dose-rate reading is identical to a chemical concentration.
D. Irradiation may occur without contamination; contamination means material transfer.

**Answer: D**

- **A:** Incorrect: irradiation can occur without material transfer.
- **B:** Incorrect: inhaled or ingested contamination can expose internally.
- **C:** Incorrect: they are different hazards and units.
- **D:** Correct: it separates energy exposure from material contamination.

- **A:** Correct: reducing hazard at source precedes emergency response.
- **B:** Incorrect: larger inventory can increase consequence.
- **C:** Incorrect: off-site action cannot replace prevention.
- **D:** Incorrect: alarm management must preserve protective function.

### SHARED PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-III Q16 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording (obvious OCR spelling normalised):** “With growing energy needs should India keep on expanding its nuclear energy programme? Discuss the facts and fears associated with nuclear energy.”

**Primary owner:** Science and Technology; shared here only for nuclear-safety and emergency-governance dimensions.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 235 words):**

Nuclear power can provide firm low-carbon electricity with high energy density and limited fuel volume, supporting energy security and grid stability. India's indigenous fuel-cycle effort and expanding electricity demand therefore justify a continuing, calibrated nuclear programme.

The benefits are not costless. Nuclear plants require large capital, long construction periods, specialised skills, secure fuel and waste management. Low-probability accidents can have high-consequence radiological, land-use and trust impacts; natural hazards can produce NaTech sequences. Routine radiation protection, spent-fuel stewardship, decommissioning and security impose lifecycle duties.

Expansion should therefore be conditional on independent and competent regulation. AERB must apply siting, design, construction, operation and decommissioning standards and review emergency plans. Operators need layered containment, safety culture, transparent incident reporting and practised plant/site plans. District authorities require off-site warning, shelter/evacuation, health monitoring and public communication, supported technically by DAE's Crisis Management Group. The principles of time, distance, shielding and containment guide protection.

Liability also matters. The Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act channels no-fault operator liability but contains statutory caps; this improves claims clarity but does not prove that compensation is adequate for every catastrophic or long-latency harm.

India should expand only where least-cost system planning, safety evidence, waste/decommissioning capacity, public trust and emergency readiness justify the site and technology. Nuclear energy is an option, not an exemption from full lifecycle accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances energy benefits and fears, names Indian safety institutions and law, and gives a conditional verdict.

### SHARED PYQ 2 — 2023 GS-III Q8 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Official wording:** “What is oil pollution? What are its impacts on the marine ecosystem? In what way is oil pollution particularly harmful for a country like India?”

**Primary owner:** Environment and Ecology Topic 24; shared here for industrial-spill preparedness and response.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\QP-CSM-23-GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-III-180923.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 145 words):**

Oil pollution is the introduction of petroleum hydrocarbons into marine or coastal waters at levels that harm organisms, habitats, uses or human welfare. It can arise from tankers, pipelines, refineries, offshore operations, storage and routine discharges.

Oil coats birds and mammals, impairs insulation and movement, causes ingestion/inhalation toxicity, damages plankton, fish, shellfish, wetlands and reefs, disrupts food webs, and contaminates beaches and fisheries. Clean-up may expose workers, while dispersants or in-situ burning shift impacts between shoreline, water column and air.

India is particularly vulnerable because of its long, densely settled coast; major ports, shipping and refining; mangroves, estuaries and coral systems; and millions of livelihoods linked to fishing, aquaculture and tourism. The December 2023 CPCL/Ennore spill is a source-dated example.

Indian Coast Guard's NOSDCP coordinates response, but preparedness, rapid containment, polluter accountability and long-term ecological/livelihood monitoring determine recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines pollution, covers ecological mechanisms, explains India-specific exposure and adds response with trade-offs.

### APPLICATION PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-IV Q10 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Official case:** “A big corporate house is engaged in manufacturing industrial chemicals on a large scale. It proposes to set up an additional unit. Many States rejected its proposal due to detrimental effect on the environment. But one State government acceded to the request and permitted the unit close to a city, brushing aside all opposition. The unit was set up 10 years ago and was in full swing till recently. The pollution caused by the industrial effluents was affecting the land, water and crops in the area. It was also causing serious health problems to human beings and animals. This gave rise to a series of agitations demanding the closure of the plant. In a recent agitation thousands of people took part, creating a law and order problem necessitating stern police action. Following the public outcry, the State government ordered the closure of the factory. The closure of the factory resulted in the unemployment of not only those workers who were engaged in the factory but also those who were working in the ancillary units. It also very badly affected those industries which depended on the chemicals manufactured by it. As a senior officer entrusted with the responsibility of handling this issue, how are you going to address it?”

**Primary owner:** Ethics case-study method; used here as industrial-risk, remediation and just-transition application.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\GENERAL-STUDIES-PAPER-IV.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 245 words):**

The officer must protect life and environment while ensuring due process and a just livelihood transition. Stakeholders include affected residents, workers, ancillary firms, farmers, animals/ecosystems, the company, regulators, police and future generations.

First, maintain peace through proportionate policing, dialogue and verified health/environment information; protect lawful protest and prevent violence. Constitute an independent multidisciplinary assessment of emissions/effluent, health records, water/soil damage, compliance and continuing risk. Preserve evidence and prevent regulatory capture.

Second, keep hazardous operations suspended where immediate danger exists, while allowing only safe maintenance and source-control work. Enforce EPA/pollution-control and occupational-safety duties, require time-bound remediation, medical screening and transparent monitoring. Use polluter-pays and applicable compensation routes; no-fault interim relief does not replace full adjudication or restoration.

Third, distinguish closure from abandonment. Secure hazardous inventory and waste, fund remediation, and hold management and complicit officials accountable after fair inquiry. If safe compliance is technically possible, reopening should require independent verification, modernised process safeguards, reduced inventory, buffer controls, on/off-site plans and community oversight—not political assurance.

Fourth, provide interim income support, retraining, placement and assistance to ancillary units; require the company to contribute to transition costs. If relocation is necessary, appraise a safer site transparently.

The ethical balance is not jobs versus health: it is lawful, evidence-based production in which the polluter bears prevention and recovery costs and vulnerable groups do not bear either pollution or abrupt closure alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies stakeholders/values, sequences lawful action, integrates industrial safety and offers a balanced livelihood solution.

### APPLICATION PYQ 2 — 2020 GS-III Q18 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording:** “Discuss the recent measures initiated in disaster management by the Government of India departing from the earlier reactive approach.”

**Primary owner:** Disaster Management Topic 02; industrial/CBRN is a hazard-specific application.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\Gen_St_P3.pdf.md`.

**Model answer (about 230 words):**

India's disaster policy has moved in direction from relief-centric response toward prevention, mitigation, preparedness and resilient recovery, although field implementation remains uneven.

Industrial and CBRN governance illustrates the shift. EPA safeguards, MSIHC prevention and emergency-planning duties, the Chemical Accidents Rules' crisis groups and occupier/district on-site–off-site plans seek to act before release. Process-safety audits, inventory reduction, buffer-zone planning, transport controls, public information, hospital preparedness and joint exercises reduce consequences before impact. AERB's lifecycle consents and emergency-plan review, together with DAE's technical emergency network, apply the same anticipatory logic to nuclear/radiological risk.

Technology and institutions further enable early action: material inventories, detectors, modelling, communication systems and specialist NDRF/State capacities can trigger isolation, shelter or evacuation. Recovery now requires health surveillance, compensation, environmental remediation and safer reconstruction rather than simple reopening.

However, a dense legal framework is not evidence of compliance. The local source itself identifies weak enforcement. An on-site plan may not connect to district hospitals; an inspection may not close defects; a liability cap may not cover long-tail harm; and natural hazards can defeat multiple plant systems through NaTech cascades.

The proactive transition should therefore be judged by barrier reliability, exercised plans, warning/action time and verified recovery—not the number of laws, plans or equipment sets.

**Why this earns marks:** It establishes the policy shift, applies it across prevention-to-recovery and critically identifies implementation metrics.

### APPLICATION PYQ 3 — 2024 GS-III Q17 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Official wording:** “What is disaster resilience? How is it determined? Describe various elements of a resilience framework. Also mention the global targests of Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015-2030)”

**Primary owner:** Disaster Management Topic 01. “targests” is retained from the official local English extraction.

**Local official paper:** `knowledge-export\Mains PYQ\03 UPSC 2024 Paper-III.md`.

**Model answer (about 245 words):**

Disaster resilience is the capacity of a system or community to resist, absorb, adapt, transform and recover while maintaining essential functions. It is determined by hazard and exposure, physical/social/environmental vulnerability, institutional and financial capacity, redundancy, learning and recovery time.

A resilience framework contains risk assessment; safe siting and prevention; structural/non-structural mitigation; warning and risk communication; inclusive preparedness; robust health and lifelines; coordinated response; social/financial protection; and Build Back Better with measurable outcomes.

For industrial/CBRN risk, resilience means inherent safety and layered barriers; current hazardous-material information; enforced on-site and off-site plans; chemical crisis groups linked to DDMA; AERB-regulated nuclear preparedness and DAE technical support; trained incident command; agent-specific PPE, decontamination and triage; hospital protection; no-fault interim relief plus broader compensation; and long-term health/environmental remediation. NaTech screening tests whether floods, cyclones or earthquakes can disable several safeguards simultaneously.

Sendai's seven global targets seek reductions in mortality, affected people, economic loss and critical-infrastructure/basic-service damage; more national/local DRR strategies; enhanced international cooperation for developing countries; and wider access to multi-hazard early warning and risk information. Target E used 2020; the remaining target horizon is 2030.

Thus resilience is not a safety certificate or drill count. It is demonstrated when barriers work, warnings produce protective action, hospitals remain functional and recovery lowers future exposure.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines/determines resilience, covers framework and Sendai, and gives a hazard-specific evidence-led application.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish on-site and off-site emergency plans for a major chemical-hazard installation.

**Model answer (about 140 words):**

An **on-site emergency plan** is prepared by the occupier/operator for events within the installation. Under MSIHC Rule 13, it should organise alarm, shutdown and isolation, firefighting, worker accountability, rescue, first aid, site evacuation, hazardous-material information and liaison. Plant management controls the source.

An **off-site emergency plan**, under Rule 14, is prepared by the designated public authority in consultation with the occupier and services. It addresses consequences beyond the boundary: public warning, shelter or evacuation, traffic, hospitals, environmental monitoring, mutual aid and verified information. District civil authority coordinates public protection.

The plans are complementary. The operator must rapidly communicate substance, quantity, release and likely plume; the district must translate this into external action. Joint drills should test handover, hospitals and last-mile messages.

A plan document alone is not readiness: update, exercise, corrective-action closure and public comprehension are necessary.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives rule-level ownership, functional distinctions, the interface and an implementation caveat.

### ORIGINAL 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain the distinction between exposure and contamination in a radiological emergency and its importance for triage.

**Model answer (about 130 words):**

**Exposure** means ionising radiation has deposited or may deposit energy in a person. It can occur externally without radioactive material being transferred. **Contamination** means radioactive material is present on the skin, clothing, wound or inside the body; it can continue exposing the person and may spread.

The distinction changes triage. A seriously injured but uncontaminated exposed patient needs dose and clinical assessment, not automatic decontamination. A contaminated casualty requires controlled entry, contamination survey and agent-appropriate decontamination while urgent lifesaving care continues. Receiving hospitals must be warned so ambulances, staff and treatment areas are protected.

Time, distance and shielding reduce external dose; containment and contamination control prevent material spread. Neither visible symptoms nor the word “radiation” proves contamination or dose. Specialist measurement must guide action.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms, applies them to triage and prevents a common CBRN response error.

### ORIGINAL 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Examine whether India's chemical-accident problem is primarily a gap of law or a gap of implementation.

**Model answer (about 230 words):**

India possesses a dense chemical-safety framework, so the dominant problem is often implementation and coordination, though legal updating and harmonisation remain necessary.

The EPA 1986 requires prescribed safeguards for hazardous substances. MSIHC Rules 1989 cover prevention, notification, safety reporting and on/off-site emergency planning. Chemical Accidents Rules 1996 create Central, State, District and Local Crisis Groups. The labour-safety framework has transitioned from the Factories Act hazardous-process provisions to the OSHWC Code commenced in November 2025. PLI provides no-fault interim relief; NGT offers a distinct compensation/restoration route.

Yet compliance depends on current inventories, competent inspection, closed audit findings, maintained alarms/interlocks, trained workers, compatible storage, practised plans and land-use control. Parallel crisis and general DM structures can blur command. Informal settlements may expand into buffers, while hospitals may lack chemical information or decontamination controls. NaTech events can exceed design assumptions.

Legal gaps also matter: overlapping definitions and regulators require harmonisation, new processes/transport risks need current standards, and liability caps or schedule-based relief may under-cover catastrophic and delayed harm.

Therefore, the answer is not law **or** implementation. Enforce existing source-control and planning duties, update standards through notified law, publish compliance/outcome evidence, protect whistle-blowers and communities, and link operator, regulator and district accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It cites the statutory chain, evaluates implementation failures, acknowledges genuine legal gaps and reaches a nuanced verdict.

### ORIGINAL 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Discuss the principles of triage, decontamination and incident command in a civilian CBRN emergency.

**Model answer (about 225 words):**

CBRN consequence management begins by identifying the likely agent and preventing responders and hospitals from becoming additional casualties.

**Incident command** establishes common objectives and controlled hot/exclusion, warm/contamination-reduction and cold/support zones. Facility staff provide inventories and source knowledge; district administration coordinates public protection; fire, police, health, NDRF/State teams and specialist agencies operate through one information and task structure.

**Decontamination** prevents transfer of hazardous material. Casualties are directed through controlled entry, contaminated items are isolated, and dry or wet methods are selected for the agent. Water is not universal because some chemicals react or contaminated runoff can spread harm. External radiation exposure alone does not require decontamination unless radioactive material is present.

**Triage** prioritises immediate threats to life while recording contamination/exposure and protecting staff. Airway, breathing and circulation cannot be delayed mechanically for perfect identification, but receiving facilities need warning, protected routes and casualty distribution. Biological incidents may require infection-control rather than a chemical wash line.

Public action—shelter, evacuation, food/water restriction or all-clear—must be authorised and agent-, plume-, dose- and time-specific. Drills should test interfaces and close deficiencies.

Thus unified command provides coordination, while hazard-specific science determines PPE, decontamination and medical care.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats all three requested elements, differentiates CBRN pathways and balances lifesaving urgency with contamination control.

### ORIGINAL 5 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Critically analyse India's institutional architecture for chemical and nuclear/radiological emergencies.

**Model answer (about 285 words):**

India's architecture is layered and hazard-specific, but overlapping mandates and uneven implementation can weaken the last mile.

For chemicals, the EPA 1986 anchors hazardous-substance safeguards. MSIHC Rules place prevention, safety-report and on-site duties on occupiers and off-site planning on designated authorities. Chemical Accidents Rules create Central, State, District and Local Crisis Groups. PESO regulates explosives, petroleum and assigned hazardous-chemical domains; CPCB/SPCBs address pollution-control functions. General DDMA/SDMA machinery and response forces support consequence management.

For nuclear/radiological risk, the Atomic Energy Act is the legal base. AERB sets safety codes, grants lifecycle consents, inspects and reviews emergency preparedness; operators control the facility. For public-domain emergencies, DAE's Crisis Management Group supplies specialist assessment and advice, while district/State authorities implement public protection with national support.

Strengths include specialised expertise, multi-level planning, no-fault relief under PLI, nuclear-liability architecture and NDMA guidance. Weaknesses include regulator/operator information asymmetry, stale or unexercised plans, unclear interface between crisis groups and general incident command, uneven hospital/decontamination capacity, buffer-zone encroachment, hazardous transport and NaTech common-cause failure.

Reform requires a unified district command matrix; interoperable inventories; independent audits and public closure of findings; joint on/off-site exercises; risk-based land use; hospital networks; updated guidance; worker/community right-to-know; and recovery monitoring. Liability must distinguish immediate relief, full compensation, criminal accountability and environmental restoration.

The architecture is therefore institutionally rich but resilience-poor wherever plans and regulation do not produce functioning barriers, rapid protective action and verified remediation.

**Why this earns marks:** It maps precise actors and laws, evaluates interfaces and proposes enforceable, outcome-linked reform.

### ORIGINAL 6 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Develop a prevention-to-recovery framework for a major toxic release affecting a city and an adjoining river.

**Model answer (about 290 words):**

The scenario combines worker, urban, public-health and environmental risk; command must control the source while protecting people and the river.

**Prevention:** require accurate hazardous inventory; safer substitution and minimum stock; compatible storage; corrosion and interlock assurance; secondary containment; protected utilities; flood/seismic screening; transport/waste controls; worker training; and independent closure of audit findings. Land-use planning should prevent new dense exposure while treating existing settlements fairly.

**Preparedness:** the occupier's Rule 13 on-site plan and designated authority's Rule 14 off-site plan should share plume/runoff scenarios, contacts and mutual aid. Hospitals need chemical information, protected entry and decontamination capability. Public messages must distinguish shelter from evacuation; drills must test vulnerable groups and river-intake shutdown.

**Response:** establish unified incident command and hot/warm/cold zones; identify the chemical from reliable records/instruments; stop or contain release if trained teams can do so; monitor air and water; select safe shelter/evacuation routes; conduct contamination-aware triage and agent-appropriate decontamination; distribute casualties; protect responders; and issue regular verified updates.

**Relief and recovery:** provide medical care and no-fault interim relief where applicable; continue epidemiological/clinical surveillance; sample soil, groundwater, sediment and biota; remediate under polluter-pays responsibility; restore drinking water, fisheries and livelihoods; investigate causes and regulatory failure; prosecute where evidence supports; and redesign or relocate unsafe systems.

Trade-offs include speed versus evidence, evacuation versus plume exposure and plant closure versus employment. Transparent uncertainty, independent monitoring and worker/community participation are essential.

Success is measured by source-control time, exposure avoided, hospital safety, contaminant decline, compensation speed, livelihood restoration and non-recurrence—not by the number of agencies deployed.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers the full cycle, river pathway, command/medical detail, liability and measurable recovery.
