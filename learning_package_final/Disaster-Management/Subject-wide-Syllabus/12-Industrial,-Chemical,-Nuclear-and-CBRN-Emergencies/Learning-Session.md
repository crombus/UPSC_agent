## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Define the emergency before choosing the response

| Category | Core mechanism | Typical public-protection concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial accident** | unintended failure in extraction, process, manufacture, storage, transport, use or waste handling | fire, explosion, release, structural failure or service disruption |
| **Chemical emergency** | toxic, corrosive, reactive, flammable or explosive substance escapes control | plume/contact/ingestion, fire, contamination and delayed toxicity |
| **Biological emergency** | pathogen or biological toxin causes illness; may transmit between hosts | surveillance, infection control, treatment and public-health measures |
| **Radiological emergency** | radioactive source/exposure or contamination without requiring a nuclear chain event | dose control, contamination control and specialist monitoring |
| **Nuclear emergency** | event involving a nuclear facility, reactor/fuel-cycle or nuclear-chain context | radiological release plus possible heat, pressure and facility consequences |
| **CBRN** | umbrella: chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear | category for coordination—not one mechanism or one protocol |

Hazard, incident and disaster are distinct. A release is a **hazard occurrence**; exposed people and ecosystems, vulnerability and failed capacity determine whether it becomes a disaster. Deliberate hostile use belongs operationally to Internal Security; this topic covers civilian preparedness and consequence management.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** CBRN is an umbrella for coordination, not permission to merge toxic exposure, infection and ionising-radiation pathways.

**Must-write keywords:** industrial accident · toxic release · biological transmission · radiological contamination · nuclear emergency · CBRN · consequence management.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* the substance or energy pathway determines protection. *Evidence:* chemical plumes, infectious agents and radioactive contamination require different detection and medical routes. *Analysis:* a generic “evacuate and deploy NDRF” answer can worsen exposure. *Qualification:* unified command is still needed for shared logistics and public information.

### 2. Industrial risk is a life-cycle and barrier-management problem

```text
HAZARD INVENTORY / ENERGY
        ↓
design + siting + process choice
        ↓
engineering barriers + alarms + interlocks + containment
        ↓
operation + inspection + maintenance + management of change
        ↓
deviation / corrosion / fatigue / utility failure / human-organisational error
        ↓
barriers fail → fire / explosion / toxic or radioactive release
        ↓
people + environment + services exposed
```

Prevention follows the entire chain: substitute a less hazardous material where feasible; minimise inventory; assess compatibility, flammability, toxicity and explosion risk; design independent safeguards; maintain and inspect; control contractor work and changes; protect utilities; and learn from near misses.

An “operator error” may be the last act in a deeper system failure involving design, workload, training, alarm quality, maintenance and safety culture.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Industrial disasters are rarely one mistake; they are failures of layered barriers across a hazardous material's life cycle.

**Must-write keywords:** inherent safety · inventory reduction · process safety · interlock · containment · management of change · safety culture · near miss.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* safety audit must test barrier health, not paperwork. *Evidence:* the local source names design review, compatibility assessment, interlocks, scrubbers and periodic audits. *Analysis:* independent layers prevent one deviation becoming loss of containment. *Qualification:* an audit completed is not evidence that findings were closed.

### 3. Hazard, trigger, exposure and vulnerability in an industrial accident

| Element | Chemical-plant example |
|---|---|
| **Hazard** | stored toxic/flammable material and process energy |
| **Conditioning weakness** | corrosion, poor layout, excess inventory, disabled alarm, weak maintenance |
| **Trigger** | valve failure, power loss, operator action, fire, flood, cyclone or earthquake |
| **Exposure** | workers, nearby settlement, road/rail users, water body and responders |
| **Vulnerability** | weak housing, no information, night-time release, limited mobility, inadequate hospital/PPE capacity |
| **Capacity** | containment, shutdown, on/off-site plans, trained teams, warning, evacuation/shelter and treatment |

The local evidence cites the 2001 Kandla earthquake–acrylonitrile release and 1999 Odisha cyclone–phosphoric-acid-sludge containment damage as **NaTech** examples: a natural hazard triggers a technological accident.

A **Major Accident Hazard (MAH) unit** is a threshold-based regulatory category for installations handling specified hazardous chemicals. The local source's count of about 1,861 units across 301 districts is document-period evidence only; do not present it as a current inventory.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A hazardous inventory becomes catastrophic when a trigger defeats weak barriers and the release intersects poorly protected workers, communities and ecosystems.

**Must-write keywords:** hazard inventory · conditioning weakness · initiating event · loss of containment · NaTech · exposure pathway · capacity.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* flood safety belongs inside chemical safety. *Evidence:* the source's Odisha cyclone containment example shows a natural trigger defeating an industrial barrier. *Analysis:* site assessment must use multi-hazard design conditions. *Qualification:* the example establishes a pathway, not universal facility failure.

### 4. On-site and off-site emergency plans are complementary

| Feature | On-site plan | Off-site plan |
|---|---|---|
| primary scope | consequences within the installation/site | consequences outside the boundary |
| primary preparation | occupier/operator under applicable law/rules | designated district/off-site authority in consultation with occupier and services |
| focus | alarm, shutdown/isolation, firefighting, rescue, accountability, first aid, site evacuation, material information | public warning, shelter/evacuation, traffic, hospitals, mutual aid, environmental monitoring, public information |
| command | site emergency controller/plant management | district civil authority/unified off-site command |
| shared interface | release data, plume/runout estimate, liaison, resource request and handover | must be tested jointly through exercises |

Under the MSIHC Rules, 1989, **Rule 13** concerns the occupier's on-site emergency plan; **Rule 14** concerns the off-site emergency plan prepared by the designated authority. The historical Factories Act, 1948, section 41B(4), also required an on-site plan and disclosure for hazardous-process factories.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The factory controls the source; the district protects the public—an emergency plan fails if that boundary becomes a coordination gap.

**Must-write keywords:** Rule 13 · Rule 14 · occupier · designated authority · site controller · district command · mutual aid · joint drill.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* an excellent on-site plan cannot protect an uninformed neighbourhood. *Evidence:* MSIHC separates occupier and off-site planning duties. *Analysis:* warning, traffic, hospitals and public shelter require district coordination. *Qualification:* exact authority and plan coverage must be checked for the installation.

### 5. Chemical-law architecture: know instrument, duty and status

```text
Factories Act 1948 (hazardous-process duties; historical foundation)
        ↓ post-Bhopal strengthening
Environment (Protection) Act 1986
        ├─ s.8: hazardous substances only with prescribed procedure/safeguards
        ├─ MSIHC Rules 1989: prevention, notification, safety reports,
        │                     Rules 13–14 emergency planning
        └─ Chemical Accidents Rules 1996:
              Central → State → District → Local Crisis Groups

Public Liability Insurance Act 1991 → no-fault immediate relief + insurance
NGT Act 2010 → adjudicatory relief/compensation/restitution route
OSHWC Code 2020 → labour-safety consolidation; commenced 21 Nov 2025
```

The exact title is **Manufacture, Storage and Import of Hazardous Chemical Rules, 1989**. Do not rename it “Hazardous Substances Rules.” The Chemical Accidents Rules create a parallel, chemical-specific crisis-group chain; they do not abolish general DM Act coordination.

The Labour Ministry/e-Gazette records commencement of the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 on **21 November 2025**. Therefore, the Factories Act should now be used as the historical origin of hazardous-process safeguards, not casually presented as the sole current labour-safety code; applicable rules and savings must be checked for a legal answer.

The **Explosives Act, 1884**, **Petroleum Act, 1934** and **Insecticides Act, 1968** remain hazard/class-specific parts of the historical statutory mosaic. The local source's Hazardous Wastes Rules, 2008 reference is also historical: current waste-law answers should check the **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016**, as amended.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** India's problem is less absence of statutes than correct allocation, harmonisation and enforcement of duties across overlapping regimes.

**Must-write keywords:** EPA 1986 · section 8 · MSIHC 1989 · Chemical Accidents Rules 1996 · OSHWC Code · crisis groups · enforcement.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* citing a list of Acts does not establish safety. *Evidence:* the local source itself says enforcement “has left much to be desired.” *Analysis:* compliance must be visible in inventories, audits, plans, drills and buffer governance. *Qualification:* legal status must be checked at the answer date.

### 6. Crisis groups, regulators and responders have different mandates

| Actor | Principal role | Not to be confused with |
|---|---|---|
| **MoEFCC / Central Crisis Group** | national chemical-accident preparedness review, coordination and guidance under 1996 Rules | plant operator or district incident commander |
| **State/District/Local Crisis Groups** | hazard-specific planning, preparedness and coordination at respective levels | general-purpose replacement for SDMA/DDMA |
| **occupier/operator** | prevent loss of control, disclose hazards, prepare/practise on-site plan and support off-site plan | regulator |
| **designated district authority** | off-site public protection and inter-service command | plant process controller |
| **PESO** | statutory licensing/safety functions for explosives, petroleum and assigned hazardous-chemical domains | sole regulator of every chemical |
| **CPCB/SPCB/PCC** | pollution-control and environmental monitoring/enforcement functions | primary fire/rescue command |
| **NDRF/State response services** | specialist consequence-management support when deployed | licensing or routine plant inspection |

General DM institutions and chemical crisis groups must be connected in the district plan. A major accident may require one operational incident command with technical advice from multiple regulators.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Regulation, source control, scientific advice and incident command are separate functions; accountability disappears when they are called simply “the authority.”

**Must-write keywords:** Central Crisis Group · State/District/Local Crisis Group · occupier · PESO · SPCB · DDMA · unified command.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* the district needs a pre-agreed command matrix. *Evidence:* chemical rules create crisis groups while the DM Act supplies general district machinery. *Analysis:* parallel structures otherwise duplicate information or delay evacuation. *Qualification:* command arrangements must follow the notified State/district plan.

### 7. Chemical emergency: route of exposure controls protective action

```text
IDENTIFY / ASSUME HAZARD SAFELY
        ↓
source control by trained facility team
        ↓
model/observe plume, runoff and contamination
        ↓
protect public: isolate · shelter-in-place OR evacuate
        ↓
decontaminate where indicated → triage/treat → monitor environment
```

Inhalation, skin/eye contact and ingestion are different exposure pathways. Shelter-in-place may reduce exposure to a short external plume when safe buildings and reliable instructions exist; evacuation may be necessary for prolonged or escalating risk but can move people through contamination. The choice is substance-, weather-, time- and location-specific.

Chemical identification should use labels, manifests, safety data and specialist instruments. Odour is not a safe detector. Water is not a universal decontaminant because some materials react with it.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Protective action must follow the agent, route, plume and time window; “evacuate everyone” is not a universally safe chemical response.

**Must-write keywords:** inhalation/contact/ingestion · plume · isolation · shelter-in-place · evacuation · source control · substance-specific.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* immediate mass movement can increase dose. *Evidence:* a toxic plume may cross an evacuation route. *Analysis:* incident command must compare indoor protection, release duration and route safety. *Qualification:* the decision requires current specialist information.

### 8. Incident command, zones, decontamination and triage

| Function | Safe principle |
|---|---|
| scene control | establish hot/exclusion, warm/contamination-reduction and cold/support zones as incident conditions require |
| responder safety | identify hazard, select compatible PPE, monitor atmosphere/radiation and prevent unprotected entry |
| decontamination | stop spread, remove/isolate contaminated items and use agent-appropriate dry or wet methods |
| triage | prioritise life-threatening conditions while accounting for contamination and responder/hospital safety |
| healthcare protection | alert receiving facilities, control entry and prevent secondary contamination |
| command | one incident objectives process with facility, district, fire, police, health, regulator and specialist advice |

Decontamination is not the same as medical treatment. **Exposure** does not always mean **contamination**: a person externally irradiated by a source may not carry radioactive material, whereas contamination involves material on/in the body. Life-saving care should not be delayed mechanically; responders balance immediate clinical need with contamination control.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** CBRN triage treats the patient and the contamination pathway simultaneously, without turning the hospital into a secondary incident site.

**Must-write keywords:** hot/warm/cold zone · PPE compatibility · decontamination · triage · secondary contamination · unified command.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* ordinary mass-casualty flow may fail in a chemical release. *Evidence:* contaminated casualties can carry material into ambulances and hospitals. *Analysis:* controlled zones and hospital notification protect the care system. *Qualification:* decontamination method must be agent-specific.

### 9. Biological, chemical, radiological and nuclear response diverge

| Hazard | Detection/tempo | Protection and health emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| chemical | often rapid symptoms or detector/plume evidence; some delayed effects | source isolation, respiratory/skin protection, decontamination and toxicology |
| biological | incubation may delay recognition; transmission pattern and laboratory confirmation matter | surveillance, infection prevention, isolation/quarantine where lawful, treatment/vaccination as applicable |
| radiological | dose-rate/source/contamination monitoring | time, distance, shielding; contamination control and dose assessment |
| nuclear | facility/criticality or fuel-cycle event with radiological consequences | plant/site/off-site plans, containment, technical command and public protection |

CBRN responders share command, cordons, PPE discipline, triage, communication and logistics. They do not share one detector, one decontamination method or one medical treatment. Biological public-health governance is developed in Topic 13; hostile-use investigation belongs to Internal Security.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Unified command should integrate agencies while hazard-specific science keeps protective and medical decisions separate.

**Must-write keywords:** incubation · toxicology · dose rate · contamination · criticality · infection control · specialist advice.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* visible casualties do not reveal the agent. *Evidence:* biological incubation and radiological contamination may be initially silent. *Analysis:* surveillance and specialist detection must accompany clinical triage. *Qualification:* avoid speculative agent attribution.

### 10. Nuclear and radiological emergency architecture

```text
PLANT EMERGENCY → limited plant area; operator command
       ↓ escalation
SITE EMERGENCY → wider site affected; site plan activated
       ↓ possible off-site consequence
OFF-SITE EMERGENCY → district/public-protection plan + DAE technical support
```

The local source's four protection principles are **time, distance, shielding and containment**. AERB, constituted on 15 November 1983 under powers of section 27 of the Atomic Energy Act, 1962, develops safety codes/standards, grants consents after review, inspects compliance and reviews emergency plans. It is regulator, not plant operator.

DAE and MHA are identified for public-domain nuclear/radiological emergency management; DAE's Crisis Management Group provides technical assessment and advice. DAE's current page records two round-the-clock emergency control rooms and 25 Radiation Emergency Response Centres; this is capacity evidence, not proof of response time or outcome.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The operator controls the source, AERB regulates safety and the district protects the public with DAE's specialist technical support.

**Must-write keywords:** plant/site/off-site emergency · time · distance · shielding · containment · AERB · CMG-DAE · RERC.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* radiological expertise does not displace civil authority. *Evidence:* DAE's CMG supplies assessment and technical advice to local authorities. *Analysis:* public warning, movement and services require unified off-site command. *Qualification:* response roles vary by emergency classification.

### 11. Liability provides relief but does not replace prevention or remediation

| Instrument | Core function | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991** | section 3 no-fault relief for specified harm from accidents involving hazardous substances; section 4 insurance duty; Environmental Relief Fund under section 7A | immediate/statutory relief is not full tort compensation, criminal guilt or ecological restoration |
| **NGT Act, 2010** | Tribunal may order relief/compensation and restitution under its statutory jurisdiction | distinct adjudicatory route; not the source of PLI's no-fault principle |
| **Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010** | channels no-fault liability to operator within statutory framework; section 6 sets incident/operator limits | capped/channelled liability and restricted recourse are not unlimited victim compensation |
| **polluter-pays/remediation duties** | restore environment and address long-term harm through applicable law/order | insurance payment does not prove remediation completed |

Under section 6 of the nuclear-liability Act, the operator limit for a nuclear reactor of 10 MW thermal or above is ₹1,500 crore unless a higher amount is notified; the Act also defines the overall national liability framework by reference to 300 million SDR. State these only as statutory limits, not as an estimate of adequate compensation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** No-fault liability accelerates access to relief; it neither proves criminal fault nor guarantees full compensation, health surveillance and environmental remediation.

**Must-write keywords:** no-fault relief · insurance · Environmental Relief Fund · NGT · capped liability · operator channeling · remediation.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* compensation cannot be the safety strategy. *Evidence:* PLI provides a rapid no-fault route, while prevention duties sit in process-safety and environmental regimes. *Analysis:* post-harm relief cannot restore life or eliminate contamination. *Qualification:* legal routes, ceilings and proof requirements differ.

### 12. Buffer zones, land use and risk communication

Buffer planning should prevent new high-density exposure around major-hazard installations and preserve emergency access. Retrospective relocation is socially and legally harder: low-income settlements may depend on nearby employment and have weak tenure. Alternatives include risk reduction at source, safer process/inventory, land-use controls for new growth, building and ventilation measures, transparent disclosure, voluntary/just relocation where unavoidable, and livelihood protection.

Risk communication must say what is known, unknown and changing; identify the responsible authority; use local languages and accessible formats; distinguish shelter from evacuation; counter rumours; and protect commercially sensitive information without concealing community safety facts.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A buffer zone is preventive land-use governance, but moving risk-bearing communities without rights, compensation and livelihoods merely redistributes disaster.

**Must-write keywords:** buffer zone · right to know · risk communication · shelter/evacuate · informal settlement · just relocation · confidentiality limit.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* secrecy can magnify chemical exposure. *Evidence:* on/off-site planning requires hazard information and public protection. *Analysis:* people cannot follow agent-specific instructions they never received. *Qualification:* disclosure can protect legitimate security information while communicating actionable risk.

### 13. Transport and oil-spill emergencies

Hazardous materials remain risky beyond the factory gate. Transport plans need route selection, manifests/labels, vehicle integrity, trained drivers, communications, safe parking/escort where legally required, district contact and rapid isolation.

For marine oil spills, the Indian Coast Guard coordinates the **National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan (NOSDCP)**. Response choices include containment/recovery, dispersants, in-situ burning or bioremediation, but each has sea-state, toxicity, air-quality, habitat and worker-safety trade-offs. The local source cites Oilzapper and Oilivorous-S as TERI-linked bioremediation examples and reports that the December 2023 CPCL spill affected at least 20 sq km of sea and the Ennore creek/Kosasthalaiyar setting; this extent remains source-dated, not a current condition.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Hazardous-industry governance follows the material from production to transport, waste and environmental recovery; the plant boundary is not the risk boundary.

**Must-write keywords:** hazardous transport · manifest · NOSDCP · Indian Coast Guard · containment · dispersant trade-off · bioremediation.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* fastest removal is not automatically least harmful. *Evidence:* dispersants and in-situ burning alter the form or location of exposure. *Analysis:* responders must compare shoreline, water-column, air and worker impacts. *Qualification:* method choice is spill- and conditions-specific.

### 14. Preparedness, response and recovery checklist

| Stage | Core action |
|---|---|
| prevention | safer substitute/process, inventory reduction, siting, buffers, maintenance and compliance |
| preparedness | on/off-site plans, hazard inventory, mutual aid, drills, hospital readiness, public instructions and continuity |
| response | source control, zones, monitoring, shelter/evacuation, decontamination, triage and unified command |
| relief | medical care, safe shelter/water, family information and interim assistance |
| recovery | health surveillance, compensation, environmental sampling/remediation, livelihood restoration and safer reconstruction |

Every drill needs an after-action report and closure of corrective actions. Every recovery claim needs a baseline, contaminant/health endpoint, responsible party, timeline and public reporting.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Preparedness is tested before an accident, response controls the event, and recovery proves whether health, environment and livelihoods were actually restored.

**Must-write keywords:** exercise · mutual aid · hospital surge · shelter-in-place · decontamination · health surveillance · remediation · after-action review.

**Paragraph model:** *Claim:* reopening a plant is not recovery. *Evidence:* contamination and delayed health effects may outlast production stoppage. *Analysis:* monitoring, remediation and livelihood/health support need long-term ownership. *Qualification:* causation and compensation require lawful evidence, not assumption.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Swiss-cheese and common-cause failure

Multiple barriers may each have weaknesses that align. Flood or power loss can disable cooling, alarms, communication and access simultaneously. Independent, diverse and fail-safe barriers plus common-cause analysis are more credible than one redundant component on paper.

### B. Substitution and inventory trade-offs

Replacing an extremely toxic input or reducing inventory lowers consequence at source, but an alternative may change process performance, cost or supply security. The correct test is comparative lifecycle risk and feasibility, not an assumption that substitution is costless.

### C. Safety culture and regulatory independence

Workers need authority to report near misses and stop unsafe work without retaliation. Regulators require expertise, access and enforceable independence. For nuclear safety, AERB's location within the atomic-energy governance structure raises an analytical independence question; do not convert that debate into an unsupported legal conclusion.

### D. Low-probability, high-consequence planning

Historical absence of a major event is weak proof of safety. Scenario planning should include beyond-design and NaTech sequences while remaining proportionate and avoiding sensationalism.

### E. Decontamination trade-offs

Rapid removal of contamination protects patients and facilities, but inappropriate water or methods can spread/react with an agent and create waste. Plans need substance-specific protocols, privacy, disability access, wastewater control and medical priority.

### F. Liability gap

Capped or schedule-based no-fault regimes speed relief and make risk insurable, but may under-cover catastrophic or long-latency loss. Separate immediate relief, full compensation, criminal enforcement, operator recourse, public contribution and environmental restoration.

### G. Information asymmetry

Operators know inventories/processes; communities and hospitals bear consequences. Public right-to-know, interoperable manifests and trusted alerts reduce asymmetry, but security-sensitive details still need controlled handling.

### H. Regulatory harmonisation and ageing guidance

The local source identified, for its period, gaps concerning harmonised classification/definitions, cryogenic storage and transport, risk assessment, labelling/packaging, compensation and international alignment. These are issues to verify against current notifications, not automatically current legal absences. NDMA's principal chemical, mass-casualty and nuclear/radiological guidelines date from 2007–2009, so plans should test whether later inventories, logistics, urban exposure and NaTech conditions are reflected.

### I. Oil-spill scale comparison

The local source uses Deepwater Horizon—about 4.9 million barrels over 87 days—as an international scale comparator, not evidence about Indian compliance. The December 2023 CPCL/Ennore case is the closer Indian governance example.

### J. Outcome dashboard

Track barrier failures and near misses; audit closure; plan/drill performance; detection-to-warning; worker/community reach; evacuation/shelter time; decontamination throughput and safety; hospital contamination; acute and long-term health; environmental levels; compensation time; restoration; recurrence. Do not substitute inspections held or kits purchased for safety outcomes.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Hazard distinctions

- industrial = system/facility/transport event.
- chemical = toxic/corrosive/reactive/flammable/explosive release.
- biological = pathogen/toxin; incubation/transmission.
- radiological = radioactive source/exposure/contamination without necessary chain event.
- nuclear = nuclear facility/fuel-cycle/criticality context with radiological consequence.
- CBRN = umbrella, not one detector/PPE/decon/treatment.

### Failure grammar

- inventory + process energy → barriers → degradation/deviation → loss of control.
- condition: corrosion, unsafe layout, excess inventory, weak maintenance.
- trigger: component/power failure, human action, fire or natural hazard.
- NaTech examples in local source: Kandla 2001; Odisha cyclone 1999.

### On-site versus off-site

- on-site: occupier/operator, source control and workers/site.
- off-site: designated district authority, public warning/protection/services.
- MSIHC Rule 13 versus Rule 14.
- joint information, mutual aid and drills bridge the boundary.

### Chemical law

- EPA 1986 s.8: prescribed procedure/safeguards for hazardous substances.
- MSIHC Rules 1989: prevention, safety information/reporting and emergency planning.
- Chemical Accidents Rules 1996: Central/State/District/Local Crisis Groups.
- Explosives Act 1884, Petroleum Act 1934 and Insecticides Act 1968: hazard/class-specific lineage.
- Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules 2016, as amended: current waste-management frame; the source's 2008 title is historical.
- Factories Act hazardous-process duties = historical foundation.
- OSHWC Code 2020 commenced 21 Nov 2025; verify rules/savings for current legal detail.

### Institutions

- occupier: prevention, on-site plan and source control.
- district authority: off-site plan/public protection.
- MoEFCC/crisis groups: chemical governance.
- PESO: explosives/petroleum and assigned hazardous-chemical functions.
- CPCB/SPCB/PCC: pollution control/environmental monitoring.
- NDRF/State services: consequence response, not regulation.
- AERB: nuclear/radiation regulator; DAE CMG: technical public-domain response support.

### CBRN response

- chemical: plume/contact/toxicity.
- biological: surveillance/transmission/infection control.
- radiological: dose and contamination monitoring.
- nuclear: plant/site/off-site classification and containment.
- zones → PPE → source control → protect public → decon → triage/treat.
- exposure ≠ contamination; water is not universal decontaminant.

### Liability and recovery

- PLI Act s.3 = no-fault statutory relief; s.4 insurance; s.7A ERF.
- no fault ≠ criminal guilt; immediate relief ≠ full compensation/remediation.
- NGT Act relief/restitution is distinct.
- CLNDA channels/caps operator liability; cap ≠ adequacy.
- recovery includes health surveillance, contamination remediation and livelihoods.

### Oil and transport

- risk follows material through transport and waste.
- Indian Coast Guard coordinates NOSDCP for marine oil spills.
- containment, dispersant, burning and bioremediation have context-specific trade-offs.
- CPCL/Ennore December 2023 is a source-dated Indian example.

### Qualified conclusion

Industrial and CBRN resilience rests on prevention-first lifecycle controls, exact mandates, practised on/off-site coordination, agent-specific protection and long-term liability/remediation—not on a long statute list or equipment inventory.
