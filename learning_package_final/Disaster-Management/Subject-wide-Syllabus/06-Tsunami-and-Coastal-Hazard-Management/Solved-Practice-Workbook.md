## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Q1. Which definition best distinguishes a tsunami from other coastal water-level changes?

A. A series of long waves produced by sudden displacement of a large water volume.
B. A periodic sea-level movement produced by lunar and solar gravitational forces.
C. A coastal water rise produced chiefly by cyclone winds and low pressure.
D. A seasonal sea-level change associated with regional ocean circulation.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Sudden displacement and a wave train define the phenomenon.
- **B — Incorrect.** This describes an astronomical tide.
- **C — Incorrect.** This describes storm surge.
- **D — Incorrect.** This describes seasonal variation, not tsunami generation.

**Examiner trap:** Classify the phenomenon by its driver, not by the shared result of coastal flooding.

### Q2. Which source condition most directly makes an undersea earthquake tsunamigenic?

A. Long felt shaking in a distant inland settlement.
B. Rapid vertical displacement of seabed and overlying water.
C. A prolonged aftershock sequence beneath continental crust.
D. A high astronomical tide at the nearest harbour.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Felt duration inland does not establish water displacement.
- **B — Correct.** Vertical seabed movement can rapidly displace the water column.
- **C — Incorrect.** Aftershocks do not by themselves establish tsunami generation.
- **D — Incorrect.** Tide can alter the coastal baseline but not the earthquake mechanism.

**Examiner trap:** Earthquake magnitude or felt shaking alone does not prove tsunami generation.

### Q3. Consider the following possible tsunami triggers:

1. Submarine landslide
2. Volcanic collapse
3. Meteorite impact

Which are recognised trigger types?

A. 1 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1, 2 and 3
D. 1 and 3 only

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Volcanic collapse and impact can also displace water rapidly.
- **B — Incorrect.** Submarine landslides are also recognised triggers.
- **C — Correct.** All three may generate a tsunami if displacement is sufficient and rapid.
- **D — Incorrect.** Volcanic collapse is wrongly omitted.

**Examiner trap:** Do not reduce tsunami causation to earthquakes alone.

### Q4. Which set correctly matches the phenomenon with its primary driver?

A. Tsunami—gravity; tide—seabed rupture; storm surge—river discharge.
B. Tsunami—wind stress; tide—air pressure; storm surge—lunar gravity.
C. Tsunami—river sediment; tide—bathymetry; storm surge—earthquake.
D. Tsunami—water displacement; tide—astronomical gravity; storm surge—wind and pressure.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** The three drivers are mismatched.
- **B — Incorrect.** This exchanges the drivers of all three phenomena.
- **C — Incorrect.** Sediment and bathymetry affect risk but are not the primary drivers.
- **D — Correct.** It preserves the geological, astronomical and meteorological distinction.

**Examiner trap:** Similar inundation does not make tsunami, tide and storm surge interchangeable.

### Q5. How can a destructive tsunami pass beneath a ship in deep ocean without appearing as a high breaking wave?

A. Its long wavelength carries energy with comparatively small surface amplitude offshore.
B. Its energy vanishes offshore and is recreated by coastal winds near land.
C. Its motion remains confined to the seabed without involving the water column.
D. Its surface height is the same at the source and receiving coastline.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Long wavelength and low offshore amplitude can conceal substantial energy.
- **B — Incorrect.** Coastal winds do not recreate tsunami energy.
- **C — Incorrect.** The water column participates in wave motion.
- **D — Incorrect.** Depth and coastal form transform the wave.

**Examiner trap:** Low visible amplitude offshore is not evidence of low coastal risk.

### Q6. What is the central physical effect of shoaling on a tsunami approaching shallow water?

A. Speed rises while wavelength and height both increase.
B. Speed falls while wavelength contracts and height or current can increase.
C. Period contracts to that of ordinary short wind waves.
D. Energy becomes independent of seabed depth and coastal form.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** Speed generally falls in shallower water.
- **B — Correct.** This is the depth-driven transformation toward coastal impact.
- **C — Incorrect.** A tsunami retains a long period relative to wind waves.
- **D — Incorrect.** Bathymetry and morphology remain important.

**Examiner trap:** Shoaling transforms an existing wave; it is not a new source.

### Q7. Which distinction between run-up and inundation is correct?

A. Run-up is offshore speed; inundation is earthquake magnitude.
B. Run-up is warning lead time; inundation is bulletin duration.
C. Run-up is vertical reach; inundation is horizontal inland flooding.
D. Run-up is tidal range; inundation is storm-surge pressure.

**Answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect.** Neither term measures source magnitude or offshore speed.
- **B — Incorrect.** They are impact measures, not warning-time measures.
- **C — Correct.** It separates vertical reach from horizontal penetration.
- **D — Incorrect.** The terms are not tide or cyclone-pressure measures.

**Examiner trap:** Wave height, run-up and inundation distance are different measures.

### Q8. Two bays receive waves from the same tsunami source but record different impacts. Which explanation is strongest?

A. Source magnitude determines the same impact at each coast.
B. Population density changes wave generation at the source.
C. A common bulletin removes local differences in water motion.
D. Bathymetry, bay shape, elevation and exposure transform the offshore hazard differently.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Source magnitude cannot determine identical local impact.
- **B — Incorrect.** Population affects exposure, not source generation.
- **C — Incorrect.** A bulletin communicates risk; it does not homogenise coastal physics.
- **D — Correct.** It links morphology and exposure to site-specific consequence.

**Examiner trap:** A regional warning does not imply uniform local inundation.

### Q9. Which coastal setting generally requires the closest scrutiny in tsunami inundation planning?

A. A low-lying, densely occupied coast connected to a funnel-shaped bay.
B. A high inland plateau separated from the sea by steep relief.
C. A sparsely used ridge outside mapped coastal flow paths.
D. A deep-ocean shipping lane distant from shoreline assets.

**Answer: A**

- **A — Correct.** Low elevation, concentrated exposure and possible bay amplification combine.
- **B — Incorrect.** Elevation and separation reduce direct inundation exposure.
- **C — Incorrect.** This setting has limited coastal-flow exposure.
- **D — Incorrect.** Offshore navigation risk differs from coastal inundation planning.

**Examiner trap:** Assess morphology, elevation and exposure together.

### Q10. What lesson follows from the 2004 observation that damage was heavier where sand dunes had been mined?

A. Dune mining changes scenery without affecting disaster risk.
B. Removing a natural barrier can increase coastal vulnerability and loss.
C. Engineered protection becomes unnecessary wherever a dune remains.
D. Dune protection is independent of form, continuity and event characteristics.

**Answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect.** The observation links degradation to greater damage.
- **B — Correct.** This is the evidence-supported vulnerability lesson.
- **C — Incorrect.** Natural and engineered measures remain complementary.
- **D — Incorrect.** Performance varies with dune form, continuity, location and event.

**Examiner trap:** Use the case as evidence for buffer protection, not proof of universal protection.

### Q11. Consider the following statements about natural coastal buffers:

Which statements are correct?

B. 2 and 3 only

- **D — Incorrect.** Evacuation remains necessary where exposure persists.

**Examiner trap:** A buffer reduces risk; it does not create a no-evacuation zone.

### Q12. Which mitigation principle best fits mangroves, dunes, reefs and engineered barriers?

A. Select one measure from average regional cost without local hazard mapping.
B. Give natural measures priority without assessing the local design hazard.
C. Treat coastal regulation as sufficient without physical protection.

**Answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect.** Coastal morphology and exposure differ.
- **B — Incorrect.** Natural measures are valuable but not uniformly superior.
- **C — Incorrect.** Regulation is one layer, not the complete system.
- **D — Correct.** This combines siting, engineering, ecosystems and residual-risk planning.

**Examiner trap:** Reject both concrete-only and ecosystem-only answers.

### Q13. Which combination gives the core observational chain used by ITEWC?

- **C — Incorrect.** This mixes tide, cyclone and drought tools.
- **D — Incorrect.** This set serves other hazards.

**Examiner trap:** Seismic detection initiates assessment; sea-level instruments test actual wave generation.

### Q15. Arrange the tsunami information chain in the most defensible order:

1. Sea-level observations update confidence.
2. Earthquake parameters are estimated.
3. Local authority takes protective action.
4. Model-based threat is assessed.

A. 1-2-4-3
B. 2-1-3-4
C. 2-4-1-3

**Examiner trap:** Keep scientific stages in order without erasing local decision authority.

- **C — Incorrect.** The stated failure is last-mile access.
- **D — Correct.** This applies the end-to-end inclusive-resilience test.

**Examiner trap:** A warning succeeds only when exposed people can understand and act.

## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### PYQ ownership and evidence note

No audited GS-III question is directly owned by this topic. Two GS-I questions are shared because Geography owns the physical/resource component while Disaster Management owns preparedness. The 2024 resilience question and 2026 mangrove question are bounded applications. Descriptive solutions are instructional, not official UPSC model answers.

### PYQ 1 — 2025 GS-I Q7 — SHARED

**Question — exact English wording with line-break normalisation:** “What are Tsunamis? How and where are they formed? What are their consequences? Explain with examples.” **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

A tsunami is a series of long waves generated by sudden displacement of a large water volume. Most originate where a shallow undersea earthquake produces vertical seabed movement, especially at subduction zones; submarine landslides, volcanic collapse and impacts can also generate them.

In deep water, the long wave travels rapidly with low visible amplitude. As depth decreases, it slows, its wavelength contracts and its height and current increase through shoaling. Coastal bathymetry and morphology then shape run-up, inundation and destructive return flow, so equal offshore energy can produce unequal local losses.

The 26 December 2004 Sumatra-Andaman event affected island and mainland coasts, disrupting lives, housing, ports, fisheries and services. Mangroves, forests, dunes and cliffs reduced damage at some sites, while mined dunes were associated with heavier damage.

Thus consequences depend on source, coast, exposure and capacity—not earthquake magnitude alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers definition, formation, location, mechanism, consequences and example while preserving the generation-versus-impact qualification.

### PYQ 2 — 2023 GS-I Q14 — SHARED

**Question — exact English wording reconstructed from official local OCR by joining one-word line breaks:** “Comment on the resource potentials of the long coastline of India and highlight the status of natural hazard preparedness in these areas.” **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

India’s long coastline supports fisheries, aquaculture, ports, shipping, tourism, offshore energy and coastal ecosystems. The same concentration of people, infrastructure and livelihoods creates exposure to cyclones, storm surge, tsunami, erosion and saline flooding. Resource development is sustainable only when preparedness is built into coastal planning.

For tsunami risk, ITEWC at INCOIS combines seismic data, numerical scenarios, Bottom Pressure Recorders and tide gauges. Its technical advisory must be converted by State, district and local authorities into closures, evacuation and an authorised all-clear. TUNAMI-N2 scenarios, bathymetry and coastal topography support inundation maps, routes and safer siting.

CRZ Notification 2019 provides category-specific coastal regulation; it should work with local hazard maps rather than be treated as one universal setback. Engineered shelters, selective retrofits, sea walls and breakwaters require location-specific design and maintenance. Mangroves, coastal forests, dunes, reefs and bio-shields add friction and ecological co-benefits but cannot eliminate extreme risk. The 2004 tsunami’s heavier damage where dunes were mined shows the cost of degrading buffers.

Preparedness remains incomplete where tourists lack route knowledge, fishers lack reliable communication, evacuation is inaccessible, or ports lack shutdown and recovery plans. Renewable community assessment, drills and livelihood-sensitive Build Back Better are therefore the bridge between coastal potential and resilience.

**Why this earns marks:** It links resource potential and preparedness, names institutions and tools, and qualifies structural and ecosystem measures.

### PYQ 3 — 2024 GS-III Q17 — APPLICATION

**Question — local official-paper OCR reproduced verbatim except joined line breaks:** “What is disaster resilience? How is it determined? Describe various elements of a resilience framework. Also mention the global targests of Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015-2030)” **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**OCR note:** `targests` is retained because it appears in the local official-paper extraction; it means “targets.”

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Disaster resilience is the ability of a community or system to resist, absorb, adapt to, respond to and recover from hazard impacts while maintaining or restoring essential functions. It is determined through hazard, exposure, vulnerability and usable capacity, tested by actual outcomes rather than plans alone.

A resilience framework includes risk knowledge; risk-sensitive land use and standards; robust and redundant infrastructure; multi-hazard warning; understandable last-mile communication; inclusive evacuation; trained institutions and communities; finance and social protection; continuity of critical services; and recovery that Builds Back Better. Tsunami preparedness illustrates the chain: ITEWC detection and modelling, BPR/tide-gauge confirmation, local evacuation authority, CRZ-linked siting, mapped refuges, layered natural and engineered protection, and repeated community drills.

Sendai’s seven global targets are to reduce: **A** mortality, **B** affected people, **C** direct economic loss relative to global GDP, and **D** critical-infrastructure damage and basic-service disruption; and to increase: **E** national and local DRR strategies, **F** international cooperation for developing countries, and **G** access to multi-hazard early warning and risk information.

Sendai is voluntary and non-binding. Resilience is proved by safer action, continuity and recovery—not by a sensor, plan or recognition alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers all four clauses, names every target and uses tsunami management as a bounded application.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish tsunami generation from astronomical tides and cyclone-driven storm surge. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

A tsunami, tide and storm surge may each change coastal water level, but their drivers and warning systems differ. A **tsunami** begins with sudden displacement of a large water volume, commonly vertical seabed movement during an undersea earthquake, and sometimes a landslide, volcanic collapse or impact. An earthquake becomes tsunamigenic only when its mechanism, depth, rupture and displacement move sufficient water.

An **astronomical tide** is the regular gravitational response of oceans to the moon and sun. A **storm surge** is an abnormal coastal rise produced chiefly by cyclone winds and low pressure, shaped by track, intensity, bathymetry and tide.

The distinction is operational. Tsunamis require seismic analysis, numerical scenarios, BPRs and tide gauges; tides use harmonic prediction; cyclone surge uses weather-ocean forecasts. Conflation can delay the correct protective action.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes cause, conditions and monitoring rather than merely listing definitions.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the tsunami detection, confirmation, advisory, evacuation and all-clear chain in India. **Answer in 150 words.**

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

India’s chain begins when seismic networks locate and characterise an earthquake. ITEWC at INCOIS compares the event with numerical scenarios and issues preliminary information. Bottom Pressure Recorders and coastal tide gauges then add observed sea-level evidence; updates may raise, lower or withdraw the assessed threat.

The current INCOIS SOP distinguishes Type I preliminary earthquake information, Type II model-based threat and Type III information incorporating observed water levels. Short-lead coasts may be warned before confirmation because waiting can consume evacuation time.

ITEWC supplies technical bulletins; State, district and local authorities convert them into closures, evacuation, refuge management and public instructions. Communities must receive, understand and be able to act through accessible routes and assistance. Because local wave behaviour varies and later waves or return flow may remain dangerous, competent local authorities—not the warning centre alone—determine the all-clear.

**Why this earns marks:** It preserves evidence stages and the advisory-versus-protective-authority boundary.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse how bathymetry and coastal morphology shape tsunami forecasting, inundation and land-use decisions. **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Tsunami impact is not fixed by earthquake magnitude. It is transformed between source and settlement by water depth and coastal form.

In deep water, long waves travel rapidly with comparatively low surface amplitude. As depth decreases, speed falls, wavelength contracts and height and current can increase. Bathymetric gradients refract and focus energy, while continental-shelf width, submarine channels, bays and estuaries influence arrival and amplification. On land, elevation, slope, dunes, vegetation, channels and built form affect run-up, inundation and return flow.

Forecasting therefore needs more than seismic detection. ITEWC combines event parameters with pre-run scenarios and observed water levels. TUNAMI-N2 modelling requires reliable bathymetry and coastal topography; weak near-shore data enlarge uncertainty. The Indian Naval Hydrographic Department’s bathymetric role and post-2004 re-charting illustrate why seabed knowledge is operational infrastructure.

Maps should guide evacuation zones and routes, designated refuges, critical-facility siting, port shutdown plans and CRZ-linked development control. They should also identify where low-lying settlements, tourists or mobility-constrained residents need assistance.

A modelled boundary is a scenario, not a guaranteed maximum. Authorities should use uncertainty buffers, update terrain and exposure data, test routes through drills and revise maps after observed events.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces mechanism to model, planning use and uncertainty.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine evacuation and natural-buffer strategies for tsunami-prone Indian coasts, including their limitations. **Answer in 250 words.**

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Evacuation and natural buffers reduce different parts of tsunami risk and must be combined.

Near-source communities may need immediate self-evacuation after strong or prolonged shaking or abnormal sea behaviour. Horizontal evacuation is preferred where signed, unobstructed routes can reach safe inland or elevated ground within the available time. Where it cannot, vertical evacuation requires an engineered, designated refuge assessed for earthquake shaking, hydrodynamic loads, debris, scour, capacity and accessibility. Plans must include tourists, fishers, children, older persons, persons with disabilities, patients and migrants; drills should test receipt, comprehension, travel time, accountability and the local all-clear.

Mangroves, coastal forests, dunes, reefs and bio-shields can add friction, trap debris, stabilise land and sustain livelihoods. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami’s heavier damage where dunes were mined is named evidence that degradation raises vulnerability. Yet performance varies with width, health, elevation, continuity, coastal geometry and event scale. Buffers cannot replace evacuation, safe siting or critical-facility protection.

Accordingly, inundation maps and CRZ-linked land use should protect natural features while pairing them with shelters, selective retrofits, breakwaters or other justified engineering. Maintenance and community stewardship matter as much as plantation announcements.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both strategies, names design and inclusion conditions, and rejects a false ecology-engineering choice.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically evaluate India’s ITEWC-centred tsunami early-warning architecture from source detection to last-mile action. **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

India’s tsunami-warning architecture is a major post-2004 capability, but its performance must be judged end to end.

**Scientific chain:** ITEWC at INCOIS operates round the clock. National and international seismic data identify earthquake location, depth and magnitude; numerical scenarios estimate threat; Bottom Pressure Recorders and tide gauges add observed sea-level evidence. INCOIS states that tsunamigenic earthquakes can be detected within ten minutes. Its SOP uses preliminary, model-based and observed-data bulletins and permits precautionary warning for short-lead coasts before confirmation.

**Institutional chain:** ITEWC is an approved UNESCO-IOC service provider under IOTWMS. It issues technical advisories; State, district and local authorities decide evacuation, closures, shelter operation and the local all-clear. This separation is sound because scientific assessment is regional while protective action is place-specific.

**Strengths:** multi-sensor redundancy, scenario modelling, round-the-clock operation, regional cooperation and exercises reduce delay. TUNAMI-N2, bathymetry and topography connect warning to inundation planning.

**Limits:** earthquake detection is not proof of wave generation; near-shore data constrain local precision; sensors and links can fail; changing assessments can erode trust if uncertainty is poorly explained. A transmitted message does not prove receipt, comprehension, accessible evacuation or safe refuge. Rare-event memory, staffing, signage and drills also decay.

India should publish end-to-end exercise results, maintain redundant sensors, update maps, integrate ports and islands, audit accessible routes and renew community preparedness. ITEWC is the technical backbone; resilience depends on whether local institutions and people can act before arrival.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates capability, authority, uncertainty, inclusion and outcomes instead of equating a fast bulletin with safety.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Design an integrated coastal-resilience framework for India’s low-frequency, high-consequence tsunami risk. **Answer in 300 words.**

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

Tsunami resilience should be organised as a maintained chain rather than a one-time post-disaster project.

1. **Understand risk:** map earthquake, landslide and volcanic sources; update bathymetry, topography, inundation scenarios, exposure and assisted-evacuation needs. Preserve uncertainty instead of publishing a false exact safety line.
2. **Govern development:** combine CRZ 2019 categories with local hazard maps, building control, port and hazardous-cargo planning, and protection of dunes, mangroves and reefs. Regulation should prevent new exposure while respecting lawful coastal livelihoods.
3. **Warn end to end:** sustain ITEWC’s seismic, modelling, BPR and tide-gauge chain; maintain redundant communications; translate bulletins into multilingual, accessible State and local instructions.
4. **Evacuate:** use signed horizontal routes where safe ground is reachable and engineered, designated vertical refuges where it is not. Drill residents, fishers, tourists, schools, hospitals and mobility-constrained groups; let local authorities issue the all-clear.
5. **Layer protection:** combine selective shelters, retrofits, sea walls or breakwaters with healthy dunes, forests, mangroves, reefs and bio-shields. Each measure should be justified against site conditions and residual risk.
6. **Protect continuity and recover:** plan port shutdown and reopening, emergency water, health and communications, and restoration of boats, landing centres, markets, housing and ecosystems through Build Back Better.
7. **Prevent readiness decay:** use renewable Tsunami Ready assessment, exercises, route-time tests, sensor maintenance and public outcome dashboards.

The 2004 event supplies institutional and natural-buffer lessons; ITEWC supplies technical capacity. The decisive test is sustained, inclusive local action—not a centre, barrier or badge viewed alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates the disaster cycle, institutions, technology, land use, ecology, equity, livelihoods and accountability.
