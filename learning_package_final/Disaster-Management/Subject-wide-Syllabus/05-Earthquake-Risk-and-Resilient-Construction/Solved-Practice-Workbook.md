# Earthquake Risk and Resilient Construction - Solved Practice Workbook

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Q1. A city in Zone III has dense unsafe masonry. Which conclusion is strongest?

A. Risk may be high because vulnerability and exposure matter alongside zone
B. Zone III guarantees low loss
C. Only Zone V requires codes
D. The next earthquake can now be predicted

**Answer: A.**

**Explanation (option-by-option):**

- **A.** Correct: zone is one risk component.
- **B.** No zone guarantees an outcome.
- **C.** Codes apply beyond Zone V.
- **D.** Zoning is not prediction.

**Trap:** Do not turn a regional hazard class into a building-safety certificate.

### Q2. A single event damages one town more than another despite the same magnitude. Why?

A. The event had two magnitudes
B. Intensity differed because of site and building conditions
C. MSK measures plate speed
D. NCS changed the energy release

**Answer: B.**

**Explanation (option-by-option):**

- **A.** One event has one source magnitude estimate.
- **B.** Correct: intensity varies spatially.
- **C.** MSK is an intensity scale.
- **D.** Reporting does not alter the event.

**Trap:** Magnitude is not a direct damage score.

## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### DIRECT PYQ - 2021 GS-III Q8 - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Discuss about the vulnerability of India to earthquake related hazards. Give examples including the salient features of major disasters caused by earthquakes in different parts of India during the last three decades.

**Alternative workbook model:** India's vulnerability combines wide seismic exposure with unsafe buildings and uneven response capacity. The Himalayan convergence zone, North-East, Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Kachchh face high hazard; about 59 per cent of land lies in Zones III-V. Yet regional zone alone does not determine loss.

Unreinforced masonry, soft-storey buildings, poor connections and vulnerable hospitals and utilities convert shaking into disaster. Latur (1993) showed damaging peninsular risk and rural masonry weakness; Bhuj (2001) exposed severe housing and urban vulnerability; Kashmir (2005) combined weak buildings with mountainous access constraints; Sikkim (2011) affected connectivity and slope stability.

Since precise prediction is unavailable, India must enforce safe new construction, audit and retrofit priority structures, train professionals, use microzonation and land-use control, strengthen lifelines and prepare EOC/IRS response. Earthquake resilience is therefore primarily a compliance and vulnerability-reduction challenge.

### 2026 bounded application

For GS-III Q7, use earthquake evidence only to support risk-sensitive siting, resilient infrastructure and lifecycle enforcement in ecologically sensitive areas. Primary ownership remains with landslide/GLOF and critical-infrastructure risk.


### ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE - WORKBOOK MODELS

### Original Mains 1 - 10 marks | 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish earthquake magnitude from intensity and explain the planning value of the distinction.

**Model answer:** Magnitude estimates energy released at the source and is commonly expressed as moment magnitude for large events. It is broadly one event-level value. Intensity describes observed effects at a location and may be expressed through MMI or MSK scales. It varies with distance, depth, soil amplification, topography and building stock. The distinction matters because magnitude alone cannot predict damage. A moderate event may produce severe local effects on soft soil or weak masonry, while better construction may limit loss under stronger shaking. Intensity observations support rapid damage mapping and response prioritisation; microzonation and site investigation support pre-disaster planning. Thus source magnitude describes the earthquake, whereas intensity connects shaking to local exposure and vulnerability.

### Original Mains 2 - 10 marks | 150 words

**Question:** Why is code adoption insufficient to establish earthquake-safe construction?

**Model answer:** A code is only the first link in a compliance chain. Safety also requires correct site data, competent structural design, ductile detailing, approved materials, trained masons, faithful workmanship, inspection, as-built verification and maintenance. Approved drawings can be defeated by altered reinforcement or poor connections. VisionIAS therefore lists weak bye-law enforcement, limited professional training and absent licensing systems as connected vulnerabilities. The National Building Code and BIS standards remain essential, but adoption does not prove local enforcement or the quality of an individual building. Governments should combine professional licensing, transparent permits, risk-based inspections, mason training and accountable completion certification. Earthquake safety is achieved through verified implementation, not the existence of a technical document.

### Original Mains 3 - 15 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Explain NDMA's six-pillar framework for seismic safety and its implementation challenges.

**Model answer:** NDMA's framework covers the full seismic-risk chain. First, earthquake-resistant new construction prevents further accumulation of unsafe stock. Second, selective strengthening and retrofitting addresses existing priority buildings and lifelines. Third, regulation and enforcement translate technical standards into practice. Fourth, awareness and preparedness shape household and institutional action. Fifth, capacity development improves engineers, architects, masons and administrators. Sixth, emergency response organises rescue, medical aid and coordination after impact. Implementation is uneven because building bye-laws are weakly enforced, professional licensing and training are incomplete, construction quality varies and retrofitting requires large finance and temporary relocation. States must identify priority structures, but identification does not prove completed work. Hospitals and EOCs also need power, water, equipment and staff continuity, not only frame safety. The framework succeeds only when all six pillars are treated as one system and progress is measured through verified construction, completed retrofits, drill learning and functional response.

### Original Mains 4 - 15 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Discuss the role and limits of seismic microzonation in urban risk reduction.

**Model answer:** Seismic microzonation refines a broad regional zone by mapping local soil, basin, slope, groundwater, liquefaction and amplification conditions together with exposure and critical facilities. It helps cities set site-sensitive design requirements, guide land use, locate hospitals and EOCs, preserve access routes and prioritise vulnerable neighbourhoods. It is especially useful where adjacent localities may experience different shaking despite being in the same national zone. Its limits are equally important. Microzonation does not predict the time or magnitude of an earthquake, certify individual buildings or automatically alter development. Maps can become outdated as urbanisation, groundwater and infrastructure change. Risk falls only when planning regulations, building design, permits and retrofitting actually use the findings. Data quality, public access and enforcement capacity therefore matter as much as the map. Microzonation is best understood as a local decision input within a wider compliance system, not as a stand-alone safety outcome.

### Original Mains 5 - 20 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine India's challenge of retrofitting existing buildings and lifeline infrastructure.

**Model answer:** Existing stock is the hardest seismic challenge because many buildings predate current standards, engineering records are incomplete and strengthening is costly. A sound programme begins with inventory and rapid screening, followed by detailed audits, priority ranking, retrofit design, finance, execution and quality verification. States are responsible for identifying priority structures, while local bodies support implementation. Hospitals, schools, bridges, dams, EOCs and utility systems merit priority because their failure produces cascading loss. Retrofitting must address both structural performance and functional continuity: a hospital frame that survives but loses power, water or equipment is not resilient. Major constraints include funding, relocation during works, shortage of skilled professionals, procurement quality, incomplete records and political preference for visible new projects. Priority lists or sanctions should not be reported as completed mitigation. India needs phased risk-based programmes, transparent audits, standard retrofit guidance, dedicated finance, third-party verification and continuity testing. New-build enforcement must continue simultaneously, or the vulnerable stock will keep growing.

### Original Mains 6 - 20 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Assess the proposition that earthquake resilience in India is fundamentally a governance and compliance problem.

**Model answer:** The proposition is persuasive because earthquake occurrence cannot be precisely prevented or predicted. Loss reduction therefore depends on governing exposure and vulnerability. India already has seismic zoning, BIS standards, the National Building Code, NDMA's six pillars, BMTPC technical resources and NCS monitoring. The central deficit is conversion into safe buildings and functioning lifelines. Weak bye-law enforcement, inadequately trained professionals, poor workmanship and incomplete retrofitting break the compliance chain. Governance also shapes land use: dense development on unstable slopes or liquefaction-prone ground can defeat building-level measures. State capacity varies because States identify priority structures and local bodies enforce many controls. Yet governance is not the only issue; scientific mapping, materials, finance and public behaviour remain necessary inputs. The correct verdict is that technology defines possible safety, while governance determines whether it reaches the built environment. India needs transparent permits, trained professionals, verified construction, lifeline audits, retrofit finance and outcome-based monitoring.
