## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. Earthquake risk is built where an uncontrollable hazard meets constructed vulnerability

```text
PLATE MOTION
        ↓
STRESS ACCUMULATES ON A FAULT
        ↓
RUPTURE RELEASES ENERGY
        ↓
SEISMIC WAVES + GROUND SHAKING
        ↓
local soil / slope / building / lifeline conditions
        ↓
damage, collapse, fire, access failure and service disruption
```

✅ Earthquakes arise from sudden release of accumulated strain along faults, commonly associated with divergent, convergent and transform plate boundaries.

✅ The canonical source states that earthquake occurrence cannot be prevented or predicted precisely in magnitude, place and time. The policy consequence is decisive: risk reduction must concentrate on exposure, vulnerability, preparedness and response.

⚠️ The earthquake is the hazard; unsafe siting, weak construction, poor maintenance and fragile services convert shaking into disaster.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Because seismic rupture cannot be prevented or precisely predicted, earthquake disaster management is primarily the governance of buildings, land use, lifelines and preparedness.

**Must-write keywords:** fault rupture · seismic waves · exposure · constructed vulnerability · non-predictability · mitigation · preparedness.

**How to write the paragraph**

- **Claim:** Similar shaking can produce very different losses.
- **Named evidence:** A code-compliant, maintained building and a non-engineered structure may occupy the same hazard zone.
- **Analysis:** Vulnerability and functional continuity, not hazard alone, determine disaster severity.
- **Qualification:** A zone or code cannot establish the safety of an individual building without site and compliance evidence.

### 2. Magnitude, intensity and seismic waves

| Concept | Meaning | Key distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnitude** | energy released at the source; large events are commonly reported using moment magnitude, Mw | one event has one preferred magnitude estimate, subject to refinement |
| **Intensity** | observed shaking and effects at a place; expressed through scales such as MMI or MSK | varies from place to place for the same event |
| **P-wave** | compressional body wave; particle motion is parallel to propagation | travels faster and is recorded before S-waves |
| **S-wave** | shear body wave; particle motion is perpendicular to propagation | slower and does not pass through liquids |

✅ The Richter scale is an older local magnitude scale and should not be treated as the universal current measure for all large earthquakes.

```text
SOURCE ENERGY ──► MAGNITUDE

SOURCE + DISTANCE + PATH + SITE + BUILDING
                         └──────────────► INTENSITY / DAMAGE
```

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Magnitude describes the event; intensity describes place-specific effects, which is why one earthquake produces an uneven damage map.

**Must-write keywords:** Mw · MMI/MSK · P-wave · S-wave · source · distance · site condition · observed effect.

### 3. India’s seismic setting and current zoning

✅ The Indian plate converges with and thrusts beneath the Eurasian plate at roughly **5 cm per year** in the canonical source, accumulating stress across the Alpine-Himalayan belt.

✅ The canonical source describes **fourteen States** across the western and central Himalaya, North-East and parts of the Indo-Gangetic basin as highly earthquake-prone. Treat the count as source-bound; use the operative BIS map for present zone classification.

India’s earthquake hazard is not confined to the Himalaya. The source also identifies:

- the Gulf of Khambhat and Rann of Kachchh;
- parts of peninsular India;
- Lakshadweep;
- Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

✅ The operative national zoning standard checked for this review remains **IS 1893 (Part 1): 2016**, with four zones: II, III, IV and V. Zone V is the highest-hazard zone.

✅ Official MoES/PIB information dated 30 July 2021 gave approximate land-area shares:

| Zone | Approximate share |
|---|---:|
| V | 11% |
| IV | 18% |
| III | 30% |
| II | remaining share |

Thus, about **59%** of India’s landmass lies in Zones III–V, described in the official source as prone to moderate-to-severe earthquakes.

Zone V includes the whole of North-East India and Andaman and Nicobar Islands, with parts of northern Bihar, Jammu and Kashmir/Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Gujarat.

📰 A MoES parliamentary reply dated **1 April 2026** states that a revised zonation was withdrawn in March 2026; IS 1893 (Part 1): 2016 therefore remained the current standard.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Seismic zoning is a regional design input, not an earthquake forecast or a certificate of safety for every structure inside a zone.

**Must-write keywords:** Alpine-Himalayan belt · Kachchh · peninsular seismicity · Zones II–V · Zone V · IS 1893:2016 · 59%.

### 4. Zoning, microzonation and site effects

```text
NATIONAL / REGIONAL ZONING
broad expected seismic hazard
              ↓
MICROZONATION
local soil + rock + slope + groundwater + fault proximity
              ↓
SITE-SPECIFIC INVESTIGATION
foundation and structural design inputs
              ↓
BUILDING-SPECIFIC SAFETY
design + materials + detailing + construction + maintenance
```

**Seismic zoning** supports broad planning and design requirements. **Microzonation** refines hazard within a city or district by examining local conditions that may amplify shaking or produce secondary effects.

Site conditions matter because soft or saturated deposits, unstable slopes and filled ground can alter shaking and ground behaviour. A zone label cannot replace a site investigation for a critical structure.

❌ A move from Zone II to Zone III does not predict an earthquake. It changes the design hazard assumptions used by regulation and engineering.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Zoning identifies broad hazard; microzonation identifies local variation; compliance determines whether a building converts that information into safety.

**Must-write keywords:** regional zone · microzonation · amplification · soil · slope · site investigation · design input.

### 5. Exposure and constructed vulnerability

Earthquake exposure includes:

- dense settlements and informal housing;
- schools, hospitals and emergency facilities;
- bridges, flyovers, ports and transport corridors;
- dams, reservoirs, power, water and communications;
- industrial facilities and hazardous contents;
- heritage and older masonry stock.

The canonical source identifies a linked vulnerability chain:

```text
weak or outdated bye-law
        ↓
untrained / unlicensed professional or mason
        ↓
unsafe design or detailing
        ↓
poor materials / workmanship
        ↓
weak inspection + unauthorised alteration + poor maintenance
        ↓
collapse / falling components / blocked access / service failure
```

This is why an adequate code can coexist with unsafe buildings.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Earthquake mortality is often a construction and compliance failure expressed through a geological event.

**Must-write keywords:** exposure concentration · non-engineered construction · code enforcement · workmanship · inspection · maintenance · lifeline.

### 6. NDMA’s six pillars of seismic safety

✅ The canonical source records six pillars:

1. **Earthquake-resistant construction of new structures.**
2. **Selective seismic strengthening and retrofitting of existing priority and lifeline structures.**
3. **Regulation and enforcement.**
4. **Awareness and preparedness.**
5. **Capacity development:** education, training, research, capacity building and documentation.
6. **Emergency response.**

```text
NEW STOCK ──► safe design and construction
EXISTING STOCK ──► audit, prioritise and retrofit
BOTH ──► regulation + trained professionals + awareness + response
```

The pillars form a system. New-build compliance cannot protect the existing vulnerable stock; retrofitting cannot compensate for continued unsafe construction; response cannot undo collapse.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** India’s six-pillar framework is strongest when read as one compliance chain rather than six independent schemes.

**Must-write keywords:** six pillars · new construction · selective retrofit · regulation · awareness · capacity · emergency response.

### 7. Resilient new construction: performance rather than “earthquake-proof”

Earthquake-resistant construction aims to protect life and preserve required functions under specified design conditions. It does not promise zero damage under every possible event.

Core design ideas:

- a clear and continuous load path;
- regular, balanced configuration that limits harmful concentration of forces;
- adequate lateral resistance and connections;
- ductile detailing so components can deform and dissipate energy without sudden brittle collapse;
- foundation and structural design matched to the site and applicable standards;
- quality materials, workmanship, inspection and maintenance;
- secured parapets, tanks, ceilings, equipment and services.

✅ The National Building Code of India 2016 provides a broad building-safety framework. NDMA’s May 2021 simplified building-safety guidance and the relevant BIS seismic standards translate technical requirements for users and professionals.

✅ NDMA’s **National Disaster Management Guidelines: Management of Earthquakes (2007)** supply the six-pillar policy framework, while its **Guidelines on Seismic Retrofitting of Deficient Buildings and Structures (June 2014)** address the existing stock.

❌ “Earthquake-proof building” is an unsafe absolute. Use “earthquake-resistant” or “designed for specified seismic performance.”

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Resilient construction is a verified performance chain from site and design to detailing, workmanship, inspection and maintenance.

**Must-write keywords:** load path · regularity · lateral resistance · ductility · detailing · non-structural anchoring · quality assurance.

### 8. Retrofitting existing buildings

**Retrofitting** is the strengthening or modification of an existing deficient structure, selected after competent assessment, to improve seismic performance.

```text
INVENTORY
   ↓
RAPID SCREENING
   ↓
DETAILED SAFETY ASSESSMENT
   ↓
PRIORITISATION
life safety + occupancy + critical service + exposure
   ↓
DESIGN + FINANCE + OCCUPANT PLAN
   ↓
EXECUTION + QUALITY CONTROL
   ↓
COMPLETION VERIFICATION + MAINTENANCE
```

✅ The canonical source places responsibility on State Governments to identify priority and lifeline structures for retrofitting through ULBs and PRIs. It names Raj Bhavans, legislatures, courts, academic institutions, reservoirs, dams and buildings of five or more storeys among the relevant categories.

✅ NDMA issued **Guidelines on Seismic Retrofitting of Deficient Buildings and Structures** in June 2014.

⚠️ Identification, audit, sanction or tender is not completed strengthening. Occupant safety, service disruption, technical quality and post-work verification must be managed.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Retrofitting is the harder policy frontier because risk is already embedded in occupied, heterogeneous and often informal building stock.

**Must-write keywords:** screening · detailed assessment · prioritisation · lifeline · retrofit design · quality control · completion.

### 9. Structural and non-structural safety

| Structural safety | Non-structural safety |
|---|---|
| frame, walls, foundation, load path, lateral resistance, ductile behaviour | parapets, ceilings, partitions, tanks, shelves, equipment, utility connections |
| aims to prevent catastrophic structural failure | aims to prevent injury, obstruction, fire and functional shutdown |
| needs engineering design and construction control | needs anchoring, bracing, placement and maintenance |

A hospital can remain standing yet become unusable if oxygen lines, power systems, water tanks, equipment or access routes fail. **Lifeline resilience** therefore requires both collapse safety and functional continuity.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Non-collapse is a life-safety threshold; resilience additionally asks whether the hospital, road, water and communication system still function.

**Must-write keywords:** structural · non-structural · anchoring · lifeline · continuity · downtime · redundancy.

### 10. Land use and development control

Risk-sensitive land use should connect:

- seismic zoning and microzonation;
- slope and ground-failure conditions;
- density and building importance;
- access for emergency services;
- open spaces and assembly areas;
- location of hazardous and critical facilities;
- development-control regulations and building bye-laws.

✅ A Home Ministry-constituted expert group recommended changes to town-and-country-planning laws, land-use and zoning regulations, development-control regulations and building bye-laws to strengthen technical rigour.

⚠️ Upgrading a hazard zone or design load can raise construction, retrofit and insurance costs. That distributional effect requires transparent transition rules and targeted support; it does not justify ignoring updated science.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A seismic map becomes mitigation only when it changes land approval, structural design, inspection and retrofit decisions.

**Must-write keywords:** risk-sensitive land use · DCR · building bye-law · development permission · emergency access · transition cost.

### 12. Preparedness and response

Preparedness includes:

- public and professional handbooks;
- homeowner guidance;
- structural-safety audit manuals;
- school, hospital and workplace drills;
- vulnerability maps and local resource inventories;
- trained NGOs and volunteer groups;
- Emergency Operations Centres and incident roles;
- post-earthquake communication and rapid damage triage.

✅ India’s Incident Response System is coordinated through local administration and the Emergency Operations Centre network, involving community, corporate and specialist teams.

✅ BhooKamp disseminates rapid earthquake-event information. It does not predict the event.

During shaking: **Drop, Cover and Hold On** where appropriate; avoid unsafe rushing. After shaking: expect aftershocks, check fire and utility hazards, avoid damaged structures and follow official information.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Because useful warning may be absent or only seconds long, earthquake preparedness must be embedded in buildings, behaviour and continuity plans before shaking begins.

**Must-write keywords:** drill · EOC · Incident Response System · aftershock · rapid assessment · public information · continuity.

### 14. Evidence ladder for seismic safety

```text
HAZARD INFORMATION
zone / microzone / site investigation
        ↓
RULE
code / bye-law / guideline / approval condition
        ↓
COMPLIANCE PROCESS
competent design / trained construction / inspection / audit
        ↓
PHYSICAL OUTPUT
completed new structure / verified retrofit / anchored contents
        ↓
PERFORMANCE
life safety / limited collapse / service continuity / recovery
```

Never jump across rungs:

- a published code ≠ adoption in a local bye-law;
- adoption ≠ design compliance;
- approved drawing ≠ construction quality;
- audit ≠ retrofit completion;
- retrofit completion ≠ zero damage;
- observatory count ≠ prediction;
- an EEW pilot ≠ national operational coverage.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Earthquake governance is auditable only when the chain from hazard map to building performance remains traceable.

**Must-write keywords:** code adoption · design review · site inspection · completion certificate · retrofit verification · performance.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Compliance is the central risk variable

⚠️ Seismic hazard cannot be controlled, but collapse risk is strongly mediated by governance. Code text, professional licensing, design review, site inspection, materials, workmanship, unauthorised alteration and maintenance form a serial chain. Failure at one link can defeat the others.

```text
CODE QUALITY
× LOCAL ADOPTION
× COMPETENT DESIGN
× TRAINED CONSTRUCTION
× INSPECTION
× MAINTENANCE
= REAL SAFETY
```

### B. Hazard maps are techno-legal instruments

A revised zone affects design loads, approvals, cost, insurance and the retrofit implications for existing stock. The withdrawal of the proposed revision in March 2026 illustrates that updating a hazard map is not purely scientific; it carries regulatory and distributional consequences.

### C. New construction versus existing stock

New-build enforcement is cheaper and administratively cleaner because safety can be incorporated before construction. Existing stock is larger, occupied, heterogeneous and costly to assess and retrofit. Priority should be risk-based:

1. life-safety risk;
2. critical post-disaster function;
3. occupancy and exposure;
4. structural deficiency;
5. feasibility and displacement cost.

### D. Lifeline resilience

Hospitals, water, power, transport and communications are interdependent. Structural survival without backup power, secured equipment, functioning access or staff continuity can still produce systemic failure. Critical-facility audits must test operations, not only frames.

### E. Equity and informality

Lower-income, rural and peri-urban households often rely on non-engineered or owner-built construction and have less access to qualified professionals, permits, credit, insurance or relocation. Enforcement without technical and financial assistance can punish residents without making them safer.

### F. Early warning versus prediction

An EEW system detects P-waves after rupture and may provide seconds before stronger shaking at more distant sites. It can trigger protective actions, but lead time varies and may be negligible near the source. Current official status is pilot-stage development; it is not deterministic prediction or nationwide protection.

### G. Finance and accountability

The canonical source identifies no verified nationwide seismic-retrofit financing programme. Disaster Mitigation Funds provide the correct ex-ante financing category, but a current claim about seismic allocation, release or completion needs its own dated evidence.

It likewise identifies no current, verified seismic-specific insurance scheme. Insurance may transfer a defined financial loss; it neither strengthens a building nor removes physical risk.

Monitor:

- buildings screened and assessed;
- priority structures selected transparently;
- retrofits sanctioned, started and completed;
- independent quality checks;
- service-continuity exercises;
- coverage of informal and public buildings;
- post-event performance.

### H. Advanced synthesis

Bhuj 2001 demonstrates destructive risk in western India; Latur 1993 and Koyna demonstrate that peninsular India is not aseismic. These examples establish spatial diversity and construction relevance. Do not attach unsourced casualty, magnitude or causal claims to them.

> **Advanced conclusion:** India’s seismic problem is less a shortage of hazard awareness than a persistent gap between known standards and verified compliance across new, existing, formal and informal construction.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Risk mechanism

- plate stress → fault rupture → seismic waves → site response → building/lifeline performance → loss.
- hazard cannot be prevented or precisely predicted.
- policy focus: prevent vulnerability and exposure; prepare for residual risk.

### Measurement

- magnitude = source energy; use Mw for large events.
- intensity = observed place-specific effects; MMI/MSK.
- P-waves: faster, parallel particle motion.
- S-waves: slower, transverse motion; do not pass through liquids.

- canonical regional frame: fourteen highly prone States across the Himalayan, North-Eastern and Indo-Gangetic belt.

2. selective retrofit of priority/lifeline structures;

Earthquake resilience cannot wait for precise prediction. It requires risk-sensitive land use, competent and enforceable construction, priority retrofitting, protected non-structural systems, rehearsed response and evidence that buildings and lifelines actually perform.
