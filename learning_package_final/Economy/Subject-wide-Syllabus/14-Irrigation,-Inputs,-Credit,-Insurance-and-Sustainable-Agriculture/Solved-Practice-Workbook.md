### MCQ 1

### VERIFIED ROUTED MAINS DEMANDS

### 2018 GS-III ROUTE — SIKKIM ORGANIC TRANSITION

**Demand:** Discuss ecological and economic benefits of Sikkim's organic-State experience.

**Model solution:** Sikkim's transition followed a 2003 Assembly resolution, phased withdrawal of chemical-input support, extension and certification before the January 2016 full-organic declaration. Potential ecological gains include reduced synthetic residues, soil-biological recovery and less water contamination; economic gains include branding, tourism and premium-market opportunity. Yet transition yields, labour, certification cost and market access matter. Sikkim's small cultivated area, hill ecology and administrative capacity limit mechanical replication. The lesson is phased, certified and market-linked transition rather than an abrupt national ban.

### 2019 GS-III ROUTE — INTEGRATED FARMING SYSTEMS

**Demand:** Explain how Integrated Farming Systems can sustain agricultural production.

**Model solution:** IFS combines crops with suitable livestock, horticulture, fisheries or agroforestry so outputs and residues circulate across enterprises. Diversified products spread weather and price risk, improve year-round cash flow and use family labour. Manure and crop residues can support soil fertility, while farm ponds or trees add resilience. However, biological cycles, skill, veterinary support, markets and working capital make integration management-intensive. It should therefore follow local agro-climate and household resources rather than a standard enterprise bundle.

### 2019 GS-III ROUTE — WATERSHED DEVELOPMENT

**Demand:** Elaborate the impact of watershed development on water-stressed agriculture.

**Model solution:** Watershed development treats a common catchment from ridge to valley through vegetation, bunding, drainage-line treatment, recharge and livelihood measures. It can reduce erosion, improve soil moisture, raise protective irrigation and stabilise rainfed production. Community institutions can coordinate shared assets and grazing or water rules. Outcomes weaken where structures are scattered, maintenance lapses, tenure is unequal or downstream effects are ignored. Impact should be measured through hydrology, crop resilience and equitable access, not the number of structures alone.

### 2020 GS-III ROUTE — JAL SHAKTI ABHIYAN

**Demand:** State the principal features of Jal Shakti Abhiyan for water conservation.

**Model solution:** Jal Shakti Abhiyan began in 2019 as a campaign in identified water-stressed districts around rainwater harvesting, renovation of traditional water bodies, reuse and recharge, watershed development and afforestation. From 2021, Catch the Rain expanded mobilisation across districts with mapping, desilting and local water-body work. Campaign visibility can coordinate departments and communities, but durable water security depends on scientific siting, aquifer budgets, maintenance and crop-water incentives. Structures and geo-tags are intermediate outputs, not proof of recharge.

### 2020 GS-III ROUTE — WATER STORAGE AND IRRIGATION

**Demand:** Suggest measures to improve water storage and irrigation under depletion.

**Model solution:** Policy should combine catchment treatment, tank restoration, reservoir and canal maintenance, field channels, drainage, aquifer recharge and demand management. Micro-irrigation and tensiometer-based scheduling can improve application, while metering, feeder reform and crop incentives address rebound and over-pumping. Water Users Associations can improve distribution if authority and finance are real. Groundwater plans need recharge-extraction budgets at aquifer scale. Storage creation must be paired with silt control, maintenance and equitable access.

### 2021 GS-III ROUTE — MICRO-IRRIGATION

**Demand:** Assess how far micro-irrigation can address India's water crisis.

**Model solution:** Drip and sprinkler systems can reduce non-beneficial loss, improve application control and enable fertigation, especially for suitable crops and terrain. They can raise water productivity and reduce labour. However, capital cost, clogging, energy, maintenance and fragmented service constrain adoption. More importantly, lower water use per hectare may induce area expansion or water-intensive cropping, so aquifer withdrawal need not fall. Micro-irrigation must be combined with water budgets, measurement, crop alignment and community groundwater governance.

### 2022 GS-III ROUTE — IFS FOR SMALL FARMERS

**Demand:** Explain the benefits of Integrated Farming Systems for small and marginal farmers.

**Model solution:** Small farms can combine crops with dairy, poultry, horticulture, fisheries or agroforestry to diversify income, recycle nutrients and use labour across seasons. Multiple outputs reduce dependence on one harvest and can improve household nutrition. Enterprise complementarities may lower purchased-input cost. Yet smallholders face working-capital, skill, animal-health, storage and market constraints. IFS succeeds when the enterprise mix matches land, water, labour and demand; simply adding activities can increase complexity and risk.

### 2024 GS-III ROUTE — IRRIGATION CHALLENGES

**Demand:** Examine challenges of India's irrigation system and government measures.

**Model solution:** India's irrigation challenge includes incomplete projects, gaps between potential created and utilised, canal maintenance, head-tail inequity, waterlogging, fragmented minor systems and groundwater over-extraction. PMKSY links AIBP, HKKP and watershed work, while PDMC under RKVY supports micro-irrigation. CADWM, tank restoration, user associations and aquifer management address different levels. Reform must measure reliable field service and water productivity, correct power and crop incentives and protect small farmers during pricing transitions.

### 2025 GS-III ROUTE — GROUNDWATER DEPLETION

**Demand:** Examine causes of groundwater depletion and government responses.

**Model solution:** Groundwater depletion reflects individually flexible pumping from a shared aquifer, cheap or unmetered power, water-intensive crop incentives, uncertain rainfall, weak recharge and limited measurement. Borewell deepening then raises energy cost and excludes poorer farmers. Atal Bhujal Yojana, aquifer mapping, watershed recharge, micro-irrigation and community water budgets are relevant responses. Yet recharge structures cannot offset unlimited extraction. Sustainable policy must align power, procurement, crop choice, measurement and local withdrawal rules.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why can micro-irrigation improve field efficiency without reducing groundwater depletion? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Micro-irrigation applies water more precisely through drip or sprinkler systems, reducing uncontrolled conveyance, evaporation and application losses at farm level. Per Drop More Crop supports this technology under RKVY from 2022-23.

Yet aquifer depletion depends on total extraction, not litres applied per hectare. Lower unit water cost can encourage farmers to expand irrigated area, grow more seasons or retain water-intensive crops. Subsidised electricity and weak pumping measurement reinforce this rebound. Some apparent field losses also return to the aquifer or downstream system, so gross application saving is not identical to basin saving.

Micro-irrigation should therefore be combined with aquifer budgets, crop incentives, reliable power measurement, water-user institutions and extension. The correct metric is sustainable consumptive use and groundwater level, not equipment installed.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish KCC, priority-sector lending and interest subvention. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Kisan Credit Card is a revolving bank-credit product for crop and eligible allied working-capital needs. Priority-sector lending is an RBI portfolio obligation: under the 2025 Directions updated 7 August 2026, applicable banks face an 18 per cent agriculture target with nested non-corporate and small-farmer sub-targets.

Modified Interest Subvention Scheme changes the price of eligible short-term KCC credit. For FY2025-26, the concessional structure retained 7 per cent interest up to Rs 3 lakh and a 3 per cent prompt-repayment incentive, reducing the effective rate to 4 per cent for eligible timely payers. Thus product access, bank target and interest support are distinct. None guarantees sanction, drawal or inclusion of undocumented tenants.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate PMKSY as an irrigation and water-management framework. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

PMKSY was designed as a convergence framework linking project completion, irrigation access, command management, watershed treatment and on-farm efficiency. ### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse India's fertiliser-subsidy architecture and nutrient imbalance. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

India uses different subsidy and pricing regimes. Urea has a government-controlled maximum retail price and a separate subsidy mechanism. Eligible phosphatic and potassic fertilisers operate under Nutrient Based Subsidy, where periodic per-kilogram rates are notified for nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and sulphur while product MRPs are decontrolled subject to oversight.

This asymmetry makes nitrogen relatively cheap and can encourage excessive urea use compared with phosphorus, potassium, sulphur, micronutrients and organic matter. The result may be lower marginal response, soil imbalance, nitrate pollution and fiscal exposure. However, a national N:P:K ratio cannot prescribe every soil or crop.

Fertiliser DBT improves transaction tracking but is not cash transferred to farmers: companies receive subsidy after authenticated retail sale through PoS, while farmers buy at subsidised prices. Soil Health Cards and integrated nutrient management can link mineral, organic and biological sources to measured deficiency. Split placement, fertigation and extension improve use efficiency.

Reform should gradually correct relative prices while protecting small farmers, strengthen soil testing and quality control, and redirect savings toward research and balanced nutrients. Nano urea should follow crop-soil recommendations; notification or sale does not prove universal replacement. The objective is nutrient productivity and soil health, not maximum fertiliser volume.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess PMFBY's design, achievements and limitations as crop-risk protection. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

PMFBY pools specified crop-production risks for notified crops, areas and seasons. Official 2026 terms retain maximum farmer premium shares of 2 per cent for Kharif, 1.5 per cent for Rabi food and oilseed crops and 5 per cent for annual commercial or horticultural crops; government subsidises the balance. Enrolment has been voluntary for farmers since Kharif 2020.

For widespread loss, the scheme primarily uses area-yield assessment based on Crop Cutting Experiments. This lowers the cost of verifying millions of individual farms and limits moral hazard. Specified prevented-sowing, localised and post-harvest losses follow separate reporting and assessment routes. YES-TECH, WINDS and CROPIC seek better yield, weather and field evidence.

The central weakness is basis risk: an individual may lose crop while the insurance-unit yield does not cross the trigger. Delayed CCEs, State premium release, notification gaps and inaccessible grievance can further weaken trust. Voluntary State and farmer participation can also narrow the pool.

Reform requires smaller credible units where feasible, independent data audits, timely premium funding, transparent claim calculation and accessible appeals. Insurance should be layered with irrigation, resilient seed, diversification, savings and catastrophe relief. PMFBY protects covered production risk; it is not price insurance or a universal income guarantee.
