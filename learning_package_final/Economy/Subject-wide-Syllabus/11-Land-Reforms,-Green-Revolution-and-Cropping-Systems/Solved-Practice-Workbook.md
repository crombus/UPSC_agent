# Land Reforms, Green Revolution and Cropping Systems — Solved Practice Workbook

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

Exactly 32 original questions appear before PYQs. Correct answers rotate A -> B -> C -> D eight times.

### MCQ 1

Which option best defines the scope of land reform?

A. It includes intermediary abolition, tenancy, ceilings, consolidation and records.
B. It refers only to redistribution of ceiling-surplus land.
C. It means replacing private farms with State farms.
D. It is identical to the introduction of high-yielding seeds.

**Answer: A.**

**Option-wise explanation:**
- **A - Correct:** The correct option states the complete institutional package. The option states: It includes intermediary abolition, tenancy, ceilings, consolidation and records.
- **B - Incorrect:** Redistribution is only one instrument. The option states: It refers only to redistribution of ceiling-surplus land.
- **C - Incorrect:** Collectivisation is not the general Indian definition. The option states: It means replacing private farms with State farms.
- **D - Incorrect:** Seed technology belongs to the Green Revolution, not land reform. The option states: It is identical to the introduction of high-yielding seeds.

**Examiner trap 1:** Do not reduce land reform to one redistributive instrument.

### 2021 GS-III ROUTE — MARGINAL AND SMALL FARMERS

**Demand:** Explain how land reforms affected the conditions of marginal and small farmers.

**Model solution:** Intermediary abolition reduced rent-receiving layers, tenancy security strengthened the cultivator's claim to output, and ceilings created a route for asset redistribution. Consolidation could lower fragmentation costs, while ownership or secure tenancy could improve access to institutional benefits. ### 2023 GS-III ROUTE — OBJECTIVES, MEASURES AND CEILINGS

**Demand:** Discuss land-reform objectives, measures and the role of land ceilings.

**Model solution:** Land reform pursued distributive justice, cultivator security and productive reorganisation. Intermediary abolition removed rent-receiving layers; tenancy laws regulated rent, eviction and ownership claims; ceilings identified surplus for redistribution; consolidation reorganised scattered parcels; and record reform made rights enforceable. Ceilings directly addressed concentration, but family definitions, exemptions, benami transfers, litigation and weak possession narrowed distributable surplus. Redistribution could still remain unproductive without viable parcels and complementary services. Therefore ceilings should be judged through declaration, verification, acquisition, allotment and mutation, and as one component of the wider reform package.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** How did colonial land-revenue systems shape the agenda of post-Independence land reform? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Colonial revenue settlements created distinct obstacles. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 placed zamindars between the State and cultivators, strengthening rent-receiving interests. Ryotwari dealt directly with recorded cultivators, but direct assessment did not guarantee affordable demand or secure title. Mahalwari made a village estate or co-sharer body responsible, tying revenue administration to local hierarchy.

Post-Independence reform therefore required several instruments. Intermediary abolition removed rent-receiving layers; tenancy laws regulated rent, eviction and ownership; ceilings addressed concentration; consolidation tackled scattered parcels; and records identified actual rights.

The causal lesson is that a revenue label did not determine one outcome. Regional variants, political power and record quality shaped each legacy. Reform had to alter both legal relationships and the administrative information through which they were enforced.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why digital land records do not automatically create conclusive title. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Digital records improve access, map consistency, mutation tracking and registration links. DILRMP 3.0 guidelines cover 2026-2031 and integrate textual records, cadastral maps and services. ULPIN identifies a georeferenced parcel, while SVAMITVA supports drone mapping and property cards in rural abadi areas.

These tools strengthen evidence, but title is a legal conclusion. Records may omit tenants, women, customary users or community rights; boundaries may be disputed; and State law determines a card's effect. Conclusive title additionally requires adjudication, notice, objections, continuous updating and rules allocating loss when the register is wrong.

Digitisation should therefore include participatory survey, accessible correction and independent dispute resolution. Technology reduces uncertainty, but treating a mapped record as indefeasible can formalise old errors.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Why did land reforms achieve deeper results in some Indian States than in others? Analyse in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

Land reform is principally implemented through State legislation, so a common national objective produced different field outcomes. Success required more than progressive statutory language.

First, political coalitions had to support cultivators against concentrated landed interests. West Bengal's Operation Barga combined sharecropper recording with mobilisation, making tenancy protection visible and enforceable. Kerala's comparatively deep tenancy and ceiling reforms similarly reflected sustained political backing. The early Big Landed Estates Abolition Act, 1950 in Jammu and Kashmir shows how timing and a distinct political setting could permit sharp restructuring.

Second, administrative information mattered. Updated records, cadastral maps and local verification identified tenants, holdings and surplus. Weak records enabled benami transfers, anticipatory partitions and exclusion of actual cultivators. Third, implementation required a chain from declaration to adjudication, possession, allotment and mutation; delay at any stage reduced the reform. Fourth, beneficiary organisation improved reporting and bargaining power. Fifth, complementary credit, irrigation, extension and market access determined whether redistributed or secured land became a viable productive asset.

Yet these examples should not be romanticised. Tenure security did not eliminate fragmentation, tiny holdings, labour underemployment or later agrarian stress. Regional success was therefore the product of aligned law, politics, records, administration and support services, not any single legal instrument.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the achievements and limitations of India's Green Revolution. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

India's Green Revolution was an institutional package: semi-dwarf cereal varieties were combined with assured irrigation, fertiliser, credit, extension, seed systems and price-procurement support. This complementarity, rather than seed alone, drove the breakthrough from the mid-1960s.

Its principal achievement was a sustained rise in wheat and later rice yields and output. Larger marketed surplus strengthened domestic food availability, reduced chronic import dependence and expanded the policy capacity for public food management. It also stimulated rural demand for pumps, fertiliser, machinery, transport and processing. ICAR, IARI, State agricultural universities, extension workers and farmers created an adaptive research-and-diffusion system.

However, the first gains were concentrated in irrigated Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and in wheat-rice systems. Rainfed regions, pulses and oilseeds benefited less initially. Unequal access to water, finance and machinery created distributional differences, while mechanisation altered labour demand unevenly.

Ecological costs accumulated through groundwater depletion, nutrient imbalance, salinity, pesticide pressure, residue burning and monoculture. Assured infrastructure and market incentives then created crop path dependence.

The balanced conclusion is not to reject productivity science but to redirect it. An evergreen-revolution strategy should combine climate-resilient varieties, balanced nutrients, water budgeting, diversified rotations, extension and viable alternative markets, preserving food security while reducing ecological and regional concentration.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Design a region-specific strategy for crop diversification in India. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

Crop diversification is a sustained change in crop or enterprise mix; it should improve risk-adjusted income and resource use rather than merely replace one crop administratively.

The starting point is agro-climatic fit. Dryland Deccan and arid regions can expand suitable millets, pulses and oilseeds; humid and hill regions may support horticulture, spices or plantation systems; irrigated north-western districts need gradual alternatives to water-intensive rice-wheat dependence. Soil, rainfall, aquifer stress and growing period should set the ecological boundary.

Farmers then require a viable risk-return proposition. Policy must compare expected net return, price volatility, labour, seed, credit, insurance and transition cost. Perishable horticulture needs grading, cold chains, processing and reliable buyers. Pulses and millets need quality seed, extension, processing and sustained consumer demand. Farmer organisations and contract safeguards can aggregate produce without transferring land.

Cropping-system design should use rotations, legumes and intercropping to improve soil and spread biological risk. Digital advisories and climate-resilient varieties can support decisions, but they cannot replace physical water management or markets.

Procurement incentives influence crop choice, although detailed MSP and food-stock design belong to Topic 12. Diversification must remain voluntary and sequenced: protect staple security, build alternative value chains, pilot locally and scale only where farm income, water use and resilience improve together.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Propose an integrated agrarian-reform architecture linking land, technology and structural transformation. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer:**

An integrated agrarian strategy must connect secure rights with productive technology and safe economic mobility.

First, States should update tenancy and leasing laws so owners can lease without losing title and cultivators receive written operational rights. Mutation drives must record women, tenants and lawful successors, while participatory mapping must protect tribal, customary and common rights. DILRMP, ULPIN and SVAMITVA can improve information, but objections, adjudication and State-law recognition must precede any claim of conclusive title.

Second, holdings need workable organisation. Consolidation, voluntary pooling, custom-hiring services and producer organisations can reduce fragmentation costs without compulsory ownership concentration. Beneficiaries of redistribution require irrigation, soil improvement, credit, extension and market access.

Third, technology policy should move from a uniform input package to location-specific sustainable productivity. Climate-resilient seed, balanced nutrients, water budgeting, rotations and diversified crop-livestock systems should address yield gaps without repeating groundwater and monoculture stress.

Fourth, structural transformation needs land and labour mobility together. Secure leasing can allow households to take non-farm work while retaining ownership, and active cultivators can assemble operational land. Skills, rural enterprises and social protection must prevent mobility from becoming distress exit.

The governing principle is coordinated federalism: States retain the central land role, national institutions support research and interoperable records, and outcomes are judged by rights, productivity, income, resilience and inclusion together.
