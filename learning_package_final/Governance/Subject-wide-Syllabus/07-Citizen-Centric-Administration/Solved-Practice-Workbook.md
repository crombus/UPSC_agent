### VERIFIED DESCRIPTIVE PYQ — 2018 GS-II — 15 marks; 250 words

**Question:** The Citizens' Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own limitations. Identify the limitations and suggest measures for greater effectiveness of the Citizens' Charter.

**Independent model answer:**

India's Citizens' Charter initiative began in 1997 as an administrative commitment. Publishing a service standard improves transparency; it does not automatically make that standard enforceable.

**Limitations and paired corrections:** A department may promise only acknowledgement, not service completion, and may omit an accountable officer. Consult users and frontline staff, specify an outcome, the necessary documents, a named officer and an intelligible clock. A charter drafted once may become obsolete; review it periodically as the service changes. English-only online publication leaves many applicants unable to use the promise; display local-language notices at physical counters and offer assisted filing. A grievance may be transferred or closed without remedy; require reasons, feedback, reopening and independent review of recurrence and appeal reversals, not merely disposal totals. Finally, most charters carry no automatic statutory penalty. State Right to Public Services laws, pioneered by Madhya Pradesh's 2010 Act, show how a *notified* service can acquire a duty-holder, timeline, appeal and consequences. Coverage and remedies must be verified under each state's Act, rather than assumed nationally.

Even a legally enforceable clock cannot create staff or usable records. Sevottam's third pillar, delivery capability, must accompany the charter and grievance mechanism. Otherwise stronger penalties may encourage officers to refuse applications or issue hasty rejections. A good charter is therefore an accessible, measurable standard connected to feasible delivery and a reviewable remedy—not simply more published documents.

### VERIFIED DESCRIPTIVE PYQ — 2024 GS-II Q8 — 10 marks; 150 words

**Question:** The Doctrine of Democratic Governance makes it necessary that the public perception of the integrity and commitment of civil servants becomes absolutely positive. Discuss.

**Independent model answer:**

Democratic legitimacy depends on officials exercising delegated power impartially and explaining decisions to those affected. A citizen who faces an unexplained refusal or a lost pension file will judge commitment by that experience, not by an advertisement. A public Citizens' Charter sets an observable service standard; under an applicable state service-guarantee law a named officer and appeal can make default reviewable. DARPG's Sevottam model rightly joins that promise to grievance redress and the capacity to deliver.

However, an “absolutely positive” perception must be earned, not manufactured. CPGRAMS can track a participating Union department's grievance, but a transferred or closed ticket need not mean relief. Reasoned denials, fair queueing, accessible offline access, citizen feedback and review of repeat complaints provide stronger evidence than publicity or disposal totals. Trust is necessary to democratic administration; critical scrutiny is equally necessary to ensure the trust reflects integrity rather than conceals failures.

### VERIFIED DESCRIPTIVE PYQ — 2024 GS-II Q16 — 15 marks; 250 words; cross-routed to Governance 08

**Question:** The Citizens' charter has been a landmark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is yet to reach its full potential. Identify the factors hindering the realisation of its promise and suggest measures to overcome them.

**Independent model answer:**

Since India's 1997 initiative, charters have made service commitments visible. Their potential remains unrealised where public promises, remedies and actual capacity fail to meet.

**Hindrances:** Department-authored charters may measure receipt rather than completion, omit an officer or go unrevised. Many users cannot see or understand standards at the service point. A charter alone is generally administrative: it does not guarantee a statutory penalty for delay. CPGRAMS can route and track Union grievances, but a closed ticket may conceal non-resolution. State service-guarantee laws are more enforceable, yet apply to particular *notified* services, while inadequate staff or fragmented back-end records still defeat the promise. A one-stop counter does not automatically integrate departmental decisions.

**Measures:** Consult applicants and frontline workers to draft measurable, outcome-based standards; publish them in accessible local languages, and update them when processes change. Name duty-holders, timestamp requests and record reasons for rejection; link missed standards to feedback and a competent appeal. Audit actual service completion, repeat complaints and appeal reversals rather than only disposal. States may expand notification of feasible, high-impact services under their own Right to Public Services Acts, following the approach pioneered by Madhya Pradesh in 2010, while investing in records and staffing. Provide assisted and offline routes for excluded users. Penalties without capacity can induce defensive refusals; digitalisation without remedy can merely hide them. The charter fulfils its promise only when a citizen can understand a standard, obtain the service or secure a meaningful correction.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the three components of the Sevottam model and their interdependence. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

DARPG's Sevottam model treats citizen-centricity as an operating system, not a notice at a counter. Its **Citizens' Charter** states the service and measurable standard; the **Public Grievance Redress Mechanism** allows failure to be reported and corrected; **Service Delivery Capability** supplies the trained staff, accessible records and process to perform it.

These pillars condition one another. A certificate deadline without staff and records remains a paper promise. A complaint platform without a department able or willing to correct a denial measures tickets, not relief. Efficient staff without an accessible standard and review leave citizens unable to challenge inconsistency. CPGRAMS can route grievances for participating Union departments but is not a statutory service tribunal. Madhya Pradesh's 2010 Right to Public Services Act illustrates how an applicable state statute can add an enforceable timeline for notified services, but law cannot replace implementation capability. Sevottam succeeds when a realistic, public promise, usable redress and actual delivery reinforce each other.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Discuss the claim that democratic governance requires the public perception of the integrity and commitment of civil servants to be positive. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Officials in a democracy exercise public authority on behalf of citizens entitled to equal treatment. Perceived integrity affects trust and cooperation, but must be tested against conduct: a fast yet unexplained refusal can undermine both. At a district service counter, a Citizens' Charter discloses standards; an applicable state RTS law can identify a designated officer and appeal. Neither guarantees impartial behaviour alone.

DARPG's Sevottam framework connects the published standard with a working grievance route and the capacity to deliver. CPGRAMS can make Union complaints visible, but a high closure count cannot prove that a pension error was corrected. Publish reasons, provide accessible offline channels, audit appeal reversals and seek feedback from excluded users. Staffing and record quality matter alongside officials' intentions. A positive perception becomes democratically valuable when it reflects demonstrable fairness and a remedy for error, not when publicity demands uncritical admiration.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Identify the limitations of the Citizens' Charter as an instrument of organisational transparency and accountability and suggest measures for its greater effectiveness. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

India's 1997 Citizens' Charter initiative made administrative service standards visible. It improves transparency, but visibility without a practicable remedy does not ensure accountability.

**Design:** A department may measure acknowledgement instead of the outcome a citizen needs, fail to name an officer and leave an old charter unchanged. Consult users and frontline staff, specify the result, documents, time trigger and accountable officer, and revise periodically. **Access:** Online English publication may exclude intended beneficiaries. Display local-language standards at counters and offer assisted filing. **Redress:** Ticket closure may not deliver the service. DARPG's CPGRAMS can route grievances concerning participating Union entities, but departments must give reasons and correct errors; track recurrence and appeal reversals. **Enforcement:** A charter is not automatically a statute. State service-guarantee laws, beginning with Madhya Pradesh's 2010 Act, can create reviewable deadlines for notified services with consequences under each state's own rules; never presume every charter service is covered or that an officer's penalty compensates the applicant. **Capability:** Overloaded offices and inaccessible records defeat even sound standards. Invest in staff and interoperable workflows, and check that a one-stop counter has a functioning back end.

Sevottam links charter, grievance mechanism and delivery capability. Expand lawful, feasible guarantees where they help, but avoid penalties so rigid that staff reject applications to avoid starting the clock. Evaluate citizen outcomes and reasoned correction rather than counting charters or closures.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the claim that a Citizens' Charter without statutory backing is a promise without a remedy. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

The claim identifies an enforcement deficit but is too absolute. India's 1997 Citizens' Charter initiative set public administrative standards; a charter's publication alone does not create the statutory right to a time-bound service.

Consider a delayed certificate. Under an applicable state Right to Public Services Act, a *notified* service may have a designated officer, deadline, appeal and consequences for default. Madhya Pradesh's 2010 law pioneered this approach; scope and remedies vary by state. Without notification the charter is not an RTS entitlement, and a penalty on an officer does not automatically compensate a citizen.

Yet an administrative remedy may still exist. The department can consider a complaint, and CPGRAMS can route and track grievances of participating Union entities; neither route should be confused with a court or an all-purpose statutory tribunal. A claimant may obtain correction through responsive departmental review even without an RTS provision. Conversely, statutory backing is ineffective when records are missing, offices lack staff or applicants cannot discover their rights. Severe deadline pressure can induce officials to refuse to register an application. A timestamped receipt, reasons for denial, assisted access and competent appeal help prevent such gaming.

DARPG's Sevottam framework therefore couples the charter and grievance mechanism with delivery capability. Statutory enforceability substantially strengthens a promise for eligible services, but neither mere publication nor penalty alone guarantees that a citizen receives the service or a reasoned remedy.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess why citizen-centric administration in India remains short of its promise despite a charter, a grievance portal and statutory service guarantees. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

Citizen-centricity means obtaining a lawful service or a reasoned remedy without prohibitive cost, not accumulating documents, dashboards and legal provisions. DARPG's Sevottam model therefore joins a charter, grievance redress and delivery capability.

**Promise versus entitlement:** India's 1997 charter initiative publicised services but generally did not confer a statutory penalty. Standards drafted around easy-to-count acknowledgements may miss actual completion. Citizens who cannot read an online-only charter cannot even invoke it. State Right to Public Services laws, pioneered by Madhya Pradesh in 2010, add a timeline, designated officer and appeal for *notified* services, but notification and remedies vary by state; one cannot assume national universal coverage.

**Visibility versus resolution:** CPGRAMS routes and tracks grievances concerning participating Union departments, with an administrative redress and appeal route; it is not a tribunal with universal power to grant a service. DARPG's August 2024 redress guidance set administrative benchmarks, not a judicial limitation period. A transferred or closed complaint may leave the original denial intact. Compare reasoned outcomes, recurrence and appeal reversals, not just disposal totals.

**Access versus capability:** A Common Service Centre may simplify filing while multiple approval offices still lack shared records. Rural applicants may bear travel, intermediary costs and digital or language barriers. An understaffed officer facing penalties may reject papers prematurely to avoid the service clock. These failures occur after the charter is printed and before the remedy becomes usable.

Reform requires citizen-tested outcome standards and local-language instructions, timestamped receipts, lawful and feasible state notifications, named duty-holders, independent review, accessible offline assistance and investment in staffing and back-end records. Audit refusals to register as well as delay and repeat complaints. Stronger rights without operational capacity can shift blame to frontline staff; better portals without legal competence or substantive follow-through cannot repair service failure. The test is the claimant's traceable journey from request to fair decision to correction.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess how the design of a service guarantee should change when the citizen is treated as a rights-holder rather than a customer. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

Convenient counters and short queues matter, but public service is not a retail transaction. A rights-holder needs knowable eligibility, equal treatment, a competent duty-holder and review even when their complaint is inconvenient.

**Define authority and scope.** A proposed state guarantee must notify the particular service within the competent state's law, identify eligibility, documents, the designated officer and the event that starts the clock. Set feasible standards for routine and complex cases rather than promising identical outcomes regardless of legal requirements. Madhya Pradesh's 2010 Right to Public Services Act pioneered the statutory approach; its existence does not imply all services in every state are covered. Unlike a Citizens' Charter, an applicable RTS law can specify appeal and consequences for default.

**Protect fair access and process.** Publish instructions in local languages at physical counters, online and through Common Service Centres; retain assisted and offline filing for people without connectivity or literacy. Issue dated receipts and reasoned denials, and maintain an appeal under the relevant law. A fine on an officer, citizen compensation and deemed approval are different legal outcomes; do not promise any without statutory authority, funding and appropriate safeguards, especially for decisions involving safety or eligibility. An applicant is not merely a satisfaction score.

**Deliver and test the right.** Budget for trained staff and accessible back-end records. Supervise intermediaries charging unofficial fees. CPGRAMS may route participating Union grievances but is not automatically the appellate forum for a state-notified service. Track completed outcomes, processing time, registration refusals, reasoned denials, repeat complaints and appeal reversals by access channel. Audit whether penalty pressure leads to defensive rejection. Capacity without accountability leaves discretion unchecked; penalties without capacity can turn a right into a paper sanction. The best design combines service convenience with lawful authority, equality and a remedy that actually corrects error.
