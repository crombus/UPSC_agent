### Separate answer key and option-by-option reasoning

**Q1 — A.** A identifies a direct product and intended change. B promotes construction to wider causal impact; C equates distinct rungs; D assumes learning from buildings alone. **Trap:** school count cannot substitute for learning measurement.

**Q2 — B.** B names both predecessor bodies and year. A invents a later statutory origin; C confuses monetary settlement with evaluation; D confuses it with legislative scrutiny. **Trap:** DMEO was formed by merger, not wholly from scratch.

**Q3 — C.** C states the annual covered-scheme document's role. A concerns unrelated legal/fiscal links; B wrongly makes coverage universal; D invents an electoral purpose. **Trap:** covered schemes, not literally every scheme.

**Q4 — D.** D captures the delta incentive. A locks rankings to history; B reads causation into a rank; C uses only absolute performance. **Trap:** fast improvement is not evidence of a programme-caused improvement.

**Q5 — A.** A preserves the Basic source's district figures and platform. B swaps blocks and districts; C substitutes districts for KPI count; D overstates scope and changes metric. **Trap:** do not merge ADP with the Blocks Programme.

**Q6 — B.** B asks what would have happened without the programme. A measures publicity; C adds output records; D relabels a test score as finance. **Trap:** before-and-after alone cannot attribute change.

**Q7 — C.** C measures a user outcome and specifies comparison. A counts roads, B contractors and D communication; none establishes travel benefit. **Trap:** an indicator at a higher rung needs its own observations.

**Q8 — D.** D separates repeated tracking from explanatory assessment. A assigns causal proof to routine monitoring; B deletes evaluation's design function; C equates information collection with its purpose. **Trap:** a dashboard is not an impact evaluation.

**Q9 — A.** A addresses self-assessment conflict while qualifying independence. B invents attribution; C misunderstands ministries' powers to respond; D discards useful measurement. **Trap:** third-party commissioning does not guarantee independence if findings can be buried.

**Q10 — B.** B locates the Budget document correctly. A confuses platform and institution; C gives the ECI scheme powers; D substitutes a local body for a Union ministry. **Trap:** DMEO evaluates; Budget Division administers OOMF.

**Q11 — C.** C predicts Goodhart-style reporting pressure. A falsely assumes bias improves; B mistakes a target count for health; D ignores stable operational definitions. **Trap:** the reporting official may also be the person ranked.

**Q12 — D.** D exposes hidden intra-district gaps without reckless disclosure. A prevents detection; B commits ecological inference; C magnifies gaming pressure. **Trap:** an average can improve while marginalised villages deteriorate.

**Q13 — A.** A states the needed starting measure and its limit. B claims causal proof; C cancels follow-up; D mistakes an input for a social result. **Trap:** a baseline is necessary but not a sufficient counterfactual.

**Q14 — B.** B bounds experimental identification. A universalises local findings; C ignores rights and ethics; D discards outcome observation. **Trap:** randomised evidence describes the tested variant.

**Q15 — C.** C investigates mechanism. A asks a constitutional question; B claims causal certainty from temporal change; D invents a universal impact estimate. **Trap:** process diagnosis complements, not replaces, effect estimates.

**Q16 — D.** D separates installed access and quality/health. A converts connection into impact; B promotes ranking into lab test; C equates different indicators. **Trap:** count water delivered and water safe at point of use separately.

**Q17 — A.** A lists all seven owner criteria. B describes other subjects; C and D omit relevance, coherence, equity and impact. **Trap:** a criteria checklist is a frame, not evidence of good results.

**Q18 — B.** B captures scale and source dependence. A assumes causality, C assumes invisible households are recorded, D excludes lived experience. **Trap:** timely administrative records can still be systematically biased.

**Q19 — C.** C recognises selection and demands assumptions. A mislabels difference as effect; B claims randomisation without assignment; D replaces outcome evidence with messaging. **Trap:** comparison groups must be comparable.

**Q20 — D.** D keeps programme and unit distinct. A merges indicator sets; B swaps the 513 blocks into districts; C invents constitutional status. **Trap:** always attach counts to their named programme.

**Q21 — A.** A explains a false trend and how to disclose breaks. B assumes improvement; C dispenses with checks; D worsens denominator opacity. **Trap:** stable labels can hide changing definitions.

**Q22 — B.** B links published evidence to an accountable correction loop. A blocks scrutiny; C produces metrics without action; D removes the possibility of learning. **Trap:** an evaluator cannot redesign a ministry programme alone.

**Q23 — C.** C identifies people omitted by conditional sampling. A assumes complete inclusion; B assumes subgroup checks; D claims causation from a selected sample. **Trap:** measure exclusion and drop-off explicitly.

**Q24 — D.** D ties the 172 and threshold to 2026–27. A freezes a variable count; B universalises OOMF; C assigns a Budget scheme count to districts. **Trap:** never carry annual coverage figures forward undated.

## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### Source-specific evidence retained

- The ADP's **49 KPIs** span **health and nutrition; education; agriculture and water resources; financial inclusion and skill development; basic infrastructure**. District officials enter monthly data and **NITI-selected agencies validate** it: validation improves but cannot guarantee source quality, and district averages can mask block-level failure.
- The full results chain is **input → activity → output → outcome → impact**; an indicator measures a condition while a target sets an intended level. Distinct failure modes require distinct correctives: Goodhart/gaming (indicator basket and sample audit), self-reporting (independent validation), aggregate masking (disaggregation), output substitution (quality measures), selection/survivor bias (measure dropout), and definition drift (publish series breaks). An evaluation framed by DMEO's **relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, sustainability, impact, coherence and equity** still needs a baseline and appropriate attribution method.

### PYQ ownership audit

The Basic source and locally audited workbook identify **no direct routed GS-II Mains PYQ for this topic in 2018–2025**. Output/outcome distinctions may strengthen answers to other owners' scheme-effectiveness questions, but those are not reassigned or passed off as direct PYQs here. No unverified objective question or official answer key is manufactured. The six below are original practice questions retaining their original wording.

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks (about 150 words)

> Distinguish output indicators from outcome indicators in the evaluation of a government scheme, with an Indian example.

**Model answer:** Output indicators count what an intervention directly delivers; outcome indicators measure the change experienced by its intended users. For a public road project, kilometres constructed and road segments opened are outputs. Reduced travel time and improved access to markets for villagers are outcomes. Even the latter must be compared with a baseline and plausible alternative explanations before claiming the road caused wider income growth, which is a longer-term impact.

India's Union Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework asks covered major schemes to state outlays, outputs, outcomes, indicators and targets together. This prevents spending and construction counts from masquerading as proof of development. But an outcome indicator may be biased if households without access are excluded from measurement or if implementing officials alone supply the data. Record denominator, geography, gender and other relevant groups, verify independently and compare conditions appropriately. A delivered facility matters; its public value rests on whether it changes the lives of those it was meant to serve.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks (about 150 words)

> Examine the design choice of ranking districts by the pace of improvement rather than by absolute level.

**Model answer:** NITI Aayog's Aspirational Districts Programme uses delta ranking within a dashboard covering 112 districts and 49 KPIs across five themes. Ranking the pace of improvement gives a historically deprived district credit for genuine progress even if its present level remains below that of a better-off district. This can focus administrators on change rather than inherited advantage.

However, rapid improvement from a low base is not the same as adequate services, and a relative rank is not evidence that ADP caused the change. District officials supplying data can be tempted to improve the reported numerator or neglect hard-to-measure groups. A district average may conceal deteriorating conditions in remote blocks. Pair delta with absolute floors and subgroup levels; publish stable definitions and denominators, validate sample records independently and collect citizen experience. Incentives improve monitoring only if they reward sustained, inclusive outcomes rather than the easiest monthly number to raise.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks (about 250 words)

> Distinguish monitoring from evaluation as governance-information functions and assess India's architecture on that distinction.

**Model answer:** Monitoring repeatedly asks whether planned activities and indicators are on track; evaluation asks whether an intervention is relevant, implemented well, cost-effective and responsible for observed change. A dashboard recording new piped-water connections can alert a district to delayed installations. It cannot by itself show that water at the household tap is safe or that the scheme caused improved health.

India has instruments for both functions. The Ministry of Finance's Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework ties the outlays of covered major schemes to outputs and intended outcomes in the Union Budget. NITI Aayog's Aspirational Districts Programme supplies frequent district-level KPI tracking and delta rankings, encouraging improvement over an inherited low base. NITI's Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office, formed in 2015 from PEO and IEO, conducts or coordinates scheme evaluation, including third-party studies; its criteria span relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, sustainability, impact, coherence and equity.

The institutions are complementary, not a single causal pipeline. The ranked district official may also generate the monthly figures; timely reporting trades off against verification. A third-party evaluator can reduce self-assessment conflict, but independence depends on commissioning, access to records and publication. Evidence of improved outcomes requires baselines, comparison with plausible alternatives and attention to who was excluded. Above all, DMEO cannot itself change a line ministry's scheme: that ministry must record a response, change design or resources and report subsequent results. India has strengthened tracking considerably; outcome accountability remains incomplete wherever dashboards end the inquiry rather than start it.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks (about 250 words)

> Examine why attribution rather than measurement is the hard problem in evaluating a public programme.

**Model answer:** Measurement establishes whether an indicator changed. Attribution asks whether the programme caused the change rather than weather, economic conditions, other policies or the selection of beneficiaries. If travel times fall after a rural road opens, new transport services or parallel road improvements may also explain the change. A before–after comparison alone cannot isolate the programme effect.

A baseline records where beneficiaries started but does not tell us what would have happened otherwise. A comparable untreated group provides a counterfactual, although selected districts may differ in poverty or access from treated ones. Random allocation, where lawful and ethical, can identify an effect for the tested population and design; it does not automatically establish nationwide success. Quasi-experimental approaches require explicit assumptions, and process evaluation can show whether the new road remained usable and how commuters adapted without alone estimating its causal effect. Mixed methods combine estimates with evidence from those served and excluded.

Administrative dashboards such as the Aspirational Districts Programme's Champions of Change can detect monthly trends; they cannot convert correlation or delta rank into impact. The official who is ranked may also report the number, so independent verification and stable definitions matter. Disaggregate outcomes by geography, gender and other relevant groups; a district average can conceal a village left behind. For urgent course correction, imperfect monitoring may be sufficient, whereas large expenditure or claims of impact justify stronger evaluation. A defensible conclusion states the indicator, baseline, counterfactual, uncertainty and scope of inference rather than claiming that every observed improvement was produced by one scheme.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks (about 300 words)

> Assess whether India's monitoring and evaluation architecture has shifted governance from counting outputs to accounting for outcomes.

**Model answer:** India has moved the language and machinery of public finance beyond spending alone, but measuring outcomes reliably and acting on them remain unfinished. The results chain begins with inputs (money and staff), activities (training or construction), and outputs (facilities created); outcomes ask whether intended people's conditions changed, while wider impacts require even more careful attribution.

The Union Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework, administered by the Finance Ministry's Budget Division, asks covered major Central Sector and centrally sponsored schemes to present outlays beside output and outcome indicators. In its **2026–27** document the Basic owner records 172 major schemes with outlays of ₹500 crore or more; this is a dated scope, not permanent universal coverage. NITI Aayog's DMEO, formed in 2015 by combining PEO and IEO, coordinates evaluations and can commission third-party inquiry. The Aspirational Districts Programme's Champions of Change dashboard tracks 49 KPIs across five themes in 112 districts and uses delta ranking to reward improvement rather than inherited advantage.

These are substantial advances in visibility. Yet a connected water pipe is not safe water at point of use, and a monthly district KPI need not represent every remote block. Officials ranked by self-reported results have reasons to improve recorded numbers; an independent survey and disaggregation can reveal excluded households. A better trend after implementation does not prove causation without a credible counterfactual. Evaluation also fails its learning purpose if the line ministry neither publishes findings nor explains what will change; DMEO cannot unilaterally redesign that ministry's programme.

Use baselines, stable definitions, denominators, independently verified samples and mixed methods appropriate to scale. Give each finding an accountable owner, response deadline and follow-up outcome measure, while avoiding punitive incentives to conceal failure. India's architecture has made outcomes **reportable**, but will make officials accountable for them only when validated evidence changes service design and equity of access.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks (about 300 words)

> Assess the design of an indicator system for a new government programme, including the failure modes it must survive.

**Model answer:** Consider a district programme promising safer drinking water. Define a theory of change before selecting indicators: budget and technicians are inputs; testing and pipe repair are activities; functioning connections and tests completed are outputs; safe water actually consumed and reduced collection time are outcomes. Any longer-term fall in disease is an impact claim requiring attention to sanitation and other causes.

Record a pre-intervention baseline, a clear denominator (all eligible households, not only registered users), sampling frequency, responsibility for entry and independently auditable definitions. Disaggregate access and quality by hamlet, gender, disability and social group while protecting personal data. Combine household-reported interruptions with point-of-use quality tests; a national standard makes comparisons possible, but local seasonal conditions may require additional indicators. The implementing agency must know which adverse signal triggers retesting, maintenance and a grievance response.

Design against gaming. If payment depends only on connection counts, contractors may install non-functioning taps. If district officers are ranked on reported quality, they may test easy locations or exclude hard-to-reach users. Use a basket of coverage, quality, reliability and exclusion measures, independently audit random sites and publish the denominator and definition changes. A monthly dashboard enables rapid response but self-reported data are not independent impact evidence. Before–after improvements require a plausible comparison or quasi-experimental design when making causal claims; process interviews explain why a measured effect fails to reach some households.

The Finance Ministry's OOMF illustrates the value of relating allocation to results; DMEO-style independent evaluation can test whether the programme works and for whom. Findings need a named ministry/State decision-maker to modify repair contracts, outreach or resources and report later outcomes. An indicator system succeeds not because it has many metrics, but because sound measurement survives incentives and leads to corrective public action.
