# Development Processes and the Development Industry — Solved Practice Workbook

The scenarios below are analytical illustrations, not claimed programme evaluations. Sources: Governance Basic 03 §§2–13 and Advanced 03 §§2–10; audited GS-II demand ledger as recorded in the Basic owner. Attempt every question before consulting the separate key.

### Matched key: each option explained

**Q1 — A.** **A:** A field SHG translates financing into contact and service. **B:** A funder shapes priorities, not every delivery encounter. **C:** A registrar establishes legal status, not field fidelity. **D:** Tax oversight is not programme delivery. **Trap:** Separate funder, legal vehicle and implementer.

**Q2 — B.** **A:** A Section 8 company is an alternative form, not the default for endowments. **B:** Section 1 of the 1882 Act excludes charitable/religious endowments; the deed and applicable state law matter. **C:** Society law applies to associations, not all trusts. **D:** Exclusion from one Act does not remove all governance. **Trap:** Never present the 1882 Act as a uniform national incorporation statute for public charitable trusts.

**Q3 — C.** **A:** Section 135 sets a corporate CSR obligation, not association incorporation. **B:** Foreign-funding permission is a separate layer. **C:** A society fits member/general-body governance under the 1860 Act or state variants. **D:** A deed-created trust need not have the described membership structure. **Trap:** The NGO label does not specify its legal form.

**Q4 — D.** **A:** An ordinary company can distribute dividends. **B:** A CSR committee is not the proposed nonprofit legal vehicle. **C:** FCRA regulates foreign contribution rather than incorporation. **D:** Section 8 combines charitable objects, company governance and nondistribution. **Trap:** A Section 8 entity is not the same thing as a company's Section 135 duty.

**Q5 — A.** **A:** Threshold-based Section 135 CSR uses at least 2% of average net profits of preceding financial years. **B:** Schedule VII names areas, not a universal revenue levy. **C:** Section 8 governs nonprofit companies. **D:** FCRA addresses foreign contribution, not a CSR calculation. **Trap:** Do not assert that every company must spend.

**Q6 — B.** **A:** Corporate branding is not itself a statutory CSR category. **B:** Schedule VII eligibility must be established for the activity. **C:** Entity form cannot cure ineligible use. **D:** Domestic CSR is within the MCA framework, not an MHA approval test. **Trap:** Statutory CSR differs from unrestricted voluntary giving.

**Q7 — C.** **A:** Article 243ZD concerns district planning. **B:** Society law does not prescribe MCA reporting. **C:** Amended CSR Policy Rules govern specified reporting and impact-assessment requirements; check applicable year. **D:** A private deed cannot establish national CSR filings. **Trap:** Do not attribute rule-based thresholds to Section 135's text.

**Q8 — D.** **A:** Entity formation does not grant tax or foreign-funding eligibility. **B:** Darpan is not a universal permit. **C:** Schedule VII governs eligible CSR subjects. **D:** Income-tax and MHA/FCRA compliance are independent gates. **Trap:** Formation, Darpan, tax treatment and FCRA are four distinct layers.

**Q9 — A.** **A:** A legal school project may still mismatch articulated exclusion needs. **B:** Education is a listed CSR area. **C:** Nothing establishes a foreign grant. **D:** Community voice does not imply a universal private-grant licence. **Trap:** Activity eligibility does not establish demand-driven targeting.

**Q10 — B.** **A:** Advertising cannot solve duplicate siting. **B:** District-plan convergence and hamlet-wise need data can redirect investment. **C:** Output counts conceal distribution. **D:** Suppressing consultation worsens need-fit. **Trap:** Multiple projects need not mean inclusive aggregate coverage.

**Q11 — C.** **A:** An online presence does not test continuing public duty. **B:** A corporate spending test is separate. **C:** Additionality detects whether flexibility supplements or substitutes state capacity. **D:** Foreign-finance status does not answer the public-duty question. **Trap:** Emergency gap-filling need not license lasting state retreat.

**Q12 — D.** **A:** A name or form cannot finance continuation. **B:** Early attendance says nothing about post-grant staffing. **C:** Nationality is not the operating plan. **D:** Sustainability requires a responsible operator and ongoing costs. **Trap:** A completed pilot is not durable capability.

**Q13 — A.** **A:** Donor reporting cannot replace accessible complaint and correction for excluded users. **B:** The reported data could be accurate yet incomplete. **C:** No incorporation breach is stated. **D:** No public enterprise is required for a remedy. **Trap:** Compliance with upward reporting is not downward answerability.

**Q14 — B.** **A:** Disaggregated exclusion data tests need-fit. **B:** Relation to district/local plans tests local/federal convergence. **C:** Remedy tests accountability. **D:** Maintenance finance tests sustainability. **Trap:** The five tests diagnose separate design failures.

**Q15 — C.** **A:** Community approval alone does not staff a service. **B:** Registration proves legal form, not competence. **C:** A weak implementer can break a sound financing design. **D:** Replacement is not compelled by the facts. **Trap:** Trace each causal link rather than blaming one actor by default.

**Q16 — D.** **A:** Education can be Schedule VII eligible. **B:** Partner implementation is possible. **C:** Nothing requires foreign financing. **D:** Check who uses the school, maintenance and learning rather than construction alone. **Trap:** Spending, outputs and outcomes are not interchangeable.

**Q17 — A.** **A:** MHA administers the applicable FCRA gate to foreign contribution. **B:** Section 8 form cannot replace it. **C:** SFCs concern local finances. **D:** DPCs plan districts rather than license donors. **Trap:** Valid domestic incorporation does not establish foreign-funding permission.

**Q18 — B.** **A:** Income-tax compliance remains distinct. **B:** The Darpan interface is informational, not a general financing licence. **C:** Company incorporation occurs under company law. **D:** Foreign contribution is controlled through FCRA. **Trap:** A portal listing is not legal authorisation or impact evidence.

**Q19 — C.** **A:** Trustees are not universally elected by recipients. **B:** Corporate boards are not elected panchayats. **C:** Financial scrutiny upwards leaves beneficiary voice and electoral checks unresolved. **D:** State-law trusts cannot all be assumed subject to company-law disclosure. **Trap:** More reporting need not change the direction of accountability.

**Q20 — D.** **A:** Speed cannot prove fair selection. **B:** The state also has disaster functions. **C:** Applicable rules do not vanish in emergencies. **D:** Quick discretion is valuable in crisis but requires transparent targeting and longer-term coordination. **Trap:** An emergency advantage is not a blanket case for permanent service substitution.

**Q21 — A.** **A:** Article 243ZD provides the DPC's district-plan consolidation role. **B:** MCA registers companies; it does not consolidate local plans. **C:** MHA handles foreign contribution, not district plan consolidation. **D:** The tax authority does not administer Section 135 planning. **Trap:** Match the institution to its own statutory or constitutional function.

**Q22 — B.** **A:** A committee without powers may only endorse a higher-tier plan. **B:** Personnel, functions and usable finance permit decisions to follow local information. **C:** Standards and redistribution can legitimately sit higher. **D:** Tied funds can strip local discretion. **Trap:** Devolution of implementation is not devolution of planning.

**Q23 — C.** **A:** Foreign contribution was not specified. **B:** An entity's legal form does not explain this output indicator. **C:** Construction counts reward visible assets even when water service fails. **D:** No change in the CSR rate is involved. **Trap:** Output indicators can suppress evidence of use and maintenance.

**Q24 — D.** **A:** Exclusive donor control can displace local needs. **B:** Removing district coordination increases fragmentation. **C:** Private investment cannot automatically fulfil a public entitlement. **D:** District convergence, disclosed roles and a continuing duty-holder preserve additionality. **Trap:** Innovation without a post-pilot owner can disappear after funding ends.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 1 — 2025 General Studies Paper-II

**Demand:** In contemporary development models, decision-making and problem-solving responsibilities are not located close to the source of information and execution defeating the objectives of development. Critically evaluate in 250 words for 15 marks.

**Status:** Routed to this Basic owner by the audited 2024-2025 Mains ledger and confirmed against the locally held official paper. Authored model, not an official key.

**Model solution:** The statement is substantially correct when those who select a problem cannot observe its local causes or bear the cost of failure. In India, a district may implement a standard programme without discretion to adapt targeting, despite the District Planning Committee's constitutional consolidation role under Article 243ZD. Uneven DPC operationalisation and limited fiscal space weaken the information-to-decision loop.

Distance also arises outside government. A CSR committee deciding to fund school buildings under Section 135 and Schedule VII may measure buildings completed while a Gram Sabha identifies maintenance or disability access as the greater need. An NGO executing the grant then reports upwards to the company, not automatically to excluded families. A distant donor board can similarly set the agenda before consultation, reward countable outputs, impose a short project cycle and replace beneficiary challenge with grant reporting. These are mechanisms, not proof that every CSR project fails.

Yet local proximity is not a universal solution: procurement scale, health standards and equity across districts need higher-tier judgement; local capture can also silence marginal groups. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign (1996) illustrates the possibility of local priority-setting when funds, functions and functionaries accompany participation, not a guarantee in every district. Allocate standards and redistribution upwards, local selection and grievance downwards; require district-plan convergence and publicly review both beneficiary exclusion and sustained service. Distance defeats development where it blocks correction, not wherever a higher tier legitimately decides.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 2 — 2019 General Studies Paper-II

**Demand:** In the context of neo-liberal paradigm of development planning, multi-level planning is expected to make operations cost-effective and remove many implementation blockages. Discuss in 250 words for 15 marks.

**Status:** Routed historical GS-II descriptive demand, answered here under the Basic owner's superseding core routing. Authored model.

**Model solution:** Multi-level planning assigns national, state, district and local tiers decisions appropriate to their information and scale. A neo-liberal orientation favours market allocation and private provision, recasting government partly as enabler and regulator. The two can reinforce each other: a Union or State sets common standards, while a district aggregates village and municipal priorities and chooses locally feasible sites. Article 243ZD envisages District Planning Committees consolidating local plans. Removing a mismatch between a central design and a local water or transport constraint can reduce rework and implementation cost.

The claim is conditional, however. Tied grants can turn a local body into a contractor rather than a planner; competition for private investment may shift decisions to State or Union negotiators, while foundations and CSR committees pursue their own priorities. A project can meet its spending target yet duplicate a neighbouring service and leave excluded hamlets unserved. State Finance Commissions matter because financial discretion is a condition of genuine local choice. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign (1996) demonstrates the planning potential of devolved functions, funds and personnel, but cannot establish uniform performance elsewhere.

Effective planning therefore coordinates public obligations with non-state resources instead of simply reducing government. Publish local needs and costs, converge partner projects with district plans, allow frontline adaptation and audit the distribution of benefits. Higher tiers should finance cross-district equity and common standards; local tiers should determine context-sensitive priorities and complaints. Cost-effectiveness means useful outcomes per resource, not merely cheaper project completion. Without devolution and downward answerability, market-oriented multi-level planning can preserve the language of subsidiarity while relocating real power upwards or to funders.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 3 — 2022 General Studies Paper-II

**Demand:** Do you agree with the view that increasing dependence on donor agencies for development reduces the importance of community participation in the development process? Justify your answer in 250 words for 15 marks.

**Status:** Cross-cutting historical GS-II descriptive demand; core ownership here supersedes the older Advanced pointer. Authored model.

**Model solution:** I agree that donor *dependence* often sidelines participation, but reject the inference that donor finance necessarily makes community judgement less important. A grant designed before village consultation fixes the agenda; output reporting then rewards countable assets rather than slow collective decisions. Short project cycles discourage the formation of durable SHG institutions, while reports to donors can replace explanations owed to those denied a service. This is a transfer of agenda and accountability, not simply an increase in money.

Consider a hypothetical CSR-supported district health project: if an external board chooses a visible facility while women in remote hamlets prioritise outreach, compliance with Section 135 and Schedule VII does not demonstrate need-fit. The implementing society may have valid registration yet no effective grievance channel. Conversely, donor funds can pay community mobilisers and participatory monitoring; DAY-NRLM's public support for SHG federations shows why financing collective organisation is not inherently anti-participatory. This example illustrates institutional design, not a claim about any donor's observed results.

Ask whether funds add to rather than replace public duties; whether excluded groups co-decide priorities; whether beneficiaries can inspect records and obtain correction; and whether the activity can continue beyond the grant and converge with local plans. Put representatives of marginalised households in selection and review, disclose project criteria and plan a funded hand-over. Foreign grants require the applicable FCRA gate, which should not be confused with community consent. Thus dependence reduces participation where donors monopolise agenda, metric and exit; it may strengthen it when community institutions control these choices and the state retains its obligations.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the funding source, the legal vehicle and the implementing organisation in India's development industry, and explain why the distinction matters. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model solution:** Funding source, legal vehicle and implementer answer different governance questions. A company's CSR budget under Section 135 or a multilateral grant determines resources and often the programme's priorities. A society registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, a state-law public charitable trust or a Section 8 company receives and governs money through distinct membership, deed or board rules. An NGO or SHG delivering a district project determines whether the intended household actually receives a usable service.

For example, a hypothetical CSR-funded school facility may be lawfully within Schedule VII, yet an intermediary's opaque selection and a field partner's lack of local staff can exclude remote pupils. The corporate board's disclosure cannot replace a beneficiary complaint mechanism; registration does not establish tax exemption or FCRA permission either. Separating the links identifies who must alter the priority, disclose the accounts, repair delivery and finance maintenance. It also keeps elected government responsible for universal public services rather than treating a private grant as an entitlement.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why corporate social responsibility in India is a statutory obligation rather than philanthropy, and what follows for development outcomes. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model solution:** Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 applies to companies meeting specified financial thresholds and prescribes at least two per cent of average net profits of preceding financial years for CSR. Schedule VII bounds eligible activities. Unlike a voluntary donation, this is a company-law duty subject to board-level governance; CSR-2 reporting and specified impact assessments arise under the CSR Policy Rules, not Section 135 alone. A Section 8 company, by contrast, is a nonprofit legal vehicle, not the statutory spending rule.

The floor mobilises corporate resources for areas such as education or health, and the activity list guards against unrelated spending. Yet compliance can favour conspicuous infrastructure over harder-to-measure needs. A hypothetical school building may satisfy the activity criterion but remain inaccessible or unmaintained. District-level need mapping, beneficiary complaints and monitoring service use rather than construction counts convert lawful expenditure into plausible inclusion. Neither a filing nor a spending total proves an attributable learning outcome; public duties remain with the state.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the claim that public charitable trusts have the potential to make India's development more inclusive because they relate to vital public issues. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model solution:** Public charitable trusts can respond to health, education and disaster needs rapidly, but charitable purpose does not by itself establish inclusive results. A trust is governed by its deed, applicable state public-trust or endowment law and general principles: Section 1 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 excludes charitable and religious endowments, so that Act is not a uniform national registration route. This matters because oversight and beneficiary access to records can vary.

During a disaster, a hypothetical trust working with the district administration could mobilise emergency supplies before routine procurement ends. It adds capacity if its targeting reaches isolated settlements and does not let the state abandon relief duties. For a sustained school or clinic programme, the test is harder: a trustee's chosen location can overlook disability access, remote hamlets or long-term staffing. School construction is an output, not evidence of sustained attendance; a clinic opened is not the same as accessible care.

Trustees and donors review accounts upwards, while users may lack an effective route to question selection. Publish eligibility, financing and beneficiary lists with appropriate privacy safeguards; consult excluded groups, accept complaints and record corrections. Converge the intervention with the district plan and specify who funds maintenance after a grant exits. A society's membership governance or a Section 8 company's board disclosure provides different controls but neither guarantees voice; tax benefits and foreign grants add distinct compliance duties. Thus trusts offer flexibility unavailable in some routine procedures, especially in emergencies. Their inclusive potential is realised only when additionality, local need-fit, downward accountability, sustainability and convergence are demonstrated, not assumed from charitable status.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine whether increasing dependence on donor agencies reduces the importance of community participation in development. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model solution:** External finance can weaken the influence of communities without making their knowledge less important. Dependence arises when a donor board can withdraw money unless local partners meet its predetermined priorities. If the funder decides to build visible facilities before a village diagnoses its needs, residents become consultees, not co-designers. A count of facilities rewards easily reported outputs rather than accessibility; a short funding cycle discourages the slower formation of durable SHG federations; and upward reporting can leave excluded households without a hearing.

Consider a hypothetical grant to a district society for a rural water facility. Members may approve accounts under the Societies Registration Act framework, yet women collecting water may identify poor siting or unreliable operation that the donor's annual report misses. Meeting eligibility or filing requirements proves neither usable water nor local control. Decisions on site and grievance must involve users and the competent local body; maintenance must have a named post-grant financier. If the foreign donor provides contribution, the society additionally needs the applicable FCRA registration or prior permission; that compliance does not substitute for consent.

The opposing case is real in principle: external funds can finance facilitators, accessible consultations and community monitoring where local bodies lack resources. DAY-NRLM's SHG-federation model illustrates the institutional value of collective organisation, though it does not prove that all grants produce participation. Disclose spending and selection, allow marginalised households to challenge exclusions, and align projects with district plans. Donor dependence reduces effective participation when the funder monopolises agenda, metrics and exit; co-decision and public responsibility can reverse that design choice.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the place of multi-level planning in a development paradigm that favours market allocation and private provision. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model solution:** Multi-level planning distributes priority-setting and resources among Union, State, district and local tiers. A market-oriented paradigm can improve efficiency where public bodies set standards and regulate, while private providers respond to demand; it can also relocate public decisions to investors and unelected funders. The assessment depends on which tier retains authority over priorities, rather than on how many planning committees exist.

Article 243ZD provides for District Planning Committees to consolidate rural and urban local plans. They could coordinate a district's public health network with a hypothetical CSR-funded clinic, avoiding duplication and locating underserved hamlets. State Finance Commissions are essential to this approach because local decisions without fiscal discretion are implementation assignments, not planning. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign, begun in 1996, illustrates the possibility of combining local priorities with functions, personnel and resources; uneven implementation elsewhere warns against treating institutional creation as success.

Market allocation can offer innovation and flexible delivery; charitable trusts may respond quickly to disaster needs, while Section 8 companies bring formal board governance. However, a company bound by Section 135 and Schedule VII may favour a visible school building over disability access; a donor's grant cycle may end before maintenance becomes secure. Tied transfers and competition to attract investment can shift effective choice upwards. An NGO executing the project is answerable to its funder, but an excluded beneficiary cannot vote out the board. Multiple projects without district convergence can raise apparent investment while leaving deficits intact.

Consequently, use a division of decisions: national and State levels finance equalisation, standards and scale; local bodies identify exclusion, select sites and operate complaints; DPCs consolidate; private actors add finance or methods without displacing public entitlements. Publish disaggregated needs, specify maintenance and evaluate accessibility and sustained service rather than spending alone. Multi-level planning remains indispensable as a democratic coordination and correction mechanism. Its cost-saving promise is credible only where lower tiers control usable resources and external finance fits accountable public plans, not where market provision merely disguises central or donor control.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the claim that in contemporary development models decision-making sits too far from the source of information and execution. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model solution:** Decision-making is too distant when those choosing an intervention neither possess the local information required nor face meaningful correction from those implementing or receiving it. This may result from administrative centralisation, but a private development chain can recreate the same problem without any government order. The question is therefore about the location of discretion and accountability, not geography alone.

Article 243ZD envisages District Planning Committees combining village and municipal plans. Where they lack operational authority or finance, a central template can override frontline knowledge. At the same time, a CSR committee constrained by Section 135 and Schedule VII may select a visible school facility while a field NGO hears that the actual bottleneck is disability access. The project's corporate report can show construction completed; neither attendance nor an accessible grievance route follows automatically. A foreign donor's project metrics can similarly reward easily counted outputs, shorten institution-building, and transfer community decisions to a distant board. These are plausible mechanisms, not measured outcomes of a named programme.

Proximity is not enough. Local elites can capture site selection, while national standards, pooled procurement or cross-district redistribution may require higher-tier decisions. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign (1996) illustrates that local planning can be meaningful when funds, functions and functionaries accompany local voice; it is no universal guarantee. State Finance Commissions provide a fiscal route for local bodies, but a formal recommendation alone does not establish usable resources.

Correct by type of decision: higher tiers set service standards and equalisation; district and local bodies diagnose exclusion, adapt sites and accept complaints; funders publish criteria and co-decide with affected groups; implementers report service use and errors back to both beneficiaries and financiers. Plan for post-grant operation and converge CSR or donor projects with public plans. The claim is strong where a remote metric overrides beneficiary experience, but excessive localisation can also harm equity. Development succeeds when information travels to the competent decision-maker and users can demand a correction.
