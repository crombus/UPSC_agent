### Separate answer key and option-by-option reasoning

**Q1 — A.** A tracks idle balances and times release against need. B confuses identity with fund parking; C removes rather than improves expenditure control; D counts approval, not utilisation. **Trap:** payment eligibility cannot cure an agency float.

**Q2 — B.** B separates bank credit from actual withdrawal. A mislabels an access problem as eligibility; C assumes sanction failed despite credit; D invokes unrelated procurement law. **Trap:** an electronic success receipt is not a welfare outcome.

**Q3 — C.** C preserves the source's three DBT categories. A drops in-kind/enablers; B mistakes one possible authentication method for the whole reform; D absorbs unrelated procurement payments. **Trap:** DBT is broader than cash but narrower than all expenditure.

**Q4 — D.** D repairs who belongs on the list and provides review. A changes settlement speed only; B may intensify exclusion; C confuses wrongful denial with savings. **Trap:** accurate execution of a defective register remains defective targeting.

**Q5 — A.** A captures the CSS-specific agency/account design and its historical place. B universalises it to procurement; C extends it to central-sector schemes; D mistakes balance visibility for impact. **Trap:** distinguish the 2021 SNA design from the later SPARSH rail.

**Q6 — B.** B names the three integrated systems. A substitutes identity/local institutions; C lists oversight bodies, not the release interface; D names rules plus a bank but omits State IFMIS and RBI settlement. **Trap:** e-Kuber is infrastructure, not an eligibility authority.

**Q7 — C.** C is the administrative home identified by the owner. A governs personnel; B concerns the financial-services sector, not PFMS administration; D implements sector programmes. **Trap:** banking jurisdiction is not PFMS ownership.

**Q8 — D.** D distinguishes normative coverage from observed operations. A assumes flawless reconciliation; B invents a financing change; C confuses scheme classification with its release rail. **Trap:** an OM does not certify every rollout.

**Q9 — A.** A follows when duplicates are removed. B describes the opposite risk to eligible people; C concerns purchased quality; D arises when cash is substituted for a good. **Trap:** deduplication can lower E1 without automatically lowering E2.

**Q10 — B.** B identifies an eligible person wrongly denied and the matching correction. A would require ineligible payment; C presumes unproved diversion; D contradicts non-payment. **Trap:** an identity mismatch is not a ghost beneficiary.

**Q11 — C.** C locates the two distinct financial layers. A gives PFMS rule-making power it lacks; B reverses functions; D wrongly restricts both to FC transfers. **Trap:** agency-flow visibility and end-beneficiary delivery are not synonyms.

**Q12 — D.** D describes whose efficiency improves and whose planning burden rises. A concerns benefit form, B concerns purchasing quality and C concerns transaction privacy; none describes intergovernmental release timing. **Trap:** faster Union reconciliation can impose State adaptation costs.

**Q13 — A.** A tackles the three quality constraints beyond process legality. B applies welfare identity to contracting; C equates price with performance; D treats records as services. **Trap:** an auditable bad purchase is still bad value.

**Q14 — B.** B correctly separates Panchayat clause (bb) from municipal clause (c). A covers parliamentary finance bills/budgets, C elections and D judicial remedies. **Trap:** the Union FC recommends augmentation of State funds, not direct constitutional control of municipal taxation.

**Q15 — C.** C identifies all three finance sources and grant design. A equates spending money with autonomy; B rejects a legitimate resource; D turns settlement infrastructure into revenue. **Trap:** tied funds can deliver priorities without permitting local choice.

**Q16 — D.** D acknowledges choice and household price exposure. A assumes functioning markets; B erases distribution cost; C confuses benefit form with eligibility. **Trap:** equivalence of nominal value does not ensure equivalent access to food.

**Q17 — A.** A respects differing disclosure scopes. B promises universal access not established by a lookup; C denies actual payment tracking; D infers outcomes from transactions. **Trap:** traceability is not full public disclosure.

**Q18 — B.** B locates e-Kuber in intergovernmental settlement. A belongs to programme rules; C to procurement authorities; D to evaluators. **Trap:** a payment rail is an enabling component, not itself policy.

**Q19 — C.** C separates a recorded fiscal claim from net welfare. A equates different quantities; B conceals E2; D abandons a useful but insufficient indicator. **Trap:** report exclusion alongside gross savings, not as its footnote.

**Q20 — D.** D correctly locates contracting, balances and lists at distinct stages. A relocates procurement to identities; B calls fund parking a final outcome; C puts authentication inside legislative appropriation. **Trap:** diagnose stage before prescribing tool.

**Q21 — A.** A tests access and substantive change separately. B treats access infrastructure as nutrition; C substitutes procurement activity for child status; D confuses allocation with causal effect. **Trap:** a financial output cannot alone certify a welfare outcome.

**Q22 — B.** B recognises the unequal human cost and designs an exception path. A falsely equalises harms; C asserts an automatic win-win; D deletes a necessary subsistence safeguard. **Trap:** preventing E1 can unintentionally produce E2.

**Q23 — C.** C accurately identifies Union procedural rules and their limits. A misstates legal status; B gives a general rulebook scheme-listing power; D confuses it with a payment platform. **Trap:** compliant procedures are necessary but not sufficient for good procurement.

**Q24 — D.** D preserves confidentiality while enabling competent scrutiny. A conceals an award risk; B compromises protected material; C subordinates integrity to price alone. **Trap:** confidentiality governs how to disclose, not whether to warn decision-makers.

### Sourced evidence retained from the previous workbook

- **JAM and institutional boundary:** Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar identity and Mobile notifications supply the payment-access foundation; the Department of Financial Services describes itself as the nodal department for banking, financial services and insurance, **not** as the administrator of PFMS (Department of Expenditure). Account existence does not establish usability.
- **Successive CSS release designs:** The 2021 designated SNA agency/account arrangement made State scheme balances visible. Department of Expenditure OM No. **1(27)/PFMS/2020 dated 13 July 2023** introduced SNA-SPARSH; the **10 June 2025** OM extended it to **37 further CSSs from 1 July 2025**, with notified applicability from **1 November 2025** to all CSSs in States/UTs with legislatures. These dates and the applicability/operational-onboarding distinction were verified by the Basic owner on **13 August 2026**. PFMS–State IFMIS–RBI e-Kuber settlement is the new just-in-time mechanism, not an across-government replacement for all expenditure.
- **Distinct error/accountability sites:** Inclusion error (an ineligible recipient paid), exclusion error (an eligible recipient unpaid), diversion, inefficient purchase and unmet outcome require different evidence and remedies. GFR **2017** sets Union expenditure/procurement procedure; payment-status lookup is not unrestricted disclosure of internal PFMS records. The outcome-budgeting/OOMF link belongs at the final outcome stage, not the payment stage. For local-body finance, Articles **280(3)(bb)** and **280(3)(c)** concern augmentation of State funds for Panchayats and Municipalities on State Finance Commission recommendations; own revenue and State devolution also matter.

### Verified PYQ — 2022 GS-II Q8 (10 marks; 150 words)

> Reforming the government delivery system through the Direct Benefit Transfer Scheme is a progressive step, but it has its limitations too. Comment.

**Model answer:** Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) is progressive because it changes the route of welfare delivery: JAM-enabled identification and bank access allow benefits to reach verified recipients with fewer intermediaries. Deduplication of beneficiary registers, including ration and job-card records, can curb ghost entries; electronic payments also improve traceability and timeliness. DBT includes authenticated in-kind benefits and some service-enabler transfers, not merely cash.

Yet payment accuracy is not targeting accuracy. An outdated eligibility list can omit a deserving household even while every listed payment succeeds. Biometric failure, seeding errors, dormant accounts and distant banking correspondents compound exclusion; cash cannot replace food where markets fail or prices surge. Moreover, deletion of entries is not equivalent to a net welfare gain.

Thus assisted/offline alternatives, named-officer exception handling, periodic list revision, accessible time-bound grievance redress and reporting of wrongful denials alongside savings are indispensable. DBT is a valuable delivery reform, not a substitute for inclusive entitlement design and reliable provision.

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks (about 150 words)

> Distinguish the roles of Direct Benefit Transfer, the Public Financial Management System and the Single Nodal Agency architecture within India's public financial management.

**Model answer:** India's welfare payment chain has three distinct financial layers. Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) concerns delivery to verified recipients: cash, authenticated in-kind benefits and certain service-enabler transfers. By removing fake or duplicate entries it limits identity-based leakage, but cannot repair an obsolete eligibility register.

The Public Financial Management System (PFMS), administered by the Department of Expenditure, follows sanctions, releases and agency expenditure; its visibility can expose idle balances and diversion upstream of the beneficiary. It does not itself determine entitlement or make every utilisation record publicly available.

The 2021 Single Nodal Agency account design made CSS balances in designated State agencies visible. SNA-SPARSH subsequently integrated PFMS, State IFMIS and RBI e-Kuber to support just-in-time release against expenditure claims rather than advance parking. That improves cash discipline but adds State reconciliation obligations; notified applicability must not be confused with verified onboarding everywhere. Within the GFR 2017 procedural framework, these instruments complement rather than replace one another: trace the beneficiary, agency and release stages separately.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks (about 150 words)

> Explain how just-in-time release in the current centrally sponsored scheme architecture alters Centre-State fiscal relations.

**Model answer:** Centrally sponsored schemes require coordination between Union releases, State contributions and expenditure by implementing agencies. Under the earlier SNA account model, designated State-level balances became more visible, but money could remain parked. SNA-SPARSH uses PFMS–State IFMIS–RBI e-Kuber integration to release shares against expenditure claims, reducing idle float and improving Union oversight.

This changes the distribution of risk rather than merely speeding payment. The Union can avoid unnecessary advance transfers and see expenditure more promptly; the State loses some cash buffer and must submit accurate claims and reconcile its share on time. Where interfaces or State data lag, a nominally efficient rail can delay frontline suppliers and recipients. A notified scheme's applicability is not proof that every State has completed onboarding.

Predictable releases, shared dashboards, correction windows for rejected claims and adequate State technical capacity therefore matter as much as the payment rail. Fiscal discipline is desirable, but must be judged by timely services and cooperative intergovernmental implementation, not lower balances alone.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks (about 250 words)

> Critically examine the proposition that Direct Benefit Transfer addresses leakage rather than targeting accuracy.

**Model answer:** DBT changes how a benefit travels, not the criteria deciding who deserves it. JAM provides an identity, bank-account and communication foundation, while authenticated transfers permit beneficiary-level deduplication. For example, removing duplicate ration or job-card records can prevent identity-based leakage and create an auditable payment trail. This is a genuine improvement over opaque intermediated distribution.

But eligibility is decided upstream by programme rules and lists. If a household that meets a pension scheme's conditions is absent from an outdated register, flawless electronic transfer cannot include it. Conversely a listed but newly ineligible person can receive a perfectly executed payment. These are targeting errors, distinct from payment diversion. Tightening authentication may reduce payments to fake identities (inclusion error) while increasing wrongful denials through biometric mismatch, missing seeding or unusable accounts (exclusion error). For subsistence benefits, the latter can cost a family food or wages, rather than merely costing the treasury.

Nor does a credited account assure cash in hand: a distant banking correspondent, network outages or household control of the account matters. Substituting cash for an in-kind benefit can also shift inflation and market-access risks onto the recipient. Reported gross savings from deleted entries should therefore not be presented as measured net welfare improvement.

A better evaluation joins PFMS payment and agency trails with revised eligibility lists, assisted/offline authentication, a named officer empowered to resolve exceptions and time-bound appeal. It should publish both wrongful denial and leakage measures, along with actual service outcomes. DBT efficiently reforms a route; inclusive targeting and adequate provision remain separate duties.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks (about 250 words)

> Examine why reported savings from authenticated benefit transfer cannot by themselves establish that welfare delivery has improved.

**Model answer:** A savings dashboard can record the estimated value of duplicate or ineligible entries removed through authentication. That measures an important reduction in a particular fiscal leakage, not necessarily a net gain to recipients. A scheme can simultaneously remove ghost entries and mistakenly drop eligible persons because of outdated lists or Aadhaar seeding errors. Counting both changes as 'savings' would mistake exclusion for progress.

The measurement problem has several layers. First, an entry deleted is not automatically a payment that would otherwise have occurred each period, so assumptions behind gross counterfactual savings must be stated. Second, where pensioners have dormant accounts or rural workers cannot withdraw money from banking correspondents, successful electronic credit does not equal receipt of usable support. Third, cash of a given face value may buy less food when prices rise or local markets lack supply; money transferred cannot stand in for the availability of services. Fourth, PFMS may show sanction and expenditure without establishing that better health or nutrition was caused by the transfer.

Credible assessment pairs independently checked duplicate-removal records with data on wrongful denials, account-credit and withdrawal failures, grievance resolution time, coverage of eligible households and the intended outcome. Compare conditions before and after carefully and check other influences, rather than attributing all change to DBT. An accessible appeal with a named officer and offline fallback should feed corrected entries back to programme administrators.

Thus savings are a useful fiscal-process signal, bounded by measurement assumptions. Welfare success additionally requires correct targeting, effective last-mile access and demonstrable improvements in beneficiaries' lives.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks (about 300 words)

> Assess India's budget-to-beneficiary chain by locating the distinct failure at each stage and the instrument that addresses it.

**Model answer:** The budget-to-beneficiary chain must be judged at six distinct stages, not from the size of an allocation or the apparent success of a single digital tool.

At **budget**, a sanctioned but unrealistic outlay can promise services without financing viable implementation; transparent estimates and legislative scrutiny test feasibility. At **release**, advances may remain parked or arrive late. The 2021 SNA account design made CSS balances more visible; SNA-SPARSH now links PFMS, State IFMIS and RBI e-Kuber to just-in-time claims and settlement. This reduces float but can strain State cash planning if integration fails.

At the **agency** stage, diversion, idle balances or late reporting obscure actual spending. PFMS, under the Department of Expenditure, follows sanction, payment and utilisation, provided implementing agencies enter reliable data. At **procurement**, weak specifications, tailored bids, cartelisation and poor contract supervision destroy value even without a formally irregular payment. GFR 2017 supplies procedural discipline; competitive design, technical evaluation, conflict disclosures and post-award management supply the missing capacity.

At **beneficiary** level, JAM-enabled DBT can limit ghost payments, but a wrong list, authentication failure or distant banking correspondent can still exclude an eligible claimant. Revise eligibility lists, offer assisted/offline alternatives, and assign time-bound complaint and appeal duties. Finally at **outcome**, even a fully spent, well-tracked allocation may deliver poor nutrition or learning. Outcome budgeting and OOMF should specify meaningful indicators, with independent verification rather than treating payments as impacts.

The architecture trades faster Union fiscal control against State flexibility, digital auditability against privacy, and strict identity checks against access. Track leakage, wrongful exclusion and efficiency separately; involve frontline agencies and publish accessible corrections. An end-to-end chain succeeds only when money reaches the right people in a usable form and produces the intended substantive result.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks (about 300 words)

> Assess whether procedural rules can deliver value for money in public procurement, and state what else is required.

**Model answer:** Procedural rules are necessary safeguards against arbitrariness, but value for money is an outcome: the State must acquire a fit-for-purpose good, work or service at a justified life-cycle cost. The General Financial Rules, 2017 govern Union financial management and procurement within the applicable rules and manuals. Documented sanctions, competitive opportunities, consistent evaluation and audit make a questionable award more detectable; they do not guarantee that what was bought works.

Suppose a district buys a complex diagnostic service using an over-specified tender. A tailored eligibility clause can deter capable competitors even if every form is complete. Conversely selecting the lowest nominal bidder for a quality-dependent service can yield equipment downtime or poor staffing and expensive later repair. Weak post-award contract management allows technically sound tenders to fail during delivery. These failures occur after, as well as before, financial approval.

Value therefore needs procurement capacity: define measurable outputs and life-cycle costs; conduct proportionate market engagement without favouring vendors; invite genuine competition; apply technical and price criteria suitable for the service; document conflicts of interest and disclose material supplier risks to the competent authority through protected channels. Independent complaint handling before award and contract milestones, verification of delivery and proportionate debarment after proven default create further incentives. Small suppliers should not be excluded by needless compliance costs.

Tighter checks can delay urgent purchasing, so risk-based scrutiny and written reasons are preferable to mechanical prohibition. Compare not only savings against estimate but service quality, delivery time, cost overruns and beneficiary outcomes; use audit findings to revise specifications. The GFR sets the integrity floor, not the performance ceiling. Only capable design, fair competition, active contract management and outcome verification convert process compliance into public value.
