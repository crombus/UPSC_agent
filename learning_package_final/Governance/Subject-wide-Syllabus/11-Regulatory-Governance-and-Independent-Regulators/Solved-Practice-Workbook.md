## Original Mains practice — fully worked

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks | about 150 words
**Question:** Discuss the role of the competition regulator in containing the abuse of a dominant position in India.

**Model answer (authored):** The Competition Commission of India (CCI) under the Competition Act, 2002, polices **abuse**, not the mere possession of market power. It first identifies the relevant product and geographic market, assesses dominance and then examines whether conduct such as discriminatory conditions, denial of access or leveraging restricts rivalry. Its investigative and reasoned-order process can require cessation and impose remedies or penalties under the Act; review protects affected parties against overreach. In Indian digital markets, CCI’s Android and app-store billing proceedings against Google show why platform terms and ecosystem dependence warrant market-specific analysis rather than an assumption that a large foreign firm is guilty. A sector regulator’s licence does not itself answer a competition-law allegation; overlapping questions require respecting both statutory remits. However, fast-changing technology, information asymmetry and prolonged challenges can delay effective relief. CCI should combine technical evidence, proportionate remedies and appealable reasoning so consumers and entrants benefit without penalising legitimate scale or innovation.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks | about 150 words
**Question:** Explain why regulatory capture is usually lawful and what disciplines it more effectively than closer ministerial control.

**Model answer (authored):** Regulatory capture describes a shift toward the regulated industry’s interests, not necessarily bribery. Firms often hold the best technical data, meet the regulator repeatedly and can offer former officials attractive careers. Consumers and prospective entrants are dispersed and less able to shape consultations. Each step may be lawful, while the accumulated evidence base and agenda become one-sided. Replacing this with ministerial approval of individual cases invites a different distortion: executive capture, including favour to politically salient incumbents. Better safeguards are transparent draft rules and consultation records; conflict and post-employment disclosures; evidence from entrants, consumers and independent researchers; reasoned orders; published impact assessments; and accessible statutory or judicial review. Appointment safeguards and predictable funding protect investigators from both firms and ministries. Parliament should scrutinise goals, budgets and outcomes, not predetermine individual sanctions. Independence is valuable when paired with open evidence and contestable decisions, rather than treated as either absolute immunity or bureaucratic subordination.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks | about 250 words
**Question:** Examine the autonomy, accountability and expertise trilemma in the design of India’s sectoral regulators.

**Model answer (authored):** Regulators such as SEBI, TRAI, CERC and CCI are assigned technically demanding statutory tasks. The trilemma is not a choice of one virtue against two others: autonomy permits credible decisions, expertise makes decisions competent, and accountability makes delegated power legitimate.

**Autonomy:** Protected tenure, transparent selection and predictable resources can insulate an investigation or licence decision from case-specific political pressure. Yet formal tenure is hollow when staff and money remain contingent on executive approval. Each regulator’s actual protections must be checked in its parent Act; they are not uniform.

**Expertise:** A multidisciplinary staff can define markets, evaluate telecom interconnection or inspect financial risk. Excessive reliance on incumbent-supplied evidence, however, invites lawful regulatory capture and can overlook consumer and entrant costs. Publish evidence, diversify consultation and manage revolving-door conflicts.

**Accountability:** Statutory policy directions, annual reporting, parliamentary scrutiny, consultation, reason-giving and appeals control delegated discretion. But a minister should not dictate the result of a named case: democratic control of sector policy differs from adjudicating individual rights. Conversely, an expert regulator cannot claim immunity from procedural fairness and independent review.

A practical design pairs *each insulation with a check*: secure appointments with conflict declarations; flexible rule-making with consultation and impact assessment; enforcement discretion with recorded reasons and review; funding autonomy with audit and outcome reporting. India’s distinct regulator statutes necessitate sector-specific calibration rather than copying one body’s appointments or appeals across all. The aim is not maximum distance from government but credible technical choices under law, with remedies when either industry or executive influence distorts public interest.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks | about 250 words
**Question:** Examine why comprehensive regulatory-architecture reform meets greater resistance in India than incremental single-sector adjustment.

**Model answer (authored):** A common architecture promises consistent treatment of similar risks, yet reforming an entire regulatory system changes who decides, who pays and who bears transition risk. FSLRC (2013), chaired by Justice B.N. Srikrishna, proposed regulation by activity and a Unified Financial Agency covering most financial regulators while leaving the RBI’s banking and payments remit separate. The proposed agency has **not** been implemented in full; the FMC–SEBI merger in 2015 illustrates a narrower change, not fulfilment of the blueprint.

A cross-sector overhaul would reallocate functions and personnel among SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA and other institutions, reconcile different parent statutes and appeals, migrate data and live cases, and settle conflicting supervisory cultures. Incumbents may defend established jurisdiction; firms fear uncertainty in licences and compliance; consumers may face gaps during transition. A unified supervisor could limit arbitrage where similar products fall under different rules, but could also concentrate mistakes, lose specialist knowledge and underweight local sector risks. Parliamentary enactment, coordination with RBI and credible sequencing thus matter more than a commission recommendation alone.

An incremental reform can target a demonstrable overlap, preserve ongoing decisions and test coordination arrangements without transferring every mandate at once. Its disadvantage is fragmented oversight and residual arbitrage. The better route is staged convergence: map activity and risk, publish a transition plan, protect appeal and enforcement continuity, align disclosure and consumer-protection standards across agencies, then assess results before any larger consolidation. Selective implementation is not proof that the comprehensive diagnosis was wrong, but it is not proof that a partial merger has solved it.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks | about 300 words
**Question:** Assess the claim that the Indian tribunal question is now a question about how members are chosen rather than about whether specialised adjudication is desirable.

**Model answer (authored):** Specialist tribunals remain attractive because technical appeals may demand sector knowledge and faster resolution than already burdened ordinary courts. The decisive qualification is that expertise cannot compensate for adjudicators dependent on a frequent litigant—the executive—for appointment, continuation or resources. Thus the selection question has moved to the centre of constitutional scrutiny, but does not exhaust the policy debate.

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 abolished specified appellate bodies and shifted functions mainly to courts, while providing service and appointment conditions for those remaining. Its rationalisation may reduce duplicative infrastructure, but High Courts may inherit caseload and lose specialist capacity. Whether litigants actually obtain faster, affordable decisions needs court-wise evidence, not an assumption based on the number of tribunals abolished.

The Supreme Court’s judgment of 19 November 2025 (2025 INSC 1330) invalidated re-enacted provisions on minimum age, the two-name recommendation panel and merely preferable appointment timeline, four-year tenure and the impugned allowance framework. It referred to earlier *Madras Bar Association* safeguards as controlling pending compliant legislation and directed establishment of a National Tribunals Commission. These holdings make independence, credible selection and security of service integral to a tribunal’s legitimacy. A direction to establish a commission does not itself establish that it has begun operating; any claim about implementation requires a later check.

Reform should use independently credible selection, predictable tenure and timely appointments; publish vacancies and case-disposal data; preserve reasoned decisions, impartial hearings and judicial supervision; and compare retained specialist tribunals with transferred High Court jurisdiction for delay and accessibility. Too many thinly staffed fora can also fragment justice; merger or transfer may sometimes be justified by demonstrable caseload.

The claim is therefore **largely right, but incomplete**. Proper appointments are a constitutional precondition; jurisdiction design, staffing, budget, litigant access and post-transfer court capacity determine whether the specialist promise materialises. The audited 2025 PYQ belongs to the Polity tribunal owner, not this original exercise.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks | about 300 words
**Question:** Assess how a newly created regulator for an emerging sector should be designed so that it is legitimate rather than merely independent.

**Model answer (authored):** A new regulator must be able to decide technically difficult questions without either ministerial favour or capture by the firms it supervises. Legitimacy comes from authorised, reasoned, reviewable choices, not the word “independent” in its title.

**Mandate:** The parent law should state objectives, regulated activity, jurisdictional boundary with existing bodies, permissible rule-making, investigative and sanction powers, and policy-direction limits. For an Indian digital market, coordinate competition-law issues with CCI rather than presuming an industry licence displaces the Competition Act.

**People and resources:** Use transparent, competence-based appointments, protected but accountable tenure, adequate specialist staff, conflict disclosures and proportionate post-employment restrictions. Predictable funds permit investigations; public audit and parliamentary reporting test use of resources. Protect individual enforcement cases from ministerial instructions while retaining lawful political responsibility for broad policy.

**Rule-making:** Publish draft rules, supporting evidence and compliance-cost assumptions; hear small entrants, consumers and workers as well as established firms; explain departures from submissions. Evaluate effects on innovation, access and distribution: equal nominal compliance can exclude smaller entrants. Review rules when technology or evidence changes. Where urgent interim measures are authorised, provide prompt subsequent hearing.

**Enforcement and remedy:** Use notice, hearing, reasons, proportionate penalties and clear statutory appeal or judicial review. Escalate from corrective notices for curable breaches to stronger authorised sanctions for deliberate or harmful conduct; do not invent crimes through circulars. Publish aggregate service and compliance results, not merely counts of fines.

Finally, periodically audit whether rules achieve statutory purposes, allow affected persons to challenge mistakes, and report gaps to Parliament for legislative correction. Too much insulation risks an unanswerable expert elite; too much control risks executive capture. A regulator earns continuing legitimacy when independent decisions are transparent, proportionate, and reversible on law and evidence.
