### VERIFIED HISTORICAL MAINS PYQ — 2020 GS-I (15 marks)

**Question (routed demand):** The interlinking of rivers can provide viable solutions to droughts, floods and interrupted navigation. Critically examine. (250 words)

**Independent model answer:** River interlinking attempts to transfer water between basins by storage and conveyance. Its merits, however, depend on the season and the particular hazard. A receiving basin may gain dry-season supply if a donor has dependable water after its own irrigation, ecological-flow and downstream obligations. A canal cannot create water in a simultaneous monsoon deficit. A reservoir may attenuate a flood peak only if it has free storage, forecasts and operating rules at the time of the peak; transfers can even shift risk downstream. Navigation requires sustained depth and suitable channel geometry, locks and maintenance: an inter-basin link does not automatically make every river navigable.

The Ken-to-Betwa proposal illustrates the distribution of costs and benefits: a potential receiving-region water gain must be weighed against changes to the donor river, Panna landscape, displacement and the costs of lifting and conveyance. Monsoon variability, sedimentation and evaporation complicate estimates of a fixed perennial surplus. Moreover, states sharing a basin must agree on allocations and environmental safeguards. Watershed restoration, aquifer recharge, demand management, floodplain zoning and targeted navigation investments should be compared against links on the same benefit-cost basis. **Thus, carefully appraised, adaptive links may complement—but cannot substitute for—basin-specific drought, flood and navigation policy.**

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE — FULL MODEL ANSWERS

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain how a river’s energy–load relation creates erosional and depositional landforms. (10 marks; about 150 words.)

**Model answer:** A river’s work reflects flow energy relative to the calibre and quantity of its load. Steep headwater reaches possess a strong vertical component of erosion: hydraulic action and load abrasion deepen V-shaped valleys; differential resistance and plunge-pool scour help waterfalls retreat and leave gorges. Material moves as bed traction, saltation, suspended load or dissolved load, not all by the same mechanism. Where gradient and transport competence decline, large grains settle first. In bends, stronger outer-bank flow undercuts a river cliff while slower inner-bank flow deposits a point bar; a neck cutoff leaves an ox-bow. Overbank floodwater loses competence and leaves coarse natural levees beside a finer floodplain. At a suitable mouth, net sediment input can build a delta, as on the Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna coast. These are tendencies, not immutable age stages: floods, rock resistance, uplift, changing base level and dams can reset incision or sediment delivery.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Differentiate antecedent drainage, superimposed drainage and river capture with diagnostic evidence. (10 marks; about 150 words.)

**Model answer:** Antecedent drainage is older than a later uplift: the channel maintains its path by incision as the structure rises, creating a transverse gorge if incision keeps pace. Its evidence concerns relative timing of uplift and course, not just the existence of a gorge. Superimposed drainage begins on younger cover and retains its inherited alignment after erosion uncovers differently oriented underlying rocks; a cross-structural course plus evidence of vanished cover is its diagnostic combination. Capture is diversion of one stream into another basin, commonly through headward erosion across a divide. A sudden elbow, a wind gap or beheaded valley, and a captor with enlarged discharge can support that reconstruction. Hence antecedence tests persistence through uplift; superimposition tests inheritance from cover; capture tests a changed drainage divide. A transverse stream alone proves none of the three without geological and catchment evidence.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Compare Himalayan and Peninsular drainage on regime, structure, valley form and mouth type. (15 marks; about 250 words.)

**Model answer:** Himalayan and Peninsular drainage differ because relief, water inputs and structural history differ. In the Himalayan system, monsoon rain and snow or glacier melt supply many channels: annual flow is perennial but highly seasonal. Steep mountain reaches incise deep valleys and gorges; downstream, voluminous water and sediment spread onto alluvial plains, where braided reaches, migration, floodplains and delta-building are possible. The Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems are large, though individual tributaries have different regimes. Peninsular rivers largely rely on monsoon rainfall; dry-season flows are often much lower. Older hard-rock terrain and the eastward plateau tilt guide the Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri and Mahanadi towards Bay of Bengal deltas. The Western Ghats divide lies near the west coast; many west-flowing channels are short and steep. Narmada and Tapi are important west-flowing structural-trough exceptions and enter estuarine mouths. Neither system is uniform: tectonics and rock resistance alter incision, dams alter seasonal discharge and sediment, and mouth form depends on tidal energy and sediment balance. Therefore river origin does not, by itself, determine perenniality, navigability or whether a delta will advance.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess why deltas facing the same sea-level trend can advance or retreat differently. (15 marks; about 250 words.)

**Model answer:** A delta’s trajectory is a sediment-budget result, not simply a sea-level thermometer. Sediment supplied by the river and redistributed among distributaries must offset wave and tidal removal, subsidence and relative sea-level rise. Where supply exceeds combined losses, the shoreline can prograde. Where reservoirs trap load, a distributary is deprived of sediment, or waves export it, a sector may erode even as another sector accretes. Local land subsidence increases relative sea-level rise and salinity risk independently of global mean change. The Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta illustrates a complex system: tidal reworking, channel migration, cyclones, subsidence, embankments and upstream sediment changes act together in the Sundarbans; one eroding island does not establish the balance of the whole delta. Compare paired delta sectors with river discharge and suspended-load records, shoreline and elevation measurements, subsidence, wave and tidal conditions and changes in distributary connectivity. Dams may alter supply but do not explain every retreat. A responsible response combines sediment-aware reservoir and embankment operations, maintained distributary connectivity where feasible, mangrove protection and risk-sensitive settlement; do not promise that one intervention reverses coastal change everywhere.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically examine river interlinking for droughts, floods and navigation. (20 marks; about 300 words.)

**Model answer:** A link moves water across a divide; it does not manufacture a seasonally dependable surplus. Drought mitigation requires that the donor basin has transferable water after its domestic, agricultural and environmental requirements, at the time the receiving basin needs it. The links’ canals and storage could improve reliability in some places, but a widespread failed monsoon can reduce both donor and recipient supply. Flood mitigation requires enough unoccupied reservoir volume and timely operation before a flood peak; carrying an average surplus through a canal does not ensure peak attenuation. Navigation, meanwhile, needs sufficient minimum depth, suitable channel dimensions, locks and maintenance. A transfer designed for irrigation cannot by itself guarantee those conditions. The Ken-to-Betwa proposal tests real trade-offs: receiving-area water gains have to be weighed against donor-basin environmental flows, Panna landscape impacts, displacement, energy use and inter-state coordination. Changing sediment delivery can also affect downstream floodplains and mouths. Compare such schemes with watershed management, groundwater recharge, irrigation efficiency, demand control, floodplain zoning and targeted navigation infrastructure under the same seasonal and ecological assumptions. Transparent basin data, joint monitoring and adaptable allocation rules are necessary because climate variability can invalidate a fixed surplus estimate. The verdict is conditional: a demonstrably viable link may supplement—but cannot replace—a portfolio tailored separately to drought, floods and navigation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Design integrated basin governance for Indian rivers under climatic and sediment uncertainty. (20 marks; about 300 words.)

**Model answer:** Governance must follow the catchment rather than a single dam or administrative border. Begin with seasonal basin water accounts: measure precipitation, river flow, groundwater, withdrawals and environmental flows in the headwaters, tributaries and downstream reaches. Couple these with sediment surveys upstream of reservoirs, along braided and meandering floodplains and at the coast; a storage gain upstream may mean sediment starvation downstream. Joint state–centre basin forums should publish transparent forecasts and agree on drought priorities, flood-warning thresholds and reservoir-release rules rather than extrapolate one historical annual mean. Test climate sensitivity through several wet and dry scenarios. In flood-prone alluvial plains, protect room for channel migration, avoid avoidable occupation of the active floodplain and maintain early-warning and evacuation links; embankments alone can relocate hazards. In water-stressed areas, compare demand management, irrigation efficiency, aquifer recharge and watershed restoration before authorising costly transfers. At deltas, evaluate relative sea-level rise, subsidence and sediment distribution together. Where links or dams are proposed, appraise downstream releases, habitat connectivity, displacement and cumulative effects, including the Ken–Betwa and Panna landscape test. Publish monitoring results and periodically revise rules when flows, sediment and land use change. The central principle is adaptive allocation of water, space and sediment while preserving livelihoods and ecosystems.
