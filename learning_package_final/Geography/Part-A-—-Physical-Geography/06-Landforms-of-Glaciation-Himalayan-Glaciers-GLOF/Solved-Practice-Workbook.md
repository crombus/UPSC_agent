### VERIFIED HISTORICAL MAINS PYQ — 2023 GS-I (10 marks; 150 words)

**Demand:** How are fjords formed? Why do they constitute some of the most picturesque areas of the world?

**Independent model answer:** A fjord begins as a mountain river valley subsequently occupied by a thick valley glacier. Basal abrasion and plucking widen, straighten and overdeepen the valley into a steep-sided U-shaped trough; tributary glaciers commonly erode less deeply, leaving hanging side valleys. When the ice retreats and the sea enters the overdeepened lower trough, a long, narrow marine inlet results. A shallower rock threshold near the outlet may survive where erosion differed along the ice path. The scenery arises from strong relief contrast: sheer rock walls rise beside deep water, tributary waterfalls descend from hanging valleys, and glacially shaped headwalls, snow and vegetation frame the inlet. Norway’s fjords are a well-known example. A ria differs because it is a drowned **river-cut** valley without the characteristic large glacial U-shaped cross section. Picturesqueness is a perceptual description; the examinable explanation is the sequence of ice erosion followed by marine inundation.

### VERIFIED HISTORICAL MAINS PYQ — 2020 GS-I (10 marks; 150 words)

**Demand:** How will the melting of Himalayan glaciers have a far-reaching impact on the water resources of India?

**Independent model answer:** Himalayan glacier ice stores precipitation and releases meltwater, supplementing rainfall in river headwaters. An initial period of faster melt may temporarily increase warm-season runoff; sustained net ice loss eventually reduces the ice reservoir and can weaken its buffering contribution, particularly in dry months and highly glacier-fed headwaters. Retreat can expose basins that impound meltwater behind ice or moraine dams: failure may cause a glacial-lake outburst flood and damage downstream water-supply, hydropower or irrigation works. Sediment pulses can change channel stability and reservoir performance. Impacts differ among tributaries because monsoon precipitation, snowpack, altitude, groundwater and storage have different weights. Thus melting does not imply that the Ganga or every Himalayan river immediately runs dry; it changes timing, reliability and hazard exposure. Basin-wise monitoring, early warning and adaptive storage and demand management are more defensible than a universal flow prediction.

## ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE — FULL MODEL ANSWERS

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain glacier mass balance and why an ice stream can flow forward while its snout retreats. (10 marks; 150 words.)

**Model answer:** Glacier mass balance is accumulation minus ablation over a chosen interval. Snow compaction supplies ice above the equilibrium line, while melting and other losses dominate lower down. Ice remains a gravity-driven deforming body: particles can continue downhill even if more ice melts at the snout than is replaced by incoming flow. Hence a retreating terminus is a changing boundary, not ice moving uphill. Sustained negative balance usually reduces stored mass and often leads to retreat, but the response can lag and varies with valley shape and debris. Thin debris can absorb heat while thick cover can insulate ice, so a single photograph of snout position cannot quantify annual basin runoff. Monitor accumulation, surface elevation, flow and melt over time.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Differentiate principal erosional and depositional valley-glacier landforms. (10 marks; 150 words.)

**Model answer:** Ice abrades rock with embedded clasts and plucks blocks from fractured bedrock. At valley heads, this cuts cirques; competing cirques sharpen an arête or horn. Thick valley ice straightens and deepens a former river valley into a U-shaped trough, truncating spurs; weaker tributary glaciers can leave hanging valleys. The same ice carries a mixed load of boulders and fines. When it melts, unsorted till may form lateral, medial or terminal moraines according to the depositional position. Meltwater behaves differently: flowing water sorts sand and gravel into outwash and can leave a sinuous esker in a former ice tunnel. Thus location alone does not identify a glacial deposit: cross section, clast sorting, erosional surface and flow geometry must all be checked.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Explain fjord formation and distinguish fjords from rias. (15 marks; 250 words.)

**Model answer:** The fjord sequence is a pre-existing river valley, intense valley-glacier erosion, ice retreat and marine flooding. Basal abrasion and plucking widen and overdeepen the main valley into a U-shaped trough with steep rock walls, truncated spurs and, where tributary ice cuts less deeply, hanging side valleys. After deglaciation, seawater fills the lower trough, giving a long, narrow inlet; a shallower threshold can survive near the seaward end. Norway’s fjords display the steep wall–deep water–waterfall contrast. By contrast, a ria is a drowned river valley, generally preserving fluvial branching and a V-shaped cross section rather than the strongly overdeepened U-shaped glacial trough. Both are marine inlets, but marine drowning alone does not prove glaciation. Look for ice-shaped cross sections, striations, hanging valleys and associated moraines to discriminate. Bedrock resistance, ice thickness and sea-level history alter local form; no single threshold depth is diagnostic.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess far-reaching effects of Himalayan glacier loss on Indian water resources. (15 marks; 250 words.)

**Model answer:** Glaciers store winter precipitation and release meltwater into headwater channels, supplementing but not replacing the monsoon. Accelerated melt can first increase runoff from stored ice, then diminish the available ice reservoir and weaken dry-season buffering. Outcomes depend on glacier coverage, altitude, snow accumulation, rainfall and groundwater in each sub-basin; a uniform percentage for all Himalayan rivers would be unsound. Retreat can expose overdeepened depressions and impounded moraine lakes. Avalanches, slope failure or overtopping may breach a barrier and send a flood into narrow valleys, damaging bridges, hydropower, irrigation and water intakes. Meltwater and flood sediment can impair reservoirs and change channel behaviour. In some places altered flow timing matters more to agriculture than annual total discharge. Evaluate basin-specific measurements of mass balance, snowpack and flows; inventory growing lakes, model inundation, improve warnings and plan releases and demand with climate uncertainty. Neither immediate drying of all rivers nor certain GLOFs from every retreating glacier follow from the evidence.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Analyse GLOF risk as cryospheric change, geomorphic instability and valley-floor development interact. (20 marks; 300 words.)

**Model answer:** Retreating ice may leave a deep hollow that fills with meltwater behind a moraine or ice barrier. Continued melt can enlarge the lake, but a large lake alone is not proof of imminent failure. Rock or ice avalanche into water, intense precipitation, overtopping and erosion of a weak dam are possible trigger-to-breach pathways. An outburst moves rapidly down steep valleys, may entrain sediment and damage crossings and hydropower intakes. Risk depends jointly on breach hazard, downstream exposure and vulnerability: two lakes of similar size need not cause similar losses. South Lhonak in Sikkim is a useful case for mapping lake–channel–settlement connections, without inventing a particular initiating cause or event date from general geomorphology. Inventory lake growth using repeat imagery, measure dam and slope condition, model downstream pathways and warnings, prohibit or redesign high-exposure structures and train evacuation. Structural lake lowering where justified is one measure, not a substitute for observation and preparedness. State claims about triggers only when event-specific evidence exists.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Design Himalayan cryosphere governance integrating observation, warning, land use and infrastructure. (20 marks; 300 words.)

**Model answer:** First maintain an accessible inventory of glacier extent, mass balance, snow cover and proglacial lakes, with repeated field checks where remote sensing is uncertain. Map unstable slopes, moraine geometry and downstream channel constrictions; model multiple breach and rain-on-snow scenarios rather than fix one forecast. Join upstream observations to usable downstream warning triggers and communications, then rehearse evacuation with communities. Land-use plans should avoid the highest exposure on narrow valley floors and river terraces. Design roads, bridges and hydropower intakes for debris-laden floods, with safe shutdown and redundant access. Coordinate across basin and state boundaries, publish uncertainty and review conditions as lakes and glaciers evolve. South Lhonak illustrates the need to check a named lake’s actual location and event-specific evidence before attributing a single cause. Meanwhile glacier mass loss alters seasonal water supply even where no outburst occurs: irrigation and reservoir operation must reflect measured rain, snow and melt contributions. Monitor effectiveness after each season and update thresholds; reducing exposure is as essential as attempting to stabilise one dam.
