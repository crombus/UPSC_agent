## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### TRANSPARENT ZERO-DIRECT-PYQ AUDIT

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why Mediterranean climates receive winter rain and experience summer drought. Answer in about 150 words.

**Independent model solution:**

Cs occurs mainly on warm-temperate western continental margins, illustrated by the Mediterranean basin, California and central Chile. In summer the subtropical high shifts poleward, producing descending, stable air, clear skies and drought. In winter the high moves equatorward; westerlies and their travelling depressions reach the coast, bringing frontal rain and generally mild conditions. The essential *sequence* is belt migration, not a monsoon reversal or a permanent absence of rain. Maritime location and local relief change temperatures and rainfall amounts, while the identifying regime remains summer dry and winter wet. This mechanism also explains why Mediterranean scrub conserves summer water and why winter cereals differ from irrigated summer fruit cultivation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish Sirocco, Mistral and Bora as local Mediterranean winds. Answer in about 150 words.

**Independent model solution:**

The Sirocco carries hot, dusty air from the Sahara toward Mediterranean lands and can raise temperature and deposit dust. The Mistral, in contrast, is a cold, often strong wind channelled down the Rhône valley toward the Mediterranean; it is not another hot desert wind. The Bora is a cold local wind affecting the Adriatic margin. These are local or regional winds superimposed on the wider seasonal Cs circulation. In an exam table compare **origin, direction/route, temperature and weather effect** rather than simply memorise three names. Do not treat their occasional occurrence as the cause of the entire winter-rain climate: subtropical-high displacement and westerly depressions remain the general explanation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Explain how Mediterranean vegetation is adapted to summer drought and fire. Answer in about 250 words.

**Independent model solution:**

Hot, dry summers under the subtropical high produce soil-water stress. Mediterranean maquis, California chaparral and Australian mallee are regional scrub/woodland expressions with small leathery or waxy leaves, deep roots and often thick bark; these traits limit transpiration and tap or protect scarce moisture. Winter rain and milder spring conditions allow seasonal growth, whereas a continuous rainforest-like shady canopy is not typical. Wet periods can build plant fuel; summer desiccation, heat, ignition and wind then raise wildfire danger. Some vegetation recovers from fire, but a changed frequency or intensity can damage soil, settlements and biodiversity. Do not attribute every single fire to climate change without evidence: fuel management and human ignition also matter. The causal answer connects seasonal circulation → moisture deficit → plant form → fire exposure.

Deep rooting gives some plants access to residual soil water, while bark protects stems against drying and some fire exposure; no trait makes shrubs immune to extreme heat or repeated fires. Natural wildland fuel conditions also depend on management: abandonment can increase connected fuel, whereas indiscriminate clearing can raise erosion during winter downpours. Use the annual cycle—winter water input, spring plant growth, summer desiccation—rather than label maquis or chaparral as always fire-prone. Local winds can accelerate spread without causing the general summer drought. A vegetation cross-section should contrast open sunlit shrub crowns with Af's dark closed rainforest floor.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Why is Mediterranean agriculture dominated by orchards, vines and high-value horticulture? Answer in about 250 words.

**Independent model solution:**

The Cs regime combines winter rain with sunny but dry summers, creating a specialised agricultural calendar. Wheat and barley can use cool-season moisture; deep-rooted olives, vines and other fruit trees persist through drier months. Citrus and summer vegetables benefit from sunshine but often need irrigation and managed storage when evaporation and urban/tourism demand peak. The Mediterranean basin, California and central Chile illustrate broadly similar fruit-and-vine specialisation, not identical ownership or productivity. Leong's historical world-wine share must not be quoted as a current statistic. Water costs, drought, wildfire and erosion after intense winter rain limit expansion. The strongest conclusion is that seasonal climate favours horticulture when institutions, capital, market access and sustainable water supply make that choice viable—not that climate by itself determines every farmer's crop.

On sloping ground, terraces or careful soil cover help retain the winter rain that fuels later crops; storage and water-efficient irrigation bridge the summer deficit. Land tenure, access to export markets and transport networks help explain why a California enterprise and a small Mediterranean-basin farm differ despite a shared climate. Vines and orchards require time to establish, creating risk under prolonged drought or wildfire. Comparing the Cape and southwest Australia also shows convergence in climate-adapted crop *types* without implying identical species or identical market structure. Conclude by weighing horticultural value against finite water, urban demand and soil conservation rather than treating the winter-rain pattern as a guarantee of prosperity.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Compare classic Mediterranean agriculture with India's Himalayan fruit-belt analogue. Answer in about 300 words.

**Independent model solution:**

Classic Cs landscapes occupy western continental margins around 30°–45° and face summer subtropical-high drought and winter westerly rain. This supports vines, olive and citrus orchards with winter cereals and managed summer irrigation. India has no extensive equivalent Cs region in the source; Kashmir Valley, Kullu–Shimla and Uttarakhand hills instead support temperate apples and other fruit through appropriate winter chilling, growing-season conditions and sheltered sites. Maharashtra's Nashik–Sangli grape belt offers a *viticulture* analogue under a different rainfall regime. Thus similarity of orchard output does not prove identity of climatic mechanism. Indian fruit growers also face hail, frost pockets, transport delays and perishability; summer water competition and fire are prominent Cs concerns. Compare crop strategy and market dependence while keeping winter chilling, monsoon exposure and western-margin pressure-belt seasonality distinct.

Add a seasonal comparison diagram: Cs summer high → water stress for vines/olives, winter westerlies → rain for cereals; Himalayan winter cold → chilling for apples, warmer-season temperatures and local rain → orchard growth. The same crop-family label cannot replace a rainfall calendar. In the Himalayan belt, cold-air drainage creates frost pockets on some valley floors, and hail or unseasonal rain threaten quality; roads, grading and cold storage affect market returns. Nashik/Sangli grape systems involve their own management and cannot be mapped onto Mediterranean winter rainfall without a local climate check. This is an analogy of specialised high-value horticulture, not of global circulation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Design a climate-resilient strategy for India's apple economy without treating current anomalies as a climate classification. Answer in about 300 words.

**Independent model solution:**

For Kashmir Valley, Kullu–Shimla and Uttarakhand hill orchards, first map cultivar-specific winter chilling and growing-season suitability rather than declare every warmer year a new Köppen climate. Warmer winters may impair bud-break at some elevations, while hail, late frost, rain or fog near maturity and valley-floor cold-air pooling threaten fruit quality and yield. Promote locally tested varieties and rootstocks, slope-sensitive siting, orchard protection where appropriate and water-efficient management, with trials before costly shifts in elevation. Apples are perishable: repair roads and cold-chain bottlenecks and improve producer access to markets; climatic suitability alone does not secure returns. Track dated local weather and horticultural yields by cultivar and altitude, and compare farmer income and water use, not just planting area. Distinguish an observed anomaly, a long-term trend and a formal climate class; avoid unsupported counts of affected farmers or precise upslope migration.

For each proposed orchard adaptation, assess trade-offs: a new cultivar may require a different chilling profile and market acceptance; shifting upslope can harm forest, water supply or communities if land-use change is unmanaged; irrigation may solve a short dry spell but heighten pressure on shared sources. Weather forecasts can guide hail protection and harvest timing, while long-run trials reveal whether winter chilling is shifting enough to change suitable sites. Track price, wastage and transport outages separately from fruit yield to diagnose income losses. Formal Köppen classification requires multi-year monthly climate evidence, not one adverse harvest or a policy headline about apple imports.
