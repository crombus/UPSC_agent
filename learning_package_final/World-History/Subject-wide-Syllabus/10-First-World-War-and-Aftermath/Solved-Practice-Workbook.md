### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why did the western front become a war of attrition? Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

The western front became a war of attrition because the failure of a quick decision met a battlefield on which defence was stronger than attack. At the Marne in September 1914, the German advance was checked; the failure of rapid victory left armies facing each other in entrenched positions.

Machine-guns, heavy artillery and fixed defences could punish advancing infantry faster than attackers could move reserves, supplies and communications across damaged ground. Even local gains rarely became strategic breakthroughs. At Verdun and the Somme in 1916, prolonged offensives inflicted enormous losses for limited territorial change: the object increasingly became to wear down the opponent's manpower and endurance. Industrial output, transport and the ability to replace casualties therefore mattered alongside battlefield skill.

Stalemate was not universal: the eastern front remained more mobile, and later coordination and new methods helped restore movement in 1918. But in the west the interaction of the Marne's failed quick-war outcome and a persistent defensive advantage made attrition the dominant, not the only, mode of fighting.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why did unrestricted submarine warfare fail strategically? Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Unrestricted submarine warfare failed strategically because a campaign that endangered Allied shipping neither forced Britain out nor prevented an even stronger coalition from forming. Britain retained command of the sea and blockaded the Central Powers' supplies. Jutland was tactically indecisive, but it did not break British naval control; Germany consequently sought to attack commerce with U-boats instead of winning a surface-fleet decision.

Germany resumed unrestricted attacks in January 1917. The gamble threatened Britain's maritime lifeline, yet the Allied convoy system reduced shipping losses and improved the survival of essential supplies. Meanwhile attacks on shipping helped bring the United States into the war in April 1917. American finance, food, shipping and morale sustained the Allies, with troops arriving in increasing strength later. Thus a weapon intended to break the blockade instead amplified Germany's resource disadvantage.

The campaign was a serious tactical threat, not an immediate or inevitable failure: without effective convoy protection it might have done greater damage. Its strategic failure lay in the contrast between temporary disruption at sea and the enduring American entry and Allied capacity it helped provoke.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Why did the Central Powers lose? Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

The Central Powers lost through cumulative exhaustion across land, sea and coalition politics, not because of a single decisive battle. The failure of the Schlieffen Plan left Germany facing the western front and Russia simultaneously, while Britain's blockade constrained supplies and civilian endurance. Jutland did not end British naval control. Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917 threatened Allied shipping, but convoys blunted it and the gamble helped bring the United States into war in April. American food, finance, shipping and morale, followed by troops, enlarged Allied staying power.

Russia's Bolshevik armistice and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 formally removed Russia, temporarily freeing German forces for a western decision. Yet the spring Ludendorff offensive exhausted German reserves without forcing victory. Improved Allied coordination under Foch, followed by counterattacks and the breaking of the Hindenburg Line, converted Germany's failed opportunity into military retreat. Bulgaria, Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire then collapsed before Germany: a coalition could not survive once its weaker partners ceased to hold their fronts.

These factors reinforced one another. The blockade and two-front burden weakened the system over time; American entry offset Russia's exit, and Allied operational recovery exploited Germany's final gamble. The armistice of 11 November 1918 marked military cessation, not the later Versailles peace or mandates. Germany sought it before deep occupation of German soil. Defeat was therefore a military and political collapse under interacting pressures, rather than a victory attributable solely to US troops, blockade or Foch.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the First World War as total war. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

The First World War was a “total war” because belligerent states mobilised their economies and societies as well as their armies. Its scale mattered less than the way warfare made industry, food, labour and civilians parts of military strategy. States directed production and transport; machine-guns, artillery, submarines and other industrial weapons made sustained manufacturing essential. Women entered factory and other wartime work on a larger scale, while governments used propaganda and controls to sustain morale and supplies. Imperial manpower broadened the conflict beyond Europe, making colonial populations contributors rather than spectators.

At the same time, Britain's blockade exposed civilians in the Central Powers to scarcity; attacking the opponent's capacity to provision its population became a strategic instrument. Such pressures linked the home front to the battlefield without making their experiences identical. Prolonged mobilisation also strained political legitimacy. Russia's revolutions installed Bolshevik rule; Germany's revolution led to the Weimar Republic, while the Habsburg and Ottoman imperial orders disintegrated. The relative position of the United States and Japan strengthened as European powers emerged weakened.

Total mobilisation did not mean that every society was mobilised equally or that imperialism ended in 1918. The later League of Nations introduced a peacekeeping experiment, but German colonies and former Ottoman territories became mandates: international trusteeship language coexisted with continuing imperial control. Thus “total” identifies the war's penetration of state, society and empire; the political and territorial changes were consequences of that mobilisation and defeat, not a definition of total war itself.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically evaluate the post-1919 peace settlement. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

The post-1919 settlement achieved a formal peace and created institutions, but its mixture of security, national self-determination and victor control left an unstable order. The fighting had stopped under the armistice of 11 November 1918; legal settlement followed in several distinct treaties. Versailles dealt with Germany, St Germain with Austria, Trianon with Hungary and Neuilly with Bulgaria. Sèvres addressed Turkey, but Turkish nationalists under Mustafa Kemal rejected it; Lausanne in 1923 recognised Turkish sovereignty over eastern Thrace and Anatolia, including Smyrna/Izmir. This revision showed that treaty terms depended on political and military capacity to enforce them.

Versailles returned Alsace-Lorraine to France and gave West Prussia and Posen to Poland; Danzig became a free city and the Saar came under League administration. It forbade union with Austria, limited Germany's army to 100,000, prohibited conscription, tanks, submarines and military aircraft, and demilitarised the Rhineland. These terms answered French security fears and some national claims, yet left minorities and revisionist grievances. Article 231 established liability for Allied loss and damage as the legal basis for reparations, but became politically notorious as the “war-guilt clause”; reparations were fixed in 1921 at £6,600 million.

The League of Nations represented an attempt at collective order. Nevertheless, German colonies and former Ottoman lands became mandates administered under conditional trusteeship, retaining imperial power despite professed preparation for self-government. Neither simple vindictiveness nor inevitable failure captures the result: some territorial settlements reflected real national aspirations, and later Depression-era crises and political choices intensified the breakdown. The settlement embittered Germany without making it permanently powerless; its durability depended on sustained enforcement or reconciliation that the victors did not consistently provide.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** How did the First World War transform Europe and the wider world? Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

The First World War transformed the world through total mobilisation, regime collapse and a disputed peace, but did not end empire or settle nationality. On the battlefield, industrial weapons made production and manpower decisive; at home, state controls, propaganda and food policy brought civilian life into military strategy. Women worked in factories on a larger scale, imperial subjects supplied manpower, and Britain's blockade exposed civilians to hardship. These experiences altered arguments over the state's economic responsibilities and women's place in society, although changes varied by country and did not automatically bring equality.

Defeat and exhaustion destroyed the Habsburg, Ottoman, Russian and German imperial orders. Bolshevik rule followed the Russian revolutions; Germany's revolution produced the Weimar Republic; the Habsburg lands became successor states. These were not identical outcomes: the Turkish nationalist movement under Mustafa Kemal forced Sèvres to be revised at Lausanne in 1923. The armistice of 11 November 1918 had ended fighting before these diplomatic outcomes were fixed. Italy's postwar crisis would later be exploited by Mussolini, but the war did not predetermine his victory.

The League of Nations offered a new peacekeeping framework, while the Versailles-led treaty cluster redrew borders and left minorities and revisionist grievances. German colonies and former Ottoman territories became mandates; Britain held Transjordan and Palestine and France held Syria. Although formally framed as preparation for self-government, these arrangements preserved European imperial authority. Thus the land empires of the defeated fell even as the victorious maritime empires acquired further responsibilities. Europe's human and economic losses strengthened the relative position of the United States and Japan, without instantly replacing European dominance everywhere.

The war's broad legacy was a new political and international order, not a final resolution: total-war governance, contested national borders and the tension between self-determination and mandates remained to shape the interwar world.
