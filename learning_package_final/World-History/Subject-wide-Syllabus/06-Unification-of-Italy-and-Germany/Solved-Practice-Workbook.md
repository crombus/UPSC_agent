### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why was Piedmont-Sardinia decisive in Italian unification? Answer in about 150 words.

**Model thesis:** It supplied the diplomatic, administrative and military state frame that converted Mazzini's legitimacy and Garibaldi's mobilisation into a recognised monarchy.

**Claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification:**

- Giuseppe Mazzini founded Young Italy in 1831 and gave republican, unitary nationalism an organised ideological voice.
- Piedmont-Sardinia, strengthened at Vienna by Genoa, became the functioning state nucleus of Italian unification.

**Detailed examiner-grade model answer:**

Piedmont-Sardinia converted nationalism into a state-building project. Strengthened by Genoa after Vienna, its government and army could lead a divided peninsula.

Mazzini's Young Italy, founded in 1831, gave republican unity an organised voice, but not the means to expel Austria. Cavour combined diplomacy with French military assistance in 1859 to defeat Austria and secure Lombardy. Garibaldi's Red Shirts conquered Sicily and Naples in 1860; their popular-military success extended Cavour's northern campaign. Piedmont's institutions absorbed these gains: the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed under Victor Emmanuel II in 1861.

This leadership was indispensable, not exclusive. Popular mobilisation and foreign help mattered, while the resulting monarchy did not realise Mazzini's republic. Nor was unification territorially complete in 1861: Venetia joined in 1866 and Rome in 1870. Piedmont was decisive because it connected disparate nationalist forces to a viable state, not because it acted alone.

**Detailed examiner-grade model answer:**

The Seven Weeks' War of 1866 resolved the leadership question that Vienna's thirty-nine-state German Confederation had left open. Prussia's defeat of Austria ended Austrian dominance in German affairs and cleared the way for the Prussian-led North German Confederation.

This result gave unification a specific territorial and constitutional direction: a “small-German” arrangement excluding Austria, rather than a state embracing every German-speaking land. It also shifted the centre of decision-making from liberal assemblies toward Prussia's monarchy, army and Bismarck's diplomacy. The earlier joint Prussian-Austrian campaign against Denmark in 1864 had opened the Schleswig-Holstein issue; the struggle between the two victors in 1866 settled which of them would direct the German project.

Yet the war did not itself complete national unity. Southern German states joined the final process in the context of war with France in 1870–71, and the Empire was proclaimed under William I at Versailles in January 1871. Thus 1866 was decisive for who would lead and which Germany would emerge, not the date at which a united empire already existed.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Detailed examiner-grade model answer:**

Mazzini, Cavour and Garibaldi contributed different, sometimes conflicting, means to Italian unification. Mazzini shaped its national purpose; Cavour constructed a viable interstate route; Garibaldi expanded the territory that could be united. No single figure both conceived and completed the process.

Mazzini founded Young Italy in 1831 and argued for a unified republic. His organised language of national belonging challenged Vienna's division of the peninsula, but the failures of revolutionary action, including in 1848, showed the limits of relying on ideals and insurrection alone. Cavour worked instead through the existing monarchy of Piedmont-Sardinia. His diplomacy secured French military support in the 1859 struggle against Austria and brought Lombardy into Piedmont's orbit. He used a functioning state and an external ally; he was not trying to realise Mazzini's republican programme.

Garibaldi supplied the popular-military element unavailable to diplomatic negotiation. In 1860 his Red Shirts conquered Sicily and Naples, extending unification to the south. Yet the southern conquest did not become an independent republican state. It was incorporated into the monarchy, and the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861 under Victor Emmanuel II. This outcome demonstrates both the importance of popular action and its political absorption by the Piedmontese state.

Their roles therefore intersected rather than forming three interchangeable stages: ideological legitimacy, diplomacy backed by armies, and volunteer conquest each answered a different obstacle. Unification remained incomplete in 1861—Venetia and Rome joined later—and it did not remove all regional divides. The Italian nation-state was a compromise between nationalism from below and consolidation from above.
