### PYQ DEMAND CARD 1 — 2021 GS-I · 15 marks · 250 words

**Demand:** Evaluate the challenge to the democratic state system between the two World Wars.

**Status:** Verified neutral rendering from the audited local routing ledger; not claimed as verbatim.

**Model solution:**

Interwar democracy was challenged both by authoritarian movements and by the failure of democratic institutions to retain social and elite support. In Italy, disappointment with the postwar settlement, inflation, strikes and factory occupations made parliamentary coalitions appear unable to preserve order. Property-owners and other conservatives feared the left; fascist violence and propaganda supplied an alternative. Yet Mussolini did not defeat the state in battle: in October 1922 King Victor Emmanuel III refused resistance to the March on Rome and invited him into office.

Germany's Weimar Republic carried the Versailles stigma and the “stab-in-the-back” myth. Fragmented coalitions, 1923 hyperinflation and the Depression after 1929 undermined confidence. Nazi mobilisation exploited unemployment and fear of communism, but never secured an overall electoral majority. Conservative elites enabled Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January 1933; the Enabling Law of March then converted an office gained legally into dictatorial power. The challenge thus combined pressure from below with surrender from above.

It was not a uniform fascist takeover. In Japan the Manchurian seizure of 1931 and Prime Minister Inukai's assassination in 1932 accompanied army dominance within surviving imperial institutions, not an Italian-style party revolution. Spain's republic confronted deep social division before civil war in 1936; Franco's victory in 1939 established another distinct authoritarian route. These cases show how economic insecurity, anti-left alliances and coercion could disable democratic checks, but their different institutions and routes prevent treating the interwar democratic crisis as an inevitable, single-model collapse.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why did Mussolini reach office in 1922? Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Mussolini reached office because postwar discontent created an audience for fascism and Italy's established elites chose not to defend parliamentary government. The disappointment of “mutilated victory”, inflation, debt and unemployment weakened faith in coalition cabinets. Strikes and factory occupations alarmed property-owners and business interests; fascist squads used anti-socialist violence to present themselves as defenders of order. Parts of the Church and a divided opposition also helped the movement gain room to manoeuvre.

Mussolini's Fasci began in 1919 and his National Fascist Party was organised in 1921, but electoral mobilisation alone did not install him. The March on Rome in October 1922 was more bluff than military conquest. King Victor Emmanuel III refused to authorise resistance and invited Mussolini to form a government: a transfer from the existing state, not its defeat in battle.

Economic crisis and class fear explain the rise in support; royal and elite decisions explain the precise transfer of office. Without that institutional concession, a march alone need not have produced the premiership.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Why is Japan not safely described as fascist in the strict sense? Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

Japan resembled interwar fascist states in extreme nationalism, anti-parliamentary pressure and external aggression, but its route and institutional form differed. Economic hardship, political corruption and hostility to civilian parties strengthened the army. Its occupation of Manchuria in 1931 showed military initiative abroad; the assassination of Prime Minister Inukai in 1932 marked the weakening of civilian authority. Army leaders increasingly dominated policy without creating an Italian- or German-style mass fascist party that seized the state.

Imperial and constitutional forms survived, even as their practical operation bent to military priorities. In 1937 full-scale war in China extended the army-led expansion that later fed into the Pacific conflict. Coercion and expansion therefore establish a family resemblance; they do not prove identical ideological organisation or a single leader-party regime.

“Military authoritarianism” is the safer label. It captures effective army dominance while leaving room to compare Japanese nationalism with fascism without erasing the different social base and mechanism of takeover.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Compare Italian fascism and German Nazism. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

Italian fascism and German Nazism shared anti-communism, extreme nationalism, leader-centred propaganda and hostility to parliamentary pluralism. Both built single-party rule and controlled labour, education and the public sphere. Their similarities, however, should not conceal the different ideological and institutional weight of race.

Mussolini's movement promoted national unity, discipline, autarky and violence. The Acerbo Law helped it dominate parliament, while the Corporate State banned strikes and channelled labour through state-controlled institutions. The Lateran Treaty of 1929 accommodated the papacy, showing that an independent focus of loyalty remained. Fascist youth organisation and the cult of il Duce widened influence, but the king and Church continued to limit complete state penetration. Anti-Jewish measures arrived only in 1938–39.

Nazism joined national rebirth and militarisation to Aryan supremacy and anti-Semitism as defining principles. Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933; the March Enabling Law opened the way to rapid destruction of opposition. Trade unions were crushed; Hitler Youth and schools, Goebbels's propaganda, police and camps carried control deeper into everyday life. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 institutionalised racial exclusion early in Nazi rule, unlike Italy's later turn.

Both regimes used consent, spectacle and coercion, but the Italian dictatorship was less systematically racialised and never eliminated all competing authorities. Nazism made biological race constitutive of the state and pursued more thorough, brutal control. Calling both authoritarian is justified; treating their ideology and practice as identical is not.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** How did interwar democracies enable authoritarian takeover? Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

Interwar democracies enabled authoritarian takeover when economic and political crisis undermined legitimacy and existing elites supplied the institutional keys to power. Neither Italy nor Germany was simply conquered by a successful armed rebellion. In Italy, postwar disappointment, inflation, debt, unemployment and fear of socialist revolution discredited fragmented coalitions. Fascist squads attacked opponents, yet the March on Rome in October 1922 was more bluff than a battlefield victory. King Victor Emmanuel III refused resistance and invited Mussolini to govern.

Weimar Germany faced a different sequence. Versailles humiliation and the “stab-in-the-back” myth tainted the republic; fragmented coalitions and political violence limited its authority. Hyperinflation in 1923 damaged confidence, recovery relied on American loans after 1924, and the Depression after 1929 brought mass unemployment. Nazi organisation and propaganda attracted voters, but the party never won an overall electoral majority. Conservative intrigue secured Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January 1933; the Enabling Law of March 1933 then gave him the legal instrument to dismantle constitutional restraint.

In both countries, grievance and violence worked from below while established authorities opened the door from above. Differences matter: Italy's king made the immediate transfer; German conservative bargaining and a legislative act carried an elected republic into dictatorship. Economic distress did not make these outcomes inevitable; elite choices, weak opposition and the willingness to trade pluralism for anti-left order converted vulnerability into takeover.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Evaluate the challenge to the democratic state system between the World Wars. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

The challenge to the democratic state system between the wars came from a common crisis of confidence, but it took distinct national forms. In Italy, disappointment at the “mutilated victory”, debt, inflation, unemployment, labour unrest and fear of socialism damaged weak coalition cabinets. Fascist squads and propaganda cultivated support among property-owners and business interests. Yet Mussolini entered government not by defeating the army in the October 1922 March on Rome, but because King Victor Emmanuel III refused resistance and invited him to form a cabinet. Authoritarianism grew through elite surrender as well as mass pressure.

Germany's Weimar Republic bore Versailles stigma and the “stab-in-the-back” myth; fragmented coalitions, political violence and the hyperinflation of 1923 weakened its legitimacy. Reliance on American credit after 1924 made recovery fragile; Depression-era unemployment strengthened Nazi appeal. Still, the Nazis never won an overall majority. Conservative elites made Hitler Chancellor in January 1933, and the Enabling Law of March transformed legal entry into dictatorial power. Nazi racial ideology and coercive organisation then attacked pluralism more systematically than Italian fascism.

Japan's parliamentary government weakened under army pressure and economic crisis; occupation of Manchuria in 1931 and Prime Minister Inukai's assassination in 1932 marked a shift toward military dominance within surviving imperial institutions, not a European mass-party seizure. Spain's authoritarian outcome followed a civil war beginning in 1936 and Franco's victory in 1939 with German and Italian help. These regimes shared anti-left politics and coercion but not one blueprint.

The challenge therefore lay in both movements hostile to democracy and democratic leaders' unwillingness or inability to defend it. Crisis made anti-parliamentary answers persuasive; kings, conservative elites and legal procedures converted persuasion into power. Economic distress alone could not dictate that choice, nor does Japan's military route make it an identical fascist party-state.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Compare fascism, Nazism, Japanese militarism and Francoism. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

Fascism, Nazism, Japanese militarism and Francoism formed an authoritarian family, not a single regime type. All weakened pluralism and used nationalism, anti-left politics or coercion; their ideologies, social bases and methods of taking power were different.

Italian fascism grew from postwar disappointment and class fear. The Fasci began in 1919 and the National Fascist Party followed in 1921; Mussolini's March on Rome in October 1922 ended with royal invitation, not military conquest. Its leader cult, anti-communism, autarkic ambition, propaganda and corporate labour control aimed to mobilise society, although the king and Church remained independent centres of power. The Lateran Treaty of 1929 accommodated the latter.

German Nazism drew on Weimar's weakness and the Depression; conservative elites appointed Hitler Chancellor in January 1933 and the March Enabling Law enabled dictatorship. Nazi national rebirth and militarisation rested centrally on Aryan supremacy and anti-Semitism. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935, destroyed unions, Goebbels's propaganda and camps demonstrate more systematic, racialised and brutal control than in Italy, where anti-Jewish laws arrived later. Shared one-party ambition does not erase that constitutive difference.

Japan retained imperial and constitutional forms while army influence displaced civilian direction. The occupation of Manchuria in 1931 and Inukai's assassination in 1932 signalled this shift; full-scale war in China followed in 1937. Its military and bureaucratic elites did not build an Italian-style mass party-state. In Spain, the monarchy fell in 1931 and civil war began in 1936; Franco won in 1939 with German and Italian support. His anti-left authoritarianism shared practices with fascists but did not reproduce their mass-mobilising project of national rebirth.

The useful comparison is thus graded: Italy pioneered party fascism, Germany radicalised it through biological race, Japan followed an army-led path, and Spain a civil-war path. Similar crises and coercive responses explain resemblance; institutions and racial doctrine explain why an unqualified fascist label for all four would mislead.
