### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the principles of legitimacy, balance of power and compensation at Vienna. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model thesis:** The principles restored dynasties, contained France through buffers and redistributed territory among victors, producing order without popular consent.

**Claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification:**

- The principle of legitimacy restored dynasties, including the Bourbons in France, and treated inherited rule rather than national consent as the basis of valid government.
- Balance-of-power policy contained rather than destroyed France, built buffers and prevented another single continental hegemon.
- Compensation redistributed territory among victors, strengthening Prussia and Sardinia-Piedmont while enlarging Austrian and Russian influence.
- Belgium and Holland were joined in the Kingdom of the Netherlands as a northern buffer against France, an arrangement broken by Belgian independence in 1830.

**Qualified conclusion:** The principles restored dynasties, contained France through buffers and redistributed territory among victors, producing order without popular consent.

**Detailed examiner-grade model answer:**

At Vienna in 1814–15, the victorious powers used three related principles to rebuild Europe after Napoleon. Legitimacy meant restoring hereditary rulers, notably the Bourbons in France, rather than grounding authority in popular or national consent. It answered revolutionary upheaval with dynastic continuity.

Balance of power aimed to prevent a new continental hegemon without permanently destroying France. Joining Belgium and Holland in the Kingdom of the Netherlands created a northern buffer; strengthening Sardinia-Piedmont with Genoa also helped check France. Compensation rewarded and secured the victors: Prussia gained western territory and part of Saxony, Austria gained Lombardy and Venetia, and Russia obtained control over most of the Duchy of Warsaw. Such reallocations made strategic equilibrium possible but transferred populations without asking their wishes.

The principles therefore produced a workable interstate order, not a settlement of national claims. Belgian independence in 1830 exposed the limits of the Dutch-Belgian buffer. Vienna's achievement in restraining great-power rivalry must be distinguished from its failure to secure consent among the peoples it rearranged.

Neither body was a modern rights-based security organisation: one managed relations among powers, the other proclaimed dynastic solidarity.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Was the Concert of Europe an instrument of peace or repression? Answer in about 250 words.

**Detailed examiner-grade model answer:**

The Concert was an instrument of peace among the major powers and, at times, of repression within states. These judgements concern different beneficiaries of the post-Napoleonic order, not two mutually exclusive descriptions.

Through periodic congresses, rather than a permanent treaty-state, the Concert offered governments a forum to manage disputes without war. Reintegrating France at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 avoided isolating the defeated power. Acceptance of Belgian independence in 1830, despite Vienna's Dutch-Belgian union, showed that compromise could preserve stability. It was more than the symbolic Holy Alliance of Russia, Austria and Prussia.

But peace for cabinets did not mean political freedom for their subjects. The Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 imposed censorship, university control and investigation in the German states to suppress liberal and nationalist activity. At Troppau and Laibach in 1820–21, intervention against revolutions became an asserted principle of conservative cooperation. Verona in 1822 approved French intervention in Spain and was the last major effective congress of the earlier system. This record reveals how external order could protect dynastic power at the expense of popular claims. Yet members differed about intervention, so the Concert was never a seamless police authority.

Its success in moderating great-power conflict was real but bounded: Vienna's consultation coexisted with a legitimacy deficit among Germans, Italians and others excluded from the settlement. Selective adaptation in Belgium also shows why it should not be portrayed either as purely repressive or as unchanging after Verona.
