*Session 1 visual. Ownership control prevents drift into Topic 18 or Topic 20.*

“Foreign policy” is a modern analytical label for Mughal relations with other
imperial courts and with the political world beyond the empire's north-western
core. It is useful for grouping diplomacy, frontier security, dynastic claims,
trade, pilgrimage, embassies and war. It becomes anachronistic if it implies:

- a modern nation-state, foreign ministry or permanent diplomatic service;
- fixed surveyed international borders;
- equal sovereign states governed by modern international law;
- one written and continuous “national-security doctrine”; or
- a clean division between domestic frontier administration and external
  relations.

The Mughal court pursued recognisable interests, but policy was made through the
emperor, princes, nobles, frontier governors, commanders, envoys, merchants and
slow-moving information. Use the exam-safe formulation:

> Mughal foreign policy was a changing court practice in which the security of
> the Indian revenue empire, Timurid dynastic memory, imperial honour,
> commercial movement and feasible military reach interacted.

### SESSION 2 — STRATEGIC GEOGRAPHY: KABUL, QANDAHAR, BALKH AND THE OXUS

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** The north-west worked as a route system, not as a row of isolated places.

**Technical definition:** Kabul was the indispensable base; Qandahar the fortified hinge; Balkh the forward buffer problem; the Oxus an obstacle, not an automatic permanent frontier.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Map the topic as Kabul base -> Qandahar hinge -> Balkh buffer before you discuss rulers.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Kabul base**
- **Qandahar hinge**
- **Balkh buffer**
- **Hindukush line**
- **Oxus caution**

**How to use them:** Attach one function to each place so the map becomes argument.

```text
SAMARQAND/BUKHARA -> OXUS -> BALKH/BADAKHSHAN -> HINDUKUSH -> KABUL -> GHAZNI -> QANDAHAR -> MULTAN -> INDUS/SEA
```

```text

                                  |
                              TRANSOXIANA
                                  |
                        OXUS / AMU DARYA
                                  |
                         BALKH -- BADAKHSHAN
                            \        /
                             HINDUKUSH
                                 |
                               KABUL
                                 |

                                 |
              HERAT -------- QANDAHAR -------- MULTAN
                |                  |                 |
          KHURASAN / IRAN     SOUTHERN ROUTES     INDUS -> SEA
```

This is a schematic relation map, not a modern border map.

### Why each zone mattered

| Place/zone | Foreign-policy function | Essential qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Kabul | indispensable Mughal base and shield for the Indian core | required subsidy, routes, garrisons and local cooperation |
| Khyber-Peshawar-Atak corridor | access between Punjab and Kabul | control was seasonal and contested, not a line on a map |
| Ghazni | link in the Kabul-Qandahar defensive sequence | a corridor node, not an independently sufficient frontier |
| Qandahar | fortified junction toward Kabul, Herat, Multan and the Indus | value combined security, commerce and prestige |
| Herat/Khurasan | Safavid-Uzbek contest and road network | not interchangeable with Qandahar |
| Balkh | forward buffer/client-state problem north of the Hindukush | occupation was costlier than holding Qandahar |
| Badakhshan | mountainous buffer and Timurid-linked political field | limited revenue and difficult defiles reduced annexation value |
| Oxus/Amu Darya | route and military obstacle crossed at fords | not a self-enforcing “scientific frontier” |

### The north-west was a system

A viable frontier required more than a fort:

`fort + water/stores + road/pass + garrison + intelligence + local allies +
fodder/grain + artillery/powder + seasonal timing + relief capacity`.

Therefore distinguish a map claim from actual control. Terrain, winter,
distance, hostile settlements, moving Uzbek forces and contested passes could
turn nominal possession into an expensive liability.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

A disciplined geography paragraph can claim that Mughal north-west policy worked through a layered route system rather than one neat border line. The named evidence is Kabul as base, Qandahar as the fort-road-commercial hinge and Balkh beyond the Hindukush as a costlier buffer. The analysis is that each site carried a different strategic function. The qualification is that terrain mattered only when linked to stores, routes, intelligence and local cooperation.

**UPSC trap:** Do not confuse Qandahar with Gandhara or treat Balkh as if it performed the same role as Qandahar.

### SESSION 3 — POLITICAL VOCABULARY, EMBASSY AND INFORMATION

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Mughal diplomacy worked through missions, letters, gifts and reception rather than resident embassies.

**Technical definition:** Terms such as embassy, alliance, suzerainty, occupation and annexation must be separated carefully; an elchi or safir carried political claims and information together.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> An embassy in this topic is simultaneously message, theatre and information channel.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **elchi**
- **safir**
- **status language**
- **gift exchange**
- **information lag**

**How to use them:** Name the envoy and the status language together so ritual and practical function appear in one frame.

```text
letter + gifts + spoken message -> ELCHI/SAFIR -> audience & rank display -> information & response
```

*Session 3 visual. Diplomatic ritual and intelligence-gathering moved together.*

| Term | Exam-safe meaning |
|---|---|
| embassy / mission | temporary envoy-led communication carrying letters, gifts, messages and information |
| alliance / common front | contingent cooperation against a shared rival; not necessarily permanent |
| neutrality / non-entanglement | refusal to join a campaign while retaining relations with both sides |
| buffer / client arrangement | support for a friendly ruler whose survival keeps danger away without direct annexation |
| suzerainty | unequal superior-subordinate relation while the lesser ruler may retain a court and internal initiative |
| sovereignty claim | assertion of supreme authority, often expressed through titles, letters, coins or conquest rhetoric |
| occupation | military possession, which may remain temporary and contested |
| annexation / direct incorporation | formal absorption into imperial territory |
| durable control | repeated ability to administer, supply, tax, defend and secure cooperation |

Courtesy, gift exchange or “brother” language does not prove alliance,
subordination or abandonment of a territorial claim. Likewise, occupation does
not automatically establish annexation, and annexation on paper does not prove
durable control.

---

The usual diplomatic mode was the exchange of missions. An ambassador could be
called **elchi** or **safir**.

### What an embassy carried

- a ruler's letter;
- news of victories or political changes;
- requests, warnings, promises or professions of friendship;
- gifts and regional rarities;
- verbal instructions not always preserved in the written letter; and
- escorts, retainers and servants.

### Status language

Drafting and reception asked whether another ruler was:

- equal, superior or inferior;
- elder, younger or “brother”;
- a sovereign, ally, client or rival.

Titles, robes, seating, embraces, gifts and return missions staged rank. Their
meaning could be disputed. Diplomatic ceremony is evidence of a relationship
being negotiated, not automatic proof of political hierarchy.

### Information function

There were no permanent resident embassies. Repeated missions nevertheless
created a flow of information. Envoys reported roads, people, court mood and
military-political conditions; princes, nobles, merchants and frontier officers
supplemented them. Slow travel produced information lag, making local
preparedness and delegated command vital.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

A high-value diplomatic paragraph can claim that Mughal embassies were institutional practices of rank and information rather than decorative ceremonies. The named evidence is the elchi or safir, calibrated address formulae and the movement of letters and gifts. The analysis is that diplomacy helped stage parity, carry claims and gather news while buying time. The qualification is that no elegant mission could replace a prepared fort or rapid relief.

**UPSC trap:** Do not translate elchi or safir into a permanent modern ambassador with resident embassy.

**Mains angle:** This session gives the basic institutional paragraph for diplomacy or source-method answers.

**Study link:** Traveller and merchant evidence can support these points but cannot replace court letters for status language.

### SESSION 4 — TIMURID MEMORY AND THE FOUR-COURT FIELD

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Timurid memory mattered, but it did not amount to a permanent reconquest programme.

**Technical definition:** Babur’s Samarqand experience, Humayun’s Safavid refuge, Uzbek pressure, Safavid rivalry and Ottoman prestige formed a four-court field in which sectarian rhetoric never fully determined alignment.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Timurid ancestry explains some ambition; balance and security explain the recurring structure of policy.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Samarqand memory**
- **Humayun-Tahmasp**
- **Uzbek pressure**
- **Safavid counterweight**
- **Ottoman prestige**

**How to use them:** Start with memory, then immediately qualify it by naming the living rival courts.

| Court | What it meant | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Uzbeks | pressure in Transoxiana/Balkh | risk to Kabul if concentrated |
| Safavids | aid tradition + rival claim | counterweight and competitor |
| Ottomans | prestige + maritime context | background, not command authority |
| Mughals | Indian revenue empire + Timurid memory | security first, ambition qualified |

*Session 4 visual. The Mughals operated in a four-court political field rather than a single bilateral rivalry.*

### Babur as prerequisite, not the whole explanation

- Uzbek consolidation expelled Timurid princes from much of Transoxiana.
- Babur repeatedly sought Samarqand and briefly regained it in **1511** with
  Safavid support after Shah Ismail's defeat of Shaibani Khan.
- The recovery was short-lived. Babur's durable base became **Kabul (1504)**.
- Babur briefly held Qandahar in **1507** and gained firmer control in **1522**.
- Detailed Babur chronology remains Topic 12.

The dynasty inherited Timurid genealogy, memories of Samarqand and a language
of recovering ancestral lands. That inheritance could shape prestige and
ambition. It did not create a continuous, operational programme to reconquer
all Central Asia.

### Humayun as prerequisite

- After expulsion from India, Humayun sought help from **Shah Tahmasp**.
- Safavid assistance, court ritual and the Qandahar bargain were political and
  source-mediated; they do not prove simple sectarian conversion or permanent
  Mughal dependence.
- Humayun recovered **Qandahar and Kabul in 1545** and used them in the struggle
  against Kamran.
- Detailed exile and restoration remain Topic 13.

These episodes establish the long-term triangle: the Safavids could be patrons
and strategic partners, the Uzbeks were rivals in the Timurid homeland, and
Qandahar remained a negotiable but disputed hinge.

---

### Uzbeks

The Uzbeks displaced the Timurids, contested Khurasan with Iran and could
threaten Balkh, Badakhshan, Kabul and connected frontier societies. They were
not a single permanently united power: dynastic and tribal divisions repeatedly
changed the danger.

### Safavids

Safavid Iran was simultaneously:

1. a counterweight restraining Uzbek pressure;
2. an old source of aid to Babur and Humayun;
3. a commercial and cultural partner; and
4. a rival claimant to Qandahar.

“Friendship with Persia” was therefore a strategic keynote, not uninterrupted
harmony.

### Ottomans

The Ottomans:

- controlled major West Asian and eastern Mediterranean zones;
- claimed wide Islamic prestige and used titles such as
  **Padshah-i-Islam**;
- fought Safavid Iran over Anatolian, Iraqi and Iranian theatres;
- confronted Portuguese pressure in Red Sea and Persian Gulf commerce; and
- possessed naval strength that the land-centred Mughals did not match.

The Mughals did not accept Ottoman superiority or join a sustained
Ottoman-Mughal-Uzbek military bloc. Distance, status rivalry, Uzbek
unreliability and the value of Iran as a counterweight limited such schemes.

### Why sectarian labels are insufficient

Sunni and Shia identities mattered in rhetoric and legitimacy, but did not
mechanically determine alignment. The Mughals could be Sunni while cooperating
with Safavid Iran and rejecting Uzbek calls for anti-Shia conquest. The exam
rule is:

> state the sectarian language, then test it against security, balance,
> commerce, distance and imperial status.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

A good background paragraph can claim that Timurid memory was real but structurally secondary to the balance among Uzbeks, Safavids and Ottomans. The named evidence is Babur’s 1511 Samarqand episode, Humayun’s Safavid refuge and the continuing Uzbek pressure on Balkh and Kabul. The analysis is that memory widened the horizon of Mughal ambition while security and counterweight politics dictated the practical choices. The qualification is that sectarian rhetoric still mattered as language and legitimacy, even when it did not settle policy.

**UPSC trap:** Do not reduce the whole topic either to Sunni-versus-Shia struggle or to pure nostalgia for Samarqand.

**Mains angle:** This session helps you balance prestige and strategy without flattening either.

**Study link:** Use Humayun only as bounded background; his full exile story belongs to Topic 13.

### SESSION 5 — AKBAR AND THE UZBEK-SAFAVID BALANCE

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Akbar refused an anti-Safavid crusade because it would damage Mughal security more than it would help it.

**Technical definition:** The 1572-73 and 1583 Balkh seizures, the 1577 proposal, the 1585 Kabul turn and the 1595 Qandahar acquisition form one sequence of balance under pressure.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Akbar’s north-west policy is best read as anti-sectarian realism under real Uzbek pressure.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Abdullah Khan**
- **1577 refusal**
- **balance logic**
- **Kabul 1585**
- **Qandahar 1595**

**How to use them:** Use the dates in order so the answer reads as policy under stress rather than a slogan about tolerance.

| Date | Event | Policy meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1572-73 | first Uzbek seizure of Balkh | pressure rises |
| 1577 | anti-Safavid invitation | Akbar rejects sectarian conquest logic |
| 1583 | Balkh retaken by Abdullah | renewed danger |
| 1585 | Badakhshan + Kabul turn | frontier concern sharpens |
| 1595 | Qandahar acquired | line strengthened |

*Session 5 visual. The chronology shows why Akbar’s refusal was strategic, not abstract.*

### Exact Balkh-Badakhshan chronology

| Date | Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1572-73 | Abdullah Khan Uzbek first seized Balkh | buffer pressure increased |
| after 1572-73 | Shahrukh Mirza recovered Balkh | the first loss was not final |
| 1577 | Abdullah proposed anti-Safavid partition/conquest logic to Akbar | tested Mughal sectarian versus strategic priorities |
| 1583 | Abdullah reconquered Balkh | renewed Uzbek advance |
| 1585 | Abdullah took Badakhshan; Mirza Hakim died; Akbar annexed Kabul | Mughal and Uzbek strategic zones touched more directly |
| 1586 onward | further diplomacy and Mughal watch from the Lahore-Atak region | neutrality and frontier preparedness worked together |
| 1595 | Akbar acquired Qandahar | completed the practical Kabul-Ghazni-Qandahar line |
| 1598 | Abdullah died; Uzbek power fragmented; Shah Abbas recovered Khurasan | balance shifted again |

The dates **1572-73** and **1583** describe two Uzbek seizures separated by a
Timurid recovery; they are not contradictory dates for one event.

### Akbar's 1577 refusal

Abdullah Khan urged a joint move against Safavid Iran using Sunni solidarity
and the argument that pilgrims faced difficulty through Iran. Akbar's response
held that differences in law and religion were not sufficient grounds for
conquest.

Do not turn this into modern internationalism. Akbar's logic was strategic:

- a strong Iran checked Uzbek expansion;
- a distant war would expose Mughal interests without clear gain;
- Akbar wanted to avoid conflict unless Kabul or Indian possessions were
  directly threatened;
- Gujarat offered another route for pilgrims and maritime traffic; and
- Mughal equality and independence mattered.

### Kabul, Mirza Hakim and frontier supervision

Mirza Hakim's Kabul position intersected with the 1580-81 imperial crisis.
After his death in **1585**, Akbar annexed Kabul and supervised the north-west
from Lahore for much of **1585-98**. Kabul was not merely a dynastic appanage:
it was the anchor of India's outer defence and a node in transregional trade.

### Frontier tribes, Roshanai and route security

Uzbek policy could exploit unrest among frontier communities. The Roshanai
movement associated with **Bayazid Ansari** and later leadership, and the
activities of **Jalala** in the source narrative, combined religious,
social-political and route dimensions. Yusufzai and other Pashtun communities
were not one timeless “tribal threat”. Their leaders and coalitions responded
to local power, Mughal demands, religious authority, terrain and outside
encouragement.

Akbar moved toward **Atak/Attock** during the crisis; Raja Birbal died in the
frontier fighting. The foreign-policy relevance is bounded:

- passes and roads could not be secured by diplomacy with Bukhara alone;
- local intelligence, negotiation, forts and military movement were necessary;
- Uzbek encouragement did not erase frontier agency; and
- full internal frontier history remains subordinate to the Kabul-route
  question here.

### The Hindukush arrangement

After further exchanges, Akbar's agent **Hakim Humam** carried a letter and
verbal message to Abdullah. A practical understanding treated the Hindukush as
a strategic boundary of interest:

- Mughals limited immediate ambitions in Balkh/Badakhshan;
- Uzbeks restrained claims toward Kabul/Qandahar;
- neither side permanently renounced all claims.

“Scientific frontier” here means a defensible strategic concept, not a modern
international treaty line or control of every pass.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

A compact Akbar paragraph can claim that his Uzbek policy was anti-sectarian because it was strategically balanced. The named evidence is Abdullah Khan’s 1577 invitation, Akbar’s reply and the later centrality of Kabul and Qandahar. The analysis is that Akbar wanted Iran as a counterweight to Uzbek pressure while still strengthening Mughal frontier security. The qualification is that he was not pacifist; force still entered policy when security demanded it.

**UPSC trap:** Do not write that Akbar rejected power politics; he rejected one sectarian route into power politics.

**Mains angle:** This session directly supports a 15-mark answer on Akbar’s balance policy.

**Study link:** Mention Gujarat only as the pilgrimage-route qualification already grounded in the canonical source.

### SESSION 6 — FRONTIER SUPERVISION AND THE HINDUKUSH ARRANGEMENT

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** After 1585, Mughal diplomacy required route security, frontier watch and local intelligence as much as formal exchanges with Bukhara.

**Technical definition:** Mirza Hakim’s death, Akbar’s Lahore-Atak supervision, frontier unrest and Hakim Humam’s dealings help explain the Hindukush line as a practical understanding rather than a surveyed treaty frontier.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The Hindukush became useful only when diplomacy was backed by roads, forts, local allies and supervision.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Mirza Hakim**
- **Atak watch**
- **route security**
- **frontier overlap**
- **Hindukush arrangement**

**How to use them:** Use Kabul annexation and the pass problem together so diplomacy and local control remain linked.

```text
Kabul annexed -> Lahore/Atak watch -> passes + forts + local intelligence -> diplomacy with Abdullah -> practical Hindukush line
```

asymmetry does not imply Safavid indifference.

diplomacy. By 1595 the fort was cut off from effective Iranian support while
Uzbeks pressed nearby Zamindawar and Garmsir. The local Mirzas surrendered to

### SESSION 8 — JAHANGIR, SHAH ABBAS I AND THE 1622 LOSS

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Jahangir’s cordial diplomacy with Shah Abbas did not remove the unresolved Qandahar claim.

**Technical definition:** The 1611 embassy cycle, return mission, repeated Persian claim, limited readiness, delayed relief and Khurram’s refusal or delay together explain the 1622 loss better than one-cause stories do.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Qandahar fell in 1622 not because diplomacy was absent, but because diplomacy existed without matching readiness.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **1611 embassy**
- **Khan Alam**
- **persistent claim**
- **preparedness gap**
- **Khurram delay**

**How to use them:** Tell the story as cordiality first, then unresolved claim, then relief failure.

```text
embassy cordiality -> confidence in friendship -> Persian claim persists -> limited garrison + delayed relief -> 1622 loss
```

*Session 8 visual. The key contrast is cordiality versus readiness.*

### Embassy and trade cycle

Shah Abbas I sought Qandahar but also cultivated Jahangir:

- he disowned or distanced himself from premature attacks;
- in **1611** he sent an embassy with Gilan horses, carpets, silks and other
  gifts;
- Jahangir sent **Khan Alam** in return;
- reception at Qazvin staged “brotherly” equality and magnificence;
- Jahangir appointed **Muhammad Hussain Chalabi** as a royal trade
  commissioner; and
- Indian merchants, often described as Multanis and including Hindu and Jain
  communities, operated in Iranian cities.

Exact merchant totals vary widely in traveller accounts and must not be
presented as secure census data.

### Why Qandahar fell in 1622

Persian envoys raised Qandahar in **1620** and later missions. Jahangir
underestimated the likelihood that Shah Abbas would act against a “friend”.
When Safavid forces attacked in **1622**:

1. the Persian claim had been persistent;
2. Safavid initiative and preparation were superior;
3. the Mughal garrison was limited and command was not sufficiently energetic;
4. long-distance relief required coordination and time; and
5. Prince Khurram's refusal/delay formed part of a wider court-succession
   crisis.

The *Iqbalnama-i Jahangiri* gives **3,000** Mughal troops; Jahangir's memoir
mentions only **300 or 400 servants**, probably the commandant's personal
following. Preserve the source distinction rather than creating one false
precise number.

Do not explain 1622 solely through a “Nur Jahan junta”. Topic 20 owns the full
court-politics debate. Topic 19's lesson is:

> diplomatic cordiality cannot substitute for a prepared fort, capable command
> and timely relief.

The loss ended the exceptionally cordial phase, not all later
Mughal-Safavid diplomacy.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

An examiner-grade 1622 paragraph can claim that Jahangir lost Qandahar because diplomatic cordiality outpaced strategic preparation. The named evidence is the 1611 embassy exchange, the continuing Persian claim, the limited garrison evidence and Khurram’s role in the relief crisis. The analysis is that friendship language lulled the Mughal court while a rival claim backed by preparation remained alive. The qualification is that court politics still mattered; unreadiness failed inside a wider succession environment.

**UPSC trap:** Do not explain 1622 solely through Nur Jahan or through one forced troop figure pulled from a single text.

**Mains angle:** This session supplies a direct causal answer and a model of multi-causal reasoning.

### SESSION 10 — SHAH JAHAN: 1638 RECOVERY, BALKH 1646-47 AND LIMITS OF FORWARD DEFENCE

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Shah Jahan partly restored the Mughal position at Qandahar, then stretched the empire beyond a sustainable client arrangement in Balkh.

**Technical definition:** The 1636 proposal to Murad IV remained diplomatic aspiration; Ali Mardan Khan’s 1638 transfer recovered Qandahar; Nazr Muhammad’s appeal and Murad’s coercive entry into Balkh shifted a client-buffer design toward expensive occupation.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Shah Jahan’s north-west policy was most convincing when it used diplomacy and defection, and most fragile when easy occupation tempted it beyond a viable client plan.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Murad IV proposal**
- **Ali Mardan Khan**
- **Nazr Muhammad**
- **client-buffer logic**
- **1647 battlefield success**

**How to use them:** Keep the sequence tight: 1636 aspiration, 1638 recovery, 1645 appeal, 1646 occupation, 1647 victory and withdrawal.

| Stage | Event | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1636 | letter to Murad IV | anti-Persian aspiration stayed diplomatic |
| 1638 | Ali Mardan Khan defects | Qandahar returns without a full siege war |
| 1645-46 | Nazr Muhammad appeals; Murad enters Balkh | client plan shifts toward direct occupation |
| 1647 | Aurangzeb wins in field, then withdraws | military success proves unsustainable politically |

*Session 10 visual. Shah Jahan’s successes and limits came from different policy instruments.*

After Abdullah Khan's death, Uzbek politics fragmented. Imam Quli emerged in
Bukhara/Balkh, while Nazr Muhammad later controlled Balkh and Badakhshan.
Uzbek raids and manoeuvres around Kabul continued.

### Selected sequence

- **1621:** Imam Quli's mother sent an embassy to Nur Jahan; a return mission
  followed.
- After **1622**, Mughal-Uzbek discussions revived a possible Sunni common
  front against Iran.
- Uzbek leaders simultaneously informed or courted Iran, showing the fragility
  of bloc diplomacy.
- Yalingtosh raided toward Kabul/Ghazni and tried to mobilise Hazara and Afghan
  disaffection; Mughal defence and artillery checked these efforts.
- **1628:** Nazr Muhammad attacked Kabul, occupied the city and invested the
  fort, then withdrew before Mughal reinforcements; Mughals occupied Bamiyan
  temporarily and used diplomacy to isolate him.
- **1633-39:** frequent missions accompanied discussions of a wider
  anti-Safavid arrangement.

### The 1636 proposal

In **1636** Shah Jahan wrote to Ottoman Sultan **Murad IV** proposing pressure
on Iran from India, Turan and Ottoman territory. This shows diplomatic
aspiration, not an operational three-power alliance:

- Uzbeks were unreliable;
- Ottomans were too distant for timely action;
- Ottoman superiority claims offended Mughal equality; and
- Shah Jahan did not abandon the advantages of diplomatic flexibility.

---

Shah Jahan recovered Qandahar through the defection of its Safavid governor,
**Ali Mardan Khan**, in **1638**.

Use precise vocabulary:

- the recovery came through political inducement and transferred allegiance;
- it was not a successful siege of Iran;
- Ali Mardan's later Mughal career as commander/engineer is relevant only as a
  bounded consequence; and
- Shah Jahan still sent cordial embassies and even offered a payment equivalent
  to the fort's revenue, demonstrating that possession and diplomacy could
  coexist.

Shah Safi prepared a recovery expedition. Shah Jahan moved toward Kabul and
entrusted Dara with artillery for defence. Shah Safi died during the **1642**
movement, postponing the confrontation.

---

### Background

- Imam Quli had divided authority with his brother **Nazr Muhammad**.
- Nazr Muhammad later united the Uzbek khanate but faced revolt by his son
  **Abdul Aziz**.
- Threatened at Balkh, Nazr Muhammad appealed to Shah Jahan in **1645**.
- A friendly ruler at Balkh/Bukhara could keep Uzbek power divided and reduce
  pressure on Kabul.

### Force and plan

The Part II narrative attributes to the **1646** force:

- **50,000 horse**;
- **10,000 foot**;
- musketeers, rocketeers and gunners; and
- a Rajput contingent.

Attribute this figure to the source and campaign. Do not turn it into the
permanent size of the Mughal army or attach an unverified total cost.

Shah Jahan instructed Prince **Murad** to treat Nazr Muhammad carefully and
restore Balkh under a friendly arrangement. If Nazr Muhammad sought Samarqand
and Bukhara, Mughal help might follow. The economical policy was therefore a
client/buffer configuration, not necessarily direct annexation.

### How Murad changed the problem

Murad entered Balkh coercively and demanded Nazr Muhammad's personal
attendance. The Uzbek ruler fled. The Mughals now had:

- possession without a locally legitimate client;
- a hostile population;
- Abdul Aziz mobilising across the Oxus;
- reluctant Mughal and Rajput nobles; and
- a growing supply and climate problem.

Shah Jahan recalled/punished Murad and sent **Sadullah Khan** for
administration, then **Aurangzeb** with Ali Mardan Khan for the military crisis.

### Aurangzeb's 1647 battlefield success

Aurangzeb did not attempt to make the Oxus an absolute defence line. Because it
was fordable near Balkh, he used strategic pickets and retained a mobile main
force with artillery. Abdul Aziz crossed; Mughal artillery and deployment
helped rout the Uzbek force outside Balkh in **1647**.

This was a real military success. It did not solve:

- local hostility;
- absence of a viable client;
- roaming Uzbek bands;
- insufficient supplies;
- approaching winter;
- difficult terrain and forage;
- noble reluctance; or
- Persian hostility toward durable Mughal influence.

### Withdrawal and judgement

Shah Jahan reverted toward restoring Nazr Muhammad, but demanded humiliating
submission. Nazr Muhammad did not appear. The Mughals withdrew in
**October 1647** as winter approached and supplies failed; Aurangzeb's firmness
prevented a worse rout.

#### What Balkh proves

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| total Mughal military defeat | false; the Mughals won significant fighting |
| permanent conquest of Central Asia | false; occupation was abandoned |
| pure Timurid nostalgia | incomplete; prestige enlarged ambition, but buffer/security logic was real |
| fixed Oxus “scientific frontier” | false; the river was fordable and not self-defending |
| pure success | false; battlefield success did not produce political sustainability |
| beginning of automatic Mughal decline | too simple; the campaign exposes limits of projection, not total imperial collapse |

The strongest verdict is:

> Balkh was militarily successful and strategically intelligible as forward
> defence, but politically and logistically unsustainable after the client plan
> became direct occupation.

---

#### PARAGRAPH DEMONSTRATION

A high-value Shah Jahan paragraph can claim that his foreign policy moved from strategic recovery to overextended forward defence. The named evidence is the merely aspirational 1636 Ottoman proposal, the 1638 recovery of Qandahar through Ali Mardan Khan and the 1646-47 Balkh campaign built around Nazr Muhammad’s appeal. The analysis is that diplomacy and defection could restore a frontier fort more cheaply than direct conquest, whereas occupation beyond the Hindukush demanded a viable client, supplies and local acceptance. The qualification is that the aims were not irrational; they became unsustainable after the client design collapsed.

**UPSC trap:** Do not write that Shah Jahan conquered Persia in 1638 or that Balkh was simply a march to Samarqand.

**Mains angle:** This session is the bridge between Qandahar recovery and the Balkh experiment in any long answer.

**Study link:** Use the 50,000 horse and 10,000 foot figure only as a campaign-specific source figure, not as the permanent size of the Mughal army.

### SESSION 11 — 1649-53 QANDAHAR FAILURES, SOURCES AND THE FINAL VERDICT

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** The late Qandahar failures show why field power, prestige and heavy effort still did not guarantee a recovered fort.

**Technical definition:** The 1649, 1652 and 1653 attempts failed because prepared fortification, Lahore-based supply, engineering limits, winter pressure and time worked against the besieger; the broader evidence must be read through chronicles, letters, merchant accounts and material remains with explicit historiographical caution.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Balkh taught that battlefield success did not ensure occupation; Qandahar taught that field strength did not ensure a breached fort.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **1649-53 sieges**
- **Lahore supply**
- **prepared fort**
- **source criticism**
- **qualified verdict**

**How to use them:** List the three attempts, explain the structural siege problem, then close with the balanced India-centred judgement.

| Problem | Why it mattered | Safe conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Prepared fort | walls, stores, water, defenders | field success != siege success |
| Lahore supply line | long-distance provisioning burden | duration became costly |
| Winter and season | limited investment time | campaign timing mattered |
| Sources and debate | chronicles, memoirs, merchants, forts | compare evidence classes |

*Session 11 visual. The late failures are best read as a siege-logistics and evidence-reading problem inside a larger strategic reassessment.*

Shah Abbas II recovered Qandahar in **1649**. The loss followed the Balkh
setback, renewed frontier unrest and Persian confidence.

### Exact expedition sequence

| Attempt | Commander | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1649 | Aurangzeb | Mughal field success outside the fort did not capture it |
| 1652 | Aurangzeb | second siege failed |
| 1653 | Dara Shukoh | large army and very heavy guns failed to force surrender |

### Why the expeditions failed

1. **Prepared fort:** strong walls, water, stores and determined Safavid
   command favoured the defender.
2. **Siege versus field warfare:** cavalry and artillery success in open battle
   did not guarantee a breach or surrender.
3. **Medieval artillery limits:** large guns were difficult to transport and
   were not automatically decisive against a strong fort.
4. **Long supply line:** Mughal supplies had to come from **Lahore**.
5. **Scorched-earth pressure:** local provisioning was deliberately reduced.
6. **Climate and season:** a winter siege was impracticable.
7. **Time and cost:** prolonged investment consumed fodder, grain, powder,
   animals, money and prestige.
8. **Command competition:** princes led separate high-prestige attempts, but no
   single operational design overcame the structural problem.

Do not write “Mughal artillery was weak”. Mughal artillery contributed to the
Balkh field victory. The Qandahar result was a specific fortification,
engineering, supply and seasonal failure.

By the 1650s weakening Uzbek and Safavid power reduced some of Qandahar's
earlier strategic urgency. Repeated failure damaged prestige more than the loss
of the “mass of rocks” alone. No unsupported total fiscal cost should be quoted.

Aurangzeb later abandoned futile Qandahar recovery and resumed diplomacy with

### Field warfare

- cavalry manoeuvre;
- musketeers;
- artillery and rocketeers;
- mobile reserves and pickets;
- intelligence about crossings and enemy movement.

### Siege warfare

- heavy guns and engineers;
- trenches, mines and approaches;
- water and food denial;
- continuous powder and ammunition;
- protection of the besieger's supply line;
- time before winter or relief; and
- willingness of commander and garrison to continue.

### Logistics chain

```text
Indian revenue core
      -> treasury and appointments
      -> Lahore / Punjab mobilisation
      -> Kabul or Qandahar route
      -> grain + fodder + animals + powder + guns
      -> local stores / allies / intelligence
      -> field presence
      -> fort capture or political settlement
```

Break any link and military power may fail to become durable control. Climate
does not act alone: winter, defiles and forage matter through transport,
animals, provisioning, morale and campaign time.

### Source method

For every evidence unit ask:

1. Who produced it?
2. For whom?

### Debate 1: India-centred defence or ancestral reconquest?

- **India-centred reading:** the durable concern was security of Kabul and the
  Indian fiscal core, supported by diplomacy and Qandahar.
- **Ancestral-homeland reading:** Timurid memory of Samarqand influenced Mughal
  imagination and prestige.
- **Graded verdict:** security and commerce explain the recurring structure;
  Timurid memory explains why ambition sometimes expanded beyond it.

### Debate 2: “Scientific frontier”

Satish Chandra uses the language of a defensible north-west system based on the
Hindukush, Kabul-Ghazni and Qandahar. Do not convert it into:

- an ethnographic “natural border”;
- a fixed legal line;
- permanent control of every pass; or
- the Oxus as a self-defending boundary.

### Debate 3: Balkh - realpolitik or adventurism?

**Riazul Islam** criticised Shah Jahan's adventurism and Timurid obsession.
The evidence also shows a rational effort to divide Uzbek power and restore a
friendly client. The campaign shifted toward overreach after easy occupation
encouraged wider ambition and Murad destroyed the client design.

### Debate 4: personal whim or enduring interest?

Rulers' pride, prejudice and succession politics mattered in an autocratic
court. Yet security of Kabul, balance against Uzbeks, equality with major
courts and trade protection persisted across reigns. “National interests” is
modern analytical shorthand, not contemporary nationalism.

### Debate 5: why did Qandahar matter?

Reject every monocausal answer:

- prestige only;
- trade only;
- defence only;
- Timurid sentiment only.

Its importance lay in the interaction of fortification, roads, water,
commerce, intelligence and honour, with changing value over time.

**Deeper — Qandhar/Balkh as strategic choices, not imperial fantasy.**

✅ Qandhar dominated roads to Kabul and Herat, was a strong-watered fort, and linked India-Central Asia trade via Multan and the Indus.
- ✅ Satish Chandra cites the Kabul-Ghazni-Qandhar line as a strategic and logical frontier; for Iran, Qandhar was more an outpost than a vital bastion.
- ✅ Shah Jahan's Balkh expedition was meant to prop up Nazr Muhammad and prevent a united Uzbek threat to Kabul, not fix the frontier on the Oxus.
- ✅ The Mughals won militarily at Balkh, but could not hold the region due to hostile local population, climate, supply problems, Persian hostility and noble reluctance.
- ✅ The final assessment: Mughal foreign policy gave India long security from foreign invasions and promoted commercial interests through Kabul/Qandhar and Iran.
