## MATCHING MCQ ANSWER KEY AND REMEDIATION

### Workbook MCQ 01 — A
**Answer: A** - She combined Iltutmish's nomination with urban support in Delhi and division of the opposing elite.

- A. **Correct.** Her accession combined Iltutmish's own nomination with active Delhi mobilisation and elite division, so ability plus faction management put her on the throne, not birth order alone.
- B. **Incorrect.** There was no uncontested male primogeniture rule in this decade; Ruknuddin, Bahram and Masud were all removed by faction, not by an orderly succession law.
- C. **Incorrect.** No papal decree existed in the Delhi Sultanate; this option imports an unrelated medieval-Christian institution.
- D. **Incorrect.** Raziya did not conquer Ghazni; that theatre belonged to Iltutmish's earlier campaigns against Yildiz, a different ruler and a different decade.

**Trap: treating Raziya's accession as either pure hereditary right or pure popular acclaim, instead of nomination plus mobilisation plus elite division.**

### Workbook MCQ 02 — B
**Answer: B** - As amir-i-akhur he controlled royal stables and proximity to the sovereign, challenging Turkish monopoly over strategic access.

- A. **Incorrect.** Raziya's Delhi Sultanate had no Portuguese navy; the Portuguese arrive in Indian waters only from the late fifteenth century.
- B. **Correct.** The amir-i-akhur controlled the royal stables and, through them, physical proximity and access to the sovereign — a genuinely strategic post, which is why its holder's identity mattered politically.
- C. **Incorrect.** Bengal land revenue was governed by its own governors and later by Iltutmish's son, not by the amir-i-akhur, an entirely different court office.
- D. **Incorrect.** Yaqut was never caliph of Baghdad; conflating a royal-stable office with the Abbasid caliphate erases a real institutional distinction the question is testing.

**Trap: reducing Yaqut's promotion to personal favour and missing that the office itself, not merely the man, threatened Turkish elite access.**

### Workbook MCQ 03 — C
**Answer: C** - That a romantic intimacy was the decisive cause of her fall.

- A. **Incorrect.** Noble resentment at Yaqut's elevation is exactly what Minhaj-based accounts do support, so this option is a true, well-evidenced claim, not the one lacking support.
- B. **Incorrect.** Yaqut's amir-i-akhur post is directly attested and central to the analysis, so this is also a supported claim.
- C. **Correct.** No contemporary source supports a decisive romantic intimacy between Raziya and Yaqut; Minhaj records resentment over office and rebellion, not an affair, so this causal claim is a later, unsupported addition.
- D. **Incorrect.** Yaqut's death during the rebellion against Raziya is part of the same near-contemporary narrative and is not in dispute.

**Trap: the romance narrative is memorable and often repeated, which is precisely why an exam question isolates it as the one unsupported claim among otherwise solid facts.**

### Workbook MCQ 04 — D
**Answer: D** - Gendered legitimacy interacted with noble faction, provincial force, strategic office and coalition failure.

- A. **Incorrect.** Yaqut held one sensitive office, not total control of all policy; his death does not by itself explain the wider noble rebellion.
- B. **Incorrect.** Gender alone cannot explain why specific governors (Lahore, Tabarhinda) rebelled over specific offices and alliances — it explains vulnerability, not the whole mechanism.
- C. **Incorrect.** Delhi's population initially supported her accession at the congregational mosque; opposition grew later through elite and provincial action, not from unanimous early hostility.
- D. **Correct.** A complete explanation must combine gendered legitimacy, noble faction, provincial military force and coalition failure together, since each factor alone is insufficient to explain her deposition.

**Trap: picking one true-sounding partial factor (gender, or one noble, or one city) instead of the required multi-causal synthesis.**

### Workbook MCQ 05 — A
**Answer: A** - Hereditary legitimacy remained useful, but effective monarchy depended on control of nobles, offices and troops.

- A. **Correct.** The repeated depositions after Raziya show that hereditary claim alone could not secure the throne — actual control of nobles, offices and troops determined who stayed in power.
- B. **Incorrect.** The Abbasid caliph never personally selected Delhi's sultans; investiture (as with Iltutmish in 1229) was retrospective recognition, not appointment.
- C. **Incorrect.** Provinces such as Bengal became intermittently autonomous, not permanently independent; Delhi repeatedly reasserted suzerainty across this period.
- D. **Incorrect.** Delhi retained functioning institutions (iqta, army, chancery) throughout the crisis; the instability was political, not institutional collapse.

**Trap: assuming repeated depositions mean the state itself had no institutions, rather than that hereditary title and operational power had become separated.**

### Workbook MCQ 06 — B
**Answer: B** - Pious withdrawal is a strong literary image, but Balban's dominance was real, contested and not uninterrupted.

- A. **Incorrect.** Royal acts and administrative business continued under Nasiruddin Mahmud despite the pious-withdrawal image; a reigning sultan issuing zero acts for two decades is not evidenced.
- B. **Correct.** Balban was removed from his dominant position in 1253 and later returned by 1255, which proves his control over Nasiruddin Mahmud's court was real but contested, not a fixed, uninterrupted fact.
- C. **Incorrect.** Alauddin Khalji's market reforms belong to a different ruler and a later reign (Topic 04), decades after Nasiruddin Mahmud.
- D. **Incorrect.** Nasiruddin Mahmud reigned from 1246 to 1266, roughly two decades, not a single year.

**Trap: taking the moralising 'pious, withdrawn sultan' image literally instead of reading it as a literary device that still allows for real, contested politics underneath.**

### Workbook MCQ 07 — C
**Answer: C** - Minhaj lost office under Raihan, so institutional self-interest may shape blame.

- A. **Incorrect.** Minhaj was not related to Raihan; no father-son or family relationship is recorded between them.
- B. **Incorrect.** Minhaj, not Raihan, is the author of the *Tabaqat-i Nasiri*; this option reverses authorship.
- C. **Correct.** Minhaj lost his own office when Imaduddin Raihan rose to prominence, so his hostile account of Raihan carries a personal, institutional stake that a careful reader must weigh before accepting it as neutral.
- D. **Incorrect.** Minhaj and Raihan were contemporaries in the mid-thirteenth century, not separated by two centuries.

**Trap: treating a chronicler's hostile portrayal of a rival as simple, disinterested reporting rather than checking the author's own institutional position first.**

### Workbook MCQ 08 — D
**Answer: D** - A powerful naib and kingmaker whose supremacy was periodically challenged by other nobles.

- A. **Incorrect.** Balban held no Portuguese office; European colonial administration in Goa begins centuries later.
- B. **Incorrect.** The historical Abbasid caliph resided at Baghdad; Balban was never caliph, only a Delhi-based deputy and later sultan.
- C. **Incorrect.** Balban did not rule Bengal independently before 1266; Bengal's governors (including the later rebel Tughril) operated under separate, shifting arrangements.
- D. **Correct.** Before his formal accession in 1266, Balban was already the dominant naib (deputy) and kingmaker under Nasiruddin Mahmud, with his supremacy periodically challenged by rival nobles such as during the Raihan episode.

**Trap: dating Balban's political importance only from his 1266 coronation and missing the two preceding decades as dominant naib.**

### Workbook MCQ 09 — A
**Answer: A** - It transformed hierarchy and royal distance into visible awe over noble peers and visitors.

- A. **Correct.** Balban's court splendour converted physical distance, ceremony and restricted access into visible awe, turning ritual into a political tool that placed the sultan above noble peers.
- B. **Incorrect.** Barani's dramatic account is a later, normative narrative and cannot be treated as a literal, verified transcript of every court event.
- C. **Incorrect.** Court ceremony supplemented, rather than replaced, Balban's army, barids and frontier defences — it was one instrument among several.
- D. **Incorrect.** Court splendour was about hierarchy and awe, not a succession guarantee; Balban's own succession plan later collapsed despite decades of ceremonial authority.

**Trap: dismissing court ritual as empty decoration instead of recognising it as a deliberate technology of rule.**

### Workbook MCQ 10 — B
**Answer: B** - As a Persianate-Islamic claim that exalted the sultan's delegated sacred prestige above nobles, not literal godhood.

- A. **Incorrect.** The title does not abolish or replace Islam; it operates within an Islamic political vocabulary of delegated authority.
- B. **Correct.** *Zil-i-allah* ('shadow of God') is a Persianate-Islamic political idiom that elevated the sultan's delegated prestige above the nobility; it is a claim about rank and legitimacy, not literal divinity.
- C. **Incorrect.** It is a legitimising title, not a category of taxation; conflating the two erases the term's actual political function.
- D. **Incorrect.** No modern parliamentary election existed in the thirteenth-century Sultanate; the phrase describes monarchical, not representative, legitimacy.

**Trap: reading a medieval religio-political title literally as a theological claim to godhood rather than as a rank-elevating idiom.**

### Workbook MCQ 11 — C
**Answer: C** - They dramatised royal subordination but could conflict with theologians' view that prostration belonged to God.

- A. **Incorrect.** These terms name court rituals, not coins; no numismatic unit called sijda or paibos existed.
- B. **Incorrect.** Sijda and paibos are Persian-Arabic ritual terms, not the names of any Mongol fortification.
- C. **Correct.** *Sijda* (prostration) and *paibos* (foot-kissing) visibly dramatised a noble's subordination to the sultan, but some theologians objected because such prostration was traditionally reserved for God alone — creating genuine religious controversy.
- D. **Incorrect.** They describe bodily court gestures, not Rajput fiscal categories; the two belong to entirely different institutional domains.

**Trap: seeing sijda/paibos only as effective political theatre and missing that the same rituals provoked a genuine theological objection.**

### Workbook MCQ 12 — D
**Answer: D** - It is a later shorthand and must be tied to specific coercive policies rather than presented as a verified quotation.

- A. **Incorrect.** The phrase is used for Balban's law-and-order and frontier coercion generally, not narrowly for architecture.
- B. **Incorrect.** No coin evidence carries this phrase; it is a modern historiographical label, not a numismatic legend.
- C. **Incorrect.** The phrase has nothing to do with Raziya or clothing; it describes coercive statecraft under Balban, a different ruler and a different topic.
- D. **Correct.** "Blood and iron" is a later historians' shorthand for Balban's coercive practices; it should be tied to specific named measures (forest clearance, punitive campaigns) rather than quoted as if Balban himself said it.

**Trap: treating a vivid historian's shorthand as an authentic primary-source quotation that can be cited as Balban's own words.**

### Workbook MCQ 13 — A
**Answer: A** - Independent intelligence on officials, provincial events and oppression.

- A. **Correct.** Balban's *barids* existed mainly to give the centre independent intelligence on officials, provincial conditions and oppression, reducing his dependence on the self-interested reports of governors themselves.
- B. **Incorrect.** Barids were an intelligence/reporting institution, not a translation bureau; Sanskrit-text translation is not their attested function.
- C. **Incorrect.** Royal stables fell under the amir-i-akhur's charge (as with Yaqut under Raziya), a different office from the barid network.
- D. **Incorrect.** Sea-customs collection in Gujarat is a fiscal-administrative function unrelated to, and not evidenced for, the barid intelligence system.

**Trap: confusing the barid (intelligence) with other named offices (amir-i-akhur, revenue collectors) that appear elsewhere in the same topic.**

### Workbook MCQ 14 — B
**Answer: B** - They illustrate an ideal and possible exemplary enforcement, not a complete record of equal justice.

- A. **Incorrect.** Slavery as an institution continued through this period; these anecdotes are about elite punishment, not the abolition of slavery.
- B. **Correct.** Barani's stories about punishing officials like Bakbak and Haibat Khan illustrate the ideal of impartial royal justice and show it was sometimes enforced, but they were selected exemplary tales, not a comprehensive record of equal justice for all subjects.
- C. **Incorrect.** These are thirteenth-century chronicle narratives by Barani, not modern court judgments with formal legal records.
- D. **Incorrect.** The stories do name specific officials (Malik Bakbak of Badaun, Haibat Khan of Awadh), which is precisely why they function as evidence at all.

**Trap: treating a small set of dramatic, selected justice anecdotes as proof of a complete and equal judicial system.**

### Workbook MCQ 15 — C
**Answer: C** - To reconnect revenue assignment with actual military service and reduce unearned retention.

- A. **Incorrect.** The reform targeted specific unfit assignment-holders; it was not an attempt to abolish the *diwan-i-arz*, the department that oversaw army administration itself.
- B. **Incorrect.** There is no record of a mass transfer of soldiers to Bengal as part of this specific administrative reform.
- C. **Correct.** Balban's inquiry into aging soldiers who still held village revenue assignments aimed to reconnect those assignments with actual military service, reducing revenue retained without corresponding duty.
- D. **Incorrect.** The measure pushed against unearned, entrenched holdings; it did not aim to create hereditary zamindari-style landholding, which would contradict its stated purpose.

**Trap: confusing an accountability reform aimed at unearned military assignments with an unrelated administrative abolition or land-grant scheme.**

### Workbook MCQ 16 — D
**Answer: D** - It restored capital-route security through forest clearance, posts and settlement, but involved severe violence and displacement.

- A. **Incorrect.** Mewat lies inland near Delhi; this was a land-based law-and-order campaign, not a naval operation.
- B. **Incorrect.** Resistance around Delhi and the doab recurred across reigns; the campaign reduced but did not permanently end all regional resistance.

### Adjacent PYQ 01 - 2019 Prelims GS-I Q12: revenue administration
**Verified question:** Consider the following statements:

1. In the revenue administration of Delhi Sultanate, the in-charge of revenue collection was known as "Amil".
2. The Iqta system of Sultans of Delhi was an ancient indigenous institution.
3. The office of "Mir Bakshi" came into existence during the reign of Khalji Sultans of Delhi.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

- A. 1 only
- B. 1 and 2 only
- C. 3 only
- D. 1, 2 and 3

**INFERRED ANSWER - NOT OFFICIALLY VERIFIED LOCALLY:** A, 1 only. Confidence: high.

#### Solved explanation

- Statement 1 is correct: *amil* was a revenue-collection official.
- Statement 2 is incorrect: iqta had West and Central Asian antecedents. Under Delhi it became an adapted fiscal-military assignment; it was not an ancient indigenous institution.
- Statement 3 is incorrect: *Mir Bakshi* belongs to the Mughal administrative order. The Sultanate military department was associated with the *ariz* and *diwan-i-arz*.
- Topic significance: The question tests whether institutions are placed in the correct polity and whether iqta is mistaken for an indigenous hereditary land system.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates every statement independently, distinguishes institutional origin from Indian adaptation and avoids projecting a Mughal office backward.

**Demand decoding:** This is a three-statement elimination question, not a definition question — each statement names a distinct institution (*amil*, iqta, *Mir Bakshi*) and the correct response requires verifying each independently rather than treating the set as one claim about "Sultanate administration."

**Executable exam-length answer / compression plan:** State the *amil* fact first since it is uncontested; then attack statement 2 by naming the iqta's West/Central Asian antecedents (this is the trap statement, since "ancient indigenous" sounds plausible but is factually reversed); then eliminate statement 3 by placing *Mir Bakshi* correctly in the Mughal, not Sultanate, administrative order; conclude with option A.

**How to improve this answer:** Add the specific counter-fact that the Sultanate's own military department used the *ariz*/*diwan-i-arz* terminology, so a candidate who only remembers "Mir Bakshi is Mughal" without the Sultanate-era equivalent term loses precision marks in a written elimination note.

### Adjacent PYQ 03 - 2020 GS-I Mains: Persian literary sources
**Verified question:** "Persian literary sources of medieval India reflect the spirit of the age." Comment. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

- **Direct thesis:** Persian works reflect the age because their genres record conquest, court hierarchy, political ethics, urban culture and administrative aspiration; yet they reflect an elite courtly age more fully than the whole population.
- **Early conquest and legitimacy:** Hasan Nizami's *Taj-ul-Ma'asir* celebrates Ghurid-Aibak success in ornate panegyric. Its language reveals the ideology of victory and new court patronage, although literary display and selective chronology limit factual extraction.
- **Dynastic and institutional world:** Minhaj-us-Siraj's *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* preserves the Ghurid-Mamluk sequence, Bakhtiyar's career, Iltutmish's consolidation and Raziya's politics. Minhaj's praise of Raziya's qualities followed by a gendered judgement exposes both the possibility of female sovereignty and clerical-patriarchal assumptions.
- **Kingship and aristocracy:** Barani's later *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi* depicts the Chihalgani, Balban's court ritual, noble exclusivity, intelligence and justice. His *Fatawa-i Jahandari* reveals an aristocratic normative order, but his hostility to low-born advancement and chronological distance require corroboration.
- **Military-political advice:** Fakhr-i Mudabbir's writing illuminates ideals of army, hierarchy and Persianate statecraft. Prescriptive ideals cannot be equated with uniform practice.
- **Silences and checks:** These texts privilege male elites, capitals, war and office. Coins, inscriptions, Qutb architecture, local traditions and regional evidence are needed for currency, patronage, craft, non-elite life and implementation.
- **Conclusion:** Persian literary sources do reflect the spirit of medieval court and state formation, but only a source-critical, comparative reading converts that spirit into social history.

**Why this earns marks:** It comments rather than lists, uses four named authors and works, links evidence to political, gender and social meaning, and explicitly qualifies patronage and silence.

**Demand decoding:** "Comment" requires a direct evaluative position on whether Persian sources reflect "the spirit of the age," supported by named authors and texts, not a descriptive list of chronicles.

**Executable exam-length answer / compression plan:** Open with the qualified thesis (reflects the age, but an elite courtly one); take Hasan Nizami, Minhaj-us-Siraj, Barani and Fakhr-i Mudabbir as four evidence units in claim-evidence-limit form; close with the silences (coins, inscriptions, architecture, regional/non-elite life) that Persian literary sources alone cannot supply.

**How to improve this answer:** Name one specific silence the model leaves generic — for example, state explicitly that no Persian chronicle in this set reports the material terms of a specific iqta grant, so numismatic or epigraphic evidence must supply that gap rather than court narrative.

### Mains 15M-3 - Early Sultanate architecture

**Question:** Early Sultanate architecture was produced through conquest, reuse and technical adaptation rather than a simple story of either destruction or synthesis. Discuss. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

- Direct thesis: The Qutb-Ajmer monuments record coercive appropriation and temple-material reuse, while also documenting Indian craft labour, new congregational forms and gradual mastery of arcuate construction. Both rupture and adaptation must be retained.
- Conquest and reuse: ASI records that Quwwat-ul-Islam's cloisters incorporated members from twenty-seven Hindu and Jaina temples, as stated in its entrance inscription. Adhai Din ka Jhonpra likewise occupies an earlier Sanskrit-college/temple context. This is evidence of conquest and appropriation, not neutral recycling.
- Expediency and claim: Reused pillars supplied material and skilled carving for rapid monumental assertion. Victory inscriptions and congregational plans transformed the political meaning of components; expediency does not erase ideology.
- Craft and form: Indian masons and stone-carvers executed the buildings. Local trabeate/corbelled methods and motifs coexisted with West Asian arch, screen, calligraphy and mosque planning. "Synthesis" is meaningful only when these specific processes are named.
- Technical sequence: Aibak began Qutb Minar and Iltutmish added major storeys. Iltutmish's tomb shows square-to-dome experimentation; Balban's tomb contains the first surviving true arch identified by the assigned source; Alai Darwaza later shows mature technique.
- Limits: Buildings have additions and repair phases; monuments represent elite patronage more than ordinary social experience. "First arch in India" is an overclaim because arches were known earlier.
- Conclusion: Early Sultanate architecture is best read as a conflict-laden laboratory where reused material, local skill and new political-religious forms produced a changing architectural language.

**Why this earns marks:** It names four monuments, uses ASI inscriptional evidence, distinguishes ideology from expediency and traces technical development with a precise true-arch caveat.

### Mains 20M-1 - Nature of the early Sultanate

**Question:** Was the early Delhi Sultanate a centralised territorial state or a negotiated network of military power? Analyse. (20 marks, 300 words)

#### Model answer

- Direct thesis: It was centralised in sovereign claim, capital institutions and fiscal-military aspiration, but negotiated and uneven in operation. The apparent alternatives describe different levels of the same state.
- Central apex: Aibak's separation from Ghazni and Iltutmish's defeat of Aram, Yildiz and Qubacha established independent sovereignty. The sultan distributed office and iqta, commanded the army, minted currency and claimed royal justice. Delhi's court, chancery and monumental programme made the apex visible.
- Institutional density: Wazir, ariz, insha, qazi-sadr functions, barids, khalisa and iqta differentiated central tasks. Iltutmish's tanka-jital and 1229 investiture strengthened fiscal and symbolic authority. The exact departmental form evolved and cannot be treated as a modern bureaucracy.
- Negotiated implementation: Muqtis, fort commanders, tributary rulers and local khuts, muqaddams, chiefs and accountants translated orders into revenue and force. An iqta was transferable in principle, but distance and armed followings enabled autonomy.
- Regional evidence: Bengal repeatedly submitted and rebelled; Iltutmish's Gujarat venture failed; Ranthambhor could be held indirectly; western Punjab was exposed to Mongols. Balban killed Tughril but Bughra Khan later detached Bengal.
- Social composition: The ruling elite was initially Turkic-Tajik and Persianate, yet the state relied on Indian Muslim officers, Hindu chiefs, soldiers, artisans and agrarian intermediaries. It was an institutional form of conquest, but not socially self-contained.
- Centralising turn: Balban's court ritual, spies, direct force, road security and iqta supervision increased royal capacity. His dependence on personal coercion, narrow high-born ideology and failed succession reveals the limit.
- Conceptual qualification: It was neither a modern unitary territorial state nor a formless confederacy. "Feudal" and "theocratic" labels require precise definitions.
- Conclusion: The best model is a hierarchical military-fiscal network: dense at Delhi and the upper doab, thinner and more negotiated toward Punjab, Bengal and Rajasthan.

**Why this earns marks:** It resolves the binary through level-of-analysis, uses institutions plus four regional tests, includes social composition and ends with a spatially graded verdict.

**Demand decoding:** "Analyse" a stated either/or ("centralised territorial state OR negotiated network") requires the answer to resolve the binary rather than pick one side — the highest-scoring move is showing both descriptions are true at different levels (apex versus operation).

**Executable exam-length answer / compression plan:** In 300 words, structure by level, not by ruler: apex/sovereign claim, institutional density (wazir/ariz/insha/barid), negotiated implementation (muqtis, local intermediaries), then four regional stress-tests (Bengal, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab) before the "hierarchical military-fiscal network" conclusion.

**How to improve this answer:** Add one explicit sentence stating why "feudal" and "theocratic" are inadequate labels here — iqta was transferable state revenue, not hereditary property, and caliphal idiom did not equal caliphal administration — since blocking those two mislabels is itself a mark-earning move in this specific question.

### Mains 20M-3 - Sources and historiography of the Mamluk century

**Question:** Evaluate the textual and material sources for the Mamluk period and show how they shape modern interpretations. (20 marks, 300 words)

#### Model answer

- Direct thesis: The Mamluk century is reconstructed from Persian court texts, advice literature, coins, inscriptions and monuments. Their combined strength is institutional and political detail; their shared weakness is elite selection, uneven chronology and silence on much of society.
- Hasan Nizami: Taj-ul-Ma'asir offers an early Ghurid-Aibak conquest narrative and language of legitimation. Ornate panegyric, selective chronology and court dependence require separation of report from rhetoric.
- Minhaj-us-Siraj: Tabaqat-i Nasiri is indispensable for Ghurid genealogy, Bakhtiyar, Iltutmish, Raziya and the early Balban world. Minhaj's contemporary position improves access, but clerical values, patronage and office interests shape his account; his judgement on Raziya exposes gender bias.
- Fakhr-i Mudabbir: Political-military advice illuminates ideals of army, hierarchy and literate administration. Prescriptive norms cannot prove implementation.
- Barani: Writing decades later, he supplies the Chihalgani label, Balban's ceremonies, noble exclusivity, barids and justice. His high-born prejudice and normative theory make literal numbers, speeches and motives unsafe without corroboration.
- Coins and inscriptions: Aibak-Iltutmish titulature, mints, tanka-jital circulation, caliphal formulas and Qutb inscriptions offer more contemporary public claims. Circulation and titulature do not map uniform power.
- Architecture: Quwwat-ul-Islam, Qutb Minar, Ajmer, Sultan Ghari, Iltutmish's and Balban's tombs reveal patronage, spolia, craft and technical sequence. Repair phases and elite survival complicate reading.
- Silence and social history: Court texts marginalise cultivators, artisans, women outside the palace and regional voices. Local intermediaries and craft agency must be inferred cautiously from institutions and material practice.
- Historiography: Colonial "Slave dynasty/despotism" labels over-literalised status and rupture; institutional studies recovered iqta and state formation; Persianate-network, gender and architectural readings add mobility, source position and material complexity.
- Conclusion: Strong interpretation comes from triangulation: every claim should name evidence, state significance and expose what the archive cannot prove.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates five source classes, names works and monuments, links bias to specific interpretations and ends with a reusable historical method.

**Demand decoding:** "Evaluate ... and show how they shape modern interpretations" is a two-part demand — assess each source class's strength/limit, and then explicitly connect that limit to a named historiographical shift (colonial, institutional, gender-critical), not just list sources.

**Executable exam-length answer / compression plan:** In 300 words, take six source classes (Hasan Nizami, Minhaj, Fakhr-i Mudabbir, Barani, coins/inscriptions, architecture) as evidence units, then use one closing paragraph to name the three historiographical schools (colonial "despotism," institutional/material, gender/source-critical) each source class helped produce or correct.

**How to improve this answer:** Add the specific link that Minhaj's own gendered verdict on Raziya is the same passage gender-critical historians later reread to expose clerical bias, since naming that exact textual link (not just "gender bias exists") is what proves the "shape modern interpretations" half of the demand.
