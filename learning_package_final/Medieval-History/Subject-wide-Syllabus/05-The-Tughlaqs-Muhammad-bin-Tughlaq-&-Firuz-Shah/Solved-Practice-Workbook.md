### MCQ KEY AND EXPLANATIONS

### Workbook MCQ 01 — Key
**Answer: A** - a fortified dynastic statement of military austerity and new legitimacy.

- A. **Correct.** The fortress-capital, together with Ghiyasuddin's elevated domed tomb, announced a new dynastic idiom of austere fortified strength in place of ornament.
- B. **Incorrect.** Tughlaqabad's battered walls, high platforms and fortified circuit are defensive and ceremonial architecture; nothing in the complex resembles a pleasure garden.
- C. **Incorrect.** Portuguese establishments in India begin after 1498, more than one hundred and seventy years after Tughlaqabad was built.
- D. **Incorrect.** Mughal rule begins in 1526, and the observatory tradition associated with Jai Singh belongs to the eighteenth century.

**Trap: Reading a fourteenth-century fortress-capital as decoration, or as a later Portuguese or Mughal structure, instead of as a dynastic legitimacy statement.**

### Workbook MCQ 02 — Key
**Answer: B** - The secure event is the pavilion collapse; conspiracy and curse narratives belong to layered and disputed source traditions.

- A. **Incorrect.** The Nizamuddin Auliya curse belongs to a legendary layer of the tradition and cannot be tested as historical evidence.
- B. **Correct.** The secure minimum is the collapse of the welcome pavilion at Afghanpur in 1325; accident, negligence and conspiracy remain distinct and unresolved source layers.
- C. **Incorrect.** Contemporary suspicion existed, but the advanced source states that modern research does not support a proved murderous conspiracy.
- D. **Incorrect.** Barani, Ibn Battuta and later traditions all report the episode, so the difficulty is contradiction between the sources rather than their silence.

**Trap: Choosing certainty where the evidence supports only a layered account, either by convicting Muhammad or by promoting the curse legend to the status of fact.**

### Workbook MCQ 03 — Key
**Answer: C** - Devagiri was already a strategic Deccan base and more central to the enlarged imperial space.

- A. **Incorrect.** Daulatabad lies in the northern Deccan; the Himalayan direction belongs to the entirely separate Qarachil expedition.
- B. **Incorrect.** Deogir was an established Yadava capital with an existing population and fortifications, which is part of why it was selected.
- C. **Correct.** Devagiri had been raided by Alauddin, used later as a Delhi base and commanded by Muhammad himself as prince, so it was a known node closer to the enlarged empire's centre.
- D. **Incorrect.** Delhi had campaigned against and extracted tribute from Devagiri since Alauddin's raid, so the connection long predated the renaming.

**Trap: Treating the second capital as an arbitrary site instead of a pre-existing Deccan base that Delhi had already raided, used and governed.**

### Workbook MCQ 04 — Key
**Answer: D** - He imposed severe movement pressure, but mainly on elites and associated groups; Delhi remained inhabited and functioning.

- A. **Incorrect.** Deogir was renamed Daulatabad and developed with an organised road, ward divisions and housing grants; denying the project replaces one error with another.
- B. **Incorrect.** The sources record organised, coerced migration and real hardship on the journey, so the correction is against totality, not against movement.
- C. **Incorrect.** Bengal was a recurrently centrifugal province, not a source of migrants to the Deccan; this confuses two separate strands of the reign.
- D. **Correct.** Pressure fell chiefly on officers, nobles, leading men, Sufis, ulema, merchants and artisans, while Delhi retained both its population and its mint.

**Trap: Over-correcting the emptied-Delhi myth into a denial that any coerced migration, surveillance or hardship took place at all.**

### Workbook MCQ 05 — Key
**Answer: A** - paper currency.

- A. **Correct.** The coins were copper and brass; calling them paper currency imports the Chinese precedent into India and misstates the physical object.
- B. **Incorrect.** Fiduciary is exactly the right description, because the metal's intrinsic worth lay below the value the state declared for it.
- C. **Incorrect.** Private imitation and the inability to authenticate official issue form the package's central explanation of the failure.
- D. **Incorrect.** Credibility is the correct analytical frame, since a declared value survives only while the public trusts the issuer.

**Trap: Repeating the textbook line that Muhammad bin Tughlaq issued paper money, when the paper precedent named by the sources is Chinese rather than Indian.**

### Workbook MCQ 06 — Key
**Answer: B** - Failure to maintain trust by controlling authentication and counterfeiting.

- A. **Incorrect.** Military spending was heavy, and Barani connects the issue to military ambition rather than to any absence of it.
- B. **Correct.** Without a mint monopoly and a workable test of authenticity the state could not separate its own issue from imitations, and confidence collapsed.
- C. **Incorrect.** The problem was the acceptance of too many coins of uncertain origin, not a boycott of metal currency by the nobility.
- D. **Incorrect.** The experiment was withdrawn by Muhammad himself, decades before Firuz Shah's accession in 1351.

**Trap: Attributing the collapse to novelty, to elite hostility or to a later reign, instead of to the missing verification machinery.**

### Workbook MCQ 07 — Key
**Answer: C** - Assessment by standard yield and official prices rather than actual output and market reality.

- A. **Incorrect.** Collection remained firmly in official hands and was intensified, with cattle branded and houses counted.
- B. **Incorrect.** The crisis arose from a raised and rigidly enforced demand, not from any remission of revenue.
- C. **Correct.** Standard yield and official prices fix the demand against a notional harvest and a notional market, so the real burden rises whenever output or prices fall.
- D. **Incorrect.** Canal irrigation belongs to Firuz's later northern programme and carried a water charge; it was never offered free across India.

**Trap: Reading the doab crisis as a simple increase in the tax rate rather than as a change in the method of assessment operating under famine conditions.**

### Workbook MCQ 08 — Key
**Answer: D** - the severity of scarcity and pestilential conditions around Delhi during the agrarian crisis.

- A. **Incorrect.** The move was an administrative and sanitary response to crisis conditions, not a religious withdrawal from rule.
- B. **Incorrect.** Swargadwari was a temporary camp; Bengal in this reign is a rebellious province rather than a royal residence.
- C. **Incorrect.** The Bahmani breakaway occurred in the Deccan near Daulatabad and belongs to a separate strand of the reign.
- D. **Correct.** The advanced source links the move to famine, rising grain prices, relief camps, grain brought from Awadh and pestilential conditions around Delhi.

**Trap: Detaching the Swargadwari camp from the doab famine sequence and presenting it as an eccentric personal decision.**

### Workbook MCQ 09 — Key
**Answer: A** - extend cultivation onto banjar land while improving crops through loans and supervision.

- A. **Correct.** The advanced source insists the target was banjar or fallow land, worked through sondhar loans and supervised by about a hundred appointed shiqdars.
- B. **Incorrect.** The department was agricultural; no maritime or customs institution is attributed to it anywhere in the sources.
- C. **Incorrect.** Its stated purpose was to extend cultivation and improve crop quality after the doab crisis, not to remove land from production.
- D. **Incorrect.** The scheme appointed shiqdars as revenue-development officials; it did not restructure the nobility or transfer office to priests.

**Trap: Accepting Barani's sharper wording that genuinely barren usar soil was being farmed, which converts a risky programme into an absurdity.**

### Workbook MCQ 10 — Key
**Answer: B** - Chroniclers inflate its scale and aim, so it should be treated as a strategic frontier project rather than automatic proof of world-conquest fantasy.

- A. **Incorrect.** The project arose directly from Transoxianan, Iranian, Ghazni and Afghan frontier politics after the Tarmashirin episode.
- B. **Correct.** The defensible reading limits the aim to a forward frontier and prestige objective and marks the reported scale as chronicler claim rather than audited fact.
- C. **Incorrect.** Khurasan was a plan supported by recruitment; Qarachil was an executed Himalayan campaign with an entirely different failure mode.
- D. **Incorrect.** The army was never launched as envisaged, and Iraq appears only in Barani's expanded phrasing about the aim.

**Trap: Converting Barani's expansive phrasing about Khurasan and Iraq into proof of a coherent transcontinental conquest programme.**

### Workbook MCQ 11 — Key
**Answer: C** - a Himalayan campaign wrecked by terrain and over-extension.

- A. **Incorrect.** No naval enterprise toward Arabia is attributed to the reign in any of the sources used here.
- B. **Incorrect.** Firozabad on the Yamuna is Firuz Shah's urban foundation, not a campaign undertaken by Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
- C. **Correct.** The advanced source places the campaign in the Kulu-Kangra and Kumaon-Kashmir horizon, where terrain, climate and supply destroyed the force.
- D. **Incorrect.** Jizya was not abolished; Firuz later made it a separate tax and extended it to Brahmans.

**Trap: Repeating the later claim that Qarachil was an invasion of China, which the advanced source explicitly rejects.**

### Workbook MCQ 12 — Key
**Answer: D** - Plague, distance and repeated rebellion weakened Delhi's direct rule, creating the setting for successor powers such as Vijayanagara and Bahmani.

- A. **Incorrect.** The outcome was fragmentation into competing successor states, not a federation of any kind.
- B. **Incorrect.** The far south is precisely where Delhi's control collapsed first and most completely.
- C. **Incorrect.** The Bahmani breakaway came from foreign nobles near Daulatabad in the Deccan, not from Bengal.
- D. **Correct.** Plague during the Mabar campaign, sheer distance and repeated revolt broke Delhi's direct rule, and Harihara and Bukka and the Bahmani founders emerged in that setting.

**Trap: Importing the full institutional history of Vijayanagara or the Bahmani kingdom into Topic 05, which owns only the Delhi-side conditions that allowed them to form.**

### Workbook MCQ 13 — Key
**Answer: A** - He is strongest on courtly and travel experience, not on comprehensive village-level structural data.

- A. **Correct.** His strength is courtly ceremony, punishments, gifts, roads, products and urban scale, and the concise source notes his limited interest in ordinary lives.
- B. **Incorrect.** He lived for years at Muhammad bin Tughlaq's court and held judicial office there, which is why his testimony matters.
- C. **Incorrect.** His Indian service belongs to Muhammad bin Tughlaq's reign, not to Firuz Shah's.
- D. **Incorrect.** The Gupta period ends centuries earlier; his subject is the fourteenth-century Sultanate.

**Trap: Treating an eyewitness traveller's vividness as statistically representative evidence about village society.**

### Workbook MCQ 14 — Key
**Answer: B** - Barani is indispensable but rhetorical, later and aristocratic, so his claims require cross-checking and minimum-secure phrasing.

- A. **Incorrect.** The Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi and the Fatawa-i Jahandari are Barani's own compositions, not Ibn Battuta's.
- B. **Correct.** Barani writes later, from an aristocratic position and in a rhetorical register, so his figures and speeches require cross-checking and minimum-secure phrasing.
- C. **Incorrect.** He is indispensable for the policy sequence, elite ideology and the experiments themselves, so total rejection destroys the evidence base.
- D. **Incorrect.** Coins, canals, forts and pillars are exactly the corrective the package applies to literary claims.

**Trap: Swinging from literal acceptance of Barani's rhetoric to wholesale rejection of the only detailed narrative the reign possesses.**

### Workbook MCQ 15 — Key
**Answer: C** - Because it is a ruler's self-presentation and must be checked against other narrative and material evidence.

- A. **Incorrect.** It is a fourteenth-century record of Firuz's works and religious policy, composed long before Portuguese arrival.
- B. **Incorrect.** Its account of taxation, orthodoxy and public works is intensely political throughout.
- C. **Correct.** It is the ruler's preferred account of himself and must be checked against other narrative and material evidence.
- D. **Incorrect.** Chengiz Khan belongs to the early thirteenth-century Mongol expansion, not to Tughlaq Delhi.

**Trap: Using a ruler's own self-presentation as a neutral audit of his reign's achievements.**

### Workbook MCQ 16 — Key
**Answer: D** - They anchor claims in material action while reminding us that physical survival does not automatically equal full administrative control.

- A. **Incorrect.** Delhi mint continuity, Tughlaqabad, Kotla Firoz Shah and the transported pillars are all medieval material evidence.
- B. **Incorrect.** Material survival confirms activity but cannot verify reported totals of slaves, troops or tankas.
- C. **Incorrect.** Without the chronicles the material record would carry no policy narrative against which it could be tested.
- D. **Correct.** They anchor literary claims in demonstrable action while reminding the reader that surviving fabric is not proof of uniform administrative control.

**Trap: Treating monuments and coins either as decorative background or as an omniscient substitute for the written record.**

### Workbook MCQ 17 — Key
**Answer: A** - an attempt to answer orthodox hostility by reinforcing symbolic legitimacy.

- A. **Correct.** After qazis issued a fatwa making rebellion legally possible, the Sultan placed the Caliph's name on his coins and later obtained a manshur from the Cairo Abbasid line.
- B. **Incorrect.** Seeking caliphal recognition intensifies Islamic political language rather than rejecting it.
- C. **Incorrect.** Bengal was a breakaway province; the capitals in question are Delhi and Daulatabad.
- D. **Incorrect.** Canal digging belongs to Firuz Shah's later reign and had no connection with caliphal recognition.

##### Adjacent PYQ 03 - UPSC GS-I 2020 (adjacent but highly useful source-criticism question; wording preserved in repository)

**Question:** Persian literary sources of medieval India reflect the spirit of the age. Comment. (Answer in 250 words)

**Model answer:**

Persian literary sources are indispensable for the Delhi Sultanate because they preserve the political language, moral anxieties and courtly horizons of the age. Yet they reflect the spirit of the age only when read with **genre, patronage and author-position caution**.  

Topic 05 offers an ideal demonstration. **Barani** is central for Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token currency, Daulatabad, doab crisis and elite theory. But he was a later aristocratic moralist with open prejudice against the "low and ignoble"; his accounts often magnify exemplary success or failure to teach political lessons. **Isami** is useful for Deccan dislocation and Delhi overreach, but he writes from displacement and retrospective memory. **Ibn Battuta** gives vivid evidence on court ritual, punishments, gifts, roads and city-life under Muhammad, yet his traveller's gaze highlights the remarkable rather than the statistically normal. Under Firuz, **Afif** and the *Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi* show public works, benevolence and orthodoxy, but they also stage the ruler's preferred self-image.  

These texts therefore reflect not a neutral mirror-image of society, but the **refracted political culture** of their age: ideas of just rule, elite resentment, religious legitimacy, expansion, urbanity and fear. When paired with coins, canals, monuments and inscriptions, they become even more powerful.  

Hence Persian literary sources do reflect the spirit of the age, but the spirit they reveal is courtly, argumentative and mediated - never transparent fact.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses Topic 05's source bank directly, names authors, explains what each source proves, and converts "reflect" into a method question rather than a slogan.

**Demand decoding:** “Comment” on whether Persian literary sources reflect the spirit of the age demands a direct position on the word “reflect”: the answer must decide whether these texts mirror their society or refract it, and must prove the decision with named authors rather than with a general defence of Persian historiography.

**Executable exam-length answer / compression plan:** Open by conceding indispensability and immediately qualifying “reflect”. Then run four named units in 250 words - Barani on Muhammad's experiments with his aristocratic moralism; Isami on Deccan dislocation with his displacement; Ibn Battuta on court, roads and punishments with his traveller's preference for the remarkable; Afif and the *Futuhat* on Firuz's works with their self-fashioning. Close on refracted political culture checked against coins, canals and monuments.

**How to improve this answer:** The weakest move is the generalisation that these texts are “courtly”. Replace it with one concrete instance - Barani's line that every Hindu house became a mint - and state precisely what it can and cannot prove: it establishes that counterfeiting was widespread enough to destroy confidence, but it cannot establish how many forgeries existed or where they circulated.

##### Adjacent PYQ 04 - UPSC GS-I 2023 (adjacent broader Sultanate question; wording preserved in repository)

**Question:** What were the major technological changes introduced during the Sultanate period? How did those technological changes influence the Indian society? (Answer in 250 words)

**Model answer:**

The Sultanate period mattered technologically not because it created an entirely new civilisation, but because it accelerated the diffusion and combination of techniques in construction, military administration, irrigation and craft production. Their influence was strongest in **state capacity, urban growth and social differentiation**.  

In construction, the stronger use of the **true arch, dome and lime mortar** enlarged the scale of buildings and changed the skyline of power. Topic 05's Tughlaq phase reveals a further turn toward battered defensive walls, elevated platforms and large repair projects under Firuz. In irrigation, canals associated with Firuz Shah show technology as agrarian development and revenue policy, not merely engineering. Their impact lay in settlement growth, cultivation expansion and the strengthening of the north-Indian core.  

Administrative technology also mattered. Horse-branding, descriptive rolls and revenue audit systems show that information itself was a technology of rule. Even Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token-currency scheme, though it failed, belonged to the same world of technical experimentation tied to fiscal governance.  

The social effect was uneven. These changes empowered states, towns, merchants, military systems and courtly centres more than they democratised society. Monumental architecture, canals and money policies deepened stratification even while widening circulation and urban connection.  

Thus Sultanate technological change shaped Indian society by linking technique to power, production and urban order.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both parts, uses Tughlaq-era examples rather than generic listing, and converts technology into social consequence.

**Question:** Why should the Daulatabad and token-currency measures of Muhammad bin Tughlaq be analysed as problems of administrative credibility rather than as mere eccentricity?
