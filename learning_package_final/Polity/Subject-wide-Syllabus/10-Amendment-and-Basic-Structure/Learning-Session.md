## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### SESSION 1 — WHY A CONSTITUTION NEEDS CHANGE WITHOUT SELF-DESTRUCTION

**Stage:** Foundation

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL CHANGE
   |
   +--> routine adjustment through an express ordinary-law route
   +--> entrenched change through Article 368 special majority
   +--> federal-core change through special majority + State ratification
   |
   `--> ALL ROUTES REMAIN UNDER CONSTITUTIONAL SUPREMACY
          Article 368 may transform the charter; it cannot destroy its identity.
```

*The visual separates procedural flexibility from the substantive identity limit.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Constitutional amendment** is formal alteration of the Constitution under an authorised constitutional route. Article 368 is the principal route and describes amendment by **addition, variation or repeal**.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> India combines a living Constitution with an entrenched identity: change is authorised, but constitutional self-destruction is not.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**Part XX · Article 368 · constituent power · addition · variation · repeal · constitutional supremacy · flexibility-rigidity synthesis**

#### Core idea

- [FACT] Article 368 is the sole Article in Part XX, “Amendment of the Constitution”.
- [FACT] Parliament uses the ordinary Houses, yet acts in a special constituent capacity rather than through ordinary legislative competence alone.
- [ANALYSIS] Flexibility answers whether the text can respond to new needs; rigidity answers how much consent change requires; basic structure answers whether even procedurally valid change preserves constitutional identity.
- [LIMIT] “Flexible” and “rigid” are comparative descriptions. The legal answer must identify the actual constitutional route.
- [ANALYSIS] The correct conflict is constitutional supremacy versus claims of unlimited parliamentary power, not a simplistic judiciary-versus-democracy contest.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** India uses graded entrenchment. **Evidence:** Article 368 supplies constituent power and special majorities, while Articles 2-4 and similar provisions authorise selected ordinary-law changes. **Analysis:** The Constitution can adapt without making every provision equally easy or equally difficult to alter. **Qualification:** *Kesavananda Bharati* adds that even the strictest procedure cannot validate destruction of basic structure.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Part XX; Article 368(1); Articles 2-4; *Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973); *Minerva Mills* (1980).

#### PRELIMS TRAP

“Constituent power” does not mean legally sovereign or immune power.

#### MAINS USE

Use this as the introduction to procedure, legitimacy, limited-power or constitutional-identity questions.

#### MINI RECAP

Three procedural levels enable change; basic structure supplies the substantive stop-line.

### SESSION 2 — THREE ROUTES AND THE MAJORITY VOCABULARY

**Stage:** Foundation

#### VISUAL FIRST

| Route | Parliamentary threshold | Additional consent | Legal character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Express ordinary-law route | Ordinary majority unless the enabling provision adds a special trigger | None under Article 368 | Not deemed an Article 368 amendment |
| Article 368 special majority | Majority of total membership **and** at least two-thirds present and voting in each House | None | Most entrenched provisions |
| Article 368 federal route | Same dual special majority | Ratification by not less than half the States | Exact proviso subjects only |

*The table prevents “simple”, “effective” and Article 368 special majorities from being mixed.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

India uses **graded entrenchment**: the required consent rises with the constitutional route selected by the text.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> The Constitution does not rank amendments by political importance; it assigns a route through the enabling provision and the Article 368 proviso.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**outside Article 368 · ordinary law · total membership · present and voting · effective majority · enabling provision · Article 169 qualification**

#### Route 1 — constitutionally authorised ordinary law outside Article 368

These are commonly called “amendments by simple majority”, but the precise proposition is that a constitutional provision authorises ordinary legislation and often states that the change is not deemed an Article 368 amendment.

| Constitutional field | High-yield change | Precision |
|---|---|---|
| Articles 2-4 | Admission/establishment of States; formation, area, boundary or name changes; consequential First/Fourth Schedule changes | Article 4 supplies the non-Article-368 rule |
| Article 11 | Acquisition, termination and other citizenship law | Detailed citizenship doctrine belongs to Topic 6 |
| Article 169 | Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council | State Assembly first uses majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting; Parliament’s Act is ordinary |
| Article 239A and related UT grants | Constitutionally authorised UT legislature/executive arrangements | Check the exact enabling provision |
| Fifth Schedule para 7; Sixth Schedule para 21 | Schedule amendment through their own mechanisms | Expressly outside Article 368 |
| Parliamentary functioning | Quorum, salaries/allowances, procedure, privileges and authorised language adjustments | Does not authorise rewriting every related constitutional rule |
| Elections and delimitation | Ordinary election machinery/readjustment where constitutionally authorised | Entrenched State representation remains different |
| Judiciary details | Number of Supreme Court judges and further jurisdiction within an express grant | Altering the Union Judiciary chapter triggers ratification |
| Second Schedule matters | Authorised emolument/allowance adjustments | Apply the specific constitutional grant |

#### Route 2 and Route 3

- [FACT] Both House limbs are simultaneous: a majority of **total membership** plus not less than **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
- [FACT] “Total membership” uses full sanctioned strength; vacancies do not reduce it.
- [FACT] Abstentions are excluded from “present and voting”, but not from total membership.
- [FACT] The federal route adds State ratification; it does not replace either House threshold.

#### Effective majority — relevant contrast, not an Article 368 rule

**Effective majority** means a majority of the House’s then-existing strength after vacancies and is used for selected removal resolutions elsewhere. Article 368 instead says **majority of total membership**, so the effective-majority denominator must not be imported.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Constitutional change follows three routes. **Evidence:** Article 4 excludes specified State reorganisation changes from Article 368; Article 169 uses an ordinary parliamentary law after a specially passed Assembly resolution; Article 368 demands two House majorities and sometimes State ratification. **Analysis:** Different consent costs preserve adaptability and entrenchment. **Qualification:** “Simple majority” is a shorthand, not proof that every step uses an ordinary majority.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Articles 4, 11, 169, 239A and 368; Fifth Schedule paragraph 7; Sixth Schedule paragraph 21.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

Effective majority is not the “majority of total membership” limb in Article 368.

#### MAINS USE

Use for flexibility-rigidity, classification and procedural-limit answers.

#### MINI RECAP

First identify the enabling provision; then calculate the correct threshold.

### SESSION 3 — ARTICLE 368 PROCEDURE FROM INTRODUCTION TO ASSENT

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
BILL IN EITHER HOUSE
 minister OR private member | no prior Presidential recommendation
        |
        v
EACH HOUSE PASSES SEPARATELY
 total-membership majority + 2/3 present and voting
        |
        +--> NO JOINT SITTING
        |
        v
STATE RATIFICATION, ONLY IF PROVISO APPLIES
        |
        v
PRESIDENT SHALL ASSENT
        |
        v
CONSTITUTION STANDS AMENDED ACCORDING TO THE ACT'S COMMENCEMENT TERMS
```

*The process contains sequential veto points and no shortcut around either House.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **Article 368 amendment procedure** is the mandatory sequence governing initiation, separate House passage, conditional State ratification and Presidential assent.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Article 368 gives both Houses equal constituent authority: failure in either House is final for that Bill because no joint sitting exists.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**either House · minister/private member · no prior recommendation · each House separately · no joint sitting · President shall assent · no referendum**

#### Exact procedure

1. [FACT] Only a Bill in either House of Parliament can initiate an Article 368 amendment; a State legislature cannot introduce it.
2. [FACT] A minister or private member may introduce it.
3. [FACT] Prior Presidential recommendation is unnecessary.
4. [FACT] Each House separately passes both majority limbs.
5. [FACT] Article 108 joint sitting is unavailable for rejection, disagreement or delay.
6. [FACT] If the proviso applies, State ratification must occur before presentation to the President.
7. [FACT] The President **shall give assent**; the Twenty-fourth Amendment made the command obligatory. The Bill is not returned or withheld like an ordinary Bill.
8. [FACT] On assent, the Constitution stands amended, subject to the Act’s own commencement design.
9. [FACT] Article 368 creates no referendum and no special constitutional convention or constituent assembly.
10. [LIMIT] The Constitution fixes no general time limit for parliamentary passage or State ratification.

#### Dissolution precision

Article 107(5) says a Bill pending in Rajya Sabha that Lok Sabha has not passed does not lapse merely because Lok Sabha is dissolved. This narrow posture must not become the slogan “constitutional amendment Bills never lapse”.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** The process uses ordinary institutions under extraordinary rules. **Evidence:** Either House may initiate, both Houses must independently pass the dual majority, Article 108 is unavailable and the President shall assent after the Twenty-fourth Amendment. **Analysis:** Independent bicameral passage prevents one House from converting electoral strength into unilateral constituent power. **Qualification:** The protected Rajya Sabha dissolution posture is narrow and does not answer every lapse scenario.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Articles 107(5), 108 and 368; Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

No prior Presidential recommendation, but mandatory Presidential assent at the end.

#### MAINS USE

Use as the procedural half of the 2025 GS-II demand.

#### MINI RECAP

Either House can begin; both must separately finish; the President must assent.

### SESSION 4 — FEDERAL RATIFICATION: EXACT SUBJECTS AND STATE ROLE

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
PARLIAMENT PASSES BOTH SPECIAL-MAJORITY LIMBS
                     |
                     v
DOES THE BILL CHANGE AN EXACT PROVISO SUBJECT?
       | NO                              | YES
       v                                 v
PRESIDENTIAL ASSENT          RESOLUTIONS OF >= HALF THE STATES
                                      |
                                      v
                             PRESIDENTIAL ASSENT
```

| Exact proviso cluster | Textual subjects |
|---|---|
| Named Articles | 54, 55, 73, 162, 241 and 279A |
| Named Chapters | Chapter IV of Part V; Chapter V of Part VI; Chapter I of Part XI |
| Federal distribution | Any List in the Seventh Schedule |
| State voice | Representation of States in Parliament |
| Amending rule | Article 368 itself |

*The visual replaces vague “federal matters” language with the closed textual route.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Federal ratification** is additional consent by State legislatures for amendments changing the subjects expressly listed in the proviso to Article 368(2).

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> State consent is constitutionally decisive but selectively triggered: the proviso protects the federal compact, not every provision with a federal consequence.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**not less than one-half · resolutions · before Presidential assent · Articles 54/55/73/162/241/279A · named Chapters · Seventh Schedule · State representation**

#### State procedure and limits

- [FACT] Parliament must first pass the Bill in both Houses by the dual special majority.
- [FACT] Ratification is by resolutions of the legislatures of not less than half the States.
- [FACT] The State resolution uses an ordinary/simple present-and-voting majority; Article 368 prescribes no special State threshold.
- [FACT] In a bicameral State the legislature’s required Houses participate in the resolution; no joint sitting substitutes for failure.
- [FACT] The Governor’s assent is not a separate requirement because the State act is ratification by resolution, not enactment of a State Bill.
- [FACT] States may ratify or refuse; they cannot introduce the Article 368 Bill or amend its text.
- [FACT] Union Territory legislatures do not count as States.
- [FACT] Article 368 sets no ratification deadline and is silent on withdrawal of a resolution already passed; avoid a categorical rule without authority.
- [LIMIT] Conditions of the Governor’s office are not a proviso subject. Article 241 is separately named, so “Supreme Court and High Courts” is incomplete shorthand.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** State participation is a selective constitutional veto. **Evidence:** The proviso lists executive-power Articles, judiciary chapters, legislative distribution, State representation, Article 279A and Article 368. **Analysis:** These fields alter either the federal division or the States’ national voice. **Qualification:** States cannot initiate or rewrite the Bill, and the Constitution requires half—not all or three-fourths—to ratify.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Article 368(2) proviso; Articles 54, 55, 73, 162, 241 and 279A; Seventh Schedule; One Hundred and First Amendment.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

Governor conditions are excluded; Article 162 and any Seventh Schedule List are included.

#### MAINS USE

Use for State-role, amendment federalism and procedure questions; cross-link wider federal theory to Topic 12.

#### MINI RECAP

Parliament proposes and passes; selected State legislatures ratify; the President assents.

### SESSION 5 — ORDINARY LEGISLATIVE POWER, CONSTITUENT POWER AND ARTICLE 13

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

| Question | Ordinary law | Constitutional amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Source of power | Legislative entries/specific grants | Article 368 constituent power or a special non-368 route |
| Product | Statute | Constitutional Amendment Act |
| Primary review | Competence, Part III and other constitutional commands | Procedure plus basic-structure limit |
| Article 13 | Directly controls ordinary “law” | Article 13(4) excludes Article 368 amendments after the Twenty-fourth Amendment |
| Basic structure | Not a free-standing invalidation formula | Direct substantive limit on amendment |

*The table shows why the same constitutional value operates through different legal tests.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Ordinary legislative power** governs within the existing Constitution. **Constituent power** formally alters constitutional text but remains a Constitution-created and limited power.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Article 13 disciplines ordinary law directly; basic structure disciplines constitutional amendment, while ordinary statutes still require a concrete constitutional defect.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**legislative competence · Article 13(2) · Article 13(4) · Article 368(3) · constituent power · basic-structure ceiling · express constitutional hook**

#### Relationship and consequence

- [FACT] Article 13(2) invalidates State law that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights to the extent of contravention.
- [FACT] *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh* treated constitutional amendments as outside “law” in Article 13.
- [FACT] *Golak Nath* treated amendment as law, restricting future Fundamental Rights amendment through prospective overruling.
- [FACT] The Twenty-fourth Amendment inserted Article 13(4) and Article 368(3), expressly excluding amendments under Article 368 from Article 13 and affirming constituent power.
- [FACT] *Kesavananda Bharati* upheld broad amendment power but replaced an absolute rights lock with the basic-structure limit.
- [ANALYSIS] Fundamental Rights are amendable, but their essential contribution to constitutional identity cannot be destroyed.
- [LIMIT] A court reviewing an ordinary statute must identify competence, Part III or another constitutional violation; the basic-structure label alone is insufficient after *Anjum Kadari*.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Article 13 and Article 368 answer different questions. **Evidence:** Golak Nath used Article 13 against future amendments; the Twenty-fourth Amendment excluded Article 368 amendments; Kesavananda retained review through basic structure. **Analysis:** Parliament recovered wide constituent capacity without acquiring sovereign power. **Qualification:** Ordinary laws remain fully reviewable, but through concrete constitutional provisions rather than a free-standing amendment doctrine.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Articles 13(2), 13(4), 368(1) and 368(3); *Shankari Prasad*; *Golak Nath*; Twenty-fourth Amendment; *Kesavananda Bharati*; *Anjum Kadari*.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

“Not law under Article 13” does not mean “not subject to any constitutional review”.

#### MAINS USE

Use to explain how the Court moved from rights amendability to constitutional identity.

#### MINI RECAP

Article 13 is not the present amendment test; basic structure is.

### SESSION 6 — FROM SHANKARI PRASAD TO THE TWENTY-FIFTH AMENDMENT

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
1951 SHANKARI PRASAD -> amendment not Article 13 law; First Amendment upheld
1964/65 SAJJAN SINGH -> rule reaffirmed; separate opinions raise structural doubts
1967 GOLAK NATH     -> amendment treated as law; FR protected prospectively
1971 24TH AMENDMENT -> constituent power + Article 13 exclusion + President shall assent
1971 25TH AMENDMENT -> property changes + Article 31C for Article 39(b)/(c)
1973 KESAVANANDA    -> wide amendability retained; destructive effect prohibited
```

*The timeline is a Parliament–Court dialogue, not a disconnected case list.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **pre-Kesavananda amendability controversy** asked whether Parliament could alter Fundamental Rights and whether an amendment counted as “law” under Article 13.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> The doctrine emerged from a constitutional dialogue: judicial limits triggered amendment responses, and Kesavananda converted the conflict into a synthesis of wide power and preserved identity.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**Shankari Prasad · Sajjan Singh · Golak Nath · prospective overruling · Twenty-fourth Amendment · Twenty-fifth Amendment · Article 31C**

#### Exact propositions

| Authority | Holding or response | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| *Shankari Prasad* (1951) | Amendment under Article 368 was not “law” under Article 13(2); Fundamental Rights could be amended | Upheld the First Amendment |
| *Sajjan Singh* (decision 1964; reported 1965) | Reaffirmed *Shankari Prasad* and upheld the Seventeenth Amendment | Separate opinions of Mudholkar and Hidayatullah JJ raised early doubts about basic features |
| *I.C. Golak Nath* (1967) | Amendment treated as Article 13 law; Parliament could not abridge Fundamental Rights prospectively | Earlier amendments were not invalidated because the Court used prospective overruling |
| Twenty-fourth Amendment (1971) | Made constituent power express, inserted Article 13(4)/368(3), and made Presidential assent mandatory | Direct constitutional response to *Golak Nath* |
| Twenty-fifth Amendment (1971) | Replaced “compensation” with “amount” in the property framework and inserted Article 31C for Article 39(b)/(c) laws | The attempted conclusive exclusion of nexus review did not survive *Kesavananda* |

#### Prospective overruling

Prospective overruling changes the governing rule for the future while protecting completed past legal effects. It is a temporal remedy, distinct from **severability**, which asks which parts of a legal text can continue after an offending provision is removed.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** The case sequence alternated judicial rule and parliamentary response. **Evidence:** Golak Nath used Article 13 prospectively; the Twenty-fourth Amendment restored express constituent power; the Twenty-fifth inserted Article 31C. **Analysis:** The conflict concerned both social-reform capacity and constitutional supremacy. **Qualification:** Kesavananda did not simply restore Shankari Prasad; it added a substantive identity limit.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

First, Seventeenth, Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Amendments; Articles 13, 31C and 368; the five authorities in the timeline.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

Golak Nath used prospective overruling; it did not propound the basic-structure doctrine.

#### MAINS USE

Use as the chronology paragraph before explaining the Kesavananda settlement.

#### MINI RECAP

Early amendability -> rights lock -> amendment response -> identity-based compromise.

### SESSION 7 — KESAVANANDA BHARATI: THE LIMITED HOLDING AND ITS REMEDIAL LOGIC

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
WHAT THE 13-JUDGE BENCH SETTLED (7:6)

WIDE SUBJECT-MATTER POWER                 SUBSTANTIVE LIMIT
Parliament may amend every Part     +     No amendment may damage or destroy
including FR and Preamble                  the Constitution's basic structure
                 \                         /
                  \                       /
                   LIMITED CONSTITUENT POWER

NOT SETTLED AS ONE CLOSED LIST -> individual opinions supplied overlapping features
```

*The holding concerns the nature and limit of amendment, not one unanimous feature catalogue.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **basic-structure doctrine** is the rule that Article 368 permits amendment of every constitutional provision but not damage or destruction of the Constitution’s basic framework or identity.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Kesavananda rejected both constitutional petrification and parliamentary absolutism: every Part is amendable, but the Constitution must remain recognisably itself.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**13 judges · 7:6 · 24 April 1973 · every Part amendable · damage/destroy · basic structure · identity · non-exhaustive**

#### What the Court held

- [FACT] The thirteen-judge Bench decided the controlling issue by **7:6** on 24 April 1973.
- [FACT] Parliament may amend any Part, including Fundamental Rights and the Preamble.
- [FACT] The Twenty-fourth Amendment was upheld subject to the substantive limit.
- [FACT] Parliament may not damage, destroy or abrogate the Constitution’s basic structure.
- [FACT] The original Article 31C protection connected to Article 39(b)/(c) substantially survived, but the clause attempting to make the legislative declaration conclusive against judicial inquiry did not.
- [ANALYSIS] The legal question became one of **degree and effect**: does the amendment preserve the feature while regulating it, or disable the feature’s constitutional function?

#### What the Court did not hold

- It did not make every word of Fundamental Rights unamendable.
- It did not hold the Preamble alone to be the basic structure.
- It did not publish one unanimous exhaustive list.
- It did not apply the doctrine as a free-standing formula against all ordinary statutes.
- It did not forbid transformative constitutional reform.

#### Severability

Where valid and invalid parts can operate independently, the offending part may be removed while the remainder survives. The treatment of Article 31C demonstrates why amendment review need not be all-or-nothing.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Kesavananda preserved broad amendment while constitutionalising an effect-based limit. **Evidence:** The 7:6 majority upheld power over every Part but rejected destructive change; the Article 31C disposition preserved a bounded protection while refusing conclusive exclusion of review. **Analysis:** This allows social transformation without constitutional replacement. **Qualification:** Feature attribution must come from specific opinions and later cases, not a fictional unanimous list.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973); Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Amendments; Articles 13, 31C and 368.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

“Every Part is amendable” and “basic structure cannot be destroyed” are simultaneous propositions.

#### MAINS USE

Use as the core holding in every Article 368 answer.

#### MINI RECAP

Wide power over text, limited power over constitutional identity.

### SESSION 8 — INDIRA GANDHI, THE FORTY-SECOND AMENDMENT AND MINERVA MILLS

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
39TH AMENDMENT -> election-dispute insulation
        |
        v
INDIRA NEHRU GANDHI (1975)
free and fair elections + rule of law + judicial review
        |
        v
42ND AMENDMENT (1976)
SECTION 4: Article 31C expanded to all DPSPs
SECTION 55: Article 368(4)-(5), review exclusion + unlimited power
        |
        v
MINERVA MILLS (1980)
sections 4 and 55 invalid -> FR-DPSP harmony + judicial review + limited power
```

*The sequence shows application, attempted override and doctrinal reinforcement.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **self-enlargement problem** arises when an amending body tries to remove review of its own power or declare that power unlimited.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Minerva Mills exposed the constitutional contradiction in self-enlargement: a limited power cannot validly erase the limitation that constitutes it.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**Thirty-ninth Amendment · free and fair elections · Forty-second Amendment section 4 · section 55 · Article 368(4)-(5) · FR-DPSP harmony · limited amending power**

#### Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975)

- [FACT] The Thirty-ninth Amendment attempted to insulate specified high-office election disputes from ordinary judicial adjudication.
- [FACT] The Court invalidated the offending part and used basic features including free and fair elections, rule of law and judicial review.
- [LIMIT] The case involved the Thirty-ninth, not the Forty-fourth, Amendment.
- [ANALYSIS] Democracy includes legally contestable elections, not only the result produced by a current majority.

#### Forty-second Amendment sections 4 and 55

- [FACT] **Section 4** expanded Article 31C protection from Article 39(b)/(c) to all Directive Principles.
- [FACT] **Section 55** inserted Article 368(4), excluding review of amendments, and Article 368(5), declaring no limitation on constituent power.

#### Minerva Mills (1980)

- [FACT] The Court invalidated sections 4 and 55.
- [FACT] Limited amending power and judicial review are basic features.
- [FACT] Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles form part of the constitutional structure; neither Part may be given absolute primacy through destructive amendment.
- [ANALYSIS] The decision demonstrates that the procedure used to amend Article 368 cannot make the power legally sovereign.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Later amendments tested whether Parliament could neutralise the Kesavananda limit. **Evidence:** The Thirty-ninth Amendment’s election immunity failed in *Indira Gandhi*; sections 4 and 55 of the Forty-second Amendment failed in *Minerva Mills*. **Analysis:** Judicial review, fair political competition and rights–governance balance protect the conditions of constitutional democracy. **Qualification:** The Court struck targeted destructive provisions, not the entire idea of election regulation, Directive Principles or constitutional amendment.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

Thirty-ninth and Forty-second Amendments; Articles 31C and 368(4)-(5); *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*; *Minerva Mills v. Union of India*.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

Section 4 concerned Article 31C; section 55 concerned Article 368(4)-(5).

#### MAINS USE

Use to prove why Parliament cannot enlarge limited power into absolute power.

#### MINI RECAP

Election immunity failed; unlimited and unreviewable amending power failed.

```text
                                   I.R. COELHO IMPACT TEST
                            affected Part III right
                                      -> basic feature
                                      -> damage/destruction?
```

*The test turns on the inserting amendment’s date and constitutional effect.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Ninth Schedule review** examines whether a constitutional amendment granting Article 31B immunity after the Kesavananda cutoff damages basic structure through its impact on protected rights.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Article 31B is a shield against ordinary Part III invalidation, not a post-1973 vault against the Constitution’s basic structure.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**Article 31A · Article 31B · Ninth Schedule · 24 April 1973 · Waman Rao · I.R. Coelho · impact test · reviewable not automatically void**

#### Architecture and cutoff

- [FACT] The First Amendment inserted Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule in 1951.
- [FACT] Article 31A protects specified categories; Article 31B validates listed laws. The mechanisms are related but distinct.
- [FACT] *Waman Rao* (decision 1980, reported 1981) fixed **24 April 1973** as the prospective dividing line for amendments inserting laws.
- [FACT] Pre-cutoff insertions were not reopened on the later basic-structure ground; post-cutoff insertions are open to challenge.

#### I.R. Coelho (2007)

- [FACT] The nine-judge Bench unanimously rejected blanket post-cutoff immunity.
- [FACT] Review proceeds through the effect on Part III rights whose essence forms part of basic structure.
- [ANALYSIS] The court moves from the right affected to the underlying feature and then to the degree of damage produced by immunisation.
- [LIMIT] A post-1973 insertion is **reviewable**, not automatically invalid; the damaging effect must be established.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Ninth Schedule protection is temporally and substantively limited. **Evidence:** Waman Rao supplies the cutoff; Coelho supplies the rights-to-feature impact test. **Analysis:** Parliament cannot use Article 31B to achieve indirectly what Article 368 cannot do directly. **Qualification:** Judicial review preserves legislative space because only basic-structure damage, not every rights abridgment, triggers invalidity.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

First Amendment; Articles 31A and 31B; *Waman Rao v. Union of India*; *I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu*.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

The cutoff is 24 April 1973, and reviewability is not automatic invalidity.

#### MAINS USE

Use as evidence of effect-based review and constitutional supremacy.

#### MINI RECAP

Date -> affected right -> basic feature -> damaging effect -> remedy.

### SESSION 11 — ANJUM KADARI AND THE ORDINARY-LAW BOUNDARY

**Stage:** Core

#### VISUAL FIRST

```text
CHALLENGED MEASURE
   |
   +--> CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT
   |       procedure + basic-structure review
   |
   `--> ORDINARY STATUTE
           legislative competence
           + Part III
           + other express constitutional provisions
           + basic features as interpretive context, NOT a free-standing invalidation ground
```

*The same constitutional value can matter in both fields, but the legal cause of invalidity differs.*

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **ordinary-law boundary** prevents the amendment-specific basic-structure doctrine from becoming an undefined general test for statutes.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> After Anjum Kadari, “the Act violates basic structure” is incomplete; the answer must identify the Articles or competence rule that the Act violates.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**2024 INSC 831 · ordinary legislation · legislative competence · Part III · another constitutional provision · free-standing test · interpretive context**

#### Controlling proposition

- [FACT] *Anjum Kadari* upheld the distinction between constitutional amendment and ordinary legislation.
- [FACT] An ordinary law may be invalidated for lack of legislative competence, violation of Fundamental Rights or breach of another constitutional provision.
- [FACT] It cannot be invalidated solely because a court labels its object inconsistent with an undefined basic feature.
- [ANALYSIS] Secularism, federalism, judicial independence or separation of powers may still guide interpretation and identify relevant constitutional provisions.
- [LIMIT] The judgment does not weaken amendment review, post-1973 Ninth Schedule review or ordinary rights review.

#### Why the distinction matters

An amendment changes the higher legal framework and therefore faces a doctrine protecting constitutional identity. A statute operates within that framework and must be tested against the written distribution of powers, rights and institutional commands. Collapsing the two would let judges create an open-ended statutory veto untethered from enacted constitutional text.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** Basic structure directly limits constitutional amendments, not statutes as a free-standing formula. **Evidence:** Anjum Kadari requires competence, Part III or another constitutional hook. **Analysis:** The rule preserves written-constitutional review while avoiding impressionistic invalidation of ordinary policy. **Qualification:** Basic features remain relevant as interpretive principles and in locating the concrete Article breached.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

*Anjum Kadari v. Union of India*, 2024 INSC 831, judgment dated 5 November 2024; Articles 14, 21, 21A and 30 in the case context.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

“Basic structure does not apply directly to statutes” does not mean statutes are immune from constitutional review.

#### MAINS USE

Use to show doctrinal self-restraint and distinguish amendment review from ordinary-law review.

#### MINI RECAP

Amendment -> basic structure; statute -> express constitutional defect.

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

The **counter-majoritarian difficulty** asks why unelected judges may invalidate change passed by elected constitutional majorities; the reply rests on constitutional rather than parliamentary supremacy.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING LINE

> Basic structure is defensible as disciplined judicial review, but loses legitimacy when an undefined feature becomes a judicial policy veto.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**constitutional supremacy · counter-majoritarian · democratic self-destruction · uncertainty · judicial self-restraint · actual damage · case attribution · narrow remedy**

#### Evaluation

- [ANALYSIS] The critique is real because the doctrine is judicially inferred, open-ended and capable of defeating national and State super-majorities.
- [ANALYSIS] Yet electoral legitimacy is not exhausted by the present majority. Constitutional democracy protects the rules of future competition, minority liberty, federal participation and independent adjudication.
- [FACT] *Minerva Mills* supplies the strongest logical defence: limited power cannot make itself unlimited and unreviewable.
- [ANALYSIS] Judicial review becomes supremacy if courts substitute institutional preference for a demonstrated constitutional limit.
- [ANALYSIS] A disciplined test asks: What feature? What text and precedent? What function does it perform? How does the amendment damage that function? Can the defect be severed?
- [LIMIT] Courts should distinguish regulation, rebalancing and experimentation from disabling destruction.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE ANSWER PARAGRAPH

**Claim:** The doctrine has both democratic cost and constitutional necessity. **Evidence:** A 7:6 Kesavananda majority created an open test, but section 55 of the Forty-second Amendment shows why self-enlargement needs an external check. **Analysis:** Review protects the conditions under which democracy can continue. **Qualification:** Legitimacy depends on transparent attribution, a high damage threshold and remedial modesty.

#### NAMED EVIDENCE

*Kesavananda Bharati*; Forty-second Amendment section 55; *Minerva Mills*; *I.R. Coelho*; *Anjum Kadari*.

#### PRELIMS TRAP

Judicial review and judicial supremacy are not synonyms.

#### MAINS USE

Use for “guardian or overreach”, legitimacy and reform questions.

#### MINI RECAP

Defend the doctrine conditionally: constitutional limit, real damage, reasoned restraint.

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

Exactly **32 original MCQs** precede the PYQ block. Keys rotate `ABCDABCDABCDABCDABCDABCDABCDABCD`. Every option has a substantive question-specific explanation and every question ends with a distinct Examiner trap.

### Q1. With reference to Article 368, which formulation is the most accurate?

- A. Parliament exercises constituent power to amend by addition, variation or repeal, subject to the prescribed procedure and the basic-structure ceiling.
- B. Parliament becomes legally sovereign whenever half the States ratify an amendment.
- C. Presidential assent makes a constitutional amendment immune from judicial review.
- D. Article 368 is ordinary legislative power confined to provisions outside Part III.

**Answer: A.**

- **A:** Correct: Article 368(1) uses “constituent power” and the forms addition, variation and repeal; Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills supply the substantive limit.
- **B:** Incorrect: State ratification is required only for the proviso subjects, and even a ratified amendment remains subordinate to the Constitution’s basic structure.
- **C:** Incorrect: Assent completes the formal process, but courts may still examine procedural compliance and basic-structure damage.
- **D:** Incorrect: Article 368 can reach every Part, including Fundamental Rights, while ordinary legislative power operates on a different constitutional footing.

**Examiner trap:** Constituent power is wider than ordinary law-making, but it is not sovereign or unlimited.

### Q2. In a House with a total membership of 245, 180 members are present and voting on an Article 368 Bill. What is the minimum number of affirmative votes needed?

- A. 120, because only two-thirds of members present and voting matters.
- B. 123, because both the total-membership majority and the two-thirds present-and-voting limb must be satisfied.
- C. 164, because two-thirds of the total membership is required.
- D. 91, because a majority of members present is sufficient.

**Answer: B.**

- **A:** Incorrect: 120 reaches two-thirds of 180 but fails the separate requirement of a majority of the House’s total membership.
- **B:** Correct: A majority of 245 requires 123, while two-thirds of 180 is 120; the higher simultaneous threshold controls.
- **C:** Incorrect: Article 368 does not demand two-thirds of the full House strength; it combines two differently calculated limbs.
- **D:** Incorrect: An ordinary present-and-voting majority cannot substitute for either constitutional threshold.

**Examiner trap:** Calculate both limbs independently and use the higher minimum; “special majority” is not one denominator.

### Q3. Which statement correctly describes creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council?

- A. Parliament and the State Assembly both act by an ordinary present-and-voting majority.
- B. The Governor creates or abolishes the Council after Parliament approves a resolution.
- C. The State Assembly first passes the Article 169 resolution by its prescribed special majority; Parliament then enacts an ordinary law outside Article 368.
- D. The change always requires an Article 368 amendment ratified by half the States.

**Answer: C.**

- **A:** Incorrect: Parliament’s eventual law is ordinary, but the initiating Assembly resolution needs a majority of total membership plus two-thirds present and voting.
- **B:** Incorrect: The Governor cannot create or abolish a Legislative Council by executive action.
- **C:** Correct: Article 169 deliberately combines a special initiating resolution in the State Assembly with a parliamentary law not deemed an Article 368 amendment.
- **D:** Incorrect: Legislative Council creation or abolition follows Article 169, not the federal-ratification proviso.

**Examiner trap:** “Simple-majority constitutional change” describes Parliament’s law here, not every prior procedural step.

### Q4. Which group lies entirely within the State-ratification proviso to Article 368?

- A. Article 241, Fundamental Duties and the Second Schedule
- B. Articles 54 and 55, plus conditions of the Governor’s office
- C. Chapter IV of Part V, Article 324 and the Tenth Schedule
- D. Article 279A, Chapter I of Part XI and representation of States in Parliament

**Answer: D.**

- **A:** Incorrect: Article 241 is included, but Part IVA and the Second Schedule are not named proviso subjects as such.
- **B:** Incorrect: Articles 54 and 55 are included; conditions of the Governor’s office are not.
- **C:** Incorrect: The Union Judiciary chapter is included, whereas Article 324 and the Tenth Schedule do not independently appear in the list.
- **D:** Correct: The GST Council article, the legislative-relations chapter and State representation are all expressly protected by the additional federal-consent stage.

**Examiner trap:** Use the exact Articles, Chapters, Lists and representation limb; loose “federal matters” shorthand over-includes.

### Q5. A Constitution Amendment Bill is pending in Rajya Sabha and has not been passed by Lok Sabha when Lok Sabha is dissolved. Which statement is correct?

- A. It does not lapse merely because of the dissolution, under the narrow rule in Article 107(5).
- B. It automatically lapses because both Houses must exist simultaneously throughout the process.
- C. It must be sent to a joint sitting after the new Lok Sabha is constituted.
- D. Rajya Sabha may send it directly to the President after the dissolution.

**Answer: A.**

- **A:** Correct: Article 107(5) protects a Bill pending in Rajya Sabha that Lok Sabha has not passed from lapse merely on dissolution.
- **B:** Incorrect: Dissolution does not erase this specifically protected Rajya Sabha posture.
- **C:** Incorrect: Article 108 joint-sitting machinery cannot rescue or complete a constitutional amendment.
- **D:** Incorrect: Passage by both Houses remains indispensable; Rajya Sabha cannot bypass Lok Sabha or Presidential-stage prerequisites.

**Examiner trap:** Do not generalise the narrow Rajya Sabha rule into the false slogan that amendment Bills never lapse.

### Q6. Consider the following statements about introduction of a Constitution Amendment Bill: 1. It may be introduced in either House. 2. It may be introduced by a private member. 3. It needs prior Presidential recommendation. Which are correct?

- A. 2 and 3 only
- B. 1 and 2 only
- C. 1, 2 and 3
- D. 1 only

**Answer: B.**

- **A:** Incorrect: A private member may introduce the Bill, but prior Presidential recommendation is not a constitutional condition.
- **B:** Correct: Either House and either ministerial or private-member sponsorship are permitted.
- **C:** Incorrect: Statement 3 wrongly imports a recommendation requirement from other legislative categories.
- **D:** Incorrect: This omits the valid private-member route recognised for amendment Bills.

**Examiner trap:** Separate the absent recommendation at introduction from the President’s mandatory assent at the end.

### Q7. Which combination is most defensibly classified as constitutionally authorised ordinary-law change outside Article 368?

- A. Amending Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and the Preamble
- B. Rewriting the Union Judiciary chapter, altering Article 279A and changing Article 54
- C. Forming a new State under Articles 3-4, regulating citizenship under Article 11 and amending the Fifth Schedule through its express mechanism
- D. Changing Article 368, State representation in Parliament and List I

**Answer: C.**

- **A:** Incorrect: These entrenched constitutional fields ordinarily require Article 368, subject to the relevant route.
- **B:** Incorrect: Each item falls within the Article 368 proviso’s federal-consent architecture.
- **C:** Correct: Each example rests on a separate constitutional authorisation for ordinary law or a non-Article-368 deeming mechanism.
- **D:** Incorrect: Article 368 itself, State representation and any Seventh Schedule List require the additional State-ratification route.

**Examiner trap:** A constitutional effect does not automatically make the measure an Article 368 amendment; inspect the enabling provision.

### Q8. Which statement best describes institutional features absent from India’s Article 368 process?

- A. A referendum is mandatory, but a constituent convention is optional.
- B. A joint sitting is available, though State ratification is not.
- C. A special constituent body must approve every amendment before Parliament votes.
- D. There is no referendum, no special constituent convention and no joint sitting to resolve House disagreement.

**Answer: D.**

- **A:** Incorrect: Article 368 contains no referendum stage of any kind.
- **B:** Incorrect: Joint sitting is unavailable, while State ratification is indispensable for the listed proviso subjects.
- **C:** Incorrect: The ordinary Houses of Parliament exercise the constituent function under special constitutional rules.
- **D:** Correct: India entrusts amendment to Parliament and, selectively, State legislatures without direct popular ratification or a separate amending assembly.

**Examiner trap:** Do not confuse Parliament acting in a constituent capacity with the creation of a separate constituent body.

### Q9. Which proposition belongs to Shankari Prasad (1951)?

- A. A constitutional amendment was not “law” under Article 13(2), so Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.
- B. Fundamental Rights were prospectively placed beyond amendment.
- C. The basic-structure doctrine limited constituent power.
- D. Article 368(4) and (5) were void because amending power is limited.

**Answer: A.**

- **A:** Correct: The Court distinguished constitutional amendment from ordinary “law” under Article 13 and upheld the First Amendment.
- **B:** Incorrect: The prospective rights lock was the position adopted later in Golak Nath.
- **C:** Incorrect: Basic structure emerged in Kesavananda Bharati, not in the 1951 case.
- **D:** Incorrect: The attack on clauses (4) and (5) belongs to Minerva Mills after the Forty-second Amendment.

**Examiner trap:** Shankari Prasad concerns Article 13 and amendability; it did not anticipate the basic-structure ceiling.

### Q10. The distinctive remedial technique used in Golak Nath (1967) was:

- A. requiring State ratification for every Fundamental Rights amendment.
- B. prospective overruling, leaving earlier amendments undisturbed while restricting future abridgment of Fundamental Rights.
- C. testing only future Ninth Schedule insertions for basic-structure damage.
- D. suspending the judgment until Parliament held a referendum.

**Answer: B.**

- **A:** Incorrect: Golak Nath did not enlarge the Article 368 proviso or federalise every rights amendment.
- **B:** Correct: The Court changed the rule prospectively rather than unsettling constitutional amendments already made.
- **C:** Incorrect: The post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule cutoff was formulated in Waman Rao after Kesavananda.
- **D:** Incorrect: No referendum condition formed part of the judgment or Article 368.

**Examiner trap:** Prospective overruling protects past legal effects; it is different from severing an offending clause.

### Q11. Which statement most accurately states the constitutional response made by the Twenty-fourth Amendment?

- A. It created the Ninth Schedule and Article 31B.
- B. It inserted Article 31C for Article 39(b) and (c) laws.
- C. It expressly affirmed constituent power, excluded Article 368 amendments from Article 13 and made Presidential assent obligatory.
- D. It declared every amendment immune from judicial review and the amending power unlimited.

**Answer: C.**

- **A:** Incorrect: Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule came through the First Amendment in 1951.
- **B:** Incorrect: Article 31C was the distinct intervention of the Twenty-fifth Amendment.
- **C:** Correct: The 1971 amendment answered Golak Nath through coordinated changes to Articles 13 and 368 and the word “shall” for assent.
- **D:** Incorrect: The attempt to exclude review and announce unlimited power came through section 55 of the Forty-second Amendment.

**Examiner trap:** Keep the Twenty-fourth Amendment’s three moves together: constituent power, Article 13 exclusion and mandatory assent.

### Q12. What was the legally important treatment of Article 31C as inserted by the Twenty-fifth Amendment in Kesavananda Bharati?

- A. The whole Twenty-fifth Amendment was struck down without severance.
- B. Article 31C was upheld as absolute immunity for every Directive Principle.
- C. Only the replacement of “compensation” by “amount” was examined; Article 31C was left undecided.
- D. The protection connected with Article 39(b) and (c) was substantially sustained, but the clause excluding judicial scrutiny of the claimed nexus was not.

**Answer: D.**

- **A:** Incorrect: The Court did not invalidate the amendment as an indivisible whole.
- **B:** Incorrect: The original Article 31C concerned Article 39(b) and (c), not all Directive Principles.
- **C:** Incorrect: Both the property-language changes and the new Article 31C architecture were part of the constitutional dispute.
- **D:** Correct: The disposition illustrates severability—constitutional protection survived in bounded form while conclusive exclusion of review failed.

**Examiner trap:** The Twenty-fifth Amendment is evidence of partial survival, not an all-valid or all-void result.

### Q13. Which statement best captures the limited holding of Kesavananda Bharati (1973)?

- A. A 13-judge Bench decided 7:6 that every constitutional provision is amendable, but Parliament cannot damage or destroy the basic structure.
- B. Fundamental Rights can never be amended, irrespective of effect.
- C. Only the Preamble lies beyond amendment.

### Solved Mains PYQ 1. UPSC 2019 GS-II Q12

**Question:** “Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot be enlarged into absolute power.” In the light of this statement explain whether Parliament under Article 368 of the Constitution can destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution by expanding its amending power. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model solution.**

Article 368 confers a broad constituent power, but the word “amend” does not authorise destruction of the constitutional order that creates Parliament.

The early cases allowed extensive amendment. *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh* excluded constitutional amendments from “law” under Article 13. *Golak Nath* reversed that view prospectively, after which the Twenty-fourth Amendment expressly affirmed constituent power and mandatory Presidential assent. In *Kesavananda Bharati*, a thirteen-judge Bench decided 7:6 that Parliament may amend every Part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot damage or destroy the basic structure.

Parliament therefore cannot enlarge its own authority into absolute power. Section 55 of the Forty-second Amendment inserted Article 368(4) and (5), excluding judicial review and declaring no limitation on constituent power. *Minerva Mills* invalidated those clauses because limited amending power and judicial review are themselves basic features: a delegated constitutional power cannot erase its source and limits. *Indira Nehru Gandhi* protected free and fair elections and review; *I.R. Coelho* preserved effect-based scrutiny of post-1973 Ninth Schedule insertions; the NJAC decision protected judicial independence.

Thus Parliament may restructure institutions and rights, but Article 368 ends where constitutional replacement begins. The doctrine permits transformation while preventing the amending body from abolishing constitutional supremacy, democratic contestability and independent review.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 204 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the self-enlargement issue through the Twenty-fourth/Forty-second Amendment dialogue, the exact Kesavananda limit and Minerva Mills rather than merely listing features.

**Answer-writing focus:** Draw `limited power -> attempted review exclusion -> Minerva Mills -> still limited` and reserve the conclusion for amendment versus destruction.

### Solved Mains PYQ 2. UPSC 2025 GS-II Q12

**Question:** Indian Constitution has conferred the amending power on the ordinary legislative institutions with a few procedural hurdles. In view of this statement, examine the procedural and substantive limitations on the amending power of the Parliament to change the Constitution. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model solution.**

The Constitution uses the ordinary Houses of Parliament for amendment, but they exercise a specially constituted and doubly limited power.

**Procedural limits:** A Bill may begin in either House and be introduced by a minister or private member without prior Presidential recommendation. Each House must pass it separately by a majority of total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting; there is no joint sitting. Amendments to the exact federal proviso subjects additionally need resolutions of not less than half the State legislatures before presentation to the President. States cannot initiate or alter the Bill. The President shall assent after the Twenty-fourth Amendment. Article 368 provides no referendum or special constituent convention.

The 2026 division on the defeated One Hundred and Thirty-first Amendment Bill illustrates the dual denominator: 298 ayes exceeded the Lok Sabha total-membership majority but fell below two-thirds of 528 present and voting.

**Substantive limit:** *Kesavananda Bharati* permits amendment of every Part but forbids damage to the basic structure. *Minerva Mills* protects judicial review and limited amending power; *Indira Nehru Gandhi*, *I.R. Coelho* and NJAC show applications to elections, Ninth Schedule immunity and judicial independence. The list is not closed, so courts must identify a textually and precedentially grounded feature and actual destructive effect.

Hence Parliament can transform the Constitution through entrenched procedure, but cannot replace its identity. Procedure checks the manner of change; basic structure checks its constitutional effect.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 237 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It visibly separates the two categories of limitation, states every decisive procedural control and qualifies the open-ended doctrine with a disciplined damage test.

**Answer-writing focus:** Use two headings—Procedural and Substantive—and do not spend the 250-word answer reciting a random amendment list.

### Six original Mains questions with model solutions

#### Original Q1. Explain how India’s constitutional amendment design synthesises flexibility and rigidity. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model solution.**

India uses graded entrenchment instead of one universal threshold. First, express constitutional provisions permit ordinary parliamentary law outside Article 368—for example, Articles 2-4 for State reorganisation and Article 11 for citizenship. Article 169 adds a qualification: Parliament’s law is ordinary, but the initiating State Assembly resolution needs its prescribed special majority.

Second, most entrenched provisions require in each House a majority of total membership plus not less than two-thirds present and voting. Failure in either House ends the Bill because no joint sitting is available. Third, changes to the federal proviso subjects also require ratification by not less than half the State legislatures.

Procedure therefore combines adaptation with graduated consent. *Kesavananda Bharati* adds substantive rigidity: every Part may be amended, but constitutional identity cannot be destroyed. India is neither wholly flexible nor mechanically rigid; it is a layered system of ordinary change, special-majority change, federal consent and identity preservation.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 150 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines flexibility through the three routes, qualifies Article 169 and adds the basic-structure ceiling without diverting into a long chronology.

**Answer-writing focus:** Convert the body into a four-step rail: ordinary route → special majority → State consent → identity limit.

#### Original Q2. The role of States in constitutional amendment is significant but narrowly confined. Discuss. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model solution.**

States have no general amendment initiative under Article 368. A Constitution Amendment Bill begins only in either House of Parliament, and States do not ratify most amendments. Their decisive role arises only when the passed Bill changes the exact proviso subjects: Articles 54, 55, 73, 162, 241 and 279A; the named Union and State judiciary chapters; Chapter I of Part XI; any Seventh Schedule List; State representation in Parliament; or Article 368 itself.

Ratification follows passage by both Houses and must precede Presidential assent. Resolutions of not less than half the State legislatures are enough; States cannot amend the text, Union Territories do not count and Article 368 fixes no deadline. A gubernatorial assent stage is not added to the resolution.

Thus State participation protects the federal compact without creating a general State veto or coequal constituent assembly. The design is federally entrenched at selected points but Parliament-centred elsewhere.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 149 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines the exact subject clusters, procedural sequence and limits on State agency in a direct federalism verdict.

**Answer-writing focus:** Contrast “selective blocking voice” with “no initiation or rewriting power”.

#### Original Q3. Critically examine the claim that the basic-structure doctrine converts judicial review into judicial supremacy. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution.**

The doctrine empowers courts to invalidate a constitutional amendment that damages constitutional identity. Because Article 368 does not enumerate the protected features, the counter-majoritarian objection is substantial.

The criticism has three strengths. *Kesavananda Bharati* was a 7:6 decision with overlapping opinions rather than one closed feature list. Later cases attributed additional applications—free and fair elections in *Indira Nehru Gandhi*, limited amending power and Rights–Directive Principles harmony in *Minerva Mills*, federalism and secularism in *S.R. Bommai*, and judicial independence in NJAC. An open list leaves elected super-majorities uncertain and gives unelected judges the final institutional word. Impressionistic feature-identification could become a policy veto.

Yet review remains defensible. Parliament is a Constitution-created body, not a legally sovereign constituent founder. Section 55 of the Forty-second Amendment tried to exclude review and make power unlimited; *Minerva Mills* exposed the contradiction of a limited power erasing its limit. Democracy also includes future elections, opposition, liberty, federal voice and independent courts. *I.R. Coelho* uses an effect test rather than automatic invalidity, while *Anjum Kadari* refuses a free-standing basic-structure attack on ordinary statutes.

Legitimacy therefore depends on restraint: identify the feature’s textual and precedential basis, prove actual damage rather than policy disagreement, address contrary authority and tailor the remedy through severability. Properly used, the doctrine is constitutional review; loosely used, it risks judicial supremacy.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 222 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It accepts the democratic objection, answers it through the Forty-second Amendment–Minerva chain and proposes an administrable restraint test.

**Answer-writing focus:** Present criticism and defence symmetrically, then make the final verdict conditional on reasoning discipline.

#### Original Q4. Explain the present constitutional position of laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution.**

Article 31B validates laws listed in the Ninth Schedule notwithstanding inconsistency with Part III, but the shield is not absolute for later constitutional amendments.

The First Amendment created Article 31B and the Schedule in 1951, initially protecting land-reform measures. *Kesavananda Bharati* then established on 24 April 1973 that Article 368 cannot damage the basic structure. In *Waman Rao*, the Supreme Court applied that doctrine prospectively to amendments adding laws after the Kesavananda date; pre-cutoff insertions were not reopened on basic-structure grounds.

*I.R. Coelho* unanimously clarified the post-cutoff test. The court identifies the Part III right affected by the listed law, asks whether the right’s essence forms part of a basic feature, and examines whether the constitutional immunity causes damaging or destructive impact. Thus a law is reviewable because the inserting constitutional amendment is subject to Article 368’s substantive limit. It is not void merely because it abridges any right.

Three distinctions are essential. Article 31A category protection differs from Article 31B listed-law validation. Judicial review concerns effect, not the political label of the statute. Finally, reviewability does not imply automatic invalidity; the required basic-structure impact must be proved.

The present position preserves land-reform and legislative space while preventing the Ninth Schedule from becoming a vault against constitutional identity and judicial review.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 212 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives origin, cutoff, the Coelho test and three close-option distinctions instead of repeating “Ninth Schedule is reviewable”.

**Answer-writing focus:** Use the chain `date of insertion → affected right → basic feature → degree of impact → remedy`.

#### Original Q5. “A constitutional amendment may transform the Constitution, but it cannot replace its identity.” Discuss. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution.**

Article 368 is a power of amendment within an existing constitutional order. Addition, variation and repeal permit profound change, but the authority remains constituted by the Constitution and cannot abolish its own source.

Transformative amendment is both legitimate and necessary. Amendments have reworked property relations, strengthened social justice, constitutionalised local government, created the GST framework and expanded representation. *Kesavananda Bharati* therefore rejected Golak Nath’s absolute rights lock: Parliament may amend every Part, including Fundamental Rights and the Preamble.

The boundary is destructive effect. A change becomes replacement when it disables the features that keep the order recognisably constitutional—constitutional supremacy, democratic contestability, judicial review, limited government and institutional independence. *Indira Nehru Gandhi* rejected election immunity for specified high offices. Section 55 of the Forty-second Amendment attempted to make constituent power unlimited; *Minerva Mills* struck it because limited amending power and judicial review are basic. NJAC shows that even institutionally ambitious reform must preserve judicial independence.

Courts should not equate novelty with destruction. They must identify a case-supported feature, compare the constitutional position before and after amendment, distinguish regulation from disabling damage and preserve valid parts through severability. Political disagreement or reduced institutional preference is insufficient.

Thus basic structure is not an anti-reform fossil. It separates constitutional transformation, which renews the charter, from constitutional replacement, which would require a new exercise of original constituent authority rather than Article 368.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 228 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It demonstrates transformative capacity with named amendment fields, then states a workable identity-and-effect test with case evidence and remedial caution.

**Answer-writing focus:** Avoid saying “basic features can never be touched”; the correct claim is that they cannot be damaged or destroyed.

#### Original Q6. Trace Parliament–Court dialogue in the evolution of the basic-structure doctrine and assess its constitutional significance. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution.**

The doctrine emerged through institutional dialogue over Fundamental Rights, social reform and the location of ultimate constitutional authority.

*Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh* allowed rights amendment by excluding constitutional amendments from Article 13. *Golak Nath* reversed that rule prospectively and treated rights as beyond future abridgment. Parliament answered through the Twenty-fourth Amendment’s express constituent power and Article 13 exclusion, and through the Twenty-fifth Amendment’s property and Article 31C changes.

*Kesavananda Bharati* produced a synthesis: every Part remained amendable, but a 7:6 majority denied power to damage the basic structure. The dialogue continued. The Thirty-ninth Amendment tried to insulate specified election disputes; *Indira Nehru Gandhi* protected free and fair elections and review. Sections 4 and 55 of the Forty-second Amendment expanded Article 31C and claimed unlimited, unreviewable power; *Minerva Mills* invalidated them while recognising Rights–Directive Principles harmony, judicial review and limited amending power.

Later cases refined application rather than replacing the settlement. *Waman Rao* fixed the 24 April 1973 cutoff; *I.R. Coelho* developed effect-based Ninth Schedule scrutiny; *L. Chandra Kumar* protected constitutional-court review; NJAC protected judicial independence. *Anjum Kadari* confined the free-standing doctrine to constitutional amendments, preserving text-linked review of statutes.

The significance is dual: democratic institutions retain broad transformative capacity, while no temporary majority can legally extinguish constitutional identity. The dialogue remains legitimate only when courts attribute features carefully and Parliament treats amendment as constitutional stewardship rather than sovereignty.

**Audited model-answer word count:** 232 words.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces claim, response and synthesis across amendments and judgments, then evaluates both transformative capacity and judicial restraint.

**Answer-writing focus:** Organise chronology as alternating institutional moves, not as an unanalysed case list.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### Amendment versus replacement and original constituent authority

- [ANALYSIS] Article 368 is a **derived constituent power** operating inside the existing Constitution. A new constitution would claim original constituent authority rather than merely alter the old charter.
- [ANALYSIS] The identity inquiry asks whether the constitutional order before and after change remains recognisably continuous. It does not constitutionalise every historical arrangement.
- [FACT] Justice H.R. Khanna’s reasoning in *Kesavananda Bharati* is central to the continuity formulation: amendment cannot mean abrogation of the Constitution’s basic framework.
- [LIMIT] A sweeping textual change is not automatically replacement; functional destruction of basic features is the decisive concern.

### Transformative constitutional amendment

- [ANALYSIS] Basic structure is compatible with transformation. Amendments can redistribute power, deepen social justice, redesign institutions and expand representation.
- [ANALYSIS] The doctrine should preserve constitutional conditions for transformation—democratic participation, rights, federal voice, review and institutional independence—rather than freeze policy choices.
- [LIMIT] “Transformative” is not an immunity label. A reform still fails if its means disable a basic feature.

### Comparative models of unamendability

| Model | Source of limit | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Express eternity clause, as in Germany’s Basic Law | Written constitutional text | Higher predictability | Text may be rigid or under-inclusive |
| Implied basic structure, India | Text, structure, history and precedent | Adaptable to new threats | Uncertainty and judicial discretion |
| Political-only amendment control | Super-majorities/elections | Strong immediate majoritarian legitimacy | Weak defence against democratic self-destruction |

Comparative constitutional courts in some jurisdictions have used identity, substitution or basic-structure reasoning, but Indian Article 368 and Supreme Court precedent remain controlling.

### Institutional self-restraint and remedies

A disciplined court should identify the precise feature, show its textual and precedential basis, test the amendment’s actual operation, confront less restrictive interpretations and preserve valid portions through severability. It should not treat novelty, institutional discomfort or policy disagreement as constitutional destruction.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

#### Article 368 architecture

- Part XX; constituent power; addition, variation or repeal.
- Parliament uses ordinary legislative institutions in a special constitutional capacity.
- Every Part is amendable; basic structure cannot be damaged or destroyed.
- Constitutional supremacy rejects both parliamentary absolutism and unreasoned judicial supremacy.

#### Three constitutional-change routes

1. **Outside Article 368:** express ordinary-law mechanisms—Articles 2-4, 11, 169, 239A, Fifth Schedule paragraph 7, Sixth Schedule paragraph 21 and other specifically authorised operational fields.
2. **Article 368 special majority:** majority of total membership + not less than two-thirds present and voting, in each House separately.
3. **Federal route:** Route 2 + ratification by resolutions of not less than half the State legislatures.

**Majority trap:** Article 368 total membership is not effective strength. Abstentions leave the first limb unchanged and are excluded from the second denominator.
