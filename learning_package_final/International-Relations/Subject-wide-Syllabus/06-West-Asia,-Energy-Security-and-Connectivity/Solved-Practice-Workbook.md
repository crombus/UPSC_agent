## Routed PYQs — independently answered

### Routed PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-II Q9 (10 marks)
**Ledger-grounded demand, not a reconstructed quotation:** Discuss the depth and diversity of India's relations with Israel. The printed word-limit tail was corrupted in the scanned paper; the ledger assigns 150 words. Topic 06 Core route supersedes the older Advanced route.

**Model answer (independent; not an official key):** India's full diplomatic relations with Israel date from 1992; the first Indian prime-ministerial visit to Israel in July 2017 signalled a more open political partnership. Depth is visible in defence procurement, counter-terrorism and Barak-8 co-development. Diversity extends to Indo-Israel Agricultural Project Centres of Excellence for irrigation and protected cultivation, and the 2017 industrial R&D and technological innovation fund. I2U2 provides a wider setting for food, water and technology cooperation with the UAE and US, although an announced minilateral agenda is not proof of delivered projects. These strands answer distinct Indian security, farming and innovation needs. Yet defence import costs and technology-transfer depth remain contested; demonstration centres need local extension to scale. As Tharoor argues, cooperation with Israel must not come at the expense of Arab and Muslim-world friendships. India's recognition of Palestine and support for a viable negotiated two-state solution make the relationship deep but not exclusive.

### Routed PYQ 2 — 2018 GS-II Q20 (15 marks, 250 words)
**Ledger-grounded demand, not a reconstructed quotation:** In what ways does the US-Iran nuclear-pact controversy affect India's national interest, and how should India respond? Do not retrofit later events into the knowledge available to a 2018 candidate.

**Model answer (period-appropriate; independent):** The JCPOA, agreed in July 2015 and endorsed by UNSC resolution 2231, offered a diplomatic route for limiting Iran-related nuclear tensions. The US announcement of withdrawal in May 2018 threatened that route and exposed Indian interests to restored US sanctions. Three transmission channels mattered. First, Iranian oil had been valuable to India's energy security; switching sources could raise procurement costs and complicate refinery operations tailored to Iranian crude, as Tharoor observed. Second, banks, insurers and shipping firms could avoid Iran-related transactions even where India itself had made no equivalent legal prohibition. Third, prospective Chabahar-based access towards Afghanistan and Central Asia might become harder to finance and sustain. At the same time, India valued stronger ties with the United States and Gulf states: an exclusively pro-Iran or pro-US posture would sacrifice other interests.

India should defend the nuclear agreement's diplomatic purpose, engage Washington and Tehran to clarify lawful commercial channels and any available, explicitly time-limited exception, and keep working with European and regional partners to protect essential connectivity. It should diversify oil suppliers while accounting for refinery compatibility, rather than treating substitution as costless or as a cure for import dependence. Consular planning for Indians in the wider region and maritime-supply contingency arrangements add resilience. No bilateral assurance guarantees permanent immunity from another state's future sanctions decisions. The appropriate response is pragmatic strategic autonomy: preserve constructive relations with Iran without forfeiting US and Gulf cooperation, and judge each energy or transport project against actual legal, financial and security conditions.

**Later evidence, not part of the 2018 answer:** India's Iranian-crude purchases ceased after US exceptions ended in May 2019; the May 2024 Chabahar contract and September 2025 waiver reversal later illustrated the reversibility of exemptions. The E3's 2025 snapback action and disputed UN-measures status must not be smuggled into a purported 2018 prediction.

### Routed PYQ 3 — 2025 GS-II Q19 (15 marks, 250 words)
**Original prompt reproduced in the Basic owner:** “Energy security constitutes the dominant kingpin of India's foreign policy, and is linked with India's overarching influence in Middle Eastern countries.” How would you integrate energy security with India's foreign policy trajectories in the coming years?

**Model answer (independent; not an official key):** Energy security is an organising interest, not a sufficient definition, of India's West Asia policy. PPAC reported crude-oil import dependence of 88.2% in FY2024-25; the owner's FY2025-26 figure of about 88.7% is provisional and should not be projected backwards into the 2025 paper. Diversifying suppliers and taking equity in overseas oilfields can soften concentration risk but cannot by themselves reduce aggregate dependence. Security of supplies must be reconciled with affordability and the transition to sustainable energy.

Diplomatically, the India-GCC Joint Action Plan 2024–28, adopted in Riyadh in September 2024, offers a dated platform for dialogue on energy, security and people. Deep bilateral relations with the UAE and Saudi Arabia matter, but influence also requires protecting expatriates and working with host governments during crises. Israel contributes defence, agricultural and innovation technologies; India should maintain those ties alongside Arab partnerships and its commitment to a negotiated two-state solution. Iran remains relevant for energy and Chabahar-based access, yet sanctions exposure and maritime-route risk demand commercial due diligence. I2U2 can organise cooperation on food, water and energy; the 2023 IMEC MoU expresses connectivity intent, not a completed operating corridor.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks; answer in 220–250 words
**Analyse how US-Iran sanctions dynamics affect India's energy and connectivity choices without conflating exposure and legal obligation.**

**Model:** India's Iran policy joins energy to continental access. The JCPOA of 2015, endorsed by UNSC resolution 2231, opened diplomatic space; the United States announced withdrawal in May 2018 and restored sanctions in stages. After US Significant Reduction Exceptions ended in May 2019, India ceased Iranian crude purchases and substituted suppliers. Tharoor's observation that some Indian refineries suit Iranian grades explains why a supply shift can involve technical as well as diplomatic costs. Substitution did not eliminate India's overall import dependence.

Connectivity illustrates a second transmission channel. India's ten-year Chabahar contract was signed in May 2024 to support a port route that could bypass Pakistan's land-access obstacle. The earlier US Chabahar waiver was revoked effective September 2025. Banks, insurers and operators may then reassess Iran-linked exposure even where India has not adopted a corresponding domestic ban. US sanctions posture and India's international legal obligations are not the same thing. The owner cites a limited assurance of non-exposure only through April 2026; a later status requires fresh dated verification.

There is also a separate multilateral-law question: after E3 snapback action in 2025, Iran, Russia and China disputed the status of restored UN measures. It would be inaccurate to say that the JCPOA simply functions normally or to equate the disputed UN status with US domestic sanctions. India should maintain dialogue with Iran and the US, diversify energy sources, verify lawful finance/insurance channels and assess Chabahar's physical links independently. The choice is not blanket abandonment versus guaranteed immunity, but a reversible, evidence-based balance.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks; answer in 270–310 words
**Examine how India should manage simultaneous Iran, Israel and Gulf relationships during a regional crisis while preserving connectivity ambitions.**

**Model:** India's relationships in West Asia form a portfolio, not three mutually exclusive choices. The Gulf matters for imported energy and the welfare of large Indian communities. Israel contributes defence co-development such as Barak-8 and agricultural technology. Iran remains important for energy options and Chabahar access toward Afghanistan and Central Asia. Tharoor's formulation—Israel ties not at the expense of Arab or Muslim-world friendships—captures active balancing, not neutrality about attacks on civilians.

In a crisis, priorities must be sequenced. First, cooperate with host governments on citizen safety, consular contact and evacuations: Operation Sindhu brought back 4,415 nationals from Iran and Israel by 27 June 2025. Second, prepare for energy disruption via diversified producers, stocks and shipping contingencies; even varied Gulf suppliers may use the Strait of Hormuz. Third, articulate India's condemnation of terrorism, demand humanitarian protection and hostage release, and reaffirm a negotiated viable two-state settlement alongside its Israel relationship. The Abraham Accords created cooperation space but did not settle Palestine.

Connectivity requires separate realism. Chabahar's ten-year contract signed in May 2024 cannot insulate financiers indefinitely from the US waiver's revocation effective September 2025. This raises external sanctions exposure without automatically converting US domestic law into an Indian legal obligation. IMEC's 2023 eight-party MoU and I2U2's four-party agenda are diplomatic-economic frameworks, not verified operating corridors. Conflict may disrupt investment, clearance and public acceptance across either route. In addition, the JCPOA cannot be described as normally operating after subsequent withdrawal and contested UN snapback developments; legal positions must be dated and separated.

India should preserve dialogue with all partners, use the GCC strategic framework and bilateral channels for practical assurances, insist on humanitarian credibility and publish project-specific implementation evidence before promising connectivity. Balance is sustainable only when the interests of partner states and the safety of people constrain ambition.
