### SESSION 1 — The portfolio and its boundaries

#### VISUAL

```text
                          INDIA'S WEST ASIA PORTFOLIO
                                     |
        +----------------------------+----------------------------+
        |                            |                            |
   GULF / GCC                     ISRAEL                        IRAN
 energy + diaspora          defence + technology       energy + connectivity
        |                            |                            |
        +----------------------------+----------------------------+
                                     |
                         PALESTINE + HUMANITARIAN POSITION
                                     |
                     I2U2 / IMEC / BILATERAL AGREEMENTS
                                     |
                 conflict, chokepoint, sanctions and delivery risks
```

*One region, several partners, different interests and no automatic harmony among them.*

#### Core idea

✅ Shashi Tharoor describes a **“Look West”** policy parallel to Look East. In this topic the
phrase is a regional engagement doctrine, not a treaty or a single scheme. India's policy is a
portfolio because the three principal tracks do different work:

| Track | Main Indian interest | Typical instrument | Persistent constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gulf/GCC | energy, expatriate welfare, trade and investment | India-GCC dialogue; bilateral strategic partnerships; CEPA | regional conflict, route concentration, partner diversification |
| Israel | defence, counter-terrorism, agriculture, water and innovation | bilateral cooperation; I2U2 | Palestine question; Arab-world sensitivities; technology-transfer depth |
| Iran | energy and Chabahar-linked connectivity | nuclear diplomacy context; port contract; bilateral engagement | sanctions exposure, finance/insurance, US-Iran rivalry |
| Palestine | principled and humanitarian position | diplomatic recognition, aid, two-state advocacy | conflict volatility; credibility across competing partnerships |

⚠️ **Topic boundary:** Economy owns import-bill, price and tariff mechanics; Topic 09 owns
general diaspora diplomacy; Topic 10 owns the institutional comparison of minilaterals; Topic 05
owns Chabahar's Central Asian route; Geography owns detailed chokepoint mapping; Internal
Security and Disaster Management own operational security and relief cycles. This topic owns
their **external-policy connection**.

#### Exam link

Open with the portfolio, not with a list of countries:

> ⚠️ India's West Asia policy is a non-exclusive portfolio in which energy is the organising
> interest, diaspora welfare is an immediate obligation, Israel and Iran supply distinct strategic
> goods, and connectivity initiatives add opportunity without removing regional risk.

#### Traps

- ❌ West Asia policy = oil policy.
  ✅ Energy is central, but diaspora, defence, technology, Palestine and connectivity are integral.
- ❌ Better ties with Israel imply abandonment of Arab partners or Palestine.
  ✅ The canonical principle is simultaneous, non-exclusive engagement.
- ❌ A doctrine label creates a legal obligation.
  ✅ “Look West” is a policy description, not a treaty.

#### Recap

**Portfolio, not bloc; differentiated interests, not one regional relationship.**

### SESSION 2 — Energy security: dependence, diversification and the trilemma

#### VISUAL

```text
                         ENERGY TRILEMMA
                    +----------+----------+
                    |          |          |
                SECURITY   AFFORDABILITY  SUSTAINABILITY
              reliable flow  price/cost    transition
                    |          |          |
                    +----------+----------+
                               |
                   DIVERSIFY SUPPLIERS + ROUTES
                               |
                  reduces concentration risk, BUT
                               |
              does not by itself reduce import dependence
```

#### Start with the exact distinction

| Term | Meaning | What it does **not** mean |
|---|---|---|
| Energy security | reliable availability of energy | cheapest energy at all times |
| Affordability | manageable price and fiscal/consumer exposure | guaranteed low prices |
| Sustainability | compatibility with long-term transition | immediate exit from hydrocarbons |
| Supplier diversification | wider source basket | lower aggregate import dependence |
| Equity investment abroad | ownership stake supporting access/risk-spreading | immunity from conflict, transport or sanctions risk |

📰 The canonical PPAC anchor records crude-oil import dependence of **88.2% in FY2024-25** and
about **88.7% in FY2025-26 (provisional)**. ✅ Rajiv Sikri's “approximately 70 per cent” belongs
to his book period and is useful only as a historical baseline.

📰 The Economic Survey 2025-26 records a wider supplier base in April-November FY26: the US
share rose to **8.1% from 4.6%**, and the UAE share to **11.1% from 9.4%**; the shares of
**Egypt, Nigeria and Libya also rose**, while those of **Russia, Saudi Arabia, Iraq and
Venezuela declined**. This is the complete shift stated in the canonical owner. The correct
inference is **diversification**, not dependence-reduction; Economy owns the trade and price
mechanics.

✅ Sikri identifies equity investment in overseas oilfields as a response to import dependence.
⚠️ Such investment spreads source risk; it cannot eliminate common-route exposure, price shocks
or the need for a domestic energy transition.

#### Mechanism

```text
HIGH IMPORT DEPENDENCE
        |
        +--> supplier disruption risk
        +--> price / freight / insurance exposure
        +--> vulnerability at shared maritime routes
        |
POLICY RESPONSE
        +--> supplier broadening
        +--> equity stakes
        +--> strategic reserves / alternatives (cross-owner detail)
        +--> energy and technology diplomacy
        |
RESIDUAL RISK
        +--> aggregate dependence remains high
        +--> refineries cannot switch every crude grade instantly
        +--> war or sanctions may hit finance and shipping together
```

#### India-centric example

✅ Tharoor notes that many Indian refineries were configured for the quality of Iranian crude.
⚠️ This reveals a **switching cost**: diplomacy may identify an alternative supplier faster than
physical refining, contracting and logistics systems can adapt.

#### Exam link

For a question on energy security and foreign policy, integrate:

1. dependence baseline;
2. diversification and equity investment;
3. Gulf institutions and bilateral ties;
4. Iran/Israel/Palestine balancing;
5. diaspora protection;
6. corridor and chokepoint risk;
7. sustainability as the long-run limit.

#### Traps

- ❌ Diversification = self-sufficiency.
- ❌ A lower share for one supplier = lower total import dependence.
- ❌ Energy security is purely an Economy topic.
- ❌ An overseas asset guarantees delivery during conflict.

#### Recap

**Diversification changes the distribution of risk; transition changes the level of dependence.**

### SESSION 3 — Gulf and GCC architecture

#### VISUAL

```text
INDIA -- REGIONAL --> GCC STRATEGIC DIALOGUE
   |
   +-- BILATERAL --> UAE: Comprehensive Strategic Partnership + CEPA
   |
   +-- BILATERAL --> Saudi Arabia: Strategic Partnership Council
   |
   +-- BILATERAL --> Qatar: Bilateral Strategic Partnership agreement
   |
   +-- BILATERAL --> Oman: CEPA signed, then entered into force
```

#### Dated instrument table

| Instrument | Exact canonical anchor | Evidentiary level |
|---|---|---|
| India-GCC Joint Action Plan | **2024-2028**, adopted at the first India-GCC Joint Ministerial Meeting for Strategic Dialogue, Riyadh, **9 September 2024** | regional political/working framework |
| India-UAE CEPA | in force since **1 May 2022** | trade agreement in force |
| India-UAE relationship | Comprehensive Strategic Partnership since **25 January 2017** | bilateral partnership status |
| India-Saudi Strategic Partnership Council | second meeting, Jeddah, **22 April 2025** | bilateral institutional mechanism |
| India-Qatar bilateral strategic partnership | agreement signed **18 February 2025** | signed bilateral agreement |
| India-Oman CEPA | signed **18 December 2025**; entered into force **1 June 2026** | trade agreement in force; signing and operation remain distinct milestones |

⚠️ These instruments are not interchangeable. A strategic partnership is broader than a trade
agreement; a meeting is an institutional event, not proof that every announced project was
delivered; a signature is not entry into force. 📰 PIB records that the India-Oman CEPA was
signed in Muscat on 18 December 2025 and, after both sides completed internal processes, entered
into force on 1 June 2026.

#### Partner asymmetry

India seeks reliable energy, investment, markets and expatriate welfare. Gulf partners also have
their own agency: economic diversification, technology, food security, investment returns and
relations with several major powers. ⚠️ Therefore, “India needs the Gulf” is only half an answer.
The other half asks why each partner finds India useful and where priorities diverge.

#### Exam link

Use one regional anchor and one bilateral anchor. Then show the result and limit:

> 📰 The 2024-2028 Joint Action Plan institutionalises India-GCC engagement, while bilateral
> mechanisms with the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Qatar allow issue-specific depth; however, the
> existence of forums does not prove implementation, and Gulf states retain diversified external
> partnerships.

#### Traps

- ❌ GCC dialogue and bilateral strategic partnerships are the same level.
- ❌ Signed CEPA = automatically in force.
- ❌ A summit or council meeting = measurable outcome.
- ❌ Gulf states are passive suppliers with identical policies.

#### Recap

**Regional breadth plus bilateral depth; partner incentives must be written alongside India's.**

### SESSION 4 — Diaspora welfare: asset, exposure and consular obligation

#### VISUAL

```text
LARGE INDIAN PRESENCE IN THE GULF
          |
          +--> people-to-people link / economic contribution
          +--> host-country stake in stable relations
          +--> remittance context (ECONOMY owns data)
          |
          +--> crisis exposure
                   |
        warning + documentation + access + evacuation diplomacy
                   |
        transport/relief execution (cross-owned operationally)
```

#### Evidence discipline

✅ Sikri cites a **5 million** Gulf diaspora; ✅ Tharoor cites nearly **6 million** Indians in the
Arab world. Both are period-specific book figures. 📰 MEA's January 2026 population table records
**4,344,008** overseas Indians in the UAE and **2,750,551** in Saudi Arabia. These are
population-stock estimates, not counts of citizens or Overseas Citizens of India.

📰 **Operation Sindhu** evacuated **4,415 Indians** from Iran and Israel by **27 June 2025**
(3,597 from Iran and 818 from Israel). It is the canonical illustration of crisis-driven
consular diplomacy.

#### Three distinctions

| Distinction | Correct treatment |
|---|---|
| Diaspora stock vs citizenship | overseas-Indian estimates do not automatically equal citizens abroad |
| Consular diplomacy vs evacuation logistics | IR owns host-government coordination; operational relief is cross-owned |
| Asset vs vulnerability | a large community deepens ties but increases exposure during crises |

#### Exam link

Do not mention diaspora only as a remittance source. In a West Asia answer it performs three
analytical jobs: creates a welfare obligation, increases the cost of regional instability, and
gives bilateral relations a social foundation.

#### Traps

- ❌ Combining book-period and current estimates into one “fixed” number.
- ❌ Treating population stock as citizenship or OCI data.
- ❌ Calling every assisted departure a military operation.
- ❌ Discussing remittance amounts without Economy evidence.

#### Recap

**Diaspora is simultaneously constituency, bridge and crisis exposure.**

### SESSION 5 — India-Israel: what “depth and diversity” means

#### VISUAL

```text
FULL DIPLOMATIC RELATIONS (1992)
              |
POLITICAL UPGRADE (first Indian PM visit, July 2017)
              |
SPECIAL STRATEGIC PARTNERSHIP (26 February 2026)
              |
   +----------+-----------+-----------+------------+
   |                      |           |            |
DEFENCE              AGRICULTURE    WATER      INNOVATION
Barak-8             Centres of      irrigation  I4F, 2017
procurement /       Excellence      practices   industrial R&D
co-development
              |
         MINILATERAL EXTENSION: I2U2
```

#### The relationship by domain

| Domain | Named evidence | What it proves | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political | full diplomatic relations in **1992**; first Indian PM visit in **July 2017**; bilateral ties elevated on **26 February 2026** to a **“Special Strategic Partnership for Peace, Innovation and Prosperity”** | successive political institutionalisation of an already diverse relationship | this is the status declared in a joint statement, not a treaty-alliance obligation |
| Defence/security | procurement, counter-terrorism and **Barak-8** co-development; the 2026 joint statement welcomed the Defence Cooperation MoU signed **4 November 2025** and recorded a future roadmap | specialised capability, institutional continuity and prospective co-development | a roadmap or MoU must still be separated from delivered platforms and technology transfer |
| Agriculture/water | Indo-Israel Agricultural Project and Centres of Excellence | technology demonstration at farm level | scaling, extension and affordability determine wider impact |
| Innovation | India-Israel Industrial R&D and Technological Innovation Fund, launched **2017**; the 2026 statement identified AI, cybersecurity, semiconductors, quantum computing, biotechnology and space, and welcomed an NSA-led Critical and Emerging Technologies initiative | civilian and frontier-technology diversification beyond defence | stated cooperation priorities and funding mechanisms must be separated from measurable outcomes |
| Minilateral | I2U2 with UAE and US | bilateral ties can be embedded in a wider regional format | forum/dialogue activity must be separated from a four-government decision or delivered project |

#### Why the relationship can be called deep and diverse

“Deep” refers to sustained political and security trust; “diverse” means multiple civilian and
regional domains. ⚠️ Merely listing sectors is insufficient: explain the function of each sector.
The partnership is difficult to reverse because capabilities, institutions and applied projects
create constituencies beyond ceremonial diplomacy. 📰 The 26 February 2026 joint statement is
the latest status anchor: it **elevated** the relationship; it did not create a collective-defence
alliance or prove that every technology and defence objective had been delivered.

#### Traps

- ❌ India-Israel relations are only defence relations.
- ❌ Centres of Excellence automatically imply nationwide productivity gains.
- ❌ A research fund announcement proves commercial scale.
- ❌ I2U2 converts the bilateral relationship into an alliance.

#### Recap

**Depth = durable political/security trust; diversity = defence plus agriculture, water,
innovation and minilateral cooperation.**

### SESSION 6 — Palestine, Arab normalisation and India's balancing position

#### VISUAL

```text
                INDIA'S BALANCING POSITION
                         /       \
                        /         \
          practical ties           principled position
           with Israel             on Palestine
               |                        |
 defence / technology       recognition (1988) + negotiated,
 agriculture / innovation   sovereign, viable two-state solution
                        \         /
                         \       /
              Arab/Gulf friendships + humanitarian diplomacy
```

#### Stable anchors

- ✅ India recognised the **State of Palestine in 1988**.
- ✅ India supports a negotiated, sovereign and viable **two-state solution** alongside Israel.
- ✅ The **Abraham Accords (2020)** normalised Israel's relations with several Arab states and
  widened space for economic/minilateral cooperation; they did not settle the Palestinian issue.
- ⚠️ In responding to the conflict beginning in October 2023, the canonical owner records India's
  condemnation of terrorism, call for humanitarian protection and release of hostages, support
  for dialogue and a two-state settlement, continued Israel relations and assistance to
  Palestinians. Treat event-specific developments as volatile and verify them separately.
- ✅ Tharoor's durable balancing line is that India values Israel ties **“not at the expense of”**
  friendships with Arab and other Muslim states.

#### Balancing is not equidistance

| Concept | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| Balancing / non-exclusive engagement | actively pursuing different interests with competing partners |
| Equidistance | trying to remain equally distant; not the canonical description |
| Endorsement | accepting every act or position of a partner; not implied by engagement |
| Humanitarian position | protecting civilians and supporting aid/dialogue; not identical to alliance choice |

#### Arab Peace Initiative close-option lesson

The 2023 Prelims demand tested whether candidates could distinguish:

1. actual diplomatic relations between Israel and some Arab states; and
2. the **Arab Peace Initiative**, which must not be misdescribed as an agreement signed by Israel
   and the Arab League merely because Saudi Arabia promoted it.

No official 2023 key is held locally, so this session teaches the distinction without claiming an
official answer letter.

#### Traps

- ❌ Israel ties and Palestine support are mutually exclusive.
- ❌ Arab normalisation resolved the Palestinian question.
- ❌ Diplomatic engagement equals endorsement of all conduct.
- ❌ A peace initiative is necessarily a signed settlement.

#### Recap

**India's balance is active and interest-based, but its credibility depends on a consistent
two-state and humanitarian position.**

### SESSION 7 — Iran, JCPOA, sanctions exposure and Chabahar

#### VISUAL

```text
JCPOA, 14 JULY 2015 -- endorsed by UNSC Resolution 2231
             |
US WITHDRAWAL, 8 MAY 2018
             |
restored US sanctions + end of Significant Reduction Exceptions, May 2019
             |
India substitutes crude suppliers
             |
ENERGY TIE NARROWS, BUT CONNECTIVITY INTEREST CONTINUES
             |
Chabahar 10-year contract, 13 May 2024
             |
US waiver revoked effective 29 September 2025
             |
conditional non-exposure period through 26 April 2026
             |
period expired; current commercial / secondary-sanctions exposure must be assessed
             |
SANCTIONS EXPOSURE / FINANCING RISK != AUTOMATIC INTERNATIONAL OBLIGATION
```

#### What the controversy does to India's interests

| Transmission channel | Effect on India |
|---|---|
| Energy | loss or constriction of a historically important supplier; substitution cost |
| Refining | configuration for Iranian crude can slow switching |
| Finance/insurance | third-country restrictions affect payment, cover and project execution |
| Connectivity | Chabahar's commercial and financing environment becomes uncertain |
| Strategic autonomy | India must preserve Iran ties without allowing them to derail other major partnerships |

✅ The JCPOA was concluded on **14 July 2015** between Iran and the E3/EU+3 and endorsed by
UN Security Council Resolution **2231**. The United States announced withdrawal on **8 May
2018** and restored sanctions in stages. India ceased Iranian crude purchases after US
Significant Reduction Exceptions ended in **May 2019** and substituted other suppliers.

📰 India signed a **10-year Chabahar contract on 13 May 2024**. The United States announced
revocation of the sanctions exception effective **29 September 2025**, but subsequently provided
a **conditional waiver/non-exposure period through 26 April 2026**, as recorded by MEA in Lok
Sabha Question 2080. That period has now expired. The post-expiry issue is current exposure of
Indian entities to US commercial, banking, insurance and secondary-sanctions risk; do not convert
that exposure into a UN or general international-law obligation. Separately, the canonical owner
warns that the status of restored UN measures following the 2025 E3 snapback action is disputed
by Iran, Russia and China; do not describe the JCPOA as normally functioning.

#### Sanctions exposure versus obligation

```text
UN / binding international obligation? ---- verify legal source and status
                    !=
another state's unilateral sanctions ---- commercial, banking, insurance,
                                         technology and secondary-risk exposure
```

⚠️ India may alter behaviour because exposure is costly. That practical constraint is not the
same as saying international law automatically obliges India to follow another state's domestic
sanctions. The expired conditional period matters to the **current risk environment**; it does
not retrospectively turn a US executive assurance into a multilateral legal rule.

#### Chabahar: instrument and limit

Chabahar serves an Indian connectivity interest and can bypass Pakistan for access toward
Afghanistan/Central Asia. Yet a contract is not a guaranteed route outcome. Finance, insurance,
sanctions, partner implementation and onward links determine delivery. ✅ The historical
Iran-Pakistan-India pipeline proposal provides the parallel energy lesson: Tharoor records that it
remained “a serious attraction” despite US pressure, but India was reluctant to route energy
security through Pakistani territory. ⚠️ The proposal illustrates geopolitical and route-risk
constraints, not an operating pipeline; Geography owns detailed routing and Economy owns project
economics.

#### Traps

- ❌ US unilateral sanctions = universal international-law obligation.
- ❌ A waiver is a permanent legal right.
- ❌ The conditional Chabahar non-exposure period still runs. → It expired on **26 April 2026**.
- ❌ The JCPOA is functioning normally.
- ❌ A 10-year port contract proves uninterrupted corridor operation.
- ❌ Supplier substitution reduced India's total import dependence.

#### Recap

**Iran policy is the clearest test of strategic autonomy under external financial constraint.**

### SESSION 8 — I2U2, IMEC and the corridor delivery ladder

#### VISUAL

```text
I2U2                                     IMEC
India-Israel-UAE-US                     India-Middle East-Europe
issue-based economic minilateral        corridor initiative
food | water | energy | technology      eight-party MoU, 9 Sep 2023
        |                                       |
forum / project platform                political route framework
        |                                       |
        +------------- BOTH REQUIRE -------------+
          finance + contracts + construction +
          regulatory alignment + security +
          commercially viable operation
```

#### I2U2

I2U2 joins **India, Israel, the UAE and the United States** around food security, water, energy
and technology. It is a **minilateral**, not a treaty alliance. Its value lies in combining
Indian scale, Israeli technology, UAE capital/logistics and US convening/technology. ⚠️ That is a
functional complementarity claim, not proof that every proposed project has been delivered.

✅ The official MEA record establishes the **inaugural I2U2 Business Forum at Abu Dhabi on
22 February 2023**, where public- and private-sector representatives assessed the grouping's
announced food-security and clean-energy projects and discussed investment. A later
**N7 Initiative-hosted high-level dialogue in New Delhi on
23 April 2025** brought together senior government officials and experts from all four countries,
assessed ongoing projects and explored renewable energy, critical minerals, artificial
intelligence, infrastructure and IMEC linkages. ⚠️ The latter was a documented high-level
convening, not a four-government leaders' or ministerial meeting and not a joint governmental
outcome. A 13 February 2025 India-US statement separately recorded an intention to convene
partners. State each evidentiary level rather than claiming inactivity or delivery.

#### IMEC

📰 The IMEC MoU was signed on **9 September 2023** by eight parties: India, the United States,
Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the European Union, France, Germany and Italy. MEA's 24 June 2024
India-UAE readout referred to “commencement of work”. The canonical owner records no officially
established completed construction or operating segment.

#### The corridor delivery ladder

```text
CONCEPT
  -> political announcement
  -> MoU / framework
  -> route and standards design
  -> finance and contracts
  -> construction / upgraded nodes
  -> trial movement
  -> operating segment
  -> end-to-end commercial service
  -> measured economic outcome
```

Never jump over a rung.

#### I2U2, IMEC and Chabahar are not substitutes

| Instrument | Form | Principal strategic use | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| I2U2 | minilateral forum/project platform | food, water, energy, technology cooperation | delivery and continuity |
| IMEC | corridor MoU/framework | India-Gulf-Levant-Europe connectivity concept | conflict, financing, route continuity, implementation |
| Chabahar | port contract/bilateral connectivity instrument | Iran/Afghanistan/Central Asia access | sanctions, finance and onward connectivity |

#### Traps

- ❌ I2U2 is a collective-defence alliance.
- ❌ IMEC MoU = operating corridor.
- ❌ “Commencement of work” = completed segment.
- ❌ Corridor competition means India must choose only one route.
- ❌ Announcement creates commercial viability.

#### Recap

**Minilateral convening and corridor conception are useful diplomatic outputs; infrastructure
delivery is a separate evidentiary question.**

### SESSION 9 — Conflicts, chokepoints and compounding risk

#### VISUAL

```text
REGIONAL ESCALATION
        |
        +--> ENERGY: supply / freight / insurance
        |
        +--> DIASPORA: safety / consular access / evacuation
        |
        +--> CONNECTIVITY: route continuity / finance / political acceptance
        |
        +--> DIPLOMACY: pressure from competing partners
        |
        +--> HUMANITARIAN: civilian protection / aid / legal and reputational costs
```

#### Strait of Hormuz

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow outlet from the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the
principal common-route chokepoint for Gulf energy shipments. ⚠️ A wider supplier basket does not
remove shared-route exposure if several suppliers' ships still use the same chokepoint.

The 2026 Prelims demand asks which listed countries' ships must cross Hormuz to reach the Indian
Ocean. The locally held provisional Set-A key records **B**; this package publishes it only as a
provisional, non-final key.

#### Why West Asian risk is “compounding”

In many bilateral relationships, a crisis affects one major channel. In West Asia the same event
can affect fuel access, transport costs, citizens abroad, corridor plans and India's political
credibility at once. This is **covariance**: supposedly diversified interests remain exposed to a
common shock.

#### Conflict-writing protocol

1. date the event or omit it;
2. attribute contested claims to the party making them;
3. distinguish verified event, legal characterisation and inference;
4. connect the event to an Indian interest through a mechanism;
5. state alternatives and residual risk;
6. avoid forecasting escalation or settlement.

#### Traps

- ❌ More suppliers remove chokepoint risk.
- ❌ Any conflict reference proves energy disruption.
- ❌ A corridor is insulated from the politics of territories it crosses.
- ❌ A partner's military action automatically becomes India's endorsed position.
- ❌ Evacuation success proves the underlying regional risk has ended.

#### Recap

**West Asia creates correlated risk: one shock can reach energy, citizens, connectivity and
diplomacy simultaneously.**

### SESSION 10 — How to write the integrated answer

#### VISUAL

```text
QUESTION
   |
1 DEFINE THE INTEREST precisely
   |
2 MAP ACTORS: Gulf/GCC | Israel | Iran | Palestine | external powers
   |
3 CHOOSE LEVEL: bilateral | regional | minilateral | multilateral
   |
4 CITE INSTRUMENT + EXACT STATUS + DATE
   |
5 EXPLAIN MECHANISM and outcome so far
   |
6 TEST CONSTRAINT: conflict | sanctions | asymmetry | route | delivery
   |
7 OFFER CALIBRATED RESPONSE
   |
8 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION: diversified, non-exclusive, delivery-aware
```

#### Directive map

| Directive | What the examiner expects |
|---|---|
| Discuss | breadth plus limitations; not a one-sided list |
| Analyse | explain mechanisms and interrelations |
| Examine | test a proposition against evidence |
| Critically examine/evaluate | assess delivery and counter-evidence, not intentions |
| “In what ways” + “How should” | separate effects from response |

#### Mark-wise scaling

- **10 marks / 150 words:** thesis + three dimensions + one named instrument + one qualification.
- **15 marks / 250 words:** five dimensions + four to six named anchors + explicit counterpoint.
- **20 marks / up to 300 words:** portfolio architecture + causal chain + actor asymmetry +
  alternatives + implementation risks + graded verdict.

#### Stable anchor bank

| Use | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Energy vulnerability | PPAC **88.2% FY2024-25**; about **88.7% FY2025-26 provisional** |
| Regional framework | India-GCC Joint Action Plan **2024-2028**, adopted **9 September 2024** |

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

> Use this block to upgrade a sound core answer. Do not let it displace the Basic portfolio.

### 1. Dependence has three layers

```text
VOLUME DEPENDENCE  -> how much must be imported
SOURCE DEPENDENCE  -> how concentrated the suppliers are
ROUTE DEPENDENCE   -> how concentrated the transport chokepoints are
```

⚠️ Supplier diversification can improve the second layer while leaving the first and third
largely intact. This is why a statement that “diversification solved dependence” is analytically
wrong even if supplier shares changed.

### 2. Refinery lock-in as a diplomatic constraint

✅ Tharoor's observation about refineries configured for Iranian crude converts an engineering
fact into an IR insight. Supplier choice is not frictionless. Crude grade, refinery design,
contracts, payment systems, shipping and insurance form a chain; a break anywhere can slow
substitution. ⚠️ Foreign-policy flexibility therefore depends partly on domestic technical
capacity.

✅ A second historical constraint is the proposed **Iran-Pakistan-India pipeline**. Tharoor
describes it as “a serious attraction” despite US pressure, while also recording India's
reluctance to route energy security through Pakistani territory. ⚠️ It demonstrates how transit
dependence and bilateral insecurity can defeat resource complementarity. It is historical
analytical evidence, not a current project-status claim; Geography owns route detail and Economy
owns pipeline viability.

### 3. The compounding-risk matrix

| Shock | Energy | Diaspora | Connectivity | Diplomacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gulf maritime disruption | route/freight exposure | possible crisis movement | shipping nodes affected | partner reassurance needed |
| Israel-Palestine escalation | indirect market/route effects | safety and evacuation exposure | political acceptance and route risk | balance Israel ties with Palestine/Arab position |
| Iran-related sanctions change | supplier/payment constraint | consular and travel effects | Chabahar finance/insurance | manage US-Iran pressures |

⚠️ The table maps transmission routes; it does not claim that every shock always produces every
effect.

### 4. Sanctions exposure is a spectrum

```text
direct legal prohibition on an Indian entity
        |
secondary-sanctions risk
        |
banking / insurance over-compliance
        |
reputational caution
        |
project delay without formal prohibition
```

An advanced answer identifies where a constraint sits on this spectrum. It avoids both extremes:
“India is legally bound by every foreign sanction” and “foreign sanctions do not matter”.

### 5. Corridors are political economies, not lines on maps

Delivery requires:

1. compatible infrastructure;
2. customs and standards coordination;
3. bankable freight volumes;
4. financing and insurance;
5. security and political acceptance;
6. interoperable digital/logistics systems;
7. credible end-to-end time/cost advantage.

⚠️ A route may be geopolitically attractive but commercially weak, or commercially plausible but
politically blocked. IMEC and Chabahar should therefore be assessed through **delivery conditions**,
not celebratory map arrows.

### 6. Minilateral advantage and weakness

I2U2's small size can support focused project cooperation and complementary resources. But a
minilateral has no automatic continuity, collective-defence clause or supranational enforcement.
Its output depends on political attention, project finance and partner convergence.

### 7. Partner asymmetry as a two-sided test

| Indian expectation | Partner question |
|---|---|
| reliable energy | what market, investment or strategic return does the supplier receive? |
| technology transfer | what protects intellectual property and commercial incentives? |
| corridor access | who finances, secures and uses the route? |
| diaspora welfare | what host-country labour and regulatory interests apply? |
| diplomatic support | where do partners preserve independent relations with India's competitors? |

### 8. Advanced thesis

> ⚠️ India's West Asia policy is an exercise in managing **three dependencies**—volume, source
> and route—through a **three-track relationship** with the Gulf, Israel and Iran, while a large
> diaspora and the Palestine question convert material exposure into a continuing political and
> humanitarian obligation. Minilaterals and corridors diversify instruments, but their strategic
> value depends on delivery, partner convergence and resilience to common shocks.
