**Original model answer (150-word exam route):**

India's long relationship with Sri Lanka gained practical meaning when the 2022 foreign-exchange crisis disrupted essentials. By 3 May 2022, India's High Commission recorded over USD 3 billion in assistance: a USD 1 billion facility for essentials, a USD 500 million fuel credit line, about USD 1 billion in Asian Clearing Union payment deferment and a USD 400 million RBI currency swap. Fuel deliveries under the credit line approached 400,000 tonnes across all 25 districts. Combining supplies, credit and payment relief helped ease immediate scarcity and financing stress; this is stronger evidence of friendship than rhetorical solidarity.

Yet assistance did not resolve Sri Lanka's structural debt, revenue and governance problems. Durable recovery required Sri Lankan decisions and IMF-supported adjustment. Later cooperation, including the seven MoUs exchanged in April 2025, indicates an agenda beyond emergency relief, but an MoU is not a completed project. India's first-responder credibility rests on timely assistance; lasting partnership requires consent, delivery and Sri Lankan ownership.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain the core principles of the Gujral Doctrine and show how they address the structural asymmetry between India and its smaller neighbours. Answer in about 150 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

India's size can make a smaller neighbour fear that every negotiation will reflect unequal bargaining power. The Gujral Doctrine responds with non-reciprocal accommodation: India need not demand an equivalent concession for each benefit it offers smaller neighbours. This does not make cooperation a one-sided claim on their sovereignty.

The other principles require non-interference in internal affairs, respect for territorial integrity, prevention of the use of one's territory against another, and peaceful bilateral settlement of disputes. Those restraints are mutual security assurances, not an Indian right to dictate domestic policy. In practice, a power or transit link can bring benefits to both sides even if India bears more initial cost; Bhutanese hydropower cooperation illustrates the potential, while ecological effects and project costs require partner consent.

Non-reciprocity is therefore a method for converting asymmetry into reassurance. It cannot eliminate water scarcity, local political disagreement or unequal implementation capacity; sustained consultation and delivered projects determine whether goodwill becomes trust.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Examine why the durability of India's neighbourhood engagement is tested by political transitions in partner states rather than by the number of agreements signed. Answer in about 150 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

A signed agreement establishes an intention or commitment; it does not establish popular consent, operating infrastructure or trust after a change of government. Sikri therefore urges a move from excessive regime-oriented diplomacy to people-to-people links. A project valued by local users and institutions is likelier to survive an incumbent's departure, though no transition guarantees its continuation.

Bangladesh's interim arrangement began in August 2024 and its election was held in February 2026. India also engaged Nepal's interim government welcomed in September 2025, followed by its March 2026 election. These are dated tests of diplomatic continuity, not proof that all earlier projects were delivered. In Sri Lanka, seven MoUs exchanged in April 2025 covered energy and development, but Sampur's 120 MW solar project reached ground-breaking rather than commissioning.

Durability therefore requires both consent and execution: customs working on cross-border routes, financed construction and benefits surviving political change. Meeting a successor government matters, but measurable, locally accepted delivery matters more.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the observation that regional-framework cooperation appeals more to India's smaller neighbours than purely bilateral engagement. Answer in about 250 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

India's size relative to its neighbours creates an asymmetry even where interests converge. Sikri observes that Bhutan, Nepal, Maldives and Sri Lanka have historically been more receptive to regional economic cooperation than larger Pakistan and Bangladesh: negotiating alongside several partners provides a sense of security. The observation is a structural tendency, not an invariant preference attributed to every government.

Regional settings such as SAARC or BIMSTEC can provide political reassurance, opportunities to compare positions and a less unequal forum. Yet a regional meeting cannot by itself build a railway or settle a power-purchase contract. Bangladesh, Bhutan, India and Nepal signed the BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement in 2015 to facilitate cross-border movement, but Bhutan's non-ratification limits four-country implementation. Bilateral engineering and regulatory work remain necessary. The Akhaura–Agartala India–Bangladesh rail connection was inaugurated on 1 November 2023; actual use still depends on customs and last-mile infrastructure. With Bhutan, the Mangdechhu hydropower project was commissioned in 2019 and handed over in 2022, an example of delivery that also raises cost, ecology and distribution questions.

The Gujral Doctrine offers an additional response to anxiety: non-reciprocal accommodation for smaller neighbours coupled with respect for sovereignty, non-interference, non-use of territory against each other and peaceful bilateral settlement. Neither regional membership nor Indian generosity eliminates a partner's ability to choose other external ties. Success requires combining regional reassurance with consensual bilateral implementation and judging outcomes by accessible trade and power, not meeting attendance.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the implementation-credibility gap in India's neighbourhood connectivity and development commitments. Answer in about 250 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Neighbourhood First turns a political priority into tangible links only through several distinct stages: agreement, financing, construction, operationalisation and sustained use. Treating all stages as completed when a document is signed exaggerates delivery and can damage trust in a region where India's scale already invites sensitivity.

The Kaladan corridor illustrates partial progress. Sittwe Port and its waterway were operationalised in May 2023, but the road component still lagged when India and Myanmar called for completion on 1 June 2026; an operating port is not an end-to-end safe route. In Bhutan, the 29 September 2025 MoU identified Kokrajhar–Gelephu and Banarhat–Samtse rail links, not commissioned services. Yet the 720 MW Mangdechhu plant, commissioned in 2019 and handed over in 2022, shows that delivery is possible. In Sri Lanka, the April 2025 visit inaugurated rooftop solar units while the 120 MW Sampur solar project only broke ground. These are different statuses, not interchangeable indicators of success.

Bottlenecks are country- and sector-specific: Myanmar's conflict and road security, Bhutanese environmental and cost concerns, and customs and trans-shipment barriers to Bangladesh connectivity. India must consult border communities, state governments and partner authorities instead of assuming signed commitments create local consent. More agreements may signal momentum but cannot prove increased traffic, affordable power or public trust. The credible metric is a functioning, mutually accepted link that continues to produce benefits across changes of government, with partner ownership and residual risks reported honestly.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess how India's neighbourhood doctrine performs when water-sharing and security conditions are linked. Answer in about 300 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

The Gujral Doctrine's non-reciprocal accommodation helps a much larger India reassure smaller neighbours. But shared water is finite and politically contested; generosity cannot substitute for allocations accepted by both sides. Its other principles—sovereign equality, territorial respect, non-interference, non-use of territory against neighbours and peaceful bilateral settlement—remain essential when water intersects with security.

India's unresolved Teesta water-sharing issue with Bangladesh illustrates the domestic-federal dimension: Indian state-level stakeholders shape what the Union can promise. The 1996 Ganga Waters Treaty is a separate bilateral instrument, due to expire in December 2026. As India stated on 13 February 2026, renewal talks had not begun at that date. Neither fact establishes the outcome of later negotiations. Trade, rail and power cooperation with Bangladesh should not be reduced to the water dispute, but a prospective water settlement needs credible river information and bilateral consent.

Pakistan raises a different security linkage. On 23 April 2025 the Cabinet Committee on Security said the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty would be held in abeyance until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjured support for cross-border terrorism. This is India's stated conditional executive decision; it should not be silently described as joint treaty termination. India's separate position on the Court of Arbitration's legitimacy is likewise its position, not an uncontested multilateral adjudication. The May 2025 understanding to stop military activities is not a new comprehensive water settlement.

These cases qualify any assertion that non-reciprocity is unconditional. The Gujral framework was designed to reduce domination fears, yet domestic scarcity, security threats and counterpart agency can alter feasible concessions. The way forward is specific rather than rhetorical: keep treaty text and status distinct, consult affected Indian states and partner communities, use transparent technical discussions, and preserve peaceful channels even under strain. Measure success by legitimate, implementable arrangements, not by a unilateral declaration that every dispute has ended.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess India's role in Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis as a test of first-responder credibility and durable partnership. Answer in about 300 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Friendship with Sri Lanka is a diplomatic claim; rapid relief in an acute economic crisis is an observable test. As imports of essentials became difficult in 2022, India combined instruments rather than offering only symbolic support. By 3 May 2022 its High Commission recorded over USD 3 billion in assistance: a USD 1 billion facility for food and medicines, a USD 500 million petroleum credit line, around USD 1 billion in Asian Clearing Union payment deferment and a USD 400 million RBI currency swap. Nearly 400,000 tonnes of fuel had reached all 25 Sri Lankan districts under the petroleum line. The full-year support is commonly described at roughly USD 4 billion; these figures have different time windows and should not be added.

The mix matters causally. Credit and fuel addressed immediate shortages; payment deferment and the swap eased near-term external financing stress. India could thus act as a first responder while retaining Sri Lankan agency over how to stabilise the economy. Neither credit nor supplies removed structural debt, revenue shortfalls or governance problems. Sustainable recovery required domestic adjustment and an IMF-supported programme; attributing the whole recovery to India would overstate its role.

The partnership test continued beyond the emergency. During the April 2025 visit, the sides exchanged seven MoUs on matters including HVDC electricity interconnection, digital cooperation, the Trincomalee energy hub, defence cooperation, Eastern Province grants and health. Solar rooftop units were inaugurated; the 120 MW Sampur project only broke ground. Future benefits depend on finance, local consent, ecological safeguards and durable delivery.

The 2022 package demonstrated a capacity to provide timely public goods; it did not confer control over Sri Lanka or complete all later projects. Neighbourhood First becomes credible when urgent solidarity is followed by mutually chosen, demonstrably operational connectivity and development.
