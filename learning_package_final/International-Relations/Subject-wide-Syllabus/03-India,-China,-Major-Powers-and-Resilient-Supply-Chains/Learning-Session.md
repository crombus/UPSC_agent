### SESSION 1 — FOUNDATION — The analytical map: five relationships, one resilience problem

#### VISUAL

```text
                         INDIA'S INTERESTS
 security · technology · energy · growth · autonomy · regional influence
                                  |
          +-----------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
          |           |           |           |           |
          v           v           v           v           v
        CHINA         USA        RUSSIA      JAPAN         EU
  trade + mistrust  tech +      defence +  security +   market +
  + boundary        defence     energy      technology   standards
          \           |           |           /           /
           +----------+-----------+----------+-----------+
                                  |
                                  v
                    RESILIENT SUPPLY CHAINS
         diversify sources · build capacity · preserve engagement
                                  |
                                  v
                    STRATEGIC-AUTONOMY TEST
         Does capability grow faster than dependence on any one partner?
```

*The topic is not a list of countries. It is a comparison of structurally different
relationships through one interest–instrument–outcome–constraint chain.*

#### EXPLANATION

This topic owns India's bilateral relations with **China, the United States, Russia, Japan and
the European Union**, together with the diplomacy of **critical and emerging technology** and
**resilient supply chains**. It also owns the strategic implications of the Belt and Road
Initiative (BRI) and the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) for India in South Asia.

Use the master framework in this order:

```text
INDIA'S INTEREST
      ↓
PARTNER'S INTEREST AND EXTERNAL CONTEXT
      ↓
INSTRUMENT: agreement / initiative / dialogue / framework / project
      ↓
IMPLEMENTATION AND VERIFIED OUTCOME
      ↓
CONSTRAINT: mistrust / dependence / sanctions / finance / delay / partner agency
      ↓
CALIBRATED RESPONSE: diversify / localise / hedge / sequence / build alternatives
```

The five relationships are not interchangeable:

| Relationship | Core structure | Main opportunity | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| China | Economic interdependence plus strategic mistrust | Trade and selective engagement | Unresolved boundary, asymmetry and influence competition |
| United States | Technology- and defence-led convergence | Critical technology, industry and intelligence-enabling cooperation | Export controls, policy volatility and dependence risk |
| Russia | Defence-energy complementarity and continuity | Installed-base support, high-end defence and energy | Concentration, sanctions exposure and supply disruption |
| Japan | Security, infrastructure and technology partnership | Logistics, infrastructure and supply-chain diversification | Delivery pace and limited substitutability |
| European Union | Market, technology and regulatory partnership | Standards, green transition and diversified technology access | Regulatory differences and implementation gaps |

#### EXAMPLE

✅ India can use the United States for semiconductor and critical-technology cooperation, Russia
for defence spares and energy, and Japan for infrastructure and supply-chain diversification
without treating any relationship as an exclusive patron-client arrangement.

#### EXAM LINK

- **Prelims:** classify the legal or political character of each instrument.
- **Mains:** compare interests, instruments, outcomes and constraints; do not narrate visits.

#### TRAPS

- Major-power partnership ≠ alliance.
- Trade relationship ≠ strategic trust.
- Diversification ≠ complete substitution.
- Grouping profiles of the Quad and I2U2 are owned by Topic 10; use them here only as
  non-exclusive instruments.
- Tariff, balance-of-payments and production mechanics are owned by Economy.

#### RECAP

> 🔑 **Answer formula:** partner structure → Indian interest → named instrument → implementation
> status → constraint → autonomy-preserving response.

### SESSION 2 — CORE — India–China: interdependence without strategic trust

#### VISUAL

```text
ECONOMIC TRACK                         STRATEGIC TRACK
trade and inputs                       unresolved boundary
commercial engagement                  military risk and mistrust
manufacturing dependence               regional influence competition
          |                                      |
          +------------------+-------------------+
                             |
                             v
                 INDIA–CHINA PARADOX
     cooperation can continue in one domain while distrust persists in another
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ Rajiv Sikri's book-period assessment deliberately separates **trade interdependence** from
**strategic trust**. China could be described in that period as India's largest trading partner
while the unresolved boundary and competition for influence sustained “enduring mistrust and
suspicion.” The durable lesson is not the historical ranking; it is the analytical separation.
Any present-day ranking requires current official trade data.

The boundary vocabulary must remain precise:

| Term | Meaning | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|
| Border claim | A state's stated territorial position | A mutually agreed boundary |
| Line of Actual Control perception | A party's understanding of the line separating actual control | A jointly demarcated international boundary |
| Patrol arrangement | Rules or practical access for patrolling specified areas | Final settlement |
| Disengagement | Separation or pullback at a specified friction point | Complete de-escalation |
| De-escalation | Reduction of forces, readiness or tension | Boundary delimitation |
| Delimitation/demarcation | Agreement on the boundary in text/on maps/on ground | Automatically completed by a dialogue |
| Boundary settlement | Final political-legal resolution | The same as restoration of a patrol pattern |

📰 On **21 October 2024**, MEA announced an agreement on patrolling arrangements along the Line
of Actual Control leading to disengagement and resolution of issues that had arisen in specified
areas since 2020; the canonical owner identifies Depsang and Demchok. The **23rd Special
Representatives' meeting on 18 December 2024** remained a dialogue process. Neither instrument
should be called a final boundary settlement.

The later process must be described with equally strict verbs:

- On **19 August 2025**, the **24th Special Representatives' talks** were co-chaired in New Delhi
  by India's National Security Adviser Ajit Doval and China's Special Representative Wang Yi.
  The official ten-point consensus **agreed to set up** an Expert Group under the WMCC to explore
  “Early Harvest” of boundary delimitation on appropriate sectors, a WMCC Working Group for
  effective border management, and General-Level Mechanisms in the Eastern and Middle sectors.
  It also agreed to re-open three traditional border trading markets; the canonical Indian route
  identifies the designated points as **Lipulekh, Shipki La and Nathu La**. These are agreed
  mechanisms and directions; they are not a delimited boundary or proof that every mechanism or
  trading route was already operational that day.
- Prime Minister Modi and President Xi met at **Tianjin on 31 August 2025**. The MEA bilateral
  brief verifies the meeting; a leader-level meeting is not itself a border agreement.
- The **35th WMCC met on 27 May 2026** and the official MEA page records the **36th WMCC on
  6 August 2026**. WMCC is a consultation and coordination mechanism. Its continuation
  demonstrates process, not settlement. The 36th-meeting page's full text was not retrievable in
  this audit, so no more specific 36th-WMCC outcome is claimed.
- The later official record is the **25th Special Representatives' talks in Beijing on
  25 August 2026**, between Wang Yi and Ajit Doval. Its **eight-point consensus** recorded that
  the Expert Group on Boundary Delimitation and Working Group on Border Management would first
  agree their respective Terms of Reference; agreed **two additional military meeting points**
  and **two additional border-military hotline channels** in the Eastern and Middle sectors; and
  appreciated that the **three designated border trading points had reopened**. These are
  process and border-management developments, not a boundary settlement.

#### EXAMPLE

An answer may say: “Commercial engagement can reduce the cost of conflict, but it cannot by
itself settle territorial disagreement or eliminate military mistrust.” This links evidence to a
mechanism without claiming that trade causes peace.

#### EXAM LINK

- **Prelims:** distinguish patrol arrangement, disengagement and settlement.
- **Mains:** write two parallel tracks—economic and strategic—before explaining their interaction.

#### TRAPS

- “High trade means normal political relations.”
- “An LAC arrangement settles the border.”
- “Dialogue round = agreed outcome.”
- “Agreement to create a mechanism = that mechanism is already fully operational.”
- “Every China-related question is only a security question.”

#### RECAP

India–China relations are best understood as **managed coexistence between cooperation and
competition**, not as either friendship or total disengagement.

### SESSION 3 — CORE — Trade-pattern asymmetry and the logic of de-risking

#### VISUAL

```text
CONCENTRATION IN A CRITICAL INPUT
            ↓
external shock / coercion / logistics failure
            ↓
production interruption + bargaining vulnerability
            ↓
DE-RISKING RESPONSE
multiple suppliers + inventories + domestic capacity + trusted partnerships
            ↓
engagement continues, but single-source exposure falls

DE-RISKING ≠ DECOUPLING ≠ AUTARKY
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ Sikri records a book-period grievance in which India exported largely raw materials and
commodities while China exported manufactured goods, alongside a trade deficit of over
US$10 billion in the 2007-era context. This is **historical period evidence**, not a current
trade figure. Its analytical use is to show that the economic relationship itself carried a
structural asymmetry.

| Concept | Precise meaning |
|---|---|
| Diversification | Expanding sources, destinations or production locations |
| De-risking | Reducing excessive concentration while retaining engagement |
| Decoupling | Severing or sharply unwinding the relationship |
| Friend-shoring | Shifting selected critical links toward trusted or aligned partners |
| Reshoring | Moving production back to the home economy |
| Autarky | Attempted self-sufficiency with minimal external dependence |

⚠️ India's posture is best framed as **de-risking, not decoupling**. Electronics, active
pharmaceutical ingredients and other manufactured intermediates cannot be replaced instantly.
Resilience therefore combines diversification abroad with capacity-building at home.

#### EXAMPLE

If India adds a Japanese or American supplier for a critical component but continues purchasing
from China, concentration risk falls without decoupling. If domestic fabrication is announced
but not operational, only the policy input has changed—not the measured supply outcome.

#### EXAM LINK

Use four tests: **concentration, substitutability, time-to-replace and switching cost**. A supply
chain is resilient when it can absorb disruption, recover and adapt; it is not resilient merely
because a new MoU exists.

#### TRAPS

- Historical deficit figure presented as current.
- Friend-shoring treated as ordinary low-cost offshoring.
- One new supplier treated as full resilience.
- Resilience treated as import elimination.

#### RECAP

> 🔑 **Best phrase:** diversification of dependence—not a fantasy of dependence-free growth.

### SESSION 4 — CORE — India–United States: technology-led convergence without alliance

#### VISUAL

```text
POLITICAL CONVERGENCE
       |
       +──▶ DEFENCE ENABLERS
       |     LEMOA 2016 → logistics
       |     COMCASA 2018 → secure communications
       |     BECA 2020 → geospatial information
       |
       +──▶ TECHNOLOGY COOPERATION
       |     iCET: announced May 2022
       |     inaugural NSA meeting: 31 Jan 2023
       |     second NSA review: 17 Jun 2024
       |
       +──▶ SUPPLY-CHAIN COOPERATION
             semiconductors · AI · space · defence industry · clean energy

LIMITS: export controls · commercial terms · policy volatility · dependence risk
STATUS: partnership / initiative / enabling agreement — NOT collective defence
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ Shashi Tharoor recommends treating India–US ties as a **“tactical partnership”**, not an
alliance, and cautions against hyperbole. The relationship can be deep because interests converge,
yet it remains non-alliance-based because no collective-defence obligation binds India.

The three enabling agreements improve practical cooperation:

| Instrument | Function | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| LEMOA (2016) | Reciprocal logistics support | No automatic basing right |
| COMCASA (2018) | Secure communications | No collective-defence guarantee |
| BECA (2020) | Geospatial-information exchange | No surrender of independent policy |

The United States designated India a **Major Defence Partner in 2016**. This is a bilateral
partnership designation, not treaty-alliance membership.

✅ The **initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET)** was announced in **May 2022**,
held its inaugural National Security Adviser-level meeting on **31 January 2023**, and a second
NSA-level review on **17 June 2024**. It covers semiconductors, artificial intelligence, space and
defence-industrial cooperation. It is a **cooperation initiative**, not a treaty or framework
agreement.

📰 Bilateral clean-energy supply-chain commitments announced on **21 September 2024** extended
the resilience agenda into clean-energy manufacturing inputs. An announcement is evidence of
commitment, not proof of relocated production.

#### THE 2025–26 US ARCHITECTURE — FOUR DIFFERENT LEGAL/POLICY CATEGORIES

| Item | Date and actor | Exact status | What may safely be claimed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **COMPACT** — Catalyzing Opportunities for Military Partnership, Accelerated Commerce & Technology | US President and Indian Prime Minister, 13 February 2025 | New bilateral **initiative** launched in the joint leaders' statement | It organises a results-driven military, commerce and technology agenda; it is not a treaty |
| **TRUST** — Transforming the Relationship Utilizing Strategic Technology | Same joint statement, 13 February 2025 | Critical-technology **initiative** launched | It covers government, academic and private-sector cooperation in defence, AI, semiconductors, quantum, biotechnology, energy and space; the statement does not say it terminated iCET |
| **Mission 500** | Same joint statement, 13 February 2025 | Political **goal** of US$500 billion bilateral trade by 2030 | A target, not an achieved outcome. The statement announced BTA negotiations; a February 2026 US fact sheet records a framework for an Interim Agreement and continuing broader BTA negotiations |
| **Framework for the U.S.-India Major Defense Partnership** | US Secretary of Defense and Indian Defence Minister, Kuala Lumpur, 31 October 2025 | Signed **ten-year defence framework** | A guiding framework for cooperation and interoperability, not a mutual-defence treaty |

The relationship's 2025–26 tariff cycle is a crucial counterweight to a one-directional
“fostering India” narrative:

```text
EO 14326, 31 Jul 2025
India rate in Annex I = additional 25%
effective 7 Aug 2025
        ↓
EO 14329, 6 Aug 2025
additional 25% on covered Indian imports linked to Russian-oil purchases
effective 27 Aug 2025
        ↓
EO 14384, 6 Feb 2026
oil-linked additional 25% removed from 7 Feb 2026
        ↓
EO 14389, 20 Feb 2026
termination of specified IEEPA additional-duty collection directed
as soon as practicable
        ↓
USTR final Section 301 action, 23 Jul 2026
10% on products of India except listed exemptions
applicable from 24 Jul 2026
```

These were **executive or statutory tariff measures**, not treaty obligations. The July 2026
Section 301 action addressed forced-labour import-prohibition practices across 60 trading
partners; it is legally and analytically distinct from the earlier reciprocal and
Russian-oil-linked duties. The sequence proves policy reversibility and continuing trade friction,
not the collapse of the strategic partnership.

✅ Tharoor also notes continuing restrictions on the sale of US high technology to India. That
friction is analytically important: convergence does not guarantee unconditional access.

#### EXAMPLE

iCET can expand India's capabilities if cooperation produces research, co-development,
manufacturing or transferable know-how. If India merely replaces one imported dependency with
another, the partnership may deepen while strategic autonomy does not.

#### EXAM LINK

For every US instrument write: **purpose → exact status → capability gain → remaining friction**.

#### TRAPS

- iCET = treaty.
- LEMOA = US base in India.
- Major Defence Partner = NATO ally.
- COMPACT/TRUST = ratified agreements.
- Mission 500 = achieved trade volume.
- The 2025 defence framework = mutual-defence treaty.
- One tariff order = permanent bilateral trade status.
- Technology dialogue = guaranteed transfer.

#### RECAP

India–US convergence is strongest when it produces Indian capability; it becomes constraining
when access remains conditional and dependence becomes concentrated.

### SESSION 5 — CORE — India–Russia: complementarity, installed-base dependence and Ukraine

#### VISUAL

```text
INDIA'S NEEDS                       RUSSIA'S OFFER
defence platforms/spares     ↔      installed military ecosystem
high-end systems             ↔      specialised equipment access
energy security              ↔      energy-surplus economy
            \                         /
             \                       /
              v                     v
           DEFENCE–ENERGY COMPLEMENTARITY
                         |
                         v
         DEPENDENCE TEST: spares · sanctions · payments · alternatives
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ Sikri describes the economic logic as an **energy-deficient India** engaging an
**energy-surplus Russia**. He also identifies Russia as a source of high-technology defence
equipment, including nuclear-powered submarines, unavailable to India from other suppliers in the
book's period.

The relationship is more than procurement:

- Su-30MKI licensed production and the BrahMos joint venture illustrate co-production.
- The **2018 S-400 contract** is a named high-end acquisition.
- The AK-203 joint venture at Korwa illustrates the attempt to localise production.

Yet “complementarity” must not conceal a narrower **installed-base and spares dependence**.
Operational fleets require maintenance, components, upgrades and ammunition. Substitution takes
years, so sudden rupture carries direct readiness costs.

⚠️ The S-400 purchase also demonstrates **CAATSA exposure**. A third state's domestic sanctions
law can create commercial and diplomatic pressure on India, but that exposure is not itself an
international-law obligation on India.

📰 President Vladimir Putin's state visit to India on **4–5 December 2025** included the
**23rd India–Russia Annual Summit** with Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The official joint
statement, dated **5 December 2025**, is titled **“India–Russia: A Time-Tested Progressive
Partnership, Anchored in Trust & Mutual Respect.”** The leaders welcomed adoption of the
**Programme for the Development of Strategic Areas of India–Russia Economic Cooperation till
2030**. The summit and programme are political/institutional commitments and a work programme;
they do not prove implementation of every listed objective or any unverified equipment-delivery
or trade-volume claim.

#### RUSSIA–UKRAINE APPLICATION

✅ Russia's full-scale military action against Ukraine began in **February 2022**. The canonical
owner records India's repeated emphasis on dialogue and diplomacy, sovereignty and territorial
integrity, humanitarian assistance, and abstention on polarising UN resolutions.

```text
UN-CHARTER PRINCIPLES
        + NATIONAL INTERESTS
energy · fertiliser · defence spares · partner access
        + EXTERNAL PRESSURES
sanctions · payments · logistics · reputation
        ↓
CALIBRATED POSTURE
dialogue + humanitarian support + selective abstention + diversified partnerships
```

The case shows strategic autonomy as a difficult allocation of costs, not as moral indifference.

#### EXAM LINK

Use “complementarity” for the relationship, “dependence” for the installed base, and
“sanctions exposure” for third-party pressure. These three labels describe different things.

#### TRAPS

- Russia tie = only nostalgia.
- Complementarity = absence of dependence.
- CAATSA exposure = treaty obligation on India.
- Programme 2030 adopted = every objective delivered.
- Diversification = immediate replacement of the installed base.

#### RECAP

The sustainable objective is continuity without veto: maintain necessary cooperation while
expanding domestic maintenance, co-production and supplier diversity.

### SESSION 6 — CORE — Japan, the European Union and Germany: diversification beyond a US–China binary

#### VISUAL

```text
                         DIVERSIFICATION PILLARS
        +----------------------+----------------------+
        |                      |                      |
      JAPAN                    EU                  GERMANY
 security + logistics     market + standards    technology +
 infrastructure + tech    green + digital       industrial partner
        |                      |                      |
        +----------------------+----------------------+
                               |
                               v
        wider capital, technology, standards and production options
```

#### EXPLANATION

**Japan**

- ✅ India–Japan ties were elevated to a **Special Strategic and Global Partnership in 2014**.
- 📰 The third Foreign and Defence Ministers' **2+2 meeting on 20 August 2024** agreed to revise
  the 2008 Joint Declaration on Security Cooperation.
- ✅ The reciprocal supplies-and-services agreement was signed in **September 2020** and became
  operational in **2021**. Logistics access improves interoperability; it is not collective defence.
- ✅ Australia, India and Japan launched the **Supply Chain Resilience Initiative (SCRI) in
  April 2021**. It promotes diversification, digital tools and buyer–seller matching. Dialogue
  and matching do not themselves prove that production relocated.
- The Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed rail project links concessional finance, Shinkansen technology
  and training, while retaining delivery and localisation risks.

**European Union**

- The EU–India Trade and Technology Council is a channel for technology, green-transition and
  standards cooperation.
- It widens India's options beyond a binary choice between American and Chinese ecosystems.
- On **27 January 2026**, the European Commission and India **concluded negotiations** on an
  EU–India Free Trade Agreement. The Commission published negotiated texts for information but
  expressly states that they may change during legal revision, become final only on signature and
  become binding only after each party completes internal procedures for entry into force.
- Therefore the precise current description is **negotiations concluded; legal revision and

### SESSION 7 — CORE — How resilient supply chains actually work

#### VISUAL

```text
MAP THE CHAIN
raw material → processing → component → assembly → logistics → market
      ↓             ↓           ↓          ↓          ↓
  identify concentration, chokepoints and non-substitutable nodes
      ↓
REDUCE RISK
supplier diversity + geographic diversity + inventories + standards compatibility
      ↓
BUILD CAPABILITY
domestic production + skills + R&D + finance + infrastructure
      ↓
TEST OUTCOME
continuity under shock · recovery time · cost · quality · strategic control
```

#### EXPLANATION

A supply chain is resilient when it can **anticipate, absorb, recover from and adapt to**
disruption. Diplomacy supports resilience, but resilience is delivered through firms,
infrastructure, finance, standards, skilled labour, logistics and regulatory coordination.

| Layer | Diplomatic instrument | Delivery test |
|---|---|---|
| Critical minerals | Exploration partnerships, processing cooperation and long-term sourcing for inputs such as rare earths, lithium and cobalt | Diversified mine/processing access actually available |
| Semiconductors | Research, fabrication, packaging and workforce cooperation | Operating capacity, reliable inputs and viable scale |
| Clean energy | Manufacturing and supply-chain commitments | Plants, components and procurement—not announcement alone |
| Defence industry | Co-development, co-production, licensing | Transfer depth, local value addition and lifecycle support |
| Digital/AI | Research networks and standards dialogue | Compute, data, talent, trusted hardware and usable access |

Critical minerals and semiconductors are both economic and strategic. Economy owns cost,
production and trade mechanics; IR owns external concentration, technology access, partner choice
and coercion risk.

⚠️ Friend-shoring may reduce geopolitical exposure but can raise cost, create a new concentration
or divide standards. Therefore, “trusted” must not become “single.”

#### EXAMPLE

A semiconductor cooperation announcement passes the **instrument** test. It passes the
**implementation** test only when finance, site, equipment, skills and supply contracts align. It
passes the **outcome** test only when production is reliable and economically usable.

#### EXAM LINK

For the 2024 supply-chain PYQ, separate:

1. why Western economies seek diversification;
2. why India is attractive;
3. which instruments embody that interest;
4. why India is an alternative node, not a complete replacement for China;
5. how strategic autonomy qualifies the partnership.

#### TRAPS

- MoU = factory.
- investment announcement = operating capacity.
- friend-shoring = resilience under all shocks.
- diversification = one-for-one replacement of China.

#### RECAP

Resilience is a measured system property, not a diplomatic slogan.

### SESSION 8 — CORE — BRI as geo-economic statecraft

#### VISUAL

```text
CONNECTIVITY DEFICIT
roads · rail · ports · power · telecom · logistics
                 ↓
CHINESE FINANCE + STATE-LINKED DELIVERY CAPACITY
loans · grants · policy banks · firms · special-purpose vehicles
                 ↓
INFRASTRUCTURE ASSET
        +--------+--------+
        |                 |
        v                 v
 commercial return   strategic externality
 trade/jobs/access   leverage/standards/presence
        \                 /
         v               v
 viability · debt · transparency · sovereignty · local ownership test
```

#### EXPLANATION

⚠️ The **Belt and Road Initiative is not one road, one treaty or one institution**. It is an
umbrella for policy coordination, infrastructure, trade, finance, logistics, digital links and
people-to-people engagement through overland corridors and a Maritime Silk Road.

**Geo-economics** means using economic instruments to pursue or generate strategic effects.
It does **not** mean every commercially financed asset is secretly military.

| Dimension | Possible gain | Strategic/political-economy risk |
|---|---|---|
| Physical connectivity | Lower transport time; fill infrastructure gaps | Route and operator dependence |
| Finance | Capital where alternatives are slow | Foreign-currency debt and opaque liabilities |
| Energy | Generation and transmission | Payment stress, fuel dependence or stranded capacity |
| Ports/logistics | Trade and trans-shipment | Long leases, access influence and dual-use concern |
| Digital links | Connectivity and services | Standards, cyber, data and vendor lock-in |
| Political visibility | Fast, visible delivery | Elite incentives, procurement opacity and backlash |

The correct causal language is conditional: concentrated finance, construction, operation,
standards and diplomatic sponsorship **can** generate strategic leverage. Project status,
contract terms and host-state choices determine whether they do.

#### EXAMPLE

A port's location plus Chinese finance justifies scrutiny. It does not by itself establish
military use. The argument becomes stronger only with evidence of physical capacity, operator
control, recurring security use and host-state consent.

#### EXAM LINK

Open a BRI answer with architecture, not with the phrase “debt trap.” Then separate connectivity
benefits, political economy and strategic externalities.

#### TRAPS

- BRI = treaty organisation.
- Every BRI project = Chinese grant.
- Commercial asset = military base.
- Host state = passive object.

#### RECAP

BRI converts finance and delivery capacity into potential influence; actual strategic effect must
be demonstrated project by project.

### SESSION 9 — CORE — CPEC, sovereignty and differentiated South Asian agency

#### VISUAL

```text
                         SOUTH ASIAN BRI THEATRE
                                      |
       +---------------+--------------+--------------+---------------+
       |               |              |              |               |
    PAKISTAN        SRI LANKA      BANGLADESH       NEPAL         MALDIVES
 CPEC/Gwadar      ports/urban      pragmatic       mountain       visible
 sovereignty +    debt/viability   diversification feasibility    projects +
 two-front risk   + politics       among partners  + transit      fiscal politics
       \               |              |              |               /
        +--------------+--------------+--------------+--------------+
                                      |
                                      v
                PARTNERS HEDGE, BARGAIN AND RETAIN AGENCY
```

#### CPEC: THE CATEGORICAL OBJECTION

✅ CPEC combines transport, energy, Gwadar and related economic-zone projects. India's official
objection is that the corridor passes through territory in the former State of Jammu and Kashmir
under Pakistan's control, thereby impinging on India's sovereignty and territorial integrity.

The defensible formulation is:

> India does not oppose connectivity as such; it opposes connectivity that disregards
> sovereignty, territorial integrity, transparency and local ownership.

Third-country financing or participation cannot cure the sovereignty objection because joining a
project does not determine title to disputed territory.

#### COUNTRY DIFFERENTIATION

| State | Dominant pattern | Mechanism relevant to India |
|---|---|---|
| Pakistan | CPEC energy, transport, Gwadar and economic zones | Sovereignty, China–Pakistan alignment, two-front and Arabian Sea concerns |
| Sri Lanka | Ports, urban, power and transport projects | Debt and viability interact with domestic choices and external shocks |
| Bangladesh | Transport, power and port-related cooperation | Pragmatic diversification, not automatic alignment |
| Nepal | Proposed road, rail, dry-port, energy and communication links | Mountain feasibility, finance and transit bargaining |
| Maldives | Housing, bridge, airport and visible development projects | Small-state fiscal exposure and domestic sovereignty politics |
| Afghanistan | Limited implementation relative to others | Conflict, recognition, sanctions and security dominate feasibility |

⚠️ South Asian states are not chessboard squares. They use external options to bargain, hedge and
diversify. Domestic politics can support, renegotiate, delay or reject a project.

#### EXAM LINK

The 2026 GS-II BRI question requires at least one differentiated country comparison. A generic
“China encircles India” paragraph is not enough.

#### TRAPS

- Every participant is aligned with China.
- CPEC objection = opposition to all connectivity.
- State agency disappears because finance is asymmetric.
- A change of government automatically ends the relationship.

#### RECAP

South Asia is a theatre of competition because infrastructure choices affect access, standards
and bargaining—not because every state has joined a Chinese bloc.

### SESSION 10 — CORE — Debt distress and dual use: two evidence tests

#### VISUAL 1 — DEBT-DISTRESS TEST

```text
PROJECT VIABILITY
      +
FINANCING TERMS
      +
BORROWER CHOICES / GUARANTEES
      +
PRE-EXISTING FISCAL FRAGILITY
      +
EXTERNAL SHOCKS
      +
RESTRUCTURING LEVERAGE ACTUALLY SOUGHT
      ↓
CAUSAL JUDGMENT
```

#### VISUAL 2 — DUAL-USE TEST

```text
physical capability
      + operator control/access
      + recurrent verified security use
      + significance of civilian use
      + host-state law and consent
      ↓
dual-use assessment
```

#### EXPLANATION

“Debt-trap diplomacy” is a claim about **intentional design and strategic extraction**. It cannot
be proved merely by observing debt distress after a project. Test:

1. expected demand and revenue;
2. currency, maturity, interest, grace period and security;
3. borrower procurement and guarantees;
4. pre-existing debt and fiscal structure;
5. exchange-rate, commodity, tourism, conflict and interest-rate shocks;
6. the concession or leverage actually sought during restructuring.

The balanced verdict is:

> Not every BRI project is a deliberate trap; however, opaque, loan-heavy and weakly appraised
> projects can generate asymmetric dependence and bargaining leverage whether or not distress
> was deliberately engineered.

Dual-use analysis is equally evidence-sensitive. Ports, airports, grids, cables and digital
systems may support civilian and security functions. Potential is not proof of militarisation.

#### EXAMPLE

A deep-water port can physically receive naval vessels. To call it a military base, an answer
still needs evidence of control, agreement, recurrent use, deployment or host-state permission.

#### EXAM LINK

In “analyse” answers, present the proposition, causal mechanism, counter-evidence and qualified
verdict. Avoid slogans.

#### TRAPS

- Debt distress = lender intent.
- Long lease = military base.
- Strategic location = verified security use.
- Host-government choice = irrelevant.

#### RECAP

Strong IR analysis distinguishes **risk indicator** from **proved outcome**.

### SESSION 11 — CORE — India's response: competitive public goods, not veto politics

#### VISUAL

```text
CHINESE OFFER / PARTNER DEMAND
              ↓
INDIA'S RESPONSE CHOICE
  oppose rhetoric only ───────────────┐
  OR                                  │ weak influence
  deliver credible alternatives ──────┘
              ↓
consultation + speed + transparency + viability + local capacity
              ↓
regional connectivity + maritime awareness + domestic resilience
```

#### RESPONSE ARCHITECTURE

| Instrument | Strategic function | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood First | Political priority, development partnership and crisis support | Credibility depends on consultation and timely delivery |
| SAGAR | Maritime security, capacity building and regional growth | A declared vision needs resources and implementation |
| MAHASAGAR | Global South vision announced at Port Louis on 12 March 2025: trade for development, capacity building and mutual security, with technology sharing, concessional loans and grants as cooperation modes | A declared vision, not a treaty or quantified funding commitment; the announcement says India is moving ahead with SAGAR while going beyond it |
| BIMSTEC | Bay of Bengal subregional cooperation | Institutional capacity and delivery matter |
| BBIN connectivity | Road/transit integration | Consent and operating procedures determine use |
| Chabahar | Access toward Afghanistan and Central Asia bypassing Pakistan | Sanctions, finance and route viability constrain scale |
| IMEC | Proposed westward multimodal diversification | A memorandum/corridor concept is not an operating corridor |
| Lines of Credit, grants and capacity building | Demand-driven alternatives and institutional ties | Speed, maintenance and outcome evaluation are essential |
| Domestic border, maritime and industrial capacity | Reduces vulnerability independently of partner choices | Requires sustained finance and execution |

The best response is **competitive public goods**:

1. match projects to partner demand;
2. publish finance and implementation status;
3. deliver faster without bypassing fiscal, environmental or local consultation;
4. combine finance with skills, maintenance and market access;
5. link bilateral projects through consensual subregional platforms;
6. protect security through surveillance, cyber standards and domestic capacity rather than
   demanding exclusive alignment.

**MAHASAGAR's exact place:** Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced **“Mutual and Holistic
Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions”** on **12 March 2025** during the
India–Mauritius joint press statement in Port Louis. It is a policy vision for the Global South.
The source says India is “moving ahead with the SAGAR Vision” and, building upon it, going beyond
SAGAR. Therefore MAHASAGAR is neither a repeal of SAGAR nor a legally binding replacement.

#### EXAMPLE

India cannot preserve influence by asking a neighbour to reject all Chinese finance while offering
no timely alternative. It can preserve influence by becoming a reliable, transparent and
consultative partner.

#### EXAM LINK

For BRI questions, the way forward should answer the mechanism: if Chinese influence grows through
finance and delivery, India's reply must improve finance quality and delivery—not merely repeat a
principle.

#### TRAPS

- India can veto a neighbour's sovereign partner choices.
- Every alternative corridor is already operational.
- Grants alone establish durable influence.
- MAHASAGAR is a treaty or a quantified fund.
- Speed and safeguards are mutually exclusive.

#### RECAP

India competes best on **trust, speed, sustainability and partner ownership**.

| Label | Correct use |

6. Identify dependence, mistrust, sanctions, finance, delivery or partner-agency constraints.
7. Offer a calibrated response.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A1. The three-track hedge is a portfolio, not a midpoint

⚠️ Hedging does not mean maintaining equal distance from China, the United States and Russia.
India assigns different partners to different functions:

```text
China  → trade engagement + risk reduction + border management
US     → critical technology + defence enablers + industrial cooperation
Russia → installed-base continuity + defence co-production + energy
Japan/EU → infrastructure + standards + diversification
```

The portfolio fails if one partner gains veto power over India's choices or if switching costs
become prohibitive.

### A2. Technology access has three levels

| Level | What it gives | Why it may be insufficient |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase | Access to a product | External lifecycle dependence remains |
| Licensed production | Some domestic manufacturing | Critical components/design may remain external |
| Co-development / indigenous capability | Skills, design, IP and production ecosystem | Cost, time and absorptive capacity still matter |

⚠️ “Technology transfer” should therefore be disaggregated. A memorandum, assembly line and
indigenous design capability are not equivalent.

### A3. Minilateral balancing without alliance

Quad- or I2U2-linked cooperation may assist technology, infrastructure or supply-chain
diversification. These are **small, issue-specific and non-exclusive arrangements**; their
institutional profiles belong to Topic 10. They do not create a collective-defence obligation.
Coordination costs, differing priorities and voluntary implementation limit delivery.

### A4. The autonomy–dependence equation

```text
external partnership
      ↓
capability gain + access gain
      ↓
possible concentration in finance / technology / spares / standards
      ↓
future switching cost
      ↓
AUTONOMY expands only if domestic and diversified capability grows faster
```

This produces a double-sided judgment:

- Russia can preserve operational capability and simultaneously create spares concentration.
- US technology cooperation can expand capability and simultaneously create access dependence.
- China trade can support production and simultaneously create critical-input vulnerability.

### A5. Stronger BRI causal analysis

The advanced answer avoids both extremes:

- **Too weak:** “BRI is only infrastructure.”
- **Too strong:** “Every BRI project is an intentional military/debt trap.”

Instead, test whether finance, construction, operation, standards and diplomatic sponsorship are
concentrated; identify the host state's choices; then establish the commercial and strategic
effects separately.

### 1. Core thesis

- India manages **five differentiated major-power relationships** through strategic autonomy.

- US = technology/defence convergence + access and policy constraints.

- Japan/EU = infrastructure, markets, standards and diversification.

  evidence, not current data.
- Trade pattern recorded: Indian raw materials/commodities versus Chinese manufactures.
- Boundary claim ≠ LAC perception ≠ patrol arrangement ≠ disengagement ≠ de-escalation ≠
  settlement.
- 21 October 2024: patrolling arrangement; Depsang and Demchok in the canonical record.

### 8. Debt and dual-use tests

### 9. India's response

- Competitive public goods, not veto politics.
- Neighbourhood First, SAGAR, MAHASAGAR, BIMSTEC, BBIN, Chabahar, IMEC, development partnership
  and domestic capacity are distinct instruments.
- MAHASAGAR: announced Port Louis, 12 March 2025; a Global South vision “going beyond SAGAR”
  while moving ahead with SAGAR; not a treaty, replacement or quantified fund.
- Corridor MoU ≠ operating corridor.
- Improve consultation, speed, transparency, viability, maintenance and partner ownership.

### 10. Evidence and classification discipline

- Agreement, initiative, framework and treaty are not synonyms.
- Minilateral ≠ alliance.
- Signature ≠ ratification/entry into force.
- Announcement ≠ implementation ≠ output ≠ measured outcome.
- Sequence ≠ causation.
- Participation ≠ alignment.
- Historical book evidence ≠ current statistic.

### 12. Mains answer spine

1. Define the relationship or supply-chain problem.
2. State India's interest and partner incentive.
3. Separate cooperation from competition.
4. Cite a named instrument with date and exact status.
5. Explain the implementation mechanism.
6. State outcome only to the verified level.
7. Add dependence, mistrust, finance, sanctions, delivery or partner-agency constraint.
8. Offer diversification, localisation, transparent delivery or capability-building.
9. Conclude: autonomy requires options plus the capacity to bear switching costs.

### Qualified conclusion

India's major-power policy is not a choice between China, the West and Russia. It is an attempt
to convert differentiated partnerships into security, technology, energy and production
capability while preventing any one dependence from becoming a veto. Resilient supply chains and
credible regional alternatives are therefore instruments of strategic autonomy—but only when
agreements are implemented, capacities become operational and outcomes are measured.
