### PYQ 1 — 2019 GS-II Q9 · Comment · 10 marks · 150 words

**Audited demand (neutral rendering):** India–Japan contemporary global and strategic partnership.

**Model (approx. 150 words):** India–Japan relations are a strategic partnership because security consultation, industrial cooperation and connectivity reinforce one another, not because the states have a collective-defence treaty. Their 2014 elevation to a *Special Strategic and Global Partnership* provides political direction. The foreign and defence ministers’ 2+2 dialogue, including the August 2024 New Delhi meeting, provides a forum to align security priorities; the September 2020 reciprocal supplies agreement, operational from 2021, makes exercises and humanitarian assistance easier without automatic basing or mutual defence. Beyond security, Japan’s Mumbai–Ahmedabad High-Speed Rail support links finance with technology and training, though construction and localisation remain difficult. With Australia, the April 2021 Supply Chain Resilience Initiative encourages alternative supplier networks; buyer–seller matching alone cannot prove factories have relocated. Japan therefore expands India’s room for maritime cooperation and economic diversification while respecting its strategic autonomy. Its value depends on timely delivery and Indian capacity, not a claim that consultation itself deters every regional threat.

**Why this earns marks:** Evaluates the partnership across differentiated instruments and names delivery limits rather than listing visits.

### PYQ 2 — 2019 GS-II Q20 · Explain with suitable examples · 15 marks · 250 words

**Audited demand (neutral rendering):** Friction in India–US ties and India's place in US global strategy. The instruction-block word limit is 250; the neutral rendering is not asserted to be the printed stem.

**Model (approx. 230 words):** India is important to US strategy as a major Indo-Pacific partner and possible technology and production diversification node, but it is not an alliance subordinate. This combination explains why cooperation and friction coexist. US recognition of India as a Major Defence Partner in 2016 and the LEMOA logistics, COMCASA secure-communications and BECA geospatial agreements progressively improved practical interoperability. They enable cooperation rather than obligate India to fight US wars. The ten-year defence-partnership framework signed in 2025 strengthened the political architecture but did not change that legal distinction.

Technology is another convergence point: iCET, announced in 2022 and reviewed at NSA level in June 2024, targets semiconductors, space, AI and defence industry. Yet Shashi Tharoor's warning about restrictions on US high-technology sales identifies a concrete gap between strategic rhetoric and actual access. India’s Russian-origin installed defence base and energy interests complicate US expectations: the S-400 purchase created possible CAATSA exposure, not an international-law duty imposed on India. More recently, US reciprocal and Russian-oil-linked duties applied in August 2025; the latter was removed from February 2026, while other duties also changed. Such reversible measures show why trade pressure must not be portrayed as permanent alignment failure. Conversely, India gains from cooperation but cannot assume unconditional transfer or surrender freedom of choice. Tharoor’s “tactical partnership” captures the bargain: shared interests support close working relations while differing Russia policies, export controls and US domestic trade instruments preserve real friction. Negotiated access and diversified suppliers make the partnership durable without fictional alliance obligations.

**Why this earns marks:** Distinguishes US strategy from Indian agency; links named enabling instruments, export-control and sanctions exposure, and dated tariff reversal to a qualified verdict.

### PYQ 3 — 2021 GS-II Q10 · Explain · 10 marks · 150 words

**Audited demand (neutral rendering):** The USA facing an existential challenge from China. This is not a quotation of the printed question.

**Model (approx. 150 words):** The “existential” description reflects a perceived systemic challenge to US primacy, not proof that China can imminently destroy the American state. Chinese manufacturing scale makes concentrated supply chains vulnerable to coercion or disruption; technology competition over semiconductors and standards can translate production advantage into strategic leverage. Competing regional security preferences compound those economic concerns. The US therefore looks to partners, including India, to spread sourcing and sustain an open Indo-Pacific. The later iCET process, with its June 2024 NSA-level review of chips, space and AI, illustrates this response; it is a post-question example, not evidence available to a 2021 candidate. India can gain technology and investment, but geography, unresolved boundary tensions and trade dependence mean it cannot simply replace Chinese capacity. Nor do India–US enabling defence agreements make India a formal ally. The better explanation separates US threat perception from India’s independent de-risking: competing powers may share selected interests without holding identical threat assessments or accepting the same obligations.

**Why this earns marks:** Explains the systemic mechanism and identifies the time boundary of later examples instead of asserting a literal existential war.

### PYQ 5 — 2026 GS-II Q20 · Analyse · 15 marks · 250 words

**Printed text as recorded in Basic 03:** “China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has transformed South Asia from a regional space into a theatre of great power competition.” Analyse the strategic implications of the BRI for India's security and regional influence in South Asia.

**Model (approx. 245 words):** BRI converts Chinese finance, construction and infrastructure access into opportunities for regional influence. It also gives South Asian governments options beyond India; that is competition, not a China-aligned bloc. The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor is the sharpest Indian security concern because its route crosses territory in the former State of Jammu and Kashmir under Pakistan’s control, contrary to India’s sovereignty position. Pakistan’s transport, energy and Gwadar projects deepen China–Pakistan coordination, but their operational outcomes require separate evidence.

In Sri Lanka, ports and other projects can yield trade and urban-development gains while raising questions about repayment, contractual control and potential access near India’s maritime approaches. Bangladesh uses infrastructure partners pragmatically; Chinese finance there does not establish military alignment. Nepal’s proposed mountain links and transit diversification raise a different challenge: India’s reliability as a neighbour. Maldives’ fiscal exposure and domestic sovereignty politics magnify the effects of visible infrastructure offers. A port’s possible dual use warrants scrutiny, not the claim that every Chinese-financed asset is a military base. Equally, debt distress reflects domestic borrowing decisions and external shocks as well as opaque terms; “deliberate debt trap” requires proof of intent.

India’s response should defend the CPEC sovereignty principle while competing on delivery and consent: faster development partnerships, BBIN and BIMSTEC links where partners agree, credible maritime capacity under SAGAR, and transparent finance and maintenance. India cannot veto neighbours’ choices; investment quality and trust, rather than automatic claims of encirclement, determine whether its regional influence can be sustained.

**Why this earns marks:** Addresses security and influence separately, differentiates countries and debt/dual-use evidence, and offers an implementable India-centric response.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks · 250 words

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks · 300 words

**Prompt:** Assess India’s simultaneous engagement with the US, Russia and China in the context of supply-chain shocks and border insecurity. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model (approx. 285 words):** Strategic autonomy is an ability to preserve choices, not a pledge of equal intimacy with every major power. India has three different dependencies: China-linked manufactured inputs despite territorial mistrust; US technology opportunities constrained by access rules; and an installed Russian defence base combined with energy complementarity. Treating these as one bloc contest misses the specific remedy for each.
