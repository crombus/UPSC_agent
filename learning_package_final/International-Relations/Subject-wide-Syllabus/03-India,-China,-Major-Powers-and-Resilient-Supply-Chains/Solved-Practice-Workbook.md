## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Q1. Which formulation best captures the organising logic of this topic?

A. India uses differentiated major-power relationships to gain security, technology, energy and
production capacity while limiting dependence on any one partner.
B. India maintains comparable distance from competing powers across issue areas.
C. Supply-chain resilience requires ending trade with strategically difficult partners.
D. A deep defence partnership carries alliance obligations even without a treaty.
**Answer: A**

India's relationships with China, the United States, Russia, Japan and the EU have different
structures. Strategic autonomy permits simultaneous engagement; it does not require equal
distance. De-risking reduces concentration without demanding decoupling, and partnership does not
automatically create collective-defence obligations.

### Q2. Which relationship is most accurately described as economic interdependence combined with strategic mistrust?

A. India–Japan
B. India–China
C. India–EU
D. India–Russia

**Answer: B**

✅ Sikri's book-period assessment separates substantial India–China trade from enduring mistrust
rooted in the unresolved boundary and competition for influence. Japan and the EU are
diversification partners; Russia is framed through defence-energy complementarity.

### Q3. Consider the following statements about the India–China boundary vocabulary.

1. The 25th Special Representatives' talks on 25 August 2026 reached an eight-point consensus.
2. Disengagement necessarily constitutes a final boundary settlement.
3. That consensus recorded Terms-of-Reference work for the expert groups, two additional military
meeting points and two hotline channels, and confirmed the reopening of three border trading points.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: C**

Statements 1 and 3 reproduce the official Chinese MFA record of the Wang Yi–Ajit Doval talks in
Beijing. The first task of the Expert Group on Boundary Delimitation and Working Group on Border
Management was to agree their respective Terms of Reference. Statement 2 is false: the eight
outcomes concern negotiation, border management and cooperation; they do not settle the boundary.

### Q4. Which sequence most accurately moves from a supply-chain vulnerability to a resilient outcome?

A. Friend-shoring → announcement → resilience → concentration mapping
B. Domestic production → complete decoupling → lower cost → resilience
C. New MoU → operating capacity → finance → skills
D. Map concentration → diversify sources → build capacity → stress-test continuity
**Answer: D**

Resilience begins by locating chokepoints and non-substitutable inputs. Diversification and
domestic capability are responses; continuity, recovery time, cost and quality are outcome tests.
A memorandum is not operating capacity, and resilience does not require autarky.

### Q5. De-risking is best understood as:

A. Reducing excessive concentration while continuing engagement.
B. Suspending strategic-competitor trade is the principal de-risking instrument.
C. Domestic relocation of the full production chain defines resilience.
D. Procurement from treaty allies is sufficient for supply-chain resilience.
**Answer: A**

De-risking lowers overdependence through supplier, geographic or technological diversification.
Decoupling implies severance, reshoring concerns domestic relocation, and friend-shoring does not
require a treaty alliance.

### Q6. Which statement correctly distinguishes friend-shoring from ordinary offshoring?

A. Friend-shoring generally reduces cost by narrowing suppliers to trusted partners.
B. Friend-shoring prioritises geopolitical trust alongside cost
C. Friend-shoring is identical to autarky.
D. Friend-shoring proves that production has already relocated.

**Answer: B**

Friend-shoring favours trusted partners for selected critical links. It may raise costs and create
new concentration; it does not mean self-sufficiency or establish relocation without delivery
evidence.

### Q7. Which of the following is the best use of Sikri's book-period trade evidence on China?

A. To establish China's current rank in India's trade without new data.
B. To prove that economic engagement eliminates boundary tension.
C. To show a historical raw-material/manufactures asymmetry that can reinforce strategic mistrust.
D. To establish that the 2007-era deficit is the present deficit.

**Answer: C**

The durable inference is structural: the recorded pattern placed commodity exports against
manufactured imports and added an economic grievance to strategic mistrust. Historical ranking
and deficit figures cannot be presented as current.

### Q8. A candidate says, “India must replace China completely in global supply chains.” Which correction is strongest?

A. China should instead be treated as an ally.
B. Supply-chain resilience is analytically separate from foreign policy.
C. One new agreement already proves replacement.
D. India is one node; de-risking avoids requiring complete substitution.
**Answer: D**

China's manufacturing scale and embedded input networks make immediate substitution implausible.
The defensible claim is diversification of dependence through alternative suppliers and domestic
capacity.

### Q9. Which statement about iCET is correct?

A. An India–US cooperation initiative, not a treaty or defence alliance.
B. It is a treaty-based commitment among the four Quad members.
C. It entered into force after parliamentary ratification.
D. It guarantees technology transfer across each covered sector.
**Answer: A**

✅ iCET was announced in May 2022, held its inaugural NSA-level meeting on 31 January 2023 and a
second review on 17 June 2024. Its coverage includes semiconductors, AI, space and
defence-industrial cooperation. None of that creates a treaty or guaranteed transfer.

### Q10. Match the India–US enabling agreement with its principal function.

| Agreement | Function |
|---|---|
| 1. LEMOA | i. Secure communications |
| 2. COMCASA | ii. Geospatial-information exchange |
| 3. BECA | iii. Reciprocal logistics support |

A. 1-i, 2-iii, 3-ii
B. 1-iii, 2-i, 3-ii
C. 1-ii, 2-i, 3-iii
D. 1-iii, 2-ii, 3-i

**Answer: B**

LEMOA (2016) concerns logistics, COMCASA (2018) secure communications and BECA (2020)
geospatial information. Together they enable cooperation; they do not create collective defence
or automatic basing.

### Q11. Which statement correctly classifies the 2025 India–US architecture?

A. Mission 500 records an achieved US$500 billion trade outcome.
B. TRUST legally terminated iCET.
C. Initiatives COMPACT/TRUST differ from the ten-year defence framework; none is an alliance.
D. The February 2025 BTA announcement meant the agreement entered into force that day.

**Answer: C**

The 13 February 2025 joint statement launched COMPACT and TRUST and set Mission 500 as a goal
while announcing BTA negotiations. The 31 October instrument is a framework. None is an achieved
trade outcome or collective-defence treaty, and the official text does not say TRUST terminated
iCET.

### Q12. Which development most directly proves that deep strategic cooperation and coercive or competitive trade measures can coexist?

A. The 2016 Major Defence Partner designation alone
B. The launch of SCRI
C. The 2014 India–Japan partnership upgrade
D. The tariff cycle alongside COMPACT, TRUST and defence cooperation
**Answer: D**

EO 14326 imposed an additional 25% from **7 August 2025**; EO 14329 added an oil-linked 25% from
**27 August 2025**. EO 14384 removed the latter from 7 February 2026 and EO 14389 directed
termination of specified IEEPA duties. A separate USTR Section 301 action applied 10%, subject to
exemptions, from 24 July 2026. “Fostering” was therefore conditional.

### Q13. India–Russia relations are described as defence-energy complementarity because:

A. Complementary Indian needs and Russian energy-defence capacity.
B. Russia and India have a collective-defence treaty.
C. Russian systems constitute India's complete defence inventory.
D. complementarity eliminates sanctions and logistics risks.

**Answer: A**

✅ Sikri's formulation pairs energy-deficient India with energy-surplus Russia and notes access to
specialised defence equipment. Complementarity is an overall structure; it does not erase
installed-base dependence or external pressure.

### Q14. Which is the most precise description of India's Russia-related defence vulnerability?

A. Historical friendship
B. Installed-base, maintenance and spares concentration
C. Membership of a Russian military alliance
D. Absence of any domestic production

**Answer: B**

Existing platforms require spares, repair, upgrades and ammunition. This creates switching costs
even while licensed production and joint ventures build capacity. The vulnerability is narrower
and more precise than a claim of total dependence.

### Q15. CAATSA exposure arising from the S-400 purchase is best classified as:

A. a binding UN Security Council obligation on India.
B. an obligation under an India–US mutual-defence treaty.
C. Sanctions pressure, not an international-law obligation for India.
D. evidence that the S-400 procurement did not reach contractual status.
**Answer: C**

Third-party domestic sanctions can affect finance, suppliers and diplomacy. The master framework
requires separating such exposure from a treaty or international-law duty.

### Q16. Which statement correctly treats the 23rd India–Russia Annual Summit?

A. It placed each Programme 2030 objective into immediate operation.
B. It created an India–Russia collective-defence treaty.
C. It proves a current S-400 delivery figure.
D. The summit adopted Programme 2030, but did not prove implementation.
**Answer: D**

The official 5 December statement records the summit title and adoption of the **Programme for
the Development of Strategic Areas of India–Russia Economic Cooperation till 2030**. Status
discipline stops at adoption unless later delivery evidence is supplied.

### Q17. India–Japan relations were elevated in 2014 to:

A. Special Strategic and Global Partnership.
B. a collective-defence alliance.
C. an economic and monetary union.
D. a customs union.

**Answer: A**

The named status is a strategic partnership. The relationship combines security,
infrastructure and technology, but the title does not create collective defence.

### Q18. The India–Japan reciprocal supplies-and-services agreement is significant mainly because it:

A. settles maritime boundaries.
B. enables logistics cooperation without a defence guarantee.
C. establishes a common external tariff.
D. replaces the need for domestic military logistics.

**Answer: B**

Signed in September 2020 and operational in 2021, the agreement supports logistics and
interoperability. Access arrangements and alliance commitments are distinct.

### Q19. Consider the following statements about the Supply Chain Resilience Initiative (SCRI).

1. It was launched by Australia, India and Japan in April 2021.
2. It promotes diversification, digital tools and buyer–seller matching.
3. Its launch by itself proves that manufacturing shifted out of China.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 2 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: C**

Statements 1 and 2 are source-grounded. Statement 3 confuses an initiative and facilitation
mechanism with measured production relocation.

### Q20. Which statement correctly describes the EU–India FTA status verified on 26 September 2026?

A. The FTA entered into force on 27 January 2026.
B. The TTC supplied the ratification required for the FTA.
C. Negotiations were still unopened.
D. Concluded negotiations await signature and domestic procedures.
**Answer: D**

The European Commission's disclaimer distinguishes conclusion of negotiations from final text,
signature, binding force and entry into force. “Negotiations concluded” is therefore not
“operative treaty”; the FTA is **not in force**.

### Q21. The Belt and Road Initiative is best described as:

A. a multi-sector cooperation umbrella rather than one project.
B. a treaty-based mutual-defence organisation coordinating member forces.
C. a single concessional-grant programme for one transnational corridor.
D. an international court resolving cross-border infrastructure disputes.
**Answer: A**

BRI combines multiple instruments and corridors. Legal form, financing terms, ownership and
implementation vary project by project.

### Q22. India's categorical objection to CPEC rests principally on:

A. opposition to cross-border connectivity projects as a category.
B. territorial integrity over Pakistan-controlled territory.
C. the absence of any energy project.
D. China's non-membership of SAARC.

**Answer: B**

The objection is route- and sovereignty-specific. The canonical correction is that India opposes
connectivity that disregards sovereignty, transparency, viability and local ownership—not
connectivity as such.

### Q23. Which statement best incorporates South Asian state agency?

A. Chinese project finance places recipients within a common strategic bloc.
B. Domestic politics does not affect externally financed projects.
C. States may hedge, bargain, diversify partners and renegotiate projects.
D. Infrastructure finance determines territorial title.

**Answer: C**

Partners have preferences, elite incentives, fiscal constraints and electoral politics. Their
choices mediate external influence; finance does not erase sovereignty or agency.

### Q24. Which conclusion follows from the six-variable debt-distress test?

A. Any repayment problem proves deliberate lender entrapment.
B. External shocks are irrelevant.
C. Borrower choices should be excluded.
D. Unproven intent can coexist with project-induced dependence.
**Answer: D**

Project viability, financing terms, borrower choices, prior fragility, external shocks and actual
restructuring leverage must all be examined. The balanced conclusion rejects both universal-trap
and no-risk claims.

### Q25. Which evidence would most strengthen a claim that a commercially used port has acquired a military function?

A. Verified recurrent security use combined with access/control arrangements.
B. Its location near a sea lane alone.
C. The nationality of the construction company alone.
D. A newspaper description of “strategic location” alone.

**Answer: A**

Dual-use assessment requires physical capability, operator control, recurrent use, civilian
significance and host-state consent. Location and finance are risk indicators, not proof.

### Q26. Which country–mechanism pair is correctly matched in the canonical South Asia matrix?

A. Bangladesh — automatic alliance after project acceptance
B. Pakistan — CPEC sovereignty and two-front implications for India
C. Nepal — mature maritime-base network
D. Afghanistan — the region's most mature BRI project theatre

**Answer: B**

Pakistan is the distinctive CPEC case. Bangladesh is described through pragmatic diversification,
Nepal through mountain feasibility and transit bargaining, and Afghanistan through limited
implementation constrained by conflict and recognition issues.

### Q27. The strongest analytical caution in using Sri Lanka as a debt case is that:

A. port and infrastructure projects are strategically neutral commercial assets.
B. lender intent is sufficient to establish debt-trap diplomacy.
C. multiple causes beyond financing terms.
D. debt restructuring removes the possibility of strategic leverage.
**Answer: C**

A monocausal narrative is inadequate. The case can still reveal sovereignty and leverage risks,
but causal attribution must include borrower and external variables.

### Q28. Which statement about Bangladesh, Nepal and Maldives is least defensible?

A. Each has its own domestic political economy.
B. Each can use external partners to widen bargaining options.
C. Project acceptance need not equal defence alignment.
D. Their choices follow India–China rivalry alone, irrespective of local demand.
**Answer: D**

Treating neighbours as passive objects is the precise error the country matrix corrects. Local
demand, fiscal capacity, electoral politics and project viability matter.

### Q29. Which response best matches “competitive public goods”?

A. Offer viable, transparent finance plus delivery, skills and maintenance.
B. Make acceptance of Indian finance the condition for project cooperation.
C. Classify Chinese-financed ports as military facilities at the proposal stage.
D. Use diplomatic objections in place of competitive project delivery.
**Answer: A**

India's influence depends on credible alternatives and timely implementation. Exclusivity demands
can undermine partner agency and trust.

### Q30. Which pairing is correctly classified?

A. IMEC — operating corridor with complete end-to-end freight service
B. MAHASAGAR: a vision, neither treaty nor SAGAR replacement
C. SAGAR — binding maritime-security treaty with enforcement provisions
D. BIMSTEC — collective-defence alliance carrying military commitments
**Answer: B**

MAHASAGAR was announced at Port Louis as “Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth
Across Regions.” The same official statement says India is moving ahead with SAGAR while going
beyond it. IMEC is a corridor concept/MoU, SAGAR is a vision and BIMSTEC is not a military alliance.

### Q31. Which statement correctly separates instrument from outcome?

A. A signed memorandum proves resilience.
B. A summit declaration proves production relocation.
C. An agenda, not proof of implementation.
D. A dialogue round proves dispute settlement.

**Answer: C**

Status discipline prevents announcement inflation. Initiatives, dialogues, frameworks and
agreements can be important inputs without establishing delivery or measured effects.

### Q32. Chronological sequence supports a causal inference only when:

A. the later event is politically desirable.
B. both events involve the same countries.
C. a joint statement uses ambitious language.
D. only after testing mechanisms, counterfactuals and alternative explanations.
**Answer: D**

“After” does not mean “because of.” Chronology supports—rather than by itself establishes—a
causal inference only when a mechanism, counterfactual and alternative explanations are assessed.

### Q33. Which is the decisive distinction between a minilateral and an alliance?

A. Minilaterals vary; alliances entail security commitments.
B. Minilateral cooperation is defined by confidential decision-making.
C. Alliance status arises from sustained economic cooperation.
D. Membership size alone determines collective-defence obligations.

**Answer: A**

Quad and I2U2 are non-treaty, issue-specific arrangements. Small membership does not create an
alliance; obligations depend on the instrument.

### PART 2 — DIRECTLY OWNED MAINS PYQS

#### PYQ-3 · 2019 · GS-II · Question 9 · 10 marks · 150 words

> “‘The time has come for India and Japan to build a strong contemporary relationship, one
> involving global and strategic partnership that will have a great significance for Asia and
> the world as a whole.’ Comment.”

**Demand decoding:** “Comment” requires a reasoned position: explain the partnership's regional
and global significance, then qualify its limits.

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

India–Japan relations are strategically significant because they combine security, infrastructure,
technology and support for a plural Asian order. The relationship's elevation to a **Special
Strategic and Global Partnership in 2014** recognised this wider role. Security cooperation and
the reciprocal supplies-and-services agreement improve practical interoperability without forming
a collective-defence alliance. Economically, Japanese finance, Shinkansen technology and training
in the Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed rail project connect strategic trust with long-term
infrastructure. The **Australia–India–Japan Supply Chain Resilience Initiative, launched in April
2021**, adds diversification and trusted production networks.

The partnership also gives both states alternatives amid concentrated Asian power and supports
rules-based maritime access. However, it cannot substitute for India's own capability: project
delays, regulatory differences and Japan's security constraints limit delivery. Thus, the
relationship matters not as an anti-China bloc, but as a non-alliance partnership that widens
India's strategic and technological options.

**Why this earns marks:** direct thesis; four dimensions; named status and SCRI evidence; clear
non-alliance qualification and reasoned verdict.

#### PYQ-4 · 2019 · GS-II · Question 20 · 15 marks · 250 words

> “‘What introduces friction into the ties between India and the United States is that Washington
> is still unable to find for India a position in its global strategy, which would satisfy India's
> national self-esteem and ambitions.’ Explain with suitable examples.”

**Demand decoding:** explain both convergence and friction, connect examples to India's demand for
autonomy and status, and avoid portraying the relationship as either alliance or hostility.

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

India–US ties deepen when interests converge, but friction appears when Washington expects
alignment while India seeks recognition as an autonomous pole. This gap between partnership and
hierarchy explains the proposition.

**Convergence is real.** India became a Major Defence Partner in 2016; LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA
enable logistics, secure communications and geospatial exchange. iCET extends cooperation to
semiconductors, AI, space and defence industry. On 13 February 2025, the leaders launched
**COMPACT** and **TRUST**, set **Mission 500** as a US$500-billion-by-2030 trade goal and announced
BTA negotiations. A ten-year Major Defense Partnership **framework** was signed on
31 October 2025.

**Yet autonomy and policy volatility create friction.** These initiatives and the framework are
not collective-defence treaties, and the official text does not say TRUST terminated iCET.
India's Russian installed base cannot be discarded instantly. Moreover, EO 14326 and EO 14329
imposed additional duties in August 2025; later executive orders altered them, while a separate
10% Section 301 action applied from 24 July 2026. Thus technology partnership and trade coercion
can coexist.

India expects capability, predictable access and respect for diversified ties—not merely a role
against China. The durable formula is co-development and rule-based trade with recognition of
India as an autonomous contributor, not a subordinate ally.

**Why this earns marks:** explains the mechanism behind friction; uses six named instruments or
examples; balances convergence and autonomy; closes with a graded solution.

#### PYQ-5 · 2021 · GS-II · Question 10 · 10 marks · 150 words

> “‘The USA is facing an existential threat in the form of a China, that is much more challenging
> than the erstwhile Soviet Union.’ Explain.”

**Demand decoding:** explain why China presents a wider challenge than the Soviet Union, but
qualify the word “existential” and avoid a simplistic historical equivalence.

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

China challenges the United States across more domains than the Soviet Union did. It combines
military modernisation with deep participation in global trade, manufacturing and technology
networks. Competition therefore spans semiconductors, artificial intelligence, standards,
critical minerals and supply chains alongside deterrence and regional security. BRI-linked
finance can also convert economic capacity into diplomatic influence.

The challenge is especially complex because economic interdependence raises the cost of
containment: allies and firms may support de-risking while resisting complete decoupling. Unlike
the more separated Cold-War blocs, today's competition operates inside shared markets and
institutions.

However, “existential” is contestable. The United States retains alliances, innovation and
financial power, while China faces constraints; strategic competition does not automatically put
state survival at stake. China is a broader, embedded challenger, but outcomes depend on
technology, alliances, domestic capacity and crisis management—not a predetermined Cold-War
replay.

**Why this earns marks:** comparison is mechanism-based; economic and strategic dimensions are
integrated; “existential” is explicitly qualified.

#### PYQ-6 · 2024 · GS-II · Question 9 · 10 marks · 150 words

> “‘The West is fostering India as an alternative to reduce dependence on China's supply chain and
> as a strategically to counter China's political and econimic dominance.’ Explain this statement
> with examples.”

**Text note:** reproduced from the local official English text with its apparent grammatical and
spelling defects; no missing noun is reconstructed.

**Demand decoding:** explain Western economic and strategic motives, show why India is attractive,
give examples, and qualify “alternative” and “counter.”

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

Western interest in India has economic and strategic roots. Concentrated Chinese production
creates disruption and coercion risks; India offers market scale, skills and a potential
technology base. iCET links India and the US in semiconductors, AI, space and defence industry.
On 13 February 2025, **TRUST** added critical-technology and resilient-supply-chain cooperation,
while **COMPACT** and **Mission 500** widened defence and trade ambition. The ten-year defence
**framework** signed on 31 October 2025 institutionalised cooperation without creating an
alliance. SCRI provides another diversification channel.

However, India is an alternative node, not a complete replacement for China. It pursues
de-risking, retains China trade and Russia ties, and protects strategic autonomy. The 2025–26 US
tariff cycle—duties imposed, later altered, followed by a separate July 2026 Section 301
measure—also proves that “fostering” is conditional. Thus Western support is interest-based
convergence, not unconditional patronage or Indian bloc entry.

**Why this earns marks:** explains both motives; uses three dated instruments; rejects replacement
and alliance exaggeration; retains Indian agency.

#### PYQ-7 · 2026 · GS-II · Question 20 · 15 marks · 250 words

> “‘China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has transformed South Asia from a regional space into a
> theatre of great power competition.’ Analyse the strategic implications of the BRI for India's
> security and regional influence in South Asia.”

**Demand decoding:** “Analyse” requires causal architecture, differentiated country effects,
security and influence consequences, counter-arguments and a calibrated response.

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

BRI turns infrastructure finance into potential geo-economic influence by combining capital,
state-linked delivery, logistics, standards and diplomatic sponsorship. In South Asia, this widens
neighbours' choices and makes connectivity a field of India–China competition, though it does not
create a uniform Chinese bloc.

**Security implications are substantial.** CPEC crosses territory in the former State of Jammu
and Kashmir under Pakistan's control, directly engaging India's sovereignty and linking
China–Pakistan continental cooperation with Gwadar and Arabian Sea access. Ports, airports,
telecom and grids may have dual-use potential, creating surveillance, logistics, cyber and
standards risks. Yet strategic location or Chinese finance alone is not proof of militarisation;
operator control, recurrent security use and host-state consent must be shown.

**Regional influence changes through partner agency.** Pakistan presents alignment and two-front
concerns. Sri Lanka illustrates how project viability, domestic borrowing choices and external
shocks interact with debt and sovereignty sensitivities. Bangladesh diversifies pragmatically;
Nepal uses transit and mountain-connectivity options; Maldives combines visible projects with
small-state fiscal and domestic-political constraints. These states bargain rather than merely
choose camps.

India therefore loses influence where its projects are slow or appear conditional, but competition
can improve Indian delivery. The response should combine Neighbourhood First, BIMSTEC and BBIN
connectivity, Chabahar and proposed IMEC options, transparent development finance, capacity
building, maritime awareness and domestic border/industrial capability.

BRI creates real strategic risks, but a universal “debt-trap” or “base” narrative is inadequate.
India must compete through trust, speed, viability and partner ownership while defending the
specific CPEC sovereignty objection.

**Why this earns marks:** architecture, CPEC, five-country differentiation, debt/dual-use
qualification, security and influence effects, and an instrument-linked response are all covered.

#### PYQ-8 (application) · 2020 · GS-II · Question 20 · 15 marks · 250 words

> **Neutral rendering from the official local paper:** What is the significance of Indo-US defence
> deals over Indo-Russian defence deals? Discuss with reference to stability in the Indo-Pacific
> region.

**Primary owner:** Topic 04 — Indo-Pacific, Indian Ocean and Maritime Security.
**Topic 03 contribution:** India–US enabling agreements, Russian installed-base
complementarity, dependence and strategic autonomy.

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

India–US and India–Russia defence ties serve different functions; their significance for the
Indo-Pacific should be assessed through capability and autonomy rather than a zero-sum choice.

India–US agreements—LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA—improve logistics, secure communications and
geospatial awareness. Exercises, intelligence-enabling cooperation and defence-industrial links
can strengthen maritime domain awareness, interoperability and deterrence in the Indo-Pacific.
They also connect India to advanced technology networks.

India–Russia deals serve continuity. The Russian-origin installed base, Su-30MKI production,
BrahMos joint venture and the 2018 S-400 contract support air defence and operational readiness.
Abrupt substitution would create spares, training and maintenance gaps. Russia's contribution is
therefore not confined to the maritime theatre; it underwrites capabilities India also employs
there.

The comparison has limits. US cooperation is not a collective-defence alliance or automatic
basing arrangement. Russian concentration creates dependence and sanctions exposure, while US
technology access remains conditional. Stability also depends on ASEAN agency, crisis management,
international law and India's own maritime capacity—not on procurement alone.

India should diversify suppliers, deepen co-development and indigenous lifecycle support, and use
each partnership for a defined capability. Indo-Pacific stability is best served when external
deals increase India's independent deterrence and public-good capacity without allowing either
partner a veto over Indian choices.

**Why this earns marks:** compares rather than ranks; links instruments to regional effects;
preserves the Topic 04 maritime owner and adds Topic 03's autonomy/dependence analysis.

#### PYQ-9 (application) · 2023 · GS-II · Question 19 · 15 marks · 250 words

> “‘The expansion and strengthening of NATO and a stronger US-Europe strategic partnership works
> well for India.’ What is your opinion about this statement? Give reasons and examples to support
> your answer.”

**Primary owner:** Topic 10 — Regional, Global and Minilateral Groupings.
**Topic 03 contribution:** consequences for India's US, EU and Russia relationships.

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

A stronger US–Europe partnership can benefit India, but NATO expansion does not work
unambiguously in India's favour.

On the positive side, transatlantic coordination can support stable technology, investment and
standards partnerships. India–US iCET cooperation and the EU–India Trade and Technology Council
offer channels for semiconductors, digital rules and green technologies. A capable Europe can
share security burdens and widen India's options beyond dependence on any single major power.

However, NATO is a treaty alliance centred on Euro-Atlantic collective defence; it should not be
equated with the non-treaty Quad or treated as India's security platform. Expansion can intensify
Russia–West confrontation. That creates sanctions, payment, energy and defence-spares pressures
for India because Russian-origin systems remain embedded in its inventory. It may also divert
Western attention and resources from the Indo-Pacific or demand sharper political alignment from
New Delhi.

India therefore benefits from a cohesive but strategically open transatlantic partnership, not
from uncontrolled bloc confrontation. It should deepen technology and economic ties with the
United States and EU, preserve necessary Russia channels, diversify defence supply, and resist
automatic bloc discipline.

Thus, stronger US–Europe cooperation works for India when it expands public goods, technology and
strategic options; it works against India when alliance expansion hardens rivalry and narrows
India's autonomous room for manoeuvre.

**Why this earns marks:** gives a qualified opinion; distinguishes NATO from minilaterals; links
benefits and costs to India's three-track major-power strategy.

#### PYQ-10 (application) · 2025 · GS-II · Question 10 · 10 marks · 150 words

> “‘With the waning of globalization, post-Cold War world is becoming a site of sovereign
> nationalism.’ Elucidate.”

**Primary owner:** Topic 11 — Globalisation, Trade Agreements and External-Policy Effects.
**Topic 03 contribution:** de-risking, technology controls and resilient supply chains as
major-power applications.

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

The post-Cold-War expectation of steadily integrating markets is being qualified by states'
renewed use of economic policy for security and autonomy. Supply chains once organised mainly for
cost are increasingly judged through concentration, trusted partners, critical minerals,
semiconductors, data and defence technology. De-risking and friend-shoring therefore place
sovereign resilience alongside efficiency.

Major-power competition accelerates this shift. The US seeks diversified technology networks;
China combines manufacturing scale with geo-economic instruments; India uses iCET, TRUST and
SCRI to widen options without decoupling. The 2025–26 US tariff sequence and July 2026
Section 301 action show how national law reshapes commerce even within a strategic partnership.

Yet trade, finance, technology and energy remain interdependent, making autarky costly. The order
is better described as **selective globalisation**: states retain exchange while screening
strategic sectors and building redundancy. Sovereign nationalism modifies globalisation; it does
not abolish cross-border dependence.

**Why this earns marks:** elucidates the mechanism, uses precise concepts and examples, and avoids
the false binary of globalisation versus autarky.

### PART 4 — ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE WITH MODEL ANSWERS

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish de-risking from decoupling. Why is the distinction central to India's
economic engagement with China? *(Answer in 150 words.)*

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

**De-risking** reduces excessive concentration in a supplier, route or technology while retaining
economic engagement; **decoupling** severs or sharply unwinds that engagement. The distinction
matters because India–China relations combine indispensable commercial links with strategic
mistrust.

Sikri's book-period evidence records a trade pattern of Indian raw-material and commodity exports
against Chinese manufactured imports, showing structural asymmetry. Electronics, active
pharmaceutical ingredients and other intermediates cannot be replaced immediately. Full
decoupling would therefore impose production and consumer costs, while unchanged dependence would
leave disruption and coercion risks.

India's rational course is diversified sourcing, domestic processing and manufacturing,
inventories, standards partnerships and trusted external suppliers through instruments such as
SCRI and technology cooperation. Progress must be measured by concentration, substitutability and
recovery capacity, not by announcements.

Thus, de-risking preserves beneficial exchange while lowering strategic vulnerability; it is
compatible with strategic autonomy, whereas abrupt decoupling may reduce both growth and policy
room.

**Why this earns marks:** clear distinction, historical evidence used with a date caveat,
mechanism, policy tools and a reasoned conclusion.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** India–US critical-technology cooperation is deep but not alliance-bound. Explain
with reference to iCET and the defence-enabling agreements. *(Answer in 150 words.)*

**Model answer (within 150 words):**

India–US cooperation is deep because it joins technology, defence and supply-chain interests, but
its instruments do not create collective defence. LEMOA (2016), COMCASA (2018) and BECA (2020)
enable logistics, secure communications and geospatial exchange. They improve interoperability
without granting automatic bases or requiring India to fight for the United States.

iCET, announced in May 2022, inaugurated at NSA level on 31 January 2023 and reviewed again on
17 June 2024, covers semiconductors, artificial intelligence, space and defence-industrial
cooperation. It is an initiative, not a treaty. Its value depends on whether dialogue produces
co-development, manufacturing, skills and usable technology access.

TRUST and COMPACT, launched on 13 February 2025, broadened critical-technology and
defence-commerce cooperation, while the 31 October 2025 instrument remained a defence
**framework**, not an alliance. Tharoor's “tactical partnership” description is therefore apt.
The partnership strengthens autonomy when it builds capability, not a new concentrated dependence.

**Why this earns marks:** exact instruments and dates, correct legal character, named friction and
an autonomy-based verdict.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** India's relationship with Russia is simultaneously complementary and dependence-
creating. Analyse this proposition. *(Answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

India–Russia relations are complementary at the strategic level but contain dependence at the
operational level.

**Complementarity** arises because, as Sikri notes, energy-deficient India engages an
energy-surplus Russia, while Russia has supplied specialised defence equipment, including
nuclear-powered submarines, unavailable from other sources in the book's period. Su-30MKI
licensed production, the BrahMos joint venture and the AK-203 venture show that the relationship
includes co-production, not only purchase. The 2018 S-400 contract adds high-end air-defence
capacity.

**Dependence**, however, follows from the installed base. Platforms require Russian-origin spares,
maintenance, upgrades and ammunition. This creates long switching periods and exposes readiness
to logistics, payment and geopolitical disruption. The S-400 case also creates possible CAATSA
pressure under US domestic law, though such exposure is not an international-law obligation on
India.

The Russia–Ukraine conflict sharpened the trade-off: India had to combine sovereignty and
dialogue language with energy, fertiliser and defence-spares interests, while managing sanctions
and reputational costs.

The 23rd Annual Summit on 4–5 December 2025 and adoption of the bilateral economic-cooperation
Programme till 2030 demonstrate continuity, but adoption is not implementation. India should
localise maintenance and components, diversify future procurement and deepen co-development.
The relationship is sustainable when complementarity does not become a Russian veto.

**Why this earns marks:** distinguishes levels, gives five named examples, analyses Ukraine and
sanctions, and offers an instrument-linked solution.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the contribution of Japan and the European Union to India's resilient
supply-chain strategy. *(Answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer (within 250 words):**

Japan and the European Union broaden India's supply-chain options beyond a narrow US–China frame
by combining capital, technology, standards and market access.

**Japan provides a security-infrastructure-technology pillar.** The relationship became a Special
Strategic and Global Partnership in 2014. Its reciprocal supplies-and-services agreement, signed
in September 2020 and operational in 2021, enables logistics without collective defence. The
Australia–India–Japan Supply Chain Resilience Initiative, launched in April 2021, promotes
diversification, digital tools and buyer–seller matching. Japanese finance, Shinkansen technology
and training in the Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed rail project illustrate long-term infrastructure
and capability cooperation.

**The EU provides a market-regulatory-technology pillar.** The EU–India Trade and Technology
Council offers a channel for digital, green-transition and standards cooperation. It can help
India diversify technology sources and engage in rule-shaping rather than accept standards made
elsewhere. FTA negotiations concluded on 27 January 2026, but the published texts remain subject
to legal revision and become binding only after signature and internal entry-into-force procedures.

The contribution has limits. SCRI facilitation does not prove relocated production; logistics
access is not an alliance; infrastructure faces delivery risks; and concluded negotiations are
not an operative FTA. Neither partner can substitute for domestic skills, manufacturing and logistics.

India should use both relationships to build interoperable standards, co-development and
multiple suppliers while measuring actual capacity and continuity under shock. Their strategic
value lies in expanding options, not in replacing one external dependence with another.

**Why this earns marks:** compares distinct partner functions, uses dated evidence, identifies
delivery limits and ties the assessment to resilience.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** “India's major-power strategy is a three-track hedge rather than a binary choice.”
Critically examine with reference to China, the United States and Russia. *(Answer in 300 words.)*

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

India's major-power policy is a three-track hedge because it pursues different interests through
China, the United States and Russia without accepting exclusive bloc discipline. Hedging here is
active portfolio management, not equal distance.

**China: engage and de-risk.** Trade and critical inputs coexist with an unresolved boundary and
strategic mistrust. Sikri's book-period raw-material/manufactures asymmetry shows vulnerability.
The 25th SR talks on 25 August 2026 recorded an eight-point consensus, Terms-of-Reference work,
two additional military meeting points and hotlines, and three trading points reopened—process,
not settlement.

**United States: deepen capability without alliance.** LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA improve logistics,
communications and geospatial awareness. iCET extends cooperation to semiconductors, AI, space and
defence industry; TRUST/COMPACT and the October 2025 defence framework deepen it. Yet the
2025–26 tariff cycle shows that convergence remains conditional and non-alliance-based.

**Russia: preserve continuity while reducing concentration.** Energy complementarity,
Su-30MKI/BrahMos cooperation and the S-400 contract support capability, while the installed base
creates spares, maintenance and sanctions exposure. The December 2025 summit and Programme 2030
show continuity, not complete implementation. Abrupt substitution is costly.

The hedge has three weaknesses. First, each track can generate a new dependence. Second,
partner rivalries may force costly choices. Third, diversified diplomacy without domestic
industrial and technological capacity may produce only symbolic autonomy.

India should localise lifecycle support, diversify suppliers and energy routes, insist on
co-development, build semiconductor and critical-mineral capacity, and keep commitments
issue-specific and reversible.

Thus the three-track strategy is rational, but not automatically sustainable. Strategic autonomy
expands only when the capabilities obtained from all three relationships grow faster than the
switching costs and veto risks they create.

**Why this earns marks:** three balanced relationship sections; named instruments; genuine
criticism; capability–dependence test; precise conclusion.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** BRI's strategic effect in South Asia depends as much on host-state agency and project
political economy as on Chinese power. Discuss and suggest an effective Indian response.
*(Answer in 300 words.)*

**Model answer (within 300 words):**

BRI can convert Chinese finance, construction capacity, standards and diplomatic sponsorship into
strategic influence, but host states and project economics determine the extent and direction of
that influence.

**Chinese power matters.** Integrated finance and delivery can fill roads, ports, power and
digital deficits quickly. Concentrated financing, construction and operation may create standards
dependence, access influence and bargaining leverage. For India, CPEC is categorically different
because its route through territory in the former State of Jammu and Kashmir under Pakistan's
control impinges on sovereignty and links China–Pakistan cooperation with Gwadar and two-front
concerns.

**Yet agency differentiates outcomes.** Pakistan's strategic alignment is not a template for all
neighbours. Sri Lankan outcomes reflect project viability, domestic borrowing decisions, prior
fragility and external shocks. Bangladesh pragmatically diversifies partners; Nepal weighs
mountain feasibility and transit options; Maldives combines visible infrastructure demand with
small-state fiscal and domestic-political constraints. Governments may renegotiate, delay or
rebrand projects.

Debt and dual-use claims therefore need tests. Intentional debt-trap diplomacy requires evidence
of financing design and concessions actually sought. A port's military potential requires
physical capability, operator control, recurrent security use and host-state consent; location
alone is insufficient.

India should compete through public goods: consult on partner demand, publish terms, improve
delivery speed, combine finance with training and maintenance, and support BIMSTEC/BBIN,
Chabahar, maritime awareness and viable corridor alternatives. Domestic border, cyber, port and
industrial capacity should reduce vulnerability regardless of neighbour choices.

India cannot preserve influence through veto rhetoric. It must defend the specific CPEC
sovereignty objection while becoming the region's most reliable, transparent and locally owned
development partner.

**Why this earns marks:** integrates Chinese capability, five-country agency, debt and dual-use
tests, CPEC doctrine and a mechanism-matched Indian response.
