## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

> Forty questions, ordered to follow the session spine: scope → interest and determinants →
> constitution and machinery → non-alignment → Panchsheel → strategic autonomy → multi-alignment →
> hedging → principles and interests → instruments → evidence discipline → the trade-off → dated
> anchors → advanced traps → previous-year routing. Forms are deliberately varied: statement sets,

### Q1. Consider the following statements about what this topic analytically owns.

1. It supplies the vocabulary — national interest, non-alignment, strategic autonomy,
   multi-alignment, hedging — that later International Relations topics reuse.
2. The doctrinal development of Article 51 of the Constitution belongs to this topic.
3. The Cold-War chronology of the Non-Aligned Movement's founding is owned by World History.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 3 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: A**

Statement 1 is the defining feature of the topic: it is a vocabulary topic whose terms are borrowed
by Topics 02–12. Statement 3 is one of its three firewalls. ✅ Statement 2 is wrong — Article 51 is
named here in a single line as the constitutional anchor, while its doctrinal treatment belongs to
the Polity owner. Selecting 2 is the commonest scope error and produces answers that spend GS-II
words on Polity material.

### Q2. Which of the following is **not** one of the five national-interest categories used in this framework?

A. Reputation and rule-shaping
B. Domestic judicial treaty review
C. Diaspora and people-to-people
D. Strategic autonomy

**Answer: B**

The five categories are security; economic growth and resources; diaspora and people-to-people;
reputation and rule-shaping; and strategic autonomy. Options A, C and D are three of them. B is a
question of domestic legality — a matter for the courts and for Polity — and is not an external
interest India pursues. ⚠️ The trap works because judicial review sounds institutionally serious; the
test is whether it names something India seeks *abroad*.

### Q3. Consider the following statements.

**Assertion (A):** Geography is treated as a determinant of India's foreign policy, not merely as
background description.
**Reason (R):** Geography can sharply constrain or make a connectivity option prohibitively costly,
regardless of diplomatic effort.

A. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
B. A is true but R is false.
C. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
D. A is false but R is true.

**Answer: C**

✅ Sikri's observation that a Eurasian energy pipeline avoiding Afghanistan/Pakistan must route via
Xinjiang and across the Karakoram and Himalaya is precisely a case of geography sharply constraining
an option. That constraint *is* why geography counts as a determinant rather than scenery, so R
explains A. A is not "not explained" (ruling out A-option), A is not false (ruling out D), and R is
not false (ruling out B).

### Q4. Arrange the six links of the results chain in the correct analytical order.

1. Constraint or trade-off
2. India's interest
3. Verified outcome
4. Instrument chosen
5. Calibrated way forward
6. External context or actor

A. 2-4-6-3-1-5
B. 2-6-3-4-1-5
C. 6-2-4-3-1-5
D. 2-6-4-3-1-5

**Answer: D**

Interest → external context/actor → instrument → verified outcome → constraint → calibrated way
forward. Option A places the instrument before the actor, which is impossible since the actor
determines which instruments are available. Option B places the outcome before the instrument that
produces it. Option C starts from the actor, which inverts the framework's central claim that the
interest is the fixed starting point.

### Q5. Consider the following statements about Article 51 of the Constitution.

1. It directs the State to promote international peace and security.
2. It directs encouragement of settlement of international disputes by arbitration.
3. Being a Directive Principle, it is enforceable by a court at the instance of an aggrieved person.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: A**

✅ Statements 1 and 2 restate two of the four clauses; the other two are maintaining just and
honourable relations between nations, and fostering respect for international law and treaty
obligations. ✅ Statement 3 inverts the defining characteristic of Part IV — Directive Principles are
non-justiciable. The item works because candidates correctly recall the content and then wrongly
upgrade its legal force.

### Q6. A vision announced at a summit has produced no delivered project three years later. Within this topic's institutional map, where is the failure most directly located?

A. In Article 51, because the State failed to respect its constitutional values.
B. In the implementation chain — line ministries, budgetary provision and missions abroad.
C. In the courts, which should have compelled performance of the announced commitment.
D. In Parliament, whose approval is constitutionally required before each external commitment.
**Answer: B**

✅ Delivery runs through line ministries, the budget voted in Parliament and the missions that
execute on the ground; a break anywhere in that chain leaves an announcement an announcement. A
misuses a non-justiciable directive as a causal explanation. C assumes an enforceable duty that
Article 51 does not create. D overstates Parliament's role: foreign policy is overwhelmingly an
executive function, with Parliament acting through law, budget, debate and committee scrutiny
rather than case-by-case authorisation.

### Q7. Which of the following was **not** part of Nehru's stated formulation of non-alignment?

A. No military alliances with countries of either bloc.
B. An independent approach to foreign policy.
C. Equal distance from both blocs across every foreign-policy issue
D. Maintenance of friendly relations with all countries.

**Answer: C**

✅ Nehru's formulation had exactly three elements — A, B and D. C is the equidistance misreading, and
it is the single most consequential error in this topic, because it converts an active doctrine
(two of whose three elements are affirmative) into passivity. Non-alignment refused *alliance*; it
never promised equal proximity.

### Q8. ✅ Bipan Chandra characterises non-alignment as "an approach, a framework, a method… not a straitjacket but a lodestar". This characterisation matters in this topic mainly because:

A. It proves non-alignment was abandoned at the end of the Cold War.
B. It establishes non-alignment as a legally binding commitment.
C. It shows that non-alignment and Panchsheel were the same concept.
D. It explains continuing doctrinal evolution from non-alignment to strategic autonomy after the Cold War.
**Answer: D**

A method survives a change of system; a blueprint does not. That is exactly why the disappearance of
the blocs changed the label without destroying the principle — the hinge of the entire continuity
argument. A inverts that argument. B upgrades a policy posture into law. C conflates a code of
interstate conduct with a systemic-alignment posture (see Q11).

### Q9. Which of the following is **not** one of the five Panchsheel principles?

A. Collective self-defence against external aggression
B. Mutual non-aggression
C. Equality and mutual benefit
D. Mutual non-interference in each other's internal affairs

**Answer: A**

✅ The five are mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty; mutual non-aggression;
mutual non-interference in internal affairs; equality and mutual benefit; and peaceful coexistence.
B, C and D are three of them. A is an *alliance* concept — collective self-defence presupposes a
mutual-defence obligation, which is conceptually opposite to a non-alignment-era code of restraint.

### Q10. Match List I with List II.

| List I | List II |
|---|---|
| 1. Instrument carrying the five principles | (i) 29 April 1954 |
| 2. Date of signature | (ii) Preamble |
| 3. Location of the principles within the instrument | (iii) Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India |

A. 1-(i), 2-(ii), 3-(iii)
B. 1-(iii), 2-(i), 3-(ii)
C. 1-(ii), 2-(iii), 3-(i)
D. 1-(iii), 2-(ii), 3-(i)

**Answer: B**

✅ The container is the India–China Agreement on Trade and Intercourse with the Tibet Region of
China; it was signed on 29 April 1954; and the five principles appear in its **preamble**. Option D
is the near-miss that most candidates pick, because it gets the instrument right and then swaps the
date and the location. Getting all three particulars — instrument, date, placement — is what a
Prelims item on Panchsheel actually tests.

### Q11. Which statement correctly distinguishes Panchsheel from non-alignment?

A. Panchsheel is a bloc-avoidance posture, while non-alignment is a code of interstate conduct.
B. Both are codes of interstate conduct adopted at the same conference.
C. Panchsheel governs interstate conduct; non-alignment governs systemic alignment.
D. Both are treaty commitments carrying dispute-settlement machinery.

**Answer: C**

Panchsheel answers *how states should treat one another*; non-alignment answers *which camp India
will join*. A inverts the two definitions. B invents a shared conference — ✅ Panchsheel belongs to a
bilateral 1954 agreement context, and World History owns the separate conference chronology. D
upgrades both: Panchsheel sits in a preamble with no compliance machinery, and non-alignment is a
posture, not a treaty.

### Q12. Consider the following statements about strategic autonomy.

1. It is exercised issue by issue rather than declared once for all cases.
2. Deep engagement with a single major power necessarily ends it.
3. It depends on the material capacity to bear the cost of refusing.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 2 only
D. 1 and 3 only

**Answer: D**

Statements 1 and 3 are the framework's first and third defining properties. ⚠️ Statement 2 is wrong
and is the option that catches most candidates: autonomy is *compatible with depth*, provided no
relationship becomes exclusive. India can be deeply engaged with one power on technology and another
on defence supply and remain autonomous in both. Statement 3 is the under-written property —
autonomy is a capacity to absorb the cost of a choice, not a declaration.

### Q13. ✅ Sikri's assessment that India "has wisely diversified its foreign policy options, but must retain flexibility in order to be able to pursue an independent foreign policy" is best used to:

A. It links non-alignment to strategic autonomy despite systemic transformation after the Cold War.
B. Prove that India has acceded to a multi-alignment treaty.
C. Demonstrate that diversification has already eliminated India's external dependencies.
D. Show that India has adopted equidistance between competing powers.

**Answer: A**

The sentence pairs a completed change (diversification) with an unchanged requirement (flexibility
for an independent foreign policy) — which is precisely the continuity claim. B invents a legal
instrument; multi-alignment is an analytical label, not a treaty. C overstates: diversification
reduces concentration, it does not eliminate dependence — as the autonomy–dependence trade-off
insists. D imports equidistance, which this framework explicitly rejects.

### Q14. Which of the following pairings does **not** appear in Tharoor's stated multi-alignment formulation?

A. The United Nations and the G20
B. The Non-Aligned Movement and NATO
C. The G77 and IOR-ARC
D. SAARC and ASEAN-linked engagement

**Answer: B**

✅ The formulation pairs the Non-Aligned Movement with the **Community of Democracies**, not with a
treaty alliance. The substitution in B is doubly wrong: it misstates the text, and it contradicts
the concept, since multi-alignment is defined here by *non-exclusive, platform-matched* engagement and a
collective-defence alliance is exactly the exclusive commitment it avoids.

### Q15. What is the correct status of "multi-alignment" within this framework?

A. An official Indian doctrine carrying treaty status.
B. A binding obligation arising from Non-Aligned Movement membership.
C. A retrospective analytical label for non-exclusive engagement across multiple issue-specific platforms.
D. A synonym for neutrality adapted to a multipolar order.

**Answer: C**

⚠️ Multi-alignment describes observed conduct; it is not something India has signed. That matters
practically: you may use it to *analyse*, but you may not cite it as a commitment. A and B both
commit the legal-status error in different directions. D reintroduces equidistance — ⚠️ multi-alignment
involves deep, asymmetric engagement, which neutrality by definition excludes.

### Q16. Match each posture with its defining relation to a stronger power.

| Posture | Relation |
|---|---|
| 1. Bandwagoning | (i) Align against it |
| 2. Balancing | (ii) Engage more than one while mitigating risk from each |
| 3. Hedging | (iii) Align with it |
| 4. Neutrality | (iv) Equal distance or non-participation |

A. 1-(i), 2-(iii), 3-(ii), 4-(iv)
B. 1-(iii), 2-(i), 3-(iv), 4-(ii)
C. 1-(ii), 2-(i), 3-(iii), 4-(iv)
D. 1-(iii), 2-(i), 3-(ii), 4-(iv)

**Answer: D**

✅ Bandwagoning aligns *with*; balancing aligns *against*; hedging engages several while mitigating
risk from each; neutrality is equidistance or abstention. Option A swaps bandwagoning and balancing.
Option B swaps hedging and neutrality — the swap that destroys the concept, since hedging is active
and neutrality is not. Option C misassigns hedging to bandwagoning's slot.

### Q17. Which statement best explains why hedging is not fence-sitting?

A. Hedging builds capabilities with several partners; fence-sitting avoids commitment.
B. Hedging requires matching investment levels across partner relationships.
C. Hedging is a formal status that fence-sitting lacks.
D. Hedging is confined to trade and investment relationships.
**Answer: A**

⚠️ Hedging is defined by *simultaneous depth*, not by withholding. B smuggles equidistance back in —
asymmetry is the point of hedging, not a defect in it. C invents a status: hedging is a described
method, not a category a state is admitted to. D is too narrow: hedging operates across defence,
technology, energy and diplomatic domains alike.

### Q18. A state reduces the share of a single foreign source in a critical supply chain while continuing to trade with that source. This is best described as:

A. Decoupling
B. De-risking
C. Balancing
D. Neutrality

**Answer: B**

⚠️ De-risking reduces dangerous concentration *inside a continuing relationship*; decoupling severs
the relationship, so A misdescribes the stated facts, which expressly include continued trade. C is
an alignment posture, not a supply strategy. D is abstention, which is inconsistent with actively
restructuring a supply chain. ⚠️ The distinction is the economic form of the hedging-versus-balancing
distinction.

### Q19. Consider the following statements.

**Assertion (A):** In Indian diplomatic practice, principled language and interest-based goals are
not separable.
**Reason (R):** Normative framing builds the coalition legitimacy through which interest-based goals
are pursued.

A. A is true but R is false.
B. A is false but R is true.
C. Both A and R are true, and R explains A.
D. Both A and R are false.

**Answer: C**

⚠️ Because the same sentences that assert sovereign equality or development space also recruit
co-sponsors for India's institutional-voice objectives, the two motivations cannot be pulled apart
in practice — and R states exactly the mechanism that makes them inseparable. Treating them as a
trade-off, or sorting Indian foreign policy into an "idealist phase" and a "realist phase", imposes
a scheme the evidence does not support.

### Q20. Consider the following statements about the instrument toolkit.

1. A single interest is frequently worked at more than one level simultaneously.
2. The minilateral examples used here are issue-specific coalitions without collective-defence obligations.
3. Development-partnership instruments are a subset of multilateral engagement rather than a
   distinct instrument.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1 and 2 only

**Answer: D**

⚠️ Statement 3 is wrong: lines of credit, capacity building and technical cooperation function as a
*distinct* diplomatic instrument alongside classical security and trade tools, and they operate
bilaterally as much as multilaterally. Statements 1 and 2 are both central: multi-level working is
why "India pursues X through the Quad" usually understates the strategy, and the flexible,
non-alliance form of these examples makes them suitable for hedging.

### Q21. The decisive difference between an issue-based coalition and an alliance is:

A. The absence of any binding mutual-defence obligation within the coalition.
B. The number of participating states.
C. Whether the grouping meets at summit level.
D. Whether the grouping maintains a permanent secretariat.

**Answer: A**

⚠️ The distinction is one of *legal character*. B, C and D name incidental features: a coalition may
be large or small, may meet at summit level, and may or may not have a secretariat, without any of
that changing whether its members owe each other collective defence. ⚠️ Getting this wrong produces
the alliance–autonomy conflation gap, in which a deepened partnership is written up as an alliance.

### Q22. Which statement correctly separates legal status from delivery?

A. An MoU ranks below a framework agreement as a matter of legal form.
B. Entry into force concerns legal status; actual project implementation concerns verified delivery.
C. Treaty signature merely records negotiation and cannot express consent to be bound.
D. Project completion by itself proves strategic impact.

**Answer: B**

✅ Legal status and delivery are separate tracks. An MoU or framework has no fixed legal rank without
its text; treaty signature may express consent in some cases; and implementation does not by itself
prove impact. B alone makes a precise, claim-specific distinction.

### Q23. Which of the following pairs may correctly be treated as equivalent?

A. Signature of a treaty and ratification of that treaty
B. Observer status in a body and membership of that body
C. None of the pairs listed in this question
D. A corridor Memorandum of Understanding and an operating corridor

**Answer: C**

Each of A, B and D collapses a distinction: ⚠️ signature, ratification and entry into force depend on
the treaty's terms; observer, partner, dialogue-partner and guest are distinct from membership; and a
corridor MoU does not establish an operating corridor. Because no listed pair is equivalent, C is the
only defensible option.

### Q24. A candidate needs to establish whether a multilateral treaty has entered into force. Which source is definitive?

A. A summit declaration referring to the treaty
B. A news report of the signing ceremony
C. A book analysis written in the year the treaty was negotiated
D. The treaty's designated depositary record

**Answer: D**

✅ The designated depositary record is authoritative for recorded signature, consent, party count and
entry into force, although it is silent on political salience and implementation quality. A is political commitment
language, not a legal record. B reports the wrong stage entirely: signature is not entry into force.
C is period-bound analysis that must be date-checked before being cited as current, and in any case
predates the outcome being tested.

### Q25. Under this framework, a summit followed by an increase in bilateral trade establishes that the summit caused the increase only when:

A. A tested mechanism, counterfactual and alternative explanations
B. The two events are close together in time
C. The joint statement issued at the summit mentions trade
D. Both governments describe the outcome as a success

**Answer: A**

⚠️ Chronology, summit language, a trade change, a deployment or a completion does not by itself
establish strategic effect. B is bare sequence, which is the error being tested. C is commitment
language, not causation — the trade change may have been driven by prices, exchange rates or third
markets. D is the parties' own characterisation, which is interested testimony rather than evidence
of mechanism.

### Q26. Consider the following statements about the autonomy–dependence trade-off.

1. Deepening a partnership simultaneously buys capability and builds dependence.
2. Past a threshold, dependence can narrow the range of positions a state can afford to take.
3. A state's declaration of strategic autonomy is sufficient to establish it.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 3 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: B**

Statements 1 and 2 together state the paradox that makes the doctrine credible: the portfolio built
to protect autonomy can, past a point, consume it. ⚠️ Statement 3 is the error that hollows out every
"critically examine" answer — autonomy is a *material capacity* to bear the cost of a choice, not a
declaratory posture. A state that cannot afford to refuse has autonomy in language only.

### Q27. ✅ Sikri's caution about enmeshing India's military planning and systems with those of the United States while India remains heavily dependent on Russian military equipment is best used in an answer to:

A. Argue that India should abandon one of the two relationships outright
B. Show that defence-sector dependence prevents any workable hedging strategy.
C. Show how supplier dependence can narrow future autonomy.
D. Prove that defence cooperation agreements are legally binding alliances

**Answer: C**

✅ The caution is expressly about dependence and the absence of reciprocal technology-sharing, which
is exactly the mechanism by which capability purchases narrow future choice. A converts a warning
about calibration into a recommendation to exit, which the source does not support. B overstates it
into impossibility. D is a status error: a cooperation agreement is not a collective-defence
alliance.

### Q28. Consider the following statements.

**Assertion (A):** India's Gulf energy exposure is analysed as both a vulnerability and a source of
leverage.
**Reason (R):** ✅ Sikri records that a Gulf diaspora of "5 million" gives India "vital interests in,
and considerable influence over, this energy-rich region".

A. A is false but R is true.
B. A is true but R is false.
C. Both A and R are true, but R does not explain any part of A.
D. Both A and R are true, and R explains the leverage element of A.

**Answer: D**

⚠️ The vulnerability arises because supply and price shocks transmit directly; the leverage arises
because diaspora, investment and trade ties create reciprocal stakes for the partner — and R states
precisely that second mechanism. C is wrong because R does explain half of A. ⚠️ An answer that names
only the vulnerability has told half the story and forfeits the more interesting half.

### Q29. ✅ Sikri's observation that a Eurasian energy pipeline avoiding Afghanistan and Pakistan must be routed via Xinjiang and across the Karakoram and Himalayan ranges is cited in this topic in order to establish that:

A. Some limits on India's autonomy are physical and cannot be negotiated away
B. India's Eurasian pipeline interest is marginal to its energy strategy.
C. Pipeline routing can be assessed independently of strategy and geography.
D. Detailed connectivity route analysis is owned by this topic rather than by Geography

**Answer: A**

The point is that autonomy has non-policy constraints — no amount of diplomacy relocates a mountain
range. B reverses the implication: the constraint matters *because* the interest is real. C strips
out the strategic dimension the passage is making. D is a firewall error — Geography owns the route
detail, while this topic owns only the autonomy implication drawn from it.

### Q30. Match List I with List II.

| List I (SAGAR) | List II |
|---|---|
| 1. Expansion of the acronym | (i) Mauritius, 12 March 2015 |
| 2. Place and date of articulation | (ii) Declared vision |
| 3. Evidentiary status | (iii) Security and Growth for All in the Region |

A. 1-(i), 2-(iii), 3-(ii)
B. 1-(iii), 2-(i), 3-(ii)
C. 1-(ii), 2-(i), 3-(iii)
D. 1-(iii), 2-(ii), 3-(i)

**Answer: B**

✅ SAGAR expands to "Security and Growth for All in the Region"; ✅ it was articulated in Mauritius on
12 March 2015; ⚠️ and its status is a declared vision, not a treaty or a funding instrument. Option D
is the near-miss, correctly identifying the expansion and then swapping the date with the status —
the same failure mode as the Panchsheel matching item at Q10.

### Q31. Which of the following is **not** part of the stated content of the MAHASAGAR announcement?

A. Trade for development
B. Capacity building for sustainable growth
C. A binding collective-security guarantee for all participating states
D. Mutual security for a shared future

📰 **Answer: C**

✅ The announcement names trade for development, capacity building for sustainable growth, and
mutual security for a shared future, to be delivered through technology sharing, concessional loan
and grants — so A, B and D are all in the text. ⚠️ C exploits the proximity of "mutual security for a
shared future" to alliance vocabulary: vision language expressing a shared security aspiration is
not a collective-defence guarantee, and MAHASAGAR creates no obligation at all.

### Q32. The relationship between MAHASAGAR and SAGAR is best described as:

A. Replacement of SAGAR by a treaty framework
B. Two alternative names for the same 2015 vision
C. Repudiation of SAGAR's regional maritime framing
D. An extension of SAGAR that explicitly reaffirms the earlier vision
📰 **Answer: D**

✅ The 12 March 2025 Port Louis announcement presents MAHASAGAR as "going beyond SAGAR" for the
Global South while explicitly reaffirming continued movement on the SAGAR Vision in the Indian Ocean
region. A commits two errors at once — replacement and treaty status. B ignores that the two are
ten years and two different geographic framings apart. C misreads "beyond" as "instead of", which is
the specific trap this fact is set to catch.

### Q33. Consider the following statements about the Voice of Global South Summit.

1. Three editions are officially recorded, the third held on 17 August 2024.
2. All recorded editions were convened virtually by India.
3. A fourth edition is officially recorded as of the latest verification carried in this package.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 1 and 3 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

📰 **Answer: A**

✅ The three editions are 12–13 January 2023, 17 November 2023 and 17 August 2024, all convened
virtually by India. ⚠️ Statement 3 is false and is the trap: the third edition remains the latest
officially recorded one, and any further edition requires its own dated verification against an
official source before being asserted. Inventing a fourth edition is the classic "keep the series
running" error.

### Q34. Convening the Voice of Global South Summit is cited in this topic principally as evidence of:

A. India's treaty leadership of a formal international institution
B. India's autonomous agenda-setting through its own convening platform
C. The delivery of specific institutional-reform outcomes
D. India's withdrawal from major-power partnerships

📰 **Answer: B**

⚠️ Convening a platform of one's own is the clearest available demonstration of autonomous
agenda-setting, which is what makes it useful evidence for strategic autonomy. A upgrades a summit
process into an institution with treaty status. C breaches the framework's own limit — convening
demonstrates intent and capacity, not institutional outcomes, which need separate evidence. D
misreads the simultaneity that multi-alignment predicts: the Global South track and the major-power
track run in parallel, not in substitution.

### Q35. Match each issue-based coalition with the interest around which the Advanced owner describes it as formed.

| Coalition | Interest |
|---|---|
| 1. Quad | (i) United Nations reform |
| 2. I2U2 | (ii) Financial-architecture voice |
| 3. G4 | (iii) Maritime |
| 4. BRICS | (iv) Technology, food and energy |

A. 1-(iv), 2-(iii), 3-(i), 4-(ii)
B. 1-(iii), 2-(iv), 3-(ii), 4-(i)
C. 1-(iii), 2-(iv), 3-(i), 4-(ii)
D. 1-(i), 2-(ii), 3-(iii), 4-(iv)

**Answer: C**

⚠️ Quad is described as formed around maritime interests, I2U2 around technology, food and energy,
G4 around United Nations reform, and BRICS around financial-architecture voice. Option B is the
near-miss, correct on the first two and swapping G4 with BRICS. ⚠️ The underlying point matters more
than the mapping: each is formed around **one interest, not a bloc**, which is why none carries a
mutual-defence obligation.

### Q36. The contemporary treatment of India's Non-Aligned Movement membership within this framework is that:

A. The Movement remains the primary orientation of India's foreign policy
B. India's membership has lapsed
C. The Movement has been converted into a treaty alliance
D. Retained membership, now one platform among many
**Answer: D**

⚠️ Retention plus narrowed salience is exactly what platform-matched engagement predicts: a platform
that delivers little on a given interest receives correspondingly little effort, without being
abandoned. A overstates its current weight, which examiners expect candidates to qualify. B asserts
a lapse that has not occurred. C is a status error of the most serious kind, since the Movement's
defining feature is the absence of alliance commitments.

### Q37. A candidate writes that "the Global South supported India's position". Which named analytical gap does this most directly risk?

A. The homogeneity gap
B. The legal-status gap
C. The corridor–conception gap
D. The sanctions–obligation gap

**Answer: A**

⚠️ The homogeneity gap is the failure of treating a neighbourhood, the Global South or a grouping as
a single bloc with one position; the correction is to note internal diversity of interest. B concerns
signature versus ratification versus entry into force. C concerns route conception versus operating
infrastructure. D concerns another state's unilateral measure being described as a legal obligation
on India. None of those is engaged by the sentence quoted.

#### PYQ-3 (application) · 2025 · Mains · General Studies Paper II · Question 10 · 10 marks

**Owner: Topic 11 — Globalisation, Trade Agreements and External Policy Effects.**

> Official English text as printed in the paper export

>
> "'With the waning of globalization, post-Cold War world is becoming a site of sovereign
> nationalism.' Elucidate.
> (Answer in 150 words)"

⚠️ **Marks note.** The printed English block for this question in the available export carries the
word-limit instruction without a separately printed mark figure on the same line; the surrounding
questions in that section are 10-mark, 150-word items. The mark value is therefore stated as it
appears in the paper's section structure and is not asserted beyond the export.

**What Topic 01 contributes to this answer.**

| Element of the demand | Vocabulary this topic supplies |
|---|---|
| "waning of globalization" | ⚠️ The autonomy–dependence trade-off: states reduce dependence when interdependence starts to transmit coercion |
| "sovereign nationalism" | ⚠️ Sovereign equality and non-interference as normative anchors (✅ Panchsheel), distinguished from autarky |
| "post-Cold War world" | ✅ The bipolar → multipolar shift that converted non-alignment's question into strategic autonomy's |
| "Elucidate" | ⚠️ Directive fidelity: make the mechanism clear and illustrate it; do not merely assert the trend |

**Model solution outline (150 words, claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification).**

⚠️ Globalisation has not ended, but states increasingly subordinate integration to security and
national control. This produces **sovereign nationalism**: the reassertion of authority over trade,
technology, borders and strategic industries.

Its manifestations include tariffs, local-content rules, domestic-manufacturing subsidies,
sanctions, sensitive-technology export controls, investment screening and friend-shoring.
Supply-chain shocks and rivalry have exposed interdependence as potentially coercive. States
therefore diversify suppliers, reshore selected production and protect critical sectors.

At the same time, globalisation persists through trade, finance and FTAs. The shift from stalled
multilateral liberalisation toward bilateral or plurilateral agreements fragments rather than
abolishes integration. India similarly combines WTO engagement and market-access agreements with
domestic industrial policy and supply-chain resilience.

Thus, the post-Cold-War order is moving toward **managed interdependence**: states remain connected
but seek greater sovereign control over the terms and risks of connection. The trend raises costs
and can provoke retaliation, so nationalism and globalisation remain co-present rather than
sequential eras.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

> Distinguish non-alignment, strategic autonomy and multi-alignment as successive framings of a
> single underlying foreign-policy principle. (Answer in 150 words) **10**

**Demand decoding.** *Distinguish* is the directive: definitions carry the marks, and the
distinctions must be explicit. But the stem also concedes "a single underlying principle", so an
answer that only separates the three and never unifies them has answered half the question. Structure:
name the constant, then separate the three labels, then show why the label changed.

**Model answer (≈150 words).**

⚠️ The common principle is independent, interest-based decision-making; the labels changed with the
international system.

✅ **Non-alignment** was the bipolar-era posture. Nehru associated it with avoiding military
alliances with either bloc, retaining an independent foreign policy and maintaining friendly
relations with all. Bipan Chandra accordingly called it a method and lodestar, not a straitjacket.

⚠️ **Strategic autonomy** is the broader post-Cold-War principle: India preserves issue-specific
freedom of choice even while building deep partnerships. Sikri captures this as diversification
without surrendering flexibility for an independent policy.

⚠️ **Multi-alignment** is the contemporary practice of that principle: simultaneous,
non-exclusive engagement through platforms suited to different interests. Tharoor illustrates it
through engagement with both the UN and G20, and both NAM and other groupings.

Thus, non-alignment is a posture, strategic autonomy a principle and multi-alignment a practice.
However, none guarantees autonomy; effective freedom depends on material capability and manageable
dependence.

**Marks rationale.** 4 marks for the three precise definitions; 3 for the unifying principle stated
explicitly; 2 for the posture/principle/practice discrimination; 1 for the qualification. **Common
loss:** writing three chronological paragraphs with no unifying claim.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

> "Strategic autonomy is not neutrality." Explain this distinction with reference to India's
> simultaneous engagement with competing major powers. (Answer in 150 words) **10**

**Demand decoding.** *Explain* demands mechanism before illustration. The stem supplies the claim, so
no marks are available for restating it — the marks are in showing *why* the two differ and in the
"simultaneous engagement" evidence the stem explicitly asks for.

**Model answer (≈150 words).**

⚠️ Neutrality implies equidistance or non-participation; strategic autonomy means active,
issue-specific choice. India may cooperate with competing powers without accepting exclusive bloc
discipline.

✅ India pursues technology and defence cooperation with the United States, defence and energy ties
with Russia, and energy and diaspora interests with Gulf states. Its Quad engagement concerns
maritime and functional cooperation, while I2U2 addresses technology, food and energy; neither
creates a collective-defence obligation.

⚠️ Through **issue separation**, each partnership serves a distinct interest; cooperation with one
actor does not determine India's position toward another. Neutrality implies withholding or equal
distance; autonomy requires choosing, often asymmetrically.

The approach nevertheless has costs. Partners may seek exclusivity, while dependence on one
supplier for critical defence, energy or technology inputs can narrow future choices. Strategic
autonomy is therefore the freedom to commit on India's terms, supported by diversified capability,
not the refusal to commit.

**Marks rationale.** 3 marks for the definitional contrast; 3 for the three-partner simultaneity
evidence; 2 for the issue-separation mechanism; 2 for the cost qualification. **Common loss:**
describing India as "balanced between" the major powers, which is the neutrality error the stem is
testing.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

> Examine the proposition that India's foreign-policy instruments are selected by matching the level
> of engagement to the interest at stake. Illustrate with reference to the bilateral, regional,
> minilateral and multilateral levels. (Answer in 250 words) **15**

**Demand decoding.** *Examine* requires both support and limits. The stem names four levels, so all
four must appear. The highest-value move is to show a case where one interest is worked at several
levels at once, because that is where the simple "one interest, one forum" reading breaks.

**Model answer (≈250 words).**

⚠️ India generally selects forums by matching their scale and character to the interest involved,
but it often pursues one interest through several levels simultaneously.

✅ **Bilateral** engagement permits direct negotiation through summits, consular arrangements and
defence or trade instruments. It therefore carries primary weight for protecting nationals or
managing a specific relationship. **Regional** forums such as BIMSTEC and IORA address problems
shaped by neighbourhood or geography. **Minilaterals** such as the Quad and I2U2 bring a small set
of partners together around defined functional interests without a collective-defence obligation.
**Multilateral** bodies such as the UN, WTO and G20 are necessary for rule-shaping, legitimacy and
broad coalition-building.

⚠️ Yet level-matching is not one-to-one. Diaspora welfare, for example, relies primarily on
bilateral consular and labour arrangements, but also benefits from regional migration dialogue,
multilateral labour standards and people-to-people outreach. Development cooperation through
grants, concessional credit, capacity building and technical assistance can likewise operate across
levels.

Capability also constrains the choice. India may prefer development partnership where it cannot
offer security guarantees, while an announced MoU cannot be treated as delivered cooperation.
Declared visions such as SAGAR and MAHASAGAR organise choices across levels but are not themselves
treaties or projects.

Thus, level-matching explains instrument selection only when understood as a portfolio: choose the
forum suited to the task, combine levels where necessary, and state each instrument's actual legal
and implementation status.

**Marks rationale.** 5 marks for all four levels correctly characterised; 3 for distinguishing the
minilateral examples from defence alliances; 3 for the multi-level illustration; 2 for the doctrine-label placement with
correct status; 2 for the capability qualification. **Common loss:** listing forum names without
saying why that forum suits that interest.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

> "Strategic autonomy carries an autonomy–dependence trade-off." Critically examine this proposition
> with reference to India's defence, energy and connectivity choices. (Answer in 250 words) **15**

**Demand decoding.** *Critically examine* means the trade-off is the core of the answer, not an
afterthought. The stem names three domains, so all three must appear. An answer that asserts autonomy
without costing it cannot score on this directive.

**Model answer (≈250 words).**

⚠️ Strategic autonomy requires partnerships that provide capability, but deep partnerships also
create dependencies that can restrict later choices. The trade-off is therefore inherent, though
manageable.

✅ **Defence:** Sikri questions deep enmeshment with United States systems while India remains heavily
dependent on Russian equipment without reciprocal technology-sharing. Dependence extends beyond
purchase to spares, upgrades, interoperability and assured supply.

✅ **Energy:** India's Gulf exposure combines vulnerability and leverage. Supply and price shocks can
transmit rapidly, while diaspora, trade and investment create reciprocal stakes. Diversifying
suppliers and expanding domestic alternatives therefore matter as much as diplomatic goodwill.

✅ **Connectivity:** Sikri notes that a Eurasian energy pipeline avoiding Afghanistan and Pakistan
would have to traverse Xinjiang, the Karakoram and the Himalaya. Geography can thus make a route
costly or impracticable irrespective of diplomatic preference.

⚠️ These constraints do not make autonomy impossible. Dependence becomes autonomy-reducing when it
crosses a threshold and alternatives disappear. India can preserve room for manoeuvre by
diversifying suppliers and routes, building domestic defence and technology capacity, seeking
technology transfer, and keeping commitments reversible where possible.

Hence, strategic autonomy is neither isolation nor equidistance. It is the continuing ability to
make issue-specific choices while ensuring that acquired capabilities do not create a new
single-point dependence.

**Marks rationale.** 4 marks for stating the mechanism rather than asserting the cost; 6 for all
three named domains with their evidence; 3 for the threshold-and-mitigation counter-argument; 2 for
the qualified verdict. **Common loss:** treating the trade-off as a concluding sentence rather than
as the analytical spine.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

> Evaluate the claim that India's foreign policy exhibits doctrinal continuity beneath successive
> relabelling. In your answer, integrate the security, economic, technological, Global South and
> institutional-reform dimensions. (Answer in 300 words) **20**

**Demand decoding.** *Evaluate* demands a judgement on outcomes, not a narration of intentions. The
stem names five dimensions, and a 20-mark answer must integrate rather than list them. The structure
that works: state the constant, test it across the five dimensions, then state where the continuity
claim is weakest.

**Model answer (≈300 words).**

⚠️ India's foreign-policy labels have changed, but the central doctrine has remained the freedom to
decide each issue according to national interest. Non-alignment, strategic autonomy and
multi-alignment are therefore successive operational expressions of one principle.

✅ Nehru's non-alignment combined refusal of bloc alliance with an independent policy and friendly
relations with all. Bipan Chandra called it a method rather than a straitjacket. Sikri's
post-Cold-War formulation retains flexibility after diversification, while Tharoor's
multi-alignment describes simultaneous engagement through the UN, G20, G77 and smaller platforms.

⚠️ **Security continuity** appears in non-treaty, issue-specific cooperation such as the Quad,
which permits functional coordination without collective-defence obligations. **Economic and
technological autonomy**, however, increasingly depends on resilient supply chains, diversified
sources, domestic manufacturing and research capacity; diplomatic posture alone cannot offset
concentrated dependence.

✅ **Global South engagement** also shows continuity. India convened three virtual Voice of Global
South Summits in 2023–24, demonstrating agenda-setting capacity. MAHASAGAR, announced at Port Louis
on 12 March 2025, frames trade, capacity building and mutual security through development
partnership; it remains a declared vision, not a treaty.

⚠️ **Institutional reform** reveals the limit. G4 belongs to the UN-reform dimension, while IBSA
supports South-South consultation; neither should be misclassified as a security coalition.
Convening and advocacy have not by themselves produced reform outcomes.

Overall, doctrinal continuity is strong, but delivery is uneven: stronger in diversified
partnerships and coalition-building, weaker where defence, technology and institutional dependence
persist. Continuity of purpose will become effective autonomy only if domestic capability expands
faster than external dependence.

**Marks rationale.** 4 marks for the constant stated with named sources; 10 for genuine integration
across all five dimensions with a differentiated verdict on each; 3 for correctly statused dated
anchors; 3 for an explicit evaluative judgement rather than description. **Common loss:** describing
continuity without ever evaluating whether it has delivered.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

> Discuss how India's constitutional values, institutional machinery and material capability jointly
> determine the effective scope of its strategic autonomy. (Answer in 300 words) **20**

**Demand decoding.** *Discuss* requires both sides — what enables autonomy and what limits it. The
stem names three determinants and the word "jointly", so the answer must show their *interaction*,
not treat them serially. This is also the question where the Polity firewall matters most: name
Article 51 and move on.

**Model answer (≈300 words).**

⚠️ Constitutional values set India's external-policy direction, institutions convert direction into
decisions, and material capability determines which choices India can sustain. Strategic autonomy is
effective only when all three align.

✅ **Values:** Article 51, a non-justiciable Directive Principle, asks the State to promote
international peace, just relations, respect for international law and arbitration. Panchsheel adds
sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, equality and peaceful coexistence. These norms provide
legitimacy, but do not determine every interest or supply the means to pursue it.

⚠️ **Institutions:** The Prime Minister and Cabinet provide executive direction; MEA negotiates and
coordinates; the National Security Council system and line ministries supply strategic and sectoral
inputs. Parliament acts through law, budget, debate and committees, while courts review domestic
legality. Foreign affairs and treaty power remain Union responsibilities, but States can influence
implementation and engage paradiplomatically on border, water, trade and diaspora concerns. Missions
and implementing agencies determine whether commitments are delivered.

⚠️ **Capability:** Economic scale, technology, energy security, defence-industrial depth, fiscal
space and diplomatic capacity determine whether India can bear the cost of refusing pressure.
Sikri's warning about concentrated defence-supplier dependence shows how acquired capability can
also narrow choice. Geography similarly constrains connectivity options.

The factors interact. Values without capability produce declaratory autonomy; capability without
coordination produces inconsistent signalling; institutions without resources cannot implement
commitments. Therefore, India's effective autonomy is set by its weakest link, usually material
capacity. Expanding it requires domestic economic, technological and defence capability, coherent
institutions and diversified external partnerships.

**Marks rationale.** 5 marks for accurate and appropriately brief constitutional treatment
(over-writing Article 51 loses marks by breaching the Polity firewall); 5 for the machinery including
the federal constraint; 5 for capability as the binding determinant with named evidence; 5 for the
explicit interaction analysis and narrowest-link conclusion. **Common loss:** writing three
disconnected sections and never addressing "jointly".
