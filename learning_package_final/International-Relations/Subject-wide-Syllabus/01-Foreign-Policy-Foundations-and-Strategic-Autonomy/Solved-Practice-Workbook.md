### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish non-alignment, strategic autonomy and multi-alignment as successive framings of one underlying foreign-policy principle. Answer in about 150 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Non-alignment answered the Cold War question of whether India should enter either military bloc. Nehru combined refusal of such alliances with independent external judgment and friendship with all. It was active, not neutral. Bipan Chandra calls it a method rather than a rigid blueprint: its enduring feature was decision-making freedom, not a permanent inventory of partners.

After bipolarity, **strategic autonomy** names that continuing capacity to select positions issue by issue according to security, growth and other national interests. Sikri argues that diversified options must preserve flexibility. **Multi-alignment**, Tharoor's description of engagement across forums such as the UN and G20, describes the breadth of instruments used to exercise that capacity. India can develop US technology cooperation, retain Russian ties and convene Global South consultations without joining either alliance.

Thus non-alignment is a historically specific bloc posture, autonomy the durable decision rule, and multi-alignment its present portfolio. The test is whether deep partnerships leave India practical freedom to disagree, not whether it meets every partner equally.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Explain why Panchsheel and non-alignment are related but analytically distinct, and why the distinction matters for an answer on India's normative vocabulary. Answer in about 150 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Panchsheel names five norms for relations among states: respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-aggression, non-interference, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence. They appeared in the preamble to the India–China agreement on trade and intercourse with the Tibet Region signed on 29 April 1954. Their function was to articulate how states ought to conduct relations; their inclusion cannot by itself prove subsequent compliance.

Non-alignment addressed a different question: whether India should join either Cold War military bloc. Nehru's formulation added independent decisions and friendly relations with all, making it a strategic posture rather than a neutrality pledge. Sovereign equality and opposition to domination could support the case for bloc independence, but the two concepts were neither identical nor one treaty.

In a GS-II answer, cite Panchsheel when assessing norms such as non-interference; cite non-alignment when assessing alliance choice and diplomatic agency. Then examine conduct and constraints separately: invoking peaceful coexistence cannot establish that a security dispute has been settled.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the claim that strategic autonomy is best understood as a method of instrument selection rather than as a fixed alliance preference. Answer in about 250 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Strategic autonomy is India's capacity to decide external questions by its own interest calculus. Non-alignment historically expressed this through non-membership of either Cold War military bloc; it was not an instruction never to cooperate closely. As Bipan Chandra argued, the doctrine was a method rather than a straitjacket. Multipolarity expands available instruments.

Begin with the objective, not the partner. Security and technology needs can warrant a bilateral understanding; rule reform can warrant a G4 coalition; maritime cooperation can warrant issue-based minilateral engagement; developing-country voice can warrant an India-convened summit. The Quad, for instance, is a cooperative grouping, not a mutual-defence treaty. A choice of forum must be matched to its mandate: a coalition may advocate institutional reform, but cannot itself confer a permanent UNSC seat. Likewise, the third Voice of Global South Summit, convened virtually on 17 August 2024, evidences India's capacity to convene, not a completed reform outcome.

Yet choice faces material constraints. Sikri stresses diversification alongside flexibility, warning that reliance on a critical external defence supplier can restrict future decisions. India can pursue US defence-technology cooperation while retaining Russian defence and energy ties, but friction or interoperability costs may make rejection of a partner's demand expensive. Nor should a declared vision such as MAHASAGAR, announced in 2025, be mistaken for an implemented financing programme.

Hence autonomy is not equidistance and alliance absence is insufficient proof. Assess a proposed instrument by counterpart consent, legal status, implementation and whether credible substitutes let India choose differently on the next issue.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine hedging as India's operational method toward competing major powers, and state the conditions under which it becomes costly. Answer in about 250 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Hedging combines engagement with several competing powers and insurance against reliance on any one of them. It is distinct from bandwagoning with the stronger actor, balancing through an exclusive counter-alliance, and remaining neutral. The operative question is whether pursuing one partnership leaves India free to sustain another when their sponsors disagree.

India's technology and defence engagement with the United States has coexisted with Russian defence and energy ties. The ten-year US–India Major Defense Partnership framework was signed on 31 October 2025; the India–Russia annual summit proceeded on 4–5 December 2025. The sequence shows parallel relationships, not defence guarantees. India also pursues distinct G20 and Global South agendas. Diversification can reduce supplier leverage.

The hedge becomes costly when partners attach incompatible conditions or material exit options disappear. An additional US duty linked to Russian-oil purchases took effect on specified Indian goods on 27 August 2025 and was removed from 7 February 2026: a dated instance of external trade cost, not proof of permanent diplomatic rupture. Sikri also warns that reliance on Russian military equipment while enmeshing systems with US technology without reciprocal transfer can compound dependence. Gulf energy exposure and hard Central Asian pipeline geography restrict substitution further.

Thus assess hedging by both achieved cooperation and residual vulnerability. Domestic capability, diversified procurement and negotiated technology access can improve room for manoeuvre; summits or equal rhetorical distance alone cannot. India's choice is autonomous only to the extent it can absorb disagreement and retain credible alternatives.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess whether India's declared Global South visions and convening platforms strengthen strategic autonomy or merely restate it. Answer in about 300 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Strategic autonomy is not only the ability to decline another power's demands; it also includes capacity to set agendas with partners. India-convened Global South platforms and regional development visions can therefore expand its options, but their existence alone proves intent and convening, not institutional transformation.

The third Voice of Global South Summit was held virtually on 17 August 2024. It demonstrates an India-led forum for aggregating developing-country concerns alongside India's engagement with major powers. This is multi-alignment in Tharoor's sense: participation in different forums serves distinct interests. The forum makes institutional representation more visible. It does not confer voting discipline on participating states or resolve contested UN Security Council reform. India must listen to their separate priorities rather than assume that attendance constitutes endorsement of every Indian position.

The normative vocabulary has a practical dimension. Anti-colonial solidarity and sovereign equality may lend legitimacy to demands for a stronger developing-country voice, while India's interest in rule-shaping provides an additional motive. The motives need not be mutually exclusive. Likewise SAGAR, articulated in Mauritius on 12 March 2015, offered a maritime security-and-growth vision. MAHASAGAR, announced in Port Louis on 12 March 2025 as going beyond SAGAR, envisaged broader trade, capacity-building and mutual security, with technology sharing, concessional loans and grants as possible instruments. It was an extension in declared scope, not a repeal of SAGAR or a ratified defence treaty.

The hard test is follow-through: identify signed projects, financing actually disbursed, partner ownership, delivery capacity and outcomes, rather than counting declarations. Development partners have their own fiscal and political priorities; India cannot equate leadership language with automatic consent. A summit may strengthen autonomy by widening diplomatic coalitions, but financing constraints, implementation gaps or unreformed institutions may leave the gain largely rhetorical. Agenda-setting reinforces autonomy only when it yields verified capabilities and results.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the autonomy-dependence trade-off as the binding constraint on India's diversified foreign-policy portfolio. Answer in about 300 words.

**Original model answer (not an official marking scheme):**

Multi-alignment gives India several relationships, but strategic autonomy depends on usable freedom to act when those relationships collide. The binding constraint is therefore not the number of forums attended; it is whether critical supplies, finance and technology can be replaced at acceptable cost. Non-alignment's historical rejection of Cold War bloc discipline survives as a decision principle, not as a promise of costless independence.

Defence provides the clearest test. Sikri warns that continuing dependence on Russian military equipment, coupled with deeper integration into US systems without reciprocal technology sharing, can constrain planning. India's ten-year Major Defense Partnership framework with the US, signed on 31 October 2025, and the India–Russia annual summit of 4–5 December 2025 demonstrate concurrent engagement, not proof that either supplier could be replaced immediately. Energy exposes the same problem: India's relationships with Russia and the Gulf serve supply needs while creating exposure to external markets and partner policies. A Russian-oil-linked additional US duty effective 27 August 2025, and removed from 7 February 2026, illustrates a real, time-bound trade cost of maintaining an independent sourcing decision. Neither submission nor costless autonomy follows.

Geography constrains alternatives too: Sikri notes that a Central Asian land pipeline avoiding Afghanistan and Pakistan would have to negotiate the Xinjiang–Karakoram–Himalaya route. Diversification requires viable, financed routes. Multilateral leadership can help shape norms, but the virtual Voice of Global South Summit of 17 August 2024 did not by itself deliver UNSC reform. MAHASAGAR's 2025 development ambition likewise requires separate evidence of financing and implementation.

The remedy is to distinguish political choice from operational capacity: diversify contracts and technology sources, develop domestic capability, preserve partner agency and sequence commitments against supply-chain risk. Judge outcomes by credible exit options and measured delivery, not by simultaneous photographs or the absence of an alliance treaty. Autonomy is strongest when India can disagree without jeopardising essential functions.
