### DIRECT PYQ 1 — 2018 GS-II Q10 — ledger rendering only

**Demand recorded in the audited ledger:** Outside powers in Central Asia and India joining the Ashgabat Agreement — discuss the implications. **10 marks; 150 words.**

**Provenance limitation:** The ledger records the route and format, but the inspected scan does not provide a safely recoverable exact English stem. No quotation marks are used and no OCR gap is reconstructed.

**Model solution:**

Central Asia attracts Russia, China and other powers because of its location and resources; India’s entry into the Ashgabat Agreement should therefore be assessed as strategic diversification, not route completion. The 25 April 2011 agreement creates a multimodal transport-and-transit framework linking Central Asia with the Persian Gulf/Gulf of Oman; India joined with effect from 3 February 2018. It can complement Chabahar and INSTC, reduce dependence on direct transit through Pakistan and widen India’s diplomatic-economic presence.

However, membership supplies rules, not infrastructure finance. Its utility depends on connected Iranian ports and land links, customs coordination, banking, insurance, security and Central Asian consent. Russia-China proximity and wider power competition also limit unilateral Indian influence.

Thus, joining improves India’s legal-administrative access architecture, but its geopolitical significance rests on converting facilitation into reliable, commercially usable movement without overstating completion.

**Model-answer word count:** 136.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the implications demand, uses the agreement’s exact dates and function, and balances access gains against delivery and power constraints.

### DIRECT PYQ 2 — 2024 GS-II Q10 — exact official wording

**Question:** “Critically analyse India's evolving diplomatic, economic and strategic relations with the Central Asian Republics (CARs) highlighting their increasing significance in regional and global geopolitics.” **Answer in 150 words. 10 marks.**

**Model solution:**

India’s relations with the five CARs have evolved from the broad-based Connect Central Asia Policy (2012) to dedicated and multilateral mechanisms. Diplomatically, the first India-Central Asia Summit (27 January 2022), the fourth Dialogue (6 June 2025) and SCO membership sustain consultation. Economically, India seeks energy, resources and Eurasian market access; the India-EAEU process remained under negotiation after its second round in June 2026. Strategically, counter-terrorism, Afghanistan and great-power competition increase the region’s salience.

Yet evolution has not removed the binding constraint: India lacks direct land access. Transit via Pakistan/Afghanistan is politically and security constrained; the Xinjiang-Karakoram alternative is difficult and China-dependent. Chabahar and INSTC are partial Iran-routed work-arounds with operating segments, not a verified full-capacity corridor, and finance remains sanctions-exposed.

Therefore, Central Asia’s importance is rising, but India’s influence depends on converting diplomatic density into reliable connectivity while respecting CAR agency.

**Model-answer word count:** 142.

**Why this earns marks:** It addresses evolution, all three named dimensions, increasing significance and the critical limitations within the word limit.

### APPLICATION PYQ 1 — 2021 GS-II Q19 — owned by Topic 10

**Question:** “Critically examine the aims and objectives of SCO. What importance does it hold for India?” **Answer in 250 words. 15 marks.**

**Ownership note:** Exact stem confirmed in Topic 10 against a locally held official paper. Topic 10 owns the institution; Topic 05 supplies the Central Asia/connectivity application.

**Model solution:**

The SCO is a broad Eurasian intergovernmental forum whose utility must be judged against divergent member interests. Its core aims include regional security cooperation, political consultation and wider economic-cultural engagement. For India, it provides a continental seat alongside the Central Asian republics, Russia, China, Pakistan and Iran.

Its importance is threefold. First, it supports dialogue on terrorism, extremism and Afghanistan, issues directly affecting India’s security. Second, it gives India institutional access to Central Asia despite the absence of a direct land border. Third, it permits advocacy of connectivity and economic interests. India's official statement at the 25th SCO Summit in Tianjin in 2025 explicitly named Chabahar and INSTC; the 26th Summit statement in 2026 referred to connectivity and sovereignty generally without naming them.

The importance is nevertheless conditional. Pakistan’s membership does not create a transit right, China and Russia possess greater proximity and influence, and mixed threat perceptions dilute common action. A summit declaration is a political output, not an operating corridor or binding implementation guarantee. The dedicated India-Central Asia Dialogue may therefore be more focused for region-specific issues, while SCO remains useful for the wider continental setting.

Thus, the SCO is important as a platform, not as privileged access. India should retain participation, press workable security and connectivity cooperation, and diversify bilateral and alternative-route instruments so that a blocked forum does not determine its Eurasian policy.

**Model-answer word count:** 227.

**Why this earns marks:** It examines aims, identifies India-specific importance, distinguishes forum access from transit access and supplies a calibrated conclusion.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine India’s Central Asian connectivity instruments through the stages of conception, rules, finance, commissioning and operation. Answer in not more than 250 words.

**Demand decoding:** Answer the directive; distinguish instrument from outcome; use named evidence; state the limiting mechanism; reach a qualified verdict.

**Model answer:**

India’s alternative-access architecture is real, but its components occupy different evidentiary stages.

**Conception and route:** INSTC envisages multimodal connectivity from India through Iran towards Central Asia and Europe. The 2025 Prelims paper tests this alignment.

**Rules framework:** The Ashgabat Agreement, signed on 25 April 2011 and joined by India with effect from 3 February 2018, facilitates multimodal transport and transit between Central Asia and the Persian Gulf/Gulf of Oman. It is not a finance mechanism.

**Operation, contract and finance:** Lok Sabha Q2080 confirms that IPGCFZ has operated Shahid Beheshti Terminal since 2018 and India's USD 120 million commitment was fully transferred by August 2025. Separately, the Government of India DD News report of 13 May 2024 records the 10-year contract and USD 250 million credit window. The window does not prove disbursement or corridor completion.

**Commissioning and operation:** The canonical owner verifies operating INSTC segments, but does not provide a safe list of every commissioned segment, throughput or frequency. Segment operation cannot be upgraded to completed full-capacity end-to-end service.

**Constraint:** The United States announced waiver revocation effective 29 September 2025, then provided a conditional waiver that ended on 26 April 2026. Its expiry creates exposure for commercial entities but is not automatically a legal obligation on India and does not itself prove that operations ended. Customs, insurance, banking, security and return-load viability also matter.

Therefore, India has assembled complementary instruments, but the correct verdict is partial and developing connectivity, not either a paper-only route or a completed corridor.

**Model-answer word count:** 249.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer follows claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification and avoids status inflation.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Discuss how sanctions exposure differs from legal obligation, and assess its effect on India’s Iran-routed Central Asia strategy. Answer in not more than 250 words.

**Demand decoding:** Answer the directive; distinguish instrument from outcome; use named evidence; state the limiting mechanism; reach a qualified verdict.

**Model answer:**

A legal obligation binds an actor through applicable international or domestic law; sanctions exposure is the risk that banks, insurers, shippers or firms may face penalties under another state’s domestic sanctions regime. The two can produce similar commercial caution without having the same legal source.

This distinction matters for Chabahar. Lok Sabha Q2080 confirms terminal operation by IPGCFZ since 2018, full transfer of India's USD 120 million commitment by August 2025 and expiry of the conditional waiver on 26 April 2026. Separately, the Government of India DD News report of 13 May 2024 records the 10-year contract and USD 250 million credit window.

The waiver's expiry does not automatically impose an international-law duty on India to abandon the project, nor does Q2080 state that operations ended. Yet exposure can deter finance, insurance, technology suppliers and cargo operators. Hence legal freedom does not equal commercial feasibility.

India should separate sovereign policy from entity-level risk, seek transparent financing and compliance pathways, diversify logistical options, and avoid promising timelines unsupported by verified contracts and commissioned links.

Thus, sanctions are best understood as an external constraint on financeability and participation, not as a simple legal prohibition binding India.

**Model-answer word count:** 194.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer follows claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification and avoids status inflation.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Critically evaluate the evolution of India’s Central Asia policy from broad-based engagement to Eurasian connectivity architecture. Answer in not more than 300 words.

**Demand decoding:** Answer the directive; distinguish instrument from outcome; use named evidence; state the limiting mechanism; reach a qualified verdict.

**Model answer:**

India’s Central Asia policy has evolved from a declared engagement approach into a layered diplomatic, economic and connectivity architecture, but implementation remains constrained.

**Policy foundation:** The Connect Central Asia Policy (2012) defined political, security, economic and cultural engagement with Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan. This breadth corrected an energy-only conception.

**Institutional deepening:** The first India-Central Asia Summit (27 January 2022), fourth Dialogue (6 June 2025) and third Security Council Secretaries/NSA meeting (16 October 2025) sustain dedicated engagement. **Economic integration:** The proposed Comprehensive Economic Cooperation Agreement is pursued through India-EAEU FTA negotiations, formally begun on 3 June 2017; a second round occurred on 22–25 June 2026. The verified status remains negotiation, not an in-force agreement.

**Question:** Assess whether India can convert strategic interest in Central Asia into reliable access amid great-power competition and partner agency. Answer in not more than 300 words.

**Demand decoding:** Answer the directive; distinguish instrument from outcome; use named evidence; state the limiting mechanism; reach a qualified verdict.

**Model answer:**

India can improve access, but cannot convert interest into reliability through unilateral action because every route and institution is co-produced by other states.

**Strategic basis:** Central Asia matters for security, Afghanistan, energy, natural resources, markets and continental presence. The 2012 policy and dedicated Summit/Dialogue give these interests diplomatic form.

**Available instruments:** SCO membership provides a seat; India-EAEU negotiations pursue market integration; Ashgabat supplies a transit framework; Chabahar and INSTC offer an Iran-routed alternative. These instruments diversify dependence and keep India present in Eurasia.

**Geographic vetoes:** India lacks a direct CAR border. Pakistan/Afghanistan constrain the direct route, while the Xinjiang-Karakoram-Himalaya alternative is difficult and China-dependent. Geography therefore assigns transit states continuing leverage.

**Great-power and partner agency:** Russia and China possess proximity and established influence, but the CARs are sovereign multi-vector actors rather than passive objects. Indian proposals must meet their interests and implementation choices. Sikri’s call for India-China energy understanding shows that functional coordination may sometimes be more rational than pure rivalry.

Hence India can build resilient, plural access rather than dominance. The realistic objective is sustained optionality—multiple corridors, bilateral trust, dedicated regional dialogue and exact status monitoring—so that no single transit state, great power or forum can block the whole strategy.
