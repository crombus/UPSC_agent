# Global South and Development Partnering — Solved Practice Workbook

## Verification note

- The 40 questions in Part I are original and use the exact key sequence `A, B, C, D` repeated ten times.
- Direct, shared and application PYQs are labelled separately. No objective PYQ is directly routed to Topic 08.
- **Official key** means a locally held UPSC answer key supplies the letter.
- **Inferred key** means no local official key was available; the answer is derived transparently from authoritative institutional evidence.
- Minor spacing, punctuation and obvious OCR defects in official papers are silently normalised; substantive wording and all options are preserved.

## PART I — 40 ORIGINAL MCQS

### Q1. Which description best captures the “Global South” in this topic?

A. A broad political-development category without a single treaty constitution or fixed membership roll
B. A UN specialised agency whose members negotiate and administer development finance collectively
C. A formal military-neutrality alliance created by treaty after the Bandung Conference
D. A fixed legal category identical to the current membership of BRICS

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** A is correct because the category is broad and non-institutional. B invents a UN agency. C turns a historical connection into a treaty alliance. D wrongly equates a category with one grouping.

### Q2. Consider the following pairs:

1. G77 — UN-system negotiating coalition
2. NAM — bloc-avoidance and solidarity heritage
3. BRICS — identical membership to the Global South

Which of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

A. 1 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pair 1 identifies G77's negotiating role and pair 2 captures NAM's heritage. Pair 3 is false because BRICS has a specific membership and only partially overlaps the Global South.

### Q3. With reference to the G77's institutional machinery, consider the following statements:

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1, 2 and 3
D. 1 and 3 only

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** All three are recorded by the G77 Secretariat. A, B and D each omit one valid feature. The institutional facts do not make G77 synonymous with the Global South.

## PART III — VERIFIED MAINS PYQS

### Mains PYQ 1 — Direct ownership — 2019 GS-II Q19

> “The long-sustained image of India as a leader of the oppressed and marginalised nations has disappeared on account of its new found role in the emerging global order.” Elaborate.
> **15 marks; 250 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper; line breaks normalised. No official model answer exists.

**Model answer — within the 250-word limit**

India's image has changed more than it has disappeared. Earlier leadership rested primarily on anti-colonial solidarity, non-alignment and advocacy for political and economic decolonisation. India's emerging-power role now adds markets, technology partnerships and participation in major forums, but it also supplies more concrete development instruments.

Continuity is visible in G77 and NAM participation, demands for representative institutions and three Voice of Global South Summits in 2023-24. Lines of Credit, grants, ITEC training and technology centres give material content to partnership. The treaty-based International Solar Alliance illustrates institution-building, while the African Union's permanent G20 membership in September 2023 shows a bounded representation gain.

The criticism nevertheless has substance. India increasingly negotiates as a major economy seeking energy, technology, investment and strategic influence. Its interests may diverge from LDCs, SIDS or resource exporters. Aggregate credit commitments do not establish completed impact; summit convening does not confer authority to represent every developing state; and Global South positions differ on climate burdens, trade and great-power rivalry.

Therefore, the older image has been transformed from declaratory leadership into aspirational, instrument-based voice aggregation. India remains credible when it co-designs projects, protects partner ownership and converts agenda-setting into measurable outcomes. It should claim to speak within the Global South and earn broader leadership issue by issue, rather than treat historical solidarity as a permanent mandate.

### Mains PYQ 2 — Application ownership — 2021 GS-II Q9

> “If the last few decades were of Asia's growth story, the next few are expected to be of Africa's.” In the light of this statement, examine India's influence in Africa in recent years.
> **10 marks; 150 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper; line breaks normalised.

**Model answer — within the 150-word limit**

India's influence in Africa rests on partnership. Lines of Credit, grants, ITEC training, digital and health cooperation support capacity while India's trade, energy and maritime interests provide reciprocity.

Its comparative advantage is a demand-driven vocabulary of national ownership and mutual benefit. The India-Africa Forum Summit process, technology training and affordable development solutions can create durable institutional relationships. India's successful advocacy of permanent African Union membership in the G20 in 2023 also connected bilateral and continental engagement to representation reform.

However, influence is uneven. Project delays, limited diplomatic capacity, distance, commercial competition and the diverse priorities of African states constrain conversion. Aggregate finance is not equivalent to disbursement or local impact, and African agency must not be reduced to a theatre of external rivalry.

India should prioritise timely completion, local skills, maintenance, transparent evaluation and continentally representative consultation. Its influence will grow through reliable co-development, not inherited claims of solidarity.

### Mains PYQ 3 — Application ownership — 2022 GS-II Q20

> “‘Clean energy is the order of the day.’ Describe briefly India's changing policy towards climate change in various international fora in the context of geopolitics.”
> **15 marks; 250 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper; punctuation and spacing normalised.

**Model answer — within the 250-word limit**

India's climate diplomacy has moved from principally defending development space to combining equity with solution-oriented institution-building. It continues to emphasise common but differentiated responsibilities, climate finance and technology access, but increasingly demonstrates domestic and international initiatives.

In multilateral negotiations, India argues that mitigation burdens must reflect historical responsibility and capacity. This protects energy access for developing states and aligns with the Global South's climate-justice concern. At the same time, the International Solar Alliance—whose Framework Agreement entered into force in 2017—turns a clean-energy norm into a treaty-based institution. CDRI extends cooperation to resilient infrastructure, while biofuel and digital initiatives seek practical coalitions beyond universal treaty processes.

Geopolitics complicates this evolution. Energy-security shocks, technology concentration, green industrial policy and carbon-related trade measures can narrow developing-country policy space. Global South states also differ: small islands prioritise survival and adaptation, exporters fear transition costs, and emerging economies defend growth.

India's changing policy is therefore neither climate obstruction nor simple alignment with developed states. It is a strategy of equity-based transition: retain differentiated responsibility, seek finance and technology, scale domestic capacity, and build issue coalitions. Credibility, however, depends on implementation and on avoiding the assumption that one Indian pathway represents every developing country. The strongest diplomacy links climate justice to verifiable clean-energy and resilience delivery.

### Mains PYQ 4 — Application ownership — 2025 GS-II Q9

> “India-Africa digital partnership is achieving mutual respect, co-development and long-term institutional partnerships.” Elaborate.
> **10 marks; 150 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper.

**Model answer — within the 150-word limit**

India-Africa digital partnership can embody South-South cooperation when technology is adapted through African ownership rather than exported as a fixed package. India's contribution includes training under ITEC, IT centres, digital public-infrastructure experience and institution-to-institution cooperation.

Mutual respect arises when priorities are partner-defined and data, law and procurement choices remain locally accountable. Co-development means joint design, skills transfer and interoperable systems instead of vendor dependence. Long-term partnership follows when universities, regulators and public agencies retain the capacity to operate, audit and improve systems after initial deployment.

The claim must be tested against connectivity, finance, cybersecurity, data protection, maintenance and local capacity. A memorandum or pilot is not national-scale delivery.

India should therefore combine open standards, local-language design, training, independent safeguards and lifecycle finance. Digital partnership strengthens Global South credibility only when local institutions acquire durable capability and citizens receive measurable public value.

### Mains PYQ 5 — Application ownership — 2025 GS-II Q20

> “The reform process in the United Nations remains unresolved, because of the delicate imbalance of East and West and entanglement of the USA vs. Russo-Chinese alliance.” Examine and critically evaluate the East-West policy confrontations in this regard.
> **15 marks; 250 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper.

**Model answer — within the 250-word limit**

East-West confrontation helps explain UN reform deadlock, but it is not the sole cause. Security Council reform requires agreement on enlargement, categories, regional representation and veto, followed by Charter amendment and ratification, including by all permanent members. Rivalry therefore gives each P5 state incentives to resist changes that may dilute influence or advantage competitors.

The United States and European powers differ from Russia and China on conflicts, sanctions and institutional priorities, while both sides use procedural control and alliance networks. This narrows consensus on new permanent members and veto reform. The representation deficit consequently persists, especially for Africa, Latin America and major developing states.

Yet the East-West frame is incomplete. Aspirants and regional rivals also disagree: expansion proposals produce competing claims, and the wider membership has no settled formula. Global South coalitions can aggregate dissatisfaction, but they are internally diverse. India's G4 diplomacy and Voice of Global South convening create pressure, not amendment authority. The African Union's permanent G20 membership demonstrates that forum-specific reform is possible, though it does not alter the UN Charter.

Reform thus requires parallel movement: P5 accommodation, regional compromise, transparent criteria and feasible intermediate gains. India should continue coalition-building while distinguishing elected Security Council participation from permanent reform. The impasse reflects great-power confrontation, but also institutional self-preservation and unresolved representation bargains across regions.

### Mains PYQ 6 — Shared ownership — 2026 GS-II Q10

> “BRICS acts as a powerful counterweight in global governance, actively amplifying the voice and influence of the Global South.” Explain the role of BRICS in projecting itself as an alternative to other groupings.
> **10 marks; 150 words.**

**Source status:** Exact wording from the locally held official paper.

**Model answer — within the 150-word limit**

BRICS projects an alternative through reform advocacy and practical cooperation. It seeks greater developing-country voice, coordinates major emerging economies, and offers the New Development Bank alongside established institutions.

Expansion and the partner-country category widen outreach. Its declarations support reform, development finance and policy autonomy without creating a treaty alliance. This can amplify concerns over representation, finance and technology.

However, “counterweight” does not mean a unified bloc. Members differ on security, economics and external alignments; consensus limits enforcement. Partner status is not full membership, and alternative finance does not displace Bretton Woods institutions.

BRICS is therefore a platform for diversification and agenda influence, not a coherent replacement order. Its credibility depends on delivery, institutional coherence and accommodation of the wider Global South's diversity.

## PART IV — SIX ORIGINAL MAINS PRACTICE QUESTIONS

### Original 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the Global South, G77 and BRICS. Why does this distinction matter for Indian diplomacy?

**Model answer — within the 150-word limit**

The Global South is a broad political-development category linked by development challenges and unequal representation; it has no fixed membership or binding authority. G77 is a specific UN negotiating coalition with 134 members and its own machinery. BRICS is a smaller, distinct grouping focused on governance reform, development finance and cooperation.

The distinction matters because India uses each at a different level. It may aggregate concerns through the Voice of Global South Summit, negotiate through G77, and pursue financial or governance alternatives through BRICS. Precise naming prevents a summit statement from being mistaken for a binding decision and prevents BRICS positions from being attributed to all developing states.

India's advantage is multi-platform access, but its claim must remain issue-specific. It should show which actor adopted a position, which instrument delivered an output and what constraint remained. Accurate categories therefore improve both diplomatic credibility and UPSC answer quality.

### Original 2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain how triangular cooperation can strengthen South-South cooperation without displacing Southern ownership.

**Model answer — within the 150-word limit**

Triangular cooperation is a Southern-driven partnership among two or more developing countries supported by a developed state or multilateral organisation. It can add finance, technology and specialised expertise to a programme whose priorities and implementation remain owned by Southern partners.

Its value lies in scale and complementarity. A partner-designed training or infrastructure programme can combine India's relevant development experience, another institution's finance and local administrative knowledge. This may reduce duplication, widen technical choice and strengthen capacity.

Ownership is protected only when the beneficiary defines the problem, participates in design, controls data and procurement choices, and retains skills for maintenance. Conditional finance, externally selected technology or donor-driven evaluation can reproduce the hierarchy that South-South cooperation seeks to avoid.

Thus triangular cooperation does not replace North-South assistance or dilute South-South principles by definition. Its legitimacy depends on sovereignty, equality, non-conditionality, mutual benefit and demonstrable local control.

### Original 3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess the Voice of Global South Summit as an instrument of India's foreign policy.

**Model answer — within the 250-word limit**

The Voice of Global South Summit is India's dedicated, virtual convening platform for aggregating developing-country priorities. Three editions are officially recorded: January 2023, November 2023 and August 2024. It serves India's interests in reputation, rule-shaping and strategic autonomy by connecting partner concerns to larger forums.

Its principal strength is representation through consultation. The first summit created a common platform; the second relayed India's G20 outcomes; and the third produced the proposed Global Development Compact covering trade, capacity building, technology sharing and project-specific concessional finance and grants. Repeated convening can help India frame debt, food, health, climate-finance and technology-access questions as shared development concerns.

The instrument also complements India's material partnerships through Lines of Credit, ITEC and institutions such as ISA. This combination is more credible than declaratory leadership alone.

However, VoGSS has no binding decision-making authority, fixed membership or autonomous financing mechanism. Participation does not prove agreement on every issue, while Southern states differ on climate burdens, debt, trade and geopolitical alignment. Nor can the summit itself change UNSC membership or Bretton Woods voting shares.

VoGSS is therefore a useful agenda-setting and listening instrument, not a substitute for formal institutions or project delivery. India should publish follow-up matrices, co-design priorities with partners and connect each proposal to the competent forum. Its success should be judged by representation and implementation outcomes, not summit frequency.

### Original 4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** “India's Global South credibility rests as much on delivery as on diplomacy.” Discuss.

**Model answer — within the 250-word limit**

Diplomacy gives India visibility, but delivery gives its Global South claims credibility. Through G77, BRICS, G20 and the Voice of Global South Summit, India can aggregate concerns over debt, climate finance, food, health, technology and representation. Yet partners ultimately judge whether cooperation creates usable capacity.

India's delivery instruments include Lines of Credit, grants, ITEC training, experts and IT centres. MEA's aggregate record—more than 260 credit lines above USD 26 billion across about 62 countries—shows scale. The International Solar Alliance demonstrates institution-building, while digital and training partnerships can create long-term administrative links.

These instruments support diplomacy through a clear mechanism: partner demand selects a project; finance and expertise create an output; local skills and maintenance produce an outcome; successful use builds trust and coalition support. The African Union's permanent G20 membership illustrates a separate diplomatic outcome in representation.

However, commitments are not disbursements, construction is not durable use, and membership is not impact. Delays, debt burdens, inappropriate technology, weak maintenance or inadequate local consultation can undermine legitimacy. India also faces limited diplomatic and implementation capacity across a wide geography.

Therefore, diplomacy and delivery are mutually reinforcing but analytically distinct. India should publish project-level status, evaluate partner outcomes, expand local procurement and training, and use triangular finance where useful without sacrificing Southern ownership. Leadership is earned through reliable, partner-defined results.

### Original 5 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Critically examine the proposition that the Global South is unified by grievance rather than by homogeneity.

**Model answer — within the 300-word limit**

The proposition is substantially correct. “Global South” is not a treaty organisation or fixed bloc; it is a political-development category encompassing states with different income levels, geographies, commodity structures and strategic alignments. Its most durable common ground is dissatisfaction with unequal representation and unequal access to development resources.

The grievance has several dimensions. UNSC composition and Bretton Woods voting structures are seen as reflecting an older distribution of power. Debt stress, climate-finance shortfalls, food and health insecurity, and concentrated technology deepen the belief that those most affected possess insufficient voice. G77 bargaining, BRICS reform advocacy and the Voice of Global South Summit give these concerns institutional or diplomatic channels. The African Union's permanent G20 membership in 2023 demonstrates that focused representation claims can yield bounded outcomes.

Homogeneity, however, is absent. Small island states face existential climate risks; resource exporters fear transition costs; large emerging economies defend development space; LDCs may prioritise grants and debt relief; and security alignments shape positions on conflicts and sanctions. BRICS, NAM and G77 have different memberships and purposes. Even agreement on a grievance does not establish agreement on remedy, burden-sharing or leadership.

This diversity does not make coalition-building meaningless. It changes its form. Effective cooperation must be issue-specific, procedurally inclusive and grounded in partner ownership. India can act as a convenor and norm entrepreneur by framing common concerns, but it must test support rather than presume representation. Development delivery can strengthen trust, while formal reform still depends on the competent institution.

Thus grievance supplies a minimum coalition platform, whereas policy unity must be negotiated. The Global South's strength lies not in acting as one bloc on every issue but in building flexible majorities around specific inequities and feasible reforms.

### Original 6 — 20 marks, 300 words

**Question:** Evaluate whether India can convert Global South convening capacity into verifiable development and governance outcomes.

**Model answer — within the 300-word limit**

India possesses significant convening capacity but conversion into outcomes requires a chain from listening to implementation. Its advantages include membership in G77, NAM, BRICS and G20; three Voice of Global South Summits; development instruments such as Lines of Credit and ITEC; and institution-building through the International Solar Alliance.

Convening can generate outcomes in three ways. First, it aggregates dispersed concerns into a negotiable agenda, as seen in the Global Development Compact's four proposed limbs. Second, it connects bilateral delivery to wider norms: training, technology cooperation and concessional finance can demonstrate that partnership is practical. Third, it can carry coalition demands into decision venues; the African Union's permanent G20 membership in September 2023 is a concrete representation gain.

Yet each step faces a distinct gap. Summit participation does not equal consensus. A proposal is not a funded programme. Credit extended is not necessarily disbursed, completed or developmentally effective. Institutions such as the UNSC and IMF retain their own decision rules. Southern states also diverge on climate, debt, trade and security, while India's rising-power interests may create suspicion that voice aggregation masks self-interest.

Conversion therefore requires institutional design. India should publish summit follow-up matrices, identify responsible agencies and financing, co-design projects, disclose lifecycle status, build local skills and maintenance, and evaluate partner-defined outcomes. Triangular cooperation can add resources if Southern ownership is preserved. Diplomatically, India should form differentiated coalitions and pursue achievable forum-specific gains while sustaining broader reform demands.

India can convert convening into influence, but only selectively. The valid measure is not the number of meetings or announced funds; it is whether partner priorities produce completed capabilities, changed institutional representation or durable rule influence. Leadership remains earned, issue-specific and revisable.
