### 2019 GS-II Q19 — independent substantial model answer (250 words)

India's image as a defender of marginalised nations originated in its anti-colonial experience, non-alignment and advocacy of a fairer international order. Its emergence as a major economy complicates that identity, but does not make it vanish. The proposition is therefore too absolute: what changes is the need to reconcile solidarity with the interests and responsibilities of a rising power.

India still works through developing-country coalitions. The G77's UN negotiating role gives smaller states a platform for shared development claims; India's presence there need not preclude separate strategic partnerships. The International Solar Alliance, whose framework entered into force in December 2017, illustrates an attempt to convert shared access to clean energy into a standing institution. India also uses technical cooperation and development credit to build capacities, although announced support must be distinguished from results used by recipients. These instruments can sustain an image of partnership where design respects national ownership rather than demanding alignment with India's preferences.

Conversely, India pursues major-power recognition, security relationships and market access; its positions will not always match those of the least developed or small island states. Southern countries have different trade, climate and security priorities. Merely invoking non-alignment cannot establish that India represents them. Nor can India's aspirations substitute for their consent or for project-level evidence.

India can therefore lead on specific equitable-development issues without claiming a monopoly on the voices of the marginalised. Credibility rests on consultation, continued commitment to inclusive institutions and verifiable locally valued delivery. A more prominent global role puts its older solidarity to a harder practical test; it does not, by itself, extinguish it.

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks, ~150 words

**Question:** Differentiate the Global South, G77, NAM and BRICS, and explain India's distinct status in a dedicated Voice summit.

**Model answer (~150 words):** The Global South is a loose political-development category, not a membership body. Tharoor calls G77 a coalition of over 120 developing countries that negotiates within the UN system; its own chairmanship and South Summit belong to that organisation, not the entire South. NAM retains an anti-bloc historical legacy, while BRICS has a smaller, distinct grouping and a finance-governance agenda. BRICS' partner-country category, introduced in 2024, is not identical to full membership. India participates in overlapping coalitions but separately **hosts** the virtual Voice of Global South Summit: MEA records its first three editions in January and November 2023 and August 2024. Hosting lets India solicit priorities but gives it no membership authority over a homogeneous world bloc. Sikri's description of India as a *potential* leader is an aspiration conditional on partners' interests. Thus the exam distinction is between category, coalition, grouping, participation status and convening power; none alone proves a mandate to speak for all developing states.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks, ~150 words

**Question:** Distinguish South-South from triangular cooperation and explain why neither extinguishes North-South responsibilities.

**Model answer (~150 words):** South-South cooperation is collaboration among developing states on capacities, technology and development priorities. UNOSSC describes solidarity, national and collective self-reliance, sovereignty, national ownership, independence, equality, non-conditionality, non-interference and mutual benefit as its guiding principles. The Buenos Aires Plan of Action on technical cooperation among developing countries, endorsed by the UN General Assembly in 1978, anchors the practical emphasis on pooling resources and solving common problems. Triangular cooperation retains **Southern leadership** but adds support from a developed country or multilateral organisation to a programme involving two or more developing countries. Northern funding need not displace Southern agenda-setting; a Southern label alone cannot certify fairness. India's technical-training partnerships can serve this logic where recipients define skills and use them locally. Neither mode eliminates Northern assistance or climate-finance obligations: complementary resources may be indispensable, especially to least developed, landlocked and small island states. Test who sets priorities, pays and receives measurable benefits.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks, ~220–250 words

**Question:** Evaluate whether India's Voice of Global South Summit can remedy developing countries' representation deficit.

**Model answer (~235 words):** India's Voice of Global South Summit is a convening device, not an elected legislature for developing countries. Its three recorded virtual editions—January 2023, November 2023 and August 2024—offered a channel to identify concerns about debt, food, health, technology and climate finance. At the third edition India proposed a Global Development Compact combining trade for development, sustainable-growth capacity building, technology sharing, and project-specific concessional finance and grants. No funded bank or binding vote follows.

The representation grievance concerns where decisions are actually made. Developing states seek greater voice in institutions such as the UN Security Council and the IMF and World Bank. A host can aggregate demands and frame equitable technology access; it cannot by declaration change UNSC seats or quota rules. India operates within G77, NAM and BRICS, with different mandates. Credibility depends on partners, not presumed unanimity.

A concrete, bounded success illustrates the scale: the African Union became a permanent G20 member at the New Delhi summit in September 2023. This changes representation in one forum; it does not itself reform the UN or release development finance. India's July 2026 launch of a 2028–29 non-permanent UNSC candidature is another dated *ask*, not an elected or permanent seat.

The summit can help define common demands and monitor follow-up. Remedying a representation deficit requires the relevant institutions' decisions, meaningful participation by varied Southern states and demonstrable financial or governance outcomes. Convening begins the process; it cannot complete it alone.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks, ~220–250 words

**Question:** Assess India's South-South development partnership using the UNOSSC principles, with a qualified Latin American example.

**Model answer (~235 words):** UNOSSC's South-South doctrine links solidarity and collective self-reliance to sovereignty, national ownership, independence, equality, non-conditionality, non-interference and mutual benefit. These principles test *who directs* cooperation rather than whether the donor and recipient share a geographical label. The UN-endorsed 1978 Buenos Aires Plan of Action favoured pooling technical resources and building national capacities, especially for states facing acute constraints. India's technical training and Lines of Credit can advance such purposes, but the first is a skills instrument while the second finances projects and may create repayment obligations. MEA's global figure of more than 260 LoCs worth over USD 26 billion across roughly 62 countries is a reported footprint, not money fully disbursed.

Latin America illustrates opportunity and the need to respect national differences. Brazil and India cooperate across BRICS, IBSA, G20 and G4 as well as bilaterally; common grouping membership does not demonstrate a delivered agricultural or energy project. India's limited goods-preference agreement with MERCOSUR, operational since June 2009, is not a comprehensive free-trade area. Its expanded Chile PTA and CEPA negotiations (with a fourth round recorded in December 2025) are distinct from a CEPA already concluded. India–CELAC dialogue offers regional contact, while distance, language and connectivity limit automatic trade conversion.

Triangular cooperation can bring Northern or multilateral funds to Southern-led projects without turning the donor into the owner. India's contribution is strongest when partners identify the need, choose institutional arrangements and report completed outcomes. Neither South-South rhetoric nor headline credit authorisations substitutes for borrower sustainability, local skills and independently assessed reciprocal benefit.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks, ~270–310 words

**Question:** Analyse how India might turn Global South convening into delivered finance, technology and representation, distinguishing all stages of the chain.

**Model answer (~290 words):** A Global South summit is a starting point for collective agenda-setting, not a financing or constitutional authority. India's three recorded virtual Voice of Global South Summits culminated in the August 2024 proposal of a Global Development Compact: trade for development, capacity building for sustainable growth, technology sharing, and project-specific concessional finance and grants. These pillars name potential tools, but neither a dedicated Compact bank nor a portfolio of disbursed Compact projects follows from the proposal. The first test is whether partner governments jointly identify priorities and a responsible implementing institution.

India has separate instruments that could support follow-through. MEA records more than 260 Lines of Credit valued above USD 26 billion across roughly 62 countries worldwide. A credit facility moves through approval, disbursement, project construction, maintenance and finally measured local benefit; the headline figure sits at an earlier stage. ITEC-type training develops personnel rather than financing an asset. The India-headquartered treaty-based ISA can coordinate solar cooperation; CDRI's June 2026 total of 58 countries and 12 partner organisations denotes network participation, not 70 completed projects. The Global Biofuels Alliance's July 2026 count of 25 countries and 12 international organisations *agreeing to join* is even more expressly a participation measure.

Representation requires action by decision-making bodies. The AU became a permanent G20 member in September 2023, a genuine but forum-specific gain. Finally, small island states, resource exporters and large emerging economies may disagree over climate costs and debt. India can coordinate issue-specific positions only where partners accept them. A credible chain traces consultation to adopted policy, committed resources to disbursements, and membership gains to actual influence while publishing limitations; summit publicity cannot replace these tests.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks, ~270–310 words

**Question:** Critically examine whether the Global South is held together more by representation grievances than by homogeneous interests, using India and Latin America as examples.

**Model answer (~290 words):** 'Global South' is an analytical category, not a treaty organisation with a single membership roll. States share concerns about development space, unequal finance and under-representation in older governance structures, yet differ by income, exposure to climate change, energy endowments and strategic alignment. The UN Security Council's permanent-member design and the IMF/World Bank's voting arrangements provide powerful shared grievances, not identical national negotiating positions. A small island state's immediate adaptation needs and an emerging resource exporter's transition costs may pull in different directions even when both seek fair finance.

India responds through overlapping tracks: as participant in G77, NAM and BRICS, and as host of three virtual Voice of Global South Summits recorded through August 2024. G77's UN negotiating machinery and regionally rotating chair do not turn it into the entire Global South; NAM's anti-bloc heritage is not BRICS' financial-governance agenda. India can frame equity in vaccines, DPI and climate finance as shared demands—norm entrepreneurship—but agenda-setting does not confer a universal mandate. Sikri's 'potential leader' formulation acknowledges that partners' assent, not Indian assertion, determines leadership.

Latin America resists easy generalisation. Brazil's overlap with India in BRICS, IBSA, G20 and G4 provides diplomatic opportunities, while limited India–MERCOSUR goods preferences and still-negotiated India–Chile CEPA show very different bilateral instruments. CELAC dialogue spans diverse states; distance and uneven institutional ties matter. No one bilateral tie authorises India to speak for an entire region. Conversely, the AU's permanent G20 seat obtained in September 2023 shows that issue-specific representation can change an institution without requiring universal Southern agreement on every policy.

The practical path is negotiated coalitions around particular proposals, with partner-defined priorities and checks on whether commitments become delivered support. Grievance offers a basis for cooperation; representative process and locally verified benefits determine whether that cooperation deserves the name solidarity.
