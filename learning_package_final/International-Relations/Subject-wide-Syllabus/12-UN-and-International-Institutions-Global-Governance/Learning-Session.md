## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. The legal map: identify the institution before judging it

```text
CONSTITUTIVE BASIS
├─ UN Charter → principal organs and Charter powers
├─ Specialised-agency constitution → WHO/UNESCO/ILO mandates
├─ Treaty/statute → ICC, WTO, IMF, World Bank, treaty bodies
└─ Political declaration/forum → GCM, G4, IGN outcome language
        ↓
MEMBERSHIP/STATUS → MANDATE → DECISION RULE → LEGAL EFFECT
        ↓
IMPLEMENTATION CAPACITY → OUTPUT → OUTCOME → ACCOUNTABILITY
```

Global governance is the allocation and exercise of authority across States, international organisations, courts, treaty regimes and political forums. It is not world government. Sovereignty remains central because States create institutions, confer competences and usually supply implementation.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The first question in global governance is not whether an institution is important, but what legal instrument created it, who belongs to it, what it may decide and who must implement that decision.*

**Must-write keywords:** constitutive instrument; attributed powers; membership; mandate; jurisdiction; decision rule; legal effect; implementation gap.

**Paragraph model:** Claim—institutions possess only conferred authority. Evidence—the Charter gives the Security Council primary responsibility for peace and security, while the Rome Statute creates a separate criminal court. Analysis—similar subject matter does not create interchangeable powers. Qualification—informal influence may exceed formal competence, but must not be described as legal authority.

### 2. The six principal organs: one Charter, different functions

| Principal organ | Core function | High-yield limit |
|---|---|---|
| General Assembly | Universal deliberation, recommendation, elections and budget | Usually does not legislate binding rules for States |
| Security Council | Primary responsibility for international peace and security | Political selectivity and permanent-member veto |
| ECOSOC | Coordinates economic, social and related work; links specialised agencies | Coordination depends on fragmented institutions and States |
| International Court of Justice | Judicial organ for inter-State disputes and advisory opinions | Contentious jurisdiction rests on State consent |
| Secretariat | Administration and implementation under the Secretary-General | Implements mandates; does not independently amend them |
| Trusteeship Council | Supervised Trust Territories | Operations suspended on 1 November 1994; organ was not abolished |

The Charter names six, not five, principal organs. The common shortcut that omits the Trusteeship Council is factually unsafe. Specialised agencies such as WHO, UNESCO and ILO are linked to the UN system but are not principal organs. The ICC and WTO are not UN principal organs either.

The UN has 193 Member States. The Holy See and the State of Palestine are non-member observer States; observer access does not create a General Assembly vote. Under Articles 31 and 32, a non-Council UN Member—and in specified circumstances a non-member State party to a dispute—may participate in Council discussion without vote.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Institutional legitimacy begins with functional differentiation: universal voice lies in the Assembly, concentrated security authority in the Council, adjudication in the ICJ and administration in the Secretariat.*

**Must-write keywords:** Article 7; universal membership; primary responsibility; coordination; judicial organ; suspended operations.

### 3. UNGA and UNSC: recommendation, decision and voting rule

```text
UNGA
all UN Members → one vote each
important questions → two-thirds of members present and voting
other questions → majority present and voting
normal external effect → recommendation
budget/elections/internal institutional decisions → operative within Charter system

UNSC
15 members → 5 permanent + 10 elected
procedural matter → 9 affirmative votes
other matter → 9 affirmative votes + permanent-member concurrence
Article 25 → Members agree to accept and carry out Council decisions
```

UNGA resolutions are not uniformly “meaningless” and UNSC resolutions are not uniformly identical. The Assembly shapes norms, creates subsidiary bodies, approves budgets and elects members, but Articles 10–14 generally use the language of recommendation. Article 25 gives Council **decisions** a different legal position. Whether a particular paragraph is mandatory still depends on its wording, Charter basis and context; do not equate every recital or recommendation inside a Council resolution with an enforceable command.

The word **veto** is shorthand. Article 27 speaks of nine affirmative votes including concurring permanent-member votes on non-procedural matters. Established practice treats a permanent-member abstention as not preventing adoption. The compulsory abstention clause for a party to a dispute is textually limited to Chapter VI and Article 52(3).

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The Assembly supplies broad legitimacy and norm creation; the Council supplies concentrated authority, but that concentration also creates selectivity and blockage.*

### 4. Security Council reform: representation, veto and the Article 108 lock

Reform has four separable dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Representation | Who sits permanently and non-permanently? |
| Decision-making | Who can block action and on what questions? |
| Effectiveness | Can the Council act promptly and sustain implementation? |
| Accountability | Are reasons, mandates, sanctions and implementation review open to scrutiny? |

Article 23 fixes the present Council at 15 members: five permanent and ten elected for two-year terms. Article 108 requires Charter amendments to be adopted by two-thirds of General Assembly members and ratified by two-thirds of UN Members, including **all permanent Council members**. Thus the States whose privileges are debated retain a ratification lock over structural change.

The July 2026 General Assembly record continued the IGN into the eighty-first session. It welcomed models, especially the African model, and called for a consolidated model as a precursor to text-based negotiations. The accurate status is therefore **active negotiation without adopted model, negotiated text or Charter amendment**.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *UNSC reform is not one seat claim; it is a constitutional redesign problem in which representativeness must be reconciled with effectiveness and ratification by every incumbent permanent member.*

### 5. India: participation, reform coalitions and calibrated claims

India's case has three layers:

1. **Participation:** repeated elected Council service, committee responsibility and a long peacekeeping record demonstrate contribution.
2. **Representation:** India's demographic scale, developing-country role and contemporary capabilities support a claim that the 1945 distribution is under-representative.
3. **Reform design:** India works through the G4—Brazil, Germany, India and Japan—and supports expansion in permanent and non-permanent categories.

India's 20 April 2026 IGN statement supported the African model, a 26-member Council, expansion in both categories and equal privileges and responsibilities for existing and new permanent members. This is an official negotiating position, not an agreed reform. The G4 position must also be distinguished from **Uniting for Consensus**, which favours expansion centred on elected representation, and from the **Common African Position** expressed through the Ezulwini Consensus and Sirte Declaration.

Tharoor's “renewed, not retired” formulation captures India's broad approach. His separate argument for strengthening the General Assembly shows that India's reform agenda cannot be reduced to a permanent seat.

India's recent elected terms were 2011–12 and 2021–22. It launched candidature for the 2028–29 elected term on 13 July 2026. A candidature is not election. The G4 Foreign Ministers' statement of 25 September 2025 supported expansion in both categories and the Common African Position, but remained coalition advocacy. The Pact for the Future, adopted on 22 September 2024, likewise records reform intent rather than Charter change.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *India's strongest claim combines contribution with representation, but contribution creates political credibility—not a legal entitlement to permanent membership.*

### 6. Peacekeeping: mandate, consent and capability

```text
Security Council mandate
        ↓
voluntary troops/police + financing + mission leadership
        ↓
consent + impartiality + limited force
        ↓
ceasefire support / civilian protection / political process
        ↓
outcome constrained by mandate, resources, spoilers and Council unity
```

Peacekeeping is not expressly named in the Charter and evolved as institutional practice. It must be distinguished from peace enforcement. Robust peacekeeping can use tactical force when authorised, while retaining consent of the main parties; peace enforcement may operate without that consent at the strategic level.

India's long service—more than 275,000 personnel across over 50 missions since 1948 in the canonical evidence—and the first all-women Formed Police Unit deployed to Liberia in 2007 are evidence of responsible multilateral participation. They strengthen a reform argument but do not create an automatic seat. Troop-contributing countries can reasonably seek greater consultation in mandate design because they bear operational risk.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *A mandate can authorise protection, but mandate credibility depends on political consent, capabilities, clear priorities and the willingness of the Council to sustain the mission.*

### 6A. Counter-terrorism governance: obligation, monitoring and listing

Security Council resolution 1373, adopted on 28 September 2001, imposed counter-terrorism obligations on States and established the CTC to monitor implementation. CTED, created by resolution 1535 (2004), supplies expert assessment and technical-assistance facilitation; its mandate was renewed by resolution 2810 (2025) through 5 January 2029. The Delhi Declaration of 29 October 2022 addressed terrorist use of new and emerging technologies.

The CTC is not a police or prosecutorial body. Nor does it itself operate the ISIL/Al-Qaida sanctions list: listing, exemptions and delisting belong to the separate 1267/1989/2253 Committee and its supporting machinery. Effectiveness must therefore be measured through standards, assessments, assistance and improved national implementation—not attributed arrests or battlefield outcomes.

| Body | Institutional character | Core function | Not |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN Counter-Terrorism Committee (CTC) | Security Council subsidiary body | Monitors States' implementation of resolution 1373; CTED supports assessments and technical-assistance facilitation | Court, police body or FATF |
| FATF | Intergovernmental standard-setting and mutual-evaluation network | Develops and assesses AML/CFT/CPF standards | UN sanctions committee, court or police body |

FATF recommendations and mutual evaluations shape national systems but are not Security Council
sanctions or judicial orders. CTC and FATF may reinforce implementation through different mandates;
neither should be described as directly investigating or prosecuting terrorists.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The CTC's strength is universalising and monitoring a compliance baseline; its weakness is that coercive implementation remains national and politically uneven.*

### 7. ICJ and ICC: court, party, jurisdiction and effect

| Test | ICJ | ICC |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | UN Charter and ICJ Statute; principal UN organ | 1998 Rome Statute; independent treaty court |
| Who is before it? | States in contentious cases | Individuals accused of specified international crimes |
| Jurisdiction | Consent through special agreement, treaty clause or reciprocal declaration | Territory/nationality of a State Party or accepting State; State-Party referral, Prosecutor with authorisation, or UNSC referral |
| Result | Judgment final and binding only between parties and in that case | Criminal responsibility of individuals |
| Advisory role | Authorised UN organs/agencies may request opinions | No comparable general advisory jurisdiction |
| India | May appear before ICJ subject to jurisdictional basis | Neither signatory nor State Party to Rome Statute |

ICJ advisory opinions are generally non-binding, though they carry legal weight and moral authority; rare instruments may give them binding force. Contentious judgments are not universal legislation. ICC jurisdiction is also not simply synonymous with State-Party membership: a non-party situation may reach the Court through a Security Council referral or an Article 12(3) acceptance.

The ICJ's climate advisory opinion of 23 July 2025 is an example of legal clarification outside a contentious case. It must not be described as a judgment binding every State. By contrast, an ICJ contentious judgment is final and binding on the parties to that case; Article 94 of the Charter supplies the route to the Security Council if a party fails to perform.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The ICJ adjudicates State responsibility through consent; the ICC prosecutes individual criminal responsibility through a treaty-based jurisdictional gateway.*

### 8. Specialised agencies: mandate versus member control

**UNESCO** works through a General Conference of all members, a 58-member Executive Board and a Secretariat in education, science, culture and communication. It administers the 1972 World Heritage Convention through the World Heritage Committee and List. The 2019 PYQ uses funding and withdrawal to test whether formal universality can survive dependence on powerful contributors. The United States announced withdrawal in 2017, effective 31 December 2018, returned in 2023 with an arrears plan, and notified another withdrawal in July 2025, effective 31 December 2026; until that date it remains a member. India was re-elected to the Executive Board for 2025–29; office provides voice and oversight, not control over outcomes.

**WHO** works through the World Health Assembly, Executive Board and Secretariat. COVID-19 illustrated its value in alerts, standards, coordination and pooled access, while also exposing dependence on State reporting, compliance, data and supply cooperation. Its Emergency Committee did not reach a PHEIC determination at the 22–23 January 2020 meeting; WHO declared a PHEIC on 30 January. COVAX later delivered close to two billion doses to 146 economies before closing at the end of 2023, demonstrating both pooled-delivery capacity and the limits imposed by supply nationalism. The Pandemic Agreement was adopted on 20 May 2025, but the PABS annex remained under negotiation on 18 September 2026. WHO stated that finalising it would pave the way for signature and ratification; entry into force requires 60 ratifications. Separately, the 2024 IHR amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Specialised agencies can coordinate knowledge and collective action, but their authority, finance and information are mediated by sovereign members.*

### 9. Bretton Woods institutions: resources, voice and conditionality

The IMF and World Bank are specialised agencies linked to the UN but governed by their own Articles of Agreement. Their core mandates differ: the IMF supports monetary cooperation and balance-of-payments stability; the World Bank Group finances development through distinct institutions. Governance differs from UNGA's one-State-one-vote principle because quotas or share subscriptions shape voting power and access to executive boards.

The IMF's Sixteenth General Review, concluded in December 2023, approved a 50 per cent equiproportional quota increase. Because it is equiproportional, it increases resources without changing relative quota shares; representation reform remains a separate question. The canonical owner records India's quota share at about 2.75 per cent and voting share at about 2.63 per cent; these are dated institutional figures, not a claim of present economic rank. World Bank reform similarly has at least three axes—voice/shareholding, mandate and lending capacity. IDA21 was finalised in December 2024 with a stated USD 100 billion financing package for FY2025–28; resource scale does not itself redistribute votes. AIIB and the New Development Bank supplement rather than legally replace Bretton Woods institutions.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Bretton Woods reform must separate resource adequacy from voice redistribution: a larger balance sheet can coexist with an unchanged representation deficit.*

### 10. WTO: consensus, legal commitments and enforcement crisis

The WTO is a treaty-based, member-driven organisation with 166 members. Major decisions are normally taken by consensus. The Ministerial Conference is the top body and meets at least once every two years. Between ministerials, the General Council conducts business and also meets as the Dispute Settlement Body and Trade Policy Review Body.

WTO obligations arise from negotiated covered agreements, not from speeches by the Secretariat. Panels adjudicate disputes; adoption traditionally follows the negative-consensus design. The non-functioning Appellate Body prevents the completion of some appeals, illustrating an **effectiveness and accountability** problem inside a legally dense regime.

Unlike the IMF and World Bank, the WTO does not delegate ordinary decision-making to an executive board weighted by capital. Yet formal consensus does not erase unequal delegation size, technical capacity or bargaining leverage. Smaller informal consultations may aid agreement, but must feed back transparently into the full membership.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *The WTO's paradox is that consensus gives every member formal ownership while making rule-making and institutional repair exceptionally difficult.*

### 11. Global-governance evaluation: four lenses, not slogans

| Lens | Diagnostic question | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | Who has voice, vote, agenda access and permanent status? | 1945 power distribution or weighted-vote imbalance |
| Legitimacy | Is authority accepted as procedurally fair and substantively justified? | selective action, double standards, exclusion |
| Effectiveness | Are mandates matched by resources, compliance and measurable outcomes? | mandate-resource gap, veto, non-compliance |
| Accountability | Who explains, reviews and corrects decisions and implementation? | opaque appointments, sanctions or lending conditions |

These lenses can conflict. A larger Council may be more representative yet harder to coordinate. Consensus may enhance ownership yet slow agreement. Weighted voting may secure capital commitments yet weaken equality. Stronger enforcement may improve compliance yet intensify sovereignty concerns.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *Reform is credible only when it improves voice, delivery and answerability together; gains on one axis can produce costs on another.*

### 12. Instrument and status discipline

```text
NEGOTIATING IDEA
  → draft text
  → adoption
  → signature
  → ratification/accession
  → threshold for entry into force
  → domestic/institutional implementation
  → monitored outcome
```

A Charter amendment, treaty, Security Council decision, General Assembly recommendation, advisory opinion and political declaration are not interchangeable. Likewise, member, observer, signatory, party, office-holder and invited participant are different statuses.

Current examples make the rule concrete: the Pact for the Future is an adopted political outcome document; the Global Digital Compact is its annex, not a world AI treaty; the BBNJ Agreement entered into force on 17 January 2026, while India had signed but had not deposited ratification; the WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted but awaited its PABS annex before signature and ratification.

For technology governance, the Global Digital Compact supplies a political framework; GPAI is a multistakeholder partnership; and the ITU develops telecommunications and ICT standards. None is a universal AI regulator. For UN administration, the UN80 Initiative launched on 12 March 2025 addresses efficiencies, mandate review and structural realignment; it does not alter Council membership. The next Secretary-General selection was initiated under the joint General Assembly–Security Council process; office-selection reform and Council reform remain separate tracks.

**Answer-grabbing line:** *In international institutions, verbs carry law: proposed, adopted, signed, ratified, entered into force and implemented describe different evidentiary levels.*

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### 1. Input, throughput and output legitimacy

- **Input legitimacy:** representation, participation and consent.
- **Throughput legitimacy:** transparent procedure, reasons, review and consistency.
- **Output legitimacy:** delivery of peace, health, finance, trade or justice.
- A body may perform well yet remain under-representative, or be representative yet ineffective. Good answers identify the axis being criticised.

### 2. Veto as constitutional bargain and collective-action trap

The permanent-member privilege helped keep major powers inside the post-war order. Yet Article 108 also makes their ratification indispensable to Charter reform. This is a self-entrenching constitutional bargain. East-West rivalry matters, but it is not the only obstacle: G4, African and Uniting-for-Consensus models also differ over categories, regional allocation and veto.

### 3. Authority without autonomous enforcement

The CTC, WHO and many treaty bodies demonstrate indirect governance: standards, reporting, peer review, technical assistance and reputational pressure alter conduct without an international police force. Their effectiveness should be measured against their actual mandates, then qualified by State capacity and political will.

### 4. Fragmentation and regime interaction

Security, health, trade, finance, climate, migration and criminal justice are governed by overlapping institutions with different membership and legal effects. Fragmentation can create expertise and experimentation, but also forum shopping, contradictory standards and unequal compliance burdens.

### 5. Reform beyond Charter amendment

When formal constitutional reform stalls, change migrates to administrative review, new treaties, advisory opinions, coalitions and plurilateral arrangements. UN80, the BBNJ Agreement, the WHO Pandemic Agreement process and digital-governance initiatives show adaptation without altering Council composition. This is institutional evolution, not proof that the representation deficit has disappeared.


## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Institution-identification grid

- **UN principal organs:** UNGA, UNSC, ECOSOC, Trusteeship Council, ICJ, Secretariat.
- **Specialised agencies:** separate constitutive instruments; coordinated with the UN, not principal organs.
- **Independent treaty court:** ICC; prosecutes individuals. **UN judicial organ:** ICJ; contentious cases are between States.
- **Bretton Woods:** IMF—monetary/BOP stability; World Bank—development finance. Weighted voting differs from UNGA equality.
- **WTO:** 166 members; normally consensus; Ministerial Conference above General Council; General Council also meets as DSB and TPRB.

### Legal-effect ladder

- **UNGA:** generally recommends; budget, elections and internal institutional decisions remain operative.
- **UNSC:** Article 25 requires Members to accept and carry out Council decisions.
- **ICJ judgment:** final, binding between parties and for that case; jurisdiction requires consent.
- **ICJ advisory opinion:** generally non-binding but legally authoritative.
- **Treaty:** distinguish adoption → signature → ratification/accession → entry into force → implementation.
- **Political declaration/compact:** normative commitment, not automatically a treaty.

### Security Council and reform

- 15 members: 5 permanent, 10 elected for two years; no immediate re-election.
- Article 27: nine affirmative votes; permanent-member concurrence on non-procedural matters; abstention is not a veto in practice.
- Article 108: two-thirds UNGA adoption + two-thirds ratification including all permanent members.
- Four reform axes: representation, decision-making/veto, effectiveness, accountability.
- G4: Brazil, Germany, India, Japan. Counter-model: Uniting for Consensus. African position: Ezulwini Consensus + Sirte Declaration.
- July 2026: IGN continued into UNGA 81; consolidated model/text-based negotiations not yet completed.
- India, 20 April 2026: African model, both categories, 26-member Council, parity of privileges/responsibilities—position, not outcome.

### Operations, courts and agencies

- Peacekeeping principles: consent, impartiality, limited force. Robust peacekeeping ≠ peace enforcement.
- CTC is a UNSC subsidiary body monitoring resolution 1373; CTED assesses/supports; 1267 machinery lists ISIL/Al-Qaida targets.
- FATF is an intergovernmental AML/CFT/CPF standard-setting and mutual-evaluation network—not a UN sanctions committee, court or police body.
- WHO Agreement: adopted; PABS annex still negotiated on 18 September 2026; no signature/ratification yet.
- UNESCO: US withdrawal notified for 31 December 2026; membership continued on review date.
- ICC: 125 States Parties; India neither signatory nor party.

### Evaluation and answer spine

1. Identify legal basis and exact institutional status.
2. State membership, mandate, decision rule and legal effect.
3. Test representation, legitimacy, effectiveness and accountability separately.
