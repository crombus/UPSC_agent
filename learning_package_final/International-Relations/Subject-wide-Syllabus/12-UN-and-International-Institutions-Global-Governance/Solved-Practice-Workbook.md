### PYQ DEMAND CARD 1 — 2024 General Studies Paper II Question 19

**Demand:** "Terrorism has become a significant threat to global peace and security." Evaluate the effectiveness of the United Nations Security Council's Counter Terrorism Committee (CTC) and its associated bodies in addressing and mitigating this threat at the international level. (Answer in 250 words). A 15-mark demand whose word limit is printed in the question itself, confirmed word for word against the locally held official General Studies Paper II of 2024.

**Status:** Routed to this owner in the audited 2024-2025 Mains routing ledger and named by the Basic owner as the anchor demand for the counter-terrorism half of this topic. No official answer key exists for a Mains demand and none is claimed.

**Independent model answer:**

The CTC is useful when assessed against its actual Council mandate rather than as a global police force. Resolution 1373 (2001) established it to monitor states' counter-terrorism obligations and encourage cooperation; CTED supplies expert assessments and capacity-support analysis. India chaired the CTC during its 2011–12 elected Council term and hosted its 2022 special meeting; the Delhi Declaration focused attention on terrorists' use of emerging technologies. Such coordination makes national implementation more comparable and brings financing, borders and technical capability onto a shared agenda. CTED's mandate was subsequently renewed by resolution 2810 (2025), indicating continued institutional demand. But states supply information and implement measures themselves. Unequal administrative capacity and geopolitical selectivity constrain compliance; CTC cannot arrest suspects or adjudicate crimes. The separate 1267/1989/2253 sanctions committee handles ISIL and Al-Qaida listings with its Monitoring Team: attributing that power to CTED misidentifies the institution. Effectiveness therefore means better standards, monitoring and cooperation, not proof that terrorism has been eliminated. Improve transparent follow-up, technical assistance and consistent implementation while preserving rights safeguards; no committee can substitute for national action.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the actual directive with institutional precision, named evidence, a counterargument and a qualified verdict.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Comment on the proposition that the veto is simultaneously the reason the United Nations survived and the reason its Security Council cannot be reformed. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:**

The veto helped secure major-power participation after the League's failure, but it also enables any permanent Security Council member to block a substantive draft even with broad support. Article 27 distinguishes substantive voting from procedural decisions, which require nine affirmative votes without a P5 veto. Thus calling every permanent abstention a veto misstates the legal design. Reform is harder still under Article 108: a Charter amendment requires the prescribed General Assembly majority and ratification by two-thirds of members including all P5. Yet the veto is not the *only* obstacle. The G4 seeks expansion in both permanent and elected categories; Uniting for Consensus contests new permanent seats, and the Common African Position presses for representation and parity. India's proposal for a 26-member Council is an advocacy position, not an enacted reform. The veto is therefore both a stabilising bargain and an entrenched inequality; an answer must combine great-power consent with wider agreement on who gains seats and how their privileges work.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine why an evaluation of the Security Council's counter-terrorism machinery must begin by identifying which body is being evaluated. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:**

Evaluating the wrong counter-terrorism body produces a false verdict. The Counter-Terrorism Committee (CTC) is a Security Council subsidiary body established under resolution 1373 (2001) to monitor implementation of counter-terrorism obligations and coordinate capacity-building; its expert arm CTED assesses countries and identifies gaps. Under resolution 2810 (2025), CTED's mandate was renewed, evidencing the continuation of the assessment function. India's 2011–12 CTC chairmanship and its hosting of the 2022 special meeting that adopted the Delhi Declaration illustrate how members can focus attention on emerging technology misuse. These are institutional outputs, not evidence that the CTC arrests suspects. Neither committee is an international criminal court; domestic authorities must investigate, prosecute and comply with applicable human-rights obligations. Global coordination can improve compliance but geopolitical selectivity, uneven reporting and sovereign implementation limit results. One should not infer from continuing terrorism that a coordinating committee is wholly useless, or from a declaration that terror financing and recruitment have ended. A stronger institutional answer maps Council resolution → subsidiary mandate → expert assessment or listing → national implementation → measurable security outcome, and explicitly states where inference exceeds available evidence.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the four dimensions along which reform of international institutions should be analysed. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:**

International-institution reform has at least four separable dimensions: representation, mandate effectiveness, financing and legal decision rules. Representation concerns whose voice counts. The G4 seeks both permanent and elected Council enlargement, while the Common African Position and Uniting for Consensus disagree on seat design; a proposed 26-member Council does not describe the current fifteen. IMF representation involves a different instrument: the sixteenth quota review's equiproportional increase boosted resources but left relative shares and voting weight unchanged. Mandate effectiveness asks what a body can actually do. The CTC and CTED assess national counter-terrorism capacity; the separate ISIL/Al-Qaida sanctions committee lists targets. WHO can issue a PHEIC and coordinate technical guidance, but COVID-19 exposed dependence on state data and implementation. Financing determines whether nominal mandates have delivery capacity: UNESCO's US withdrawal, return and later withdrawal notification show vulnerability to a major donor, while World Bank IDA21's replenishment mobilises resources without proving every project delivered. Decision rules create the legal ceiling. An UNSC substantive resolution faces P5 veto; Article 108 requires their ratification for Charter amendment. G4 advocacy and UNGA declarations, including the Pact for the Future, cannot skip those rules. Conversely UN80 administration and IHR amendments can proceed through other tracks; even then, adopted and in-force are different stages, as the unfinished WHO Pandemic Agreement illustrates. Reform should therefore be judged by who sits at the table, what the institution is empowered to do, who pays and who can block or enforce outcomes. Without all four lenses, a new seat may leave financing unchanged, more funds may leave voting inequitable, and an ambitious declaration may leave implementation empty. The same diagnostic prevents a specialised agency's success at issuing guidance being mistaken for a coercive power it never possessed.
