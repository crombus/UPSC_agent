## BASIC LEARNING SESSION

### 1. The governing frame: integration and control coexist

```text
GLOBALISATION                         SOVEREIGN NATIONALISM
trade • capital • technology         tariffs • subsidies • controls
data • production networks           screening • sanctions • policy space
                 \                   /
                  \                 /
                   v               v
                 RECONFIGURED INTERDEPENDENCE
                    gains + exposure
                          |
                          v
              INDIA: openness + resilience
```

**Simple explanation.** Globalisation is intensified cross-border movement of goods, services,
capital, technology, data and production. Sovereign nationalism is the attempt to regain national
control over strategic sectors, borders and economic dependencies. They are co-present, not
successive eras: states can sign an FTA while simultaneously subsidising domestic production or
restricting sensitive technology.

**Origin and timeline**

| Stage | Significance |
|---|---|
| Post-1945 GATT order | Progressive tariff bargaining and non-discrimination framework |
| 1995 | WTO established; India has been a member since 1 January 1995 |
| 2001 | Doha Development Agenda launched; no comprehensive conclusion followed |
| 2008 onward | Financial stress strengthened distributional and industrial-policy concerns |
| Pandemic and geopolitical shocks | Concentration risk, export controls and resilience became central |
| Present phase | Globalisation continues through selective, security-conscious integration |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The contemporary world is not uniformly deglobalising; it is
> reorganising interdependence around security, technology, climate and domestic legitimacy.

**Must-write keywords:** co-presence; reconfigured interdependence; sovereign nationalism;
strategic sectors; policy space; selective integration.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *Globalisation persists through digital flows and preferential
agreements; however, export controls and industrial subsidies show a sovereign turn. The
coexistence of these instruments means that “waning” describes a change in form and distribution,
not the disappearance of cross-border exchange.*

**Trap:** “Waning” does not mean “ended”; nationalism does not necessarily mean autarky.

### 2. Policy instruments: tariff, non-tariff measure and strategic restriction

```text
MARKET-ACCESS MEASURES
├─ Tariff: customs duty applied at the border
└─ Non-tariff measure (NTM)
   ├─ quota / licensing
   ├─ SPS and TBT requirements
   ├─ local-content or procurement condition
   ├─ rules-of-origin administration
   └─ trade remedy: anti-dumping, countervailing, safeguard

SECURITY / FOREIGN-POLICY MEASURES
├─ sanctions: country/entity/sector/finance restrictions
├─ export controls: restrict sensitive goods or technology
└─ investment screening: review inward/outward capital on security grounds
```

**Tariff versus NTM.** A tariff changes the price of an import through a customs duty. An NTM
changes access or compliance through a rule, quota, licence, standard, test, certification or
administrative condition. An NTM is not automatically protectionist: health and safety measures
may serve legitimate purposes, though design or discriminatory application can create barriers.

**Trade remedies.** Anti-dumping duty responds to injuriously dumped imports; countervailing duty
responds to injurious subsidisation; a safeguard responds to serious injury from an import surge.
They are rule-governed contingent protection, not synonyms for an ordinary tariff.

**Sanctions and export controls.** These target states, entities, sectors, finance or technologies
for foreign-policy/security purposes. They may affect Indian firms through banks, insurance,
shipping, technology licences or secondary-risk calculations, but another state's unilateral rule
does not thereby become India's international-law obligation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** External economic pressure now travels as much through standards,
> licences, finance and technology controls as through the customs tariff.

**Must-write keywords:** tariff; NTM; SPS; TBT; trade remedy; sanctions; export controls;
investment screening; extraterritorial exposure.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *A tariff directly raises landed cost, whereas an NTM changes the
conditions of entry. EU CBAM, for example, combines reporting and certificate obligations with a
climate rationale; it therefore transmits through compliance and market access, not merely through
a conventional tariff. Its fairness and proportionality remain contestable.*

**Trap:** “Non-tariff” does not mean “non-trade”; “regulation” does not automatically mean
“disguised protectionism.”

### 3. Trade agreement, customs union and strategic framework

| Instrument | Core character | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| PTA | Preferences on a limited product set | It is not comprehensive free trade |
| FTA | Parties remove/reduce barriers among themselves; retain separate external tariffs | It is not a customs union |
| Customs union | Internal preferences plus a common external tariff | Members lose some independent tariff policy |
| CEPA/CETA/TEPA/ECTA | Labels used for agreements of differing breadth | The label alone does not prove identical coverage |
| WTO commitments | Multilateral schedules and rules | They are not a bilateral strategic partnership |
| Strategic framework/joint statement | Political coordination on selected issues | It does not itself create preferential tariff rights |

**What an agreement may cover.** Goods tariffs, services, investment, mobility, customs
facilitation, standards cooperation, intellectual property, government procurement, digital
trade, trade remedies, environment, labour and dispute settlement. The actual text—not the
title—determines coverage.

**Rules of origin (RoO).** RoO identify whether a product qualifies as originating in a party.
They prevent simple trans-shipment or trade deflection. Product-specific value-addition,
tariff-shift and certification requirements can also create utilisation costs.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A trade agreement creates legal market-access rules; a strategic
> framework creates political direction—neither should be upgraded into the other.

**Must-write keywords:** preferential access; common external tariff; rules of origin;
trade deflection; tariff schedule; services commitments; strategic framework.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *The US-India statement of 6 February 2026 announced a framework
for an Interim Agreement and continued BTA negotiations. It records intended tariff, NTM,
digital-trade and supply-chain terms, but it is not evidence that a comprehensive FTA has entered
into force.*

**Trap:** CEPA, CETA, TEPA and ECTA are names, not universal legal templates.

### 4. The legal-and-economic status ladder

```text
NEGOTIATION LAUNCHED
        ↓
ROUNDS / TEXT BARGAINING
        ↓
SUBSTANTIVE OR FORMAL CONCLUSION
        ↓
LEGAL SCRUB / AUTHENTICATION
        ↓
SIGNATURE
        ↓
RATIFICATION / DOMESTIC APPROVAL
        ↓
ENTRY INTO FORCE
        ↓
IMPLEMENTATION
        ↓
UTILISATION BY FIRMS
        ↓
MEASURED OUTPUT, DISTRIBUTION AND IMPACT
```

**Why every step matters**

- **Conclusion** means negotiations have produced an agreed text or political bargain; it does
  not itself make obligations operative.
- **Signature** authenticates/expresses consent as provided by the instrument, but domestic
  approvals or ratification may remain.
- **Ratification** is the state's formal consent process where required.
- **Entry into force** is the date legal obligations begin under the agreement's clause.
- **Implementation** requires customs systems, notifications, regulations and administrative
  capacity.
- **Utilisation** asks whether eligible firms actually claim preferences.
- **Impact** asks whether the agreement caused changes in trade, investment, jobs, resilience or
  distribution; post-agreement trade growth alone does not prove causation.

**Current worked comparison**

| Agreement | Stage on 26 September 2026 |
|---|---|
| EFTA TEPA | In force |
| UK CETA/FTA | In force |
| Oman CEPA | In force |
| EU FTA | Negotiations concluded; not treated as signed or in force |
| New Zealand FTA | Signed and ratification processes completed; entry into force scheduled for 20 October 2026 |
| Chile CEPA | Negotiations continuing |
| US-India | Framework for an Interim Agreement; broader BTA negotiation continuing |

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The legal status of an agreement proves obligation; utilisation and
> counterfactual analysis are still required to prove impact.

**Must-write keywords:** negotiation; conclusion; signature; ratification; entry into force;
implementation; preference utilisation; counterfactual; distributional outcome.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *India-New Zealand negotiations were launched in March 2025,
substantively concluded in December 2025 and the FTA was signed in April 2026. Although
ratification processes were completed in September, the official entry-into-force date is
20 October 2026; at the present cut-off, therefore, “signed and ratified” is accurate while
“in force” is premature.*

**Trap:** Never infer impact from signature or infer implementation from entry into force.

### 5. WTO: baseline rules, consensus and dispute settlement

```text
MINISTERIAL CONFERENCE — highest authority; at least biennial
                 |
                 v
GENERAL COUNCIL
├─ meeting as General Council
├─ meeting as Dispute Settlement Body
└─ meeting as Trade Policy Review Body
                 |
                 v
GOODS • SERVICES • TRIPS councils and committees
```

✅ The WTO is member-driven and decisions are normally taken by consensus. Its continuing
functions include administering agreed rules, negotiations, notifications, trade-policy review,
technical assistance and dispute settlement. The difficult conclusion of new multilateral bargains
does not make the rule baseline irrelevant.

✅ MC13 met in Abu Dhabi from 26 February to 2 March 2024. Its agenda and decisions kept
dispute-settlement reform, development treatment, least-developed-country transition and
e-commerce within the ministerial process.

**Appellate crisis.** ✅ The WTO states that its Appellate Body cannot review appeals because of
vacancies; the last sitting member's term expired on 30 November 2020. Current notified appeals
include India's DS582 notice of 8 December 2023, DS584 notice of 17 May 2023, and DS579-581
notices of 24 December 2021. ⚠️ A notified appeal is not a completed appellate ruling.

**MPIA boundary.** The canonical owner describes the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration
Arrangement as a voluntary DSU Article 25 workaround and records India outside it. Because the
current official participant table did not render reliably during this review, no participant
count is used and the membership claim should be rechecked before future publication.

**Reform agenda for India**

1. restore a credible, time-bound two-stage dispute process;
2. make notification and transparency obligations workable;
3. protect development flexibilities and special and differential treatment;
4. obtain a durable public-stockholding solution;
5. discipline trade-distorting subsidies and unilateral restrictions fairly;
6. improve committee deliberation without replacing member control;
7. preserve policy space while making rules predictable.

**MC14 caution.** ✅ WTO's official page confirms Yaoundé, 26-30 March 2026 and lists sessions on
reform, dispute settlement, fisheries, investment facilitation, e-commerce, agriculture and
development. It does not, on the page checked, substantiate the canonical numerical claim that
near-unanimous support existed for IFD incorporation; that numerical claim is not used here.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The WTO's problem is not absence of rules but the widening gap between
> negotiated rules, consensus-based updating and enforceable appellate finality.

**Must-write keywords:** member-driven; consensus; Ministerial Conference; DSB; Appellate Body;
notification; special and differential treatment; enforceability.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *WTO reform must join efficiency with legitimacy. Restoring appellate
review would improve predictability, but developing-country trust also requires meaningful special
and differential treatment and a permanent food-security solution; procedural repair without
distributive reform would remain incomplete.*

**Trap:** A listed agenda item is not a ministerial outcome; a panel report is not an appellate
resolution.

### 6. Agriculture, food security and fisheries

**Agreement on Agriculture (AoA).** It organises disciplines around market access, domestic
support and export competition. India's public procurement and food-security programmes make
this a distributive negotiation, not merely an institutional-design question.

**Public stockholding.** ✅ The 2013 Bali interim mechanism protects eligible developing-country
public-stockholding programmes from specified legal challenge subject to conditions while members
seek a permanent solution. It is not the permanent solution India demands.

**Fisheries Subsidies Agreement.** ✅ Adopted at MC12 on 17 June 2022 and in force from
15 September 2025 after two-thirds of members deposited instruments of acceptance. It prohibits
certain harmful fisheries subsidies, while negotiations on additional provisions continue.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** For India, WTO reform is distributive: it must reconcile rule
> credibility with food security, livelihood protection and unequal development capacity.

**Must-write keywords:** AoA; market access; domestic support; export competition; public
stockholding; peace clause; permanent solution; harmful fisheries subsidies.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *The Bali protection reduces litigation risk for qualifying
public-stockholding programmes, but its conditions and interim character leave India's basic
demand unresolved. A durable settlement must protect food security while addressing transparency
and spillovers into world markets.*

**Trap:** Global entry into force of a treaty does not prove a particular member's later acceptance;
India's acceptance date is withheld here because the current WTO acceptance page did not confirm it.

### 7. India's agreement portfolio: opening with safeguards

| Agreement | Strategic use | Limitation to write |
|---|---|---|
| UAE CEPA, in force 1 May 2022 | Gulf market access and commercial integration | Aggregate trade change does not isolate agreement impact |
| Australia ECTA, in force 29 December 2022 | Preferential access and services links | ECTA coverage is text-specific |
| EFTA TEPA, in force 1 October 2025 | Trade plus investment facilitation | Investment/jobs figures are objectives, not delivery |
| UK CETA/FTA, in force 15 July 2026 | Goods, services and mobility-linked cooperation | Real gains depend on utilisation and competitiveness |
| Oman CEPA, in force 1 June 2026 | Gulf diversification and market access | Entry into force precedes measurable long-run impact |
| EU FTA, negotiations concluded 27 January 2026 | Large-market and regulatory integration opportunity | Not signed, ratified or in force at cut-off |
| New Zealand FTA, signed; in-force date 20 October 2026 | Market access with sensitive-sector design | Not yet operative at cut-off |
| Chile CEPA, under negotiation | Latin American diversification | No concluded agreement may be claimed |

**RCEP choice.** ✅ India announced on 4 November 2019 that it would not join RCEP. ⚠️ This
preserved policy space against perceived adjustment risks but reduced preferential integration
opportunities. It was a choice about the terms of integration, not rejection of trade.

**Distribution test**

```text
PREFERENCE
   ├─ exporters: access opportunity
   ├─ import-competing firms: adjustment pressure
   ├─ consumers: price/variety effects
   ├─ workers/MSMEs: uneven capability and standards costs
   └─ regions/states: unequal sector exposure
        ↓
SAFEGUARDS + STANDARDS CAPACITY + ADJUSTMENT SUPPORT
```

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Trade diplomacy succeeds only when negotiated access becomes usable
> preference and its domestic adjustment costs remain politically and socially manageable.

**Must-write keywords:** sensitive sectors; safeguards; utilisation; RoO; competitiveness;
adjustment assistance; distribution; policy space.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *EFTA TEPA is legally operative, but its investment and employment
language remains an objective. Evaluation must therefore track facilitation, actual investment,
preference utilisation and sectoral distribution rather than convert a treaty promise into a
delivered outcome.*

**Trap:** “In force” proves legal operation, not uniform welfare gains.

### 8. Supply chains: efficiency, resilience and de-risking

```text
CONCENTRATION SHOCK
supplier / route / technology / finance
          ↓
MAP DEPENDENCE
          ↓
DIVERSIFY SUPPLIERS + BUILD DOMESTIC CAPACITY
          ↓
TRUSTED PARTNERS + STOCKS + RECYCLING + SUBSTITUTION
          ↓
SELECTIVE ENGAGEMENT CONTINUES
          ↓
DE-RISKING ≠ DECOUPLING
```

**Global value chains (GVCs).** Production stages are distributed across jurisdictions. This raises
efficiency but also exposes firms to chokepoints, export controls, shipping disruption, standards
changes and concentrated suppliers.

✅ The Economic Survey 2025-26 records India at 44th of 145 economies on the 2023 Harvard
Economic Complexity Index, improved from 57th in 2013 but unchanged since 2019. The index is a
composition signal, not a direct measure of supply-chain resilience or FTA impact.

**De-risking.** Reduce excessive dependence while continuing trade. **Decoupling** implies a much
broader severance. **Friend-shoring** shifts selected production toward trusted partners; it can
improve security but may raise cost and reproduce concentration within a political bloc.

**India's dual position.** ⚠️ India can attract diversification-seeking investment and still face
foreign tariffs, standards and technology restrictions. The 2024 GS-II supply-chain PYQ belongs
primarily to Topic 03; here it is an application of policy transmission.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Resilience is not self-sufficiency; it is the capacity to absorb a
> disruption without surrendering the efficiency gains of diversified exchange.

**Must-write keywords:** GVC; concentration risk; de-risking; decoupling; friend-shoring;
redundancy; trusted partners; switching cost.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *Diversifying a single-source input reduces interruption risk, but
alternative suppliers, inventories and domestic capacity impose transition costs. India should
therefore measure resilience by recoverability and substitution, not by the elimination of all
imports.*

**Trap:** Supply-chain relocation announcements are not completed relocation or proven employment.

### 9. Climate and technology as trade policy

**EU CBAM mechanism**

```text
EU CLIMATE RULE
   ↓
importer authorisation + emissions reporting
   ↓
purchase/surrender of CBAM certificates
   ↓
cost and data demands transmitted to foreign producers
   ↓
Indian response: measurement • verification • cleaner production • diplomacy
```

✅ The definitive CBAM regime applies from 1 January 2026. Covered selected sectors are cement,
iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen; certificate prices reference EU
ETS allowance auction prices.

**India's climate diplomacy.** ✅ India's updated 2022 NDC states a 45% reduction in emissions
intensity of GDP from 2005 levels by 2030 and about 50% cumulative installed electric-power
capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; net-zero 2070 remains a declared long-term goal.
⚠️ A target is not an achieved outcome. India links mitigation to equity, CBDR-RC, finance and
technology access. ✅ Loss-and-Damage funding arrangements were operationalised at COP28, but
adequacy, access and the contributor base remain contested.

**Technology controls.** Export licensing, investment screening, trusted-vendor rules and standards
can decide whether Indian firms obtain advanced inputs or enter high-value supply chains. These
are external-policy effects even when administered through domestic law.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Carbon and technology rules turn regulatory power into market power,
> transmitting a jurisdiction's internal priorities across borders.

**Must-write keywords:** CBAM; embedded emissions; EU ETS; standards power; export licensing;
technology access; CBDR-RC; climate finance.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *CBAM is legally an EU regime applied to importers, yet its data and
certificate requirements reach Indian producers through market access. India can question equity
while also improving measurement and low-carbon competitiveness; diplomacy and domestic capacity
are complementary, not alternative responses.*

**Trap:** A foreign regulation can affect India without legally binding India as a treaty party.

### 10. Tariff volatility and India's response portfolio

✅ The US-India joint statement of 6 February 2026 records a framework for an Interim Agreement,
continued BTA negotiations, an 18% reciprocal tariff on originating Indian goods, intended
market-access changes, RoO, NTM and digital-trade work, and cooperation on investment reviews,
export controls and supply-chain resilience. It also records removal of an additional 25% tariff
linked by the US side to Russian-oil purchases. ✅ A July 2026 US Section 301 action separately
directed a 10% tariff on Indian goods, with product exemptions.

⚠️ The lesson is not merely the tariff level. Rapidly changing measures alter quotations,
contracts, sourcing, inventory, investment timing and negotiating leverage. Policy volatility can
have effects even when a measure is later removed.

**India's response portfolio**

1. defend the multilateral rule baseline and restore enforceable dispute settlement;
2. negotiate selective, high-quality agreements with exact status discipline;
3. improve RoO compliance and preference utilisation;
4. diversify markets, suppliers, finance, shipping and critical technologies;
5. build standards, carbon-accounting and testing capacity;
6. use transparent safeguards and time-bound adjustment support;
7. coordinate trade, industry, climate and foreign policy without treating resilience as autarky;
8. publish distributional and utilisation evidence before claiming success.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Strategic autonomy in trade is the capacity to diversify dependence,
> contest adverse rules and retain policy choice—not insulation from the world economy.

**Must-write keywords:** tariff volatility; reciprocal tariff; Section 301; portfolio response;
rule-shaping; diversification; strategic autonomy; measurable impact.

**Paragraph demonstration:** *The US-India framework combines tariff bargaining with non-tariff,
digital and supply-chain issues, showing that modern trade diplomacy is broader than customs
duties. Yet the framework and a completed agreement are different legal stages, while a separate
Section 301 action demonstrates that cooperation and unilateral pressure can coexist.*

**Trap:** A strategic partnership does not prevent tariff conflict; a tariff concession does not
by itself establish a comprehensive trade agreement.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A. Globalisation is being segmented, not simply reversed

⚠️ Integration increasingly occurs inside overlapping preferential, technological and security
networks. An FTA can deepen market integration while export controls segment technology. This is
**selective globalisation**: openness by sector, partner and strategic sensitivity.

### B. “Weaponised interdependence”

⚠️ States controlling financial, technological, logistical or data-network nodes can use those
positions coercively. The analytical sequence is:

```text
NETWORK CENTRALITY → MONITORING/CONTROL CAPACITY → RESTRICTION
→ THIRD-PARTY COMPLIANCE → TARGET'S DIVERSIFICATION RESPONSE
```

Qualification: commercial exposure or fear of secondary measures is not identical to a binding
UN obligation.

### C. FTA proliferation: liberalisation plus fragmentation

FTAs sustain cross-border integration, but different RoO, standards, certification systems and
dispute provisions create a “spaghetti bowl” of compliance. Small firms may be legally eligible
for a preference yet unable to use it. Therefore evaluate:

`coverage → depth → implementation → utilisation → additionality → distribution`.

### D. Causal impact test

Do not write “trade rose after the agreement, therefore the agreement caused it.” Test:

1. pre-agreement trend and global demand;
2. exchange rates, commodity prices and logistics;
3. preference margin and eligible product share;
4. RoO compliance and actual utilisation;
5. investment timing and domestic reforms;
6. sectoral winners, losers and regional effects.

### E. Trade-security-climate nexus

CBAM, green subsidies, export controls and investment screening show regulatory objectives
overlapping. Classification should therefore ask both **legal form** and **policy function**.
A climate measure can have a trade effect; a trade agreement can carry strategic supply-chain
clauses; a national-security control can produce industrial-policy outcomes.

### F. Negotiating strategy for India

⚠️ India needs variable geometry rather than one universal instrument:

- WTO for baseline rules, coalitions and dispute legitimacy;
- FTAs for reciprocal market access and regulatory cooperation;
- strategic frameworks for technology/supply-chain coordination without pretending they are FTAs;
- domestic capacity for standards, logistics, innovation and adjustment;
- Global South coalitions for equity, food security, finance and technology access.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Core thesis

- Globalisation and sovereign nationalism coexist.
- Integration continues but is more selective, security-conscious and regulation-intensive.
- India is simultaneously a beneficiary of diversification and a target of foreign measures.

### Essential distinctions

- Tariff = customs duty; NTM = rule/standard/quota/licence/condition.
- Protectionism ≠ sanctions ≠ export controls.
- FTA ≠ customs union ≠ strategic framework.
- De-risking ≠ decoupling.
- Negotiation ≠ conclusion ≠ signature ≠ ratification ≠ entry into force.
- Entry into force ≠ utilisation ≠ impact.
- Announcement ≠ implementation; post-event correlation ≠ causation.

### WTO spine

- Established 1995; India member since 1 January 1995.
- Member-driven; decisions normally by consensus.
- Ministerial Conference → General Council/DSB/TPRB → councils/committees.
- Appellate Body unable to hear appeals; last member's term ended 30 November 2020.
- Reform: appellate restoration, transparency, development flexibility, public stockholding,
  subsidy discipline and predictable rules.
- Fisheries agreement: adopted 17 June 2022; in force 15 September 2025.

### Agreement-status recall

- EFTA TEPA: in force 1 October 2025.
- UK CETA/FTA: in force 15 July 2026.
- Oman CEPA: in force 1 June 2026.
- EU FTA: negotiations concluded 27 January 2026; not treated as signed/in force.
- New Zealand FTA: signed 27 April 2026; entry into force scheduled 20 October 2026.
- Chile CEPA: negotiations continuing as of 26 August 2026.
- US-India: framework for Interim Agreement; broader BTA talks continue.

### External-policy transmission

- Trade: tariffs, NTMs, remedies, RoO and market access.

- Climate: CBAM, embedded-emissions reporting and green-subsidy competition.
