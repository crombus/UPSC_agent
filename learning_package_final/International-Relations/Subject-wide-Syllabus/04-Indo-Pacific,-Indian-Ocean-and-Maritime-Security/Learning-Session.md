### SESSION 1 — FOUNDATION — Maritime vocabulary before strategy

#### VISUAL

```text
COAST ── TERRITORIAL SEA ── EEZ ───────────────── HIGH SEAS / ABNJ
          sovereignty       resource rights        beyond national jurisdiction
          + innocent        not full sovereignty   shared legal order
            passage

SLOC = route used by shipping
CHOKEPOINT = narrow passage concentrating route risk
MDA = knowing what is happening at sea
MARITIME SECURITY = protecting lawful use against coercive and non-traditional risks
```

#### EXPLANATION

- **Sea lane of communication (SLOC):** a shipping route connecting production, energy and markets.
- **Chokepoint:** a narrow passage through which concentrated traffic must move; disruption can impose delay, freight and insurance costs.
- **Maritime commons:** shared strategic space, especially the high seas, requiring cooperative governance. It is not a synonym for another state's territorial sea or exclusive economic zone.
- **Maritime domain awareness (MDA):** the capacity to detect, understand and share information about activity at sea affecting security, economy or environment.
- **Dark shipping:** vessels conceal activity, commonly by disabling or manipulating identification signals. Awareness does not itself supply enforcement capacity.
- **IUU fishing:** illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing; it is both a resource-governance and security concern.

✅ Tharoor records that more than half of India's trade traverses the Strait of Malacca. This establishes dependence; Geography owns the physical map and alternative-route detail.

#### EXAM LINK

Open with the chain **dependence → vulnerability → rules/awareness/partnerships → constrained outcome**, not with a list of warships.

#### TRAPS

- SLOC security is not only a naval problem; it is also an economic and diplomatic interest.
- MDA is not enforcement.
- EEZ is not full sovereign territory.
- Route disruption is not necessarily complete closure.

#### RECAP

India's maritime strategy begins with dependence on sea lanes and answers it through law, information and partnerships.

### SESSION 2 — FOUNDATION — SAGAR and MAHASAGAR: vision, not treaty

#### VISUAL

```text
SAGAR — Mauritius, 12 March 2015
Security and Growth for All in the Region
        │
        ├── security cooperation
        ├── development and capacity
        └── Indian Ocean focus
        │
        ▼
MAHASAGAR — Port Louis, 12 March 2025
Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions
        ├── trade for development
        ├── capacity building for sustainable growth
        ├── mutual security for a shared future
        └── technology sharing, concessional loans and grants
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ **SAGAR** is India's declared maritime vision for the Indian Ocean Region. ✅ **MAHASAGAR** was announced as a Global South vision “going beyond SAGAR”; the same announcement reaffirmed movement under SAGAR. Therefore:

1. MAHASAGAR extends scale and developmental content.
2. It does not repeal or replace SAGAR.
3. Neither label is a treaty, organisation, budget or completed project.
4. Their value lies in organising the choice of bilateral, regional and minilateral instruments.

#### EXAMPLE

The India–Maldives Joint Vision of 7 October 2024 is a partner-specific political instrument. SAGAR is the organising vision above it; implementation must be tested separately.

#### EXAM LINK

Use the status formula: **declared vision → selected instruments → dated implementation evidence → outcome and constraint**.

#### TRAPS

- Vision ≠ treaty.
- Announcement ≠ financing ≠ implementation ≠ outcome.
- “Going beyond” ≠ replacement.

#### RECAP

SAGAR supplies the Indian Ocean doctrine; MAHASAGAR broadens the developmental and Global South frame without changing its non-binding character.

### SESSION 3 — CORE — UNCLOS, maritime zones and India's legal-order posture

#### VISUAL

```text
UNCLOS RULE
│
├── territorial sea: up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline
│   └── ships of all states enjoy innocent passage, subject to Convention rules
├── EEZ: shall not extend beyond 200 nautical miles from the baseline
│   └── coastal-state resource jurisdiction; not complete sovereignty
├── navigation and overflight rules
└── dispute settlement, including Annex VII arbitration
        │
        ├── India signed UNCLOS: 10 December 1982
        ├── India ratified: 29 June 1995
        ├── India–Bangladesh award: 7 July 2014; India accepted it
        └── South China Sea statement: 12 July 2016
            freedom of navigation/overflight · peaceful settlement ·
            self-restraint · respect for UNCLOS; no pronouncement on merits
```

#### EXPLANATION

UNCLOS is the treaty framework beneath maritime diplomacy. India's use of Annex VII arbitration with Bangladesh demonstrates that “rules-based order” can mean accepting a binding process even when interests are involved. The 2016 statement on the South China Sea supported legal principles without deciding the merits of rival claims.

India's Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976 is the domestic anchor, but detailed statutory doctrine belongs to Polity.

#### EXAM LINK

In a legal-order answer, distinguish:

| Claim | Correct evidentiary test |
|---|---|
| India supports UNCLOS | ratification plus conduct |
| India accepted a specific award | identify the award and date |
| India accepted every third-party claim | not established |
| Freedom of navigation | legal principle; not identical to any particular naval operation |

#### TRAPS

- UNCLOS membership ≠ endorsement of every maritime claim.
- Innocent passage ≠ unrestricted conduct.
- EEZ ≠ territorial sea.

#### RECAP

India's strongest legal-order argument combines treaty participation, acceptance of dispute settlement and careful non-pronouncement on disputes to which it is not a party.

### SESSION 4 — CORE — BBNJ: the complete status chain

#### VISUAL

```text
ADOPTED ─────── OPENED FOR SIGNATURE ───── SIGNED BY INDIA ───── ENTRY INTO FORCE
19 Jun 2023      20 Sep 2023                25 Sep 2024           17 Jan 2026
                                                   │
                                                   └── India had not deposited
                                                       ratification as of
                                                       3 Aug 2026

SIGNATURE ≠ RATIFICATION ≠ PARTY STATUS
```

#### EXPLANATION

✅ The Agreement under UNCLOS on marine biodiversity of areas beyond national jurisdiction—BBNJ or the “High Seas Treaty”—entered into force after the required ratification threshold was reached. India signed but, on the canonical owner's dated verification, had not ratified. Thus India was a **signatory, not a party**.

⚠️ The distinction matters institutionally: participation in negotiation or signature does not automatically confer the rights of a party in implementation bodies such as the Conference of the Parties.

#### EXAM LINK

Use BBNJ to demonstrate legal-status discipline and the difference between normative support and completed consent.

#### TRAPS

- Adoption ≠ entry into force.
- Signature ≠ ratification.
- Treaty in force globally ≠ binding on every signatory.

#### RECAP

BBNJ is the cleanest maritime example of why every IR answer must name the exact legal stage.

### SESSION 5 — CORE — India's Indian Ocean institutional stack

#### VISUAL

```text
POLITICAL / INTERGOVERNMENTAL     NAVAL DIALOGUE          INFORMATION FUSION
IORA                              IONS                    IFC-IOR
23 members; 12 dialogue partners  voluntary; 2008         Indian Navy; Gurugram; 2018
Secretariat: Ebène, Mauritius     littoral navies         maritime-domain awareness
India chair: 2025–27              India chair: 20 Feb 2026

                         CHAIRSHIP ≠ CONTROL
```

#### EXPLANATION

- **IORA** is a regional intergovernmental organisation for Indian Ocean cooperation.
- **IONS** is a voluntary naval-cooperation symposium, not an intergovernmental treaty alliance.
- **IFC-IOR** is an information-sharing centre hosted by the Indian Navy. The canonical owner records that in November 2025 it collaborated with 25 partner countries and hosted liaison officers from 15.
- **ReCAAP** supports regional cooperation against piracy and armed robbery against ships.
- **Sagar Prahari Bal**, raised in 2009, protects naval bases and vulnerable points. Its operational employment belongs to Internal Security/Defence.
- **MILAN** originated at Port Blair in 1995; origin must not be confused with every edition's venue. ✅ MILAN 2024 was held at Visakhapatnam from 19–27 February 2024. 📰 PIB records MILAN 2026 at Visakhapatnam from 15–25 February 2026. The exercise is evidence of expanding naval diplomacy and interoperability, not a collective-defence commitment.
- The **Indian Ocean Conference** is a track-1.5 convening, not an intergovernmental decision-making body. The canonical owner records its 8th edition at Muscat on 16 February 2025 and 9th edition at Port Louis on 10 April 2026.

#### EXAMPLE

India can chair IORA, chair IONS and host IFC-IOR simultaneously, yet still lack authority to dictate other members' policies. These instruments supply convening, dialogue and awareness—not a regional command.

#### EXAM LINK

Always name each mechanism's function. “India uses IORA, IONS and IFC-IOR” scores little unless the answer differentiates political dialogue, naval cooperation and information fusion.

#### TRAPS

- IORA, IONS and IFC-IOR are not interchangeable.
- Host status ≠ ownership of shared data.
- Chairship ≠ decision authority.
- Information-sharing ≠ interception or prosecution.

#### RECAP

The stack progresses from political dialogue to naval networking to shared awareness, while coercive action remains nationally controlled.

#### EXPLANATION

IPOI converts broad maritime vision into voluntary, partner-led cooperation across seven pillars. It is not a treaty, alliance or centralised funding body. India's emphasis on ASEAN centrality signals an inclusive architecture wider than a China-containment coalition.

Broad forums create legitimacy and participation; smaller groups may act faster. India therefore layers ASEAN-centred institutions with minilateral and bilateral tools.

- IPOI ≠ Quad programme.
- EAS and ARF are not enforcement bodies.

India's Indo-Pacific architecture is deliberately plural: ASEAN-centred legitimacy plus flexible, issue-specific cooperation.

### SESSION 9 — CORE — IPMDA: how the SAGAR–Quad bridge actually works

#### VISUAL

```text
IPMDA — Quad Tokyo Summit, 24 May 2022
commercially available + unclassified data
AIS + commercial radio-frequency data
                    │
                    ▼
EXISTING REGIONAL NODES — not a new command
├── IFC-IOR, Gurugram
├── Information Fusion Centre, Singapore
├── Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency, Solomon Islands
└── Pacific Fusion Centre, Vanuatu
                    │
                    ▼
near-real-time integrated maritime picture
dark shipping · IUU fishing · trafficking · HADR/climate support
                    │
          awareness, not enforcement
```

#### EXPLANATION

IPMDA was announced by Quad leaders in 2022 to improve maritime-domain awareness across the Pacific Islands, Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean. Its design is distributive: commercial, unclassified data can be shared through existing regional centres without creating a classified joint command.

**Why it bridges:** India's IFC-IOR—an institution aligned with SAGAR's awareness objective—is an IPMDA delivery node. A Quad initiative therefore uses Indian institutional capacity to provide a regional public good.

**Why the bridge is incomplete:** SAGAR is India's unilateral Indian Ocean vision; Quad is a four-state, wider Indo-Pacific coalition. IPMDA connects an institution and an operating function more than it fuses the two doctrines. Technology/finance asymmetry, recipient enforcement capacity, interoperability and regional hedging limit outcomes.

#### 2026 STATUS DISTINCTION

At the 11th Quad Foreign Ministers' Meeting in New Delhi on 26 May 2026, the canonical owner records:

- launch of the **Indo-Pacific Maritime Surveillance Collaboration (IPMSC)**, initially focused on the Indian Ocean through expert exchanges and tabletop exercises; and
- separate expansion of **IPMDA** toward a comprehensive Common Operating Picture.
- India's undertaking to host the next Quad-at-Sea Ship Observer Mission for interoperability and knowledge-sharing against unlawful maritime activity.

IPMDA and IPMSC are related but not interchangeable.

#### EXAM LINK

For the 2026 direct PYQ: **define → show structural bridge through IFC-IOR → distinguish scopes → assess capability and political limits → give qualified verdict**.

#### TRAPS

- IPMDA ≠ treaty, alliance or joint command.
- It supports existing fusion centres; it did not create all of them.
- Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency (Solomon Islands) ≠ Pacific Fusion Centre (Vanuatu).
- Data picture ≠ patrol vessel, prosecution or deterrent outcome.
- IPMDA (2022) ≠ IPMSC (2026).

#### RECAP

IPMDA is a real bridge at the institutional and data-sharing level, but not a merger of SAGAR and Quad strategy.

### SESSION 10 — CORE — AUKUS and layered Indo-Pacific security

#### VISUAL

```text
AUKUS — announced 15 September 2021
Australia · United Kingdom · United States
│
├── PILLAR / FIRST INITIATIVE
│   Australia to acquire conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines
│
└── ADVANCED CAPABILITIES
    cyber · AI · quantum · additional undersea capabilities

March 2023 pathway
rotation from as early as 2027 → possible Virginia-class sale from early 2030s,
subject to US approval → later Australian-built SSN-AUKUS
```

#### EXPLANATION

AUKUS is an enhanced trilateral security partnership, distinct from Quad. Its submarine pathway deepens deterrence and technology integration but depends on industrial capacity, approvals and sustained political commitment.

India is not an AUKUS member; its Quad participation is a separate, non-treaty route.

Australia remains a non-nuclear-weapon state. The canonical owner records that an Article 14 safeguards arrangement with the IAEA for naval nuclear propulsion was still under negotiation in November 2025. That status proves neither violation nor complete resolution of non-proliferation concerns.

Australia's cancellation of the French Naval Group programme produced a EUR 555 million settlement in June 2022, illustrating that capability gains can carry alliance-management costs.

#### EXAM LINK

The 2021 PYQ asks whether AUKUS will supersede existing partnerships. The defensible answer is “unlikely”: it is capability-specific and coexists with ASEAN-centred forums, Quad, bilateral alliances and regional institutions.

#### TRAPS

- Nuclear-powered ≠ nuclear-armed.
- AUKUS ≠ Quad.
- Announced pathway ≠ delivered submarine fleet.
- Negotiating safeguards ≠ settled compliance outcome.

#### RECAP

AUKUS intensifies undersea capability and strategic competition but adds a layer; it does not erase the region's other institutions.

COMCASA 2018 → secure communications S-400 contract

#### EXPLANATION

India's simultaneous partnerships illustrate strategic autonomy. US agreements can improve logistics, communications and geospatial exchange; Russian-origin systems remain important to deterrence and readiness. The stability effect is conditional:

- **Negative:** supplier concentration, sanctions exposure, competing-bloc perceptions and crisis escalation.

#### RECAP

Defence partnerships support stability only when capability gains are combined with diversification, crisis communication, law and retained decision autonomy.

### SESSION 12 — CORE — IMO: global shipping rules, safety and environment

#### VISUAL

```text
IMO CONVENTION: adopted 1948 → in force 1958
UN specialised agency for global shipping standards
│
├── ASSEMBLY: all members
├── COUNCIL: executive organ
└── COMMITTEES: MSC · MEPC · Legal · Technical Cooperation · Facilitation

RULES
├── SOLAS → safety of life at sea
├── SOLAS Chapter XI-2 + ISPS Code → ship/port-facility security
├── SUA treaties (1988; revised 2005) → unlawful acts against ships/platforms
├── MARPOL → ship-source pollution
├── Ballast Water Management Convention → in force 2017
├── Hong Kong ship-recycling Convention → in force 26 June 2025
└── sulphur limit outside ECAs → 0.50% m/m from 1 January 2020
```

#### EXPLANATION

IMO converts transboundary shipping risks into common technical and legal standards. Safety rules, pollution controls, ballast-water governance and ship recycling show the organisation's regulatory breadth.

Its **maritime-security limb** is distinct from ordinary navigational safety. ✅ SOLAS Chapter XI-2 contains special measures to enhance maritime security and gives effect to the **International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code**. For SOLAS parties, the Code is mandatory: Part A contains requirements for governments, port authorities and shipping companies, while Part B provides non-mandatory guidance. ✅ The **SUA treaties**, adopted in 1988 and comprehensively revised in 2005, provide the international legal framework for action against unlawful acts against ships and fixed platforms, including forcible seizure, violence against persons aboard and placement of destructive devices.

The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy aims at net-zero emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, with 2030 and 2040 checkpoints. The proposed Net-Zero Framework was approved at committee level but **not adopted** in October 2025; talks were adjourned. Committee approval must not be upgraded to adoption.

India was elected to IMO Council Category (b) for the 2026–27 biennium, representing states with a major interest in international seaborne trade. Membership gives access to rule-shaping, not guaranteed outcomes.

#### EXAM LINK

Answer the 2023 PYQ through **mandate → safety rules → environmental rules → security rules (SOLAS XI-2/ISPS/SUA) → implementation mechanism → enforcement/decarbonisation limits**.

#### TRAPS

- IMO writes standards; flag, port and coastal states implement them.
- ISPS Part A is mandatory for SOLAS parties; Part B is guidance.
- Adoption of a security instrument does not remove national implementation, capacity, jurisdiction and enforcement gaps.
- Committee approval ≠ formal adoption.
- Council membership ≠ control.

#### RECAP

IMO's significance is common rule-making for a mobile global industry across safety, environment and security; its weakness is uneven national implementation, enforcement capacity and contested burden-sharing.

### SESSION 13 — CORE — Maldives, island-state agency and international competition

#### VISUAL

```text
MALDIVES' LOCATION
Eight Degree Channel: Minicoy ↔ northern Maldives
One-and-a-Half Degree Channel: through the Maldivian archipelago
        │
        ├── west–east routes: Gulf/Africa ↔ India ↔ East/Southeast Asia
        ├── India's interest: trade/energy continuity · MDA · regional stability
        ├── Maldives' interest: development · finance · autonomy · diversified partners
        └── external context: competing infrastructure, access and basing influence
                    │
                    ▼
INDIA–MALDIVES JOINT VISION
7 October 2024
Comprehensive Economic and Maritime Security Partnership
                    │
                    ▼
political commitment + specified facilities/fields
≠ treaty ≠ automatic drawdown ≠ delivered outcome
```

#### EXPLANATION

The 2024 direct PYQ makes Maldives a maritime owner, not merely a neighbourhood case. The **Eight Degree Channel** separates Minicoy/Lakshadweep from northern Maldives, while the **One-and-a-Half Degree Channel** passes through the Maldivian archipelago. Together with surrounding SLOCs, this location places Maldives beside west–east shipping connecting Gulf and African energy sources with India and East/Southeast Asian markets. The mechanism is geographic: political access, surveillance cooperation or instability near these channels affects awareness and resilience along trade and energy routes before it affects the wider security balance.

The Joint Vision is the dated bilateral instrument. It records support of USD 400 million and INR 30 billion as a currency-swap arrangement; this is an arranged facility, not proof of drawdown. FTA Terms of Reference signed and negotiations launched on 25 July 2025 are not a concluded FTA.

The advanced owner also records an INR 4,850 crore Line of Credit on 25 July 2025. It is a dated financing instrument, not by itself proof that every financed activity has been completed.

⚠️ **Island-state agency** is essential. Maldives, Mauritius, Seychelles and Sri Lanka are not passive squares on an India–China chessboard. They select partners according to domestic politics, finance and security needs.

External-power facilities such as Djibouti- and Gwadar-type bases are a structural competitive context. India manages rather than eliminates that competition.

#### EXAM LINK

For Maldives: **location and flows → bilateral instrument → maritime/security effect → international competition → island-state agency → calibrated conclusion**.

#### TRAPS

- Maldives' importance ≠ bilateral sentiment alone.
- Channel geography is the mechanism linking location to trade and energy flows; do not merely name the channels.
- Vision document ≠ binding treaty.
- Currency-swap arrangement ≠ proven utilisation.
- Smaller state ≠ state without agency.
- “Net security provider” is a commentary label, not asserted here as settled official doctrine.

#### RECAP

India's maritime influence depends on trusted, useful partnerships—not on treating island states as exclusive spheres of influence.

### SESSION 14 — CORE — Blue economy, connectivity and coastal resilience: the security–growth mechanism

#### VISUAL

```text
HEALTHY OCEAN / COAST
fisheries · habitats · coastal protection
          │
          ├── sustainable resources → food + coastal/island livelihoods
          ├── ports/connectivity → trade + services + regional access
          ├── resilient ecosystems/infrastructure → lower climate/disaster disruption
          └── lawful, visible use → less IUU extraction + greater partner trust
                                  │
                                  ▼
                 GROWTH SUPPORTS SECURITY CAPACITY
                 SECURITY PROTECTS SUSTAINABLE GROWTH
```

#### EXPLANATION

The **blue economy** is not simply extracting more value from the sea. In this topic it means international cooperation for sustainable use of fisheries and marine resources, port and maritime connectivity, coastal and island livelihoods, ecosystem resilience and reduced climate/disaster risk. It is the growth half of SAGAR's security–growth bargain.

The mechanism has four links:

1. **Sustainable fisheries/resources:** cooperative rules, science and action against IUU fishing protect stocks, food security and livelihoods.
2. **Ports and connectivity:** reliable maritime transport and transparent infrastructure connect island/littoral economies to markets and services.
3. **Coastal resilience:** reefs, mangroves and resilient infrastructure reduce exposure of settlements, ports and tourism/fishing livelihoods to climate and disaster shocks.
4. **Security–growth feedback:** MDA and lawful seas protect economic activity; inclusive growth and trusted capacity-building reduce fragility and make security partnerships more legitimate.

IPOI already contains the relevant bridge: maritime ecology, maritime resources, disaster-risk reduction, capacity building, and trade/connectivity/maritime transport. IORA and bilateral island partnerships provide diplomatic platforms; MAHASAGAR adds development, technology and concessional-finance language. Each remains an instrument or vision whose delivery must be verified.

#### TRADE-OFFS

| Choice | Gain | Risk requiring qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Port/connectivity projects | market access, logistics, services | debt/viability, habitat damage, displacement, dual-use or access perceptions |
| Fisheries expansion | food, exports, livelihoods | overfishing, IUU activity, unequal gains |
| Hard coastal infrastructure | asset protection | ecological damage or risk transfer along the coast |
| Conservation/resilience | natural buffers, long-run livelihoods | short-run livelihood and finance costs if communities are excluded |
| Security-led monitoring | lawful use and MDA | securitising ordinary fishing/livelihood activity |

#### FIREWALLS

- **Economy:** port finance, trade volumes, blue-economy accounting and project viability.
- **Environment:** fisheries science, carrying capacity, pollution law and ecosystem-restoration mechanics.
- **Geography:** coastal processes, reefs, mangroves, erosion and hazard maps.
- **Disaster Management:** preparedness, evacuation, relief, recovery and resilient-reconstruction operations.
- **IR retains:** international rules, development partnerships, financing status, partner agency, connectivity diplomacy and the security consequences of sustainable or unsustainable use.

#### EXAM LINK

Use the chain **ecosystem/resource base → livelihood/connectivity benefit → resilience/security mechanism → trade-off → partner-owned delivery**. Do not write “blue economy” as a decorative synonym for ports.

#### TRAPS

- Blue economy ≠ maximum extraction or port construction alone.
- Connectivity announcement ≠ viable or operating corridor/port.
- Conservation ≠ resilience if coastal communities bear uncompensated costs.
- Security monitoring must distinguish IUU activity from lawful livelihoods.

#### RECAP

SAGAR's security and growth are mutually reinforcing only when connectivity is viable, resources are sustainable, ecosystems reduce risk and coastal communities share the gains.

### SESSION 15 — CORE — Non-traditional threats, HADR and the operations firewall

#### VISUAL

```text
MARITIME DIPLOMACY (IR)                  OPERATIONS (route out)
information-sharing                      coastal policing / interception
exercise and readiness partnerships      naval deployment and tactics
anti-piracy agreements                    prosecution and port security
HADR coordination diplomacy              evacuation, relief logistics,
capacity building                         rehabilitation and recovery
        │                                         │
        └────────── cooperation interface ────────┘
```

#### EXPLANATION

Piracy, maritime terrorism, trafficking, IUU fishing, environmental harm and disasters require cooperation because ships, information and effects cross borders. The 26/11 attackers' maritime approach using a hijacked fishing vessel sharpened India's security consciousness.

IR asks: Which partner, agreement, fusion centre, exercise or diplomatic framework enables cooperation? Internal Security asks: How are the coast, ports, intelligence and forces secured? Disaster Management asks: How are evacuation, logistics, relief, rehabilitation and recovery executed?

#### EXAM LINK

Use HADR as evidence of trust and public-good diplomacy, then stop before operational detail displaces the IR demand.

#### TRAPS

- HADR diplomacy ≠ HADR operational cycle.
- Anti-piracy cooperation ≠ operational doctrine.
- Threat awareness ≠ neutralisation.

#### RECAP

The topic owns the international cooperation architecture; specialist subjects own execution.

### SESSION 16 — CORE SYNTHESIS — Status discipline and the answer spine

#### VISUAL

```text
LEGAL STATUS TRACK                       DELIVERY TRACK
declaration                              announced
   ↓                                     ↓
text: MoU/framework/treaty               approved / financed
   ↓                                     ↓
signature / ratification as applicable   implemented
   ↓                                     ↓
entry into force                         operating
                                         ↓
                                         output → outcome

NEVER JUMP TRACKS:
signed treaty ≠ implemented project · summit declaration ≠ security outcome
```

#### EXPLANATION

Use eight steps:

1. State India's precise interest.
2. Identify the geography/actor and the partner's own incentive.
3. Name the engagement level—bilateral, regional, minilateral or multilateral.
4. Cite one verified instrument with exact date and status.
5. Explain the mechanism: rules, awareness, capability, finance or convening.
6. State the result actually evidenced.
7. Identify the constraint: capacity, asymmetry, island-state agency, rivalry or implementation.
8. Conclude with a graded judgement, not a slogan.

#### EXAMPLE

“IPMDA bridges SAGAR and Quad” becomes: SAGAR vision → IFC-IOR institution → IPMDA data feed → improved shared picture → no automatic enforcement → qualified institutional bridge.

#### TRAPS

Vision ≠ declaration ≠ agreement ≠ treaty; signature ≠ ratification; chairship ≠ control; exercise ≠ alliance; awareness ≠ enforcement; participation ≠ membership; route disruption ≠ closure; announcement ≠ outcome.

#### RECAP

The scoring hinge is not how many institutions an answer names, but whether it assigns each the correct function, legal status and evidenced result.

## OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

### A1. The contested-order thesis

```text
TRADE/ENERGY DEPENDENCE
        ↓
institutional layering + bilateral visions + minilateral capacity
        ↓
external-power basing + island-state agency + uneven capability
        ↓
CONTESTED ORDER
India has influence and convening capacity, not uncontested regional control
```

⚠️ The Indian Ocean is not a settled Indian sphere of influence. India can build public goods and trusted partnerships, but island states retain agency and external powers retain structural presence.

### A2. Public good and soft balancing can coexist

IPMDA may provide genuine anti-IUU-fishing, anti-trafficking, HADR and dark-vessel awareness while also being perceived as a US-anchored soft-balancing architecture. These readings are not mutually exclusive. A mature answer reports the dual-use political perception without converting it into a proven military command.

### A3. ASEAN hedging

Regional recipients can accept useful data while avoiding a public appearance of choosing a bloc. This is not proof that ASEAN collectively endorses Quad strategy; it is a partner-level hedging pattern.

### A4. Technology and enforcement dependency

Commercial AIS/radio-frequency and possible radar/analytics fusion can improve detection, but:

- sensor and analytics dependence can create a new asymmetry;
- control of a Common Operating Picture is a governance question;
- a littoral state may receive information without vessels, aircraft, fuel, law or trained personnel to act;
- data-sharing sustainability must be verified beyond an announced funding cycle.

The original 2022 IPMDA investment was framed over five years. The 2026 IPMSC/COP announcement is a deepening step, not proof of post-cycle funding or completed integration.

### A5. Island-state agency as strategic autonomy at smaller scale

Maldives and other island states diversify partners to preserve room for manoeuvre, just as India seeks strategic autonomy among major powers. Their choices reflect development finance, domestic politics and security needs; they are not reducible to external manipulation.

### A6. “Net security provider” is not a settled status

The phrase can be used as a strategic-studies aspiration only with qualification. It is not treated here as a formally declared, completed regional status. Prefer “maritime-security partner” unless a dated official source supports stronger wording.

### A7. Institutional layering: gain and limit

| Layer | Gain | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| IORA | legitimacy, political agenda | consensus and slow delivery |
| IONS | navy-to-navy dialogue | voluntary, no command authority |
| IFC-IOR/IPMDA | shared picture | information without enforcement |
| bilateral vision | partner-specific commitments | political, not self-executing |
| Quad/Malabar | capability and interoperability | no collective-defence obligation |
| AUKUS | deep undersea technology integration | narrow membership, industrial and diplomatic costs |
| IMO/UNCLOS/BBNJ | common rules and legal legitimacy | consent and national implementation gaps |

### A8. External-power competition

Djibouti- and Gwadar-type facilities demonstrate that basing competition is structural. India's response—institutions, partnerships, legal positioning and capability—can manage risk but cannot abolish competing presence.

Red Sea/Bab-el-Mandeb disruption can divert Asia–Europe shipping around the Cape of Good Hope and raise time, freight and insurance costs. Quantify only with dated shipping evidence; never convert disruption into an unverified claim of complete closure.

### A9. Advanced answer framework

**Thesis:** India manages a contested maritime commons by converting sea-lane dependence into rules, awareness, capability and partner choice; the approach improves influence but remains bounded by technology dependence, external-power competition, island-state agency and the gap between information and enforcement.

**For a critical answer:**

1. define the precise instrument;
2. locate it in the institutional stack;
3. identify the mechanism;
4. state one evidenced gain;
5. price capability and political constraints;
6. distinguish announcement from outcome;
7. close with a conditional judgement.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### Core thesis

- Maritime engagement converts dependence on trade and energy routes into a strategy of **law + awareness + capability + partnership**.
- The regional order is contested; India's influence is real but not exclusive or guaranteed.

### Concept grid

- **SLOC:** shipping route.
- **Chokepoint:** narrow passage concentrating risk.
- **Maritime commons:** shared space requiring cooperative governance; not another state's territorial sea/EEZ.
- **MDA:** detect, understand and share relevant maritime activity.
- **Awareness ≠ enforcement.**
- **Island-state agency:** smaller partners choose among external offers for their own interests.

### Exact doctrine anchors

- **SAGAR:** Security and Growth for All in the Region; Mauritius, **12 March 2015**; declared vision.
- **MAHASAGAR:** Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions; Port Louis, **12 March 2025**; extension, not replacement; declared vision.
- **IPOI:** announced at 14th EAS, Bangkok, **4 November 2019**; seven voluntary pillars.

### Legal order

- UNCLOS: India signed **10 December 1982**, ratified **29 June 1995**.
- Territorial sea: up to **12 nautical miles**; innocent passage for ships of all states under the Convention.
- EEZ: not beyond **200 nautical miles** from relevant baselines.
- India–Bangladesh Annex VII award: **7 July 2014**, accepted by India.
- South China Sea statement: **12 July 2016**; navigation/overflight, peaceful settlement, self-restraint, UNCLOS; no merits pronouncement.
- BBNJ: adopted **19 June 2023**; opened **20 September 2023**; India signed **25 September 2024**; in force **17 January 2026**; India had not ratified as of **3 August 2026**.
- The 60th ratification was deposited on **19 September 2025**, triggering entry into force after the treaty's waiting period.

### Institutional stack

- **IORA:** intergovernmental; 23 members, 12 dialogue partners; Secretariat at Ebène; India chair 2025–27.
- **IONS:** voluntary naval symposium; 2008; India chair from 20 February 2026.
- **IFC-IOR:** Indian Navy, Gurugram, 2018; information fusion.
- **ReCAAP:** piracy/armed-robbery cooperation.
- **Sagar Prahari Bal:** 2009; domestic operational capacity—route details to Internal Security.
- **MILAN:** originated at Port Blair in 1995; MILAN 2024 and MILAN 2026 were held at Visakhapatnam (19–27 February 2024 and 15–25 February 2026 respectively).
- **Indian Ocean Conference:** track-1.5 convening; Muscat 16 February 2025 and Port Louis 10 April 2026 editions in the canonical record.
- **CSC:** at the seventh NSA-level meeting on 20 November 2025, Seychelles was welcomed as a full member; Malaysia was a guest.

### Indo-Pacific architecture

- ASEAN centrality + EAS + ARF provide broad legitimacy.
- Quad is a non-treaty minilateral; Malabar supplies interoperability, not collective defence.
- AUKUS: Australia–UK–US, 15 September 2021; nuclear-powered, conventionally armed submarine pathway plus advanced capabilities; not Quad.

### IPMDA answer card

- Launched: Quad Tokyo Summit, **24 May 2022**.
- Inputs: commercially available, unclassified AIS/radio-frequency data.
- Nodes: IFC-IOR; Singapore IFC; Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency (Solomon Islands); Pacific Fusion Centre (Vanuatu).
- Bridge: Quad data initiative uses India's IFC-IOR.
- Limits: SAGAR/Quad scope difference; technology/finance asymmetry; recipient patrol capacity; hedging; awareness–enforcement gap.
- 26 May 2026: IPMSC launched and IPMDA/COP expansion announced; initiatives related, not identical.
- India was to host the next Quad-at-Sea Ship Observer Mission; an announced hosting role is not a completed operational outcome.

### IMO answer card

- Convention adopted 1948; in force 1958.
- SOLAS—safety; SOLAS XI-2 + ISPS Code—ship/port-facility security; SUA treaties—unlawful acts against ships/fixed platforms; MARPOL—pollution; Ballast Water Convention—in force 2017; Hong Kong ship-recycling Convention—in force 26 June 2025.
- ISPS Part A is mandatory for SOLAS parties; Part B is non-mandatory guidance.
- Global sulphur limit outside emission-control areas: 0.50% m/m from 1 January 2020.
- 2023 GHG Strategy: net-zero by or around 2050 with 2030/2040 checkpoints.
- Net-Zero Framework: committee-approved, not adopted in October 2025.

### Maldives answer card

- Location connects the question to trade/energy SLOCs, MDA and stability.
- Eight Degree Channel separates Minicoy from northern Maldives; One-and-a-Half Degree Channel passes through the archipelago. Their SLOC setting links Gulf/African energy and west–east commerce with India and East/Southeast Asia.
- Joint Vision: **7 October 2024**; political instrument, not treaty.
- Currency-swap support recorded: USD 400 million + INR 30 billion; facility ≠ drawdown.
- FTA Terms of Reference/negotiations: 25 July 2025; negotiation ≠ concluded FTA.
- INR 4,850 crore Line of Credit recorded on 25 July 2025; financing instrument ≠ completed delivery.
- Always include island-state agency and international competition.

### Blue economy, connectivity and coastal resilience

- Blue economy = sustainable marine resources/fisheries + ports/connectivity + coastal livelihoods + ecosystem/climate resilience; not extraction or port-building alone.
- Mechanism: healthy resource base → livelihoods/food → viable connectivity → resilience to disruption → legitimate security cooperation.
- Security protects lawful economic use; inclusive growth finances capacity and reduces fragility.
- IPOI bridge: ecology, resources, capacity, disaster-risk reduction and maritime transport/connectivity.
- Trade-offs: port viability/debt/dual-use perception; habitat and livelihood displacement; overfishing/IUU; hard infrastructure versus ecosystem buffers; monitoring versus securitisation of lawful fishing.
- Detailed economics → Economy; ecosystem science → Environment; coastal processes → Geography; disaster operations → Disaster Management.

### Firewalls

- Geography: route map and chokepoint physical detail.
- Economy: freight, insurance, energy-price and trade mechanics.
- Topic 10: full Quad/grouping taxonomy.
- Topic 03: bilateral major-power defence relationship depth.
- Internal Security/Defence: coastal security, naval and anti-piracy operations.

- IR retains diplomacy, status, institutions, partner incentives and regional effects.
