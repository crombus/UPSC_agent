### PYQ 1 — 2020 GS-II Q20 · Discuss · 15 marks · 250 words

**Audited neutral demand:** Indo-US and Indo-Russian defence deals and their contribution to Indo-Pacific stability.

**Model (approx. 230 words):** India’s simultaneous US and Russian defence partnerships may support stability by improving capability and deterring opportunism, but procurement alone cannot settle the regional security dilemma. With the United States, LEMOA (2016) facilitates reciprocal logistics, COMCASA (2018) secure communications and BECA (2020) geospatial information. Exercise Malabar, with Japan and regular Australian participation from 2020, provides practice in working across navies. Together these can improve Indian maritime awareness and disaster response; none confers a NATO-style obligation or automatic foreign basing.

Russia serves a different requirement: Su-30MKI licensed production and BrahMos cooperation contribute to an installed capability base, while the S-400 contract concerns air defence. This diversity of suppliers may reduce exclusive leverage over India and support strategic autonomy. However, Russian-origin spares dependence can constrain readiness, and potential third-country sanctions pressure complicates procurement. Conversely, US technology-transfer limits and unequal treaty ties among Quad states, noted by Tharoor, prevent easy claims that maritime exercises form one integrated alliance. AUKUS and other regional capability initiatives can reinforce deterrence yet sharpen rival threat perceptions.

The net effect therefore depends on crisis communication, compliance with UNCLOS and independent Indian maritime capacity, not simply the number of signed instruments. India should use interoperability for shared safety and maintain diplomatic channels with neighbours, while diversifying inputs without pretending to replace legacy Russian platforms instantly. Defence cooperation contributes to stability when it reduces vulnerability without forcing littorals into exclusive blocs.

**Why this earns marks:** Discusses both partners through named military enablers and platform examples, then weighs escalation, legal status and readiness limits.

### PYQ 2 — 2021 GS-II Q20 · Discuss strength and impact · 15 marks · 250 words

**Audited neutral demand:** AUKUS in the Indo-Pacific and its impact on existing regional partnerships. The local printed opening clause is truncated; none is supplied here. Later roadmap details are retrospective enrichments, not 2021-exam-time facts.

**Model (approx. 235 words):** AUKUS, announced by Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States in September 2021, strengthens their long-run undersea and advanced-technology cooperation. Its submarine component supports Australia’s acquisition of **conventionally armed, nuclear-powered** boats; the additional track encompasses cyber, AI and quantum capabilities. Nuclear propulsion is not nuclear weapons, and India is not an AUKUS member.

Its strength lies in pooling industrial and technological resources with substantial deterrent potential. But potential is not delivered capability: the March 2023 pathway subsequently envisaged UK–US rotations from as early as 2027, possible Virginia-class sales from the early 2030s subject to approval, and later Australian-built SSN-AUKUS. These are conditional stages, not operational boats at the date of the original question. ### PYQ 3 — 2023 GS-II Q20 · Discuss · 15 marks · 250 words

**Audited neutral demand:** IMO’s role in protecting the environment and maritime safety.

**Model (approx. 225 words):** The International Maritime Organization is the UN specialised agency that turns common shipping risks into global technical standards; it is not a maritime police force. These instruments matter because ships routinely cross jurisdictions; national measures alone can shift harm to less regulated flags or ports.

India’s Council Category (b) election for 2026–27 offers a voice for major seaborne-trade interests, not guaranteed rule-making victory.

Unlike the UNCLOS BBNJ Agreement, which addresses biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction, IMO regulation primarily governs ship operations. Its contribution is indispensable standard-setting; the remaining challenge is implementation and equitable financing, not a lack of rule-making subjects.

**Why this earns marks:** Separates safety, multiple pollution channels and climate adoption status, then tests real compliance and India's role.
