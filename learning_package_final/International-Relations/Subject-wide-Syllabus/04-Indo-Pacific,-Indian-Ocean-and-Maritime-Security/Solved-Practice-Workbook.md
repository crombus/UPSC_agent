### Q1. Which statement best defines this topic's ownership?

A. Maritime diplomacy, law, institutions, partner agency and regional effects.
B. It includes naval tactics, force structure and coastal-policing procedure as core IR content.
C. It is limited to the physical geography of the Indian Ocean.
D. It treats maritime trade primarily as a sectoral economic-statistics question.
**Answer: A**

IR owns the external-policy purpose, instrument and consequence. Geography owns physical-route detail; Internal Security/Defence owns operations; Economy owns price and trade mechanics. The other options erase these firewalls.

### Q2. A sea lane of communication is best understood as:

A. the outer boundary of a coastal state's territorial sea.
B. a shipping route connecting production, energy and markets.
C. a naval alliance's compulsory patrol area.
D. a zone outside the treaty rules governing maritime conduct.
**Answer: B**

A SLOC is a route, while a chokepoint is a narrow passage concentrating route risk. It is neither a maritime-zone boundary nor an alliance jurisdiction.

### Q3. Consider the following statements.

1. SAGAR was articulated in Mauritius on 12 March 2015.
2. MAHASAGAR was announced at Port Louis on 12 March 2025.
3. MAHASAGAR replaced SAGAR as a binding regional treaty.

Which are correct?

A. 1 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 2 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: C**

The dates and expansions are source-grounded. Both are declared visions; the 2025 announcement reaffirmed movement under SAGAR, so replacement and treaty status are false.

### Q4. Which status statement is correct?

A. A declared vision creates treaty obligations once announced by a head of government.
B. A joint vision establishes implementation of the projects it lists.
C. “Going beyond” necessarily repeals the earlier doctrine.
D. SAGAR and MAHASAGAR organise instrument choice but are not treaties or completed outcomes.

**Answer: D**

The correct chain is vision → instrument → implementation evidence → outcome. The other options collapse separate legal and delivery stages.

### Q5. With reference to UNCLOS, which combination is correct?

A. Territorial sea up to 12 nautical miles; EEZ not beyond 200 nautical miles from the relevant baseline.

D. UNCLOS leaves the breadth of both zones to coastal-state practice.
**Answer: A**

These are the exact distinctions tested in the 2022 objective PYQ. The EEZ grants specified resource jurisdiction, not complete territorial sovereignty.

### Q6. Which statement about innocent passage is correct?

A. It is reserved for vessels registered in coastal states.
B. All states' ships enjoy it, subject to Convention rules.
C. It converts the territorial sea into high seas.
D. It displaces the coastal state’s regulatory competence in the territorial sea.
**Answer: B**

UNCLOS recognises innocent passage for ships of all states. It is a regulated passage right, not a transfer of sovereignty or unrestricted conduct.

#### PYQ 2 · 2023 · Mains GS-II · Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · DIRECT

> “‘Sea is an important Component of the Cosmos’ Discuss in the light of the above statement the role of the IMO (International Maritime Organisation) in protecting environment and enhancing maritime safety and security.”

**OCR note:** The quotation, capitalisation and wording above follow the readable official English export. The audited ledger supplies the 250-word limit from the paper's instruction block.

**Demand decoding:** *Discuss* requires both contribution and limitation. The answer must cover environment **and** safety/security, with named IMO instruments.

**Model answer (within 250 words).**

The sea is a connected ecological and commercial system; risks created by ships therefore require rules wider than any one coastal jurisdiction. The International Maritime Organization supplies that common regulatory framework.

For **safety**, SOLAS sets standards for ship construction, equipment and operation. For **security**, SOLAS Chapter XI-2 contains special measures and the ISPS Code: mandatory Part A sets requirements for governments, port authorities and shipping companies, while Part B gives guidance. The SUA treaties, adopted in 1988 and revised in 2005, provide a legal framework against forcible seizure, violence aboard ships and destructive devices affecting ships or fixed platforms.

For **environmental protection**, MARPOL regulates ship-source pollution; the Ballast Water Management Convention addresses invasive species; and the Hong Kong Convention governs ship recycling. The 0.50% global sulphur limit outside emission-control areas from 1 January 2020 demonstrates common technical rule-making. The 2023 GHG Strategy adds a net-zero-by-or-around-2050 direction, though the proposed Net-Zero Framework was not adopted in October 2025.

Limits remain national: flag, port and coastal states implement and enforce; capacity, jurisdiction, compliance and finance vary. IMO therefore supplies indispensable common standards and cooperation, but security and environmental outcomes depend on domestic execution.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately answers environment, safety and security; names SOLAS XI-2, ISPS and SUA; explains mandatory/guidance status; and identifies implementation and enforcement limits.

#### PYQ 3 · 2024 · Mains GS-II · Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · DIRECT

> “Discuss the geopolitical and geostrategic importance of Maldives for India with a focus on global trade and energy flows. Further also discuss how this relationship affects India's maritime security and regional stability amidst international competition?”

**OCR note:** The official export prints “stabiltiy”; it is transparently normalised to “stability”. Spacing is regularised; no missing content is reconstructed.

**Chronology note:** The Joint Vision cited in the updated solution below was issued on 7 October 2024, after the 2024 examination paper. It is contemporary value addition for reusing the demand, not evidence available to the candidate on the original exam day.

**Demand decoding:** The answer must cover location and flows, bilateral relationship, maritime-security effects, regional stability and international competition.

**Model answer (within 250 words).**

Maldives flanks two important passages: the Eight Degree Channel between Minicoy and northern Maldives, and the One-and-a-Half Degree Channel through the Maldivian archipelago. They sit amid west–east SLOCs linking Gulf and African energy sources with India and East/Southeast Asian markets. Cooperation or instability near them therefore affects route awareness, diversion risk and trade-energy resilience before shaping the wider security balance.

**Geopolitically**, stable relations protect India's neighbourhood interests and give Maldives development, finance and security options. The Joint Vision for a Comprehensive Economic and Maritime Security Partnership of 7 October 2024 is the concrete political instrument. Its currency-swap support should be described as an arranged facility, not presumed drawdown; later FTA Terms of Reference indicate negotiation, not a concluded agreement.

**Geostrategically**, cooperation can improve maritime-domain awareness, capacity building and HADR readiness. It complements SAGAR and IFC-IOR, but information and exercises do not themselves guarantee enforcement.

International competition raises the stakes. External-power infrastructure and basing presence in the Indian Ocean makes access and political trust consequential. However, Maldives is not a passive object of India–China rivalry: domestic politics and development needs give it agency to diversify partners.

India should emphasise reliable delivery, transparent financing, local capacity and HADR cooperation while avoiding exclusive-sphere language. The relationship strengthens security when it is useful to Maldivians and consistent with their sovereignty; pressure or unimplemented promises can widen space for competitors.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies the channel-to-flow mechanism, addresses every phrase of the stem, cites a dated instrument with correct status, includes island-state agency and gives a qualified stability judgement.

#### PYQ 4 · 2026 · Mains GS-II · Q9 · 10 marks · 150 words · DIRECT

> “‘IPMDA (Indo-Pacific Partnership for Maritime Domain Awareness) bridges the gap between India's SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region) vision and the Quad's collective Indo-Pacific strategy.’ Make a critical assessment of the statement focusing on IPMDA.”

**OCR note:** The official export contains a stray colon before “vision”; it is removed as an obvious OCR/layout artefact. No word is supplied.

**Model answer (within 150 words).**

IPMDA, announced by Quad leaders at Tokyo on 24 May 2022, uses commercially available, unclassified AIS and radio-frequency data through existing regional centres to create a shareable maritime picture.

The bridge is real because India's IFC-IOR—an institution advancing SAGAR's maritime-domain-awareness objective—is one of IPMDA's delivery nodes. Quad resources can therefore strengthen an Indian public-good platform across the Indian Ocean while supporting detection of dark shipping and IUU fishing.

However, the bridge is limited. SAGAR is India's unilateral Indian Ocean vision; Quad is a four-state, wider Indo-Pacific coalition. IPMDA connects an institution and function rather than merging doctrines. Technology and finance asymmetry, recipient patrol capacity, interoperability and regional hedging constrain outcomes; awareness is not enforcement.

The 26 May 2026 launch of IPMSC and proposed Common Operating Picture deepened cooperation, but did not prove completed integration. IPMDA is therefore a substantive yet partial bridge.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the mechanism, proves the link, supplies four limits, uses the dated update carefully and gives a graded verdict.

### PART 2 — DIRECT PYQS WITH SUPPLEMENTARY CROSS-LINKS

#### PYQ 5 · 2020 · Mains GS-II · Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · DIRECT

**Ownership:** Topic 04 directly owns the Indo-Pacific-stability demand. Topic 03 is a supplementary cross-link for India–US/India–Russia bilateral defence depth, not a co-owner.

> “What is the significance of Indo-US defence deals over Indo-Russian defence deals? Discuss with reference to stability in the Indo-Pacific region.”

**OCR note:** The official export is tokenised line by line and contains `2?Discuss`; the sentence above is a neutral reflow that preserves every readable English word and treats the stray `2` as OCR noise.

**Demand decoding:** Compare the two relationships and evaluate their consequences for regional stability. “Over” should not be converted into a claim of complete replacement.

**Model answer (within 250 words).**

India–US defence agreements and India–Russia defence ties perform different functions; their significance lies in the capability mix and constraints they create for Indo-Pacific stability.

US enablers—LEMOA (2016), COMCASA (2018) and BECA (2020)—improve logistics, secure communications and geospatial exchange. Together with Malabar, they enhance interoperability and maritime awareness with Indo-Pacific partners. This can strengthen deterrence and burden-sharing without creating a collective-defence obligation.

Russia, however, remains embedded in India's capability base through platforms and cooperation such as Su-30MKI, BrahMos and the S-400 contract. Russian-origin spares and upgrades make abrupt substitution costly. These ties also diversify India's partnerships and preserve room for independent choice.

The combined effect on stability is conditional. Diversified capability can prevent dominance and improve preparedness. Conversely, incompatible systems, sanctions exposure, supplier dependence and perceptions of bloc alignment can reduce readiness or intensify mistrust. Deep US interoperability while retaining a large Russian-origin inventory also creates integration choices.

India should therefore avoid a binary “US over Russia” strategy. It should diversify suppliers, strengthen domestic production and technology absorption, maintain crisis communication and keep cooperation consistent with international law. Stability follows when external deals expand autonomous capability rather than create a new single-point dependence.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares rather than lists, names three US agreements and Russian capability evidence, assesses positive and negative stability effects and rejects false replacement.

#### PYQ 6 · 2021 · Mains GS-II · Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · DIRECT

**Ownership:** Topic 04 directly owns AUKUS's impact on Indo-Pacific maritime balance and existing partnerships. Topic 10 is a supplementary grouping-taxonomy cross-link, not a co-owner.

> “The newly tri-nation partnership AUKUS is aimed at countering China's ambitions in the Indo-Pacific region. Is it going to supersede the existing partnerships in the region? Discuss the strength and impact of AUKUS in the present scenario.”

**OCR note:** Neutrally reflowed from the readable official English export; no word is supplied or removed.

**Model answer (within 250 words).**

AUKUS, announced by Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States on 15 September 2021, is a deep capability partnership rather than a comprehensive replacement for Indo-Pacific regional architecture.

Its principal strength is undersea deterrence. The pathway supports Australia's acquisition of conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines, while the advanced-capabilities track covers cyber, artificial intelligence, quantum and additional undersea technologies. Close technology integration, intelligence familiarity and long-term industrial cooperation can raise the cost of coercion.

Its impact is nevertheless mixed. It intensifies strategic competition and raises non-proliferation governance questions because Australia remains a non-nuclear-weapon state. Delivery depends on industrial capacity, US approvals and sustained political commitment. Cancellation of Australia's French submarine programme and the later settlement illustrate alliance-management costs. ASEAN responses have also varied, reflecting concern over arms racing alongside acceptance of a stronger balance.

AUKUS is unlikely to supersede existing partnerships. It has only three members and a capability-specific focus. ASEAN-centred EAS and ARF provide legitimacy and dialogue; Quad supplies broader functional cooperation; bilateral alliances and Indian Ocean institutions perform different tasks.

Therefore AUKUS adds a powerful undersea and technology layer but cannot substitute for inclusive institutions, crisis communication, legal order or partner agency. Its stabilising value will depend on delivery and restraint, not capability accumulation alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers strength, impact and supersession separately, distinguishes nuclear-powered from nuclear-armed and compares institutional functions.

#### PYQ 8 · 2020 · Mains GS-II · Q19 · 15 marks · 250 words · APPLICATION

**Owner:** Topic 10 — Regional, Global and Minilateral Groupings. Topic 04 contributes the maritime function.

> “‘Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad)’ is transforming itself into a trade bloc from a military alliance, in present times — Discuss.”

**OCR note:** Neutrally reflowed from the official export.

**Model answer (within 250 words).**

The premise is analytically inaccurate: Quad is neither a treaty military alliance nor a trade bloc. It is a non-treaty minilateral of Australia, India, Japan and the United States whose functional agenda has widened.

Its security relevance is real. Malabar improves naval interoperability, and IPMDA uses unclassified commercial data and existing fusion centres, including IFC-IOR, to strengthen maritime-domain awareness. Yet these activities create no collective-defence obligation or unified command. Tharoor's observation about asymmetric treaty relationships among members further weakens the alliance analogy.

Nor does Quad constitute a trade bloc. It has no common external tariff, customs union or preferential trade regime binding the four. Economic work on resilient supply chains, critical technologies, infrastructure and standards is cooperation, not trade-bloc formation.

The apparent transformation is therefore better understood as **functional expansion**: from strategic consultation toward a portfolio spanning maritime security, technology, health, infrastructure and resilience. This breadth can provide public goods and flexible coordination. However, different threat perceptions, economic exposure to China, ASEAN sensitivity and India's strategic autonomy constrain cohesion.

Quad should be judged instrument by instrument—exercise, working group, data initiative or summit commitment—rather than through alliance/trade-bloc labels. It is an evolving minilateral, not a legal transition from one bloc type to another.

**Why this earns marks:** It corrects both false classifications, proves the maritime function with Malabar/IPMDA, explains economic cooperation and supplies institutional limits.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

> Distinguish IORA, IONS and IFC-IOR, and explain how their different functions support India's SAGAR vision. (Answer in not more than 150 words.)

**Model answer (≤150 words).**

SAGAR is a declared maritime vision; IORA, IONS and IFC-IOR are distinct instruments that help operationalise it.

**IORA** is a regional intergovernmental organisation. It supplies political legitimacy, economic-security dialogue and agenda-setting among Indian Ocean states. **IONS**, established in 2008, is a voluntary forum among littoral navies; it builds professional dialogue and cooperative habits but commands no forces. **IFC-IOR**, established in Gurugram in 2018 and hosted by the Indian Navy, fuses and shares information for maritime-domain awareness.

Together they form a layered chain: political consent → navy-to-navy cooperation → usable shared picture. This supports SAGAR's security-and-growth logic by improving consultation, trust and awareness.

However, India chairing IORA/IONS and hosting IFC-IOR does not create regional control. Consensus, national enforcement capacity and partner willingness determine outcomes. The institutions operationalise SAGAR only when shared information and agreed priorities produce sustained national action.

**Why this earns marks:** Precise distinctions, functional chain, direct SAGAR link and a chairship/enforcement qualification.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

> “Maritime-domain awareness is necessary but insufficient for maritime security.” Explain with reference to IPMDA. (Answer in not more than 150 words.)

**Model answer (≤150 words).**

Maritime-domain awareness means detecting and understanding activity at sea; security additionally requires lawful and capable response.

IPMDA, announced by Quad leaders in 2022, uses commercially available, unclassified AIS and radio-frequency data through existing centres such as IFC-IOR. It can reveal dark shipping, support action against IUU fishing and improve disaster or trafficking awareness across several regions.

Yet a common picture does not intercept a vessel, preserve evidence or prosecute an offence. Smaller partners may lack patrol craft, aircraft, fuel, trained personnel or enabling law. Data standards may not interoperate, while dependence on external sensors and analytics can create technology asymmetry. Regional actors may also accept data but avoid overt Quad alignment.

Thus IPMDA is a public-good multiplier, not a security outcome by itself. Its value depends on partner-owned response capacity, legal protocols, sustained finance and trusted rules for data access and control.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

> Critically examine whether SAGAR and MAHASAGAR provide an adequate doctrine for India's role in a contested Indian Ocean. (Answer in not more than 250 words.)

**Model answer (≤250 words).**

SAGAR, articulated in Mauritius in 2015, links security with growth in the Indian Ocean. MAHASAGAR, announced at Port Louis in 2025, broadens this frame toward Global South trade, capacity building, technology sharing and mutual security. Together they provide a coherent, partner-oriented doctrine, but not an adequate strategy by themselves.

Their strengths are threefold. First, they reject a purely military understanding by combining security and development. Second, they organise multiple instruments: IORA political dialogue, IONS naval cooperation, IFC-IOR information sharing and bilateral island-state partnerships. Third, their public-good language fits HADR, maritime awareness and capacity building without requiring alliance membership.

However, both are declared visions, not treaties, budgets or implementation systems. External-power basing remains structural; island states retain agency and can diversify partners; and chairing institutions gives India convening power, not control. Awareness initiatives also expose an enforcement gap, while technology and finance dependence may narrow strategic autonomy. MAHASAGAR's broader scope can become diffuse unless partner-specific delivery is measured.

India should therefore convert doctrine into transparent, demand-led projects; strengthen local enforcement and climate/HADR capacity; support UNCLOS-based dispute management; and publish dated delivery evidence.

SAGAR and MAHASAGAR are adequate organising principles, but India's role will be judged by reliable delivery and partner trust, not doctrinal vocabulary.

**Why this earns marks:** Evaluates adequacy, covers doctrine–instrument–outcome, uses named institutions and balances public-good value against material constraints.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

> Assess the role of international law and sectoral institutions in reconciling maritime commerce, security and environmental protection. Use UNCLOS, BBNJ and IMO in your answer. (Answer in not more than 250 words.)

**Model answer (≤250 words).**

Maritime commerce depends on navigation, security requires predictable jurisdiction, and environmental harm crosses borders. International law reconciles these interests by allocating rights and creating common technical rules.

**UNCLOS** supplies the constitutional framework of the seas: territorial-sea limits, innocent passage, EEZ rights, navigation and dispute settlement. India's acceptance of the 2014 India–Bangladesh Annex VII award shows how legal process can stabilise access and jurisdiction. Yet UNCLOS does not eliminate contested claims or guarantee compliance.

The **BBNJ Agreement** extends cooperation to marine biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction. Its status chain—adoption, signature, ratification and entry into force—matters: India signed in 2024 but had not ratified on the canonical status check, illustrating a gap between normative support and party rights in implementation.

The **IMO** turns shared risks into shipping standards. SOLAS addresses safety; MARPOL pollution; ballast-water and ship-recycling conventions ecological and end-of-life risks; and the sulphur cap regulates emissions. However, flag, port and coastal states implement these rules unevenly. Climate negotiations also reveal burden-sharing conflict: committee approval is not adoption.

Together, UNCLOS allocates legal space, BBNJ governs a high-seas ecological gap and IMO regulates the vessel. Reconciliation succeeds when consent is matched by finance, technology and national enforcement.

**Why this earns marks:** Gives each institution a separate function, uses India's conduct and status evidence, and identifies implementation as the common constraint.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

> Evaluate India's layered Indo-Pacific architecture as an alternative to alliance-based regional order. (Answer in not more than 300 words.)

**Model answer (≤300 words).**

India's Indo-Pacific architecture is layered rather than alliance-centred: it combines law, ASEAN-centred forums, Indian Ocean institutions, bilateral partnerships and minilateral capability while retaining strategic autonomy.

At the broadest level, UNCLOS supplies legal legitimacy; EAS and ARF preserve ASEAN centrality and inclusive dialogue. IPOI converts India's vision into seven voluntary cooperation pillars. In the Indian Ocean, IORA provides intergovernmental consultation, IONS navy-to-navy cooperation and IFC-IOR shared maritime awareness. Bilateral visions, especially the India–Maldives document of 7 October 2024, translate regional doctrine into partner-specific commitments.

Minilateral tools add speed and capability. Malabar builds interoperability without collective defence. IPMDA channels unclassified commercial data through existing fusion centres, including IFC-IOR. India–US logistics, communications and geospatial agreements improve cooperation, while continuing Russian-origin capabilities demonstrate diversified, non-exclusive engagement.

This architecture has advantages over a formal alliance: wider participation, lower sovereignty costs, issue-specific coalitions and compatibility with island-state agency. It can supply public goods—awareness, HADR readiness and capacity—without forcing partners into binary alignment.

Its weaknesses are equally real. Consensus bodies deliver slowly; information does not create enforcement; technology and finance can remain externally concentrated; Quad members have asymmetric treaty relationships; and competing bases persist. AUKUS also shows that capability-specific coalitions can intensify arms-race and non-proliferation concerns.

India's model is therefore a credible alternative for cooperation, not a substitute for material power. It will shape order only if voluntary institutions deliver usable capacity, legal commitments are implemented and partners see Indian engagement as reliable rather than exclusive.

**Why this earns marks:** Integrates every layer, compares the model with alliance order, supplies named evidence and reaches an outcome-based verdict.

#### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

> “The Indian Ocean is a contested commons, not an Indian sphere of influence.” Discuss the implications of this proposition for India's island partnerships, maritime institutions and strategic autonomy. (Answer in not more than 300 words.)

**Model answer (≤300 words).**

Calling the Indian Ocean a contested commons recognises shared sea lanes and law, external-power presence and the independent agency of littoral states. It rejects both an ungoverned-space view and an exclusive Indian sphere.

For **island partnerships**, India must begin with partner interests. Maldives, Mauritius, Seychelles and Sri Lanka seek development finance, climate resilience, security and diversified relationships. The India–Maldives Joint Vision provides a dated cooperation instrument, but a vision is not self-executing. Reliable delivery and respect for domestic politics are more durable than pressure for exclusivity.

For **institutions**, India should treat IORA and IONS chairships as convening opportunities, not control. IFC-IOR and IPMDA can provide shared awareness, while Colombo Security Conclave categories must be stated precisely. These mechanisms gain legitimacy when information rules are transparent and local enforcement capacity is strengthened. UNCLOS and acceptance of the India–Bangladesh award support India's claim to rules-based leadership.

For **strategic autonomy**, India can cooperate with Quad and the United States while retaining Russian-origin capabilities and ASEAN-centred engagement. Yet commercial-satellite dependence, legacy spares, sanctions exposure and external basing constrain choice. AUKUS and Quad cannot replace India's own capacity or bilateral trust.

Operational naval security belongs to Defence/Internal Security, and disaster-response execution to Disaster Management; IR must focus on cooperation, legal status and regional effects.

India should therefore compete by supplying public goods, transparent finance, training and predictable implementation. Leadership in a contested commons means creating useful choices for partners under common rules—not claiming a monopoly that the region's power distribution and sovereign agency do not support.

**Why this earns marks:** Defines the proposition, answers all three named dimensions, applies the boundary firewalls and ends with a calibrated conception of leadership.
