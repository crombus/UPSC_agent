#### 2020 GS-II Q10 — 10 marks

**Provenance:** Audited 2018–2023 Mains ledger. Core route supersedes older Advanced ownership; wording verified against locally held paper. No official Mains key claimed.

**Independent model answer:** The diaspora's economic role is substantial; calling its political role invariably *decisive* is too strong. In the United States and Europe, Indian-origin professionals, entrepreneurs and researchers sustain commercial links, investment networks and knowledge exchange. Money sent home connects households to host economies; India's national remittance total must not be presented as exclusively Western. #### 2023 GS-II Q10 — 10 marks

**Independent model answer:** The diaspora's prominence in Western societies benefits India through financial, professional and political networks. ### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Describe the economic and political benefits for India of the Indian diaspora's rise in the West. Answer in about 150 words.

**Independent model solution:** India gains from diaspora achievement in the West through three distinct channels. First, income remitted to Indian households supports consumption and transnational economic links. The RBI's FY24 remittance survey identifies the United States and United Kingdom among significant sources; national inflows cannot all be assigned to the West. Second, professionals, academics and entrepreneurs build commercial and knowledge networks, opening access to research collaboration, markets and skills. Effective engagement also imposes obligations on India: grievance follow-up, access to consular services for nationals and credibility in its domestic pluralism. Economic gain, social prestige and political influence are related, but each requires separate evidence; none creates automatic control over host decisions.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine why India's diaspora-welfare instruments no longer match the fastest-growing segment of the diaspora. Answer in about 250 words.

**Independent model solution:** India's migrant-protection architecture was built around identifiable risks faced by Emigration-Check-Required (ECR) workers travelling to specified destinations. The Protector General of Emigrants oversees clearance; e-Migrate records recruitment and routes cases; compulsory Pravasi Bharatiya Bima Yojana insurance addresses covered accidental death or permanent disability. The Indian Community Welfare Fund can assist with urgent welfare and legal needs, while MADAD provides a grievance channel. These are meaningful safeguards for vulnerable labour migrants, not obsolete instruments to discard.

But the RBI's FY24 remittance survey, as reported in the Economic Survey 2025–26, shows advanced economies contributing more inward remittances than GCC countries. Remittance-source shares are not an occupational census, yet they warrant attention to professional mobility. Skilled migrants often face host-country visa rules, qualification recognition and social-security portability; Indian emigration clearance or accident insurance cannot require a foreign regulator to license an engineer or recognise accrued benefits.

The mismatch is one of instrument and negotiating counterparty, not simply budget. India should retain ECR oversight and emergency aid, improve mission–MADAD–e-Migrate case handling, and negotiate bilateral mobility and social-security arrangements where appropriate. A host-specific assessment must distinguish temporary migrants from permanent residents and avoid assuming that remittance origin directly measures legal status. Progress requires evidence of resolved cases, recognised credentials and actual uptake, not merely a portal launch. Segmented policy protects workers at risk while adapting to changing diaspora opportunities.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the claim that India's soft power is constrained more by institutional fragmentation than by external competition. Answer in about 250 words.

**Independent model solution:** Tharoor identifies a structural gap: if cultural engagement remains solely with ICCR and a central public-diplomacy division, officials managing particular country relationships may not integrate it into bilateral strategy. Mainstreaming soft-power work across MEA territorial divisions could match cultural programmes and exchanges to local languages, audiences and priorities. This is a proposal, not evidence that reform has already been implemented.

There is also an authenticity constraint. Film, yoga or educational exchange may generate goodwill, but domestic events contradicting India's plural, multi-religious and multi-linguistic character can erode the credibility of its message. Tharoor's nativist-attack example makes the point: reputation reflects lived conduct as well as promotion. Conversely, ITEC technical training can produce goodwill through practical human networks; the Economic Survey 2025–26 describes especially strong cultural-diplomacy influence in South Asia, not identical effects in every partner country. Overseas Indian communities form networks, not a uniformly obedient diplomatic constituency.

External competition and host public attitudes still matter. Internal reorganisation cannot compel another government's favourable vote or eliminate sensitivities when diaspora advocacy appears to be interference. The claim is substantially persuasive as an explanation of avoidable Indian constraints, but not an exclusive causal diagnosis. Coordinate specialist ICCR capacity with territorial desks, keep cultural representation plural, protect welfare separately and assess reciprocal ties rather than count publicity events. Country-level feedback should test that claim.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the proposition that the diaspora should be treated as a two-way bridge rather than a foreign-policy lever. Answer in about 300 words.

**Independent model solution:** A *lever* implies that New Delhi can direct a dispersed population toward a chosen foreign-policy result. A *bridge* connects societies through people with different passports, political preferences and host-country obligations. Non-Resident Indians abroad retain Indian citizenship; foreign-national Persons of Indian Origin and Overseas Citizen of India cardholders do not acquire Indian citizenship merely through ancestry or registration. One institution cannot speak for all these constituencies.

India can enable the bridge. Pravasi Bharatiya Divas creates a forum for recognition and exchange; ICCR supports cultural connections; ITEC training builds skills and relationships. Professional and business networks may help commerce and research. Indian-American organisations' contribution to the India–US civil-nuclear debate demonstrates coalition-building, not command over Congress. Indian-origin host politicians answer to host voters and laws, not to India. Visible advocacy may provoke sensitivities about foreign interference; individual security allegations must not be imputed to an entire community.

Reciprocity also requires protection. MADAD channels complaints; missions seek consular access for Indian detainees within Vienna Convention Article 36 and host law; emigrant protections matter for ECR workers. These duties cannot be replaced by celebration or an expectation of remittances. Tharoor's insistence on authentic, plural cultural representation imposes a domestic condition: exclusion at home can erode trust abroad.

The bridge metaphor does not prohibit diaspora advocacy. It makes advocacy voluntary, lawful and host-specific, alongside intergovernmental diplomacy. For example, consular officials should follow up individual welfare cases, while diplomatic desks may seek research partnerships with host universities without treating student associations as state agents. The effectiveness test is not how many delegates attended an event but whether mutual trust, lawful participation and assistance survive political disagreement. India gains more durable influence by protecting people and facilitating two-way exchange than by presuming political loyalty or guaranteed success.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess how consular protection converts a diaspora relationship into a standing obligation of the Indian state. Answer in about 300 words.

**Independent model solution:** The diaspora is not simply a remittance source or a cultural asset. For Indian citizens overseas, it creates a continuing protection workload that India must address through missions, institutions and diplomacy. Foreign-national PIOs and OCI cardholders have a different nationality status: ancestry and OCI registration do not establish identical treaty-based national-consular entitlements. Caseworkers should determine nationality first.

For an Indian national arrested abroad, Article 36 of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations supports notification, communication and access under treaty terms. India can request information, arrange suitable assistance and make representations; it cannot set aside a host conviction or demand unconditional release. MADAD provides a grievance entry point, not a guarantee of resolution. The Indian Community Welfare Fund may assist vulnerable persons with emergency welfare and legal needs under applicable rules. The Protector General of Emigrants, e-Migrate and compulsory PBBY insurance address distinct migration-stage and covered-risk problems for ECR workers; an insurance claim is not diplomatic intervention.

Crisis escalation changes the task. Operation Sindhu in June 2025 illustrates host-state coordination, consular access and travel permissions as diplomatic prerequisites for evacuation. Aircraft and relief logistics form a separate operational cycle; an extraction figure dated to a particular day does not show that every person was reached. Detention and grievance caseloads reinforce the need for continuous mission capacity, not only conspicuous rescues.

India should track case handling and vulnerable-group reach, screen nationality honestly and pursue host-authority cooperation and migrant safeguards. An unanswered complaint or denied consular visit calls for documented diplomatic follow-up, not a claim of completed rescue. Effective protection is persistent, bounded by foreign sovereignty and host law, and inseparable from—but not reducible to—India's ambition to benefit from diaspora networks.
