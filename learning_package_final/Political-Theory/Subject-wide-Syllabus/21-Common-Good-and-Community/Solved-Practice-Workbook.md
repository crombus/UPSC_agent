#### 2018 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 10 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “What is meant by liberal democracy? Does it require deeper principles for social cohesion to balance its own strong affirmation of individual rights? Give reasons from the Indian context.”

**Independent model solution:** Liberal democracy combines competitive representative government, public criticism, constitutional limits and equal individual liberties. These rules secure dissent but cannot by themselves produce trust or solidarity among deeply unequal citizens. Gauba’s common-good distinction shows why bargaining between powerful groups is not automatically good for the whole. In India, a formally open village meeting can still exclude people affected by caste hierarchy; participation needs meaningful voice and protection against domination. Green’s account of positive freedom suggests public education and access to services enable citizens to exercise liberty, while communitarians recall the social practices through which people learn reciprocal obligation. However, an imposed single culture would negate liberal democracy’s own protection of minorities. The stronger cohesion is constitutional and plural: shared commitment to fair procedures, social opportunity and the right to contest public decisions. Individual rights and social solidarity are thus mutually corrective rather than competing slogans.

#### 2018 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 10 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “Do you subscribe to the view that Indian cultural identity needs to integrate the principles of multi-culturalism and respect for the dignity of each person? Justify your answer.”

**Independent model solution:** Yes, if integration means coexistence without ranking communities or silencing persons inside them. A common good cannot be equated with the culture of the numerical majority: Gauba expressly distinguishes community welfare from majority gain. India’s linguistic and religious plurality illustrates that common institutions must leave room for varied identities and public criticism. Taylor’s dialogical account helps explain why recognition of a group can support personal dignity; the liberal rights objection insists that individuals retain freedom to dissent even from their own group’s customs. A language policy, for example, can promote shared communication while protecting minority-language schooling and participation. The contrary argument claims common symbols demand cultural uniformity, but coercive sameness can breed alienation rather than solidarity. The defensible national identity is therefore civic and revisable: it protects individual equality, enables communities to contribute and subjects every claimed common purpose to rights-sensitive public justification.

#### 2020 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 10 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “How far do the liberal democracies safeguard the interests of minorities? Evaluate critically.”

**Independent model solution:** Liberal democracies offer important minority safeguards: equal civic rights, protected expression and association, judicially reviewable rules, and an opposition able to challenge public policy. These mechanisms limit simple majoritarianism, but formal protection does not by itself cure unequal resources or exclusion from agenda-setting. Gauba’s warning that common good is not the majority’s good supplies a useful test. In India, a minority-language group may possess the vote while lacking practicable schooling in its language; effective voice and access matter as well as ballot equality. Communitarian claims for shared purposes can support belonging, yet risk cultural homogenisation unless minority dissent is protected. Conversely, absolute insulation of groups from criticism may shield domination within them. Hence liberal democracy can safeguard minorities substantially when it combines rights and contestable institutions with social opportunity and inclusion, but it cannot guarantee those interests solely by constitutional vocabulary or periodic elections.

#### 2020 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 20 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “State and examine the Gandhian concept of social development.”

**Independent model solution:** For Gandhi, development is not simply greater output or consumption: it is moral, social and economic uplift of all, especially the neglected. Sarvodaya requires self-restraint, non-violence and concern for the last person, not utilitarian maximisation that permits sacrificing minorities. Trusteeship asks owners to regard surplus wealth as a moral trust for service rather than as unlimited private dominion. Bread labour connects consumption to physical work and the dignity of labour; caste and status hierarchy are therefore incompatible with equal respect. Local self-reliance and voluntary cooperation express the priority of persons and community over centralised material expansion. In an Indian village, an industrial project cannot count as development merely because it raises revenue if displaced residents lose livelihood and voice; Gauba’s critique of sectional bargains supports this diagnosis. Gandhi’s approach also faces a serious objection: voluntary moral conversion by powerful owners may fail, and small associations can reproduce local hierarchy. Rights, transparent public institutions and remedies for those dispossessed must complement appeals to conscience. Non-violence is an ethical constraint on means, not a substitute for enforceable safeguards. Gandhian development thus supplies a demanding test of inclusion and dignity, but needs institutional design to protect those whom trustees fail.

#### 2023 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 15 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “Critically analyse the social and political significance of Ambedkar's notion of annihilation of caste.”

**Independent model solution:** Annihilation of caste attacks a graded hierarchy of status, occupation and social membership, not merely the poverty of individual members. Its social significance lies in challenging inherited exclusion, stigma and restrictions on association that make nominal equal treatment hollow. Its political significance lies in demanding equal citizenship and a public order in which subordinated people can organise and contest rules rather than depend on a dominant group’s benevolence. Gauba’s common-good test strengthens the case: no society can call itself a community of welfare when the majority’s comfort rests on an excluded group’s subordination. In India, formally open public meetings may still reproduce unequal voice if caste-based intimidation or deference shapes who speaks. Trusteeship and appeals to fraternity can motivate reform, but Ambedkarian critique asks whether rights and institutions can counter entrenched power without waiting for a voluntary change of heart. An objection holds that attacking caste risks weakening community solidarity; the reply is that solidarity predicated on fixed inferior status is not genuinely common. Effective change therefore joins anti-discrimination, material opportunity, political representation and liberty for individuals within all groups. The aim is not uniform identity but a shared civic standing with room for dissent.

#### 2024 Philosophy Optional Paper II — 15 marks (cross-applied)

**Verified prompt:** “Discuss the role of ethical principles of tolerance and coexistence for the rise of multicultural societies.”

**Independent model solution:** Tolerance restrains the impulse to suppress unfamiliar beliefs; coexistence adds conditions for different communities to share institutions without subordination. Neither entails approving every practice inside a group. Gauba’s distinction matters: common good is not state preference or majority custom, and consensus is a process of accommodating differences, not prior unanimity. In India, linguistic diversity makes a single public language policy a test of whether communication can be advanced without extinguishing minority educational access. Taylor’s recognition-based extension explains why humiliation of a community can also injure members’ standing; liberal safeguards ensure those members may dissent from leaders claiming to speak for them. Walzer’s sphere-specific view cautions against allowing money or political clout to buy cultural authority over schooling. A critic argues that tolerance alone permits parallel isolation, while coerced cultural integration risks assimilating minorities. The response is reciprocal civic participation: protect freedom of belief and expression, make access to shared goods fair, and create revisable forums where affected groups contest public reasons. Ethical coexistence is therefore more than passive non-interference; it links equal dignity to accountable institutions and a common good that remains genuinely open to minorities.

### Original GS Mains Model 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish common good from public interest and common interest. Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** Distinguish normative common good, empirical common interest and authoritative public-interest justification; give one overlap case.

**Model answer (135 words):**

The three expressions operate at different levels. Common good is the widest normative standard: the justified welfare of the whole political community beyond factional, state or numerical advantage. Common interest is narrower and empirical - an overlap among affected persons. Public interest is an authoritative policy judgment claiming to answer a collective need. A labour-management settlement may express both parties' common interest yet fail the common-good test if consumers bear hidden costs; a justified quarantine may be defended in the public interest and also serve common good. The distinction prevents state convenience or coalition strength from becoming self-certifying moral authority. The safest verdict is tripartite: common good is the widest normative standard, common interest is a narrower empirical overlap, and public interest is the authoritative policy judgment that must remain answerable to the common good.

**Why this earns marks:** Answers the specified directive with thinker-specific evidence, an Indian application and a qualified counterargument to the claim in model 1.

### Original GS Mains Model 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Why is community not identical to majority? Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** Explain why minority welfare and dissent cannot be replaced by majority preference.

**Model answer (129 words):**

A majority is a numerical relation; community in common-good reasoning is a normative whole. Gauba therefore rejects equating the common good with the preference of the largest electoral or cultural bloc. The common good may require protecting a minority against majority injustice. Consensus also means continuing accommodation, not the elimination of disagreement. When a dominant religious, caste or linguistic group presents its own way of life as the voice of everyone, sectional interest is merely disguised as community.
