### SESSION 2 — Sex, Gender and the Social Construction of Hierarchy

#### 3. Essential definitions
- ✅ **Feminism:** concern with the status and role of women in relation to men, combined with protest against injustice suffered because of sex. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ **Patriarchy:** literally rule of the father, now used for wider male domination in household, labour market and social life. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ **Sex:** biological difference, such as reproductive function and secondary characteristics. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ **Gender:** culturally constructed images, expectations and status-distinctions attached to women and men. (PDF pp.84-85)
- ✅ **Gender inequality:** a conventional or cultural inequality, hence open to criticism and change. (PDF p.86)
- ✅ **Feminist theory:** advocacy of equal rights for women and men with commitment to improve women's position in society. (PDF p.86)

#### 4. Core argument / chapter spine
- ✅ Gauba starts by defining feminism as resistance to unjust female subordination produced by patriarchy. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ He then distinguishes sex from gender so that biological difference is separated from socially assigned inferiority. (PDF pp.84-86)
- ✅ Gender roles such as aggressiveness/dominance for men and submissiveness/expressiveness for women are treated as cultural instruments of social control. (PDF p.85)
- ✅ Gauba argues that biological differences do not justify inferior status for women; the main causes lie in social arrangements and cultural conditioning. (PDF pp.86-87)
- ✅ He ends by classifying feminism into liberal, radical and socialist streams. (PDF pp.88-90)

### SESSION 3 — Core Distinctions, Thinkers and Feminist Streams

#### 5. Essential distinctions
- ✅ **Sex vs gender:** sex is natural/biological; gender is cultural/social. (PDF pp.84-86)
- ✅ **Natural inequality vs conventional inequality:** Gauba uses Rousseau's distinction to show that gender inequality belongs to the alterable second category. (PDF p.86)
- ✅ **Legal equality vs substantive equality:** early feminism attacked barriers to education, property and vote; later feminism asks whether equal law alone changes social power. (PDF pp.88-91)
- ✅ **Equal treatment vs compensatory support:** Gauba notes two broad views, one insisting on sameness of legal regime and another allowing maternity-related protections and compensatory measures. (PDF pp.92-93)
- ❌ **Biology justifies hierarchy:** Gauba explicitly rejects this. (PDF pp.86-87)

#### 6. Thinkers and positions
- ✅ **Mary Wollstonecraft:** argues that women are rational individuals and should enjoy equal rights, education, property and civic standing. (PDF p.88)
- ✅ **J.S. Mill:** argues that women are not inferior in talent and deserve full legal and political rights. (PDF pp.84, 88)
- ✅ **Liberal feminism:** seeks equality of opportunity, equal pay, removal of formal barriers and better representation. (PDF p.88)
- ✅ **Shulamith Firestone:** radical feminist who ties women's subordination to reproductive biology and seeks release from that dependence through technological change. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ **Kate Millett:** radical feminist who interprets the relation between sexes as a power relation sustained by ideology. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ **Sheila Rowbotham:** socialist feminist linking women's liberation with struggle against capitalism and other oppressions. (PDF pp.89-90)

### SESSION 6 — Later Provenance and Marxist Feminism

#### 13. Provenance beyond Gauba: sex/gender, standpoint and later feminist theory (extension — use with caution)
- ⚠️ Gauba's chapter grounds the sex/gender distinction and the liberal/radical/socialist typology; it does **not** itself discuss Simone de Beauvoir, Gayle Rubin, Ann Oakley, Carole Pateman, Susan Moller Okin, Judith Butler or Kimberlé Crenshaw. Everything below is a standard PSIR/political-theory extension used by examiners beyond this specific book, flagged ⚠️ throughout, and is not tied to a page number in the primary source.
- ⚠️ **Simone de Beauvoir** (*The Second Sex*, 1949) supplies the founding proposition — paraphrased, not quoted verbatim here since exact wording is not independently verified in this file's source base — that womanhood is a social becoming rather than a fixed biological given. Use this as the philosophical root of the sex/gender split that Gauba later states in textbook form.
- ⚠️ **Ann Oakley** (*Sex, Gender and Society*, 1972) is usually credited with popularising the working sex/gender terminology in sociology — sex as biological, gender as the culturally learned, historically variable content attached to that biology.
- ⚠️ **Gayle Rubin** ("The Traffic in Women", 1975) supplies the mechanism often paired with the distinction: the "sex/gender system" — social arrangements that transform biological sex into a hierarchy of gendered obligation, kinship exchange and constrained sexuality. Rubin explains *how* sex becomes gender, which Gauba's chapter asserts but does not mechanise.
- ⚠️ Caveat: cite these three names as standard provenance for the sex/gender distinction, but do not present a single, uncontested "origin story" as settled fact — the distinction has multiple co-formulators and was consolidated gradually; avoid taxonomic overclaim.

##### Marxist feminism, reconstructed separately from socialist feminism
- ⚠️ This basic file (like Gauba) uses "socialist feminism" as the umbrella term (Rowbotham) for the patriarchy-plus-capitalism synthesis. Marxist feminism is a related but analytically prior strand a strong answer should distinguish:
  - **Claim:** women's oppression is generated principally by the capitalist mode of production, especially unpaid domestic/reproductive labour that reproduces the workforce free of charge.
  - **Evidence:** the 1970s "domestic labour debate" treated housework as unwaged labour-power reproduction necessary for capital accumulation.
  - **Significance:** relocates the "private" household inside political economy, making family labour a class question, not only a cultural one.
  - **Limit:** a pure class-reduction cannot explain patriarchal violence or control of sexuality that predates and outlasts capitalism — which is exactly why Rowbotham's socialist-feminist synthesis treats patriarchy and capitalism as two distinct, mutually reinforcing systems rather than one reducible to the other.
- ⚠️ Exam use: if a question specifically says "Marxist feminism," lead with the domestic-labour/social-reproduction claim; if it says "socialist feminism," lead with Rowbotham's dual-systems synthesis; the two labels overlap but are not simple synonyms.

### SESSION 7 — Pateman and Okin: Contract, Justice and the Family

##### Carole Pateman and Susan Moller Okin: feminist critique of contract and justice theory
- ⚠️ **Carole Pateman** (*The Sexual Contract*, 1988): **Claim** — classical social-contract theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) silently assumes a prior "sexual contract" securing men's access to women's bodies and labour before the "original" social contract; the public contract of citizens rests on an unmentioned private contract of subordination. **Evidence** — contract narratives are silent on the marriage contract and domestic subordination that make male civic equality possible. **Significance** — reframes liberal equality as resting on an unexamined patriarchal foundation. **Limit** — the thesis reconstructs contract theory's silences; it is an interpretive lens, not a documented historical event, and should not be used as a factual claim about any real contract being signed.
- ⚠️ **Susan Moller Okin** (*Justice, Gender, and the Family*, 1989): **Claim** — theories of justice (she targets Rawls) wrongly treat the family as a pre-political given rather than a site justice must examine. **Evidence** — unequal domestic burden restricts women's access to the "primary goods" (time, income, position) public justice is meant to distribute. **Significance** — extends justice from state/market into the household. **Limit** — risks over-extending justice into every private relationship, which is why most syntheses still preserve some sphere of family autonomy alongside gender-just background conditions.

### SESSION 9 — Objection-Reply Chains and Cautious Indian Application

#### 14. Two objection–reply chains (mandatory doctrinal debate)
1. **Objection:** if gender is entirely performative/socially constructed (Butler), feminism loses a stable category of "woman" around which to organise and to claim rights.
   **Reply:** many feminist theorists respond that political claims can be built on provisionally, strategically stabilised categories even while accepting the category is historically constructed ("strategic essentialism"); legal sex-discrimination categories function pragmatically regardless of deeper philosophical instability. ⚠️ Present as a live, contested debate — not a settled result.

2. **Objection:** radical feminism's emphasis on patriarchy as the primary, near-universal structure of oppression risks treating "women" as one undifferentiated class, erasing differences of race, class and caste among women.
   **Reply:** Crenshaw's intersectionality and Marxist/socialist feminism's attention to class supply the corrective — oppression is compounded and differently configured across axes, producing a more accurate map of *whose* subordination is being explained. ⚠️ This reply strengthens rather than replaces the original radical-feminist insight that sexual/reproductive domination is analytically distinct from class or race domination.

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Practice design

- Questions 1-24 are hard core GS/Prelims diagnostics; Questions 25-32 are remedial close-option repairs.
- Formats are deliberately varied: definition control, multi-statement, sequence, matching, chronology, elimination, application, evidence and inference.
- The correct option rotates strictly `A -> B -> C -> D`, repeated eight times across the 32 questions, giving 8 A, 8 B, 8 C and 8 D.
- Because the key is a fixed rotation it carries no diagnostic value: options are matched for register and length, so an answer can only be reached by reasoning about content.
- Each of the 128 options carries its own question-specific explanation and each question closes with a distinct examiner trap.

### MCQ 1

Which proposition is correctly associated with **Feminism**?

- A. concern with the status and role of women in relation to men, combined with protest against injustice suffered because of sex.
- B. early feminism attacked barriers to education, property and vote; later feminism asks whether equal law alone changes social power.
- C. argues that women are rational individuals and should enjoy equal rights, education, property and civic standing.
- D. radical feminist who ties women's subordination to reproductive biology and seeks release from that dependence through technological change.

**Answer: A**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A States the source-recorded proposition for Feminism.
- **B:** B Attaches to Legal equality vs substantive equality, not Feminism.

### Original GS Mains Model 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish sex from gender and explain why the distinction matters politically. Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** the directive `distinguish` requires the answer to fix each side of the distinction precisely and show where it does the analytical work. **Model answer (126 words):**

Introduction: Sex ordinarily refers to bodily and reproductive characteristics, whereas gender refers to the socially organised roles, expectations and status attached to perceived sex. Core analysis: The distinction explains how a biological difference becomes a political hierarchy. Gauba treats masculinity and femininity as culturally coded patterns rather than necessary consequences of anatomy. Rousseau's natural-conventional distinction then clarifies the normative point: even where bodily differences are real, unequal education, work, authority and citizenship are conventional arrangements. Feminism can therefore challenge the inference from difference to inferiority. This complication revises the framework without restoring biological destiny. Conclusion: The distinction is politically decisive because bodily difference does not entail social rank: once gender hierarchy is recognised as historically produced, it becomes open to criticism, collective action and institutional transformation.

### Original GS Mains Model 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Critically examine Judith Butler's account of gender performativity and the category problem it creates for feminism. Answer in 250 words.

**Demand decode:** the directive `critically examine` requires the answer to test the proposition against its strongest objection before giving a verdict. Fix the boundary of examine Judith Butler's account of gender performativity and the category problem it creates for feminism in the opening line, carry the argument on Introduction, Judith Butler, Core and Performativity, and reserve the closing sentences for the qualification that conclusion: Butler strengthens feminism by exposing the repeated practices that naturalise gender, but political action still requires provisional categories whose strategic use must remain open to internal criticism and revision.

**Model answer (247 words):**

Introduction: Judith Butler radicalises the sex-gender debate by treating gender as performative: repeated, socially compelled acts produce the appearance of a natural identity. Core analysis: Performativity does not mean a freely chosen theatrical performance, nor does it deny bodies. It argues that speech, dress, gesture and institutional classification repeatedly constitute intelligible gendered subjects. Because the norm requires repetition, failed or subversive repetition can disclose its contingency. This helps feminism explain why hierarchy survives without a single commanding patriarch and why apparently personal conduct is politically regulated. Further development: Butler also challenges the standard textbook picture in which a stable biological sex merely receives a cultural gender. The categories through which bodies are recognised are themselves organised by discourse and power. Critics reply that this approach may understate material constraints, reproductive embodiment and durable institutions. Butler's contribution remains strongest as an account of naturalisation: norms appear inevitable because repetition conceals their history. Political feminism can accept this insight while retaining empirically usable categories for law and mobilisation. The category must function as a revisable coalition rather than an essence that predetermines all members. Critical evaluation: The category problem is serious: if 'woman' is wholly an unstable effect of discourse, the subject of anti-discrimination claims appears to dissolve. Yet temporary categories can harden and exclude those who fit badly. Conclusion: Butler strengthens feminism by exposing the repeated practices that naturalise gender, but political action still requires provisional categories whose strategic use must remain open to internal criticism and revision.

**Why this earns marks:** it obeys `critically examine` instead of drifting into description, attaches each claim to named evidence (Introduction, Judith Butler, Core and Performativity), converts that evidence into analysis of examine Judith Butler's account of gender performativity and the category problem it creates for feminism, and keeps the examiner-facing qualification that conclusion: Butler strengthens feminism by exposing the repeated practices that naturalise gender, but political action still requires provisional categories whose strategic use must remain open to internal criticism and revision. ### Original GS Mains Model 5 — 15 marks

**Question:** Evaluate the feminist critique of the public-private divide with reference to Carole Pateman and Susan Moller Okin. Answer in 250 words.

The thesis is interpretive, not a claim that an actual historical contract was signed. Susan Moller Okin argues that theories such as Rawls's insufficiently examine the family as a site where gendered labour produces unequal time, income and opportunity. The apparently neutral citizen entering public life may therefore carry resources made possible by another person's unpaid care and dependence. Further development: The critique also changes the meaning of citizenship. If one citizen's public participation depends on another's unequal domestic burden, formally identical rights conceal unequal effective freedom. The answer is not abolition of privacy, but justice-sensitive background conditions: freedom from violence, fair care burdens, economic security and genuine exit. ### 3. Direct-book argument reconstruction
- ✅ Step 1: feminism is introduced as protest against the inferior status of women created by patriarchy. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ Step 2: Gauba separates sex from gender so that biological difference is not confused with social hierarchy. (PDF pp.84-86)

- ✅ Step 4: using Rousseau's natural/conventional inequality distinction, Gauba shows gender inequality to be socially alterable. (PDF p.86)
- ✅ Step 5: early feminism is reconstructed through Wollstonecraft and Mill as a demand for rights, education, property and civic equality. (PDF p.88)

- ✅ Step 7: Gauba then extends the issue from rights to representation, citizenship and gender justice. (PDF pp.90-93)

### 4. Definitions and distinctions
- ✅ **Patriarchy:** male domination extending from household authority into labour market and public life. (PDF p.84)
- ✅ **Sex:** biological division of humans into male and female. (PDF pp.84-85)
- ✅ **Gender:** the cultural organization of expectations, roles and status attached to sex. (PDF pp.84-85)
- ✅ **Masculinity / femininity:** socially coded personality-traits and behaviours that naturalize domination/submission. (PDF p.85)
- ✅ **Natural inequality / conventional inequality:** Rousseau's distinction used by Gauba to show that gender hierarchy belongs largely to the second category. (PDF p.86)
- ✅ **Equal rights view vs compensatory-equality view:** one stresses same laws, the other supports differentiated protections and representation-enhancing measures. (PDF pp.92-93)

### 5. Thinker-by-thinker positions
- ✅ **Mary Wollstonecraft:** rejects female inferiority and insists that women, as rational beings, deserve education, property and civic rights. (PDF p.88)
- ✅ **J.S. Mill:** argues that women are not less talented than men and should enjoy full legal and political equality. (PDF pp.84, 88)
- ✅ **Shulamith Firestone:** radical feminist who treats reproductive biology as the ultimate historical basis of female subordination and sees technology as enabling escape from that dependence. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ **Kate Millett:** reads male-female relations as political power relations sustained by ideology, authority and economic force. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ **Sheila Rowbotham:** socialist feminist who ties women's liberation to anti-capitalist struggle and the recovery of women's hidden history. (PDF pp.89-90)
- ⚠️ Together these thinkers move the debate from rights-based equality, to patriarchy as power, to patriarchy-plus-capitalism as a combined structure.

### 6. Competing perspectives
- ✅ **Liberal feminism:** focuses on equal opportunity and the removal of formal discrimination. (PDF p.88)
- ✅ **Radical feminism:** insists that women's oppression is primary and cannot be dissolved into race or class alone. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ **Socialist feminism:** combines patriarchal analysis with Marxist class analysis. (PDF pp.89-90)
- ⚠️ Liberal feminism asks whether barriers are open; radical feminism asks who holds power over bodies and sexuality; socialist feminism asks how capitalism and patriarchy reinforce each other.
- ⚠️ For UPSC, the richest contrast is between formal equality, sexual power and structural political economy.

### 7. Internal debates and criticisms
- ✅ Gauba's own presentation shows that formal rights do not exhaust the feminist question because representation and public participation remain unequal even after suffrage. (PDF pp.91-93)
- ✅ Firestone's account gives biology a very strong explanatory role, but that is one radical position, not a universal feminist doctrine. (PDF p.89)
- ✅ Socialist feminism widens explanation but risks submerging specifically gendered experiences inside general anti-capitalist politics unless carefully handled. (inferred from PDF pp.89-90)
- ⚠️ The enduring debate is whether patriarchy is best seen as a legal exclusion, a sexual power system, or a system intertwined with class and labour.
- ❌ Treating any one school as "the" feminist position produces caricature and weak answers.
