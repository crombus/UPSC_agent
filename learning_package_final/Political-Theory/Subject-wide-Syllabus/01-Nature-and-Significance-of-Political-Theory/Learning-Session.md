#### 2A. Origin and timeline of the field

**Conceptual origin.** ✅ 'Polity', 'politics' and 'political' derive from the Greek *polis*, the ancient city-state: small communities separated by forests, mountains and seas, in which institutions and activities were knit together, and in which every activity aimed at securing the 'good life' for the community counted as part of politics (Gauba, PDF pp.15-16). ✅ Aristotle's description of the human being as by nature a 'political animal' belongs to that world; on this view whoever lived outside the state, or needed no state, was either a beast or an angel (Gauba, PDF p.16).

✅ **The modern narrowing.** Present-day usage is deliberately narrower. We draw a line between public and private, so cabinet and parliamentary decisions, election campaigns, other party activity and people's movements seeking change in law and public policy belong to politics, whereas the object of faith and worship, the content of education, and art and culture do not — *until some regulation of them becomes necessary to maintain public order and safety* (Gauba, PDF p.16).

⚠️ The examinable consequence: the public/private line is a modern analytical convention, not a natural frontier. That is precisely why later feminist and critical writers can argue about where the line is drawn without abandoning the concept of the political.

✅ **Why chronology matters here.** Gauba dates the enterprise to a tradition "spreading over two-and-a-half milleniums", and places the modern controversy in the mid-twentieth century, when exponents of the new political science questioned the continued relevance of traditional political theory (Gauba, PDF pp.23-24). ⚠️ Strip the dates out and the debate collapses into a vague "theory died and came back" narrative — the single most heavily penalised error on this topic.

### SESSION 3 — The Facts-Logic-Values Argument

#### 4. Core argument / chapter spine
1. ✅ Gauba begins by defining the political as the public sphere of rule-making, authoritative decision and regulation for the whole community (Gauba, PDF pp.15-16).
2. ✅ He then defines political theory as systematic knowledge and distinguishes empirical, logical and evaluative statements within that knowledge (Gauba, PDF p.16).
3. ✅ Positivists and behaviouralists try to restrict valid knowledge to empirical and logical statements, but Gauba rejects the move to expel evaluative inquiry from theory (Gauba, PDF pp.16-17, 23).
4. ✅ Political theory must therefore include both political science and political philosophy: science explains political reality, while philosophy asks what goals and principles should guide collective life (Gauba, PDF pp.17-18).
5. ✅ Political science works through observation, generalization, explanation, prediction and prescription; behaviouralism focuses on actual behaviour rather than merely formal institutions (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19).
6. ✅ Political philosophy is not only about ideal blueprints; it performs critical evaluation and concept-clarification, and seeks justification rather than explanation alone (Gauba, PDF pp.19-20).
7. ✅ The significance of political theory lies in control of social life, social criticism and reconstruction, clarification of concepts, and encouragement to mutual respect and toleration (Gauba, PDF pp.21-23).
8. ⚠️ The chapter's basic synthesis is simple: political theory becomes exam-usable only when facts are interpreted through concepts and judged against defensible values.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Political theory converts political facts into judgment without pretending that facts mechanically dictate values.

Analysis: evidence constrains feasible means, while normative reasons assess whether those means serve legitimate ends. Qualification: integration must preserve different standards—empirical claims answer to evidence, evaluative claims to defensible reasons.

### SESSION 4 — Science, Philosophy, Theory and Ideology

#### 5. Essential distinctions
| Distinction | What to say precisely |
|---|---|
| Public vs private | ✅ Politics concerns the public sphere; not every social or cultural activity is political unless regulation for public order or safety becomes necessary (Gauba, PDF p.16). |
| Theory vs ideology | ✅ Theory is dispassionate and disinterested; ideology is interested and justificatory toward a particular distribution of power (Gauba, PDF p.20). |
| Political science vs political philosophy | ✅ Science asks what the real situation is and which regularities govern behaviour; philosophy asks what is good, right or justified in political life (Gauba, PDF pp.17-20). |
| Explanation vs justification | ✅ Raphael's distinction, endorsed by Gauba, is that science seeks explanation whereas philosophy seeks justification (Gauba, PDF p.19). |
| Empirical/logical vs evaluative | ✅ Empirical and logical claims are presented as verifiable; evaluative claims concern value-judgment, but Gauba still treats them as rationally discussable rather than mere emotion (Gauba, PDF pp.16-17, 20). |
| Behavioural vs post-behavioural | ✅ Behaviouralism privileges scientific study of actual behaviour; post-behaviouralism restores concern for values without discarding scientific work on facts (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19, 23). |

#### 5A. The two components in operation

**Political science: the four-step method** (Gauba, PDF p.18)

```text
(a) OBSERVATION      -> sense-experience only; supernatural/metaphysical causation ruled out
        |
        v
(b) GENERALIZATION   -> regularities, relations and correlations between variables
        |                 +-- INDUCTIVE : particular cases -> a general rule
        |                 `-- DEDUCTIVE : a postulated rule -> confirmed in particular cases
        |               stated as a general rule, preferably quantitative and verifiable
        v
(c) EXPLANATION      -> reasons for the rule; without it a correlation may be mere coincidence
        |
        v
(d) PREDICTION AND PRESCRIPTION -> anticipate outcomes; propose measures for efficiency,
                                   stability and satisfaction
```

- ✅ **Behavioural approach:** studies the actual behaviour of human beings in a political situation instead of describing the salient features and legal position of institutions; formal institutions are dissolved into 'systems' and 'processes' so that attention falls on the conduct of political actors, which alone is treated as capable of scientific study (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19).
- ✅ **Post-behavioural approach:** makes the achievements of political science subservient to human values and ends, heralding a revival of concern with 'values' *without compromising scientific method for the study of facts*, and calls for applying political science to overcome the prevailing crises of human life (Gauba, PDF p.19).

**Political philosophy: why 'real versus ideal' is the wrong contrast** (Gauba, PDF pp.19-20)

- ✅ It is often alleged that science deals with the 'real' and philosophy with the 'ideal'. Gauba, following Raphael, rejects this as an inadequate definition of philosophy's scope.
- ✅ Raphael's counter-example is Plato: some classical philosophers did set out ideal forms of society, but that was not their central concern — even in Plato, the purpose of depicting an ideal society is to criticise the existing society and to promote understanding of general social concepts such as justice.
- ✅ Philosophy is therefore recognised by its **critical** function; 'justification' means giving rational grounds either for accepting or for rejecting beliefs that we normally take for granted.
- ✅ **Concept clarification** has three related purposes, with Raphael's own illustrations:

| Purpose | What it does | Raphael's example |
|---|---|---|
| Analysis | ✅ Specifies the elements of a concept, often by way of definition. | ✅ Defining sovereignty as supreme legal authority. |
| Synthesis | ✅ Shows the logical relationship between two concepts. | ✅ Showing that the concept of a right involves that of an obligation. |
| Improvement | ✅ Recommends a definition or use that assists clarity and coherence. | ✅ Recommending that sovereignty be applied only to the legal authority of a state, and not to its coercive power. |

- ✅ Gauba adds that political philosophy itself arose from critical reflection on political activity that existed long before it, exemplifying the Socratic function of 'speaking truth to power' (Gauba, PDF p.20).

**Theory versus ideology: Hacker's test and Gauba's illustrations** (Gauba, PDF p.20)

- ✅ A theory — whether it takes the character of science or of philosophy — must be dispassionate and disinterested. Where the theorist has no personal interest in a political arrangement, the vision of reality and image of the good life are not clouded and the theory is not special pleading.
- ✅ The ideologue, by contrast, is an interested party: the interest may be to defend things as they are, or to criticise the status quo in the hope that a new distribution of power will come into being.
- ✅ Gauba's paired illustrations: upholders of capitalism treat private property as most conducive to justice and social progress, while upholders of socialism treat social ownership of the means of production as the suitable method for the same ends; upholders of nationalism place national pride and interest above every other goal, while supporters of internationalism disregard national boundaries in fixing the index of human progress.
- ✅ Consequences run both ways. When political philosophy is clouded by ideology it is reduced to rationalisation for current or future arrangements; when political science is clouded by ideology it is reduced to partial or selective depiction, producing distorted description or explanation. **Scholarly detachment is the keynote of political theory in the real sense of the term.**
- ⚠️ Answer use: the decisive question in an exam is never "does this argument have values?" but "is this argument protected from testing?" Detachment is a discipline about standpoint, not an absence of commitment.

#### 7. Illustrative examples
- ✅ A question such as "why do riots occur?" belongs to political science when it seeks causal explanation through observation and generalization (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19, 21).
- ✅ A question such as "what is a good society?" belongs to political philosophy because it asks about ends, justification and value (Gauba, PDF p.17).
- ✅ Clarifying whether "freedom" means non-interference, self-development or something else is part of political philosophy's concept-clarifying role (Gauba, PDF pp.19, 22).
- ✅ Gauba warns that vague political language allows demagogues and autocrats to exploit words like liberty, equality and democracy for emotional manipulation or legitimation (Gauba, PDF p.22).
- ⚠️ A strong answer can therefore use data to explain unemployment, but it still needs a philosophical yardstick to judge whether a given policy is fair, free or humane.

#### 7A. The four heads of significance, as the chapter states them

Gauba organises significance under four headings. A "why does this matter" paragraph that names all four, with their own illustrations, outscores a generic tribute to political theory.

```text
SIGNIFICANCE OF POLITICAL THEORY
        |
        +-- 1. CONTROL OF SOCIAL LIFE ......... (political science side)
        |        analogy rail: geology -> earthquakes | physics -> electricity
        |                      medical science -> disease
        |        so political science -> causes of conflict, violence, instability
        |
        +-- 2. SOCIAL CRITICISM AND RECONSTRUCTION ... (political philosophy side)
        |        Plato | Aristotle | St Augustine | Machiavelli | Hobbes | Locke
        |        Rousseau | Hegel | Marx | Mill | Macpherson
        |
        +-- 3. CLARIFICATION OF CONCEPTS
        |        Wolin: parallel usages -> 'authority of the father / church / Parliament'
        |        risk: demagogues and autocrats exploit vague terms
        |
        `-- 4. MUTUAL RESPECT AND TOLERATION
                 Hacker: theory is a never-ending conversation among theorists
```

1. ✅ **Control of social life.** Scientific analysis of political life lets us understand and address social problems. Gauba's analogies are explicit: as geology explains the causes of earthquakes and gives insights for preventing the havoc they cause, and physics enables electricity to be generated from thermal and water resources, and medical science enables diseases to be controlled and cured, so political science enables us to understand the causes of conflict and violence, to secure social development from human resources, and to find remedies for political instability and social crises (Gauba, PDF pp.20-21).
2. ✅ **Social criticism and reconstruction.** Political philosophy is concerned with right and wrong, good and evil in social life. A galaxy of philosophers — Plato, Aristotle, St Augustine, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, Mill and Macpherson — pointed to prevailing ills and offered their own schemes of reconstruction. ✅ Gauba's worked instances: Plato exposed the modus operandi of selfish and cunning politicians in a democracy; Machiavelli described the character of selfish and greedy people; Marx analysed the sources of conflict between owners and non-owners of property; and Macpherson pointed to the intricacies of the contemporary power structure that obstruct the creative freedom of the individual (Gauba, PDF p.21). ⚠️ Crucially, Gauba does *not* ask us to accept any of these as final truth — they supply insights from which we draw our own scheme.
3. ✅ **Clarification of concepts.** Clarification is difficult in political theory because, as Wolin points out, the same words are used for non-political phenomena: 'the authority of the father', 'the authority of the church' and 'the authority of Parliament' are parallel usages, unlike the restricted technical vocabulary of mathematics and the natural sciences. Terms such as authority, social class, liberty, equality, justice and democracy are applied by different schools to different ideas. Agreement on meaning does not force agreement on viewpoint, but it opens the way to dialogue — if a liberal and a socialist accept the same meaning of 'freedom' or 'equality', each is likelier to appreciate the other's position (Gauba, PDF pp.21-22).
   - ✅ The danger of imprecision is stated concretely: some people apply terms cleverly to conceal a weak point in their argument; selfish leaders and demagogues use them to mislead by creating emotional appeal and evading reason; and autocrats use them to legitimise oppressive regimes, **as Mussolini (1883-1945) did in Italy** (Gauba, PDF p.22).
   - ✅ The positive corollary: a precise, widely accepted definition lets each thinker build on sound footing — as an innovative mechanic need not reinvent the wheel to assemble a new machine, a new thinker need not devise fresh terminology to state a position (Gauba, PDF p.22).
4. ✅ **Encouragement to mutual respect and toleration.** The tradition encourages dignified debate between rival viewpoints. As Hacker observes, political theory is a never-ending conversation among theorists; the greatest debates are never resolved, yet the criticisms writers make of each other are the most vivid and illuminating, and the final judgments about political reality and the good life are the responsibility of all who study theory (Gauba, PDF pp.22-23). ⚠️ This is the ethical, not merely academic, payoff: theory generates mutual respect and prompts peaceful resolution of differences.

✅ **How the four heads fold into the three functions.** Taken together, the two branches perform description, criticism and reconstruction: political science, relying on empirical method, specialises in description, while political philosophy, concerned with value-judgment, specialises in criticism and reconstruction (Gauba, PDF p.23).

### SESSION 8 — The Decline and Revival Debate

#### 12. The decline/revival debate in full (self-contained)
##### 12.1 The problem posed
- ✅ Alfred Cobban, in 'The Decline of Political Theory' (*Political Science Quarterly*, 1953), described a decline of political theory under both capitalist and communist systems in the mid-twentieth century, yet still insisted that politics required criteria of judgment that narrow social-science methodology alone could not supply (Gauba, PDF p.25).
  - ✅ His grounds on the capitalist side: these systems were inspired by the idea of 'libertarian democracy' while producing no political theorist of democracy, and were characterised by an overwhelming role of bureaucracy and the creation of a huge military machine, leaving political theory practically no role in sustaining the system.
  - ✅ His grounds on the communist side: a new form of party organisation and the rule of a small oligarchy, under which political theory had taken a back seat.
  - ✅ His diagnosis of the inherited tradition: Hegel and Marx were each interested in only a small part of the universe — Hegel primarily with the 'territorial state' and Marx with the 'proletariat class' — and each sought what was predestined within his own frame of reference, whereas contemporary politics operated on a scale no partial or narrow theory could analyse. He also blamed the logical positivists who concentrated on facts to the exclusion of values.
  - ✅ His conclusion, which candidates routinely omit: **all was not yet lost.** Political science must answer questions the methodology of the social sciences cannot, and must therefore evolve criteria of judgment that will revive its relevance.
- ✅ Seymour Martin Lipset went further in *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics* (1960): in the mid-twentieth-century United States he argued the values of contemporary society had already been decided and the age-old search for the 'good society' had come to an end, because the prevailing form of democracy in that country was "the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation" — a claim tied to the wider end-of-ideology context of `Political-Theory/basic/02_Ideology-and-End-of-Ideology.md`, not a free-standing timeless verdict (Gauba, PDF p.25).
- ✅ David Easton's 1953 complaint, in *The Political System: An Inquiry into the State of Political Science*, held that traditional political theory was based on mere speculation, devoid of acute observation of political reality, and needed rescuing from the study of classics and the history of political ideas (Gauba, PDF p.24).
  - ✅ His historical argument: traditional theory was the product of the turmoil of past ages — it flourished in Greece in pre-Plato days, in Italy in the fifteenth century, in England in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and in France in the eighteenth — periods of widespread social and political upheaval, so it had no relevance in contemporary society.
  - ✅ His disciplinary argument: there had been no outstanding political philosopher after Marx (1818-83) and J. S. Mill (1806-73), so why live parasitically on century-old ideas? Economists and sociologists had produced systematic studies of human behaviour in their own spheres while political scientists lagged behind, failing to acquire research tools capable of accounting for the rise and continuance of fascism or communism.
  - ✅ His institutional grievance: during the Second World War (1939-45) economists, sociologists and psychologists played an active part in decision-making, but political scientists were ignored.
  - ✅ His remedy: build a behavioural political science, closer to the other social sciences, that could take its due place in decision-making. Traditional theory had been concerned with evolving suitable values for society; modern political science need not, because values represent individual or group preferences relative to the social conditions in which they develop, and contemporary society would evolve its own value system from its own experience. Political scientists should focus on building **causal theory** to explain political behaviour.

##### 12.2 Decline of a mode, not disappearance of theory
- ⚠️ The decisive move for an unfamiliar question is this distinction: Cobban, Lipset and Easton(1953) describe the decline of one particular MODE of political theorising — classical, speculative, historicist and explicitly normative — not the disappearance of political theory as an enterprise. Conflating the two is the single most common trap on this topic.
- ✅ Easton's own 1969 post-behavioural turn makes the distinction explicit from inside the behavioural camp itself: he called on political science to become practically relevant to a crisis-ridden society and to restore concern with values without discarding scientific method for the study of facts (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25).

##### 12.3 The revival
- ✅ Leo Strauss, in 'What is Political Philosophy?' (*Journal of Politics*, 1957) and in 'An Epilogue' to *Essays on the Scientific Study of Politics* (ed. Herbert J. Storing, 1962), argued that the new science of politics was itself a symptom of the alleged decline: by adopting a positivist approach it ignored the challenge of normative issues (Gauba, PDF pp.25-26).
  - ✅ His precise objection: empirical theory asserts the equal importance of all social values and denies that certain things are intrinsically high while others are intrinsically low. It therefore obliterates the distinction between men and brutes, "as if it destroys the identity of clean water by mixing it with dirty water".
  - ⚠️ Exam translation: a genuinely value-neutral political science cannot even rank tyranny below justice, because ranking is precisely what it has forsworn.
- ✅ Dante Germino, in *Beyond Ideology: The Revival of Political Theory* (1967), identified two causes of the earlier decline across most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — (a) the rise of positivism, which produced a craze for science, and (b) the prevalence of political ideologies culminating in Marxism — and argued that theory was again in the ascendant (Gauba, PDF pp.26-27).
  - ✅ His conception of the revived enterprise: political philosophy is a critical study of the principles of right order in human social existence, involving inquiry into right and wrong. It is neither reductionist behavioural science, where everything is reduced to sense-experience, nor opinionated ideology, which accepts principles as true without inquiring into their validity; it comprehends both the knowledge of facts and the insight with which that knowledge is grasped.
  - ✅ His key distinctions: political philosophy deals with **perennial problems** of social existence; **detachment is not ethical neutrality**, so a political philosopher cannot remain indifferent to the political struggles of the time as a behaviouralist would claim; and the gulf between the traditionalist and behaviouralist components had become so wide that they could not simply be 'reunited'.
  - ✅ His charge and his warning: behavioural political theory has often implicitly or uncritically endorsed the policies and practices of the established order instead of performing the Socratic function of 'speaking truth to power'; and full recovery of critical political theory cannot be achieved within the positivist universe of discourse.
- ✅ Herbert Marcuse (1898-1979) warned that when the language of social science tries to conform to the language of natural science, it tends to lend support to the status quo, because scientific terminology is then defined in terms of operations and behaviour capable of observation and measurement, leaving no scope for a critical vision (Gauba, PDF p.27).
  - ✅ His worked example: when people's participation is estimated only by the number of voters who turn up at elections, we never ask whether the prevailing electoral system conforms to the spirit of democracy. On that method social science ceases to be an instrument of social inquiry and becomes an instrument of social control.

##### 12.4 What actually happened after the 1970s
- ✅ Gauba records that since the 1970s the dispute between political science and political philosophy has largely subsided, and that the convergence ran in **both** directions (Gauba, PDF p.27).
- ✅ From the science side, Easton himself showed renewed concern with values in his post-behavioural approach. From the philosophy side, exponents of political philosophy did not hesitate to test their assumptions by empirical method: Karl Popper (1902-94), an eminent exponent of scientific method, drew conclusions about social values; John Rawls (1921-2002) adopted empirical method in arriving at his principles of justice; C. B. Macpherson (1911-87) attacked the empirical theory of democracy propounded by Joseph Schumpeter (1883-1950) and Robert Dahl and advanced his own radical theory of democracy; and Marcuse and Jürgen Habermas brought strong empirical insight to their critical analysis of contemporary capitalism.
- ✅ The chapter's closing formula follows from this: political science, like other social and natural sciences, enables us to strengthen our **means**, but we must resort to political philosophy to determine our **ends** — and because means and ends are interdependent, the two play complementary roles.
- ⚠️ Answer use: this paragraph is the strongest available refutation of the "theory died" story, because the reconciliation is evidenced by named philosophers adopting empirical method, not merely asserted.

##### 12.5 Critical verdict (answer-ready)
- ⚠️ The debate operates on two levels at once: a methodological dispute over what counts as valid evidence (Easton 1953 vs Strauss/Germino), and a civilizational dispute over whether politics can ever avoid argument about ends (Strauss/Germino/Marcuse vs Lipset's "good society already found" thesis). Gauba's closing formula — science strengthens means, philosophy judges ends (Gauba, PDF p.27) — settles the first dispute in favour of integration without conceding the second, i.e. theory can be scientifically disciplined and normatively serious at the same time.
- ⚠️ Defensible one-line verdict for an unfamiliar question: political theory did not die; a narrow, ahistorical, facts-only variant of it lost credibility, and the discipline recovered by re-admitting values without discarding evidence.
- ❌ Do not write "political theory ended in the 1950s and was revived in the 1970s" as flat chronology. Cobban, Lipset and Easton(1953) are three distinct claims with different scope, and Easton's own 1969 correction already begins the revival from inside behaviouralism itself, well before the 1970s consolidation Gauba records (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25, 27).

#### 14. Doctrine architecture: premises, mechanism, conclusion
- ✅ **Premises:** (a) the political is the public/common sphere of authoritative rule (Gauba, PDF pp.15-16); (b) knowledge divides into empirical, logical and evaluative statements (Gauba, PDF p.16); (c) positivists/behaviouralists wish to restrict valid knowledge to the first two categories alone (Gauba, PDF pp.16-17).
- ✅ **Mechanism:** political life generates factual questions ("why do riots occur?") and value questions ("what is a good society?") simultaneously; a theory that excludes evaluative statements cannot fully account for political phenomena, so political science supplies explanation while political philosophy supplies justification and concept-clarification — two angles on the same subject-matter (Gauba, PDF pp.17-20).
- ✅ **Conclusion:** political theory as a whole must integrate both components; its significance lies in control of social life, social criticism/reconstruction, concept-clarification and cultivation of mutual respect and toleration (Gauba, PDF pp.21-23).
- ⚠️ This premises → mechanism → conclusion spine is the fastest route to an unfamiliar question such as "Can political theory be value-free? Discuss," without needing to memorise the chapter verbatim.

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION

### Practice design

- Questions 1-24 are hard core GS/Prelims diagnostics; Questions 25-32 are remedial close-option repairs.
- Formats are deliberately varied: definition control, multi-statement, sequence, matching, chronology, elimination, application, evidence and inference.
- The correct option rotates strictly `A -> B -> C -> D`, repeated eight times across the 32 questions, giving 8 A, 8 B, 8 C and 8 D.
- Because the key is a fixed rotation, it carries no diagnostic value: correct and distractor options are matched for length and register, so an answer can only be found by reasoning about content.
- Each of the 128 options carries its own explanation and each question closes with a distinct examiner trap.

### MCQ 1

Which statement most accurately defines political theory in Gauba's opening formulation?

- A. Systematic knowledge of political phenomena, including factual and evaluative inquiry.
- B. A specialised study of constitutional offices and their legal powers.
- C. A normative blueprint for an ideal state independent of political facts.
- D. A strategy by which political groups justify an existing distribution of power.

**Answer: A**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A is the source-grounded definition and preserves the empirical-logical-evaluative range.
- **B:** B narrows the field to formal institutions, while the chapter includes behaviour, concepts and values.
- **C:** C reduces theory to ideal construction, ignoring science, criticism and concept clarification.
- **D:** D describes ideology's interested justificatory role rather than political theory as a whole.

> **Examiner trap:** Treating the word 'theory' as either an institutional manual or a utopian plan.

### MCQ 2

Consider the following statements: 1. An empirical statement rests on observation. 2. A logical statement rests on reasoning. 3. An evaluative statement judges what is right or desirable. Which are correct?

- A. 1 and 2 only
- B. 1, 2 and 3
- C. 2 and 3 only
- D. 1 and 3 only

**Answer: B**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A wrongly excludes evaluative judgment from the scope of political theory.
- **B:** B correctly reproduces all three statement-types in the chapter.
- **C:** C wrongly omits the observation-based empirical category.
- **D:** D wrongly omits logical or inferential statements.

> **Examiner trap:** Assuming that because evaluative claims are not experimentally verified, they are absent from theory.

### MCQ 3

The most useful distinction drawn by D. D. Raphael for Political Theory is that:

- A. science studies public institutions whereas philosophy studies private morality.
- B. science predicts the future whereas philosophy records the past.
- C. science seeks explanation whereas philosophy seeks justification.
- D. science is value-laden whereas philosophy is necessarily value-neutral.

**Answer: C**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A invents a public-private division between the disciplines that Raphael does not make.
- **B:** B confuses method with temporal subject matter.
- **C:** C states Raphael's explanation-versus-justification criterion.
- **D:** D reverses the usual value-neutrality claim and misstates both fields.

> **Examiner trap:** Choosing a broad science/philosophy contrast instead of Raphael's precise functional test.

### MCQ 4

Andrew Hacker, whom Gauba follows here, insists that political theory be kept distinct from ideology. Which feature marks that distinction most clearly?

- A. Theory examines only values and ultimate ends, whereas ideology confines itself to recording observed facts about political behaviour.
- B. Theory belongs to democratic orders, whereas ideology appears only under authoritarian rule.
- C. Theory avoids collective purposes, whereas ideology alone discusses goals for political action.
- D. Theory tests claims through evidence and criticism; ideology justifies a preferred distribution of power.

**Answer: D**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A inverts both sides: theory includes empirical, logical and evaluative inquiry, while ideology is an interested justification rather than a fact-recording exercise.
- **B:** B is false because ideology may operate in any political order.
- **C:** C is false because political theory explicitly examines collective ends.
- **D:** D captures Hacker's test: a theorist without a personal stake reasons dispassionately, whereas the ideologue is an interested party defending or attacking a system of power.

> **Examiner trap:** Using subject matter to separate theory and ideology when the decisive issue is standpoint and testing.

### MCQ 5

Andrew Hacker's 'double role' of the political theorist implies that:

- A. factual understanding and reflection on political goals must inform one another.
- B. political philosophy should absorb political science completely.
- C. a theorist should alternate between academic and party-political employment.
- D. scientific findings become valid only after ideological endorsement.

**Answer: A**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A correctly links the scientist's grasp of facts with the philosopher's concern for goals.
- **B:** B destroys the complementarity Hacker defends.
- **C:** C literalises 'double role' into an occupational claim.
- **D:** D subordinates inquiry to ideology, the opposite of Hacker's warning.

> **Examiner trap:** Reading 'part scientist and part philosopher' as two jobs rather than two intellectual tasks.

### MCQ 6

Which combination best states the principal functions of political theory in Gauba's conclusion?

- A. Observation, legislation and adjudication.
- B. Description, criticism and reconstruction.
- C. Prediction, propaganda and mobilisation.
- D. Classification, administration and enforcement.

**Answer: B**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A mixes a method with functions of state organs.
- **B:** B is the chapter's express three-function formulation: science specialises in description, philosophy in criticism and reconstruction.
- **C:** C wrongly treats propaganda as a theoretical function.
- **D:** D substitutes administrative activities for intellectual functions.

> **Examiner trap:** Confusing the functions of political theory with functions of government.

### MCQ 7

Which sequence most closely follows Gauba's account of scientific inquiry in politics?

- A. Prescription -> observation -> explanation -> generalisation -> verification.
- B. Explanation -> evaluation -> observation -> reconstruction.
- C. Observation -> generalisation -> explanation -> prediction and prescription.
- D. Generalisation -> ideology -> prediction -> verification.

**Answer: C**

**Option-by-option explanation**
- **A:** A begins with prescription before any evidence, then appends verification to a rule that has not yet been formed, reversing the chapter's stated order.
- **B:** B inserts evaluation and reconstruction into the scientific sequence.

### ORIGINAL GS MAINS PRACTICE WITH MODEL SOLUTIONS

### Original GS Mains Model 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain why political theory cannot be reduced either to empirical political science or to normative political philosophy. Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** 'Explain' asks for a reasoned account rather than a debate. Set out the two reductions separately, defeat each with a named proposition, and close on the integration that survives both objections. Listing thinkers without refuting both reductions under-answers the directive.

**Model answer (136 words):**

Political theory is systematic knowledge of political phenomena. It cannot be reduced to political science because public choices also require judgments about justice and legitimate ends; nor can it be reduced to philosophy because prescriptions must understand institutions, behaviour and consequences. D. D. Raphael clarifies the division: science seeks explanation, while philosophy seeks justification. Thus, observation may explain why riots occur, but it cannot by itself decide which restrictions are fair. Conversely, a moral ideal unsupported by facts may be infeasible or harmful. Andrew Hacker's claim that every political scientist is partly scientist and partly philosopher captures this interdependence. The synthesis does not erase standards: empirical claims remain answerable to evidence, while evaluative claims require coherent public reasons. Political theory is therefore a disciplined bridge—science clarifies facts and means; philosophy clarifies concepts and judges ends.

**Why this earns marks:** It refuses both reductions in turn, anchors each refusal in a named proposition from Raphael and Hacker, and closes on the means-and-ends formula instead of a general tribute to the subject.

### Original GS Mains Model 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish political theory from ideology and explain why the distinction matters. Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** Two directives are joined. 'Distinguish' requires one decisive criterion rather than a list of differences, and 'explain why it matters' requires a stated consequence. The criterion is standpoint and openness to testing; the consequence is that identical vocabulary can illuminate or conceal power.

**Model answer (143 words):**

Political theory and ideology both use concepts such as liberty, equality and order, but they differ in intellectual posture. In Gauba's account, theory submits claims to evidence, logic, criticism and revision; ideology defends or challenges a distribution of power from an interested standpoint and often treats its premises as settled. The distinction matters because identical vocabulary can either illuminate politics or conceal domination. For example, a theory of property must test its effects and justificatory principles, whereas an ideology may present one property regime as self-evidently just. Yet the contrast is not absolute: theorists have social locations and theory can become ideological when it hides assumptions or ignores counter-evidence. The practical safeguard is transparent premises, reasoned dialogue and revisability. Thus, political theory is not value-free, but it remains different from ideology because it treats even its own values as open to critical justification.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies a single decisive criterion, illustrates it with rival property regimes, and then concedes that theory itself can become ideological - the qualification that turns a definition into an argument.

### Original GS Mains Model 3 — 10 marks

**Question:** How do concept clarification and reasoned disagreement explain the significance of political theory? Answer in 150 words.

**Demand decode:** 'How' demands a mechanism, not an assertion of importance. Show the three clarification operations, then show precisely what clarification achieves and what it cannot achieve. Significance must be derived from the mechanism rather than announced.

**Model answer (137 words):**

Political concepts are powerful precisely because they are general and contested. Gauba, drawing on D. D. Raphael, treats clarification as analysis of a concept's elements, synthesis of its relations and improvement of its use. This matters because words such as liberty, authority, equality and democracy can otherwise become slogans through which demagogues conceal weak arguments. Clarification does not compel agreement; it reveals what the disagreement is about. Andrew Hacker's image of political theory as a continuing conversation adds the democratic function: rival positions expose their premises, recognise strengths and weaknesses and remain open to persuasion. The limitation is that shared definitions do not remove conflicts of interest or values. Nevertheless, precise concepts make those conflicts more honest and peaceful. Political theory therefore contributes not only knowledge but also a public ethic of reason-giving, mutual respect and toleration.

**Why this earns marks:** It moves from Raphael's analysis-synthesis-improvement to the democratic function Hacker describes, and it states the limit: clarification makes disagreement precise without dissolving conflicts of interest.

### Original GS Mains Model 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Examine the claim that the decline of political theory was the decline of one mode of inquiry rather than the disappearance of theory. Answer in 250 words.

**Demand decode:** 'Examine' requires testing a stated proposition. Separate the three decline claims, weigh the revival evidence against them, and deliver a verdict that accepts, rejects or reformulates the claim. Narrating the debate without adjudicating it under-answers the directive.

**Model answer (232 words):**

The mid-twentieth-century decline thesis is persuasive only if 'decline' refers to a traditional mode of political theorising, not to the disappearance of systematic reflection on politics. David Easton's 1953 critique targeted speculation, dependence on classic texts and weak causal tools; his behavioural remedy prioritised observable conduct and explanatory theory. Alfred Cobban diagnosed marginalisation under both capitalist and communist systems, yet still insisted that politics required criteria of judgment beyond narrow social-science method. Seymour Martin Lipset made a different, contextual claim: United States liberal democracy had approximated the good society, apparently narrowing the normative search. These arguments weakened classical, historical and openly evaluative theory, but they did not remove theoretical assumptions about relevance, evidence or desirable order. The strongest counter-evidence came from the revival. Strauss argued that value-neutral inquiry could not rank tyranny below justice. Germino recovered political philosophy as critical inquiry into right order, while Marcuse warned that measurement could normalise domination. Most decisively, Easton's 1969 post-behavioural turn restored relevance and values without abandoning scientific inquiry. The decline thesis therefore identifies a real methodological correction: theory had to become more empirical and contemporary. It fails, however, as a death certificate. Political inquiry continued to interpret concepts and choose ends even when it claimed neutrality. The better verdict is transformation: an insufficiently empirical mode declined, a facts-only remedy proved incomplete, and political theory revived as a more self-conscious integration of explanation and justification.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates Easton, Cobban and Lipset instead of merging them, uses Easton's own 1969 reversal as decisive internal counter-evidence, and reformulates the claim as transformation rather than death.

### Original GS Mains Model 5 — 15 marks

**Question:** Discuss the contribution of behaviouralism and post-behaviouralism to the development of political theory. Answer in 250 words.

**Demand decode:** 'Discuss' requires balanced coverage of both movements plus a linking judgment. Each movement needs a stated contribution and a stated limit; treating post-behaviouralism only as a rejection of behaviouralism misses the continuity being tested.

**Model answer (219 words):**

Behaviouralism and post-behaviouralism contributed successive corrections to political theory. Behaviouralism redirected inquiry from formal institutions and inherited texts towards actual political behaviour. In Gauba's reconstruction, Easton's 1953 programme demanded observation, comparison, causal theory and closer engagement with other social sciences. Its durable contribution was methodological discipline: political claims had to be tested against evidence rather than protected by tradition. Its limitation followed from its strongest ambition. By suspending the quest for values, behaviouralism could explain conduct and consequences but could not decide which political ends deserved pursuit. Strauss's objection exposes the problem: a method that refuses qualitative judgment cannot adequately distinguish tyranny from justice. Marcuse adds that measurement may become administrative adjustment and stabilise the status quo. Post-behaviouralism, associated with Easton's 1969 turn, answered this failure by demanding relevance, action and responsiveness to social crisis while retaining scientific investigation of facts. Germino's distinction between scholarly detachment and ethical neutrality explains the new balance: inquiry need not become partisan merely because it acknowledges normative responsibility. The post-behavioural contribution is therefore not a return to unsupported moralism. It preserves behaviouralism's empirical gains and reconnects them with criticism, public purpose and justified ends. Together, the two movements transformed political theory from either textual speculation or narrow scientism into a plural discipline in which evidence constrains claims and normative argument evaluates their significance.

**Why this earns marks:** It credits behaviouralism with methodological discipline before naming its value-blindness, uses Strauss and Marcuse as the objections, and shows post-behaviouralism preserving the empirical gain.

**Model answer (206 words):**

Political theory strengthens General Studies answers by supplying the concepts and standards through which governance facts become political analysis. Its first contribution is clarification. Raphael's explanation-justification distinction prevents a candidate from treating administrative performance as proof of legitimacy. Coverage, speed or compliance are empirical achievements; fairness, dignity and voice require additional reasons. Second, theory exposes hidden power. Wolin's common-sphere idea identifies authoritative collective decisions, while feminist criticism qualifies any rigid public-private boundary by showing that informal domination may have public consequences. Third, theory enables criticism and reconstruction. Held's warning against leaving politics to ignorance, self-interest or will to power explains why institutions must be assessed against defensible ends, not described mechanically. The strongest objection is that theoretical language can become abstract or ideological. That risk is real when concepts replace constitutional provisions, current evidence or implementation detail. The answer is disciplined use, not exclusion: make a claim, attach a named proposition or verified Indian example, explain what it proves, and state the limit. Political Theory in this repository therefore remains a GS/Prelims foundation, primarily for GS-II; it does not own questions from other UPSC papers or substitute for Polity and Governance facts. Properly used, it converts data into judgment while keeping judgment accountable to evidence.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives three distinct contributions with named anchors, states the abstraction objection at full strength, and resolves it with a rule of disciplined use plus an explicit ownership boundary.

### 2. Scope, assumptions and conceptual boundary
- ✅ This debate emerges inside Gauba's broader definition of political theory as including both political science and political philosophy; it is not a separate problem from the opening definition (Gauba, PDF pp.17-20, 23-27).
- ✅ The behavioural challenge is directed chiefly against traditional speculation, historical classicism and explicit normativity, not against all theory whatsoever (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25).
- ⚠️ The key exam boundary is this: post-behaviouralism criticizes value-neutral closure, but it does not reject empirical inquiry or scientific discipline.
- ⚠️ The decline/revival debate is best read as a dispute over the proper balance between explanation and justification, not as a simple fight between "modern" and "old" writers.

### 3. Direct-book argument reconstruction
1. ✅ Gauba reports David Easton's 1953 complaint that traditional political theory relied on speculation, lacked acute observation of political reality, stayed tied to classics and history of ideas, and had little relevance for contemporary politics (Gauba, PDF p.24).
2. ✅ Easton argued that there had been no outstanding political philosopher after Marx and J.S. Mill and that political scientists lacked research tools capable of explaining fascism, communism and policy relevance in wartime decision-making (Gauba, PDF p.24).
3. ✅ His remedy was behavioural political science: causal theory, closer integration with other social sciences, and suspension of the quest for values in favour of explaining political behaviour (Gauba, PDF p.24).
4. ✅ Gauba then notes Easton's own 1969 shift: the post-behavioural revolution asked political science to address contemporary crisis and restored concern with values while retaining scientific investigation (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25).
5. ✅ Alongside Easton, Cobban described decline under both capitalist and communist systems and still insisted that political science must evolve criteria of judgment beyond social-science methodology alone (Gauba, PDF p.25).
6. ✅ Lipset went further by claiming that in the United States the long search for the good society had effectively ended because liberal democracy itself approximated it in operation (Gauba, PDF p.25).
7. ✅ Against this, Strauss argued that positivist political science ignored normative issues and flattened qualitative distinctions among values; Germino identified revival with political philosophy; Marcuse warned that a natural-science style social science could become an instrument of social control (Gauba, PDF pp.25-27).
8. ✅ Gauba closes by saying that, since the 1970s, political science and political philosophy increasingly test and inform each other rather than remaining sealed apart (Gauba, PDF p.27).

### 3A. Germino's revival roster (enrichment only)
- ✅ In *Beyond Ideology: The Revival of Political Theory* (1967), Germino located the ascendancy of theory in the work of Michael Oakeshott, Hannah Arendt, Bertrand de Jouvenel, Leo Strauss and Eric Voegelin (Gauba, PDF p.26).
- ✅ He expanded that list in a subsequent paper (1975) to include John Rawls, C. B. Macpherson, Christian Bay, Robert Nozick, Herbert Marcuse, Jürgen Habermas, Alasdair MacIntyre and Michael Walzer, whose works he treated as reviving the grand tradition of political philosophy (Gauba, PDF p.26).
- ⚠️ Use for enrichment only. A core GS answer needs the *argument* of the revival — right order, perennial problems, detachment without ethical neutrality — not the roster of names. Reciting the list without the argument gains nothing.
- ⚠️ Note also Germino's harder claim, which the Basic session deliberately keeps brief: he held that the gulf between traditionalist and behaviouralist components had grown so wide they could not be 'reunited', and that full recovery of critical theory was impossible within the positivist universe of discourse. This is a stronger position than Gauba's own complementarity settlement, and the two should not be blurred (Gauba, PDF pp.26-27).

### 4. Definitions and distinctions
| Concept | Advanced clarification |
|---|---|
| Causal theory | ✅ A theory explaining relations of cause and effect, central to Easton's behavioural ambition (Gauba, PDF p.24). |
| Behaviouralism | ✅ A scientific approach centred on actual political behaviour, causal explanation and methodological affinity with other social sciences (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19, 24-25). |
| Post-behaviouralism | ✅ Easton's 1969 turn toward applying science to crisis and reconnecting inquiry with values and public relevance (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25). |
| Positivism | ✅ The view that scientific method is the only source of true knowledge and that knowledge rests on sense-experience (Gauba, PDF p.26). |
| Political philosophy | ✅ Critical inquiry into right and wrong, right order, justification and perennial social questions, not reducible to behavioural metrics (Gauba, PDF pp.19-20, 26-27). |
| Detachment | ✅ Germino's point is that detachment is not the same as ethical neutrality; one may critically engage without becoming partisan ideology (Gauba, PDF p.26). |

| Thinker | Position to retain |
|---|---|
| David Easton (1953) | ✅ Traditional theory was speculative, historically bound and methodologically weak; political science needed causal, behavioural, social-scientific foundations (Gauba, PDF p.24). |
| David Easton (1969) | ✅ Political science should become practically relevant to crisis-ridden society and revive concern with values without discarding scientific inquiry (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25). |

### 6. Competing perspectives
| Perspective | Strongest claim | Gauba's treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviouralist | ✅ Valid theory should explain political behaviour causally and scientifically (Gauba, PDF p.24). | ✅ Gauba takes the scientific demand seriously but does not let it exhaust theory (Gauba, PDF pp.18-19, 27). |
| Positivist/value-neutral | ✅ Values are subjective preferences or beyond reliable knowledge (Gauba, PDF pp.16-17, 26). | ✅ Rejected as too narrow because public policy needs value-judgment and justification (Gauba, PDF pp.17, 23). |
| Classical normative/traditionalist | ✅ Political inquiry must ask about right order, justice and the good society (Gauba, PDF pp.19-20, 26). | ✅ Preserved, but not as licence for ungrounded speculation. |
| Post-behavioural synthesis | ✅ Facts and methods matter, but relevance, values and crisis-response matter too (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25, 27). | ✅ This is the chapter's most balanced settlement. |

### 7. Internal debates and criticisms
- ✅ Easton's early criticism bites hardest where traditional theory merely repeats classic texts without explaining contemporary political behaviour; Gauba does not deny that problem (Gauba, PDF p.24).
- ✅ Yet Easton's own post-behavioural turn is itself evidence that pure behavioural closure was insufficient for political inquiry in times of crisis (Gauba, PDF pp.24-25).
- ✅ Cobban and Lipset represent different decline narratives: one says modern systems marginalize theory; the other says liberal democracy has already solved the central normative search, at least in affluent Western context (Gauba, PDF p.25).
- ✅ Strauss rejects behavioural flattening of value-rankings; Germino rejects any reunion that strips theory of perennial moral concerns; Marcuse rejects metrics that convert critique into social control (Gauba, PDF pp.25-27).
- ⚠️ The debate therefore has two levels: a methodological dispute over evidence, and a civilizational dispute over whether politics can ever avoid argument about ends.
- ⚠️ Gauba's own synthesis is closer to "critical empiricism" than either crude positivism or free-floating moralism.

### 8. Comparative matrix
| Issue | Early behavioural critique | Normative revival critique | Gauba's settlement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem in old theory | ✅ Too speculative, too historicist, too detached from causal analysis (Gauba, PDF p.24). | ✅ Science-first inquiry neglects right and wrong, hierarchy of values and critical vision (Gauba, PDF pp.25-27). | ⚠️ Both criticisms capture part of the truth. |

| Role of empirical method | ✅ Central and foundational (Gauba, PDF p.24). | ✅ Not rejected, but subordinated to broader critical purposes by many revivalists (Gauba, PDF pp.26-27). | ✅ Essential for understanding facts and strengthening means (Gauba, PDF p.27). |
| Public relevance | ✅ Behavioural science wanted policy relevance and decision-making status (Gauba, PDF p.24). | ✅ Critics wanted relevance in the sense of speaking truth to power and resisting status quo legitimation (Gauba, PDF pp.26-27). | ⚠️ Relevance requires both explanatory power and normative courage. |
| Final verdict | ❌ Political theory is dead. | ❌ Science is useless. | ✅ Neither claim holds; theory survives as an integrated enterprise (Gauba, PDF p.27). |

### 9. Application without current-affairs dependence
- ⚠️ If asked to analyse a policy on preventive detention, a behavioural approach can study compliance, opposition and institutional effects, but a philosophical approach must still ask whether liberty is being unjustifiably restricted.
- ⚠️ If asked whether elections deepen democracy, science can measure turnout and participation, while philosophy must judge whether those patterns amount to meaningful freedom, equality and legitimacy.
- ⚠️ If a regime claims "efficiency" as its justification, behavioural data may show administrative output, but political philosophy asks whether efficiency without justice or consent is politically defensible.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### From polis to public sphere: the origin that fixes the boundary

- **Etymology and first scope:** 'polity', 'politics', 'political' come from the Greek *polis*; in the city-state every activity aimed at the community's 'good life' counted as politics.
- **Aristotle:** the human being is by nature a 'political animal'; whoever lives outside the state is either a beast or an angel.
- **Modern narrowing:** politics = the public sphere. In: cabinet and parliamentary decisions, election campaigns, party activity, movements for change in law and policy. Out: faith and worship, the content of education, art and culture — **until regulation is needed for public order and safety**.
- **Tradition length:** roughly two-and-a-half millennia; the modern controversy is mid-twentieth century.
- **Trap:** treating the public/private line as natural. It is an analytical convention, which is why it can be contested without abandoning the concept of the political.

### Political boundary and definition

- **Political:** public/common sphere of authoritative collective decision; Wolin anchors what is common to the community. The public-private line is analytical, not absolute.
- **Political theory:** systematic knowledge of political phenomena.
- **Three statement-types:** empirical (observation), logical (reasoning), evaluative (right/good/desirable). Evaluative claims are not sense-verifiable, but remain reasoned claims.
- **Answer line:** political theory begins where public power must be both explained and justified.

### Science, philosophy and theory

- **Political science (four steps):** observation (sense-experience only; supernatural/metaphysical causation excluded) -> generalisation (inductive: particular to general; deductive: general tested on particulars; preferably quantitative and verifiable) -> explanation (reasons for the rule, otherwise a correlation is mere coincidence) -> prediction and prescription (efficiency, stability, satisfaction).
- **Behavioural approach:** dissolves formal institutions into 'systems' and 'processes' to reach the actual behaviour of political actors. **Post-behavioural approach:** subordinates those achievements to human values and ends, restoring values *without compromising scientific method for the study of facts*.
- **Political philosophy:** not 'ideal versus real'. Raphael's Plato point — even an ideal society is depicted in order to criticise the existing one and clarify concepts such as justice.
- **Clarification triad with Raphael's own examples:** analysis (sovereignty = supreme legal authority); synthesis (a right involves an obligation); improvement (restrict sovereignty to a state's legal authority, not its coercive power).
- **Raphael:** explanation versus justification. **Catlin (1957):** both are components of theory; asking "What is the good society?" is already philosophy. **Hacker (1961):** theorist is part scientist and part philosopher.
- **Socratic function:** philosophy arose from critical reflection on political activity and speaks truth to power.
- **Settlement:** science clarifies facts, causes and means; philosophy judges ends and legitimacy.

### Theory and ideology

- **Hacker's test:** theory — as science or as philosophy — must be dispassionate and disinterested; the ideologue is an interested party, whether defending things as they are or attacking them in the hope of a new distribution of power.
- Theory tests validity through evidence, argument, counter-position and revision.
- **Ideology (defined):** ideas and arguments used to defend an existing or proposed distribution of power, accepted as true without inquiry into validity; a ruling class propagates ideology to strengthen itself, opponents use it to mobilise replacement of the order. **Both directions are ideological.**
- **Paired illustrations:** capitalism/private property versus socialism/social ownership; nationalism's priority for national pride and interest versus internationalism's disregard of national boundaries.
- **Damage both ways:** ideology reduces philosophy to rationalisation and science to selective, distorted depiction. **Scholarly detachment is the keynote of political theory.**
- Same vocabulary can occur in both; openness to criticism is the decisive test.
- **Qualification:** theory can become ideological when it hides assumptions or suppresses evidence.

### Significance

Four heads, each with its own named illustration:

1. **Control of social life** (science side) — as geology explains earthquakes, physics generates power and medicine cures disease, political science explains conflict, violence and instability.
2. **Social criticism and reconstruction** (philosophy side) — Plato on cunning politicians in a democracy; Machiavelli on selfish and greedy people; Marx on owner/non-owner conflict; Macpherson on power structures obstructing creative freedom. None is accepted as final truth; each supplies insight.
3. **Clarification of concepts** — Wolin's parallel usages ('authority of the father / of the church / of Parliament'); shared meaning enables dialogue without forcing agreement; vagueness lets demagogues conceal weak arguments and lets autocrats legitimise oppression, **as Mussolini (1883-1945) did in Italy**; a settled definition means a new thinker "need not reinvent the wheel".
4. **Mutual respect and toleration** — Hacker: theory is a never-ending conversation; the great debates are never resolved, yet the criticisms are the most illuminating part.

- **Fold-in:** the two branches together perform description (science) plus criticism and reconstruction (philosophy).
- **Held (1991):** without systematic theory politics risks surrender to the ignorant, the self-interested or those with a "will to power".
- **Trap:** clarification makes disagreement precise; it does not guarantee agreement.

### Decline and revival chronology

- **1953 Easton**, *The Political System*: speculation, classicism, no causal tools; theory flourished only in ages of upheaval (pre-Plato Greece, fifteenth-century Italy, sixteenth-seventeenth-century England, eighteenth-century France); no major philosopher after Marx (1818-83) and Mill (1806-73); political scientists sidelined in Second World War (1939-45) decision-making -> behavioural political science.
- **1953 Cobban**, 'The Decline of Political Theory' (*Political Science Quarterly*): libertarian democracy with no theorist of democracy, bureaucracy and a huge military machine; communist party oligarchy; Hegel's 'territorial state' and Marx's 'proletariat class' were partial frames; logical positivists also to blame — **yet "all was not yet lost": criteria of judgment must be evolved.**
- **1960 Lipset**, *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics*: contextual United States claim that its democracy was "the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation"; never universalise it.
- **1957/1962 Strauss**, 'What is Political Philosophy?' and 'An Epilogue': empirical theory asserts the equal importance of all values and denies the intrinsically high and low, so it cannot rank tyranny below justice — "as if it destroys the identity of clean water by mixing it with dirty water".
- **1967 Germino**, *Beyond Ideology*: decline caused by (a) positivism's craze for science and (b) ideologies culminating in Marxism; revival = political philosophy as critical inquiry into right order; perennial problems; detachment is not ethical neutrality; the two components cannot simply be 'reunited'.
- **Marcuse (1898-1979):** measuring participation only by voter turnout stops us asking whether the electoral system conforms to the spirit of democracy; social inquiry then becomes social control.
- **1969 Easton**, APSA presidential address: post-behavioural relevance, crisis-response and values, with scientific investigation retained; pure science becomes applied science.
- **1970s-80s:** revival with new value horizons — environmentalism, feminism, human rights, subaltern social justice. Convergence is two-way: Popper (1902-94) drew conclusions on social values, Rawls (1921-2002) used empirical method for principles of justice, Macpherson (1911-87) attacked the empirical democracy theory of Schumpeter (1883-1950) and Dahl, and Marcuse and Habermas analysed capitalism with empirical insight.
- **Verdict:** decline of one mode, not disappearance of the discipline. Science strengthens **means**; philosophy determines **ends**; means and ends are interdependent.
