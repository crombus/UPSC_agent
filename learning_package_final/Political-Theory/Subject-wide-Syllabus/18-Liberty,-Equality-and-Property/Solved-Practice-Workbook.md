**2025 Q1(e) [10] — Discuss the salient features of equality according to J.S. Mill.**

**Model:** Mill's equality concerns equal civic standing and freedom to develop individuality, not an instruction to make every person identical. His criticism of inherited subordination, notably women's inequality, challenges conventions that prevent individuals from pursuing work, education and public roles. The case against excluding women cannot be met by alleging their natural incapacity while law denies them the chance to demonstrate ability. Equal liberty therefore requires removal of artificial barriers; competition and personal choice can still produce diverse lives. His harm principle supplies a presumption against coerced conformity, but Mill's arguments on women's status and education require their own moral defence rather than being mechanically deduced from it. In India this distinction helps separate formally open institutions from social constraints on women's usable access. Equality for Mill is a condition of genuine individuality and civic respect, qualified by the equal freedom of others, not numerical levelling.

**2025 Q4(a) [20] — How are both equality and liberty inadequate as social and political ideals without justice? Discuss.**

**Model:** Liberty without an account of justice may allow owners' freedom of contract to coexist with workers' desperate dependence. Equality without justice may demand identical treatment where need and historical disadvantage differ. Justice asks which restraints and differences are defensible and by which procedures. Mill protects choice against mere majority offence while requiring justification for coercion; Green asks whether schooling and material security make choice effective. Rawls prioritises equal basic liberties, then fair opportunity and inequalities benefiting the least advantaged. Nozick objects that even desirable end states cannot excuse violation of just holdings, though he recognises rectification where acquisition was unjust. A property regime can secure personal independence and yet concentrate control over employment and public voice. In India formal equal access to schooling is not fair opportunity if inherited deprivation prevents participation. Justice therefore connects rights to their distribution, background conditions and rectification, while constraining redistributive authority through reason-giving, proportionality and contestability. Neither liberty nor equality should be discarded: each becomes politically adequate when linked to defensible institutions and equal standing.

**2024 Q2(b) [15] — Critically evaluate the concepts of liberty and equality as political ideals.**

**Model:** Liberty protects a person's scope for chosen action, whereas equality affirms equal moral worth and opposes unjust status privilege. Berlin's negative liberty exposes censorship; Green's enabling freedom identifies alterable barriers such as unaffordable education. Treating the first as sufficient ignores the poor person's unusable permission; treating the second as authority to prescribe a “true” life invites paternalism. Formal equality before law is essential, but the same examination rule may yield unequal chances where schooling and resources diverge. Conversely substantive equality does not require identical outcomes; need, function and responsibility matter. A defensible order protects non-interference and equal legal standing while correcting specified background disadvantages with reviewable, proportionate measures. Property illustrates the tension: a home secures independence, but concentrated productive ownership can constrain others' choice. Liberty and equality are thus complementary under justifiable institutions, not interchangeable ideals or inevitable enemies.

**2022 Q1(d) [10] — “Complete liberty may lead to inequality while order and restrictions imply a necessary loss of freedom.” Critically discuss.**

**Model:** Unrestricted owner choice may leave workers formally free to contract but dependent on one employer; licence for the strong can diminish equal liberty. A rule against monopoly interferes with owners but may increase workers' choices. Berlin's negative liberty correctly registers that legal restraint narrows one option; republican non-domination asks whether contestable law removes another actor's unchecked power. Green adds that schooling and security enable people to exercise choices already nominally available. Yet order alone is not freedom-enhancing: an arbitrary ban on criticism or an unreviewable regulator can replace private with public domination. The appropriate test is whether a lawful, proportionate and contestable restriction protects equal agency against demonstrable harm or dependency. Complete licence invites inequality, but reasoned restraints need not imply an overall loss of freedom.

**2019 Q4(b) [15] — Does liberty put limitations to equality? Discuss.**

**Model:** Liberty limits coercive attempts to enforce equality, but equality also makes liberty available to more people. Mill's harm principle prevents compelling adults merely to conform to a preferred life plan; Berlin warns that a state claiming to know citizens' higher self may suppress dissent. Yet formally open jobs are not fair opportunities when inherited poverty prevents preparation. Rawls gives equal basic liberties priority while placing fair opportunity and the difference principle on background institutions; Dworkin distinguishes resource disadvantages from consequences of responsible choice. Nozick argues that patterned distribution can breach just entitlement, but actual histories may require rectification. The tension therefore depends on which equality is sought: equal civic standing and fair opportunity can support liberty, while forced identical outcomes or unreviewable officials can diminish it. Protect choice and dissent while addressing demonstrable structural barriers under public rules.

**2026 Q3(c) [15] — “Negative liberty seems to embody the idea of ultimate personal freedom, whereas positive liberty enables individuals to achieve their full potential. Discuss.”**

**Model:** The contrast identifies two distinct questions rather than a ranking of human worth. Negative liberty, in Berlin's account, asks whether others obstruct a person's chosen action; it protects expression and movement from coercion. Berlin's positive pole concerns self-mastery and carries a risk if rulers impose what they call the citizen's real will. Green's enabling positive freedom instead attends to socially alterable obstacles: a pupil may be free to enter school yet lack transport or nutrition. Neither provision nor non-interference suffices alone. Republican non-domination adds a third test: a benevolent master can refrain from interference while retaining arbitrary power; answerable law may interfere yet reduce dependence. Policies supporting capability should preserve personal choice, judicial remedy and reviewability, not dictate an official ideal of self-realisation. Negative and enabling positive freedoms can reinforce one another, with the vocabulary of positive freedom stated explicitly.

**2022 Q2(c) [15] — Explain the difference between the notion of equity and equality with reference to Marxian philosophy.**

**Model:** Equality can refer to the same legal standing or rule for everyone; equity asks what differentiated provision is required to address unequal need. Marx's critique of bourgeois right shows the limit of applying an equal measure to people with different capacities and family burdens. Even remuneration proportional to labour can reproduce unequal outcomes because workers' needs differ. Marx's higher-phase formula, contribution according to ability and provision according to need, points beyond the equivalent exchange that structures a formal equality of labour contribution. However it does not follow that an administrator may ignore liberty or that all differences are unjust. An Indian welfare policy can illustrate the distinction: identical formal access may leave deprived households unable to use it, while need-sensitive provision can improve effective standing. Equity is a corrective principle for unequal conditions; equality remains the aspiration of common dignity rather than mechanical sameness.

**2021 Q1(b) [10] — How does Rousseau distinguish between natural and artificial inequality ? Explain.**

**Model:** Rousseau separates natural or physical differences, such as bodily strength or age, from moral or political inequalities established by convention, such as privilege attached to wealth and rank. This is not a claim that all natural differences are just: rather, the object of political analysis is how institutions turn differences into dependence and power. Property claims and social esteem may make one person's choices answerable to another. Equal legal standing is necessary to counter inherited rank but can remain hollow where economic vulnerability persists. An Indian example is a formally open competition in which inherited educational advantage determines who can participate meaningfully. Reform should target remediable privilege and oppressive dependence, not promise identical natural characteristics. Rousseau's distinction gives a causal starting point for substantive equality without reducing equality to a demand that everybody have the same capacities or possessions.

**2020 Q1(a) [10] — Is the concept of liberty realizable in the modern technological society? Explain.**

**Model:** Technology can increase freedom of information, association and access to services, but its design may also concentrate the capacity to monitor, exclude and manipulate users. Berlin's negative liberty asks whether people are prevented from expressing views or choosing alternatives; Green's enabling approach asks whether digital skills and access make formal choices usable. A republican test asks whether a platform or authority can change conditions arbitrarily without notice or appeal. For an Indian applicant facing an online-only service, availability on paper does not equal meaningful use if connectivity is absent. Conversely regulation that guarantees accessible channels and reviewable decisions may increase autonomy while limiting a provider's discretion. Liberty is realisable where technological systems protect privacy, choice, capability and contestability; it is not guaranteed simply because tools multiply, and it need not disappear whenever lawful protective rules constrain providers.

**2018 Q2(a) [20] — How far can liberty and equality be considered as distinctive features of democracy ? Discuss.**

**Model:** Democracy promises equal political standing and freedom to participate in collective rule. Equal voting status contrasts with inherited privilege, while speech, association and dissent let citizens contest authority between elections. But procedure alone is insufficient: intimidation can make a vote unusable and socio-economic dependence can silence nominally free speech. Berlin protects non-interference as a necessary sphere; Green draws attention to material prerequisites; Rawls combines equal basic liberties with fair opportunity and an institutional test for distributive inequality. Democracy also creates risks: majorities may restrict dissent, and formally universal rules may exclude those unable to access education or public forums. India offers the conceptual contrast between an equal franchise and unequal ability to organise, speak or hold office. Civil protections, enabling services and contestable decisions make political equality more than arithmetic. Liberty and equality distinguish the democratic ideal but are not automatically realised by calling a state democratic: justice requires institutions that defend dissent and remove remediable barriers without treating all outcomes as identical.

### Original GS Mains Model 1 — 10 marks

**Question:** Explain why Mill's distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct does not make every socially consequential act coercible. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Mill's harm principle creates a presumption of liberty for competent adults: coercion against their will is justified presumptively to prevent harm to others, not merely because conduct is disliked, offensive or imprudent. Core analysis: The self-regarding and other-regarding distinction identifies the primary direction of an act's effects, but social consequence is broader than harm. Expression, consumption and unconventional living may influence others, alter norms or impose discomfort without violating a protected interest. If every indirect effect counted as harm, the principle would license majority morality and destroy the experimental individuality Mill values. Regulation therefore requires a specified victim, sufficiently serious setback, causal connection and proportionate response. That difficulty shifts the burden to justification; it does not erase the presumption for liberty. Conclusion: Mill's principle is therefore a strong presumption for individuality under a harm-based burden of proof, not a mechanical rule that converts every social effect into legitimate coercion.

### Original GS Mains Model 2 — 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish formal equality, substantive equality, equality of opportunity and equality of outcome. Can differential treatment serve equality? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Equality contains several standards that answer different questions. Formal equality removes explicit legal privilege, while substantive equality asks whether equal status can actually be used under unequal social conditions. Core analysis: Equality of opportunity concerns genuinely fair access to offices and advantages, not merely an open rulebook. Equality of outcome examines whether final distributions leave avoidable deprivation or domination intact; it need not demand identical shares and may instead defend thresholds or a social minimum. Differential treatment can serve equality where identical rules reproduce a demonstrated disadvantage. A legitimate measure must identify the barrier, connect the classification to its removal, protect individual standing and remain proportionate, contestable and open to revision. Conclusion: Differential treatment serves equality only when it removes a specified barrier to equal standing or fair opportunity, remains proportionate and reviewable, and does not become an unexamined substitute for structural reform.

### Original GS Mains Model 3 — 10 marks

**Question:** Compare Berlin's two concepts, Green's positive freedom and republican non-domination. Does MacCallum dissolve their differences? Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Berlin distinguishes negative liberty as non-interference from positive liberty as self-mastery, while warning that a ruler may coercively claim to embody a person's higher self. Green uses a different positive language: freedom requires social conditions for moral development and meaningful action. Core analysis: Republican non-domination shifts the question from actual interference or capacity to the status of dependence under arbitrary power. The benevolent-master case shows why a person may face little current interference yet remain unfree through the master's standing capacity and impunity. MacCallum's formula - X is free from Y to do or become Z - places all freedom claims in one triadic grammar and exposes dichotomies. Berlin, Green and republicanism retain different diagnoses and different risks: neglect of capacity, paternalism and institutional overbreadth. Conclusion: MacCallum reveals a shared grammatical structure, but Berlin, Green and republicanism remain substantively distinct because they identify different constraints, purposes and institutional dangers.

### Original GS Mains Model 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Compare Locke, Hegel and Marx on the moral significance of property. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Locke, Hegel and Marx treat property as morally significant through labour, personhood and social production respectively. Their disagreement concerns both the source and the limits of ownership. Core analysis: Locke grounds initial appropriation in labour under enough-and-as-good and spoilage provisos; money bypasses spoilage and enables accumulation, while survival of the first proviso remains contested. Hegel treats an external property sphere as the first embodiment of free will within Abstract Right, explaining why secure control matters for personality without proving an unlimited title to accumulation. Marx shifts from individual title to productive relations: personal use is not the main target, whereas private control of the means of production separates workers from product, activity and social power. Property then becomes alienation and class domination. Critical evaluation: Labour can justify an initial claim, personhood a protected sphere, and social production a structural critique. No one argument by itself validates every inherited, concentrated or productive holding. An Indian land-title dispute illustrates the distance between registered ownership, usable possession and equitable access to productive resources. Social regulation can protect a household’s secure dwelling while constraining monopoly power over labour; its justification must identify affected interests, lawful means and public accountability. Conclusion: Property is defensible neither as an absolute natural title nor as an undifferentiated evil: its justification depends on acquisition, personhood, social function and whether ownership enables autonomy or domination.

### Original GS Mains Model 5 — 15 marks

**Question:** The apparent conflict between liberty and equality is mediated by the distribution of property and social power. Evaluate through Rawls, Dworkin and Nozick. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Liberty and equality appear opposed when property is treated as a pre-political domain immune from scrutiny. Once ownership is recognised as a distribution of resources and power, the dispute becomes one about background institutions, responsibility and history. Core analysis: Rawls gives equal basic liberties lexical priority but requires fair equality of opportunity and permits inequality only where it benefits the least advantaged. Dworkin's equality of resources uses hypothetical auction and insurance to neutralise brute luck while retaining responsibility for option luck. Nozick rejects patterned end-state rules and asks whether holdings arose through just acquisition and transfer under a modified Lockean proviso, with rectification for injustice. These standards expose different defects: structurally unfair starting points, unequal resource endowments and coercive or unjust historical title. An Indian land-title dispute illustrates why documented title, accessible procedure and contestability are distinct from redistribution. Further development: The comparison also changes the unit of evaluation. Rawls examines the basic structure, Dworkin the resource position of responsible agents, and Nozick the historical chain of holdings. A conclusion must therefore state whether institutions, endowments or transactions are being judged before calling an inequality unjust. Critical evaluation: The theories cannot be fused. A defensible judgment protects basic agency and title while testing whether property institutions reproduce avoidable dependence and whether rectification is sufficiently specified. Conclusion: The liberty-equality conflict is best treated as a dispute over fair background institutions, resources and historical entitlement, with property justified only where its distribution survives all three forms of scrutiny.

### Original GS Mains Model 6 — 15 marks

**Question:** Can affirmative action and redistribution enhance equal liberty without becoming paternalistic or arbitrary? Discuss using Mill, Berlin, Green, Rawls and Nozick. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (original practice, not official):**

Introduction: Affirmative action and redistribution can enlarge liberty where deprivation and inherited exclusion make formally equal choice unusable. Their legitimacy nevertheless depends on preserving agency and constraining public discretion. Core analysis: Mill's harm principle resists coercion based merely on a person's own good, but his broader egalitarian and social reforms show that liberty need not mean laissez-faire. Green treats education, security and material capability as conditions of effective freedom. Rawls protects equal basic liberties while requiring fair opportunity and benefit to the least advantaged. Berlin supplies the warning: officials must not claim authority to impose citizens' true purposes. Nozick adds historical-title and rectification tests and rejects patterned seizure of just holdings. Affirmative action should therefore be justified through specified compensatory, anti-subordination, representational or opportunity mechanisms rather than the label of benevolent policy. Further development: Republican non-domination adds a further institutional test: a corrective measure should reduce arbitrary dependence rather than replace private domination with unreviewable official discretion. Effective notice, reason-giving, accessible appeal and periodic evaluation connect Green's enabling purpose to Berlin's and Nozick's limits on coercive power. Critical evaluation: Correction becomes arbitrary when classifications lack evidence, remedies are disproportionate or affected people cannot contest them. It enhances equal liberty when rules are transparent, reviewable, time-sensitive and directed at demonstrable barriers. Conclusion: Group-sensitive correction can enlarge equal liberty when it removes demonstrable barriers under transparent, proportionate and contestable rules, while preserving agency, basic liberties and historical-title scrutiny.
