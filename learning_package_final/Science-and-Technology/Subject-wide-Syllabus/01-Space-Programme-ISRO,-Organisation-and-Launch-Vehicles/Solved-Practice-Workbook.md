## Six original Mains questions with model answers

### Original Mains 1 — 10 marks
**Question:** Distinguish the roles of DoS, ISRO, IN-SPACe and NSIL. Answer in about 150 words.
**Model answer:** DoS provides the governmental umbrella and policy direction for India’s civilian space activity. ISRO is the public technical agency: its centres design launchers, propulsion and spacecraft and run missions. VSSC leads launcher development, liquid-propulsion work involves LPSC and IPRC, and SDSC-SHAR integrates vehicles and conducts launch operations. The centres are not independent space ministries. IN-SPACe, under DoS, promotes, authorises and supervises non-government space activity; a permission is not a launch-service purchase. NSIL is the public-sector commercial arm, arranging launch, satellite and technology-transfer services; a contract is not a regulatory clearance. Indian Space Policy 2023 allocates these functions and encourages private participation, but policy is not a comprehensive parliamentary space statute. International responsibility for national private activity remains with the State under the Outer Space Treaty. Thus differentiation can increase industrial capacity while preserving mission assurance, authorisation and continuing supervision; collapsing all four bodies into “ISRO” obscures both accountability and practical delivery.

### Original Mains 2 — 10 marks
**Question:** Differentiate PSLV, GSLV, LVM3 and SSLV without reducing the comparison to payload size. Answer in about 150 words.
**Model answer:** First specify the mission: orbit, spacecraft mass, deployment profile and assurance requirements. PSLV is a versatile workhorse for Earth-observation, navigation and mixed payloads; its name does not confine every deployment to polar orbit. GSLV adds a cryogenic upper stage for heavier communication-class missions to geosynchronous transfer orbit. Transfer injection must not be confused with a satellite’s subsequent circularisation in geostationary orbit. LVM3 is a heavier operational vehicle and the base for human-rated Gaganyaan development; its ordinary service record alone does not certify crew carriage. SSLV addresses smaller payloads with a responsive, lower-infrastructure launch model and is not merely a miniature PSLV variant. Solid, earth-storable liquid and cryogenic stages serve different parts of ascent, with distinct propellant handling and performance. Vehicle selection therefore depends on energy, stage design, ground infrastructure and acceptable risk, not a single “maximum payload” quoted without orbit and configuration.

### Original Mains 3 — 15 marks
**Question:** Explain how propulsion and orbit requirements shape launcher choice. Answer in about 250 words.
**Model answer:** Launchers expend propellant to accelerate spacecraft onto a required trajectory. Initial acceleration depends on thrust relative to total weight, not on thrust in isolation; as propellant burns and spent stages are discarded, upper stages need not replicate the first-stage thrust. Their exhaust efficiency, dry mass, control and intended vacuum operation matter. Solid motors supply robust high-thrust ascent but ordinary designs cannot be shut down or throttled at will. Earth-storable liquid systems have different handling characteristics; cryogenic oxygen and hydrogen provide high upper-stage efficiency but impose low-temperature storage and complex engineering. A developmental semi-cryogenic oxygen-kerosene upgrade is not a flown hydrogen upper stage. Orbit choice creates a second constraint. Low Earth orbit is an altitude region; polar describes inclination; sun synchronism requires precession giving comparable local illumination. These are not synonyms. A communication spacecraft may instead be launched into elliptical GTO; its own propulsion then raises and circularises the orbit to an equatorial geostationary slot. Reaching LEO with one mass does not prove the same launcher can inject a heavier mass into transfer orbit. Hence PSLV can serve varied deployment profiles, GSLV uses cryogenic propulsion for heavier GTO-class missions, LVM3 supports heavier missions and a separately qualified human-rated path, while SSLV serves smaller responsive launches. An honest vehicle comparison specifies spacecraft mass, orbit, stage configuration and whether performance is planned or demonstrated. India’s sovereign access also needs range safety, propellant logistics and integration capacity alongside engine design.

### Original Mains 4 — 15 marks
**Question:** Assess Indian space reform as institutional differentiation rather than privatisation. Answer in about 250 words.
**Model answer:** Indian space reform separates public research, authorisation and commercial service rather than simply transferring all public duties to private operators. DoS remains the governmental umbrella and ISRO retains technical research, national missions and launch-system development. IN-SPACe promotes and authorises non-government actors and exercises supervision. NSIL organises commercial access to launch, satellite and technology-transfer services. A private company may therefore need permission from one institution while contracting with another and obtaining engineering support from a third. Indian Space Policy 2023 expresses this division but is executive policy, not a comprehensive domestic space statute. That distinction matters for enforceable authorisation conditions, safety practice and liability allocation. Under the Outer Space Treaty the State remains internationally responsible for its national private space activities, which require authorisation and continuing supervision; the Liability and Registration Conventions also make launch and object records public-law concerns. SSLV technology transfer to HAL can expand industrial manufacture, but it does not dissolve ISRO or assign IN-SPACe’s licensing function to HAL. Nor does a new contract certify mission reliability. The reform’s promise is shorter commercial pathways and more production capacity while public institutions focus on advanced technology and national applications. Its risks include regulatory conflicts, inadequate mission assurance and treating proposed capacity as an operational achievement. A sound test of success asks whether authorisation, contracting, certification, range safety and post-launch supervision actually function separately and coherently. Reform is therefore institutional specialisation with continuing public accountability, not withdrawal of the State.

### Original Mains 5 — 20 marks
**Question:** Evaluate India’s access-to-space ecosystem through vehicles, infrastructure, industry and mission assurance. Answer in about 250 words.
**Model answer:** Sovereign access to space requires a complete delivery chain rather than possession of a launch-vehicle design. Vehicle diversity addresses distinct mission energies and market needs: PSLV supports varied Earth-observation and mixed launches; GSLV employs a cryogenic upper stage for heavier transfer-orbit missions; LVM3 provides heavier lift and is the base for separately human-rated development; SSLV addresses responsive small-satellite demand. Orbit and spacecraft mass must be specified before comparing capacities. Technical readiness extends beyond engines. Launch sites require integration bays, pads, propellant storage, tracking, range safety and tested operational procedures. A newly approved Third Launch Pad or a complex at Kulasekarapattinam adds prospective capacity but cannot be counted as commissioned merely because it was announced. Industrial scale matters as well: SSLV technology transfer to HAL may widen manufacturing and commercial pathways, while NSIL can offer services and IN-SPACe can authorise non-government participation. Neither transfers reliability responsibility to a press release. Mission assurance tests components, validates interfaces and investigates failures before claims about operational cadence. Human carriage requires additional redundancy, monitoring, margins and abort provisions. SLV-3 and ASLV belong to the learning lineage, whereas NGLV is an approved future development plan, not a proven reusable service. Success should therefore be measured through dated evidence of manufactured vehicles, qualified facilities, successful mission-specific injections and sustained oversight. The result is resilient national access, not merely an impressive rocket inventory.

### Original Mains 6 — 20 marks
**Question:** Design a status-disciplined roadmap from the current fleet to future launch capacity. Answer in about 250 words.
**Model answer:** Begin by classifying evidence. SLV-3 and ASLV illustrate earlier technology learning; PSLV, GSLV and LVM3 are operational launcher families in the canonical source; SSLV belongs to a separate small-payload track. NGLV has Cabinet approval as a development programme envisaging a recoverable first stage. Approval must not be described as flight, demonstrated recovery or routine reflight. A semi-cryogenic oxygen-kerosene stage under development is not interchangeable with a proven cryogenic hydrogen-oxygen upper stage. Next define each capability gate: ground tests of propulsion and control; integration with a specified vehicle; mission-specific qualification; flight evidence; and only then repeatable service. For crewed missions, an existing LVM3 base needs human-rating evidence on failure tolerance, monitoring and abort, not merely an uncrewed launch record. Infrastructure follows similar gates: approved Third Launch Pad and planned Kulasekarapattinam complex require construction, commissioning and range integration before they can increase cadence. Transfer of SSLV technology to HAL supports industrial production but does not remove public authorisation or safety oversight. IN-SPACe should assess private activity and NSIL should organise appropriate commercial contracts while ISRO preserves advanced technical capability. Finally publish dated, configuration-specific evidence for payload, orbit, anomalies and operational status. This roadmap links fleet, industry and physical range capacity without converting intentions into achievements.
