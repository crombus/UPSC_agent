### PYQ DEMAND CARD 1 — 2018 GS-I Q20

**Demand:** Audited ledger rendering: communalism from power struggle or relative deprivation; argue with illustrations. 15 marks, 250 words. This is not claimed as verbatim paper text.

**Model answer:** Communalism converts religious identification into a claim that communities have opposed political interests. Neither a struggle for power nor relative deprivation alone explains when that conversion succeeds. Power struggle identifies organisers seeking control of office, status or resources; deprivation identifies a perceived unfair comparison that their appeal can exploit.

Consider a municipal allocation dispute: unequal market licences may produce resentment, but an organiser must frame individual claims as the grievance of an entire community for a communal campaign to emerge. The rival explanation that deprivation always produces conflict is weakened when residents use neutral appeal mechanisms instead. Equally, competition for office need not be communal if parties contest services rather than ascribed identities. Historical grievances can lend credibility to a mobilisation, but their continued existence during years of peaceful cooperation shows why timing, organisation and incentives matter. A rumour about a personal incident can accelerate polarisation where residential segregation has reduced corrective contact; it is a trigger, not evidence that one faith is inherently conflict-prone.

The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 illustrates an institutional attempt to limit one category of historically framed site dispute, but it cannot substitute for even-handed local policing or equitable access to services. Thus power competition often supplies the organisational vehicle and perceived deprivation its persuasive material; local institutional impartiality helps determine whether either yields violence. The same mechanism varies with neighbourhood networks and administrative credibility.

**Why this answers the directive:** It argues against a false either-or, offers concrete Indian municipal and statutory illustrations without inventing a riot event, and states the limits of each explanation.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 2 — 2023 GS-I Q20

**Demand:** Audited ledger rendering: discuss the impact of the post-liberal economy and ethnic identity on communalism. 15 marks, 250 words. No verbatim wording or official answer is claimed.

**Model answer:** The post-liberal economy has not mechanically produced communalism. It has changed employment, mobility and competition unevenly, giving identity entrepreneurs new material with which to frame grievances. Ethnic identity names a sense of collective belonging; communalism specifically mobilises religious identity as opposed political interests. The two should not be collapsed.

Where new service jobs arrive unevenly, a comparison between neighbourhoods can become a story of group exclusion. A local organiser may turn a market-allocation or housing grievance into collective resentment, while segregated residence reduces routine correction of exaggerated claims. Digital rumour can accelerate that shift, but neither liberalisation nor a forwarded message is sufficient for violence. The countervailing effects deserve equal weight: shared supply chains and joint workplaces may create interdependence, while transparent allocation and impartial complaint-handling can prevent a grievance from being communalised. Different castes, classes and genders within either community do not gain or lose uniformly; ethnic labels cannot stand in for disaggregated evidence.

For example, an equal-access municipal licensing process is directly relevant to livelihood competition, whereas Section 4 of the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 addresses a distinct form of historically framed religious-site contest. Confusing these policy domains misses the question's economic mechanism. The defensible conclusion is conditional: economic changes reshape the field of competition and opportunity, but organisation, cross-group networks and trustworthy institutions determine whether identity becomes a communal line of mobilisation. No national reform alone explains a specific incident.

**Why this answers the directive:** It addresses both economic and identity dimensions, specifies mediating mechanisms and contrary effects, and avoids a false claim that reform caused particular riots.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish communalism from religious belief and explain why the distinction changes the policy recommendation. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** Religious belief concerns worship and conviction; communalism begins when an actor organises religious identity into a political or social claim that one community's interests conflict with another's. The difference is observable in action, not intensity of faith: neighbours may observe festivals differently yet cooperate in a shared market, whereas a campaign demanding exclusive control of that market on religious grounds is communal mobilisation.

Consequently the remedy must address organisation, incentives and impartial access, not punish piety. An administrator confronting an unverified message about a shop dispute should verify facts quickly, hear complainants fairly and protect both groups' access to the market. Neutral policing and documented grievance redress matter more than a prohibition on private worship; the latter could itself erode constitutional religious freedom. The Places of Worship Act illustrates a particular legal restraint on site disputes, not a licence to classify believers as communal. Examine local evidence before attributing motive to any whole community.

**Examiner check:** Definition → contrasting market example → proportional, rights-respecting policy; no generalisation about adherents.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Question:** Distinguish the majority, minority and separatist forms of communalism and state the limits of the typology. Answer in about 150 words.

**Model answer:** The typology distinguishes the position and political objective of an identity-based mobilisation. Majority communalism can assert the primacy of a dominant group, as in a local demand for exclusive use of a shared public facility. Minority mobilisation may arise defensively against exclusion or seek group advantage; an equal-access petition, however, is not automatically communalism. Separatist communalism goes further by demanding distinct political or territorial status; seeking cultural protection alone does not establish separatism.

These are analytical possibilities, not fixed traits of religious populations. A powerful majority's exclusive claim and a disadvantaged minority's protective response may both invoke identity but do not have equivalent institutional leverage or effects. In a municipal housing complaint, test the remedy requested, the history of exclusion and administrative conduct rather than infer motive from a group's size. The categories cannot explain an episode without tracing organisers, grievances and local power.

**Examiner check:** Each form defined with a bounded illustration, followed by asymmetry and an explicit classification limit.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine whether communalism is better explained as a power struggle or as relative deprivation. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** An either-or answer misses their interaction. A power struggle explains *who* competes for office, resources or symbolic authority; relative deprivation explains *why* an unfair comparison resonates with potential supporters. Communalism arises when these concerns are organised around religious identity and narrated as incompatible community interests.

Suppose two neighbourhoods dispute municipal market licences. The published allocation pattern might support a grievance, but unequal outcomes alone do not demonstrate group hostility. A political organiser can select the grievance, attach an identity label and mobilise supporters; that is a power mechanism. Conversely, when the municipality discloses criteria and offers fair appeals, disadvantaged applicants can pursue redress without communal alignment. Primordial attachment matters because people value group belonging, but belonging does not render antagonism inevitable. The coexistence of shared work, worship and commerce across communities is incompatible with such determinism.

Historical-site claims show a different terrain: Section 4 of the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 freezes religious character as of 15 August 1947, subject to exceptions, to constrain repeated conversion disputes. That law cannot resolve housing exclusion or a biased police response. Residential segregation can magnify both perceived unfairness and the effect of rumour, whereas fair complaint recording and visible impartial action can break the link from grievance to mobilisation. Neither theory alone predicts violence: identify the organised actor, the precise comparison, the local institution and the independent evidence before assigning causation.

**Examiner check:** Distinct definitions, interaction demonstrated twice, statutory example used within scope and a non-deterministic verdict.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Question:** Examine the structural and triggering layers in the escalation of a local incident into communal violence. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer:** Structural conditions create susceptibility; triggers may activate it, but neither supplies a universal riot sequence. In a town with segregated housing, scarce municipal contracts and mistrust of complaint handling, residents have fewer routine opportunities to test hostile claims. Those are long-run vulnerabilities, not proof that a riot is imminent.

Now suppose an altercation at a shop is circulated as an unverified community-wide attack. The altercation is the incident; its recoding through a rumour is the immediate trigger mechanism. Organisations may amplify it for electoral or commercial gain. Polarisation then discourages joint trading; loss of contact can feed back into distrust. Yet this chain may stop: rapid verification, even-handed recording of complaints and credible leaders' communication can prevent an individual's act being attributed to an entire faith. A similar rumour in an area with trusted police and dense intergroup networks may lead to no violence. Conversely, an organised attack may bypass parts of the suggested chain.

Prevention must therefore work on two timescales. Immediately publish verifiable facts, protect threatened residents and investigate actual perpetrators without group blame. Over time correct discriminatory allocation, strengthen impartial policing and rebuild safe commercial and civic interaction. The Places of Worship Act addresses a narrower category of historically framed site disputes, not every proximate trigger. Conclude only after checking case-specific evidence: rumour, grievance and segregation are risk factors; violence is contingent on mobilisation and institutional response.

**Examiner check:** Specifies structural versus proximate causes, gives a staged yet non-universal mechanism and matches intervention to timescale.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess institutional impartiality as the primary lever of inter-community trust repair. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** Trust after a communal incident depends less on the numerical presence of officers than on whether residents expect equal protection and fair investigation. Institutional impartiality is thus a necessary organising lever, though not the only condition of repair.

At the first stage, a district administration should verify a circulating account of a local dispute through credible channels. This interrupts rumour-driven collective blame; mere denial without evidence may deepen suspicion. Next, police must receive complaints from every affected neighbourhood, secure evidence and proceed against individual wrongdoing without assuming collective guilt. Published procedures and review of refused complaints make impartiality observable rather than a promise. Courts and administration then have complementary roles: due process limits arbitrary action while transparent decisions on relief and access signal that equal citizenship extends beyond the police station.

Impartial action has a social mechanism: people who trust official fact-finding are less likely to rely only on identity intermediaries, so a new minor incident is less readily narrated as an existential threat. Shared markets and neighbourhood projects can then restore repeated non-coercive contact. Yet trust does not follow automatically from a single reconciliation meeting. If segregated housing, unequal municipal licensing or unresolved material harm remains, fair policing alone cannot remove the grievance; relief and accessible remedies must accompany it. Nor should forced 'harmony' silence legitimate complaints or require victims to abandon due process.

For a bounded legal comparison, Section 4 of the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 attempts to constrain one kind of historical-site contest. It does not itself establish that every local officer has acted fairly. Measure repair by sustained access to complaint channels, consistency of action and safe everyday contact, not merely police deployment or meeting attendance. A sequence of verification, accountability, material redress and contact best converts formal neutrality into lived security.

**Examiner check:** Specifies institutional mechanisms, counterconditions and an observable test for durable trust; distinguishes statute from implementation.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Question:** Assess the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 as a social stability mechanism. Answer in about 300 words.

**Model answer:** The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 uses a fixed legal baseline to prevent recurrent political contests over the religious character of places of worship. Section 4 preserves that character as it stood on 15 August 1947. The social logic is prospective stability, not a judicial verdict on every claimed historical wrong.

A predetermined rule limits incentives for campaigners to repeatedly convert distant site grievances into present-day disputes. In a locality where competing religious-site narratives circulate, this can reduce one source of uncertainty and signal equal expectations of restraint. But statutory precision is indispensable. The Act excludes the Ayodhya matter and provides other exceptions, including specified ancient or historical monuments and settled or decided disputes; saying 'Ayodhya alone' or 'every worship place without exception' is wrong. Freezing religious character is also not identical to deciding private ownership or determining every question of title. A candidate must distinguish legal character, title and the conduct of contemporary administration.

The Act's stabilising effect depends on credible, even-handed enforcement and on public understanding of its scope. An inaccurate claim that a newspaper listing has definitively upheld or struck down the statute can itself intensify uncertainty. The canonical owner's July 2026 check supports only that the Act remained on the statute book and that the retrieved Supreme Court public material did not show a final merits order; any later status assertion needs a dated official case-status/order check. Equally, the Act cannot resolve unequal housing, employment competition or rumours about a personal incident: these require accessible grievance redress and impartial investigation.

The qualified verdict is that the statute constrains one pathway by which history may be weaponised in the present. It is a social-stability tool within defined exceptions, not a general substitute for constitutional equality, trustworthy institutions and inter-community contact.

**Examiner check:** Gives exact section and date, distinguishes exceptions and title, respects dated judicial-status uncertainty and evaluates practical limits.
