# Urbanisation: Problems and Remedies — Solved Practice Workbook

## BASIC MCQS / REMEDIATION
Attempt the full question set before consulting the separate key. Options test causal mechanisms, boundary conditions and evidence—not vocabulary matching.

### Q1. Two cities offer similar average wages, but the larger attracts more jobseekers. Which additional mechanism best explains this?

B. The city-size difference proves every migrant will obtain a permanent formal contract.

D. The average wage measure alone determines the direction of every household move.

### Q2. A garment worker follows a cousin to a metropolitan workshop, while another cannot afford the initial rent. What can this contrast establish?

B. Network referrals can reduce entry costs, but housing constraints can still interrupt chain migration.

## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE
The labels and route statuses below follow the audited local ledgers. Descriptive PYQs have model answers, not purported official UPSC solutions; no unverified objective key is supplied.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 1 — 2022 GS-I Q9

**Demand:** Audited ledger rendering: relate growth of Tier-2 cities, the new middle class and a culture of consumption (10 marks; 150 words).

**Verification / routing:** Audited 2018–2023 GS-I ledger; 2022 paper is scanned, so wording here is a demand rendering, not claimed verbatim.

**Model answer:**

Tier-2 city growth is not merely a smaller version of metropolitan expansion: new work and services can create a locally visible middle class and new ways of signalling status. Consider Indore as an illustration of a regional commercial centre, not evidence of a quantified citywide trend. Clustering of retail, logistics and services can improve matching for salaried and self-employed workers. More predictable earnings make durable goods, education and leisure attainable; organised retail, media and credit then convert purchasing power into aspirational, conspicuous consumption. The causal sequence is jobs and incomes → class formation → market infrastructure and social aspiration → changing consumption.

However, consumption also differentiates households. A delivery worker servicing a mall may be paid irregularly and live in insecure rental housing; the mere presence of brands does not show that such workers have joined the new middle class. Credit-financed spending is not identical to sustained prosperity, and access varies with gender, occupation and neighbourhood. Tier-2 growth thus links the new middle class to consumer culture through employment and aspiration, but the relationship describes uneven social change rather than proof of inclusive urbanisation.

**Why this earns marks:** Links all three terms through employment, aspiration and retail while qualifying the Indore illustration and unequal class gains.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 2 — 2023 GS-I Q18

**Demand:** Audited ledger rendering: does urbanisation lead to segregation or marginalisation of the poor? (15 marks; 250 words).

**Verification / routing:** Audited 2018–2023 GS-I ledger; scanned 2023 official paper prevents a verbatim-text claim.

**Model answer:**

Urbanisation creates proximity between classes, but whether this becomes segregation depends on land, housing and service institutions. It is not a necessary effect of density. In Mumbai, the juxtaposition of informal housing and expensive commercial land illustrates how access to work may coexist with exclusion from formal shelter; this illustration does not establish a uniform citywide pattern.

Agglomeration brings migrants and jobs. When affordable rental supply and serviced land lag, high prices and restrictive tenure rules push low-income households into insecure settlements. Without recognised addresses or claims, water, sanitation and electricity access may be harder to obtain; commuting costs then turn residential separation into unequal access to work. Remote clearance may supply better-built units while severing a street vendor's market and a domestic worker's employer network. This is marginalisation by location and administrative rules, not a character trait of migrants. Different definitions of “slum” also limit comparisons across jurisdictions.

Yet informal settlements often retain mutual-aid networks, and mixed neighbourhoods or in-situ upgrading show that spatial segregation is neither total nor irreversible. Part IXA of the Constitution provides an urban local-government framework, but a listed Twelfth Schedule function does not guarantee resources or neighbourhood participation. Serviced land, rental options, affordable transit, tenure-sensitive connections and participation by renters and informal workers need to be assessed together. Use both distributive justice—who receives services—and procedural justice—whose voice shapes siting and clearance.

Therefore urbanisation can intensify segregation where planning externalises the costs of growth onto the poor; its social outcome is contingent on housing, mobility and democratic access rather than predetermined by city size.

**Why this earns marks:** Answers the conditional 'does' directive with a causal chain, a counterexample and both justice dimensions.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 3 — 2024 GS-I Q5

**Demand:** Why do large citites tend to attract more migrants than smaller towns? Discuss in the light of conditions in developing countries. (10 marks; 150 words; official-paper spelling retained.)

**Verification / routing:** Official 2024 GS-I wording held locally; Geography owns the migration-theory route, while the Society Basic owner also routes its urban-social mechanism.

**Model answer:**

Large cities attract migrants because they offer a greater range of possible livelihoods, not merely a higher quoted wage. Mumbai's varied services, trade and construction illustrate labour-market depth: a newcomer can change occupations or find casual work while seeking a better match. Clustering creates supplier links and information spillovers; existing migrant networks can help with referrals and temporary accommodation, reducing the risk of arrival.

In developing-country settings, smaller towns may offer fewer formal opportunities, specialist services and transport connections. Concentration of investment and institutions therefore reinforces the perceived advantage of a metropolis. Yet the attraction is unequal: a woman seeking safe housing and work or a worker lacking networks may face higher entry barriers. Rising rent, congestion and insecure tenure also impose costs on migrants and established residents. The result is a self-reinforcing but selective pull, not evidence that metropolitan migration guarantees secure work or adequate housing.

**Why this earns marks:** Explains job diversity, matching and network effects in the required developing-country comparison without romanticising outcomes.

### PYQ DEMAND CARD 4 — 2025 GS-I Q8

**Demand:** How does smart city in India, address the issues of urban poverty and distributive justice? (10 marks; 150 words.)

**Verification / routing:** Official 2025 GS-I wording held locally; routed to this Basic owner. The question has no official UPSC model answer.

**Model answer:**

A smart city can address poverty only if better infrastructure changes the poor's effective access, not merely the appearance of a business district. For example, an area with digital monitoring and improved buses may still exclude nearby informal-settlement residents if fares, stops or complaint systems are inaccessible. Affordable water, sanitation and last-mile transit reduce time and health costs and widen access to work; assisted offline registration can keep digital governance usable for people without smartphones.

Distributive justice asks who receives the improvement. Procedural justice asks whether slum residents, renters and street vendors influence where works occur and whether upgrading is in situ or displaces livelihoods. The Smart Cities Mission provides a policy context, not proof that every participating city achieved these outcomes. Compare pre- and post-project access by income, settlement and gender, and investigate relocation costs. Thus technology is an instrument: poverty falls and justice improves only where service coverage, tenure-sensitive access and accountable local participation guide investment.

**Why this earns marks:** Evaluates the mechanism and distribution of benefits, not the technology label; distinguishes context from measured impact.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 1 — 10 MARKS

**Demand:** Discuss why large cities tend to attract more migrants than smaller towns in developing-country conditions. Answer in about 150 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; mechanism shared with the verified 2024 GS-I demand, but separately answered.

**Model answer:**

The stronger metropolitan pull reflects several linked advantages. Delhi's diversified labour market illustrates how construction, domestic work, trading and services can offer successive entry points; it does not guarantee a secure job for each arrival. Dense firms and workers lower search costs and improve matches. Migrants already living in a city may refer newcomers to employers or lodging, so network effects reinforce clustering beyond a simple wage gap.

By contrast, many smaller towns have thinner labour markets and weaker specialist services or transport connections. In a developing-country setting, concentrating investment in a metropolis can further widen this difference. However, households compare expected earnings with rent, travel, safety and family constraints; women and workers without contacts may experience the same destination differently. Large-city growth can therefore coexist with informal employment and housing shortages. Decentralised opportunity and serviced affordable rental housing can reduce the cost of concentration without blaming migrants for a rational livelihood decision.

**Why this earns marks:** Includes an Indian illustration, comparative mechanism, household constraints and the concentration trade-off.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 2 — 10 MARKS

**Demand:** Discuss how a smart city in India addresses the issues of urban poverty and distributive justice. Answer in about 150 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; distinct policy-implementation emphasis rather than a copied PYQ answer.

**Model answer:**

Smart-city investment addresses poverty when low-income residents gain usable services and livelihood access. Under India's Smart Cities Mission, a digital water-monitoring system might detect failures faster; its social value depends on whether informal and rented households receive connections and can report outages. Improved buses and walkways can lower work-trip costs for domestic workers and street vendors, whereas upgrading a showcase zone without peripheral access can increase spatial inequality.

Fair allocation is distributive justice; participation in location, tariff and relocation decisions is procedural justice. Ward consultation matters because relocation far from work may offset gains from improved housing. The Seventy-fourth Amendment provides a municipal governance framework, but actual funds, powers and metropolitan coordination differ locally. Evaluate projects with disaggregated service-access measures and resident feedback, not investment totals or app downloads alone. Technology can support equitable service delivery, but neither the mission label nor physical infrastructure by itself establishes poverty reduction.

**Why this earns marks:** Tests service incidence and voice with an explicit governance-capacity qualification.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 3 — 15 MARKS

**Demand:** Examine whether urbanisation leads to the segregation or marginalisation of the urban poor. Answer in about 250 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; focuses on the produced geography of exclusion and counter-design.

**Model answer:**

Urbanisation need not separate classes, yet housing and infrastructure rules can make its gains and costs sharply unequal. Mumbai's close proximity of high-value employment and informal shelter illustrates the puzzle: physical nearness does not imply equal access to land or amenities, and cannot be taken as a complete account of the entire metropolis.

First, diverse jobs draw people faster than affordable rental housing and serviced land may grow. Land prices, zoning and documentation requirements shift some households to insecure tenure. A settlement called a slum under one local rule need not have identical legal status elsewhere, so measured totals need definitional caution. Second, tenure-linked refusal of household connections compounds overcrowding and health risk. Third, peripheral relocation can increase commute costs and break vendors' and domestic workers' ties to clients. This converts spatial displacement into income loss. These are governance and market mechanisms, not innate consequences of density or migrant behaviour.

Counter-mechanisms are possible. In-situ improvement can retain proximity to employment while adding drainage and services; affordable rental housing can recognise mobile workers; frequent affordable transit can reduce the penalty of peripheral location. Residents' influence on siting and access rules supplies procedural justice, whereas actual coverage of poor households supplies distributive justice. The Seventy-fourth Amendment's Part IXA and Twelfth Schedule give a formal urban-governance framework, but financing and parastatal control may constrain municipal implementation.

The question therefore merits a conditional answer: urbanisation can deepen segregation when cheap labour is welcomed but affordable land and voice are denied. Measure employment access, secure services and resident participation before judging any urban remedy successful.

**Why this earns marks:** Contrasts conditional causation with an Indian illustration, housing mechanisms and feasible institutional counter-design.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 4 — 15 MARKS

**Demand:** Examine urban alienation and the weakening of community bonds as social consequences of rapid Indian urbanisation. Answer in about 250 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; focused on social networks rather than automatically equating density with pathology.

**Model answer:**

Alienation denotes weakened attachment and informal support; anomie refers to weakened shared norms. Rapid movement to cities may unsettle familiar relationships, but urban residence is neither a diagnosis nor a sufficient cause of isolation. Consider a newcomer to Bengaluru's service economy: insecure hours and frequent moves may prevent neighbourhood ties, while existing kin or co-workers may provide substantial support. This is a mechanism illustration, not a claim about all Bengaluru residents.

Job search and short-term rental turnover can weaken repeated interactions through which neighbours build trust. Long commutes reduce time for association, while unequal access to parks, safe streets and community facilities limits shared spaces. A worker without a locally accepted address may also be excluded from service networks, reinforcing isolation. Women may face a different constraint where unsafe travel narrows participation; older people can experience isolation even without migration. Anomie must not be used to infer crime: reporting, policing, inequality and local opportunity are competing explanations for recorded offences.

Nor does the rural–urban contrast describe everyone. Migrant associations, neighbourhood self-help groups, unions and resident-led services can build solidarities within cities. Rural ties often persist through circular work and remittances, so social life is better conceived as a continuum than an abrupt severance. Strengthening accessible public spaces, affordable transport, secure rental arrangements and ward-level participation can create repeated contact without demanding a return to a romanticised village community.

Alienation is thus a risk arising from insecurity and limited opportunities for association, not an inevitable product of density. Assess which groups lack supportive networks and whether institutions enable them to build new ones.

**Why this earns marks:** Defines both concepts, explains mechanisms, differentiates groups and rejects a deterministic density/crime link.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 5 — 20 MARKS

**Demand:** Assess the relationship between Tier-2 city growth, the new middle class and the culture of consumption. Answer in about 300 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; expands the 2022 demand through labour, gender, credit and spatial inequality.

**Model answer:**

Tier-2 growth links employment, changing class identities and markets, but the link is mediated by income security and unequal access. Indore may illustrate a regional service and commercial centre; it should not stand in for statistical evidence about all Tier-2 cities. As more employers, institutions and suppliers cluster, workers may find diverse jobs without migrating to a metropolis. Education, logistics, retail and health services can support a salaried and self-employed middle class.

Class formation then affects consumption. A household with predictable earnings can plan spending on education, transport and durable goods. Organised retail, online platforms, credit and media supply both goods and visible symbols of status. The culture of consumption is therefore more than increased quantities purchased: it includes aspirations about identity, mobility and what counts as a desirable urban life. Household decisions are negotiated; women earning independently may gain spending power, but unpaid care and mobility restrictions can constrain choices. For some younger workers a loan expands access; for others repayment is a new risk.

The relationship has two important limits. First, clustering also increases land prices, rents and congestion, so higher nominal earnings do not equal greater disposable resources. Second, malls and delivery networks depend on informal and casual labour whose earnings and housing security differ from those of salaried shoppers. Migrant women, vendors and renters may participate in supplying consumption without sharing its benefits. Physical access to stores or platforms is not the same as affordability.

An inclusive response combines diverse employment with affordable rental housing, reliable public transport, basic services and worker voice. Evaluation should track occupational and neighbourhood differences rather than infer welfare from retail frontage or a city's branding. Tier-2 growth can foster a middle class whose spending reshapes culture, but consumption is an expression of uneven urban restructuring, not a certificate of common prosperity.

**Why this earns marks:** Links all three terms causally, with named illustration, distributional qualifications and a reasoned verdict.

### ORIGINAL MAINS 6 — 20 MARKS

**Demand:** Evaluate the claim that urban investment becomes socially progressive only when tenure, services and voice follow the networks that attract people. Answer in about 300 words.

**Verification / routing:** Original practice; integrates growth mechanism, institutions, climate resilience and justice.

**Model answer:**

The claim captures a central contradiction of Indian urbanisation: firms, workers and contacts cluster quickly, while the rights and services needed for a dignified life may lag. Mumbai's concentration of employment near costly land provides an illustration of this tension, not a measured claim about every Mumbai household. Agglomeration and chain migration lower job-search costs; they do not themselves create affordable homes or accountable public provision.

Tenure is the first link. Where renters or occupants lack recognised claims or documents, an available water or power network need not translate into a household connection. Serviced rental housing and context-sensitive in-situ upgrading can improve access without assuming that one legal title model fits every mobile worker. Services form a second link: drainage, sanitation, water and affordable transport protect health and connect low-income residents to work. Relocation to distant housing can improve the building yet undermine incomes through longer, costlier journeys. Wetland protection and stormwater planning additionally guard against environmental risks that fall unevenly on vulnerable settlements.

Voice is the third link. Ward-level participation by renters, street vendors and informal workers helps determine project location, tariffs, accessible grievance mechanisms and displacement safeguards. This is procedural justice, complementary to distributive justice in actual service access. The Seventy-fourth Amendment inserted Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule, but listing functions does not solve municipal finance, parastatal fragmentation or coordination across a metropolitan region. Smart Cities Mission technology can help monitor delivery, yet dashboards and capital spending cannot prove an equitable result.
