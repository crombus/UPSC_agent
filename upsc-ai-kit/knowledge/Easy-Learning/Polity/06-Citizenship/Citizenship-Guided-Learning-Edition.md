## OPENING ROADMAP — BEGIN HERE

### What this topic means

Citizenship answers a basic public-law question: **who is a full legal member of India?** That membership affects political participation, some constitutional rights, passports, public offices and the legal rules for entering, keeping or losing Indian citizenship. It does not mean that non-citizens have no rights; many Fundamental Rights protect every **person**.

### Why a citizen and a UPSC candidate need it

For a citizen, this topic explains membership, voting eligibility, constitutional offices, OCI, migration-linked rules and why identity documents are not interchangeable. For UPSC, the difficult part is not memorising one definition. It is keeping separate: **citizenship, nationality, domicile, residence, migration, refugee status, statelessness and OCI**.

## MODULE 1 — MEMBERSHIP: CITIZENSHIP, NATIONALITY, DOMICILE AND RESIDENCE

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**How do four words that look similar answer four different legal questions?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Citizenship means full legal membership of the Indian political community. A citizen owes allegiance to India and receives the status, rights and political opportunities that Indian law attaches to citizenship.

**Nationality** is the wider international connection between a person and a State. In ordinary Indian discussion, nationality and citizenship are often used together, but they are not perfect synonyms in every legal system.

**Domicile** means a person's permanent legal home. It combines living in a place with an intention to treat that legal system as the permanent home. India has one national citizenship; an administrative expression such as “State domicile” usually describes a local residence rule, not a separate State citizenship.

**Residence** is a fact: where a person lives, usually or ordinarily. Residence can matter for electoral registration, education, benefits or statutory qualifying periods. Long residence by itself does not automatically create citizenship.

An **alien** is a non-citizen. That does not remove all constitutional protection. The Constitution sometimes says **citizen**, and sometimes says **person**. The exact word controls.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Think of an Indian university. **Citizenship** is like membership of the university itself. **Residence** is the hostel in which you currently stay. **Domicile** is the place you legally treat as your permanent home. This is only an analogy: constitutional membership is created by law, not by a university card.

### VISUAL FIRST

| Word | Question it answers | What it does **not** automatically prove |
|---|---|---|
| Citizenship | “Of which State am I a full legal member?” | It does not by itself place a person on an electoral roll. |
| Nationality | “Which State represents my international legal bond?” | It is not a second Indian State-level citizenship. |
| Domicile | “Which legal system is my permanent home?” | A State domicile certificate does not create State citizenship. |
| Residence | “Where do I actually or ordinarily live?” | Long residence alone does not automatically create citizenship. |

*The four terms answer different questions; replacing one with another creates most close-option errors.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** Article 5 used **domicile in the territory of India** as the gateway for citizenship at commencement. Current acquisition and loss are mainly governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955 under Article 11. In *Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* (1984), the Supreme Court emphasised one citizenship and one domicile in India, while recognising that residence-based rules may still exist.

Rights must be read textually:

- Articles **14, 20, 21 and 25** protect persons.
- Articles **15, 16, 19 and 29** use citizen-specific language.
- Article **30** speaks of religious or linguistic minorities; do not mechanically rewrite it as “citizens only”.
- Voting is a **statutory right**, not a Fundamental Right. Citizenship is necessary but not sufficient; electoral registration and ordinary-residence rules also matter.
- India does not require a “natural-born citizen” for the Presidency. Article 58 requires a **citizen of India**, along with the other qualifications.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “All Fundamental Rights belong only to citizens.” → **Correct position:** several rights protect every person. Always inspect the beneficiary word used by the Article.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Citizenship = legal membership.
2. Nationality = wider international legal bond.
3. Domicile = permanent legal home plus intention.
4. Residence = factual or ordinary living connection.
5. A non-citizen may still claim person-rights such as Articles 14 and 21.
6. Voting is statutory, not a Fundamental Right.
7. A State domicile rule is not State citizenship.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why can a non-citizen invoke Article 14 but not Article 19?
2. Why does a domicile certificate not create a second citizenship?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “Citizenship is legal membership; domicile is permanent legal home; residence is a factual connection.” **Must-write terms:** person/citizen distinction, statutory vote, single citizenship. **Diagram:** use the four-row distinction table. **Paragraph spine:** define the four statuses → show the constitutional wording → add one consequence for rights and voting → end by rejecting State citizenship.

## MODULE 2 — WHY THE CONSTITUTION NEEDED COMMENCEMENT RULES

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**Why did Articles 5-11 deal mainly with 26 January 1950 instead of writing a permanent citizenship code?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** India began constitutional government after Partition, mass migration and the creation of Pakistan. The Constitution had to answer an immediate question: who was already an Indian citizen when it commenced?

The Constitution therefore created commencement rules. It did not try to anticipate every later birth, marriage, migration, territorial change or foreign-citizenship problem. It settled the starting point and gave Parliament power to make the continuing law.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Imagine opening a new national register on its first day. The Constitution decided who entered the register at opening. Parliament then created the continuing rules for later entries and exits. This is an analogy; the legal rules are Articles 5-11 and the Citizenship Act, 1955.

### VISUAL FIRST

```text
26 JANUARY 1950: THE CONSTITUTION STARTS
          |
          +--> Articles 5-8: Who enters as a citizen at commencement?
          |
          +--> Article 9: Who is disqualified because of voluntary foreign citizenship?
          |
          +--> Article 10: Status continues, but remains subject to parliamentary law.
          |
          +--> Article 11: Parliament may make the permanent citizenship law.
                               |
                               v
                      CITIZENSHIP ACT, 1955
```

*The Constitution fixes the opening membership; Parliament manages future acquisition and loss.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** Part II contains Articles **5-11**. Articles 5-8 create commencement routes. Article 9 qualifies specified commencement claims where foreign citizenship was voluntarily acquired. Article 10 continues status subject to parliamentary law. Article 11 preserves Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all other citizenship matters.

Citizenship is not itself listed as a Fundamental Right. However, citizenship decisions are not beyond the Constitution: arbitrary action can still be tested under Articles 14, 21 and judicial review.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “Articles 5-11 are obsolete because the Citizenship Act now exists.” → **Correct position:** they remain constitutional text and explain commencement status, but later acquisition and loss are primarily statutory.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Part II = Articles 5-11.
2. The central date is 26 January 1950.
3. Articles 5-8 are affirmative commencement routes.
4. Article 9 is a foreign-citizenship disqualifier in that context.
5. Article 10 continues status subject to law.
6. Article 11 empowers Parliament.
7. Citizenship decisions remain judicially reviewable.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why is Article 11 essential even after Articles 5-10?
2. Why is it wrong to call citizenship a Fundamental Right?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “The Constitution made a transitional settlement and left the continuing architecture to Parliament.” **Must-write terms:** commencement, transitional scheme, Article 11, Citizenship Act 1955. **Diagram:** use the vertical Articles 5-11 flow. **Paragraph spine:** historical need → constitutional routes → parliamentary delegation → judicial-review qualification.

## MODULE 3 — ARTICLES 5-8 AS A DECISION TREE

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**Which commencement route applied to a person in India, a migrant across Partition, a returnee or a person of Indian origin abroad?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Do not memorise Articles 5-8 as one paragraph. First ask where the person was and whether migration across the new India-Pakistan boundary was involved.

Article 5 is the general India connection. Article 6 deals with migration **from Pakistan to India**. Article 7 deals with migration **from India to Pakistan**, followed by a narrow return route. Article 8 deals with persons of Indian origin living outside India.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Use four doors at a railway junction. Door 5 is for the general India-based claim. Door 6 is for arrival from Pakistan. Door 7 is for departure to Pakistan and a legally controlled permanent return. Door 8 is for an overseas Indian-origin claim through a diplomatic or consular representative. The doors are an analogy; each legal door has exact conditions.

### VISUAL FIRST

```text
WHO CLAIMS CITIZENSHIP AT COMMENCEMENT?
          |
          +--> In India, with domicile in India?
          |       |
          |       +--> Born in India / parent born in India /
          |             ordinary residence for 5 years --> ARTICLE 5
          |
          +--> Migrated FROM Pakistan to India?
          |       |
          |       +--> Before 19 July 1948 --> residence since migration
          |       +--> On/after 19 July 1948 --> registration + 6-month floor
          |                                               --> ARTICLE 6
          |
          +--> Migrated TO Pakistan after 1 March 1947?
          |       |
          |       +--> Normally excluded
          |       +--> Returned with resettlement/permanent-return permit
          |             --> treated through Article 6(b) route --> ARTICLE 7
          |
          +--> Indian origin, ordinarily resident outside India?
                  |
                  +--> diplomatic/consular registration --> ARTICLE 8
```

*Location and direction of migration identify the Article; dates and registration complete the test.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** **Article 5:** domicile in India **plus** birth in India, either parent born in India, or ordinary residence for five years immediately before commencement.

**Article 6:** the person, parent or grandparent had to satisfy the ancestry link based on India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935. A migrant before **19 July 1948** needed ordinary residence in India since migration. A migrant on or after that date needed registration before commencement after application and at least **six months' residence immediately before the application**.

**Article 7:** migration from India to Pakistan after **1 March 1947** normally defeated the claim. A person returning under a permit for **resettlement or permanent return** was treated through the post-19-July-1948 Article 6 registration route.

**Article 8:** a person ordinarily resident outside India, with the required self/parent/grandparent birth link, could be registered by an Indian diplomatic or consular representative. This is citizenship, not modern OCI.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “Five years' residence was enough under Article 5.” → **Correct position:** domicile in India was compulsory, plus one of the three connecting factors.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Article 5 = domicile gateway plus one connection.
2. Article 6 = migration from Pakistan.
3. 19 July 1948 divides Article 6 routes.
4. Article 7 = migration to Pakistan after 1 March 1947.
5. Return must be for resettlement or permanent return.
6. Article 8 = Indian origin abroad plus registration.
7. Article 8 is not OCI.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why does the direction of migration determine whether Article 6 or Article 7 applies?
2. What extra step applied to an Article 6 migrant arriving on or after 19 July 1948?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “Articles 5-8 are four distinct commencement channels, not one migration rule.” **Must-write terms:** domicile, 19 July 1948, 1 March 1947, permanent return, consular registration. **Diagram:** reproduce the four-door decision tree. **Paragraph spine:** Article 5 general route → Articles 6-7 Partition directions and dates → Article 8 diaspora route → one trap.

## MODULE 4 — ARTICLES 9-11 AND SINGLE CITIZENSHIP

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**How do foreign citizenship, continuity, Parliament's power and India's single citizenship fit together?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Article 9 prevents a person from using specified commencement routes after voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship. Article 10 says an existing constitutional citizenship continues, but not beyond Parliament's valid law. Article 11 gives Parliament the continuing law-making power.

India has one national citizenship. Federalism divides public power between the Union and States, but it does not create a separate citizenship of each State. Regional residence protections can exist without creating State citizenship.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

India's federation is like one national membership card used across many constitutionally empowered State institutions. A local eligibility rule may regulate a benefit or post, but it does not issue a new sovereign citizenship card. This is an analogy, not a source of legal power.

### VISUAL FIRST

| Provision or idea | Simple function | Precision |
|---|---|---|
| Article 9 | Foreign-citizenship disqualifier | Directly qualifies Articles 5, 6 and 8 commencement claims. |
| Article 10 | Continuity | Citizenship continues subject to parliamentary law. |
| Article 11 | Legislative bridge | Parliament may regulate acquisition, termination and all other citizenship matters. |
| Single citizenship | One Indian political membership | Regional safeguards do not become State citizenship. |

*Articles 9-11 connect the commencement settlement to the continuing statutory regime.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** Article 9 should be stated contextually, not as the whole later law of dual nationality. Later voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship is addressed through the Citizenship Act, especially Section 9. OCI remains a foreign-national card status and does not change this result.

Regional differentiation inside single citizenship includes:

- Article **16(3)**: Parliament may prescribe residence requirements for specified public employment.
- Article **19(5)**: reasonable movement/residence restrictions may protect Scheduled Tribes.
- Articles **371A-371J** and related arrangements: region-specific safeguards.
- Former Article **35A** is not a current constitutional exception after the 2019 changes.

These are forms of constitutional asymmetry, not separate citizenships.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “OCI proves India accepts dual citizenship.” → **Correct position:** OCI is not Indian citizenship; the cardholder remains a foreign national.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Article 9 has a commencement-route context.
2. Section 9 governs later termination on voluntary foreign citizenship.
3. Article 10 continuity is subject to parliamentary law.
4. Article 11 is the statutory bridge.
5. India has single citizenship.
6. Residence or regional safeguards do not create State citizenship.
7. Former Article 35A must not be described as current.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why is “Article 9 alone is the complete dual-citizenship code” an overstatement?
2. How can India have single citizenship and still permit regional safeguards?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “Single citizenship produces common political membership, while constitutional asymmetry protects vulnerable regions.” **Must-write terms:** Article 9 context, Article 11, Section 9, Articles 16(3)/19(5)/371. **Diagram:** one national citizenship at the centre with regional safeguards around it. **Paragraph spine:** constitutional continuity → statutory control → federal integration → bounded exceptions → no State citizenship.

## MODULE 5 — ACQUISITION BY BIRTH AND DESCENT

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**Why can two people born in India or abroad have different citizenship results because their birth dates differ?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Citizenship by birth and citizenship by descent are not timeless rules. Parliament tightened both routes at different dates. Always begin with the date of birth, then ask about the parents and any registration requirement.

Birth focuses on a person born **in India**. Descent focuses on a person born **outside India** to an Indian-citizen parent.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Think of three editions of the same rulebook. A birth in 1980 is read under the first edition. A birth in 1995 is read under the second. A birth in 2010 is read under the third. The analogy explains why the same place of birth can produce a different legal test.

### VISUAL FIRST

| Route | Period | Core rule |
|---|---|---|
| Birth in India | 26 Jan 1950-before 1 Jul 1987 | Citizen by birth, subject to statutory exceptions. |
| Birth in India | 1 Jul 1987-before 3 Dec 2004 | Either parent must be an Indian citizen. |
| Birth in India | On/after 3 Dec 2004 | Both parents citizens, or one citizen and the other not an illegal migrant. |
| Descent abroad | Before 10 Dec 1992 | Original rule ordinarily looked to the father. |
| Descent abroad | 10 Dec 1992-before 3 Dec 2004 | Either parent may supply the citizenship link. |
| Descent abroad | On/after 3 Dec 2004 | Consular registration and statutory declarations/controls apply. |

*Date first; parent status and registration second.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** Citizenship by birth is governed by **Section 3**. Section 3(2) contains exceptions involving an accredited foreign envoy with diplomatic immunity and enemy occupation/alien circumstances specified by the Act.

Citizenship by descent is governed by **Section 4**. For births abroad on or after 3 December 2004, registration at an Indian consulate within the statutory period is central, subject to the Act's permission mechanism for delay and the declaration concerning the minor's foreign passport.

The term **illegal migrant** is statutory. Broadly, it concerns entry without valid travel authority or remaining beyond the permitted period, subject to statutory provisos and exemptions.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “Anyone born in India is automatically a citizen.” → **Correct position:** that broad statement fits the first period only and remains subject to Section 3(2) exceptions.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Birth route = Section 3.
2. Descent route = Section 4.
3. Birth thresholds: 1 July 1987 and 3 December 2004.
4. Descent gender threshold: 10 December 1992.
5. Post-3-December-2004 descent requires consular controls.
6. “Illegal migrant” is a defined statutory term.
7. Diplomatic/enemy exceptions must not be forgotten.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why is date the first question in a citizenship-by-birth problem?
2. Why does birth abroad after 3 December 2004 require more than identifying a citizen parent?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “The 1955 Act moved from broad territorial birth to parent- and migration-sensitive rules.” **Must-write terms:** Sections 3-4, 1987, 1992, 2004, consular registration. **Diagram:** use the six-row date ladder. **Paragraph spine:** identify route → apply date → test parent status → add exception/registration → state qualified result.

## MODULE 6 — REGISTRATION, NATURALISATION AND INCORPORATION OF TERRITORY

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**How can a person acquire citizenship without being born in India or born abroad to an Indian-citizen parent?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Registration is a listed-category route. A person must fit a category chosen by Parliament, such as a person of Indian origin, a spouse, a minor child or a qualifying OCI cardholder, and must satisfy the route's conditions.

Naturalisation is the general route for an eligible foreigner who does not rely on a listed family or origin category. It tests residence, character, language and future connection with India.

Incorporation of territory is different. If new territory becomes part of India, the Central Government specifies who becomes a citizen because of that territorial change.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Registration is like entering through a labelled lane: “spouse”, “minor child”, “person of Indian origin” or another statutory category. Naturalisation is the general lane with a longer qualification checklist. Incorporation is a territorial change affecting a defined class by government order.

### VISUAL FIRST

```text
NOT BIRTH / NOT DESCENT
        |
        +--> Fits a listed statutory category?
        |         |
        |         +--> YES --> REGISTRATION (Section 5)
        |
        +--> Eligible foreigner using the general route?
        |         |
        |         +--> YES --> NATURALISATION (Section 6 + Third Schedule)
        |
        +--> Territory incorporated into India?
                  |
                  +--> Government order identifies citizens (Section 7)
```

*The three routes differ in their legal trigger: category, qualification or territorial change.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** **Section 5** contains registration categories and route-specific residence conditions. Relationship does not produce automatic citizenship; application, eligibility, oath and statutory scrutiny remain necessary.

Under the ordinary **Section 6 / Third Schedule** rule, naturalisation includes **12 months' continuous residence immediately before application** plus an aggregate **11 years in the preceding 14 years**, along with good character, knowledge of an Eighth Schedule language and the required intention regarding residence or service.

The Central Government may waive specified naturalisation conditions for distinguished service to science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human progress. **Waiver** means a legal power to relax a condition; it does not mean every applicant may ignore it.

**Section 7** covers incorporation of territory. A historical example is the Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “Marriage to an Indian citizen automatically creates citizenship.” → **Correct position:** marriage may open a Section 5 registration route; it does not itself confer citizenship.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Registration = Section 5 listed categories.
2. Naturalisation = Section 6 plus Third Schedule.
3. Ordinary naturalisation = 12 months plus 11 of the preceding 14 years.
4. Eighth Schedule language and good character matter.
5. Distinguished-service waiver is governmental and exceptional.
6. Incorporation = Section 7 government order.
7. Relationship or residence alone does not bypass application and scrutiny.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. What is the legal difference between registration and naturalisation?
2. Why is a waiver power not the same as automatic relaxation?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “The Act separates category-based registration from qualification-based naturalisation.” **Must-write terms:** Sections 5-7, Third Schedule, 12 months + 11/14, waiver. **Diagram:** use the three-lane tree. **Paragraph spine:** define each route → state its trigger → add one precise condition → warn against automatic citizenship.

## MODULE 7 — LOSS: RENUNCIATION, TERMINATION AND DEPRIVATION

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**How do voluntary surrender, automatic statutory consequence and compulsory government action differ?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Renunciation begins with the citizen's declaration. Termination follows voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship under the Act. Deprivation is a compulsory Central Government order, but only within the statutory category gate, on listed grounds and with safeguards.

The three routes must never be treated as synonyms. The person who starts the process, the trigger and the procedural protection are different.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Renunciation is handing back a membership voluntarily. Termination is a legal consequence triggered by taking another membership. Deprivation is cancellation by the authority for a legally specified reason after the required process. This is an analogy; the Act controls the actual consequences.

### VISUAL FIRST

| Route | Who/what triggers it? | Core safeguard or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Renunciation | Full-age citizen's declaration | Registration may be withheld during war; minor-child consequences and resumption rules apply. |
| Termination | Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship | Statutory determination; wartime treatment is specially controlled. |
| Deprivation | Central Government order | Category-limited, listed grounds, notice/opportunity and possible committee inquiry. |

*Loss routes differ by initiative, trigger and safeguard.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** **Section 8** governs renunciation. **Section 9** governs termination. **Section 10** governs deprivation.

Deprivation is not a general power over every citizen. Section 10(1)'s category gate covers a citizen by naturalisation, a citizen by virtue only of Article 5(c), and a citizen by registration **other than** Article 6(b)(ii) registration and Section 5(1)(a) registration. Citizens by birth and descent fall outside this gate. Grounds include fraud or concealment, disloyalty or disaffection towards the Constitution, unlawful enemy trade/communication during war, qualifying imprisonment within five years of registration/naturalisation, and specified continuous residence abroad. The Act also contains public-good satisfaction, notice, representation and inquiry protections.

A minor child may be affected by a parent's registered renunciation, but the Act provides a route to resume citizenship after attaining full age within the statutory time.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “Deprivation means any citizen can be stripped of citizenship whenever the Government alleges disloyalty.” → **Correct position:** first test the statutory category gate, then the listed ground, then the procedural safeguards.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Renunciation = Section 8; voluntary declaration.
2. Termination = Section 9; voluntary foreign citizenship.
3. Deprivation = Section 10; compulsory order.
4. Deprivation has a category gate.
5. Grounds are exhaustive statutory grounds, not political slogans.
6. Procedure and hearing safeguards matter.
7. Minor-child consequences are not permanent without a resumption route.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

1. Why must a deprivation answer begin with the category gate?
2. What separates termination from renunciation?

### EXAM USE

> **EXAM USE:** **Opening:** “Loss of citizenship is a three-route statutory design, not one discretionary cancellation power.” **Must-write terms:** Sections 8-10, voluntary declaration, foreign citizenship, category-limited deprivation, safeguards. **Diagram:** use the three-row comparison. **Paragraph spine:** identify route → identify trigger → state consequence → add limit/safeguard.

## MODULE 8 — ASSAM, SECTION 6A AND THE 2024 CONSTITUTION BENCH

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**How does Assam's special migration history produce a citizenship rule different from the CAA route?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Section 6A is an Assam-specific settlement connected with migration from the territory that became Bangladesh and the Assam Accord framework. It divides entrants into time bands.

The first band is treated most favourably. The middle band must pass detection and registration and temporarily loses electoral-roll inclusion. Entrants after the cut-off do not receive Section 6A's protection.

In 2024, the Supreme Court upheld Section 6A by a 4:1 majority, while also treating implementation failures seriously. The dissent would have invalidated the provision prospectively. The holding and the implementation criticism must be stated together.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

Think of three historical time windows on one Assam timeline. The window determines the statutory consequence. This is not a nationwide three-window test and must not be confused with the CAA's later 2014 cut-off.

### VISUAL FIRST

```text
SECTION 6A - ASSAM-SPECIFIC TIMELINE

Before 1 Jan 1966
        --> deemed citizens from 1 Jan 1966

1 Jan 1966 to before 25 Mar 1971
        --> detection as foreigner
        --> registration
        --> 10 years without electoral-roll inclusion
        --> then full citizenship consequences

On/after 25 Mar 1971
        --> no Section 6A protection

2024 Constitution Bench
        --> 4:1 majority upheld Section 6A
        --> implementation monitoring / deficiencies remained important
```

*Section 6A is an Assam-specific chronology; its 1971 cut-off is not the CAA's 2014 cut-off.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

## MODULE 9 — OCI: CONNECTION WITHOUT CITIZENSHIP

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**What does an OCI card provide, and why does it still not amount to dual citizenship?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** An OCI cardholder is a foreign national with a special statutory connection to India. The scheme makes travel and specified social/economic activities easier. It does not give political membership.

OCI therefore sits between an ordinary visa relationship and citizenship. It offers calibrated access but withholds the vote, Indian passport, public-employment equality and citizen-reserved constitutional offices.

## MODULE 10 — CAA 2019, RULES 2024 AND THE CONSTITUTIONAL DEBATE

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**What does the CAA actually do, and which constitutional questions remain unsettled?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** The CAA does not automatically make every migrant a citizen. It creates a facilitated statutory route for a precisely named class. The applicant must still satisfy the law, apply and undergo scrutiny.

The debate has two levels. The first is the exact statutory operation: communities, countries, entry date, exemption requirement, territorial exclusions and naturalisation modification. The second is constitutional: whether this classification is a reasonable humanitarian distinction or an impermissible religion-coded exclusion under Article 14 and secularism.

The law and Rules are operative. The latest source audit located no final Supreme Court judgment on the constitutional challenges through **21 August 2026**. The issue must therefore be described as pending, not settled.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

The CAA is a special application lane, not an automatic citizenship gate. A person must first fit every label on that lane and then complete the legal process. The analogy does not decide whether the classification is constitutionally valid.

*The statutory class is cumulative; missing one element can defeat the special route.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 inserted the specified class into the illegal-migrant proviso framework and added **Section 6B**. The naturalisation residence modification for the specified class reduces the relevant aggregate requirement from **11 years to 5 years**; it does not erase every other condition.

Territorial exclusions cover the specified Sixth Schedule tribal areas and Inner Line areas. The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 were notified on **11 March 2024**. The first certificates under the Rules were issued in **May 2024**, proving operation but not deciding constitutionality.

**Argument tree:**

- **Claim supporting validity:** Parliament identified persecuted minorities from three neighbouring States with a particular historical context; the scheme removes no existing citizen's citizenship.
- **Criticism:** religion and the limited country list may be under-inclusive and inconsistent with Article 14 and constitutional secularism; Assam also raises cultural and Accord-based concerns.
- **Exam-safe conclusion:** apply Article 14's classification analysis, distinguish statutory operation from constitutional validity and state that the challenge was pending on the latest located official record.

The **Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025** uses a **31 December 2024** entry date for specified immigration-document exemptions. It does **not** amend the CAA's **31 December 2014 citizenship cut-off**.

## MODULE 11 — STATELESSNESS, REFUGEES, MIGRATION AND DOCUMENT TRAPS

### WHAT ARE WE LEARNING?

**Why are a citizen, refugee, stateless person, foreigner, illegal migrant and document holder not the same legal category?**

### TEACHER EXPLAINS

> **TEACHER EXPLAINS:** Citizenship is membership. A foreigner is a person who is not an Indian citizen. An illegal migrant is a statutory migration category. A refugee claim concerns protection from return to serious danger. Statelessness means no State treats the person as its national under the operation of its law.

These categories can overlap, but none automatically proves another. A refugee may possess a nationality. A stateless person may or may not meet a refugee definition. An OCI cardholder is a foreign national. A long-term resident is not automatically a citizen.

Documents are evidence for particular purposes. Aadhaar establishes identity/residency functions under its statute; it does not by itself confer or prove citizenship or domicile. An electoral photo identity card relates to electoral registration; it is not a universal citizenship certificate.

### SIMPLE EXAMPLE / ANALOGY

A library card, a hostel card and a passport answer different questions. Showing one card cannot automatically prove the legal fact assigned to another. The analogy explains document purpose; actual evidentiary value depends on the relevant law.

### VISUAL FIRST

| Category | Main legal question | Do not assume |
|---|---|---|
| Citizen | Is the person a full Indian legal member? | Residence or Aadhaar alone proves it. |
| Foreigner | Is the person not an Indian citizen? | The person has no constitutional rights. |
| Illegal migrant | Did entry/stay fall within the statutory definition? | Every refugee or stateless person fits automatically. |
| Refugee claimant | Is international/protection-based non-return engaged? | Refugee status itself equals citizenship. |
| Stateless person | Does any State recognise nationality under its law? | Statelessness automatically legalises entry or stay. |
| OCI cardholder | Does the foreign national possess statutory OCI status? | The holder is an Indian citizen. |

*Membership, migration control and protection status are separate legal axes.*

### NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW

> **NOW ADD THE EXACT LAW:** The Citizenship Act defines “illegal migrant” and provides statutory provisos, exemptions and special routes. Citizenship proof is route-specific: a birth claim, descent claim, registration claim and naturalisation claim do not use one universal document list.

Aadhaar's governing framework and UIDAI's public formulation make clear that Aadhaar does not by itself confer or prove citizenship or domicile. Electoral registration is governed by election law. Passport status is important evidence and is governed by passport law, but exam answers should avoid treating one document as an irrebuttable universal answer to every citizenship dispute.

### COMMON CONFUSION

> **COMMON CONFUSION:** “If a document is accepted in one citizenship application process, it becomes conclusive proof of citizenship for every purpose.” → **Correct position:** legal purpose, route and evidentiary rule control.

### WHAT MUST I REMEMBER?

1. Citizenship = membership; migration status = entry/stay classification.
2. Refugee and stateless are not synonyms.
3. A refugee is not automatically a citizen.
4. A stateless person does not automatically acquire lawful stay.
5. OCI holder is a foreign national.
6. Aadhaar is not citizenship or domicile proof by itself.
7. EPIC is not a universal citizenship certificate.
8. Always ask which legal route and which document purpose apply.

### CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

[FACT] India has **single citizenship** despite a federal distribution of legislative and executive power. There is no citizenship of Kerala, Assam, Maharashtra or any other State separate from Indian citizenship.

[ANALYSIS] Single citizenship serves integration and equal political membership, but it does not produce identical residence benefits everywhere. The Constitution permits carefully bounded regional protection:

[LIMIT] These are asymmetrical protections inside a single citizenship. Calling them "separate State citizenship" is legally wrong.

> **Close-option rule:** Citizenship is membership; domicile is permanent legal home; residence is a factual connection. One can reside in a State without becoming a different citizen.

[FACT] The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 commenced on 1 September 2025 and repealed the

[FACT] The Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025 protects Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain,

citizenship class. The 2025 Act/Order neither grants automatic citizenship nor amends the Citizenship

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 10-marker 1: Distinguish citizenship, domicile and residence. Why does the distinction matter in Indian constitutional law? (150 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Citizenship is legal membership of India; domicile is a person's permanent legal home supported by residence and intention; residence is factual or ordinary physical presence. **Evidence:** Articles 5-11 and the Citizenship Act govern membership. Article 5 used domicile in India as a commencement gateway. *Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* stressed one Indian citizenship and one Indian domicile, while electoral law and Sections 5-6 of the Citizenship Act use ordinary residence for constituency or acquisition purposes. **Analysis:** The distinction prevents three errors. First, a State residence certificate cannot create State citizenship. Second, long residence does not automatically produce citizenship. Third, political rights require citizenship even when residence supplies an additional condition, as with electoral registration. **Qualification:** The Constitution still permits bounded regional protection through Articles 16(3), 19(5) and 371-based arrangements. **Conclusion:** India combines one political membership with differentiated residence-based administration; confusing the categories either overstates local preference or understates national citizenship.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three terms, uses Article 5, *Pradeep Jain* and specific constitutional exceptions, and ends with a qualified rather than absolute conclusion.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 10-marker 2: Explain why OCI is not dual citizenship. Evaluate the logic of its principal rights and restrictions. (150 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** OCI is a statutory foreign-national card status under Sections 7A-7D, not Indian citizenship. **Evidence:** The 2021 MHA notification describes an OCI cardholder as a foreign national holding a foreign passport and grants a lifelong multiple-entry visa, limited parity in fees, NRI-equivalent access in specified education/property/professional fields and FRRO exemption. Section 7B withholds the vote, legislative membership, Presidency/Vice-Presidency, judgeship and Article 16 public-employment equality. **Analysis:** The design supports diaspora mobility, investment and cultural connection without creating competing political allegiance. The restrictions track the distinction between social-economic access and democratic authorship. **Qualification:** OCI is not secure against all change: rights depend on notification and registration can be cancelled under Section 7D with a hearing. **Conclusion:** OCI is calibrated external membership - deeper than an ordinary visa, but deliberately short of nationality and political membership.

**Why this earns marks:** It names the statutory sections and 2021 notification, links each right/restriction to a design rationale, and avoids the "half citizenship" cliché.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 15-marker 1: The Constitution settled citizenship at commencement but left its future architecture to Parliament. Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Part II creates a founding membership settlement, while Articles 10-11 deliberately make citizenship a continuing statutory project. **Constitutional settlement:** Article 5 uses domicile plus birth, parentage or five-year residence. Article 6 regularises specified migrants from Pakistan, dividing them at 19 July 1948. Article 7 excludes post-1 March 1947 migrants to Pakistan but permits a resettlement/permanent-return route. Article 8 enables consular registration of persons of Indian origin abroad. Article 9 disqualifies voluntary foreign citizenship for the specified commencement routes. **Parliamentary continuation:** Article 10 continues status subject to law; Article 11 preserves Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all citizenship matters. The Citizenship Act, 1955 consequently supplies five acquisition routes - birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation - and three loss routes - renunciation, termination and deprivation. **Analysis:** The arrangement gives flexibility to respond to changing migration, diaspora and territorial conditions, illustrated by Section 6A, OCI and CAA amendments. **Qualification:** Parliamentary breadth is not constitutional immunity. Article 14 review, Article 21 procedure and natural justice constrain classification, deprivation and cancellation. The 2024 Section 6A judgment demonstrates this duality: wide legislative competence, but searching constitutional review and implementation directions. **Conclusion:** The Constitution fixed the Republic's first citizenry; Parliament continuously defines the membership boundary, under constitutional supervision.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both halves, covers every Article without becoming a list, links Article 11 to named statutory institutions and uses current case law as analysis.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 15-marker 2: Section 6A is constitutionally valid, but its implementation remains a governance challenge. Analyse in light of the 2024 Constitution Bench judgment. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** The 4:1 judgment separates the validity of Assam's historical settlement from the adequacy of its enforcement. **Scheme:** Section 6A deems pre-1966 Indian-origin entrants citizens; subjects the 1966-before-25 March 1971 stream to detection, registration and a ten-year electoral exclusion; and protects no post-cut-off entrant. **Majority:** Chief Justice Chandrachud and Justice Surya Kant's three-judge opinion upheld Parliament's Article 11 competence and found Assam plus the 1971 date to be a reasonable classification. They rejected challenges under Articles 6, 7, 9, 14, 21, 29, 326 and 355; Article 29 protects the capacity to conserve culture, and destruction of that capacity was not proved. **Governance deficit:** Justice Surya Kant nevertheless found tribunals and statutory machinery inadequate, stressed detection/deportation of post-1971 entrants, effective use of complementary laws and continuing judicial monitoring. **Dissent:** Justice Pardiwala argued that the absence of temporal closure and State-centred detection burden made Section 6A(3) manifestly arbitrary with time; he would invalidate prospectively while preserving past grants. **Assessment:** The dissent identifies design risk, but the majority controls. Administrative failure cannot be casually converted into invalidity; nor can validity excuse indefinite non-enforcement. **Conclusion:** Section 6A survives as law, but legitimacy now depends on timely, rights-respecting and reviewable implementation.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the exact streams, majority holdings, sole dissent and a reasoned validity-versus-administration verdict.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 20-marker 1: Critically examine the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 as a classification under Article 14 and as an instrument of humanitarian policy. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** CAA is an operative humanitarian facilitation whose constitutional legitimacy depends on whether its religion-country-date class is a reasonable response or an impermissibly under-inclusive citizenship preference. **Statutory design:** The law covers Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered on/before 31 December 2014 and fall within specified exemptions. #### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 20-marker 2: India's single citizenship coexists with differentiated regional protections. Does this strengthen or weaken the Union? Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Single citizenship supplies equal national membership; differentiated protections can strengthen that membership when they are constitutionally bounded, but weaken it when residence becomes a proxy for exclusion. **Integrative core:** Unlike classical federations with State citizenship, India gives one citizenship, one national franchise structure and interstate mobility under Article 19. *Pradeep Jain* rejects a separate State domicile in the constitutional sense. This lowers internal membership barriers and supports fraternity and a national labour market. **Protective asymmetry:** Article 16(3) allows Parliament to prescribe residence conditions for specified public posts; Article 19(5) permits tribal-protection restrictions; Articles 371A-371J protect customary law, land, local cadres or regional opportunity. Section 6A is another historical asymmetry, but it creates Indian rather than Assam citizenship. **Benefits:** These devices can reduce domination fears, preserve indigenous institutions and make national membership credible in culturally vulnerable regions. **Risks:** Excessive local quotas, vague "domicile" barriers or political hostility to interstate migrants can hollow out equal citizenship and conflict with Articles 14, 16(2) and 19. The former Article 35A debate illustrates how entrenched local privilege can become constitutionally contested. **Test:** A valid safeguard should have constitutional/statutory authority, a legitimate vulnerability or governance objective, proportional means, temporal review and no claim to a separate nationality. **Conclusion:** Differentiation strengthens the Union when it secures participation within common citizenship; it weakens the Union when it converts residence into hereditary political closure.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses named Articles, *Pradeep Jain*, Section 6A and a proportionality-style test, while balancing integration against regional vulnerability.

- Central Government Gazette order specifies persons connected to newly incorporated territory and operative date.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Loss under the Act

> **Mnemonic:** R-T-D = Renunciation, Termination, Deprivation.

- Renunciation: full-age declaration; minor child also ceases; may resume within one year after full age.
- Termination: voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship; prescribed authority resolves disputed fact/time/manner.
- Deprivation category gate:
  - naturalised citizens;
  - citizens by virtue only of Article 5(c);
  - registered citizens except Article 6(b)(ii) and Section 5(1)(a) categories.
- Citizens by birth/descent are outside Section 10(1)'s gate.
- Grounds: fraud; disloyalty/disaffection; enemy assistance; two-year sentence within five years; seven-year continuous foreign residence subject to exceptions/annual retention registration.
- Extra controls: public-good satisfaction, notice and inquiry route.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Assam Accord, Section 6A and NRC

- Before 1 January 1966: deemed citizen from 1 January 1966, subject to statutory conditions.
- 1 January 1966-before 25 March 1971: detection + registration; ten-year electoral exclusion; then citizen for all purposes.
- On/after 25 March 1971: no Section 6A protection.
- NRC/Accord memory date = midnight 24 March 1971.
- Never mix with CAA cut-off = 31 December 2014.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — In Re: Section 6A, 17 October 2024

- Constitution Bench 4:1 upheld Section 6A.
- Majority: Article 11 competence; Assam/date class reasonable; no violation of Articles 6, 7, 9, 14, 21, 29, 326 or 355.
- Article 29 claim failed because destruction of Assamese citizens' ability to conserve culture was not proved.
- Justice Surya Kant's opinion: validity yes, implementation inadequate; stronger tribunals/laws and judicial monitoring directed.
- Sole dissent, Justice J.B. Pardiwala: valid at enactment, later manifestly arbitrary due temporal open-endedness and State detection burden; would invalidate prospectively and preserve past grants.
- Present law = majority controls; implementation failure does not equal invalidity.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — OCI/PIO/NRI register

- NRI = Indian citizen residing abroad.
- OCI = foreign national with statutory card; not citizenship and not dual citizenship.
- 2003 OCI framework; 2005 expansion; 2015 PIO-OCI merger.
- Section 7A includes former citizens/eligible descendants and qualifying spouses; Pakistan/Bangladesh ancestry exclusion applies as enacted.
- 2021 facilities: lifelong multiple-entry visa; FRRO exemption; limited Indian-national parity; NRI parity in specified adoption, education, non-agricultural property and professions.
- Special permission: research; missionary/Tabligh; mountaineering; journalism; listed diplomatic work; protected/restricted/prohibited areas.
- Section 7B restrictions: no vote, legislature, Presidency/Vice-Presidency, SC/HC judgeship, Article 16 equality or ordinary public service.
- Section 7D: fraud, disaffection, enemy assistance, sentence, specified legal violation, security/public interest and spouse-marriage grounds; hearing required.
- Outside notified rights, OCI defaults to foreigner treatment.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — CAA 2019 + Rules 2024

- Communities: Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, Christian.
- Countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Pakistan.
- Entry: on/before 31 December 2014.
- Also requires coverage by statutory Passport/Foreigners Act exemptions.
- Section 6B route = application for registration/naturalisation; citizenship deemed from entry only after grant.
- Pending illegal-migration/citizenship proceeding abates on conferment, not mere application.
- Excluded territories: Sixth Schedule tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura + Inner Line areas.
- Rules notified 11 March 2024; electronic application -> District Level verification/oath -> Empowered Committee scrutiny/decision -> digital certificate.
- First certificates recorded by PIB on 15 May 2024.
- Operative law; constitutional challenge remains sub judice on the verified record.
- No unsupported "2026 Rules amendment" used.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Document and proof traps

- Aadhaar: identity/residence; not citizenship or domicile by itself.
- Birth certificate: birth facts; citizenship still depends on date and parental rule.
- Voter ID/electoral roll: citizenship legally required, but enrolment is not an irrebuttable universal citizenship certificate.
- Passport: strong ordinary evidence, not universally conclusive if nationality or issue is legally disputed.
- Domicile/residence certificate: local status, not citizenship.
- Citizenship certificate: direct registration/naturalisation evidence, subject to fraud and statutory review.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Six original solved Mains models

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 10-marker 1: Distinguish citizenship, domicile and residence. Why does the distinction matter in Indian constitutional law? (150 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Citizenship is legal membership of India; domicile is a person's permanent legal home supported by residence and intention; residence is factual or ordinary physical presence. **Evidence:** Articles 5-11 and the Citizenship Act govern membership. Article 5 used domicile in India as a commencement gateway. *Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* stressed one Indian citizenship and one Indian domicile, while electoral law and Sections 5-6 of the Citizenship Act use ordinary residence for constituency or acquisition purposes. **Analysis:** The distinction prevents three errors. First, a State residence certificate cannot create State citizenship. Second, long residence does not automatically produce citizenship. Third, political rights require citizenship even when residence supplies an additional condition, as with electoral registration. **Qualification:** The Constitution still permits bounded regional protection through Articles 16(3), 19(5) and 371-based arrangements. **Conclusion:** India combines one political membership with differentiated residence-based administration; confusing the categories either overstates local preference or understates national citizenship.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines all three terms, uses Article 5, *Pradeep Jain* and specific constitutional exceptions, and ends with a qualified rather than absolute conclusion.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 10-marker 2: Explain why OCI is not dual citizenship. Evaluate the logic of its principal rights and restrictions. (150 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** OCI is a statutory foreign-national card status under Sections 7A-7D, not Indian citizenship. **Evidence:** The 2021 MHA notification describes an OCI cardholder as a foreign national holding a foreign passport and grants a lifelong multiple-entry visa, limited parity in fees, NRI-equivalent access in specified education/property/professional fields and FRRO exemption. Section 7B withholds the vote, legislative membership, Presidency/Vice-Presidency, judgeship and Article 16 public-employment equality. **Analysis:** The design supports diaspora mobility, investment and cultural connection without creating competing political allegiance. The restrictions track the distinction between social-economic access and democratic authorship. **Qualification:** OCI is not secure against all change: rights depend on notification and registration can be cancelled under Section 7D with a hearing. **Conclusion:** OCI is calibrated external membership - deeper than an ordinary visa, but deliberately short of nationality and political membership.

**Why this earns marks:** It names the statutory sections and 2021 notification, links each right/restriction to a design rationale, and avoids the "half citizenship" cliché.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 15-marker 1: The Constitution settled citizenship at commencement but left its future architecture to Parliament. Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Part II creates a founding membership settlement, while Articles 10-11 deliberately make citizenship a continuing statutory project. **Constitutional settlement:** Article 5 uses domicile plus birth, parentage or five-year residence. Article 6 regularises specified migrants from Pakistan, dividing them at 19 July 1948. Article 7 excludes post-1 March 1947 migrants to Pakistan but permits a resettlement/permanent-return route. Article 8 enables consular registration of persons of Indian origin abroad. Article 9 disqualifies voluntary foreign citizenship for the specified commencement routes. **Parliamentary continuation:** Article 10 continues status subject to law; Article 11 preserves Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all citizenship matters. The Citizenship Act, 1955 consequently supplies five acquisition routes - birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation - and three loss routes - renunciation, termination and deprivation. **Analysis:** The arrangement gives flexibility to respond to changing migration, diaspora and territorial conditions, illustrated by Section 6A, OCI and CAA amendments. **Qualification:** Parliamentary breadth is not constitutional immunity. Article 14 review, Article 21 procedure and natural justice constrain classification, deprivation and cancellation. The 2024 Section 6A judgment demonstrates this duality: wide legislative competence, but searching constitutional review and implementation directions. **Conclusion:** The Constitution fixed the Republic's first citizenry; Parliament continuously defines the membership boundary, under constitutional supervision.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both halves, covers every Article without becoming a list, links Article 11 to named statutory institutions and uses current case law as analysis.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 15-marker 2: Section 6A is constitutionally valid, but its implementation remains a governance challenge. Analyse in light of the 2024 Constitution Bench judgment. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** The 4:1 judgment separates the validity of Assam's historical settlement from the adequacy of its enforcement. **Scheme:** Section 6A deems pre-1966 Indian-origin entrants citizens; subjects the 1966-before-25 March 1971 stream to detection, registration and a ten-year electoral exclusion; and protects no post-cut-off entrant. **Majority:** Chief Justice Chandrachud and Justice Surya Kant's three-judge opinion upheld Parliament's Article 11 competence and found Assam plus the 1971 date to be a reasonable classification. They rejected challenges under Articles 6, 7, 9, 14, 21, 29, 326 and 355; Article 29 protects the capacity to conserve culture, and destruction of that capacity was not proved. **Governance deficit:** Justice Surya Kant nevertheless found tribunals and statutory machinery inadequate, stressed detection/deportation of post-1971 entrants, effective use of complementary laws and continuing judicial monitoring. **Dissent:** Justice Pardiwala argued that the absence of temporal closure and State-centred detection burden made Section 6A(3) manifestly arbitrary with time; he would invalidate prospectively while preserving past grants. **Assessment:** The dissent identifies design risk, but the majority controls. Administrative failure cannot be casually converted into invalidity; nor can validity excuse indefinite non-enforcement. **Conclusion:** Section 6A survives as law, but legitimacy now depends on timely, rights-respecting and reviewable implementation.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the exact streams, majority holdings, sole dissent and a reasoned validity-versus-administration verdict.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 20-marker 1: Critically examine the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 as a classification under Article 14 and as an instrument of humanitarian policy. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** CAA is an operative humanitarian facilitation whose constitutional legitimacy depends on whether its religion-country-date class is a reasonable response or an impermissibly under-inclusive citizenship preference. **Statutory design:** The law covers Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered on/before 31 December 2014 and fall within specified exemptions. Section 6B enables registration/naturalisation, deems citizenship from entry upon grant and excludes Sixth Schedule tribal and Inner Line areas. The Third Schedule's aggregate period falls from eleven to five years; Rules 2024 require electronic application, documentary verification, oath and Empowered Committee decision. **Article 14 criticism:** Religion is explicit. Critics cite excluded Muslim sects and persecuted groups from other neighbours, secularism as a basic feature and the possibility that country selection is under-inclusive. Assam adds a federal-cultural objection: the 2014 date departs from the Accord's 1971 settlement. **Defence:** The State frames a narrow remedial class tied to Partition and vulnerable minorities in three selected neighbours; legislatures may address problems in stages. No existing citizen loses status and ordinary citizenship routes remain. Territorial exclusions show regional accommodation. **Assessment:** Humanitarian purpose does not end equality review; equally, under-inclusion does not automatically invalidate classification. The Court must test comparator, differentia, nexus and arbitrariness. **Conclusion:** Because the Act and 2024 Rules operate while constitutional challenges remain pending, the sound verdict is conditional: humanitarian legitimacy will endure only if the classification is judicially accepted as precise, non-arbitrary and secularism-compatible.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the exact law, separates aggregate and immediate residence, applies both sides of Article 14, adds Assam and current status, and avoids predicting the pending case.

#### SOURCE HEADING [3] — Original 20-marker 2: India's single citizenship coexists with differentiated regional protections. Does this strengthen or weaken the Union? Discuss. (250 words)

**Model answer.** **Thesis:** Single citizenship supplies equal national membership; differentiated protections can strengthen that membership when they are constitutionally bounded, but weaken it when residence becomes a proxy for exclusion. **Integrative core:** Unlike classical federations with State citizenship, India gives one citizenship, one national franchise structure and interstate mobility under Article 19. *Pradeep Jain* rejects a separate State domicile in the constitutional sense. This lowers internal membership barriers and supports fraternity and a national labour market. **Protective asymmetry:** Article 16(3) allows Parliament to prescribe residence conditions for specified public posts; Article 19(5) permits tribal-protection restrictions; Articles 371A-371J protect customary law, land, local cadres or regional opportunity. Section 6A is another historical asymmetry, but it creates Indian rather than Assam citizenship. **Benefits:** These devices can reduce domination fears, preserve indigenous institutions and make national membership credible in culturally vulnerable regions. **Risks:** Excessive local quotas, vague "domicile" barriers or political hostility to interstate migrants can hollow out equal citizenship and conflict with Articles 14, 16(2) and 19. The former Article 35A debate illustrates how entrenched local privilege can become constitutionally contested. **Test:** A valid safeguard should have constitutional/statutory authority, a legitimate vulnerability or governance objective, proportional means, temporal review and no claim to a separate nationality. **Conclusion:** Differentiation strengthens the Union when it secures participation within common citizenship; it weakens the Union when it converts residence into hereditary political closure.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses named Articles, *Pradeep Jain*, Section 6A and a proportionality-style test, while balancing integration against regional vulnerability.
