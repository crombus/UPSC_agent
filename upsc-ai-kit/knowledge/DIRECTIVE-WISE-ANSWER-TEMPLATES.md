## 1. The non-negotiable rule

A knowledgeable answer can still underperform if it obeys the topic but not the directive.
Before writing, convert the question into four separate instructions:

```text
DIRECTIVE       SUBJECT                 LIMITER                 TASKS
What operation? What is being examined? Within what boundary?  How many deliverables?
      |                   |                    |                    |
      +-------------------+--------------------+--------------------+
                                  |
                                  v
                         ONE-SENTENCE DEMAND
```

**Example:** "Critically examine the role of parliamentary committees in ensuring executive
accountability in India."

- **Directive:** critically examine.
- **Subject:** role of parliamentary committees.
- **Limiter:** executive accountability in India.
- **Tasks:** explain the mechanism, test actual effectiveness, identify limitations, give a
  reasoned judgment.
- **Demand sentence:** show how committees enable accountability, test that claim against
  institutional constraints, and conclude on their real rather than formal effectiveness.

Do not treat a directive as a decorative first word. It determines the argument's burden of proof.

## 2. Directive master matrix

| Directive | Meaning and examiner demand | Thesis style | Body architecture | Evidence pattern | Counter-position | Conclusion | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Discuss** | Present the major dimensions of an issue and connect them into a balanced account. More than a list, but no mandatory verdict unless the wording asks for one. | "The issue has constitutional, institutional and social dimensions; its effect depends on..." | Define/contextualise -> dimension 1 -> dimension 2 -> dimension 3 -> limitations or way forward. | One relevant anchor for each major dimension. | Include a contrary dimension where material; do not manufacture symmetry. | Synthesize the dimensions and state the governing condition. | Writing unrelated advantages and disadvantages without a central argument. |
| **Examine** | Inspect a proposition closely, reveal how it works, and test whether it survives scrutiny. | "The proposition is substantially valid because..., although its operation is constrained by..." | State proposition -> explain mechanism -> test with evidence -> limits/exceptions -> finding. | Evidence must test the proposition, not merely decorate it. | Use exceptions or implementation gaps that directly weaken the proposition. | Give a finding: valid, partly valid, or invalid, and why. | Merely describing the topic without testing the stated claim. |
| **Critically examine** | Inspect the claim closely and deliberately test its premises, evidence, exceptions and practical operation. | "The claim is persuasive in..., but closer examination reveals that its validity depends on..." | Clarify claim -> evidence for -> assumption/exception test -> contrary evidence -> qualified finding. | Pair formal design or stated claim with evidence of actual operation. | Mandatory: identify the strongest defect or boundary and decide whether it overturns the claim. | State what survives examination and what must be rejected or qualified. | Using "critical" to add only a generic limitations list after description. |
| **Analyse** | Break the issue into causal or functional components and explain relationships among them. | "The outcome arises through the interaction of A, B and C rather than a single cause." | Define outcome -> drivers/components -> causal links -> feedback or consequences -> synthesis. | Use mechanism evidence: sequence, comparison, trend, institution or case. | Include rival cause or intervening condition if it changes the causal story. | Reassemble the parts into the dominant mechanism. | Listing causes or features without showing how they interact. |
| **Critically analyse** | Analyse mechanisms, then test assumptions, evidence, trade-offs and alternative explanations. | "A explains much of the outcome, but its explanatory power is conditional because B and C also matter." | Claim -> causal analysis -> supporting evidence -> assumptions/limits -> alternative explanation -> qualified judgment. | Support both the main mechanism and the strongest qualification. | Mandatory: use the strongest plausible alternative, not a token negative point. | State which explanation dominates, under what conditions and with what limits. | Treating "critical" as criticism only or adding a generic challenges paragraph. |
| **Evaluate** | Apply explicit criteria to judge merit, effectiveness, impact or significance. | "Judged against criteria X, Y and Z, the measure has achieved..., but falls short on..." | Define criteria -> performance against each -> trade-offs -> comparative weight -> verdict. | Prefer outcome evidence over announcement, expenditure or formal existence. | Show where criteria conflict, such as efficiency versus equity. | Deliver a calibrated verdict tied to the criteria. | Giving pros and cons without stating the criteria or final weight. |
| **Critically evaluate** | Evaluate through explicit criteria while stress-testing evidence, design assumptions and adverse effects. | "Although the measure performs well on X, weaknesses on Y and Z prevent an unqualified positive verdict." | Criteria -> achievements -> failures/adverse effects -> counterfactual or alternative -> weighted verdict. | Use outcome evidence on both benefits and costs; distinguish attribution from correlation. | Mandatory and substantive; include unintended consequences or opportunity cost where relevant. | Rank performance and specify what would change the verdict. | Using equal numbers of positives and negatives as a substitute for judgment. |
| **Comment** | Take a reasoned position on a statement after brief explanation and support. | "The statement captures..., but overstates/understates...; a more defensible view is..." | Interpret statement -> position -> 2-4 reasons -> qualification -> closing observation. | Selective evidence supporting the position; breadth is less important than relevance. | Address the statement's strongest limitation. | Restate the refined position, not the original quotation. | Remaining neutral or producing a full generic essay on the topic. |
| **Elucidate** | Make an idea clear by unpacking its meaning, logic and implications, usually with examples. | "The statement means...; its underlying logic becomes clear through..." | Decode phrase -> explain logic -> illustration -> implications -> boundary. | One precise example for each abstract proposition. | Only clarify limits needed to prevent overstatement; full adversarial critique is not compulsory. | Express the clarified insight in plain language. | Paraphrasing the statement repeatedly without illuminating it. |
| **Elaborate** | Develop a brief proposition into a fuller explanation by adding logic, dimensions and relevant detail. | "The proposition can be developed through its conceptual, operational and practical dimensions." | Interpret proposition -> expand major dimensions -> explain links -> examples -> significance. | Use details that deepen the proposition rather than unrelated background. | A qualification is useful where elaboration would otherwise imply an absolute claim. | State the fuller meaning that emerges from the development. | Treating "elaborate" as permission to write everything known about the topic. |
| **Explain** | State what something means and show why or how it occurs. | "X occurs because A operates through B, producing C." | Definition/context -> why/how mechanism -> illustration -> implication. | Use explanatory evidence: process, sequence, institution or causal example. | Include a caveat only when the explanation is conditional. | Close with the mechanism's significance. | Substituting a feature list for causal explanation. |
| **Assess** | Estimate importance, extent, effectiveness or validity on a reasoned scale. | "X has been significant in..., but its overall extent is moderate/uneven because..." | Define assessment standard -> evidence for significance -> limiting evidence -> degree judgment. | Evidence should permit degree, trend or relative-importance judgment. | Compare with other causes or benchmarks where the question asks importance. | State degree explicitly: high, moderate, limited, uneven or conditional. | Saying "important" without establishing how important or relative to what. |
| **Substantiate** | Prove or firmly support the given claim using specific evidence and reasoning. | "The claim is borne out by constitutional, empirical and case-based evidence." | Clarify claim -> evidence cluster 1 -> evidence cluster 2 -> evidence cluster 3 -> proof statement. | Dense, precise and directly probative facts, cases, provisions or examples. | Mention an exception only to qualify scope, unless the wording also asks for criticism. | State exactly how the evidence establishes the claim. | Giving assertions, generic examples or unexplained data. |
| **Justify** | Defend a proposition or choice with reasons sufficient to show why it is warranted. | "The proposition is justified because..., provided that..." | State position -> normative/legal/practical reasons -> evidence -> objection -> defence or qualification. | Evidence must support why the position should be accepted, not only that it exists. | Address the strongest objection to the defended position. | Affirm a bounded justification rather than an absolute defence. | Repeating the proposition as if repetition were proof. |
| **Compare and contrast** | Identify material similarities and differences using common parameters, then explain their significance. | "Both share X, but differ decisively in Y and Z, leading to different outcomes." | Define units -> parameter-wise comparison -> decisive differences -> implications. | Use matched evidence under the same parameters; a table may help. | Not a separate counterargument; contrast is built into every parameter. | Identify the most consequential similarity and difference. | Writing two isolated mini-essays with no direct comparison. |
| **Distinguish** | Separate two close concepts by identifying the precise criteria on which they differ. | "Although A and B overlap in..., they are distinct in source, scope and consequence." | Brief definitions -> matched parameters -> boundary case -> significance of distinction. | Use one example or application showing why confusing them changes the answer. | Not normally required beyond acknowledging genuine overlap. | State the decisive differentiator. | Giving dictionary definitions without parameter-wise separation. |
| **Account for** | Explain why an event, pattern or condition occurred, with weighted causal reasoning. | "The outcome resulted primarily from A, enabled by B and accelerated by C." | Identify outcome -> long-term causes -> immediate triggers -> enabling conditions -> relative weight. | Use chronology and causal evidence; separate background condition from trigger. | Test rival explanations and distinguish necessary from sufficient causes. | Rank causes and identify the decisive conjunction. | Narrating what happened instead of explaining why it happened. |
| **Trace** | Follow development across time or stages, identifying turning points, continuity and change. | "X evolved from A through turning points B and C, while element D persisted." | Starting condition -> chronological stages -> turning points -> continuity/change -> present form. | Use selective dates/events that mark change, not an exhaustive timeline. | Include alternative periodisation only if it affects the interpretation. | Identify the direction and significance of evolution. | Producing a date list without explaining transformation. |
| **Review** | Reconsider the development, performance or state of an issue using organised evidence and reach an overview judgment. | "A review of X shows progress in..., persistent weakness in..., and a need to..." | Scope/benchmark -> development/performance -> achievements -> unresolved issues -> overview. | Use representative evidence across the period or dimensions under review. | Include evidence inconsistent with the dominant narrative. | Give the overall state and priority emerging from the review. | Treating review as a descriptive chronology with no appraisal. |
| **Describe** | Present the main characteristics, stages or pattern accurately and in an organised form. | "X is characterised by A, B and C, which together produce..." | Definition/location -> salient features or sequence -> illustration -> significance. | Use precise factual or spatial details; analysis is secondary unless asked. | Not compulsory, but avoid presenting disputed features as universal. | Summarise the defining pattern. | Adding unnecessary criticism while omitting core characteristics. |
| **Enumerate/List** | Identify the requested items clearly and, where space permits, give each a brief explanatory phrase. | "The principal components are A, B, C and D." | One-line frame -> numbered/classified items -> brief significance if asked. | Factual completeness and correct classification matter more than extended argument. | Not normally required unless the question adds an evaluative clause. | A short grouping or priority statement may close the answer. | Writing long analysis while omitting or obscuring the requested list. |
| **Illustrate** | Explain or establish a proposition through well-chosen examples, diagrams or cases. | "The proposition is visible in cases A, B and C, each demonstrating a different dimension." | Clarify proposition -> example 1 + inference -> example 2 + inference -> pattern. | Examples are the main proof; each must be followed by what it demonstrates. | A counter-example may define the proposition's limit. | Generalise the lesson shown by the illustrations. | Listing examples without linking them to the proposition. |
| **Highlight** | Draw focused attention to the most important features, causes, consequences or concerns. | "The most consequential aspects are A, B and C because..." | One-line context -> prioritised points -> brief significance -> closing emphasis. | Use only high-yield evidence that establishes importance. | Mention a neglected but material aspect where it alters priority. | Reaffirm the principal takeaway. | Attempting exhaustive coverage instead of prioritisation. |
| **Bring out** | Make a relationship, significance, contrast or latent feature explicit through explanation and evidence. | "The relationship becomes clear when A is connected to B through mechanism C." | State relation/feature -> demonstrate mechanism -> evidence/examples -> implication. | Evidence must reveal the requested relation, not merely describe both sides. | Address an apparent contradiction if it obscures the relationship. | State the clarified connection or significance. | Discussing the two topics separately without bringing out their link. |
| **To what extent** | Determine the degree to which a proposition is true, important or causal. | "The proposition is valid to a substantial/limited extent, chiefly in..., but not where..." | Define benchmark -> case for proposition -> boundary/counter-case -> degree judgment. | Evidence must support both the accepted domain and its boundary. | Mandatory because extent cannot be judged without limits. | Give an explicit degree and the conditions producing it. | Avoiding a degree judgment with "both sides are important." |
| **Critically discuss** | Discuss the main dimensions but interrogate their assumptions, tensions and consequences before synthesising. | "The issue contains competing claims; the stronger position is..., subject to..." | Map dimensions -> arguments -> scrutiny of each -> trade-offs -> synthesis. | Balanced but not mechanically equal evidence; weight by relevance. | Mandatory: engage the best opposing case. | Produce synthesis and priority, not a neutral summary. | Adding a final "however, challenges remain" paragraph to an otherwise descriptive answer. |

## 3. Directive families: the fastest recall system

| Family | Directives | Core mental operation | Minimum requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Breadth and exposition** | Discuss, explain, elucidate, elaborate, describe, enumerate/list, highlight | Open, clarify and prioritise the issue | Coherent dimensions, features or mechanism |
| **Inspection and causality** | Examine, critically examine, analyse, account for, trace, review | Test parts, causes, development and relationships | Mechanism plus finding |
| **Judgment** | Evaluate, assess, to what extent | Apply criteria and weigh performance | Explicit degree or verdict |
| **Proof and defence** | Substantiate, justify, comment, illustrate, bring out | Support, demonstrate or defend a position | Evidence-linked thesis |
| **Dialectical scrutiny** | Critically analyse, critically evaluate, critically discuss | Steelman, test and qualify | Real counter-position plus synthesis |
| **Relational comparison** | Compare and contrast, distinguish | Match or separate units on common parameters | Direct paired comparison |

> **Recall rule:** *Discuss opens; examine inspects; analyse connects; evaluate judges; assess
> measures degree; substantiate proves; justify defends; comment positions; elucidate clarifies;
> account for explains causes; "critical" stress-tests.*

Word-order variants carry the same demand: **"discuss critically" = "critically discuss"** and
**"comment critically" = "critically comment"**. Apply the relevant base directive, then add the
critical stress-test.

## 4. Universal answer canvas

```text
INTRODUCTION
  Directly decode the question + state the answer's governing claim
        |
        v
BODY 1: CORE RESPONSE
  Definition / mechanism / criteria / evidence required by directive
        |
        v
BODY 2: DEPTH
  Second dimension, causal layer, stakeholder, scale or comparison
        |
        v
BODY 3: TEST
  Limitation, counter-position, exception, trade-off or implementation gap
        |
        v
SYNTHESIS
  Resolve the tension; rank causes/criteria; state conditions
        |
        v
CONCLUSION
  Answer the exact question in one qualified judgment
```

The canvas is not a compulsory heading sequence. It is a logic check. Headings should be
question-specific: "Constitutional contribution", "Operational constraints" and "Reform
conditions" are better than generic "Positive/Negative/Way Forward".

## 5. Mark-wise templates

The question paper's printed word limit controls. The structures below are writing heuristics,
not official UPSC rules.

### A. 10-mark / approximately 150-word answer

```text
INTRO (20-25 words)
  Define or frame + one-line thesis

BODY (100-110 words)
  Block 1: primary argument/mechanism + evidence
  Block 2: second dimension + evidence
  Block 3: limit/counterpoint or implication

CONCLUSION (15-20 words)
  Directive-specific finding
```

**Operational target:** 5-7 substantive points, 1-2 precise evidence anchors and no detached
"way forward" unless the question asks for measures. For a two-part question, divide visible
space roughly by the marks implied by each part.

### B. 15-mark / approximately 250-word answer

```text
INTRO (25-35 words)
  Context/definition + qualified thesis

BODY I: FOUNDATION
  Mechanism, criteria or core dimensions

BODY II: DEPTH
  2-3 further lenses: institutional, social, economic, ethical,
  federal, temporal, spatial or stakeholder-based

BODY III: SCRUTINY
  Strongest limitation/counter-position + response

SYNTHESIS / WAY FORWARD
  Conditions, priorities or reform architecture

CONCLUSION (20-30 words)
  Explicit verdict linked to directive
```

**Operational target:** 8-12 substantive points, 3-4 qualified evidence anchors and at least one
clear relationship among them. A diagram or table should compress reasoning, not merely decorate.

### C. 20-mark / long-form analytical answer

Current GS-I, GS-II and GS-III papers ordinarily use 10-mark/150-word and 15-mark/250-word
questions, so this is **not** a substitute for the 15-mark GS template. Use it only where an
optional paper, GS-IV case study, repository mock or another paper actually assigns 20 marks.
The printed word limit always controls; if a practice format sets about 250 words, increase
analytical density rather than paragraph count or padding.

```text
INTRO
  Interpret key terms, delimit scope and state the judgment

I. CONCEPTUAL OR HISTORICAL FOUNDATION
  What the issue is and why the stated proposition arises

II. MULTI-LEVEL ANALYSIS
  Individual/community/institution/state/international
  OR immediate/structural causes and short/long-term effects

III. EVIDENCE AND APPLICATION
  Constitutional/legal + data/report + Indian case/example

IV. CRITICAL TEST
  Strongest counter-case, trade-off, exclusion or implementation gap

V. SYNTHESIS
  Rank priorities; specify institutional conditions and sequencing

CONCLUSION
  Calibrated verdict with constitutional or public-value direction
```

**Operational target:** coverage with hierarchy. More marks require deeper reasoning, not more
unranked bullets. Show at least one of: causal chain, criteria-based verdict, stakeholder conflict,
temporal distinction, counterfactual or implementation sequence.

## 6. Thesis templates by directive

| Directive | Safe thesis stem |
|---|---|
| **Discuss** | "X has widened/strengthened..., but its outcomes vary across..., making Y decisive." |
| **Examine** | "The claim is substantially valid at the level of..., although evidence from... reveals important limits." |
| **Critically examine** | "Closer examination supports the claim in..., but its assumptions fail where..., requiring a qualified finding." |
| **Analyse** | "The outcome is produced by the interaction of structural factor A, institutional factor B and trigger C." |
| **Critically analyse** | "A is a major explanation, but it is neither sufficient nor uniform; B and C condition its effect." |
| **Evaluate** | "Against the criteria of effectiveness, equity and sustainability, X shows mixed but improvable performance." |
| **Critically evaluate** | "X has delivered gains in..., yet design and implementation failures limit both attribution and durability." |
| **Comment** | "The statement identifies a genuine trend but overstates its universality; the stronger view is..." |
| **Elucidate** | "The proposition expresses the idea that...; its logic is visible in..." |
| **Elaborate** | "The proposition becomes clearer when developed across its conceptual, institutional and practical dimensions." |
| **Explain** | "X occurs because A changes B through mechanism C, with consequences for..." |
| **Assess** | "X has had substantial but uneven significance, strongest in... and weakest where..." |
| **Substantiate** | "The claim is supported by convergent constitutional, empirical and institutional evidence." |
| **Justify** | "X is justified on legal, ethical and practical grounds, provided safeguards Y and Z operate." |
| **Compare/contrast** | "Although A and B share..., they differ in authority, mechanism and effect." |
| **Distinguish** | "A and B overlap in..., but the decisive distinction lies in..." |
| **Account for** | "The outcome arose from long-term condition A, proximate trigger B and enabling factor C, with A carrying the greatest weight." |
| **Trace** | "X evolved through stages A, B and C, marked by change in... but continuity in..." |
| **Review** | "The record shows progress in..., stagnation in..., and an unresolved priority in..." |
| **Describe** | "X is defined by the linked characteristics A, B and C." |
| **Enumerate/List** | "The principal components can be classified as A, B, C and D." |
| **Illustrate** | "The proposition is demonstrated by A, B and C, which reveal distinct dimensions of..." |
| **Highlight** | "The principal features/concerns are A, B and C, with B carrying the greatest significance." |
| **Bring out** | "The connection between A and B operates through C and is visible in..." |
| **To what extent** | "The proposition holds to a considerable extent in..., but only partially in..., because..." |

Avoid memorising these as fixed sentences. Replace every placeholder with the question's own
substantive vocabulary.

## 7. Evidence architecture

Use evidence to perform a job in the argument.

| Evidence type | Best use | Correct deployment | Weak deployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional provision or judgment | Legality, institutional design, rights or federalism | State principle/holding and connect it to the claim | Name-dropping an Article or case |
| Official data or index | Scale, trend, distribution or outcome | Give source period, figure and inference | Undated statistic or ranking |
| Committee/report | Diagnosis or reform standard | State the relevant recommendation or finding | Listing committee names |
| Scheme/institution | Delivery mechanism or implementation gap | Explain how design changes incentives/access | Treating existence as proof of success |
| Indian case study | Feasibility, innovation or failure mechanism | Name action, result and transfer condition | Decorative success story |
| Comparative example | Alternative institutional choice | Compare matched parameters and explain transfer limits | Uncritical foreign transplantation |
| Thinker/quotation | Ethical frame or conceptual precision | Explain the idea in your own argument | Opening ornament unrelated to the body |

**Three-link rule:** `evidence -> inference -> question`. If the sentence after an example does not
show what the example proves for this question, the evidence is underused.

## 8. Counterargument and criticality

"Critical" does not mean negative. It means testing a claim against the strongest relevant
challenge.

```text
MAIN CLAIM
  |
  +--> Supporting mechanism and evidence
  |
  +--> STRONGEST OBJECTION
          |
          +--> Is it fatal, conditional or implementation-specific?
                        |
                        v
              QUALIFIED SYNTHESIS
```

Use one or more of these tests:

1. **Assumption test:** what must be true for the claim to hold?
2. **Distribution test:** who gains, who bears costs and who remains excluded?
3. **Scale test:** does the claim hold nationally, locally and across groups?
4. **Time test:** are short-term gains producing long-term costs?
5. **Attribution test:** did the policy cause the outcome, or merely coincide with it?
6. **Counterfactual test:** what likely happens without the policy/event?
7. **Capacity test:** can institutions implement the formal design?
8. **Rights test:** does efficiency impose an unconstitutional or unethical burden?

A one-line "however, challenges remain" is not critical analysis.

## 9. Special question forms

### Two or more directives

"Explain X and critically evaluate Y" requires two visible operations. Complete explanation first,
then evaluate Y through criteria. Do not let the first verb consume the answer.

### "Do you agree?"

State the degree of agreement in the introduction. Test the proposition fairly. End with the same
degree, now justified. "Partly agree" without identifying the accepted and rejected parts is empty.

### Quotation or statement

First interpret the statement. Do not assume its meaning is self-evident. Then obey the attached
directive. A quotation followed by "comment" needs a position; one followed by "elucidate" needs
clarification.

### Cause plus consequence

Keep causes and effects separate. Within causes, distinguish structural conditions, proximate
triggers and enabling factors. Within effects, distinguish immediate/long-term and intended/
unintended consequences.

### Challenges and measures

Pair each measure with its diagnosed failure:

```text
CAPACITY GAP  -> training/staffing/process reform
INCENTIVE GAP -> fiscal, regulatory or behavioural redesign
COORDINATION  -> named lead agency + information protocol
ACCOUNTABILITY-> disclosure, audit, grievance and review
ACCESS GAP    -> last-mile, language, affordability and inclusion
```

A generic final list of technology, awareness, funding and political will is not a reform
architecture.

## 10. Introduction menu

Choose one route only:

| Route | Use when | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Term is contested or technical | Define only the meaning used in the answer |
| Constitutional/legal | Normative or institutional question | Principle/provision -> present tension |
| Data/problem | Scale is central | Dated fact -> inference -> thesis |
| Historical | Evolution explains present design | Turning point -> continuity/change -> thesis |
| Current trigger | Question is linked to a recent event | Verified event -> static issue -> scope |
| Paradox/tension | Competing values are central | State both values -> qualified thesis |

Do not spend the introduction proving knowledge that belongs in the body. A 10-mark answer normally
needs two or three purposeful sentences, not a miniature background essay.

## 11. Conclusion menu

The conclusion must complete the directive:

- **Discuss:** synthesis of dimensions.
- **Examine:** finding after inspection.
- **Analyse/account for:** dominant mechanism or ranked cause.
- **Evaluate/assess/to what extent:** explicit verdict or degree.
- **Critical directives:** qualified judgment and governing condition.
- **Substantiate/justify:** proof or bounded defence.
- **Comment:** refined position.
- **Explain/elucidate:** clarified significance.
- **Compare/contrast:** decisive difference and implication.

Aspirational phrases such as "India can become a superpower" should not replace the answer's
actual finding. Constitutional values, SDGs or committee recommendations belong only when they
resolve the issue raised.

## 12. Same topic, different directive

**Topic:** local governments and democratic decentralisation.

| Question command | What changes in the answer |
|---|---|
| **Discuss** their role | Cover participation, service delivery, accountability, inclusion and constraints in a connected account. |
| **Examine** whether constitutional status produced autonomy | Test formal design against finance, functions, functionaries and state control; give a finding. |
| **Analyse** weak municipal capacity | Trace fiscal, staffing, planning and political mechanisms and their interaction. |
| **Critically evaluate** the reform record | Establish criteria, weigh achievements and failures, test attribution and deliver a verdict. |
| **Substantiate** that local bodies deepen democracy | Prove through representation, participation and accountable local delivery evidence. |
| **To what extent** are local bodies self-governing? | Define self-government benchmark, show where it exists, identify boundaries and state degree. |

The content pool overlaps; the selection, sequence and burden of proof do not.

## 13. Final 30-second audit

| Check | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Directive** | Did every major paragraph perform the required operation? |
| **Demand** | Did I answer all clauses and limiting words? |
| **Thesis** | Is my position visible in the introduction? |
| **Mechanism** | Did I explain how/why, not only what? |
| **Evidence** | Is each example qualified and linked to an inference? |
| **Criticality** | Did I engage the strongest relevant limitation rather than a token challenge? |
| **Hierarchy** | Did I rank causes, criteria or reforms instead of listing them equally? |
| **Conclusion** | Did I deliver the finding, degree or verdict demanded? |

If one body paragraph could be pasted unchanged into any question on the same topic, rewrite it to
connect explicitly to this question's directive and limiter.

## 14. Practice conversion drill

For any knowledge-file topic, write six one-line thesis statements:

1. Discuss.
2. Examine.
3. Analyse.
4. Critically analyse.
5. Evaluate.
6. To what extent.

Then write only the body headings for a 10-mark and a 15-mark version. This drill separates
content recall from command compliance and prepares the topic for the
[`PYQ trigger dictionary`](QUICK-REVISION-ASSET-ROADMAP.md), the next canonical revision asset.
