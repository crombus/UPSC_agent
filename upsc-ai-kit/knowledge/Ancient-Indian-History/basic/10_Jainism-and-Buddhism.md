## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — plural historical setting, not a single-cause protest story.**

✅ RS Sharma says numerous religious sects arose in the mid-Gangetic plains in the **sixth–fifth centuries BC**; as many as **sixty-two** are mentioned. Jainism and Buddhism were the most important and became powerful religious reform movements.
- ✅ Their rise is linked to the social and economic conditions of north-east India, including new towns, trade and questioning of ritualistic Vedic religion.
- ✅ Jainism made a serious attempt to mitigate the evils of the varna order and ritualistic religion.
- ⚠️ Both traditions used Middle Indo-Aryan speech forms and organized renunciant-lay communities; their relations with changing Brahmanical traditions included debate, competition, borrowing and accommodation.

**Standard — doctrines and institutions.**

✅ Jainism taught five doctrines: non-violence, truth, non-stealing, non-hoarding and celibacy; RS Sharma notes the fifth was added by Mahavira, while the earlier four were linked to previous teachers.
- ✅ Jain liberation can be obtained through **right knowledge, right faith and right action** — the **Triratna**.
- ✅ Buddhism explains suffering through desire and prescribes the **Eightfold Path** for ending misery and attaining nirvana.
- ⚠️ Buddhism spread across South, Central, South-East and East Asia. Many Indian monastic centres later contracted, but regional continuities and modern revivals make “extinction” too absolute.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ Sixth–fifth centuries BC saw many sects in the mid-Gangetic plains; Jainism and Buddhism became the most important.
- ✅ Jain five vows: non-violence, truth, non-stealing, non-hoarding, celibacy.
- ✅ Triratna: right knowledge, right faith, right action.
- ✅ Jainism split later into **Shvetambara** and **Digambara** traditions.
- ✅ Buddhism: desire causes sorrow; Eightfold Path leads toward nirvana.
- ✅ Buddhism spread widely outside India but declined in India by the twelfth century AD.
- ✅ **Prelims identity distinction:** *Shakyamuni* and *Tathagata* are epithets of Gautama Buddha; **Nayaputta/Nataputta** refers to Mahavira in Pali tradition.
- ✅ Jain doctrine recognizes four main forms of existence: **deva** (gods), **manushya** (humans), **naraki** (hell beings) and **tiryancha** (animals and plants).
- ✅ Upanishads predate the Puranas and do contain parables/dialogues; do not confuse later Puranic narrative with all early philosophical storytelling.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: UPSC mixes founders, doctrines and sects.

- ❌ Mahavira founded ahimsa for the first time. → RS Sharma notes earlier teachers already taught four vows; Mahavira added celibacy.
- ❌ Triratna belongs to Buddhism. → It is a Jain formulation in this context.
- ❌ *Nayaputta* is an epithet of the Buddha. → It is associated with **Mahavira**; Buddha is *Shakyamuni/Tathagata*.
- ❌ Buddhism either survived unchanged everywhere or wholly disappeared from India by one date. → Institutional contraction was regional and multicausal, with Himalayan/eastern continuities and later revivals.
- ❌ Jainism and Buddhism were only spiritual movements. → They were also responses to social, economic and ritual conditions.

### Counterpoint and balance

- Neither tradition was wholly outside the wider Indian religious field; both interacted with Brahmanical society over time.
- Do not write them as identical egalitarian revolutions; social critique, doctrine and reach varied.

### Verdict framing

- Conclude by combining reform, doctrinal distinctiveness and long-term adaptation.
- A graded verdict should say that both reshaped Indian thought, but through different social and institutional routes.

**Core firewall:** Similar ethical vocabulary does not make Jain and Buddhist doctrines identical. Nor should either tradition be reduced to a simple “protest religion,” “merchant religion,” “anti-caste movement” or product of one ruler.

### 9.1 Evidence ladder

| Evidence class | What it can establish | Mandatory caution |
|---|---|---|
| Jain and Buddhist canonical traditions | Doctrines, disciplinary ideals, remembered biographies, institutional classifications and debates | Canonical corpora were orally transmitted, layered and redacted; a prescription is not a census of practice |
| Later biographies and commentaries | Development of sacred memory, pilgrimage geography, school interpretations and literary traditions | Temporal distance and sectarian purpose limit their value for the founders' exact lives |
| Inscriptions | Donors, monastic establishments, royal support, lineages, places and some sect names | Donative and royal formulae select what patrons wished to record; silence is not proof of absence |
| Archaeology and art | Stupas, caves, monasteries, images, relic deposits, production and pilgrimage networks | Material form does not transparently reveal doctrine; dates and functions must be established contextually |
| Foreign-language canons and travel accounts | Transmission, translation and regional institutions outside the subcontinent | Translation vocabulary and outsider categories may alter an earlier Indian concept |

### 9.2 Source-critical rules

1. Separate a **traditional chronology** from a date securely established by contemporary evidence.
2. Treat the Buddha's and Mahavira's biographies as a historical core surrounded by later narrative elaboration.
3. Identify the **school and textual tradition** behind a council or canon account; Buddhist and Jain traditions do not transmit one neutral institutional chronology.
4. Use inscriptions and excavated remains to test textual claims, not merely illustrate them.
5. Distinguish the founder's probable teaching from later systematic formulations. Mature Jain logic and Buddhist philosophy cannot simply be projected into the sixth-fifth centuries BCE.
6. Reconstruct expansion through combined evidence of monks/nuns, lay donors, merchants, rulers, routes, monasteries and regional institutions.

## 10. Historical setting: necessary but not monocausal

Jainism and Buddhism developed within a **diverse śramaṇa environment** in the middle Gangetic region during the first millennium BCE. Their rise coincided with expanding settlements, agrarian surplus, towns, exchange, monetization, new states and intensified social differentiation. These conditions widened audiences and patronage networks, but none alone “caused” either tradition.

| Context | Historical mechanism | Limit to state |
|---|---|---|
| Second urbanization and trade | Towns and routes connected teachers, householders and monastic establishments | Merchant support was important, but followers also included rulers, cultivators, artisans, elites and socially marginal people |
| Territorial states and oligarchic polities | Safer routes, courts and concentrated surplus could support itinerants and monasteries | Royal patronage accelerated institutions; it did not create the doctrines or replace lay support |
| Ritual and social change | Expensive sacrifice, hierarchy and uncertainty encouraged alternative ethical and renunciatory paths | Jainism and Buddhism interacted with, rather than merely “rejected,” changing Brahmanical traditions |
| Intellectual pluralism | Wandering renouncers publicly debated action, rebirth, liberation, knowledge and discipline | Śramaṇa was a broad field including Jain, Buddhist, Ājīvika, materialist and other positions |

> **Exam-safe judgement:** Urbanization, state formation, social tension and intellectual ferment supplied a favourable ecology. The emergence of Jainism and Buddhism still requires their distinctive teachings, organizations and patronage networks.

## 11. Founders, predecessors and biography

### 11.1 Mahavira

- Jain tradition presents Vardhamana Mahavira as the **twenty-fourth Tirthankara**, not the first founder of an entirely new faith. Pārśva, the twenty-third Tirthankara, may preserve an earlier historical stratum, but claims about remote Tirthankaras require graded evidence.
- Buddhist texts refer to Mahavira as **Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta/Nayaputta**; the latter is a PYQ trap and is not an epithet of the Buddha.
- Traditions associate Mahavira with the Vajji region, renunciation, prolonged asceticism, attainment of omniscience and teaching through an order of ascetics and lay followers.
- Competing traditional and scholarly chronologies exist. Use “sixth-fifth centuries BCE” unless a question explicitly asks a named traditional date.
- Later Jain biographies are historically usable for sectarian memory and sacred geography, not as contemporary diaries.

### 11.2 The Buddha

- Siddhartha Gautama is remembered as a Śākya from the Himalayan foothill zone. Ashoka's Lumbini pillar inscription confirms the early association of the Buddha with Lumbini, but does not verify every later biographical episode.
- The renunciation, search among teachers, rejection of both indulgence and extreme self-mortification, awakening at Bodh Gaya, first sermon at Sarnath and death/parinirvana at Kushinagara form the standard sacred biography.
- The exact chronology is debated; different “long” and “short” chronological traditions exist. Avoid converting one traditional birth/death pair into uncontested history.
- **Śākyamuni** and **Tathāgata** are recognized Buddhist epithets. Nayaputta/Nātaputta refers to Mahavira.

## 12. Jain historical minimum

### 12.1 Doctrine required for historical explanation

| Concept | Precise historical use |
|---|---|
| Living and non-living reality (**jīva/ajīva**) | Jain teaching treats living beings as ensouled and karma as binding matter; this explains rigorous asceticism and radical non-injury |
| Three jewels (**triratna**) | Right faith, right knowledge and right conduct form the path to liberation |
| Five vows | Non-injury, truth, non-stealing, celibacy and non-possession; ascetic “great vows” are stricter than lay observances |
| Karma and liberation | Liberation requires stopping karmic influx and shedding accumulated karma through knowledge, conduct and austerity |
| Many-sidedness (**anekāntavāda**), conditioned predication (**syādvāda**) and standpoints (**nayavāda**) | Historically important Jain strategies against one-sided claims; their fully systematized forms are later and should not all be attributed verbatim to Mahavira |
| Four forms of existence (**gati**) | Deva, manuṣya, nāraki and tiryañca; **yakṣa is not one of the four** |

The 2023 PYQ about souls in rocks, running water and other natural objects tests Jain classification of embodied life. This is an ancient ontology connected to **ahiṃsā**, not modern biology or environmental science.

### 12.2 Community, language and gender

- The fourfold Jain community comprised monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen. Renunciation depended materially on household donors.
- Jain ethics could attract urban and mercantile groups because disciplined exchange, non-injury and lay giving were compatible with commercial networks. This is a tendency, not proof that Jainism was only a merchant religion.
- Prakrit preaching and later literary production broadened communication. Jain canonical language traditions include Ardhamagadhi and other Prakrits; regional communities also produced major Sanskrit, Kannada, Tamil and Apabhramsha literatures.
- Women entered Jain renunciant communities in large numbers in some traditions. Sectarian doctrines later differed: Digambara authorities generally held that a woman could not attain liberation in a female body, while Śvetāmbara traditions accepted women's capacity for liberation.

### 12.3 Canons, councils and sect formation

| Issue | Exam-safe account | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Pataliputra council | Jain tradition links an early effort to preserve teachings during/after famine and migration with Sthulabhadra | Details and chronology come from later sectarian traditions |
| Valabhi | Śvetāmbara tradition associates Valabhi, around the mid-first millennium CE, with the final redaction of its canon | Digambaras do not accept the Śvetāmbara Agamas as the preserved original canon |
| Digambara-Śvetāmbara formation | Differences concern clothing/“sky-clad” asceticism, scriptures, images/ritual and women's liberation | The division was gradual over centuries; do not reduce it to one council, famine or date |
| Sthānakavāsī | A non-image-worship reform tradition within the Śvetāmbara family | It is a much later development, retained here because UPSC routed a PYQ to this owner |

### 12.4 Expansion and persistence

Jain communities developed strong centres in the Gangetic region, Mathura, western India, Kalinga and parts of the Deccan and south India. Udayagiri-Khandagiri, Mathura votive tablets and inscriptions, and later Shravanabelagola traditions are useful material anchors, but each belongs to a specific period.

Jainism persisted through durable ascetic-lay networks, adaptable lay ethics, regional communities, mercantile and elite support, literary production and institutional continuity. Persistence did not mean absence of change: sects, ritual forms, temple culture, philosophical systems and regional languages evolved.

## 13. Buddhist historical minimum

### 13.1 Teaching as an institutional programme

| Teaching | Historical significance |
|---|---|
| Four Noble Truths | Suffering/unsatisfactoriness; its cause; its cessation; and the path to cessation |
| Middle Path (**majjhimā paṭipadā**) | Explicitly rejects both sensual indulgence and self-mortification; it is taught through the Eightfold Path |
| Noble Eightfold Path | Right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration, often grouped as wisdom, morality and mental discipline |
| Dependent origination (**pratītyasamutpāda/paṭiccasamuppāda**) | Phenomena arise through conditions rather than an uncaused first beginning |
| Impermanence and non-self (**anitya/anicca; anātman/anattā**) | Undermine attachment to a permanent, independent self; Buddhism must not be described as Jain soul doctrine |
| Karma and nirvana | Intention/action sustains rebirth; liberation ends greed, hatred and delusion and the rebirth process |

### 13.2 Sangha, laity and economy

- The Buddhist community is conventionally organized around Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha. Monks (**bhikkhu**) and nuns (**bhikkhunī**) followed disciplinary rules (**Vinaya**); lay followers (**upāsaka/upāsikā**) supplied food, robes, medicine and residence and sought merit and instruction.
- Monasteries interacted with routes, towns, craft groups, donors and agrarian landscapes. They were not detached from the economy merely because monastics ideally renounced property.
- Donative inscriptions show gifts by rulers, merchants, householders, monks, nuns and other individuals. “Royal religion” and “merchant religion” are therefore both inadequate single labels.
- Entry into the sangha could cross hereditary status, but the institution did not instantly abolish caste or all hierarchy. Texts contain both egalitarian ethical arguments and accommodations to existing social categories.

### 13.3 Women and institutional limits

Tradition associates Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī with the creation of the order of nuns after initial resistance. Accounts of the **eight heavy rules (garudhammas)** are textually layered and historically debated, but they reflect institutional subordination in received Vinaya traditions. Nuns were teachers and donors as well as subjects of regulation. Avoid both the claim that Buddhism created full gender equality and the opposite claim that women had no religious agency.

### 13.4 Language and communication

- Early Buddhist teaching circulated orally in Middle Indo-Aryan speech forms. **Pali is the canonical language of Theravada**, not a proven verbatim record of the Buddha's personal dialect.
- Other schools preserved texts in Sanskritized or Prakrit forms; many works survive through Chinese and Tibetan translations.
- Accessible languages aided expansion, but language alone does not explain conversion. Organization, ethical teaching, patronage, routes and regional adaptation also mattered.

## 14. Buddhist councils, canons and schools

### 14.1 Council traditions

| Council label | Conventional association | Source-control rule |
|---|---|---|
| First, Rajagriha | Recitation of teaching and discipline soon after the Buddha's parinirvana | Revered institutional memory; details are recorded in later traditions |
| Second, Vaishali | Dispute over monastic discipline, conventionally about a century later | Do not make every later school division the immediate result of one dispute |
| Third, Pataliputra | Ashoka and Moggaliputta Tissa in Theravada chronicles; purification and missions | The account is school-specific and not identically preserved in all traditions |
| “Fourth” councils | Sarvastivada tradition associates a council in Kashmir with Kanishka; Theravada history separately remembers the writing of the canon in Sri Lanka | There is no single pan-Buddhist fourth-council narrative |

The Theravada Pali **Tipitaka** comprises Vinaya, Sutta and Abhidhamma collections. Other early schools had their own Vinayas and Āgama/Abhidharma traditions. Chinese and Tibetan canons preserve materials lost in Indic originals.

### 14.2 Schools and later developments

- The early distinction between **Sthavira** and **Mahāsāṃghika** lineages belongs to complex post-Buddha institutional history. Theravada derives through a Sthavira lineage but is not a simple synonym for every Sthaviravada school.
- Lokottaravada is associated with the Mahāsāṃghika family. UPSC's 2020 framing links Mahāsāṃghika developments with conditions favourable to Mahayana, but Mahayana emerged gradually and cannot be assigned to one council or school alone.
- **Sarvastivada** became influential in north-western India, Kashmir and Central Asia. Sanghabhuti's late-fourth-century CE commentary concerns the Sarvastivada Vinaya; do not confuse him with Sanghabhadra.
- **Mahayana** grew through multiple communities and texts around the early centuries BCE/CE. The Bodhisattva ideal, **pāramitās** (perfections), expanded Buddha/Bodhisattva cults and new sutras are central, but image worship and ritual were not exclusive to Mahayana.
- **Maitreya** is the future Buddha in Buddhist tradition and is not exclusively Mahayana.
- Vajrayana was a later tantric development and should not be projected into the Buddha's age.
- “Hinayana” is a polemical Mahayana category, not a neutral synonym for Theravada.

## 15. Patronage, networks and material evidence

### 15.1 Patronage without a king-only explanation

- Early traditions remember support from rulers of Magadha and Kosala and wealthy householders such as Anathapindika and Visakha. These accounts must be read with their textual setting.
- Ashoka's inscriptions provide unusually early evidence for royal support, pilgrimage and concern with the Buddhist sangha. His wider **dhamma** policy was not simply identical with Buddhist doctrine, and Ashoka neither founded Buddhism nor single-handedly made it global.
- Post-Mauryan patrons, local rulers, merchants, monks, nuns and ordinary donors sustained Buddhist institutions. Kanishka's Buddhist role is important but also filtered through later traditions.

### 15.2 Material and spatial anchors

| Evidence | Historical use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Stupas and relic deposits | Commemoration, relic veneration, pilgrimage and donor networks | The stupa form had broader funerary antecedents; not every stupa began as a relic repository |
| Sanchi, Bharhut and Amaravati/Dhanyakataka | Regional patronage, narrative art and changing Buddhist communities | Detailed style chronology belongs to Art and Culture |
| Rock-cut caves and monasteries | Residence, worship, route-linked patronage and institutional expansion | A cave label must be matched to its inscription, phase and occupants |
| Inscriptions | Names and social profiles of donors, schools and establishments | Donor identity does not reveal the whole population's religion |
| Images and manuscripts | Devotional, scholastic and transmission histories | Buddha images cannot be reduced to a sudden Mahayana “invention” |

Dhanyakataka/Dharanikota in the lower Krishna-Andhra region is associated with the Amaravati Buddhist zone and Mahāsāṃghika traditions. It must not be confused with Gandhara, Kalinga or Magadha.

### 15.3 Expansion beyond India

Buddhism spread through overlapping monastic, mercantile, diplomatic, translation and pilgrimage networks:

1. Sri Lanka, where Theravada chronicles remember missions connected with Ashoka's reign;
2. north-west India and Central Asia, with movement along transregional routes;
3. China through translators and travelling monks, followed by Korea and Japan;
4. mainland and maritime Southeast Asia through multiple phases and school networks;
5. Himalayan and Tibetan regions through later Sanskritic and tantric transmissions.

No single route, ruler or language explains this expansion. Transmission changed texts, institutions, art and practice in each region.

## 16. Interaction, consequences and uneven trajectories

### 16.1 Interaction with Brahmanical traditions

Jain, Buddhist and Brahmanical traditions competed, borrowed, debated and shared patrons. Ideas of non-injury, renunciation, karma, pilgrimage, image worship, monastic/temple institutions and philosophical argument changed through interaction. Similarity does not prove one-way borrowing, and “Brahmanism versus Buddhism/Jainism” is too rigid for many local contexts.

### 16.2 Historical consequences

- Monastic and lay institutions created durable communities extending beyond kin and locality.
- Prakrit, Pali, Sanskrit and regional literary cultures grew through canonical, narrative, philosophical and inscriptional production.
- Stupas, caves, monasteries, images and pilgrimage landscapes became major components of South Asian material culture.
- Buddhist translation and travel connected India with Sri Lanka, Central Asia, East Asia and Southeast Asia.
- Jain ethics, scholarship and patronage shaped western and southern Indian regional histories.

### 16.3 Buddhism's relative decline and transformation in India

The weakening of many Buddhist institutions was **regional, gradual and multicausal**, not a single event:

1. changing patterns of royal and landed patronage;
2. competition and accommodation with developing Brahmanical/Puranic and devotional institutions;
3. absorption of some practices, deities and sacred places into wider religious fields;
4. economic and settlement changes affecting large monasteries;
5. institutional concentration and uneven lay-monastic relations;
6. regional political violence, including damage to some monasteries, with later raids aggravating rather than originating every decline.

Buddhism did not simply “disappear”: it remained important in Himalayan and eastern zones, flourished outside India and experienced modern revivals. Do not use Turkish raids, Brahmanical hostility, tantric “degeneration” or loss of royal patronage as a sufficient single cause.

### 16.4 Why Jainism endured

Jainism's continued presence is best explained by resilient ascetic-lay organization, localized communities, commercial and elite support, literary/institutional adaptation and less complete dependence on very large monastic establishments. This is a comparative explanation, not proof that Jain history was static or conflict-free.

## 17. Controlled Jain-Buddhist comparison

| Dimension | Jainism | Buddhism |
|---|---|---|
| Self/living being | Affirms jīva; liberation frees it from karmic matter | Rejects a permanent independent self (anātman/anattā) |
| Discipline | Rigorous asceticism and radical ahiṃsā; lay vows are scaled | Middle Path and Eightfold Path; monastic Vinaya with lay ethical support |
| Karma | Subtle material bondage to jīva | Intentional action and conditioned rebirth without a permanent self |
| Community | Monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen | Monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen |
| Canon issue | Śvetāmbara Agamas accepted; Digambara tradition holds original canon lost | Multiple school canons; Pali Tipitaka is Theravada, not the only Buddhist canon |
| Historical trajectory | Strong long-term regional continuity in India | Wider trans-Asian expansion; relative institutional decline in many Indian regions |

| Broad period | Development | Confidence rule |

## 19. Hostile-search trap register

| Unsafe claim | Marks-safe correction |
|---|---|
| “Jainism and Buddhism arose only because traders opposed Brahmanism.” | Urban, political, social and intellectual changes widened support, but causation and social bases were plural |
| “Both denied caste and created equality.” | Both questioned inherited status in important ways; institutions still operated within unequal societies and contained exclusions |
| “Pali was the language spoken by the Buddha; Mahavira spoke only Ardhamagadhi.” | Early teachings circulated in Middle Indo-Aryan forms; surviving canonical language is not automatic verbatim speech |
| “Ashoka converted India and exported Buddhism everywhere.” | Ashokan patronage was major; expansion relied on many earlier/later actors and routes |
| “All Buddhist councils are securely dated facts.” | Councils are transmitted through school-specific textual memories and require source labels |
| “Theravada is Hinayana.” | Theravada is a living school; Hinayana is a polemical and over-broad retrospective category |
| “Mahayana began at the fourth council and invented images.” | Mahayana emerged gradually in diverse communities; images and rituals had wider institutional histories |
| “Digambara and Śvetāmbara split during one famine.” | Sect formation was gradual; famine/migration narratives are later explanatory traditions |
| “Buddhism vanished because of Muslim invasions.” | Decline was regional and multicausal; later violence aggravated some already changing institutional landscapes |
| “Jainism survived because it never changed.” | Institutional, sectarian, literary and ritual adaptation helped continuity |
