## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — politics of the peninsula, AD 300–750.**

✅ RS Sharma's core frame is not just dynastic war; it is **new states + rural expansion + Brahmanization**. In the Deccan and far south, land grants, temples and agrarian settlements reshaped society.
- ✅ The main political conflict was between the **Pallavas of Kanchi** and the **Chalukyas of Badami**; the Pandyas were a weaker third participant.
- ✅ **Pulakeshin II (AD 609–642)** is known from Ravikirti's **Aihole inscription**, a Sanskrit eulogy of high poetic quality but also exaggeration.
- ✅ Pulakeshin II checked Harsha and pressed towards Kanchipuram; later **Narasimhavarman I Mahamalla** reversed Pallava fortunes.
- ✅ The Tungabhadra often formed a historic boundary between Deccan and deep-south powers.

**Standard — Brahmanization, grants and temples.**

✅ Land grants to Brahmanas stimulated agrarian expansion under the Pallavas in south Andhra and north Tamil Nadu, but RS Sharma also notes adverse effects on peasants.
- ⚠️ R.S. Sharma reads the later Velvikudi grant's memory of Kalabhra cancellation of Brahmana gifts as evidence of social conflict. Because the record is a much later Pandya charter legitimating restoration, neither a single "Kalabhra revolt" nor its class programme should be treated as established fact.
- ✅ Pallavas and Chalukyas both championed Brahmanism, performed Vedic sacrifices and made grants to Brahmanas.
- ✅ Pallavas built rock-cut and structural monuments at **Mahabalipuram/Mamallapuram** and Kanchi; Chalukyas built at **Aihole, Badami and Pattadakal**.
- ⚠️ The Bhakti beginnings of Alvars/Nayanars should be linked to temple society, vernacular devotion and changing religious culture.
- ✅ Pallava literary culture included the Sanskrit farce **`Mattavilasa-prahasana`** of Mahendravarman I; early medieval Tamil devotional songs of the **Alvars and Nayanmars** developed in the same broad south-Indian milieu.
- ✅ Pallava inscriptions often used Sanskrit for eulogistic *prashasti* and Tamil for documentary portions.

## 7. Mains angles

### Pallava analytical/source caveats (promoted from Advanced — paper-essential)

⚠️ **For mains answers on Pallavas, embed these source-method points:**
1. The **Aihole inscription** is a royal eulogy (Ravikirti's praise of Pulakeshin II), not a neutral biography; use it as evidence while noting its political purpose.
2. Pallava epigraphy often divided labour between **Sanskrit prashasti** and **Tamil documentary** sections — evidence for elite integration and regional administration.
3. Mahendravarman I's **`Mattavilasa-prahasana`** (7th-c. Sanskrit farce) satirises Buddhists, Kapalikas and Pashupatas, revealing inter-religious competition.
4. **Bhakti** (Alvars/Nayanmars) could challenge social hierarchy rhetorically yet operated within emerging temple-centred society — avoid simple "anti-caste revolution" framing.
5. Brahmanization includes land, agrarian relations, caste formation and cult appropriation — not only religious conversion.
6. Temple architecture is evidence for royal ideology, craft networks and regional identity, not just art-style chronology.

### Thesis options

| Directive | Thesis |
|---|---|
| Estimate contribution (2024 PYQ) | The Pallavas of Kanchi contributed to South Indian art and literature not merely through patronage but by creating a template where royal power, sacred geography, Tamil-Sanskrit bilingualism and craft innovation intersected — though our evidence is primarily monumental and epigraphic, not literary in the modern sense. |
| Critically examine | Pallava "contribution" must be read through source caveats: inscriptions are eulogies, architecture survives while perishable arts are lost, and the role of anonymous craftsmen and religious communities is obscured by royal attribution. |

- ⚠️ Pallava-Chalukya history is a bridge between political rivalry and temple-state formation.
- ⚠️ Land grants show how Brahmanization, agrarian expansion and rural hierarchy developed together.
- ⚠️ Peninsular temples were not only religious spaces; they became markers of power, settlement and cultural integration.

## 9. Semantic-completeness repair: scope, chronology and source method

### 9.1 What this topic owns

This owner explains peninsular political and social transformation, mainly from the
sixth to the mid-eighth century: the Pallavas of Kanchi, the Chalukyas of Badami,
their neighbours and the processes conventionally described as
**Brahmanization**, agrarian expansion and regional state formation.

| Included here | Bounded link-out |
|---|---|
| Pallava and Badami Chalukya chronology, rivalry and political geography | Topic 22 supplies the Harsha context |
| Eastern Chalukya foundation and the Rashtrakuta takeover as boundary transitions | Later Eastern Chalukya history and Imperial Cholas belong to Topic 27 |
| Grants, temples, Bhakti and language as institutions of state and locality formation | Detailed temple-style taxonomy belongs to Art and Culture |
| Brahmanization as a negotiated material, social and cultural process | Topic 26 owns the all-India ancient-to-medieval synthesis |

The label **early medieval** identifies a cluster of changes, not a simultaneous
break across the whole peninsula. Political consolidation, agrarian expansion,
trade and religious institutions developed at different rates in the western
Deccan, Andhra and Tamil regions.

### 9.2 Source hierarchy and claim limits

| Evidence | What it can establish | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Stone inscriptions and royal eulogies (*praśastis*) | Rulers, titles, donors, named campaigns, offices and political ideals | Exact frontiers, permanent annexation or neutral accounts of victory |
| Copper-plate grants | Donor, beneficiary, locality, fiscal or administrative concessions and formulas of legitimacy | That every formula was enforced identically or that the king surrendered all sovereignty |
| Coins and seals | Royal names/titles, iconography and some zones of circulation | Complete territorial control, prosperity or a fully monetized economy |
| Archaeology and settlement evidence | Material sequence, production, irrigation, occupation and exchange | Ethnicity or dynastic rule without an independent link |
| Temples, caves and sculpture | Patronage, technology, workshop mobility, ritual landscape and political communication | A rigid ethnic "Pallava" or "Chalukya" style for every object |
| Sanskrit, Tamil and Kannada works; later hagiographies | Court culture, religious argument, devotional worlds and remembered traditions | Transparent reportage, secure authorship/date in every case, or the experience of all social groups |

> **Method rule:** triangulate claim, genre, date and location. An inscription may
> prove that a court wanted a victory remembered; archaeology and rival records
> are still needed to determine duration and depth of control.

### 10.2 Origins without dynastic mythology

- The Pallavas' origin is disputed. Attempts to derive them exclusively from
  the Pahlavas, a northern lineage, a local *Tondaimandalam* group or one fixed
  ethnic community go beyond the evidence.
- Early Pallava charters appear in Prakrit and then Sanskrit; later political
  practice became increasingly Tamil-Sanskrit. This is institutional adaptation,
  not proof of one racial origin.
- The Badami Chalukyas emerged in the western Deccan. Later genealogies and
  solar/lunar or divine descent claims were legitimating devices, not literal
  records of migration.
- Do not project modern Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh or Tamil Nadu as fixed
  seventh-century nation-states. Dynastic zones overlapped language regions,
  ecological corridors and networks of subordinate chiefs.

### 10.3 Conflict as a sequence, not a two-state final

```text
Pulakeshin II expands across Deccan
              |
              v
Pallava pressure under Mahendravarman I
              |
              v
Narasimhavarman I counter-campaign -> Vatapi captured
              |
              v
Chalukya recovery under Vikramaditya I
              |
              v
Renewed attacks and counter-attacks -> Vikramaditya II at Kanchi
              |
              v
Rashtrakutas displace Badami Chalukyas; Pallavas face new rivals
```

- Ravikirti's Aihole inscription, dated to the mid-630s, praises Pulakeshin II
  and his check to Harsha. It is indispensable but eulogistic.
- Pallava records and titles such as **Vatapikonda** preserve the
  Narasimhavarman I counter-victory. A campaign success is not evidence that
  Pallavas permanently administered the whole western Deccan.
- Chalukya records celebrating Kanchi campaigns similarly encode prestige.
  They should not be converted automatically into durable annexation.
- Rivalry was not a timeless ethnic struggle. Access to routes, fertile zones,
  subordinate allies, prestige and strategic capitals changed with each phase.

### 10.4 Neighbours and cross-regional relations

| Actor | Bounded role |
|---|---|
| Pandyas | Competitors in the far south; recovered political importance alongside Pallava expansion |
| Western Gangas | Important Karnataka-region allies/rivals in Chalukya-Pallava conflict |
| Kadambas | Earlier western-Deccan power whose territories and chiefs entered Chalukya expansion |
| Alupas and local chiefs | Show that dynastic power worked through regional intermediaries |
| Sri Lanka | Pallava involvement in succession struggles, especially the Manavamma tradition, shows Bay of Bengal diplomacy; it does not make Sri Lanka a Pallava province |
| Rashtrakutas | Overthrew the Badami Chalukyas c. 753; later Deccan history is a transition link |

## 11. State formation, administration and military power

### 11.1 Layered sovereignty

Neither polity was a uniformly bureaucratic territorial state. Royal households
worked through princes, officers, tributary rulers, military retainers, local
chiefs, assemblies and landed/religious beneficiaries.

| Level | Typical evidence-led function | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| King and court | War, alliance, ritual, high appointments and grants | Titles advertise ideals as well as actual capacity |
| Province/district | Terms such as *rāṣṭra*, *viṣaya*, *kottam* or *nāḍu* vary by dynasty, date and region | Do not impose one fixed hierarchy everywhere |
| Subordinate chiefs/feudatories | Tribute, troops and locality mediation | Their loyalty could be negotiated or unstable |
| Village/town bodies | Revenue, irrigation, local transactions and endowments where records attest them | Later Chola assembly detail must not be back-projected |
| Brahmana/temple beneficiaries | Managed endowed resources and received specified dues or immunities | A grant did not make every beneficiary an autonomous sovereign |

Administration depended on access to agrarian revenue, tolls, tribute and labour.
Armies combined infantry, cavalry, elephants and contingents supplied through
subordinate relations. Fortified capitals and route control mattered, but a
victorious raid did not equal continuous occupation.

### 11.2 State-model debate

| Model | Contribution | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Indian-feudalism interpretation | Highlights grants, fiscal immunities, intermediaries, peasant dependence and possible devolution | Formulas and outcomes varied; commerce, towns and royal capacity did not disappear uniformly |
| Segmentary-state heuristic | Draws attention to ritual sovereignty and uneven control beyond a core | Developed most fully for later Chola evidence; it cannot simply be pasted onto every Pallava/Chalukya phase |
| Integrative/processual approaches | Stress alliances, cults, grants, local elites and institutions that built wider polities | Integration could also produce hierarchy, extraction and conflict |
| Peasantization/agrarian-expansion approach | Connects settlement growth, incorporation of forest/pastoral groups and new producing communities | "Tribe to peasant" was neither one-way nor identical in every ecology |

**Balanced judgement:** early medieval regional states combined coercion,
redistribution, negotiated intermediary power and ritual-cultural integration.
No single model exhausts the evidence.

## 12. Agrarian expansion, irrigation and grants

### 12.1 Grant vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning | Exam caution |
|---|---|---|
| *Brahmadeya* | Land/village granted to one or more Brahmanas | Terms and rights vary by charter |
| *Agrahāra* | Tax-privileged Brahmana settlement/endowment in many contexts | Not every agrahara began as an empty-land colony |
| *Devadāna* | Gift assigned to a deity/temple | Temple control and village obligations were not uniform |

Copper plates can list boundaries, taxes, exemptions, dues and rights over
resources or labour. Read each concession precisely: a formula cannot be assumed
to transfer every fiscal, judicial and policing function in practice.

### 12.2 Material chain

```text
Royal/local initiative + donor resources
                  |
forest, dry land or older village landscapes
                  |
settlement extension + tanks/canals/wells + cultivation
                  |
new revenue and ritual centres
                  |
Brahmana/temple/local elite power
                  |
cooperation, hierarchy and possible peasant burdens
```

- Tanks and other water-control works supported cultivation in rainfall-variable
  zones; maintenance often depended on local communities, donors and institutions,
  not the king alone.
- Grants could attract specialists, mark claims to land and reorganize dues.
  They could also confirm rights within an already occupied locality.
- Beneficiaries, peasants, pastoralists, forest communities and artisans were
  active participants. "Empty land civilized by a grant" is usually a court-centred
  description, not a neutral account of prior use.
- Agrarian expansion did not eliminate trade, pastoralism or forest-resource
  economies. Regional mixtures persisted.

## 13. Brahmanization, Sanskritization and localization

**Brahmanization** here means expanding Brahmana settlement and authority,
ritual legitimation, temple/Puranic institutions, Sanskritic idioms and
varna-jati claims. It is more than conversion and less than total homogenization.

### 13.1 Negotiated process

```text
Court seeks legitimacy and locality access
        <-> Brahmanas receive patronage/resources
        <-> Local chiefs seek rank and recognition
        <-> Cult places/deities gain wider identities
        <-> Cultivators/artisans sustain institutions
        <-> Regional languages reshape received forms
```

- **Sanskritization** can describe adoption of high-status practices; it should
  not erase material power, coercion or reciprocal adaptation.
- **Localization/vernacularization** explains how Sanskritic-Puranic forms were
  translated into Tamil, Kannada and Telugu-region political and devotional
  worlds.
- Local deities were identified with wider Shaiva, Vaishnava or Shakta forms,
  but local names, myths, festivals and social constituencies could persist.
- Brahmana migration is visible through grants and lineage references, but an
  inscription records a beneficiary network, not a complete population map.
- Varna language interacted with proliferating occupational and locality-based
  **jatis**; it did not make peninsular society a copy of a northern textual model.
- Peasants, pastoralists, artisans and service groups faced differentiated
  obligations and opportunities. Elite records leave many of their voices silent.

### 13.2 Gender and donor visibility

Royal women and other women appear as patrons and donors; the Virupaksha temple
at Pattadakal is associated with Queen Lokamahadevi and Mallikarjuna with Queen
Trailokyamahadevi. Such evidence proves female agency within particular elite
and institutional settings, not general equality. Normative texts, inscriptions
and material evidence must remain separate.

## 14. Bhakti and religious plurality

- Tamil Shaiva **Nayanars** and Vaishnava **Alvars** composed devotional poetry
  across a chronology reconstructed from poems, later compilations and
  hagiographies. Exact dates and biographical episodes are not equally secure.
- Devotion emphasized an affective relation with a chosen deity and sacred place.
  Tamil composition widened the idiom of religious expression, while Sanskrit
  ritual and temple institutions continued to matter.
- Some poems criticize Jains, Buddhists, ritual pride or social hierarchy.
  It is inaccurate to reduce the whole movement to an anti-caste revolution:
  poets came from varied locations, and later temple orders could reproduce rank.
- Jain and Buddhist institutions remained part of peninsular competition and
  patronage. Hagiographic persecution stories require corroboration; do not turn
  sectarian polemic into a simple narrative of extermination.
- Pallava and Chalukya patronage was plural and could vary by ruler, queen,
  officer and locality. A ruler's devotional title does not prove a uniform
  state religion.

**Temple-economy caution:** temples could receive land, employ specialists,
redistribute offerings, attract markets and anchor settlements. Their economic
scale and control differed; "every temple owned and administered its village" is
not defensible.

## 15. Economy, settlements, crafts and maritime links

| Dimension | Evidence-led position |
|---|---|
| Agriculture | Grain and other regional crops rested on varied wet, dry, pastoral and forest ecologies |
| Irrigation | Tanks, wells and channels joined donor action to community maintenance |
| Crafts | Stone-working, metalwork, textiles, ceramics and building trades are visible through objects, workshops and monuments |
| Towns/markets | Capitals, temple centres, ports and inland nodes performed overlapping political, ritual and exchange functions |
| Merchant bodies | Inscriptions attest organized donors and traders, but later, better-documented guild forms must not be projected wholesale backward |
| Maritime links | Coromandel and Andhra coasts connected Sri Lanka and Southeast Asian routes; contact does not prove political colonization |

Mamallapuram's coastal setting and monument complex support its role in Pallava
maritime imagination and exchange, but surviving monuments are not a freight
ledger. Trade continued alongside rural expansion; neither a total commercial
collapse nor trade-led monocausality fits every subregion.

## 16. Language, script, literature and literacy

| Context | Language/script pattern | Inference with limit |
|---|---|---|
| Early Pallava charters | Prakrit followed by increasing Sanskrit use | Shows changing chancery and prestige practice, not ethnicity |
| Later Pallava records | Sanskrit eulogy often alongside Tamil documentary content | Bilingual division of political work; not universal in every record |
| Badami Chalukya records | Sanskrit court poetry with regional southern scripts; Kannada epigraphic use also grows | Sanskrit cosmopolitanism coexisted with Kannada-region expression |
| Eastern Chalukya realm | Sanskrit prestige and evolving regional documentary practice | Stronger Telugu literary florescence belongs substantially to later centuries |

- Ravikirti composed the Aihole *praśasti* in Sanskrit.
- Mahendravarman I is associated with the Sanskrit farce
  *Mattavilasa-prahasana*; it is a satirical court text, not a census of religions.
- Pallava Grantha forms were important in writing Sanskrit in the south and in
  wider transmission, but script diffusion is not proof of direct political rule.
- Tamil Bhakti poems were transmitted and later compiled; the compilation date
  must not be mistaken for the date of every composition.
- Literacy was socially distributed and institutionally concentrated. An
  inscriptional culture does not imply mass literacy.

## 17. Architecture as historical evidence

### 17.1 Pallava trajectory

| Phase | Examples | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Mahendravarman I rock-cut phase | Mandagapattu, Mamandur and related caves | Attribution depends on inscriptions/style; "first temple" claims need qualification |
| Mamalla phase | Monolithic rathas and great relief at Mamallapuram | Rathas are modern conventional names, not proof of Pandava construction |
| Rajasimha structural phase | Kailasanatha at Kanchi; Shore Temple complex | Royal attribution does not erase architects, artisans and multiple patronage stages |
| Later Pallava | Vaikuntha Perumal, Kanchi | Sculpted succession narrative is political memory, not a neutral illustrated chronicle |

### 17.2 Chalukya experimental landscape

| Site | Historical use |
|---|---|
| Badami | Capital and rock-cut cave complex with multiple religious affiliations |
| Aihole | Diverse plans and superstructures across a long chronology; not every monument is securely dated to one ruler |
| Pattadakal | Ceremonial complex displaying northern and southern formal vocabularies |
| Virupaksha, Pattadakal | Associated with Lokamahadevi and commemoration of Vikramaditya II's Pallava victories |

Avoid treating **Nagara**, **Dravida** and **Vesara** as rigid ethnic labels.
Plan, tower, material, workshop and date must be examined separately. Monument
style indicates interaction and craft movement; it cannot independently map a
dynasty's frontier.

## 18. Transition and significance

By the mid-eighth century, Rashtrakuta ascendancy ended Badami Chalukya rule,
while Pallava power faced Pandya and other pressures. The Eastern Chalukyas
continued in Vengi and later became deeply entangled with Chola politics. This
was transformation and reconfiguration, not the simultaneous disappearance of
all institutions.

The period's durable significance lies in:

1. regionally rooted but outward-looking polities;
2. layered authority mediated by chiefs, grants and local bodies;
3. agrarian expansion linked with unequal social incorporation;
4. negotiated Sanskritic, vernacular and local cultural forms;
5. temples and inscriptions as institutions of political communication;
6. portable architectural and literary repertoires later dynasties reworked.

### 19.2 High-yield statement traps

- Aihole *praśasti* = Chalukya/Pulakeshin II/Ravikirti, not Pallava.
- Mahendravarman I = Mattavilasa, Vichitrachitta and Gunabhara.
- Narasimhavarman I = Mamalla and Vatapikonda; Rajasimha is
  Narasimhavarman II.
- Shore Temple and Kailasanatha belong to the later structural Pallava phase,
  not Mahendravarman's earliest cave phase.
- Virupaksha at Pattadakal is Chalukya and queen-associated; it is not the
  Virupaksha temple of Hampi.
- Pallava Grantha is a script tradition, not a spoken language.
- A land-grant formula is evidence of claimed rights, not automatic proof that
  all central authority vanished.
- Bhakti, Brahmanization and temple expansion interacted; none was a single,
  linear or socially uniform process.
