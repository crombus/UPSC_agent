## Ownership firewall

**Topic 04 owns:** Palaeolithic and Mesolithic chronology, stone-tool systems, hominin evidence relevant to South Asia, hunter-gatherer subsistence and mobility, habitation, burials, rock art as archaeological evidence, regional sites and the transition/coexistence problem.

## 1. Chronology: broad scaffolding, regional sequences

| Phase | R.S. Sharma's textbook band | Diagnostic tendency | Chronological caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower Palaeolithic | c. 600,000–150,000 BCE | Large core/flake tools; Acheulian handaxes and cleavers | Secure site sequences extend much deeper in some regions |
| Middle Palaeolithic | c. 150,000–35,000 BCE | Increased flake tools and prepared-core methods | Attirampakkam places a regional transition much earlier |
| Upper Palaeolithic | c. 35,000–10,000 BCE | Blades, burins, bone tools and more visible symbolic evidence | Regional dates and technological overlaps vary |
| Mesolithic | c. 9000–4000 BCE | Microlithic composite tools, broad-spectrum foraging, art and burials | Microliths can appear earlier and continue into later food-producing phases |

✅ The Palaeolithic lies mainly within the Pleistocene; the Holocene began about 12,000 years ago and frames most conventional Mesolithic contexts.

⚠️ Phase labels organise dominant archaeological tendencies. They are not synchronous all-India boundaries, biological races or stages of intellectual worth.

```text
MACRO-PHASE
    ↓
regional sequence
    ↓
dated stratum
    ↓
assemblage and context

qualified behavioural inference
```

## 2. Geological and palaeoenvironmental setting

- Pleistocene cold/warm oscillations altered rivers, sea level, vegetation and fauna.
- River terraces and gravels can preserve tools but can also redeposit them.
- Raw-stone availability, water and food repeatedly attracted occupation.
- Early Holocene ecological change opened or reorganised wetlands, forests, grasslands and coasts.
- Palaeolake, sediment, faunal, pollen and isotope evidence is regional and must be dated.

⚠️ Climate changes opportunities and costs; it does not dictate one response. Technology, knowledge, mobility and social organisation mediate adaptation.

⚠️ A tool-bearing gravel may be secondary context. The age of the sediment, tool manufacture and later deposition must not be assumed identical.

## 3. Hominin evidence and biological attribution

South Asia has abundant stone tools but a sparse authenticated hominin fossil record. Tool industries should therefore not be assigned automatically to a species.

### Narmada/Hathnora evidence

- ✅ In 1982 Arun Sonakia found a fossil cranial fragment near Hathnora on the northern bank of the Narmada, associated with vertebrate fossils and a few late Acheulian tools.
- ✅ Sonakia proposed an advanced *Homo erectus* attribution; other scholars have classified it as archaic/early *Homo sapiens* or an intermediate form.
- ✅ Later central-Narmada finds have led to suggestions that more than one hominin form may be represented.
- ⚠️ Approximate dates and taxonomic classification remain debated; do not present one species or one exact age as settled.

### Denisovans

- Denisovans are an archaic human population first identified from fragmentary remains and ancient DNA associated with Denisova Cave in Siberia.
- They are not a geological period, dinosaur group or cave system in north-east India.
- No Denisovan fossil from India is established by the repository sources.
- Ancient DNA reveals coexistence and admixture among human populations, but cannot identify the makers of an Indian tool industry without direct contextual evidence.

> 🔑 Recall phrase: **lithic abundance, fossil scarcity**.

## 4. Lithic evidence: technology, not a crude-to-refined ladder

| Term | Meaning / use |
|---|---|
| Core | Stone mass from which flakes are detached |
| Flake | Detached blank that may be used or retouched |
| Debitage | Waste produced during tool manufacture |
| Cortex | Natural outer surface of a stone nodule |
| Retouch | Secondary edge modification |
| Biface | Tool worked on two faces; many handaxes are bifacial |
| Primary context | Find remains close to its activity/deposition setting |
| Secondary context | Water, slope or later activity has displaced the find |

### Reduction sequence

```text
raw-material selection
      ↓
core preparation
      ↓
flake / blade production
      ↓
retouch, backing or hafting
      ↓
use and resharpening
      ↓
discarded tool + debitage + spatial context
```

⚠️ Finished tools alone are insufficient. Cores, rough-outs, hammerstones, waste, wear and residues reconstruct planning, function and activity areas.

## 5. Lower Palaeolithic: core/flake and Acheulian systems

### Diagnostic technology

- handaxes, often bifacial;
- cleavers with broad cutting edges;
- choppers/chopping tools and flakes;
- quarrying, rough-out preparation and repeated resharpening.

**Acheulian** is a technological assemblage label strongly associated with bifacial handaxes and cleavers; it is not an ethnicity or hominin species.

### Regional evidence

| Site/region | Association |
|---|---|
| Soan/Potwar region | Terrace and pebble/core-tool traditions; fluvial context requires caution |
| Didwana, Rajasthan | Lower, Middle and Upper Palaeolithic evidence in an arid/palaeolake setting |
| Narmada valley | Widespread lithics plus rare Hathnora hominin evidence |
| Bhimbetka, Madhya Pradesh | Long occupation, local quartzite, water and shelters |
| Hunsgi–Baichbal/Isampur, Karnataka | Acheulian landscape, quarrying, manufacture and habitation |
| Attirampakkam, Tamil Nadu | Deep Acheulian sequence and major chronology revision |

✅ Isampur preserves limestone slabs, cores, flakes, unfinished tools, debitage and hammerstones, demonstrating manufacture; use-wear and other remains also indicate habitation.

⚠️ The old north-western “Soanian” versus southern “Madrasian” opposition should not be treated as two racial or sealed civilisations. Regional assemblages are more complex.

## 6. Middle Palaeolithic: flake emphasis and prepared cores

The phase generally shows a changed balance toward smaller flakes, scrapers, points and borers, while older bifaces can continue.

### Levallois technique

```text
shape core convexities
    → prepare striking platform
    → predetermine desired flake
    → detach planned blank
```

**Levallois** is a prepared-core reduction strategy, not a finished tool type.

| Site/region | Importance |
|---|---|
| Attirampakkam | Declining bifaces, Levallois flakes/points and an early regional transition |
| Nevasa/Chirki-Nevasa | Stratified flake industry; source of the “Nevasan” label |
| Didwana/Thar | Flake contexts linked to changing palaeoenvironments |
| Son–Belan valleys | Dense sites, chert use and manufacture |
| Retlapalle | Multiple formal core strategies in a dated Middle Palaeolithic context |

✅ Luminescence dating at Attirampakkam places the end of its Acheulian and beginning of Middle Palaeolithic technology at about **385 ± 64 thousand years ago**.

⚠️ This is a site-specific regional sequence with an error range. It does not date all India or identify the tool-maker as modern human.

## 7. Upper Palaeolithic: blades, organic tools and symbolism

| Marker | Historical significance | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel-sided blades | Standardised long cutting edges | Flake/core tools continue |
| Burins | Grooving/engraving edge | Function needs wear/context |
| Bone tools | Organic toolkit diversification | Preservation is selective |
| Ostrich-eggshell beads/engravings | Ornament and symbolic communication | Exact social meaning unknown |
| Ochre, cupules and special objects | Marking, aesthetic or ritual possibilities | Multiple interpretations remain |

### Important examples

- Kurnool caves: Upper Palaeolithic bone tools and faunal evidence.
- Patne and Bhimbetka: ostrich-eggshell beads/ornament evidence.
- Baghor sites in the Son valley: lithic manufacture, possible special/cult context and Palaeolithic–Mesolithic relevance.

⚠️ Symbolic evidence challenges the stereotype of purely utilitarian Stone Age life, but it does not prove a formal religion or named deity.

## 8. Mobility, habitation and social inference

Palaeolithic communities used settlement systems rather than wandering without structure.

| Site type | Likely activity | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Quarry/factory | Stone extraction and early reduction | May also contain habitation |
| Short camp | Brief task or seasonal occupation | Thin deposits preserve little |
| Recurrent habitation | Diverse tools, hearths and repeated floors | Deposits may form a palimpsest |
| Butchery/processing area | Fauna, cut marks and task-specific tools | Association must be secure |
| Rock shelter/cave | Protection and repeated use | Shelters are over-represented by preservation |
| Landscape cluster | Linked resource and activity sites | Route and group identity remain inferential |

### Safe social conclusions

- Planned mobility required knowledge of water, food, stone and season.
- Sharing and kin-based organisation are plausible comparative models, not directly documented institutions for every site.
- Leadership, rank and gender divisions cannot be reconstructed from tool size alone.
- Gathering and plant processing may have been as important as hunting but preserve less visibly.
- Absence of permanent houses does not mean absence of social organisation.

## 9. Subsistence and adaptation

Stone Age foodways could include:

- gathered fruits, tubers, seeds and grasses;
- large and small game;
- fish, turtles and wetland resources;
- shellfish and coastal foods;
- scavenging and opportunistic capture;
- plant processing revealed by grinding stones, residues or starch.

⚠️ “Hunter-gatherer” is not synonymous with meat-dominated subsistence. Organic and plant evidence is preservation-sensitive.

⚠️ A grinding stone proves processing, not cultivation. A projectile point suggests a function only when impact damage, hafting or context supports it.

## 10. Mesolithic: regional Holocene adaptations

### Core features

- broad dependence on hunting, gathering, fishing and plant use;
- microlithic tools, often as composite inserts;
- occupation of wetlands, plains, coasts, forests and rock shelters;
- mobile, semi-sedentary and longer-resident communities;
- more visible cemeteries and rock art in some regions;
- pottery or managed/domesticated animals at some sites, often debated.

**Microliths** are small stone elements, generally under about 5 cm, commonly made on fine-grained stone.

| Type | Examples / role |
|---|---|
| Non-geometric | Blades, points, scrapers and borers |
| Geometric | Lunates/crescents, triangles and trapezes |
| Composite use | Mounted singly or in series in wood/bone for arrows, spears, knives, sickles or harpoons |

⚠️ Small size does not automatically give a Mesolithic date. Microliths occur in some late-Pleistocene contexts and persist into later food-producing cultures.

## 11. Major Mesolithic sites

| Site | Region | Marks-essential association |
|---|---|---|
| Bagor | Rajasthan, Kothari-river zone | Long microlithic sequence, stone floors, subsistence and disputed domestication |
| Langhnaj | North Gujarat | Microliths, burials, wild fauna and some pottery; arid/semi-arid adaptation |
| Adamgarh | Near Narmada/Narmadapuram, Madhya Pradesh | Palaeolithic-to-Mesolithic sequence; domestication claims weakened by uncertain dates/stratigraphy |
| Bhimbetka | Raisen district, Madhya Pradesh | Long occupation, microliths and multi-period rock art |
| Sarai Nahar Rai | Pratapgarh district, Uttar Pradesh | Oxbow-lake setting, geometric microliths, aquatic fauna and burials |
| Mahadaha | Uttar Pradesh, middle-Ganga wetland zone | Habitation/butchery areas, long-distance stone and numerous burials |
| Damdama | Uttar Pradesh, middle-Ganga zone | Thick occupation, hearths, microliths and 41 burials |
| Chopani Mando | Belan valley, Uttar Pradesh | Epi-Palaeolithic/Mesolithic sequence, huts, hearths, grinding and wild-rice evidence |
| Baghor II | Son valley | Mesolithic manufacture, shelters and strong debitage evidence |

### Semi-sedentism and residence

Thick deposits, huts, heavy grinding equipment, commensal animals, seasonality evidence and cemeteries can support repeated or year-round residence. No single marker proves permanence.

✅ Studies of Mahadaha and Damdama challenged an older purely seasonal-migration model and support occupation across different seasons.

## 12. Burials and social interpretation

| Evidence | Possible inference | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Formal cemetery within habitation | Repeated residence, memory and attachment to place | Does not itself prove territorial ownership |
| Body orientation | Shared mortuary convention | Practical/local explanations remain possible |
| Grave goods | Identity, ritual, personal equipment or disposal custom | Do not automatically prove afterlife belief |
| Multiple burial | Shared event or relationship | Kinship needs biological evidence |
| Trauma | Accident or interpersonal violence | One case cannot define the society |
| Unequal goods | Possible social distinction | Does not establish hereditary class hierarchy |

- Sarai Nahar Rai yielded 11 graves, including a multiple burial and a skeleton with a projectile embedded in the ribs.
- Mahadaha yielded burials within the habitation area and evidence for aquatic-resource use.
- Damdama yielded numerous burials, including multiple burials.

⚠️ Burial is an action by survivors, not a transparent biography of the deceased.

## 13. Domestication and food-production claims

- Bagor has reported domestic cattle and sheep/goat, but specialists disagree over identification and later-phase interpretation.
- Adamgarh has reported domestic cattle, sheep, goat, pig and dog, but widely different dates and uncertain stratigraphy weaken the early claim.
- Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama have controversial faunal identifications; a major assessment finds no securely represented domestic animals.
- Pottery can occur among foragers and may arrive through contact.
- Wild-rice and plant-processing evidence does not automatically prove cultivation.

⚠️ Use the formulation **“reported but questioned”** unless species identification, context and date are secure.

> **Boundary:** Established domestication, cultivation and village food production belong to topic 05.

## 14. Rock shelters and art

### Bhimbetka

- Located in Raisen district, Madhya Pradesh.
- Preserves occupation from the Lower Palaeolithic into historical periods.
- Combines shelters, water, local stone, subsistence resources and paintings.
- Shows changing lithic raw materials and Mesolithic microlithic evidence.

### Reading rock art

| Evidence | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Subject/theme | Hunting, animals, gathering, dance and social performance | Depiction is selective, not a census |
| Superimposition | Relative sequence of paintings | Does not alone give calendar dates |
| Style and technique | Regional/relative grouping | Style-date reasoning can become circular |
| Pigment/accretion science | Possible direct or indirect dating | Often dates a related deposit, not the image itself |
| Archaeological context | Links paintings with occupation | Painted shelter may not be a habitation area |

✅ Upinder Singh's account divides Bhimbetka paintings into several sub-phases, including Mesolithic, transitional and historical phases.

- ❌ All Bhimbetka paintings are Mesolithic.
- ✅ The shelters contain a long, layered and multi-period visual record.

> **Boundary:** Topic 04 owns prehistoric context and inferential limits. Indian Art and Culture owns detailed stylistic, aesthetic and heritage analysis.

## 15. Transition and coexistence

```text
Palaeolithic foraging and mobility
          ↓ changing proportions, not total replacement
Mesolithic microliths + wider niches + varied residence
          ↓ interaction and experimentation
plant processing / animal management / pottery at some sites
          ↓ regionally uneven thresholds
Neolithic cultivation, domestication and village systems
```

### Continuities

- hunting, gathering and fishing;
- microliths and other stone technologies;
- seasonal mobility and exchange;
- rock art and ritualised places.

### Changes

- increased microlithic composite equipment;
- wider ecological niches and aquatic/coastal adaptations;
- longer residence and formal cemeteries at some sites;
- more intensive plant processing and debated management/domestication;
- greater interaction with food-producing neighbours.

⚠️ “Transition” does not mean one revolution on one date. Foragers, herders and cultivators could coexist and exchange.

## 16. Archaeological evidence limits

- Stone tools record technological choices, not the language, race or full identity of makers.
- Surface finds establish distribution more securely than chronology or behaviour.
- River gravels can redeposit tools.
- Caves and rock shelters are disproportionately visible.
- Organic materials and plant foods preserve unevenly.
- Site function requires the whole assemblage, not a showcase artefact.
- Scientific dates apply to particular samples and events.
- Faunal domestication requires secure morphology, context and chronology.
- Ethnographic analogy suggests possibilities; it does not prove direct continuity.
- Social organisation and ritual remain graded interpretations.

## 18. Prelims chronology, site and tool traps

- ❌ Lower → Middle → Upper → Mesolithic began everywhere simultaneously.
  - ✅ Use broad bands plus regional dated sequences.

  - ✅ It is a technological assemblage.
- ❌ Levallois is a finished tool.
  - ✅ It is a prepared-core reduction strategy.
- ❌ Microliths always date to the Holocene Mesolithic.
  - ✅ They can begin earlier and continue later.
- ❌ Handaxes are the only Lower Palaeolithic tools.
  - ✅ Flakes, choppers and small tools can coexist.
- ❌ Bhimbetka is only a Mesolithic art site.

## 19. Mains comparison architecture

### Palaeolithic versus Mesolithic

| Axis | Palaeolithic tendency | Mesolithic tendency | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geological frame | Mainly Pleistocene | Mainly early/mid-Holocene | Microliths can pre-date Holocene |
| Technology | Core, flake, blade and organic tools | Greater microlithic composite emphasis | Older forms continue |
| Subsistence | Hunting, gathering and fishing | Broad-spectrum foraging and intensive processing | Farming is not yet uniform |
| Mobility | Mobile with recurrent places | Mobile to semi-sedentary continuum | Region/site-specific |
| Habitation | Camps, factory sites, shelters | Longer deposits, huts and cemeteries at some sites | Preservation bias |
| Symbolism | Ornaments, ochre and possible cult objects | Abundant rock art and formal burials in some regions | Meaning and dating remain indirect |
| Food production | No established general farming system | Disputed management/domestication at some sites | Topic 05 owns secure Neolithic development |
