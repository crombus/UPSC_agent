## 1. Source criticism: from trace to defensible claim

```text
SURVIVING TRACE
      ↓
identity and authenticity
      ↓
provenance / archaeological provenience
      ↓
date and transmission history
      ↓
author, audience, purpose and genre
      ↓
comparison and corroboration
      ↓
silence, uncertainty and alternative reading
      ↓
QUALIFIED HISTORICAL CLAIM
```

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Authenticity | Is the object, inscription or text what it claims to be? |
| Provenance | What is its documented origin, ownership and recovery history? |
| Provenience | Where exactly was an archaeological object found in three-dimensional context? |
| Date | Does the date refer to composition, redaction, manuscript copy, inscription, deposit or scientific sample? |
| Authorship | Who produced or transmitted it, and from what social or institutional position? |
| Audience | Who was expected to read, hear, see or use it? |
| Purpose | Was it an order, donation, praise text, ritual prescription, commercial guide, memory or entertainment? |
| Genre | What conventions control what can responsibly be extracted? |
| Transmission | Was it copied, excerpted, translated, orally performed or preserved through hostile quotation? |
| Interpolation/redaction | Which passages or layers may be later additions? |
| Silence | Is absence caused by non-existence, non-preservation, non-discovery, genre or elite selection? |
| Corroboration | Do independent sources converge at the same chronology, place and scale? |

⚠️ A contemporary source is temporally close, not automatically neutral. A biased source may still be excellent evidence for the interests, ideals or legitimation strategies of its producer.

⚠️ Absence becomes strong negative evidence only when the expected trace was likely to survive, the relevant contexts were adequately searched and the silence is patterned.

## 2. Source universe and question-specific weighting

| Source family | Strongest typical yield | Master limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Archaeological | Settlement, technology, production, foodways and everyday material life | Context, preservation, sampling and anonymous actors |
| Epigraphic | Dated acts, rulers, donations, institutions, language and public claims | Formula, praise, elite survival and movable records |
| Numismatic | Authority, metrology, exchange, iconography and relative chronology | Hoard selection, long circulation and uncertain mint/frontier inference |
| Literary | Ideas, norms, vocabulary, memory, narrative and institutions | Genre, chronology, interpolation, prescription and elite authorship |
| Foreign accounts | External observation, routes, synchronisms and comparison | Itinerary, language, informants, audience and transmission |
| Visual/material | Patronage, labour, technology, ritual, iconography and craft | Phase, convention, selective durability and decontextualisation |
| Linguistic/oral | Contact, borrowing, place-memory and cultural transmission | Language is not race; memory is reshaped in performance |
| Scientific/interdisciplinary | Date ranges, diet, mobility, disease, environment and material composition | Sample integrity, baselines, error ranges and social overreach |

⚠️ There is no fixed hierarchy valid for every problem. The best source is the source fit for the **specific question**, used within its demonstrable limits.

## 3. Archaeological sources

### What archaeology includes

- settlements, houses, streets, drains, fortifications and activity areas;
- tools, pottery, beads, ornaments, weapons and craft debris;
- monuments, caves, temples, stupas and sculpture;
- seals, sealings, weights and inscribed objects;
- human and animal remains, seeds, pollen, charcoal and soils;
- mines, workshops, routes, palaeochannels and landscapes.

### Analytical units

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Artefact | Portable object made or modified by humans |
| Industry | Related artefacts sharing material and technological traits |
| Assemblage | Associated artefacts, ecofacts and structures from a context |
| Archaeological culture | Recurring assemblage pattern across sites and areas |
| Context | Layer, exact location, association and formation history of a find |

❌ An archaeological culture is not automatically a race, language, tribe, dynasty or state.

### Survey and excavation

| Method | Best use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Surface/regional survey | Site distribution, route and landscape pattern | Visibility and modern land use bias |
| Remote sensing/GIS | Palaeochannels, roads, buried features and survey planning | Anomaly is not date or function |
| Vertical excavation | Stratigraphic sequence through time | Small exposed plan |
| Horizontal excavation | Layout and social organisation of one phase | Costly and may require removal of upper layers |
| Marine archaeology | Submerged settlements, anchors and coastal activity | Special recovery and conservation problems |
| Salvage archaeology | Recording threatened sites | Time pressure and incomplete sampling |

✅ In an undisturbed sequence, lower layers are generally earlier; intrusion, pits, erosion and redeposition can reverse a naive reading. Association dates a layer only when depositional security is established.

⚠️ Excavation is destructive. Recording, labelling, conservation, sampling and publication are part of the evidence, not administrative afterthoughts.

### Archaeology's strengths and limits

- strong for long processes, everyday routines and groups under-recorded in texts;
- often anonymous and weak for named events or intentions;
- preservation favours stone, pottery and metal over fibre, wood, food and speech;
- monuments over-represent durable and patronised institutions;
- an unprovenanced masterpiece may retain artistic value while losing much of its historical context.

## 4. Inscriptions and epigraphy

**Epigraphy** studies inscriptions; **palaeography** studies old writing and the development of scripts.

### Script and language

- ✅ Brahmi is normally written left to right and became the ancestor of many later South Asian scripts.
- ✅ Kharoshthi is written right to left and is associated especially with the north-west and Gandhara.
- ✅ Most Ashokan inscriptions use Prakrit in Brahmi; north-western records also use Kharoshthi, Greek and Aramaic settings.
- ✅ James Prinsep's decisive **1837** Brahmi decipherment step emerged from cumulative comparison, not isolated intuition.
- ⚠️ Script is not language: one script can record several languages, and one language can use different scripts.
- ⚠️ Palaeographic dates are approximate and region-sensitive.

### Inscription types

| Type | Main historical use | Built-in caution |
|---|---|---|
| Edict/order | Policy, administration and public communication | Intention does not prove implementation |
| Royal panegyric (*praśasti*) | Genealogy, conquest, ideology and political language | Eulogy, omission and conventional exaggeration |
| Donative/votive record | Donor, occupation, institution, gift and patronage | Formulaic survival and donor bias |
| Land/copper-plate grant | Fiscal rights, land, beneficiary and agrarian relations | Legal act does not prove uniform enforcement |
| Memorial/hero stone | Conflict, death, value and local identity | Commemorative selection |
| Label, seal or graffiti | Names, ownership, movement and literacy | Short text and uncertain representativeness |

### Dating and bias

- Explicit eras and regnal years can create narrow chronological anchors.
- Era conversion requires the era's epoch, calendar/month convention and whether the year is current or expired.
- Useful broad anchors include the Vikrama era beginning in **58/57 BCE**, the Shaka era in **78 CE** and the Gupta era in **319–320 CE**.
- Undated inscriptions may be placed relatively through script, language, formula, ruler and archaeological context.
- Royal records are strong for official self-presentation and weak as representative statements of popular opinion.
- The discovery area of inscriptions is evidence of communication and presence, not an exact imperial frontier.

## 5. Coins and numismatics

**Numismatics** studies coins as metal objects, standards, texts, images, archaeological finds and instruments of exchange.

| Feature | Historical question |
|---|---|
| Metal/alloy | Resource supply, mint practice and possible debasement |
| Weight/metrology | Standard, denomination, wear and issue relation |
| Manufacture | Cast, punch-marked, die-struck or counter-struck technique |
| Legend/script | Issuer, title, language and political message |
| Iconography | Kingship, cult, legitimacy and artistic convention |
| Die study | Sequence and approximate production relationships |
| Findspot | Local use, movement and archaeological dating |
| Hoard | Selected accumulation, burial date and circulation history |
| Distribution | Exchange and contact, not automatically sovereignty |

### What coins can establish

- names and titles of rulers otherwise poorly known;
- relative dynastic sequences through types, dies and overstrikes;
- weight standards, denominations and minting technology;
- political and religious representation;
- circulation, exchange and contact when find context is secure;
- chronological anchors for sealed archaeological layers.

### Limits

- precious coins can travel beyond the issuing polity and circulate after a dynasty ends;
- hoards are selected deposits, not random samples of all money in use;
- coin absence does **not** prove absence of trade: barter, cowries, bullion, perishable records and unexcavated contexts may matter;
- debasement may reflect metal scarcity, increased demand for currency or fiscal policy, not only collapse;
- a deity on a coin may express political legitimacy rather than exclusive personal faith;
- isolated images without metrology, legend and context are weak evidence.

## 6. Literary sources: classification before extraction

### Textual chronology

```text
composition
   → oral/performed transmission
   → additions and redaction
   → regional recension
   → surviving manuscript copy
   → critical edition / translation
   → historical interpretation
```

The date of a manuscript does not automatically date the composition, the event narrated or every layer of the text.

| Corpus/genre | Main historical yield | Required caution |
|---|---|---|
| Vedic corpus | Ritual, kinship, economy, vocabulary, political forms and ideas | Long oral layers, ritual purpose and uneven regional voice |
| Epics | Social memory, values, geography, conflict and political ideals | Expansion, recensions, interpolation and named-event historicity |
| Ancient lore (*purāṇa*) | Genealogy, dynastic memory, sacred geography and sectarian history | Mythic framework, variant lists and late layers |
| Buddhist texts | Monastic institutions, merchants, towns, ethics and alternative social viewpoints | Sectarian purpose, canon formation and redaction |
| Jaina texts | Teachers, monastic life, traders, doctrines and regional political detail | Sectarian chronology, compilation and divergent lists |
| Sangam/early Tamil literature | Chiefs, war, love, occupations, exchange and Tamilakam | Bardic convention, anthology history and dating |
| Grammar and linguistic works | Language, vocabulary, places and incidental social/economic information | Technical purpose and debated textual chronology |
| Law/statecraft | Norms, family, property, administration and political theory | Prescriptive or ideal model, not transparent practice |
| Scientific/technical literature | Medicine, astronomy, mathematics, craft and knowledge systems | Textual layers and technical rather than representative social purpose |
| Court literature, biography and drama | Courts, political memory, patronage, literary culture and ideals | Aesthetic convention, praise and selective narration |

### Manuscripts, critical editions and oral transmission

- Manuscripts may survive on palm leaf, birch bark or other media long after composition.
- Recopying preserves texts while creating variants, omissions and interpolations.
- A critical edition compares manuscript witnesses, proposes a reasoned text and records variants in a critical apparatus.
- Digitisation preserves images and access; it does not authenticate, date or critically edit a text.
- Oral traditions and genealogies preserve memory through performance, but present concerns and repeated retelling reshape them.
- Myth, praise and drama are historically useful for value, legitimation and imagination even when they are not literal event reports.

## 7. Foreign accounts within the Ancient and early-medieval scope

| Witness group | Main contribution | Filter |
|---|---|---|
| Greek/Macedonian accounts | Alexander's campaign, north-western society and chronological synchronism | Military itinerary and classical ethnography |
| Megasthenes fragments | Mauryan court, society and administration | Lost original preserved through later quotation |
| Greek/Roman commercial-geographical writing | Ports, routes, commodities and exchange | Merchant/classical categories and selective coastwise knowledge |
| Chinese Buddhist pilgrims | Routes, monasteries, learning, regional conditions and Buddhist institutions | Pilgrimage purpose and monastic emphasis |
| Arab/Persian accounts relevant to the early-medieval transition | Trade, geography, learning, religion and comparison | Language, informants, hearsay and regional access |

### Criticism checklist

1. route and duration;
2. purpose and patron;
3. language competence and interpreters;
4. direct observation versus hearsay;
5. intended audience;
6. survival through quotation or translation;
7. corroboration with Indian texts, inscriptions, coins and archaeology.

✅ Greek testimony supplies the synchronism between Sandrokottos and Chandragupta Maurya. Chinese pilgrims such as Faxian and Xuanzang are especially valuable for Buddhist geography and institutions. Commercial works such as the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* illuminate ports and exchange.

⚠️ Outsider distance changes bias; it does not remove bias. No traveller observed all India or all sections of society.

> **Boundary:** Later medieval travel literature belongs to Medieval Indian History. Early-medieval Arab/Persian material appears here only to satisfy the routed 2018 source-evaluation demand and to close the ancient-to-early-medieval evidence sequence.

## 8. Visual, architectural, material and oral evidence

| Source | What it can reveal | What must be controlled |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Patronage, technology, labour, ritual space and institutional organisation | Construction phase, repair, reuse, inscription and landscape |
| Sculpture/image | Iconography, workshop, patronage, dress and political/religious convention | Findspot, idealisation, style and audience |
| Painting | Narrative, performance, technique and visual culture | Layer, pigment, restoration and site function |
| Pottery | Sequence, technology, food practice, exchange and assemblage | Context; pottery type is not a people |
| Seal/sealing | Administration, identity marks, movement and exchange | Undeciphered signs, reuse and find context |
| Manuscript | Textual witness, scribal practice and knowledge transmission | Copy date, recension, material survival and provenance |
| Oral/genealogical tradition | Memory, lineage, identity and place association | Performance, political purpose and transformation |

⚠️ Style is normally a relative dating aid, not exact calendar proof. An image is a composed representation, not a photograph or statistical survey of society.

## 9. Scientific and interdisciplinary methods

| Method | Capability | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Radiocarbon (C-14) | Probability range for the death of organic material | Contamination, “old wood”, calibration and sample-event association |
| Thermoluminescence (TL) | Time since suitable heated minerals were last fired/heated | Sampling and environmental/laboratory assumptions |
| Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) | Time since mineral grains were last exposed to sufficient light | Incomplete bleaching and depositional disturbance |
| Archaeobotany | Crops, seeds, charcoal, fuel and plant use | Recovery method, preservation and sampling |
| Archaeozoology/faunal analysis | Hunting, domestication, diet, draught use and seasonality | Species identification, deposition and representativeness |
| Palaeoenvironmental study | Past landscape, climate, vegetation and water regimes | Correlation does not itself prove social causation |
| Stable-isotope analysis | Diet, mobility and environmental exposure | Local baselines, sample size and individual life history |
| Ancient DNA/genomics | Biological ancestry, relatedness, admixture and population history | Preservation, contamination, sample bias; cannot directly identify language, caste, religion or polity |
| Archaeometry/material science | Composition, provenance hypotheses and production technology | Source overlap and uncertain social meaning |
| Remote sensing/GIS | Non-invasive landscape pattern and survey targets | Ground verification and dating remain necessary |

⚠️ Scientific dates are probability ranges tied to samples. They are not exact calendar certainty and do not automatically date the whole site, text, people or event.

⚠️ Environmental or biological evidence does not mechanically determine society. Technology, institutions, power, culture and human choice mediate outcomes.

## 10. Chronology, decipherment and triangulation

### Chronological methods

| Method | Output |
|---|---|
| Stratigraphy and typology | Relative earlier/later sequence |
| Palaeography | Approximate script-based range |
| Scientific dating | Probability/range for a suitable sample |
| Era or regnal date | Calendar anchor after correct conversion |
| Synchronism | Link between independent chronological systems |
| Textual criticism | Relative composition, redaction and manuscript layers |

### Triangulation protocol

1. Match chronology.
2. Match geography.
3. Match scale: household, settlement, region or polity.
4. Test independence: copied reports do not count as independent witnesses.
5. Compare what each source was designed to record.
6. Preserve productive contradiction.
7. State what remains unknown.

```text
TEXTUAL CLAIM
      + INSCRIPTION / COIN
      + ARCHAEOLOGICAL CONTEXT
      + SCIENTIFIC RANGE
      ↓
CONVERGENCE, CONFLICT OR RESIDUAL SILENCE
      ↓
BOUNDED HISTORICAL INFERENCE
```

- ❌ “Archaeology proves the text” is usually unsafe.
- ✅ State that evidence is compatible, convergent or mutually constraining, and specify the exact claim supported.

## 12. Prelims traps

- ❌ Texts are transparent chronicles.
  - ✅ Identify composition layer, genre, author/audience, prescription and transmission.
- ❌ Inscriptions are unbiased because they are contemporary.
  - ✅ Contemporaneity does not erase formula, praise or official purpose.
- ❌ Coin absence proves absence of trade.
  - ✅ Other exchange media, recovery bias and limited excavation must be tested.
- ❌ Coin distribution maps an empire.
  - ✅ Trade, precious-metal value and long circulation cross political boundaries.
- ❌ An archaeological culture identifies a language or people.
  - ✅ Material pattern requires independent social identification.
- ❌ A radiocarbon result is an exact calendar year.
  - ✅ It is a calibrated probability range for a sample.
- ❌ aDNA identifies caste, language or religion.
  - ✅ It addresses biological history for sampled individuals/populations.
- ❌ Manuscript date equals composition date.
  - ✅ Composition, redaction, recension and copy are different chronological objects.
- ❌ A traveller is objective because they are foreign.
  - ✅ Route, purpose, language, informants and audience shape the account.
- ❌ More sources automatically mean certainty.
  - ✅ Independent, context-matched convergence matters.

## 13. Mains source-evaluation architecture

### 10 marks / 150 words

```text
define the source class and answer the directive
  → 2–3 named examples
  → what each example establishes
  → one limitation per evidence cluster
  → graded verdict
```

### 15 marks / 250 words

```text
thesis on relative value
  → classification by source type or analytical dimension
  → 4–6 developed examples
  → source criticism and counter-evidence
  → corroboration matrix
  → qualified conclusion
```

### 20 marks / 300–350 words

```text
define multi-source reconstruction
  → explain source criticism
  → archaeological + epigraphic + numismatic + literary + foreign evidence
  → chronology and scientific methods
  → bias, silence and cross-owner limits
  → 5–8 evidence-led examples
  → residual uncertainty and criterion-based verdict
```

### Paragraph formula

> **Claim → named source/site/object → what it establishes → limitation → corroborating source → judgement**

### Directive control

| Directive | Required move |
|---|---|
| Describe | Organise characteristics with accurate examples |
| Discuss | Develop dimensions, evidence and qualification |
| Assess | Weigh relative importance and end with a graded verdict |
| Critically examine | Test proposition with supporting and counter-evidence |
| Compare | Use parallel criteria; do not write two disconnected lists |

## 14. Rapid revision

- Source criticism: authenticity → provenance/provenience → date → author/audience/purpose/genre → transmission → corroboration → silence.
- Archaeology: context before object; vertical sequence and horizontal plan answer different questions.
- Inscription: material + script + language + formula + date + purpose + audience.
- Coin: metal + weight + manufacture + legend + image + die + findspot + circulation.
- Text: composition, oral transmission, redaction, recension, manuscript and edition are distinct.
- Traveller: route, purpose, language, informants, audience and transmission.
- Visual source: material, phase, patronage, iconography and landscape.
- C-14/TL/OSL yield ranges tied to suitable samples, not exact universal dates.
- Archaeobotany, archaeozoology, palaeoenvironment, isotope and aDNA expand questions but retain sampling and interpretation limits.
- Era conversion requires epoch and calendar convention.
- Triangulation matches chronology, geography, scale and independent origin.
- Topic 01 owns historiographical schools; topic 03 owns geography/ecology as substantive causes.
