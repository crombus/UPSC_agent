**Core proposition:** Trade policy should lower economy-wide costs and build export
capability while using time-bound protection only where learning and strategic spillovers
are demonstrable.

## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Tariff** | Tax imposed on imported goods. |
| ✅ **Non-tariff measure** | Regulatory measure affecting trade, including standards, quotas or procedures. |
| ✅ **FTA** | Agreement reducing trade barriers among members under agreed coverage and rules. |
| ✅ **Rules of origin** | Criteria determining where a product originates for preferential treatment. |
| ✅ **Protectionism** | Use of barriers or preferences to shelter domestic producers from external competition. |
| ✅ **TRIPS** | WTO agreement setting minimum standards for intellectual-property protection; it does not itself grant a patent. |

## 3. Topic mechanism

1. Tariffs, standards and trade agreements change landed prices and market access.
2. Firms respond by altering suppliers, scale, technology, product quality and investment
   location.
3. Rules of origin determine whether a product qualifies for an FTA preference.
4. Trade exposure redistributes gains and losses among consumers, exporters, import-
   competing firms and workers.
5. Adjustment support, logistics and domestic capability decide whether openness produces
   upgrading or persistent vulnerability.

## 4. Institutions and policy tools

- ✅ **WTO:** provides multilateral trade rules, negotiation forums and dispute-settlement
  architecture.
- ✅ **Department of Commerce:** negotiates trade agreements and administers trade-policy
  instruments.
- ✅ **DGTR:** investigates specified trade-remedy cases under applicable law.
- ✅ **Customs and standards or testing bodies:** implement border treatment, origin
  verification and technical compliance.

## 5. Indian applications and examples

- ✅ **Claim:** The WTO's binding dispute-settlement system has been structurally impaired
  since 2019, changing how trade disputes are actually resolved. **Evidence:** The WTO
  Appellate Body has been unable to hear new appeals since December 2019 because the United
  States blocked the appointment of new members, leaving it without the quorum needed to
  function ("Appellate Body paralysis"). **Significance:** This is essential context for
  any answer on multilateral trade governance's credibility and on why some disputes are
  now effectively unappealable in binding form. **Limitation:** Paralysis of the Appellate
  Body does not stop WTO panels from issuing first-level rulings; it only removes the
  binding appeal stage, so dispute settlement continues in a weakened, not fully absent,
  form.
- ✅ **Claim:** A subset of WTO members created a workaround arbitration mechanism, and
  India has chosen to remain outside it. **Evidence:** The Multi-Party Interim Appeal
  Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA), established in 2020 among a subset of WTO members (the EU
  and several other economies) as an alternative appeal mechanism, functions only among its
  participating members; India has not joined the MPIA. **Significance:** This is a
  precise, examinable India-specific fact distinguishing "WTO reform advocacy" from
  "participation in an interim workaround." **Limitation/status caution:** Membership and
  participation lists evolve; verify current MPIA membership and India's stated reasons from
  a dated official/authoritative source before citing specifics beyond non-membership.
- ✅ **Claim:** India replaced its trade-policy framework to shift emphasis from incentive-
  heavy export promotion to procedural ease and remission-based support. **Evidence:** The
  Foreign Trade Policy (FTP) 2023, effective 1 April 2023, replaced the FTP 2015-20 and
  emphasises process re-engineering, digitalisation (e.g., paperless approvals), and
  remission-based schemes (aligned with WTO-compatible export-support design) over
  direct incentive schemes. **Significance:** It shows trade policy responding to WTO
  disciplines (e.g., past disputes on export subsidy schemes) rather than operating in
  isolation from multilateral rules. **Limitation:** A stated policy shift towards
  remission-based, WTO-compatible support does not by itself resolve exporters' logistics,
  credit or compliance-cost constraints.
- ✅ **Claim:** Trade-remedy investigation is institutionally separated from ordinary customs
  administration to ensure a rules-based, evidence-driven process. **Evidence:** The
  Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) investigates anti-dumping, countervailing-
  duty and safeguard cases under the applicable trade-remedy law and WTO-consistent
  procedures. **Significance:** This operationalises the Section 6 distinction between
  anti-dumping, countervailing duty and safeguard action with a named investigating
  authority. **Limitation:** DGTR recommendations require final government notification to
  take effect, and remedies are time-bound and subject to review, not a permanent tariff.
- ✅ **Claim:** India has been willing to withdraw from a major regional trade negotiation
  when domestic sensitivities were not adequately addressed. **Evidence:** India withdrew
  from the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) negotiations in November
  2019, citing concerns over the trade deficit with China, market access for goods and
  dairy, and safeguard mechanisms. **Significance:** It shows that FTA/regional-agreement
  participation is conditional and contested domestically, not an automatic policy
  trajectory. **Limitation:** The RCEP withdrawal is a single case; it should not be
  generalised to predict India's approach to every subsequent negotiation (India has since
  concluded other FTAs).
- ✅ **Claim:** India has concluded several bilateral/plurilateral FTAs after RCEP,
  reflecting a selective rather than blanket approach to trade liberalisation. **Evidence:**
  India signed the India-UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA, in force
  May 2022), the India-Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA, in force
  December 2022), and the India-EFTA (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland) Trade and
  Economic Partnership Agreement (TEPA, entered into force 1 October 2025).
  **Significance:** These named, dated agreements evidence a diversification strategy
  (Gulf, Indo-Pacific, European) consistent with the topic's "diversified partnerships"
  Survey anchor. **Limitation:** Each agreement's tariff-line coverage, exclusions and
  rules-of-origin conditions differ; utilisation rates by Indian exporters vary and should
  not be assumed uniformly high across all agreements.
- ✅ **Claim:** India's most significant trade negotiations concluded by mid-2026 change the
  answer to "does India rely mainly on multilateralism or bilateralism." **Evidence:** The
  India-UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), signed 24 July 2025, entered
  into force on 15 July 2026 after a delay linked to UK steel-safeguard concerns; separately,
  India-EU FTA negotiations were announced as concluded at the technical level at the
  India-EU Summit (January 2026), with the treaty text undergoing legal scrubbing and
  signature targeted before end-2026, while an associated Investment Protection Agreement
  and Geographical Indications agreement remain under separate negotiation. **Significance:**
  These are the most current, examinable status facts for "India's trade strategy amid
  protectionism," directly supporting the 2025 GS-III PYQ theme. **Limitation/status
  caution:** As of August 2026, the India-EU FTA is concluded technically but not yet
  signed/ratified; treat its legal entry-into-force date, and any post-August-2026 UK-CETA
  implementation development, as requiring a dated official source rather than assumed
  finality.
- ✅ **Claim:** WTO agriculture negotiations retain an unresolved, examinable tension around
  India's food-security stockholding. **Evidence:** Under the WTO Agreement on Agriculture,
  India (with the G-33 group) has sought a permanent solution for public stockholding for
  food-security purposes, given that MSP-based procurement risks breaching the 10% de
  minimis Aggregate Measurement of Support ceiling calculated at external reference prices
  fixed to a 1986-88 base; a "Peace Clause" interim protection (from the 2013 Bali
  Ministerial) shields such programmes from challenge pending a permanent solution.
  **Significance:** This is a recurring, high-yield Mains link between trade rules and
  domestic food-security policy (cross-reference Topic 12/28). **Limitation/status
  caution:** A permanent solution has not been concluded as of the latest confirmed
  ministerial outcomes; state its status as "unresolved, interim Peace-Clause protected"
  rather than claiming final resolution.

- ✅ **Claim:** A new EU climate-trade instrument now directly affects India's carbon-intensive exports and has entered a legally binding phase. **Evidence:** The **EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)** requires importers of **cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity** into the EU to report/pay for the embedded (upstream) greenhouse-gas emissions of those goods; it moved from a reporting-only **transitional phase (October 2023-December 2025)** to a **definitive, financially enforceable regime from 1 January 2026**, under which importers must be authorised and must purchase and surrender CBAM certificates priced against the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) allowance price for the embedded emissions of covered imports (Iron & steel and aluminium are the sectors of greatest direct relevance to Indian exporters; cement and fertiliser exposure is smaller). **Significance:** CBAM converts a domestic EU climate-price signal into a border-adjustable cost applied to Indian exporters, making it simultaneously a trade-policy and climate-policy question — directly extending the Section-5 "protectionism/bilateralism" theme to a climate-linked, rules-based (not tariff-line) barrier. **Limitation/status caution (dated August 2026):** The definitive regime began only in January 2026; the European Commission's 2025 "Omnibus" simplification (Regulation (EU) 2025/2083) added a **50-tonne/year de-minimis import exemption**, eased quarterly certificate-holding requirements to 50% of verified emissions, and moved the annual declaration/certificate-surrender deadline to 30 September of the following year — treat these procedural parameters as subject to further EU revision and verify from the European Commission's CBAM pages before citing specifics beyond the sector list and 1 January 2026 start date.
- ✅ **Claim:** India regards CBAM as inconsistent with WTO non-discrimination rules and with the equity principles of the global climate regime, not merely as a compliance cost. **Evidence:** India's Ministry of Commerce and Industry has characterised CBAM as a **discriminatory, unilateral trade barrier** and raised it at the WTO (including a 2024 request for consultations with the EU) as a potential breach of GATT 1994 most-favoured-nation and national-treatment obligations; India separately argues CBAM undercuts the **Common but Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC)** principle under the UNFCCC/Paris Agreement, since it applies a uniform EU carbon-price-linked charge to developing-country exporters without regard to differentiated historical responsibility or development stage. **Significance:** This gives an answer a two-track objection structure — a **trade-law/WTO-consistency** track and a **climate-equity/CBDR** track — rather than treating CBAM as only an environmental measure. **Limitation/status caution:** India's WTO consultations and advocacy are a live, evolving dispute/negotiation process (including CBAM-related provisions reportedly discussed within India-EU FTA talks); state its status as contested/under negotiation rather than as a resolved WTO finding.
- ✅ **Claim:** Two frequently paired statements — whether India imports apples from the USA, and whether Indian law permits GM-food imports without approval — are analytically independent facts, and an objective item testing both must not have one inferred from the other. **Evidence:** India has been a regular importer of apples from the United States as one of several source countries for its apple imports (subject to periodic customs-duty changes, including the removal of retaliatory import duties on select US goods in 2023); separately, India's biosafety and food-safety framework — administered jointly through the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, and the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 — prohibits the import of genetically modified (GM) food without prior approval from the competent authority, and no blanket approval for GM food imports for human consumption currently exists. **Significance:** This equips a "verify the following statements" objective item testing (a) the India-US apple-trade fact and (b) the GM-food-import-law fact as two independently verifiable statements, without one explaining or being derived from the other. **Limitation/status caution:** Apple-import volumes, tariff treatment and any specific product's GM-approval status change over time; state only the qualitative trade/regulatory facts given here, and verify a current tariff rate or a specific product's approval status from a dated DGFT/FSSAI/GEAC source before citing a number.

## 5A. TRIMS, International Grains Council and edible-oil import-duty distinctions

- ✅ **TRIMS (Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures):** a goods-related WTO
  agreement, explicitly linked to GATT rather than a standalone investment-protection
  treaty, that prohibits investment measures inconsistent with GATT Article III (national
  treatment) and Article XI (elimination of quantitative restrictions). Its Annex gives an
  illustrative list of prohibited measures, principally **local-content requirements**
  (mandating a minimum share of domestically sourced inputs) and **trade-balancing
  requirements** (tying permitted import volume/value to export volume/value).
  **Statement-elimination-ready facts:**
  - TRIMS covers trade in goods and is GATT-linked — it is not a services agreement (that is
    GATS) and not an intellectual-property agreement (that is TRIPS).
  - TRIMS does not prohibit FDI or investment screening as such; it prohibits the specific
    listed investment measures that distort trade in goods, not foreign investment itself.
  - The Annex list is illustrative, not a closed literal list; an unlisted but functionally
    similar local-content-type condition is still assessed against the underlying Article
    III/XI principle.
  **Limitation/status caution:** treat any current India-specific TRIMS compliance or
  dispute status as requiring a dated WTO/DGFT source; this file states only the qualitative
  prohibition architecture.
- ✅ **International Grains Council (IGC):** an intergovernmental organisation — not a United
  Nations agency and not a food-relief body — that administers the Grains Trade Convention
  (GTC), the principal international treaty covering trade in grains, rice and oilseeds, to
  promote market transparency, information-sharing and cooperation among grain-trading
  countries. India is a member under the GTC, with the Department of Food & Public
  Distribution as the nodal agency. **Statement-elimination-ready facts:**
  - The IGC is not a UN food-relief or food-aid agency; it is an independent
    intergovernmental grain-trade body headquartered in London.
  - The IGC's administrative link to the separate Food Assistance Convention (a distinct
    legal instrument for food-aid coordination) does not make the IGC itself a relief
    agency, and India's GTC membership does not by itself make India a party to the Food
    Assistance Convention.
  - The IGC's core function is market transparency, statistics and grain-trade cooperation,
    not price-setting, procurement or buffer-stock management (those remain India's own
    FCI/state functions — see Topic 12).
  **Limitation/status caution:** this file does not record or infer the answer key for the
  2024 Prelims "India and the International Grains Council" item; only the qualitative facts
  above are stated for statement-level elimination.
- ✅ **Edible-oil imports and customs-duty policy distinction:** India meets a majority of
  its edible-oil consumption through imports (chiefly palm oil, with soybean and sunflower
  oil), reflecting rainfed, comparatively low-productivity domestic oilseed cultivation
  rather than an absence of oilseed farming. Government has used the basic customs duty on
  crude and refined edible oils as an actively and repeatedly revised lever pulling in two
  directions: raising duty (as in September 2024) to protect domestic oilseed farmers' farm-
  gate prices when they fall below MSP, and cutting duty (as in mid-2025) to ease retail
  edible-oil inflation for consumers, while maintaining a crude-versus-refined duty
  differential intended to favour domestic refining capacity. **Significance:** this is the
  precise "imported edible oils versus domestic production and customs duty" distinction the
  routed 2018 Prelims item tests — import dependence and duty direction are a policy choice
  in tension (farmer-price support versus consumer-inflation control), not a single static
  trade fact. **Limitation/status caution:** specific duty percentages and the import-
  dependence share change with each notification and crop year; cite a current rate or
  dependence percentage only from a dated PIB/CBIC/DGFT/Department of Food & Public
  Distribution source, never from a remembered figure.

## Core limitations and trade-offs

- ⚠️ CBAM's actual cost impact on a given Indian exporter depends on the **embedded-emissions intensity of the specific product**, the **prevailing EU ETS allowance price** (which fluctuates) and the evolving CBAM implementing rules (default values, de-minimis thresholds, certificate-surrender timing) — so it cannot be quoted as a fixed ad valorem tariff-equivalent rate.

- ⚠️ Appellate Body paralysis and India's non-participation in the MPIA together mean some
  trade disputes involving India may lack a binding appellate remedy, weakening enforcement
  predictability even as first-level panel rulings continue.
- ⚠️ FTP 2023's shift to remission-based, WTO-compatible export support may be more
  legally durable but can offer a less immediately visible incentive than the direct
  incentive schemes it replaced, affecting exporter cash-flow timing.
- ⚠️ A rising number of bilateral/plurilateral FTAs (UAE, Australia, EFTA, UK, prospective
  EU) increases market access but also multiplies differing rules-of-origin and compliance
  regimes, raising administrative complexity for exporters ("noodle-bowl" effect).
- ⚠️ Selective bilateralism after the RCEP withdrawal reduces exposure to the specific
  concessions India resisted but foregoes deeper regional value-chain integration available
  through RCEP-style multilateral coverage.
- ⚠️ The unresolved public-stockholding/Peace-Clause status leaves India's MSP-based
  procurement exposed to future WTO challenge risk if a permanent solution is not reached,
  a standing constraint on food-security policy space.
- ⚠️ Diversification of trade partnerships does not by itself fix domestic constraints
  (logistics cost, standards capacity, input-tariff cascading) that determine whether FTA
  market access is actually utilised by Indian exporters.

## 6. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ WTO principles include non-discrimination through most-favoured-nation and national-
  treatment obligations, subject to exceptions.
- ✅ FTAs create preferences among members and require rules of origin to prevent simple
  trans-shipment.
- ✅ Tariffs protect producers but raise input or consumer costs when imports are used
  domestically.
- ✅ Anti-dumping action addresses specified unfair pricing injury; it is not a general
  safeguard against all imports.
- ✅ Countervailing duty addresses subsidised imports causing injury; safeguards address
  serious injury from import surge without requiring dumping.
- ✅ Services trade can be supplied across borders, through consumption abroad, commercial
  presence or movement of persons.
- ✅ Trade balance concerns goods; the current account is broader.
- ✅ **EU CBAM** covers six sectors — **cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen, electricity** — and entered its **definitive (financially enforceable) regime from 1 January 2026** after a 2023-2025 reporting-only transitional phase; iron/steel and aluminium are the sectors of greatest direct relevance to India's EU-bound exports.
- ✅ India has been a regular importer of apples from the USA (an independent trade fact); separately, Indian law (via GEAC and FSSAI) prohibits importing GM food without competent-authority approval, and no blanket approval currently exists — the two facts are independent and neither should be inferred from the other.
- ✅ TRIMS is a goods/GATT-linked WTO agreement prohibiting specified investment measures (chiefly local-content and trade-balancing requirements); it does not cover services or IP and does not ban FDI or investment screening as such.
- ✅ The International Grains Council administers the Grains Trade Convention as an intergovernmental grain-trade cooperation body; it is not a UN agency and not a food-relief organisation, and India is a GTC member.
- ✅ India imports the majority of its edible-oil requirement (chiefly palm, soybean and sunflower oil); the basic customs duty on crude/refined edible oils has been used in both directions — raised to protect oilseed-farmer prices and cut to ease consumer inflation — so import dependence and duty direction are a live policy trade-off, not a fixed fact.

## 7. UPSC traps

- ❌ FTA means zero duty on every product immediately. -> Coverage, schedules, exclusions and
  phase-downs vary.
- ❌ MFN means the most favourable tariff for one chosen ally. -> It generally extends equal
  treatment across WTO members.
- ❌ Protection always creates self-reliance. -> It can preserve inefficient dependence on
  protected inputs or technology.
- ❌ Rules of origin are tariffs. -> They are eligibility criteria for preferences.
- ❌ A merchandise deficit equals a BoP deficit. -> Services, income, transfers and financial
  flows also matter.
- ❌ CBAM is a normal tariff that can be quoted as a fixed percentage. -> It is an emissions-linked certificate obligation tied to the fluctuating EU ETS price and each product's embedded emissions, not a flat customs duty.
- ❌ CBAM is India's carbon market or an Indian climate-policy instrument. -> It is an EU border-trade instrument aimed at the EU's own carbon-leakage risk; India's response is domestic MRV/CCTS reform (see Topic 25), not a CBAM equivalent.
- ❌ India does not import apples from the USA, or GM-food import approval is unrelated to that trade fact. -> India does import US apples (a trade fact); the GM-food-import-approval requirement (GEAC/FSSAI) is a separate regulatory fact that neither proves nor explains the trade fact.
- ❌ TRIMS bans foreign investment or requires investment approval. -> It prohibits specific trade-distorting investment measures (local-content, trade-balancing) under GATT Articles III/XI; it does not ban FDI or investment screening generally.
- ❌ The International Grains Council is a UN food-relief/food-aid agency. -> It is an independent intergovernmental body administering the Grains Trade Convention for grain-trade cooperation and transparency, not food relief.
- ❌ India's edible-oil import duty only ever rises to protect farmers, or India has no significant domestic oilseed production. -> Duty has been raised (farmer-price support) and cut (consumer-inflation control) within the same policy cycle, and India does cultivate oilseeds domestically even though import dependence remains high.

## 11A. Answer architecture (10/15/20-mark support)

**Directive decoder**
- "Discuss challenges of protectionism/bilateralism for India" -> requires naming the
  multilateral weakness (Appellate Body paralysis, MPIA non-participation) plus India's
  bilateral response (UAE/Australia/EFTA/UK/EU), not a generic "trade is important" answer.
- "Evaluate India's FTA strategy" -> requires distinct, dated agreements with their
  coverage/limitation, not a list of names without status.
- "Examine WTO agriculture/public-stockholding issue" -> requires the AMS/de-minimis/Peace-
  Clause mechanism precisely, plus its unresolved status — never claim a permanent solution
  exists.
- "Discuss/Examine CBAM's implications for India" -> requires the mechanism (embedded-emissions certificates, EU ETS-linked price), the definitive-phase status (from 1 January 2026), the covered sectors most relevant to India (iron/steel, aluminium), India's CBDR-equity and WTO-consistency objections, and the limitation that cost exposure varies with embedded emissions/EU price/evolving rules — never state a fixed CBAM "tariff rate."

**Evidence chain** (claim -> named evidence -> significance -> limitation)
Use the Section 5 bank: multilateral-governance questions draw on the Appellate-
Body/MPIA/DGTR units; bilateral-strategy questions draw on the UAE/Australia/EFTA/UK/EU
units; agriculture questions draw on the public-stockholding/Peace-Clause unit; climate-linked
trade-barrier questions draw on the CBAM unit (definitive regime -> covered sectors -> CBDR/WTO
objection -> embedded-emissions/EU-price limitation).

**Counter-evidence and balance**
Pair every "India signed X agreement" claim with its Core-limitation caution (rules-of-
origin complexity, utilisation-rate uncertainty, unresolved multilateral remedy) so the
answer reflects genuine balance rather than a scorecard of agreements.

**10/15/20-mark scaling**
- 10 marks (~150 words): thesis + 2-3 evidence units (e.g., Appellate Body paralysis + one
  named FTA) + one limitation + verdict.
- 15 marks (~250 words): thesis + rule-versus-relationship structure (multilateral rules ->
  bilateral/regional agreements -> domestic capability) + 4-5 evidence units + counter-
  evidence + verdict.
- 20 marks (~250-300 words): add a comparative dimension (multilateralism versus
  bilateralism as trade strategies, or RCEP-withdrawal versus subsequent FTA signings as a
  case of selective engagement) + 5-7 evidence units + explicit trade-offs + a fully
  reasoned verdict.

**Reasoned verdict template**
"As WTO dispute settlement remains impaired (Appellate Body paralysis, India outside the
MPIA), India has pursued selective bilateral/regional agreements (UAE, Australia, EFTA, UK
CETA, prospective EU FTA) to sustain market access, but [name the specific
utilisation/compliance/food-security constraint the question asks about] means bilateralism
is a partial, not complete, substitute for a functioning multilateral system — therefore
[qualified, directive-matching conclusion]."
