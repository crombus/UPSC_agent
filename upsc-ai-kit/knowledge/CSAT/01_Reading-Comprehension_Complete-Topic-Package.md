## 1. Visual foundation

```text
STEM FIRST  ->  what is asked?
   |             (idea / inference / assumption / tone / detail)
   v
READ PASSAGE ONCE  ->  mark: claim, scope words, contrast words
   |
JUDGE EACH OPTION AGAINST THE TEXT ONLY
   |
   +--> stated & on-scope?      keep
   +--> true but not stated?    reject (outside knowledge)
   +--> too broad / too narrow? reject
   +--> distorts the passage?   reject
   v
CHOOSE the option the passage can DEFEND
```

**Core proposition:** in a passage-only paper the winning option is the one **entailed by the
text**, not the one that is true in the real world.

## 2. The four question types and their tests

| Type | The stem sounds like | The correct option must... |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Central idea / main message** | "best summarises / crux / the author mainly says" | cover the **whole** passage, not one sentence. |
| ✅ **Inference / implication** | "can be inferred / most logical inference / implies" | be stated, entailed, or the **most defensible** reading with no new premise. |
| ✅ **Assumption** | "the argument assumes / rests on" | be an **unstated premise** the argument needs. |
| ✅ **Tone / attitude / scope** | "the author's tone / attitude / this passage is about" | match the **wording and range** of the text. |
| ✅ **Which statement(s) follow / are correct** | "which of the statements given above is/are correct" | be judged **one statement at a time**, then combined into the option code. |

> 🔑 **Trap:** many wrong options are **factually correct** yet not supported by the passage. In a
> passage-only item, "true" is irrelevant; only "stated or entailed" wins.

### 2.1 Three word-pairs that decide RC options

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Inference vs assumption** | An inference comes **out of** the passage (a conclusion). An assumption sits **under** it (an unstated premise the argument needs). |
| ✅ **Implication vs equivalence** | If the passage says "A leads to B", it has **not** said "B leads to A". An option that reverses the direction is a distortion, however plausible. |
| ✅ **Necessary vs sufficient** | "Forecasting is **necessary but not sufficient**" means good forecasts cannot be skipped **and** cannot finish the job alone. An option that treats a necessary condition as enough - or as useless - fails. |

- ⚠️ Passages in this paper regularly use "necessary but not sufficient", "only if", and "unless".
  Translate each into an arrow before reading the options: *"only if B" means `A -> B`*; *"unless B"
  means `not B -> not A`*.

## 3. Method (step by step)

1. **Read the stem first.** Knowing the type tells you what to hunt for.
2. **Read the passage once, actively.** Underline the author's **main claim**, **scope words**
   (some, most, all, only), and **contrast words** (but, however, although) - answers hide near these.
3. **Predict** the answer in your own words before looking at options.
4. **Test each option** against the text with the four filters (stated / scope / distortion /
   outside-knowledge).
5. **If two survive,** pick the **better-supported** one. A modest option often wins, but an
   absolute option is correct when the passage itself is absolute.

## 4. Distractor families (learn to name them)

| Distractor | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Out-of-passage truth** | A real fact the passage never states. |
| ✅ **Over-generalisation** | Turns "some/many" into "all/always/only/solely". |
| ✅ **Scope creep** | Adds a topic the passage did not discuss. |
| ✅ **Distortion** | Reverses cause-effect, or swaps a qualifier. |
| ✅ **Half-right** | One clause is supported, another is not - still wrong. |

## 5. Original solved examples

### 📝 Example A (inference)

> **Passage.** "When a city widens its roads to cut congestion, the extra capacity often fills with
> new car trips that people previously avoided. Within a few years travel times return to their old
> levels, while the city has spent heavily and lost green space. Planners increasingly argue that
> managing demand - through public transport and pricing - addresses congestion more durably than
> adding lanes."

**Q. Which inference is best supported?**
(a) Widening roads always makes congestion worse.
(b) Added road capacity can be offset by newly generated car trips.
(c) Cities should ban private cars.
(d) Public transport is cheaper than road building.

**Answer: (b).** The passage says extra capacity "often fills with new car trips", which is exactly
(b). (a) over-generalises ("always"); the passage says "often". (c) is never stated - a policy leap.
(d) is plausible in reality but the passage compares **durability**, not **cost** - out-of-passage.

### 📝 Example B (assumption)

> **Passage.** "The district should shift its midday-meal supply to nearby farmer groups. Local
> sourcing will cut transport delays and put money into the village economy."

**Q. The argument assumes that:**
(a) nearby farmer groups can supply food of adequate quantity and reliability.
(b) transport costs are the largest expense of the scheme.
(c) villagers currently oppose the midday-meal scheme.
(d) distant suppliers are dishonest.

**Answer: (a).** For "shift supply to nearby farmer groups" to work, the argument must silently rely
on those groups being **able to supply reliably** - remove (a) and the plan collapses, so it is the
assumption. (b) is an unstated ranking; (c) and (d) are not needed by the argument and are not stated.

### 📝 Example C (central idea)

> **Passage.** "Early weather warnings save lives only if the last mile works: the alert must reach a
> fisherman without a smartphone, in a language he reads, in time to act. Sophisticated forecasting
> is necessary but not sufficient; delivery decides the outcome."

**Q. The central idea is that:**
(a) forecasting technology is useless.
(b) fishermen should buy smartphones.
(c) an early warning helps only if it reaches people in a usable, timely form.
(d) India has the best forecasting systems.

**Answer: (c).** It captures the whole passage - forecasting **plus** last-mile delivery. (a)
contradicts "necessary"; (b) fixes on one detail; (d) is out-of-passage.

> 🔑 Note how Example C turns on **necessary vs sufficient**: the passage calls forecasting
> "necessary but not sufficient". Option (a) reads that as "useless" and option (c) reads it
> correctly. One word-pair decided the item.

## 6. Must-Know habits

- ✅ Read the **stem before** the passage.
- ✅ "Best supported", not "true": choose what the passage **defends**.
- ✅ Watch quantifiers - **some/most/all/only** decide most inference items.
- ✅ Watch **direction**: "A causes B" never licenses "B causes A".
- ✅ Watch **strength**: "necessary" is not "sufficient", and neither is "the only thing that matters".
- ✅ For assumptions, use the **negation test**: if negating the option breaks the argument, it is the
  assumption.
- ✅ For central idea, reject options that are **only one sentence** of a multi-point passage.

## 7. Common traps

- ❌ Picking a **true** statement that the passage never makes. -> Ask "which line proves this?"
- ❌ Turning a qualified claim ("often", "many") into an **absolute** ("always", "all"). -> Reject.
- ❌ **Reversing** a stated relation (treating `A -> B` as `B -> A`). -> Check the arrow direction.
- ❌ Reading "necessary" as "sufficient" (or as "unimportant"). -> Keep the two apart.
- ❌ Importing **subject knowledge** (economics, science) into a passage-only item. -> Text only.
- ❌ Choosing the **longest / most sophisticated** option by reflex. -> Length is not correctness.
- ❌ Answering the **wrong question type** (giving a detail when the stem wants the main idea). ->
  Re-read the stem.

## 8. Quick checks

- ✅ Can you name the question type from the stem alone? (idea / inference / assumption / tone)
- ✅ For any option you keep, can you point to the **exact phrase** that supports it?
- ✅ Did you reject at least one option specifically for being **out-of-passage**?
- ✅ Did you check that no kept option **reverses** a relation the passage stated one way?

## 9. Mini-drill (with answers and explanations)

> 📝 **Passage.** "Rooftop solar lets a household generate its own electricity and sell surplus power
> back to the grid. But adoption is slow: the upfront cost is high, and many tenants cannot install
> panels on a roof they do not own. Subsidies help with cost, yet they do nothing for the tenant who
> has no roof to use."

1. **The main point of the passage is that:**
   (a) rooftop solar has both a cost barrier and an ownership barrier, and subsidies address only the
   first.
   (b) rooftop solar is a failure.
   (c) all households should install solar panels.
   (d) subsidies are a waste of money.

2. **It can be inferred that:**
   (a) tenants are the main users of rooftop solar.
   (b) a subsidy alone will not make rooftop solar reach every household.
   (c) selling power to the grid is illegal.
   (d) rooftop solar produces no surplus power.

3. **The argument assumes that:**
   (a) installing panels normally requires control over the roof.
   (b) electricity from the grid is free.
   (c) landlords always refuse permission.
   (d) subsidies are unavailable in India.

**Answers.**

1. **(a).** It states both barriers and the limit of subsidies. (b)/(d) are over-negative and
   unstated; (c) is a policy leap.
2. **(b).** The passage explicitly says subsidies "do nothing for the tenant who has no roof", so a
   subsidy alone leaves some households out - a forced inference. (a) reverses the passage (tenants
   often **cannot** install); (c)/(d) contradict the text.
3. **(a).** "Cannot install panels on a roof they do not own" only bites if installation **needs**
   roof control - the unstated premise. (b)/(c)/(d) are not required by the argument.

## 10. Core coverage, timed execution and retry gate

### 10.1 Stem-to-method map

| If the stem asks for... | Do this first | Reject immediately |
|---|---|---|
| a stated detail | Locate the named sentence and paraphrase it narrowly. | A general opinion or a fact not in that sentence. |
| main idea/title/purpose | Write a six-to-ten-word summary covering claim **and** qualification. | A supporting detail, an example, or a new policy. |
| inference/conclusion | Ask: “What follows with no new premise?” For “most logical,” allow the smallest bridge the wording makes natural. | A stronger “must/only/all” claim than the passage licenses. |
| assumption | State the evidence-to-conclusion link, then negate a candidate precisely. | A conclusion, a desirable consequence, or a merely helpful fact. |
| tone/attitude | Circle evaluative words, irony and certainty words. | A tone inferred from the topic rather than the author's language. |
| statement code | Mark each statement T/F from the passage before looking at codes. | Treating a mixed bundle as one statement. |

> 🔑 **Inference safety rule:** “forced” is appropriate for a strict *must-follow* stem. For
> “best” or “most logical” inference, choose the option with the least unsupported extension, not a
> fact that is merely true outside the passage.

### 10.2 Timed core drill — 12 minutes, 6 marks

Use one read per passage. Mark an item **P** (out-of-passage), **S** (scope/strength),
**D** (direction), **A** (assumption) or **T** (time) beside every miss; do not read the solutions
until the timer ends.

> **Passage 1.** “A city that publishes bus-arrival data makes waiting less uncertain only when the
> data are reliable. A late but accurate update can still help a traveller choose to walk or wait;
> a confidently wrong update can make the service less usable than no update at all.”
>
> 1. Which inference is best supported?  
> (a) Reliability matters to the usefulness of arrival information.  
> (b) Every late update is useless.  
> (c) Travellers always prefer walking.  
> (d) Publishing data guarantees better bus service.
>
> 2. The passage assumes, in calling a wrong update harmful, that travellers may act on the update.  
> (a) No; this is a stated detail. (b) Yes. (c) Only if buses are privately run. (d) Only for late updates.

> **Passage 2.** “A soil test does not tell a farmer what crop to grow. It identifies nutrient
> constraints; the choice also depends on water, price risk and the farmer's objective. Treating the
> test as a crop prescription therefore mistakes one input for the whole decision.”
>
> 3. The central idea is that:  
> (a) soil tests are inaccurate.  
> (b) farmers should ignore prices.  
> (c) soil tests inform, but do not by themselves determine, crop choice.  
> (d) water never affects farming.
>
> 4. Which statement is supported?  
> 1. Nutrient information can matter to crop choice.  
> 2. A soil test alone settles every relevant farming decision.  
> (a) 2 only (b) neither (c) 1 and 2 (d) 1 only

> **Passage 3.** “The committee praises the pilot's ambition but notes that its reported success
> comes from three well-staffed districts. Before nationwide adoption, it asks whether the result
> persists where staff and connectivity are scarce.”
>
> 5. The tone is:  
> (a) wholly dismissive. (b) cautiously qualified. (c) celebratory. (d) indifferent.
>
> 6. Which close option is better supported?  
> (a) The pilot has failed outside the three districts.  
> (b) Staffing is irrelevant to the pilot.  
> (c) National adoption is impossible.  
> (d) Evidence from well-staffed districts may not generalise automatically.

**Answers and diagnosis.** 1-a (**S** if “late” or “guarantees” attracted you); 2-b (**A**); 3-c
(**S**); 4-d (**T** for statement-by-statement failure); 5-b (**S**); 6-d (**S/P**). A score of
**5/6 or 6/6 within 12 minutes** clears this Core gate. At 4/6 or below, sort misses by code, redo
only the failed passage untimed, then take a fresh six-item set on another day. Do not use Advanced
work to compensate for an uncleared Core gate.

# Reading Comprehension - ADVANCED

> **Core skill:** high-accuracy passage-only reasoning under time pressure - inference vs assumption
> vs implication, tone/scope, two-passage sets, and disciplined elimination.
> **Grounded in:** audited UPSC CSAT PYQ sets 2024-2026 (Set A). ✅ = method fact | ⚠️ = guidance | 📝 = original example.
> *Companion: [Foundation](basic/01_Reading-Comprehension.md).*

## 1. Architecture

```text
   TYPE the stem   (idea / inference / assumption / implication / tone / scope)
        |
   MODEL the argument   (claim  <-  evidence  <-  hidden premise)
        |
   +----+----------------------+
   |                           |
 INFERENCE test            ASSUMPTION test
 "must be true if           "negate it - does
  passage is true?"          the argument break?"
        |                           |
   ELIMINATE distractor families -> choose the DEFENSIBLE option
```

**Analytical claim:** every hard RC item is decided by two precise operations - **typing the stem**
and **stress-testing the option against the text** - never by outside knowledge.

## 2. Precise distinctions (the ones UPSC exploits)

| Pair | The difference that decides the answer |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Inference vs Assumption** | Inference = a **new** statement forced by the text (a *conclusion*). Assumption = an **unstated premise** the argument already **needs** (a *foundation*). |
| ✅ **Inference vs Implication** | Both must be text-defensible. For a strict “must follow” stem, it must be entailed; for “best/most logical”, prefer the conclusion requiring the fewest extra assumptions. |
| ✅ **Implication vs Equivalence** | A passage that says `A -> B` has **not** said `B -> A`. Only an "if and only if" (or an explicit definition) licenses the reverse. A reversed option is a **distortion**, not an inference. |
| ✅ **Necessary vs Sufficient** | "Necessary" = the claim fails without it; "sufficient" = it settles the claim. A passage saying "X is necessary but not enough" rules out both "X is useless" and "X guarantees the outcome". |
| ✅ **Central idea vs Detail** | Central idea spans the **whole** passage; a detail is one supporting sentence. A detail option is a classic main-idea distractor. |
| ✅ **Tone vs Content** | Tone is the author's **attitude** (neutral, critical, cautionary, optimistic), read from **word choice**, independent of the facts stated. |
| ✅ **Stated vs Entailed** | "Stated" = literally in the text. "Entailed" = must be true even if not literal. Both are acceptable; "merely true in the world" is not. |

## 3. Techniques with conditions

- ⚠️ **Negation test (for assumptions).** Negate the option; if the argument **collapses**, it is
  the assumption. *Condition:* use only on assumption stems - negating an inference option proves
  nothing.
- ⚠️ **Scope-matching (for central idea/tone).** The answer's breadth must equal the passage's
  breadth. *Condition:* reject options that add a topic the passage never raised, however sensible.
- ⚠️ **Quantifier audit.** Rank options by strength; passage-only answers are usually the **most
  modest** claim consistent with the text. *Condition:* if the passage itself is absolute ("no",
  "never"), the answer may be strong too - match the text, not a reflex.
- ⚠️ **Two-survivor rule.** When two options remain, find the **single word** that makes one
  unsupported (an added "only", "all", "because"). *Condition:* the discriminator must be a
  text-vs-option mismatch, not your preference.

### 3.1 Time-saving decision ladder (RC is where the clock is won or lost)

⚠️ Comprehension is the largest audited family, so its **time policy** matters more than any single
technique. Climb only as far as you need:

| Rung | Spend | Do this | Exit when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Type** | ~5 s | Name the stem type (idea / inference / assumption / tone / statement-set). | Always do this. |
| **2. Predict** | ~10 s | Answer in your own words **before** reading options. | A prediction exists. |
| **3. Match** | ~20 s | Scan for the option closest to the prediction. | One clear match -> **mark and leave**. |
| **4. Eliminate** | ~30 s | Kill options by out-of-passage / over-generalisation / reversal / half-right. | Two or fewer survive. |
| **5. Discriminate** | ~20 s | Find the one word that breaks a survivor; apply the negation test on assumption stems. | One survivor. |
| **6. Escalate or park** | - | If two still survive, take the **more modest** claim; if none does, **park it** and move on. | The per-item ceiling is hit. |

- 🔑 **Escalation rule:** never re-read a whole passage for one option. Re-read only **the sentence
  the option points at**. If the option points at no sentence, it is already wrong.
- ⚠️ **Statement-set items** ("which of the statements given above is/are correct") are the exception
  worth extra time: each statement is a **separate** true/false decision, and settling even two of
  three usually eliminates every option but one. Judge statements individually, never as a bundle.

## 4. Two-passage compare sets (a recurring PYQ format)

> ⚠️ **PYQ-pattern note:** the papers frequently pair **two short passages** and ask which
> statement/inference is supported by **one, both, or neither**. Read each passage's **claim**
> separately, then map each option to "P1 only / P2 only / both / neither" **before** choosing.

### 📝 Example (two-passage inference)

> **Passage 1.** "Free public data - maps, weather, transport timings - lets small firms build useful
> apps without collecting the data themselves. The public investment is repaid many times over in
> private innovation."
>
> **Passage 2.** "When public data is released without privacy safeguards, individuals can be
> re-identified by combining datasets. Openness must be paired with careful anonymisation."

**Q. Which inference is supported by the passages?**
(a) Public data should never be released. (P2 distortion)
(b) Open public data can spur innovation but needs privacy safeguards. (both)
(c) Small firms should collect their own data. (contradicts P1)
(d) Anonymisation makes data useless. (unstated)

**Answer: (b)** - it is the only statement each passage supports (P1 -> innovation; P2 -> safeguards).
Each distractor fails against at least one passage.

## 5. Harder solved examples

### 📝 Example A (implication under a qualifier)

> **Passage.** "Micro-irrigation raises yields per litre, so states subsidise drip kits. Yet where
> water is nearly free, farmers who save water often expand their cropped area, and total water use
> can stay flat or even rise."

**Q. Which is the most logical implication?**
(a) Micro-irrigation always reduces total water use.
(b) Efficiency gains may not cut total water use when water is under-priced.
(c) Subsidising drip kits is pointless.
(d) Farmers dislike micro-irrigation.

**Answer: (b).** The passage's own qualifier ("where water is nearly free ... total water use can
stay flat or even rise") forces (b). (a) is contradicted; (c) over-reaches; (d) is unstated.

### 📝 Example B (tone/attitude)

> **Passage.** "The report's glossy targets are admirable on paper. One only wishes it had spared a
> single line for how, with what money, and by whom they will be met."

**Q. The author's tone is best described as:**
(a) enthusiastic and congratulatory.
(b) neutral and descriptive.
(c) sceptical about implementation.
(d) hostile and dismissive.

**Answer: (c).** "admirable on paper" plus the wish for a line on "how, with what money" signals
doubt about **implementation**, not the goals - sceptical, not hostile. (a)/(b) miss the irony; (d)
overshoots ("hostile/dismissive").

## 6. Limitations and boundary cases

- ⚠️ A passage can support a claim you personally think is **false** - support it anyway; the test is
  the text, not the truth.
- ⚠️ "Most logical inference" can have **two defensible options**; choose the one needing **fewer
  added assumptions**.
- ⚠️ Central-idea options that are **individually true details** are the hardest distractors - always
  ask "does this cover the whole passage?"

## 7. Advanced traps

- ❌ Treating an **assumption** stem like an inference (choosing a conclusion). -> Use the negation
  test.
- ❌ Rewarding an option for **sophistication** or length. -> Support beats style.
- ❌ Turning a passage's **"can"** into **"must"**, or its `A -> B` into `B -> A`. -> That is an
  equivalence the text never granted.
- ❌ Reading "necessary" as "sufficient" (or as "irrelevant"). -> Keep the two words apart.
- ❌ Letting a **single distorted word** slip through ("because", "only", "all"). -> Two-survivor
  rule.
- ❌ In two-passage sets, crediting an option supported by **one** passage when the stem needs
  **both**. -> Map to P1/P2/both/neither first.
- ❌ Judging a statement-set item as one bundle. -> Decide each numbered statement separately, then
  read the code.
- ❌ Reading **tone** from the facts instead of the **wording**. -> Tone lives in adjectives and
  irony.

## 9. Advanced drill (with full solutions)

> 📝 **Passage.** "Ranking schools purely by average board marks pushes them to court strong students
> and quietly discourage weak ones, because a struggling child lowers the average. A fairer gauge is
> how much a school **improves** each child from where they started - its value added - though this is
> harder to measure."

1. **The central idea is that:**
   (a) board exams should be abolished.
   (b) ranking schools by average marks can create perverse incentives, and value-added is a fairer
   though harder measure.
   (c) weak students should change schools.
   (d) all schools game their rankings.

2. **The argument assumes that:**
   (a) a school's average mark can be raised by changing which students it admits or retains.
   (b) board marks are always inaccurate.
   (c) value-added is easy to compute.
   (d) parents ignore school rankings.

3. **Which inference is best supported?**
   (a) A school can score well on average marks yet add little value to its students.
   (b) Value-added measurement is impossible.
   (c) Strong students never need improvement.
   (d) Average marks and value added always agree.

4. **Which of the statements given below is/are supported by the passage?**
   1. A fairer measure can also be a harder one to compute.
   2. Any school with a high average mark must be adding little value.
   3. Discouraging weak students would tend to raise a school's average.

   (a) 1 only  (b) 1 and 3 only  (c) 2 and 3 only  (d) 1, 2 and 3

**Solutions.**

1. **(b).** Captures both the problem (perverse incentive) and the proposed fairer gauge. (a)/(c) are
   leaps; (d) over-generalises ("all").
2. **(a).** The claim that averages push schools to court strong and discourage weak students **only
   works** if admitting/retaining different students moves the average - negate it and the argument
   fails. (b)/(c) contradict the text ("harder to measure"); (d) is unstated.
3. **(a).** If average marks reward student selection while value-added measures improvement, a
   selective school can look good on averages yet add little - a forced inference. (b) contradicts
   "harder", not "impossible"; (c)/(d) are unsupported absolutes.
4. **(b).** Judge each statement separately.
   - **1 - supported.** The passage calls value-added "a fairer gauge ... though this is harder to
     measure": fairer **and** harder, exactly as stated.
   - **2 - not supported.** The passage says a selective school **can** look good on averages while
     adding little; it never says every high-average school does. Turning "can" into "must" reverses a
     one-way claim into an equivalence - the classic **implication-vs-equivalence** distortion.
   - **3 - supported.** "A struggling child lowers the average" entails that removing or discouraging
     such children raises it. That is a **direct average consequence**, not a contrapositive: a
     contrapositive of `A -> B` would be `not B -> not A`.

   ⚠️ Note the economy of this format: settling statement 2 alone kills options (c) and (d), and
   settling statement 3 then decides between (a) and (b). **You rarely need all three.**

These complete passage-based validation items are separate from the source drills. Complete them in **8 minutes**. Answers rotate A → B → C → D; answer position is never evidence.

> **Passage A.** “The district's new grievance portal records every complaint, but recording is not resolution. If no officer owns a case and no deadline is visible, a citizen gains a digital receipt rather than an effective remedy.”
>

> (a) A portal needs accountable follow-up as well as complaint recording.
> (b) Digital receipts are unlawful.
> (c) Every grievance should be decided on the day it is filed.

>
> **Q2. The argument assumes that:**
> (a) all grievances arise from technology failures.
> (b) ownership and deadlines can affect whether a complaint is resolved.

> **Passage B.** “A city can cool some streets by planting trees, but the result depends on species, water and space for roots. A target expressed only as the number of saplings therefore says less than it appears to say about eventual shade.”
>
> **Q3. The central idea is that:**
> (a) saplings should never be planted in cities.
> (b) watering urban trees is unnecessary.
> (c) sapling counts alone are an incomplete measure of eventual cooling benefit.
> (d) every tree species gives identical shade.
>
> **Q4. The author's tone is best described as:**
> (a) mocking and hostile.
> (b) unqualified celebration.
> (c) indifferent to urban heat.
> (d) cautionary about a simplistic metric.

**Solutions.** Q1 **A**: it preserves the distinction between a record and a remedy. Q2 **B**: without that link, the stated defects would not matter to resolution. Q3 **C**: it covers the claim and its conditions. Q4 **D**: “says less than it appears” is caution, not hostility.

# Final consolidated register notes

- Start with the stem; name the task before reading options.
- Main idea and title must cover the whole qualified claim, not a memorable detail.
- A stated detail is checked against its exact sentence; do not widen its scope.
- A strict must-follow inference is entailed; a “best/most logical” inference needs the least extra premise.
- An assumption is the unstated bridge from evidence to conclusion; use precise negation.
- “Only if B” means `A → B`; do not reverse it. A real contrapositive is `not B → not A`.
- Match certainty words: can, may, often, most, all, only, necessary and sufficient.
- Tone comes from wording, qualification and irony—not from the subject matter.
- For statement codes, decide each numbered statement before reading a combined option.
- Reject outside knowledge, scope creep, causal reversal, equivalence claims and half-right options.
- On close options, identify the single unsupported word rather than rereading everything.
- Use one active read, quote/locate support, and park an item at its time ceiling.
- Log P (out-of-passage), S (scope/strength), D (direction), A (assumption) and T (time) errors.
- Clear the Basic timed gate twice before treating Advanced material as optional enrichment.
- In full papers, follow the linked Master Framework for three-pass selection, negative marking and qualifying margin.
