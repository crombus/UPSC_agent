# Punctuation and Capitalisation — Core

Punctuation clarifies structure. In a correction question, preserve the
intended meaning and accept more than one standard punctuation choice where
context permits it.

## 1. Commas

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| items in a series | *data, staff, finance and time* |
| introductory phrase/clause | *After the meeting, we left.* |
| two complete clauses joined by *and, but, or, so, yet* | *The proposal was sound, but funding was absent.* |
| non-restrictive information | *The chairperson, who arrived late, apologised.* |
| direct address / answer | *Riya, please read; No, I disagree.* |
| contrast | *The decision was legal, not wise.* |

The serial/Oxford comma before `and` is a style choice: *data, staff, finance,
and time* and *data, staff, finance and time* are both standard. Use it when
it prevents ambiguity and stay consistent.

Do **not** put a comma:

- between subject and verb: `The causes of pollution, are…`;
- between verb and object: `The committee submitted, its report`;
- around an identifying/restrictive relative clause: *Students who practise
  regularly improve*;
- alone between two complete clauses (a comma splice): `The plan failed, we
  revised it`.

Repair a comma splice with a full stop, semicolon, colon where appropriate, or
a comma plus a coordinating conjunction. Context decides whether a relative
clause is essential; a sentence such as *My brother who lives in Pune…* cannot
be punctuated uniquely without knowing whether the writer has other brothers.

## 2. Semicolon, colon and dash

| Mark | Safe use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| semicolon | joins closely linked independent clauses; separates complex list items | *Evidence was incomplete; the inquiry continued.* |
| colon | introduces explanation, list, restatement or quotation after a complete lead-in | *The paper tests four abilities: comprehension, précis, usage and essay writing.* |
| dash | interruption, sharp addition or emphasis | *One point remained—the evidence was weak.* |

Avoid a colon that merely splits a verb or preposition from its necessary
object: `The abilities are: grammar and usage` is usually better as *The
abilities are grammar and usage* or *The abilities are as follows: grammar and
usage.* This is a **style and clarity preference, not an absolute grammar
ban**. A colon can follow a complete clause ending in a verb where the clause
genuinely announces what follows: *Her conclusion was clear: postpone the
launch.*

## 3. Apostrophes, quotation and terminal marks

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| singular possession | *the candidate's answer* |
| regular plural possession | *the candidates' answers* |
| irregular plural possession | *children's books* |
| contraction | *don't; it's (= it is/it has)* |
| possessive `its` | *The committee submitted its report.* |

Never use an apostrophe for an ordinary plural: `NGO's` → *NGOs*. An
apostrophe after a joint possession goes on the final name: *Ravi and
Mohan's office*; separate ownership takes both: *Ravi's and Mohan's offices*.

Use quotation marks for exact speech. Direct speech normally has a reporting
comma: *The minister said, “We must act now.”* In indirect speech remove
quotation marks and adjust grammar. British and US placement of a final full
stop relative to quotation marks can differ; follow the paper’s style unless
the quoted words themselves require it.

End statements with a full stop. Use `?` only for a direct question: *He
asked whether I was ready.* Avoid `!` in a formal answer except when quoting.

## 4. Hyphens, brackets and ellipsis

- Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun: *a well-written answer; a
  five-year plan*. Do not usually hyphenate after the verb: *the answer is well
  written; the plan is five years old*.
- Do not hyphenate an adverb ending in `-ly`: *a carefully written answer*.
- Brackets add non-essential clarification. Use sparingly in formal prose.
- An ellipsis shows omitted words in a quotation; it is not a substitute for
  planning a sentence.

## 5. Capitalisation

Capitalise the first word of a sentence, proper names, languages/nationalities,
days/months, named institutions, Acts and formally used titles: *India,
English, Monday, the Constitution of India, Parliament, the President of
India*. Use lower case for generic roles: *the minister spoke; a parliamentary
committee met*. Capitalisation does not make a common noun important.

## 6. Punctuation decision path

```text
Two complete clauses?
  ├─ joined by coordinating conjunction → comma + conjunction
  ├─ closely related without conjunction → semicolon or full stop
  └─ second explains first → colon (if first clause is complete)
Relative clause?
  ├─ identifies which person/thing → no commas
  └─ adds removable information → pair of commas
```

## 7. Timed diagnostic

Punctuate in **7 minutes**; state an accepted alternative where relevant.

1. after the interview we returned home
2. the proposal was ambitious however it lacked funding
3. the reports that were incomplete were returned
4. my only brother who lives in pune is a doctor
5. the inquiry had one conclusion postpone the launch
6. its important to check every candidates identity card

**Key:** 1 *After the interview, we returned home.* [PUNC]; 2 *The proposal
was ambitious; however, it lacked funding.* A full stop before *however* is
also accepted [PUNC]; 3 *The reports that were incomplete were returned.*
[REL]; 4 *My only brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.* [REL/CAP]; 5 *The
inquiry had one conclusion: postpone the launch.* [COL]; 6 *It's important to
check every candidate's identity card.* [APOS].

## Semantic-completeness closure — 2026-09-06

### Literal syllabus, ownership and boundary

- **Syllabus demand:** Usage and Vocabulary; structural punctuation and conventional capitalisation needed for clear, correct expression.
- **Canonical ownership:** Owns commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes, quotation marks, terminal marks, hyphens, brackets, ellipses and capitalisation.
- **Cross-topic boundary:** Relative-clause meaning and sentence grammar are introduced in Topics 01-02; Topic 03 owns the marks that encode those relations. Broader correction procedure belongs to Topic 05.

### Complete learner and answer route

1. **Clause map:** Mark independent, dependent, restrictive and supplementary units before punctuating.
2. **Introductory commas:** Separate a substantial opening phrase or clause from the main clause.
3. **Coordinated clauses:** Use comma plus coordinator, semicolon or full stop rather than a comma splice.
4. **Relative information:** Use commas only for removable non-restrictive material.
5. **Lists and contrasts:** Use consistent list punctuation and commas that prevent misreading.
6. **Semicolon:** Join closely related independent clauses or separate complex list items.
7. **Colon:** Introduce an explanation, list or restatement after a complete announcing clause.
8. **Dash and brackets:** Use sparingly for interruption, emphasis or parenthetical clarification.
9. **Apostrophe:** Distinguish possession, contraction and ordinary plural.
10. **Quotation and terminal marks:** Preserve direct speech and question/statement status.
11. **Hyphen and ellipsis:** Hyphenate useful compound modifiers; use ellipsis only for omission.
12. **Capitalisation audit:** Capitalise proper names and formal titles, not generic importance.

### Authority, variation and hostile-query gate

Judge punctuation from syntax and intended meaning. Accept legitimate British/US or serial-comma variants when consistent; reject only changes that create ambiguity, a splice or a false possession.

1. Foundation: identify the demand and state the governing rule in plain language.
2. Controlled application: correct or construct one sentence and explain the change.
