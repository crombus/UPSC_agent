## Non-negotiable scoring principle

The paper tests **correct, clear and concise English**, not ornamental writing. Prefer a short correct sentence to a long sentence whose agreement, punctuation or logic is uncertain. Answer the precise command; do not substitute outside knowledge for the passage.

|---|---:|---|

| Précis | 43 min | Map ideas, calculate target, draft, count and polish |

If a section overruns, use the 5-minute reserve first, then stop at the hard section boundary. Do not sacrifice final checking to perfect one answer.

## Error-control checklist

Before submitting, check:

1. Every sentence has a finite verb.
2. Subject and verb agree.
3. Pronouns have clear antecedents.
4. Tense does not shift without reason.
5. Articles and prepositions are idiomatic.
6. Commas do not join two complete sentences by themselves.
7. Apostrophes show possession or omission, never an ordinary plural.
8. Essay paragraphs have one controlling idea and advance the thesis.
9. Comprehension answers use the passage, answer every part and distinguish fact from inference.
10. Précis preserves the thesis, major idea units and concessions at about the instructed proportion, in independent connected prose.
11. Count essay and précis words; obey title/no-title instructions literally.
12. Strike out accidental repetition, unsupported statistics, quotations and decorative flourishes.

## PART 06 — Parts of Speech, Nouns and Pronouns — Core

Use this file before `basic/02_Sentence-Grammar.md`. In correction questions,
change only what is wrong; do not “improve” an already acceptable sentence.

## 1. Sentence map and word classes

```text
determiner + adjective + NOUN/PRONOUN (subject) + finite VERB + object/complement
                         \                         /
                    modifiers: adjective, adverb, phrase, clause
```

| Class | Main job | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | names a person, thing, quality or event | can often take a determiner/plural |
| Pronoun | replaces or points to a noun phrase | must have clear reference and case |
| Verb | expresses action, state or occurrence | finite verb changes for tense/agreement |
| Adjective | modifies a noun/pronoun | *a careful officer; officers are careful* |
| Adverb | modifies a verb, adjective, adverb or clause | *very careful; spoke carefully* |
| Determiner | identifies/quantifies a noun | *the, this, each, many, my* |
| Preposition | relates a noun phrase to another unit | *in Delhi; depend on evidence* |
| Conjunction | joins units | *and, although, because* |

Words can change class: *a **record** (noun); **record** the result (verb);
an **early** train (adjective); arrive **early** (adverb).* Use function in the
sentence, not a dictionary label.

## 2. Nouns: number and countability

### Countable, uncountable and count shifts

| Pattern | Safe use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular count noun | needs a determiner | *a book; the proposal; my answer* |
| Plural count noun | takes *many/few/fewer* | *many books; fewer errors* |
| Uncountable/mass noun | no ordinary plural; takes *much/little/less* | *much information; little evidence* |
| Measure expression | makes an uncountable noun countable | *two pieces of advice; three items of equipment* |
| Count shift | meaning changes when a mass noun is counted | *paper* (material) / *a paper* (article); *experience* (knowledge) / *an experience* (event) |

Common uncountables in examination corrections: **advice, information,
furniture, equipment, luggage, baggage, scenery, research, progress, work,
traffic, evidence, knowledge, machinery, bread, news**. Write *a piece of
furniture, an item of information, a piece of evidence, some research*.
`News` ends in *-s* but normally takes a singular verb: *The news is
encouraging.*

Do not infer that every noun is permanently countable or uncountable:

| Uncountable meaning | Countable meaning |
|---|---|
| *Chicken is expensive* (meat) | *three chickens* (birds) |
| *I need paper* (material) | *submit a paper* (essay/article) |
| *There is little room* (space) | *book a room* (place) |
| *Experience matters* (knowledge) | *it was a frightening experience* (event) |
| *Light travels quickly* (illumination) | *switch on the lights* (lamps) |

### Irregular, zero and plural-only nouns

| Type | Examples | Agreement/use |
|---|---|---|
| Irregular plural | child/children, man/men, woman/women, foot/feet, tooth/teeth, mouse/mice, person/people | plural verb: *Children are…* |
| Same singular/plural | sheep, deer, aircraft, species, series | context decides: *one species is; two species are* |
| Noun ending in *-s*, singular | news, mathematics, economics (subject), politics (subject) | usually singular: *Economics is…* |
| Plural-only | scissors, trousers, spectacles, clothes, cattle, police | plural verb: *The police are…*; *a pair of scissors is…* |
| Collective noun | committee, team, government, family, jury | singular when viewed as one unit; plural when members act separately. Keep verb and pronoun consistent: *The committee has submitted its report* / *The committee are divided in their views.* |

`People` is normally plural; `persons` is restricted to formal/legal counting.
`Data` is commonly plural in careful academic prose (*the data are*), but
singular collective *data is* occurs in modern general prose. Follow the
question’s register and remain consistent.

## 3. Pronouns: case, reference and agreement

### Case

| Function | Forms |
|---|---|
| Subject | I, we, he, she, they, who |
| Object of verb/preposition | me, us, him, her, them, whom |
| Possessive determiner | my, our, his, her, their, whose |
| Independent possessive | mine, ours, his, hers, theirs |
| Reflexive/emphatic | myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves |

Use object case after a verb or preposition: *The officer called Riya and
me; between you and me; whom did you meet?* In a comparison, supply the
missing verb when needed: *She is taller than I (am)*; *They invited her more
often than me* is common but can be ambiguous. Write the full clause in a
formal answer.

Reflexives need a same-clause antecedent or emphatic use: *She blamed herself;
the director himself signed it.* Not `Please contact myself`; use `me`.

### Reference, relatives and singular *they*

1. A pronoun must identify one sensible antecedent. Rewrite *When Ravi met
   Mohan, he was upset* as *Ravi was upset when he met Mohan*.
2. `who` is for people as subject; `whom` is object in formal style; `whose`
   shows possession; `which` normally refers to things; `that` may introduce a
   restrictive clause for people or things.
3. **Restrictive (identifying):** no commas. *Candidates who arrive late may
   not enter.* **Non-restrictive (extra):** commas and normally `who/which`,
   not `that`. *My brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.* Punctuation
   depends on intended meaning; it cannot be scored without context.
4. `each, every, either, neither, everyone, someone, nobody` normally take a
   singular verb: *Every applicant is ready.* Singular **they/them/their** is
   accepted modern formal English when sex is unknown or irrelevant: *Each
   applicant must bring their admit card.* A fully explicit alternative is
   *Each applicant must bring his or her admit card* or plural recasting:
   *Applicants must bring their admit cards.* Do not mark a clear singular
   `they` wrong merely for being singular.

## 4. Adjectives, determiners and adverbs

The usual adjective order is opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin →
material → purpose → noun: *a useful small old Indian wooden writing desk*.
It is a preference, not a mechanical rule.

| Point | Safe form |
|---|---|
| Comparison | *taller; more careful; the tallest; the most careful* |
| No double comparison | `more wiser` → *wiser* |
| Comparison target | *prefer X to Y; senior/junior/superior/inferior to* |
| Correlative comparison | *the more carefully you read, the better you write* |
| `only` placement | *Only Riya objected* (nobody else); *Riya objected only to the fee* (nothing else) |
| Fewer/less | *fewer books*; *less water*. `less than ten minutes` is accepted for quantity/time. |

Some adverbs have the same form as adjectives: *fast, hard, late, early,
daily*. `Hardly` means “scarcely,” not “with effort”: *work hard; hardly work.*

## 5. Verbs: form and transitivity

| Pattern | Principal parts | Meaning check |
|---|---|---|
| rise | rise, rose, risen | something goes up itself |
| raise | raise, raised, raised | someone/something lifts another thing |
| lie (recline) | lie, lay, lain | *The child lay down yesterday* |
| lay (put) | lay, laid, laid | takes object: *She laid the file down* |
| fall | fall, fell, fallen | no object in ordinary use |
| fell | fell, felled, felled | cut down: *fell a tree* |

Only verbs with an object normally form a passive: *The committee approved the
plan → The plan was approved.* Do not force a passive from *arrive, sleep,
occur, happen*.

## 6. Fast diagnostic

Correct each, name the error code, then check below.

1. The police **is** investigating the case.
2. She gave me two useful **advices**.
3. Between you and **I**, the plan is unsafe.
4. Every candidate should bring **his** identity card.
5. The report **that** was published yesterday, changed the debate.
6. We bought two **furniture** for the office.

**Key:** 1 *are* [AGR]; 2 *pieces of advice* [CNT]; 3 *me* [PRO-CASE];
4 *their* is accepted, or recast/pluralise [PRO-AGR]; 5 remove commas if
identifying, or use *which* with commas if extra information [REL]; 6 *pieces
of furniture* [CNT]. Time: **6 minutes**. Record every missed code in the
error log in `basic/05`.

## PART 07 — Sentence Grammar — Core

## 1. Complete sentences and agreement

A complete sentence needs a finite verb and a complete thought. Repair a
fragment (*Because the policy failed*) by attaching it to a main clause. Do not
join two complete clauses with a comma alone: use a full stop, semicolon, or
coordinating conjunction.

| Subject pattern | Verb |
|---|---|
| singular noun/pronoun | *The officer is ready.* |
| plural noun/pronoun | *The officers are ready.* |
| `each/every/either/neither` + singular noun | *Each proposal has merit.* |
| `each of/neither of` + plural noun | formal default singular: *Neither of the alternatives is acceptable.* Plural is also common with `neither of` in modern use; use singular in a correction test unless context requires otherwise. |
| `one of` + plural noun | singular: *One of the answers is wrong.* |
| `a number of` / `the number of` | plural / singular: *A number are absent; the number is rising.* |
| amount, period, distance as one unit | singular: *Ten kilometres is far to walk.* |
| `along with/as well as/together with` | agrees with first subject: *The minister, along with officials, is attending.* |
| `either...or/neither...nor` | normally agree with nearer subject: *Neither the officers nor the minister is ready.* Recast awkward mixed-person subjects: *Neither the minister nor the officers are ready.* |
| collective noun | singular unit or plural members; keep pronoun consistent: *The team has its plan / are arguing among themselves.* |
| `none of` + plural/count noun | singular is traditional when “not one”; plural is common when “not any.” Choose one form and keep the reference clear: *None of the evidence is…; none of the applicants are…* |
| fraction/percentage + `of` | agree with the noun after `of`: *Half of the work is done; half of the files are missing.* |
| `there is/are` | agree with the following real subject: *There are two errors; there is one error.* |
| gerund/infinitive clause | usually singular: *Reading carefully is essential.* |

`More than one + singular noun` normally takes singular (*More than one
candidate has applied*). `Many a + singular noun` also takes singular.

## 2. Tense and aspect: choose the time relation

| Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| fact, habit, timetable | simple present | *Water freezes at 0°C; the train leaves at six.* |
| action happening now/temporary arrangement | present continuous | *She is reading; they are staying in Delhi.* |
| completed action with present result; unfinished time | present perfect | *She has submitted the form; I have seen her today.* |
| duration continuing to now | present perfect continuous | *He has been working for five days.* |
| completed past at stated/finished time | simple past | *She submitted it yesterday; I saw her in 2024.* |
| ongoing past interrupted by another event | past continuous | *She was reading when I entered.* |
| earlier of two past points | past perfect | *The meeting had ended before we arrived.* Use it for a needed sequence, not automatically for every past sentence. |
| duration before a past point | past perfect continuous | *They had been waiting for an hour before the bus came.* |
| future prediction/decision | will + base verb | *I will call later.* |
| arranged future | present continuous | *We are meeting tomorrow.* |
| action in progress at future time | future continuous | *This time tomorrow, she will be travelling.* |
| future before a stated future point | future perfect | *By June, she will have finished.* |
| duration up to a future point | future perfect continuous | *By June, she will have been working here for a year.* |

State verbs normally avoid continuous forms in their ordinary meanings:
*know, believe, understand, own, belong, prefer, need*. Write *I know the
rule*, not usually `I am knowing`. Some change meaning: *I think it is right*
(opinion) / *I am thinking about it* (considering).

### Time clauses and conditionals

Do not ordinarily put `will` after *if, when, until, as soon as, before* for a
future condition: *If/when she arrives, I will call.* `Will` is possible when
it means willingness: *If you will wait, I will check.*

| Type | Form | Meaning/example |
|---|---|---|
| Zero | if + present, present | general result: *If water freezes, it expands.* |
| First | if + present, will/may/can/imperative | real future: *If you revise, you will improve.* |
| Second | if + past form, would/could/might + base | unreal present/future: *If I knew, I would tell you.* `were` is safest formal form: *If I were you…* |
| Third | if + past perfect, would/could/might have + past participle | unreal past: *If she had left earlier, she would have caught the train.* |
| Mixed: past condition, present result | if + past perfect, would + base | *If I had taken the job, I would live in Mumbai now.* |
| Mixed: present condition, past result | if + past form, would have + participle | *If he were more careful, he would not have made that error.* |

Do not use `would` in the ordinary if-clause: `If I would know` → *If I knew*.
With *unless*, use an affirmative condition where it means *if ... not*:
*Unless you practise, you will not improve.* Do **not** replace *if* with
*unless* if it changes polarity, emphasis, or a negative main-clause meaning.

## 3. Articles and determiners

### Article choice

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| `a/an` = one, non-specific singular count noun | *a university; an hour; an MBA graduate* — choose by sound, not spelling |
| `the` = specific, previously identified, unique in context, superlative/ordinal | *the file you sent; the sun; the first answer; the best option* |
| zero article = plural/uncountable in general | *Books are useful; Honesty matters; Water is scarce.* |
| institution purpose | *go to school/prison/hospital* as pupil/prisoner/patient; *go to the school/hospital* as a building/visitor |
| geographical names | *India, Delhi, Mount Everest*; but *the Himalayas, the Ganga, the Indian Ocean, the United States* |

A singular count noun normally cannot stand bare: `She is teacher` → *She is a
teacher.* General abstract nouns can take `the` when qualified: *Freedom is
valuable; the freedom guaranteed by law matters.*

### Determiners and quantity

| Count nouns | Uncountable nouns | Either/other |
|---|---|---|
| many, few/a few, fewer, several | much, little/a little, less | some, any, enough, more, most, all, no |

`few/little` means hardly any; `a few/a little` means some and is positive.
Use *fewer candidates* but *less water*; `less than ten candidates` is common
in measurement-style usage but *fewer* is safest in formal correction.
`much` is common in negatives/questions; *a great deal of* is natural in
positive statements. Use `some` in offers/requests expecting yes: *Would you
like some tea?*

| Determiner contrast | Safe use |
|---|---|
| each/every | both take singular noun and verb; *each* views individuals, *every* the group: *Every room; each of the rooms* |
| either/neither | normally one of/not one of two: *either answer is; neither answer is* |
| both | plural: *both answers are* |
| another/other/the other | one more; additional/different; the remaining one of two: *another book; other books; the other book* |
| fewer/less | number of countable items; amount/quantity |
| amount/number | uncountable quantity; countable items: *amount of water; number of books* |

## 4. Prepositions, verb patterns and phrasal verbs

Memorise the whole construction. A preposition may vary with meaning; do not
invent a universal rule from one example.

| Meaning | Safe construction |
|---|---|
| agree with a person / agree to a plan / agree on a point | *I agree with her; they agreed to the proposal.* |
| angry with/at a person; angry at/about an act | Both *angry with the contractor* and *angry at the contractor* are standard. |
| approve of; comply with; consist of; depend on; insist on | *comply with the order; insist on verification* |
| abstain from; distinguish X from Y; responsible for | *abstain from voting* |
| discuss/enter/approach/reach | no `about/into/to` in ordinary transitive use: *discuss the proposal; enter a room; approach the officer; reach Delhi* |
| comprise / consist | *The unit comprises five sections*; *The unit consists of five sections.* Avoid `comprises of` in formal correction. |
| wait for; listen to; belong to; suffer from | *wait for a bus; listen to advice* |

### Double objects and `provide`

Many verbs allow two patterns:

| Verb | Pattern 1 | Pattern 2 |
|---|---|---|
| give/send/show/teach | *give her the file* | *give the file to her* |
| buy/make/find | *buy her a book* | *buy a book for her* |
| provide | *provide students with meals* | *provide meals for/to students* |

`Provide someone something` is established, especially in North American
usage, but is less preferred in conservative British formal prose. It is not a
grammar error. In an Indian formal correction answer, prefer one of the two
standard `provide ... with/for/to` patterns unless the question tests a stated
house style.

### Phrasal/prepositional verbs

| Verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| look after | care for | *look after children* |
| look for | seek | *look for evidence* |
| look into | investigate | *look into the complaint* |
| look up to | admire | *look up to her teacher* |
| put off | postpone | *put off the meeting* |
| put up with | tolerate | *put up with noise* |
| carry out | perform | *carry out an inquiry* |
| bring up | mention/raise | *bring up the issue* |
| turn down | reject/reduce | *turn down the offer* |
| take over | assume control | *take over the office* |

Separable verb + pronoun: *turn the offer down / turn it down*, not `turn down
it`. Do not split inseparable *look after*: `look the child after` is wrong.

## 5. Conjunctions, parallelism and modifiers

| Rule | Correct form |
|---|---|
| No double conjunction | *Although it rained, we left*; not `Although… but…` |
| `because` gives reason; `because of` takes noun phrase | *because it rained / because of rain* |
| `despite/in spite of` takes noun phrase or *the fact that* clause | *despite the rain; in spite of the fact that it rained* |
| `not only...but also` needs matched units | *not only honest but also efficient; not only reads but also writes* |
| `either...or` / `neither...nor` / `both...and` | match grammar on both sides: *either to revise or to withdraw; both accurate and concise* |
| paired list stays parallel | *planning, coordinating and monitoring* |
| comparison compares like with like | *the population of Delhi is larger than that of Pune* |

Place a modifier beside its target:

| Fault | Repair |
|---|---|
| Dangling: `Walking to office, the rain began.` | *While I was walking to office, it began to rain.* |
| Misplaced: `She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates.` | *She served the children sandwiches on paper plates.* |
| Squinting: `Students who regularly revise improve.` | Place *regularly* beside the intended verb: *Students who revise regularly improve.* |

## PART 08 — Punctuation and Capitalisation — Core

Punctuation clarifies structure. In a correction question, preserve the
intended meaning and accept more than one standard punctuation choice where
context permits it.

## 1. Commas

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| items in a series | *data, staff, finance and time* |
| introductory phrase/clause | *After the meeting, we left.* |
| two complete clauses joined by *and, but, or, so, yet* | *The proposal was sound, but funding was absent.* |
| non-restrictive information | *The chairperson, who arrived late, apologised.* |
| direct address / answer | *Riya, please read; No, I disagree.* |
| contrast | *The decision was legal, not wise.* |

The serial/Oxford comma before `and` is a style choice: *data, staff, finance,
and time* and *data, staff, finance and time* are both standard. Use it when
it prevents ambiguity and stay consistent.

Do **not** put a comma:

- between subject and verb: `The causes of pollution, are…`;
- between verb and object: `The committee submitted, its report`;
- around an identifying/restrictive relative clause: *Students who practise
  regularly improve*;
- alone between two complete clauses (a comma splice): `The plan failed, we
  revised it`.

Repair a comma splice with a full stop, semicolon, colon where appropriate, or
a comma plus a coordinating conjunction. Context decides whether a relative
clause is essential; a sentence such as *My brother who lives in Pune…* cannot
be punctuated uniquely without knowing whether the writer has other brothers.

## 2. Semicolon, colon and dash

| Mark | Safe use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| semicolon | joins closely linked independent clauses; separates complex list items | *Evidence was incomplete; the inquiry continued.* |
| colon | introduces explanation, list, restatement or quotation after a complete lead-in | *The paper tests four abilities: comprehension, précis, usage and essay writing.* |
| dash | interruption, sharp addition or emphasis | *One point remained—the evidence was weak.* |

Avoid a colon that merely splits a verb or preposition from its necessary
object: `The abilities are: grammar and usage` is usually better as *The
abilities are grammar and usage* or *The abilities are as follows: grammar and
usage.* This is a **style and clarity preference, not an absolute grammar
ban**. A colon can follow a complete clause ending in a verb where the clause
genuinely announces what follows: *Her conclusion was clear: postpone the
launch.*

## 3. Apostrophes, quotation and terminal marks

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| singular possession | *the candidate's answer* |
| regular plural possession | *the candidates' answers* |
| irregular plural possession | *children's books* |
| contraction | *don't; it's (= it is/it has)* |
| possessive `its` | *The committee submitted its report.* |

Never use an apostrophe for an ordinary plural: `NGO's` → *NGOs*. An
apostrophe after a joint possession goes on the final name: *Ravi and
Mohan's office*; separate ownership takes both: *Ravi's and Mohan's offices*.

Use quotation marks for exact speech. Direct speech normally has a reporting
comma: *The minister said, “We must act now.”* In indirect speech remove
quotation marks and adjust grammar. British and US placement of a final full
stop relative to quotation marks can differ; follow the paper’s style unless
the quoted words themselves require it.

End statements with a full stop. Use `?` only for a direct question: *He
asked whether I was ready.* Avoid `!` in a formal answer except when quoting.

## 4. Hyphens, brackets and ellipsis

- Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun: *a well-written answer; a
  five-year plan*. Do not usually hyphenate after the verb: *the answer is well
  written; the plan is five years old*.
- Do not hyphenate an adverb ending in `-ly`: *a carefully written answer*.
- Brackets add non-essential clarification. Use sparingly in formal prose.
- An ellipsis shows omitted words in a quotation; it is not a substitute for
  planning a sentence.

## 5. Capitalisation

Capitalise the first word of a sentence, proper names, languages/nationalities,
days/months, named institutions, Acts and formally used titles: *India,
English, Monday, the Constitution of India, Parliament, the President of
India*. Use lower case for generic roles: *the minister spoke; a parliamentary
committee met*. Capitalisation does not make a common noun important.

## 6. Punctuation decision path

```text
Two complete clauses?
  ├─ joined by coordinating conjunction → comma + conjunction
  ├─ closely related without conjunction → semicolon or full stop
  └─ second explains first → colon (if first clause is complete)
Relative clause?
  ├─ identifies which person/thing → no commas
  └─ adds removable information → pair of commas
```

2. the proposal was ambitious however it lacked funding
3. the reports that were incomplete were returned
4. my only brother who lives in pune is a doctor
5. the inquiry had one conclusion postpone the launch
6. its important to check every candidates identity card

**Key:** 1 *After the interview, we returned home.* [PUNC]; 2 *The proposal
was ambitious; however, it lacked funding.* A full stop before *however* is
also accepted [PUNC]; 3 *The reports that were incomplete were returned.*
[REL]; 4 *My only brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.* [REL/CAP]; 5 *The

## PART 09 — Vocabulary, Spelling, Word Formation, Idioms and Proverbs — Core

Vocabulary questions test exact meaning, grammar and register. A “use in a
sentence” answer earns credit only if the context makes the intended sense
unmistakable.

## 1. Learn a word as a usable entry

For every new word record:

```text
headword | part of speech | plain meaning | common collocation
one near-synonym | one contrast/antonym | one original formal sentence
```

Example: **mitigate** (verb): make less severe; *mitigate a risk*; near
synonym *alleviate*; contrast *aggravate*. *Early warning systems mitigate
flood risk; they do not eliminate it.*

A synonym is rarely interchangeable in every setting. `Childish` and
`childlike` both relate to a child, but the first is usually disapproving and
the second often approving. Prefer a plain accurate word to an impressive
near-synonym with the wrong register.

| Register | Prefer | Avoid when formality needs precision |
|---|---|---|
| formal neutral | *assist, obtain, demonstrate, purchase* | casual *help out, get hold of, show off, buy up* |
| precise contrast | *economic* (economy) / *economical* (not wasteful) | treating them as synonyms |
| intensity | *alleviate* (reduce) / *eradicate* (remove completely) | claiming eradication when only reduction occurred |

## 2. Confusables and collocations

| Word 1 | Word 2 | Distinction/example |
|---|---|---|
| accept | except | receive/agree; excluding |
| advice (noun) | advise (verb) | *give advice; advise a candidate* |
| affect (usually verb) | effect (usually noun; also verb = bring about) | *affect outcomes; have an effect; effect change* |
| all ready | already | fully prepared; by now |
| altar | alter | religious table; change |
| appraise | apprise | assess value; inform: *apprise her of* |
| assure | ensure | reassure a person; make certain an outcome |
| complement | compliment | complete; praise |
| conscience | conscious | moral sense; aware |
| credible | credulous | believable; too ready to believe |
| discreet | discrete | tactful/unobtrusive; separate |
| economic | economical | relating to economy; saving money |
| eminent | imminent | distinguished; about to happen |
| farther | further | physical distance; additional/abstract (modern use overlaps) |
| principal | principle | chief/head or capital sum; rule |
| stationery | stationary | writing material; not moving |
| than | then | comparison; time/result |
| their/there/they're | possession/place/they are | *their files are there; they're ready* |
| who’s | whose | who is/who has; possession |
| climatic | climactic | climate-related; relating to climax |

## 3. Spelling: patterns, not guesses

| Pattern | Rule | Examples / caution |
|---|---|---|
| `-able/-ible` | no fully reliable sound rule; learn word families | *reliable, manageable; visible, permissible* |
| final silent `e` | usually drop before vowel suffix; retain before consonant suffix | *making, hopeful; hoped, safely* |
| `y` after consonant | change `y` to `i` before most suffixes, but not `-ing` | *carry → carried; happy → happier; carry → carrying* |
| doubled final consonant | one stressed vowel + one consonant before vowel suffix | *admit → admitted; prefer → preferred; travel → travelled/traveled* |
| `-cede/-ceed/-sede` | `-ceed` mainly *proceed, exceed, succeed* | *precede, recede; supersede* |

High-frequency spellings to retrieve exactly: **accommodate, accommodation,
achievement, argument, calendar, committee, definitely, discipline,
embarrass, environment, government, independent, maintenance, necessary,
noticeable, occurrence, privilege, receive, recommend, separate, successful,
tomorrow**. British and American variants may both be correct when used
consistently: *colour/color, organisation/organization, travelled/traveled*.

## 4. Word formation and grammatical fit

Identify the required part of speech from the slot before choosing a related
word. A verb after `to` is base form; an adjective commonly follows a linking
verb; a noun commonly follows a determiner or possessive.

| Base | Noun | Verb | Adjective | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| decide | decision | decide | decisive | decisively |
| succeed | success | succeed | successful | successfully |
| explain | explanation | explain | explanatory | — |
| benefit | benefit | benefit | beneficial | beneficially |
| differ | difference | differ | different | differently |
| govern | government | govern | governmental | — |
| create | creation | create | creative | creatively |
| approve | approval | approve | approved/approving | — |

Common negative prefixes are not freely interchangeable: *inaccurate,
illegal, impossible, irregular, non-essential*. Learn the completed word.
Common suffix signals: `-tion/-ment/-ness` (noun), `-ify/-ise/-ize` (verb),
`-ful/-less/-ive/-al` (adjective), `-ly` (often adverb). Check meaning:
`economic` is not the same as `economical`, and `historic` is not the same as
`historical`.

## 5. Synonyms, antonyms and definition questions

Give an antonym of the same grammatical class unless the question says
otherwise: *scarce → abundant; rigid → flexible; temporary → permanent;
transparent → opaque; voluntary → compulsory*. Several answers can be valid
when they preserve the required sense. For a one-word question, a key must
state an accepted set or accept a defensible equivalent; do not demand one
dictionary synonym where the stem gives no context.

For “use in a sentence,” show the word’s exact sense:

| Word | Weak | Creditworthy |
|---|---|---|
| impartial | *The judge was impartial.* | *The impartial judge heard both sides before giving reasons.* |
| feasible | *The plan is feasible.* | *The engineer showed that the bridge was feasible within the approved budget.* |
| reluctant | *She was reluctant.* | *Reluctant to sign without evidence, she asked for the full report.* |

## 6. Idioms and proverbs

An idiom has a conventional non-literal meaning; a proverb states a general
lesson. Use an idiom grammatically and a proverb only where it supports the
point.

| Expression | Meaning | Natural use |
|---|---|---|
| at sea | confused/uncertain | *Without the map, I was at sea.* |
| a thorn in the flesh | persistent irritation/problem | *The leak became a thorn in the administration's flesh.* |
| move heaven and earth | make every possible effort | *Officials moved heaven and earth to restore supply.* |
| step into someone's shoes | take over a role | *She stepped into her predecessor's shoes.* |
| know the ins and outs | know all details | *The clerk knows the ins and outs of the process.* |
| cut adrift | abandon or leave unsupported/isolated | *Small suppliers were cut adrift when credit stopped.* |
| cut corners | save time/money improperly by lowering standards | *The contractor cut corners on safety.* |
| a blessing in disguise | apparent misfortune later proves beneficial | *The delay was a blessing in disguise because it exposed the defect.* |
| A stitch in time saves nine. | early action prevents a bigger problem | — |
| Look before you leap. | consider consequences first | — |
| Actions speak louder than words. | conduct is stronger evidence than promises | — |
| All that glitters is not gold. | appearance can mislead | — |

### Mock-usage expressions

- **go berserk** — an informal verb phrase meaning *to become violently
  uncontrollable or behave wildly*, often from extreme anger, fear or
  excitement. Use it intransitively: `a person/crowd goes, went, has gone
  berserk`; do not use it for ordinary annoyance or as a transitive verb.
  *When the alarm malfunctioned, the frightened crowd went berserk and staff
  called for help.*
- **Where there is a will, there is a way.** — a fixed proverb meaning that
  determined effort and resolve can help a person find a solution. Use the
  complete sentence (or quote it); it is a motivating generalisation, not a
  guarantee that every obstacle can be overcome. *After the student mastered
  typing through persistent practice, her teacher said, “Where there is a
  will, there is a way.”*

## 7. Acquisition and timed retrieval

Use a **5–10–1** cycle: learn five entries, test after ten minutes without
looking, then use each once in a sentence the next day. Keep a two-column log:
`word confused` → `contrast and corrected sentence`. Do not rely on rereading.

In **8 minutes**, supply the requested form or choice:

1. The measure may ___ hardship. (alleviate/alleviation)
2. The committee reached a ___ after debate. (decide/decision)
3. The officer acted ___ during negotiations. (discreet/discrete)
4. The storm did not ___ the inland district. (affect/effect)
5. Correct: *The accomodation was definately insufficient.*
6. Give an antonym of *scarce*.
7. Use *cut corners* in a sentence.

**Key:** 1 *alleviate* [WF]; 2 *decision* [WF]; 3 *discreet* [CONF]; 4
*affect* [CONF]; 5 *The accommodation was definitely insufficient.* [SPL];
6 *abundant* (also *plentiful* in suitable context) [SYN]; 7 must mean
improperly reducing standards to save resources [IDIOM].

## PART 10 — Error Correction, Voice, Narration and Transformation — Core

## 1. Correction method and error codes

Read for meaning first, then scan in this order:

```text
finite verb/agreement → tense/conditional → noun/determiner → pronoun
→ verb pattern/preposition → word form/spelling → modifier/parallelism → punctuation
```

Make the smallest change that restores standard formal English. Do not mark an
accepted modern variant wrong merely because a traditional alternative exists.

| Code | Check |
|---|---|
| AGR | agreement/number |
| CNT | countability/determiner |
| PRO | pronoun case, reference or agreement |
| TNS | tense/aspect |
| COND | conditional/polarity |
| ART | article/determiner |
| PREP | preposition or verb pattern |
| PHR | phrasal-verb position/meaning |
| MOD | misplaced/dangling modifier |
| PAR | parallel structure |
| PUNC | punctuation/capital |
| SPL | spelling/confusable |
| WF | word form |
| VAR | acceptable-variant issue |

## 2. Frequent correction families

| Fault | Safe correction |
|---|---|
| `One of the applicant have…` | *One of the applicants has…* [AGR/CNT] |
| `furnitures/informations` | *furniture/information* or a measure phrase [CNT] |
| `She is senior than me` | *senior to me* [PREP] |
| `He discussed about the proposal` | *discussed the proposal* [PREP] |
| `I have been waiting since three hours` | *for three hours* [PREP] |
| `When I will arrive…` | *When I arrive…* [TNS] |
| `comprises of` | *comprises* / *consists of* [PREP] |
| `look at children` (care for) | *look after children* [PHR] |
| `more wiser` | *wiser* [WF] |
| `The woodcutter was falling trees` | *was felling trees* [WF] |

`The reason is because funding was delayed` occurs in modern English, but
some conservative examination keys reject it as redundant. To avoid a
marking dispute, write *The reason is that funding was delayed* or *Funding
was delayed because…*. A key must not present the original as objectively
ungrammatical without stating its house style [VAR].

## 3. Voice: preserve tense, object and meaning

Only a verb that has an object normally makes a passive. Move the object to
subject position; keep tense, aspect, modal and relevant adverbials.

| Active | Passive |
|---|---|
| *They write reports.* | *Reports are written.* |
| *They are writing a report.* | *A report is being written.* |
| *They wrote a report.* | *A report was written.* |
| *They had written a report.* | *A report had been written.* |
| *They have written a report.* | *A report has been written.* |
| *They will write a report.* | *A report will be written.* |
| *They can/must write a report.* | *A report can/must be written.* |

### Double objects, questions and imperatives

| Active | Accepted passive(s) |
|---|---|
| *The board gave Riya a prize.* | *Riya was given a prize.* / *A prize was given to Riya.* |
| *Someone has sent me the file.* | *I have been sent the file.* / *The file has been sent to me.* |
| *Did the officer sign the order?* | *Was the order signed by the officer?* |
| *Who wrote the letter?* | *By whom was the letter written?* (formal) |
| *Open the door.* | *Let the door be opened.* (formal, often unnatural) |
| *Do not disturb the patient.* | *Let the patient not be disturbed.* |

Omit `by + agent` when unknown, obvious or irrelevant: *The accused was
arrested.* Do not force passive with intransitives (*arrive, occur, sleep*) or
verbs that do not take the intended object. Prepositional passives are possible
where the preposition belongs to the verb: *The proposal was approved of* is
grammatical but often awkward; *The children were looked after* is natural.

## 4. Direct and indirect speech (narration)

First identify speaker, listener, reporting time and exact communicative
purpose. Pronouns, time/place words and tense change only when the new context
requires it.

| Direct form | Indirect pattern |
|---|---|
| statement: *He said, “I am tired.”* | *He said (that) he was tired.* |
| yes/no question: *She asked, “Are you ready?”* | *She asked whether/if I was ready.* |
| wh-question: *He asked, “Where do you live?”* | *He asked where I lived.* |
| command: *The officer said, “Close the door.”* | *The officer ordered me to close the door.* |
| request: *She said to me, “Please wait.”* | *She asked me to wait.* |
| negative command: *He said, “Do not move.”* | *He told us not to move.* |

In formal reported speech, a past reporting verb commonly backshifts:
`am/is → was; are → were; have/has done → had done; did → had done; will →
would; can → could`. Backshift is not automatic when the fact remains true,
the report is immediate, or the writer deliberately keeps present relevance:
*The teacher said that the Earth moves around the Sun.* Universal truths
normally remain present. Do not change a question into question word order:
`He asked where did I live` → *He asked where I lived.* `Said to` is usually
replaced by a purpose verb (*asked, told, advised, ordered*) in commands.

## 5. Meaning-preserving transformations: conditions first

A transformation is correct only if time, polarity, emphasis, agent and scope
remain the same. These are patterns, not automatic substitutions.

| Original and condition | Safe transformation |
|---|---|
| *As soon as the bell rang, students left* (past) | *No sooner had the bell rung than the students left.* |
| past event immediately followed by another | *Hardly/Scarcely had the rain stopped when/before work resumed.* |
| `if ... not` with equivalent positive condition | *Unless you practise, you will not improve.* |
| *so + adjective + that + subject cannot/could not + verb* | *too + adjective + for + object + to + verb*: *The box is too heavy for me to lift.* |
| *although + clause* | *In spite of/despite + noun phrase* or *in spite of/despite the fact that + clause*: *Despite being tired, he worked.* |
| two positive parallel facts | *He is not only honest but also efficient.* |
| two negative qualities of same subject | *She is neither careless nor dishonest.* |
| purpose | *He worked hard so that he could qualify* / *in order to qualify* (same subject only) |
| comparative degree | *No other metal is as useful as iron* → *Iron is the most useful metal* (only when the comparison set is unchanged) |
| `only` scope | *Only Meera checked the accounts* → *Meera checked only the accounts* changes focus from actor to object; use only when that exact changed focus is requested. |

Do not use an alleged `although → however + adjective/adverb` formula.
`However` is a conjunctive adverb and normally joins independent clauses:
*The task was difficult; however, she completed it.* It cannot simply replace
`although` at the start of a dependent clause.

`Being + adjective/past participle` must modify the grammatical subject:
*Being flooded, the road was closed* is valid. `Being a holiday, the office
was closed` dangles because an office is not a holiday. Use *As it was a
holiday, the office was closed* or the formal absolute *It being a holiday,
the office was closed.*

|---|---|

| *She is not ready* | *is she?* |

## PART 11 — Comprehension and Précis — MUST-DO

## 1. Comprehension: read the demand before answering

```text
Question command -> locate evidence -> identify answer type
-> list every requested limb -> answer in own concise words
-> check against passage, not outside knowledge
```

| Demand | What it asks | Safe answer move | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literal | Explicitly stated fact/reason | State the relevant claim and its stated reason | Copying an entire paragraph |
| Inferential | What follows from two or more clues | State the conclusion, then name the textual clues | Treating a plausible opinion as evidence |
| Tone/attitude | Author’s stance, not topic | Use a precise adjective plus two language/idea signals | “The tone is about pollution” |
| Purpose | Why the author uses an example/contrast | State its function in the argument | Retelling the example |
| Title | Central organising claim | Give a short title and justify it with coverage | Choosing a decorative phrase |
| Reference | What *this/it/they/such* denotes | Name the nearest logical antecedent and test the sentence | Naming the nearest noun mechanically |
| Vocabulary in context | Meaning in this passage | Give a context-fitting synonym and explain its local sense | Dictionary meaning that conflicts with context |
| Multipart | Two or more asked elements | Number or separate all limbs; allocate space to each | Answering only the first limb |

### Command decoder

| Wording | Deliverable |
|---|---|
| Why / how | Cause or mechanism, not merely result |
| Explain | Meaning plus the passage’s support |
| Distinguish | At least two contrasted bases |
| What does the author mean by… | Paraphrase the phrase and connect it to context |
| Comment / critically observe | A qualified inference anchored in the passage |
| Enumerate | All major listed points, compressed and complete |

### Multi-part allocation rule

Circle **every** noun joined by *and*, *or*, commas or a semicolon. Make a micro-plan before writing:

```text
"What financial and institutional causes …, and what follows?"
financial cause(s) | institutional cause(s) | consequence
        5 marks    |          5 marks     |    5 marks
```

The split printed in a practice key is a coaching diagnostic, not an official UPSC marking scheme. For a 15-mark response, give one compact, supported unit for every requested limb before adding explanation.

## 2. Answer calibration

- Answer from the passage even if outside knowledge differs.
- Use complete sentences unless the question specifically asks for a list.
- Start with the answer, not “The author says that…”.
- For an inference, use calibrated language: *suggests, implies, indicates*.
- Quote only a crucial word or phrase; paraphrase the rest.
- A title answer needs both a title and a one-sentence justification if the question asks for either.
- If the question asks for tone, do not call a serious argument “sad” or “informative”; identify the stance: *cautious, critical, qualified, ironic, approving, sceptical*.

## 3. Worked comprehension diagnostic

### Passage

Many municipal offices have begun to publish the number of complaints they close each week. The practice can expose neglect, but it can also mislead. A complaint may be marked closed when an officer has sent an automatic reply, transferred it to another department or asked the citizen to submit the same document again. Such actions may be necessary at times, yet they do not show that the underlying problem has been solved.

The useful question is therefore not simply how quickly a file leaves a desk. It is whether the service becomes usable. A streetlight is not repaired because a message has been acknowledged; it is repaired when it works. This distinction matters especially to people who cannot repeatedly visit an office, make phone calls during working hours or pay a private provider. Public data should consequently record both the first response and the final outcome. Otherwise, an impressive dashboard may reward administrative movement while concealing public inaction.

### Questions

1. Why can a high complaint-closure number be misleading? **(4)**
2. What distinction does the streetlight example make? **(3)**
3. What does “administrative movement” mean in context? **(3)**
4. Infer the author’s attitude to dashboards that report only closures. Give two grounds from the passage. **(3 + 2)**
5. Give a suitable title and justify it in one sentence. **(3)**

### Key with diagnostic splits

1. It may record an automatic reply, transfer or repeat-document request **(2)** even though the underlying service failure remains unsolved **(2)**.
2. It separates acknowledging a complaint **(1)** from fixing the actual service **(2)**.
3. It means procedural handling of a file—replying, transferring or recording it—without necessarily delivering a solution **(3)**.
4. The author is **critical but qualified** **(1)**: dashboards can expose neglect **(1)**, but closure-only reporting may reward activity rather than outcomes **(1)**. Any two grounded signals earn the evidence marks **(2)**.
5. **“Closure Is Not Repair”** **(1)** is suitable because the passage contrasts recorded disposal with a usable public service **(2)**.

## 4. Précis: preserve the argument, not the surface

A précis is an accurate, coherent compression of the whole passage to the instructed proportion, normally about one-third in the local paper samples.

```text
Read twice -> mark thesis / idea units / concession / conclusion
-> calculate target -> remove illustration and repetition
-> paraphrase -> connect -> count -> obey title instruction
```

### Idea-unit map

| Passage unit | Keep? | Why / treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis or central tension | Always | Opens the précis |
| Major reason, mechanism or consequence | Usually | One compressed sentence per major unit |
| Concession (*although, yet, while*) | Usually | Preserve the author’s qualification |
| Example, name, quotation, rhetorical question | Usually remove | Retain only if it carries an irreplaceable distinction |
| Repetition, decoration, minor detail | Remove | Does not change argument |
| Final recommendation/verdict | Always | Close the précis |

### Explicit word-count method

1. Count the source’s space-separated words for practice; count a hyphenated form as one word and do not count a title.
2. Divide by the instructed ratio. For one-third, a 360-word source gives a target of about 120 words.
3. Record **source → target → final** on rough work.
4. “About” is not a published tolerance. For this kit’s practice, aim within roughly 10% of the calculated target; if the paper gives a number or tolerance, that instruction overrides this coaching convention.
5. Count the final draft again after revision. Do not cut a central concession merely to reach the number.

### Paraphrase and coherence controls

- Change the sentence structure as well as isolated words.
- Retain technical terms where replacing them would reduce accuracy.
- Use indirect speech for a quoted claim.
- Keep the author’s order unless rearrangement clearly improves logic.
- Link units with logical connectors: *however, therefore, consequently, although*.
- Do not add opinion, an example, a moral or a title that the instruction forbids.

### Title-instruction variants

| Instruction | Action |
|---|---|
| “Do not give a title” | Write no heading at all. |
| “Do not give or suggest a title” | Write no heading and do not embed a title-like label. |
| “Give a suitable title” | Supply a short, accurate organising phrase after drafting; do not spend words on ornament. |
| No title instruction | Follow the exact paper wording; do not assume a title is rewarded. |

## 5. Worked précis: idea units, proportion and concession

### Source passage

Online meetings have made it easier for organisations to assemble people who live far apart. They can reduce travel, allow a specialist to join briefly and create a written record of decisions. These advantages explain why a video link is now often treated as a complete substitute for physical presence.

That conclusion is too simple. A meeting does more than transmit information. Participants notice hesitation, continue a discussion after the formal agenda and form informal relationships that make later cooperation easier. Digital meetings can reproduce some of these functions, especially when participants already know one another and the task is narrowly defined. They are less reliable when a group must build trust, settle a sensitive disagreement or learn a complex skill together.

The choice should therefore depend on the purpose rather than on fashion. Organisations should use online meetings for routine coordination, while protecting occasional in-person work for tasks requiring trust, observation and sustained collaboration. The goal is not to reject technology, but to use it without pretending that all forms of presence are identical.

### Idea-unit map

| Unit | Retain in model |
|---|---|
| Online meetings widen access and reduce travel | Yes |
| They are often treated as a full substitute | Yes |
| Meetings also build trust and informal cooperation | Yes |
| Digital works for known groups/narrow tasks but not all tasks | Yes, as concession |
| Purpose-based mixed use is the verdict | Yes |

### Model précis

Online meetings reduce travel and widen access, so they are often treated as substitutes for physical meetings. However, meetings build trust and informal cooperation. Digital formats suit known groups handling limited tasks but are less dependable for disputes, complex learning or new relationships. Organisations should choose the format by purpose, retaining in-person work where collaboration and trust are essential.

## PART 12 — Short Essay Writing — MUST-DO

## 1. Choose the question you can qualify and develop

The 600-word essay is a continuous argument, not a GS answer copied under headings. Choose the topic for which you can write a balanced thesis, four distinct paragraphs and safe examples in clear English.

| Choice test | Ask before choosing | Reject the topic if… |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Can I restate every key word? | A key abstraction remains vague |
| Position | Can I make a qualified claim, not a slogan? | I can only agree or disagree absolutely |
| Development | Can I see four non-repeating moves? | All points are synonyms |
| Examples | Can I use ordinary, defensible illustrations? | I would need invented statistics or quotations |
| Language | Can I explain it in plain English? | I would rely on memorised ornament |

## 2. Decode the topic and form a qualified thesis

```text
Topic -> key terms -> hidden tension -> qualified position
      -> reasons / consequence / limitation -> direction
```

| Prompt | Decode | Weak thesis | Qualified thesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Convenience without responsibility weakens citizenship. | Convenience, responsibility, citizenship | Convenience is bad. | Convenience can expand access, but when it removes responsibility for shared consequences it turns citizens into passive consumers. |
| Technology can widen opportunity as well as inequality. | Technology, opportunity, inequality, “as well as” | Technology is useful. | Technology expands reach only where affordability, skills, accessible design and accountable institutions prevent existing disadvantages from being digitised. |
| Reading is an exercise in freedom. | Reading, exercise, freedom | Reading gives freedom. | Reading enlarges freedom by exposing a mind to alternatives, but this freedom requires time, literacy and the willingness to question one’s own assumptions. |

## 3. Plan in six minutes

| Minute | Action | Output |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Decode the wording | Key terms and scope |
| 2 | Write one-sentence thesis | Claim + qualification |
| 3–4 | Build four body moves | Reason, mechanism, consequence, response |
| 5 | Add counterargument/limit | A real concession, not “everything has pros and cons” |
| 6 | Choose conclusion direction | Return to thesis with a practical judgement |

### Reliable architecture

```text
INTRODUCTION: interpret topic + qualified thesis
BODY 1: first reason/mechanism
BODY 2: second reason/consequence
BODY 3: counterargument, limit or trade-off
BODY 4: response, condition or institutional implication
CONCLUSION: reasoned final judgement
```

Each body paragraph uses:

```text
claim -> explanation -> safe example -> link back to thesis
```

## 4. Drafting control

- Open with the issue, not a quotation, dictionary definition or “Since ancient times”.
- State the thesis by the end of the introduction.
- Give each paragraph one controlling idea and a linking first sentence.
- Use *however, therefore, for example, consequently, yet* only where the logic requires them.
- Make the counterargument meaningful, then answer or limit it.
- Use first person sparingly; avoid rhetorical questions and exclamation marks.
- Prefer a 12–25 word sentence; split a sentence when the subject, verb or reference becomes unclear.

## 5. Examples: useful, safe and analytical

| Example type | Safe use | Unsafe use |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday public-service illustration | “A missed complaint response can make a service unusable for a worker who cannot return repeatedly.” | Invented percentage or programme outcome |
| Broad, established social example | “A library permits a reader to encounter views beyond the immediate community.” | Unverified quotation attributed to a famous person |
| Personal/observed illustration | Use briefly to illuminate a general point | Replace the argument with autobiography |
| Current example | Use only if source/date are verified and necessary | Guess a statistic, year, budget or law |

An example earns its place only if you explain **why it supports the claim**. One precise illustration is better than a list of names.

## 6. Word-count and revision checkpoints

For practice, count space-separated words; treat a hyphenated expression as one word. A title is not part of the count. This is a coaching method, not a published UPSC count convention.

| Stage | Approximate running count | Check |
|---|---:|---|
| Introduction | 70–90 | Thesis is visible |
| Four body paragraphs | 420–460 | Each has a distinct job |
| Conclusion | 60–80 | Verdict follows from argument |
| Final | About 600 | Count, then remove repetition rather than substance |

### Five-minute revision sweep

1. Underline the thesis and the counterargument. If either is missing, repair it first.
2. Check that each paragraph advances—not repeats—the thesis.
3. Remove one decorative sentence and one unsupported claim.
4. Check agreement, tense, articles, prepositions, commas and pronoun reference.
5. Count words; cut duplicate examples before cutting the conclusion.

## 7. Annotated model essay 1

### Prompt: *Public institutions earn trust through everyday conduct.*

Public trust is often discussed after a spectacular failure or a major announcement. Yet citizens usually form their judgement through ordinary encounters: whether an application is acknowledged, whether a bus arrives when it is expected, whether a hospital explains a delay, and whether an official treats a complainant with respect. Public institutions cannot avoid difficult decisions, but they earn trust when their daily conduct is reliable, intelligible and fair.

Reliability is the first condition. A rule may be sound on paper, but it inspires little confidence if its operation depends on the citizen knowing whom to call or how often to visit an office. Predictable hours, clear documents and timely responses reduce this uncertainty. They also matter most to people with little spare time or money. For a salaried worker, a delayed certificate may mean more than inconvenience; it may require a lost day’s wage. Regular service therefore communicates that public authority recognises the cost of delay borne by ordinary people.

Trust also depends on explanation. Not every request can be granted, and not every public service can be immediate. A reasoned refusal, however, is different from silence. When an institution states the rule, identifies the missing information and indicates a route for review, it allows the citizen to understand a decision even while disagreeing with it. This does not make the decision automatically just. It does make power more accountable because the official must connect an action to reasons that others can examine.

Fair conduct is equally important. A queue loses legitimacy when people believe that influence, familiarity or persistence matters more than the announced rule. Equal treatment does not mean ignoring genuine differences of need; a hospital may properly prioritise an emergency. It means that a difference must rest on a relevant reason rather than personal preference. Visible reasons, simple procedures and an accessible complaint channel help distinguish justified priority from favouritism.

Everyday conduct also produces information that an institution needs in order to improve. A complaint is not merely an inconvenience to be cleared from a register. Repeated questions about one form may show that its language is unclear. Frequent visits to the same counter may reveal that offices have divided a process in a way that citizens cannot understand. Staff who listen carefully can turn these encounters into modest corrections before frustration becomes distrust. Publishing what has changed is important: it tells the public that speaking up has an effect without pretending that every request can be accepted.

Consistency is crucial because one courteous encounter cannot compensate for a process that is unpredictable elsewhere. Citizens judge an institution across connected steps: notice, application, response, appeal and final service. A clear first step loses value if the next office cannot find the record. Everyday trust is therefore cumulative.

There is a limit to what everyday conduct can achieve. Trust cannot be manufactured by polite language if an institution is corrupt, chronically underfunded or directed by an unjust policy. Nor should citizens trust without question. Scrutiny, independent review and a free press remain necessary precisely because institutions exercise power. Still, these safeguards work better when routine records are accurate and staff do not treat the public as an interruption.

The strongest public institutions are not those that promise perfection. They are those whose ordinary actions allow citizens to predict what will happen, ask why it happened and seek correction when it goes wrong. Such conduct turns authority from a distant claim into an experienced public relationship. Trust then becomes neither blind loyalty nor public relations, but a reasonable response to dependable and fair administration.

**Practice count:** 597 words (title excluded).  
**Annotation:** Thesis in paragraph 1; reliability, explanation and fairness are distinct mechanisms; paragraph 6 is a genuine limitation; conclusion gives a qualified verdict.  
**Coaching score:** 88/100 — relevance/thesis 18, organisation 18, development/balance 22, grammar/vocabulary 23, conclusion/count discipline 7. The band is an internal model, not an official marking key.

## 8. Annotated model essay 2

### Prompt: *Technology can widen opportunity as well as inequality.*

Technology is often praised as if access to a device automatically creates opportunity. A phone can indeed connect a student to a lesson, a worker to a vacancy and a patient to information that was previously distant. But the same system can exclude people who lack a stable connection, usable language, confidence with forms or protection from error. Technology widens opportunity only when its design and surrounding institutions prevent old disadvantages from being reproduced in a digital form.

Its potential is real because distance and time can be reduced. A learner in a small town can consult material that would once have been available only in a large library. A person seeking work can learn about openings without travelling repeatedly. Digital payments can make small transactions easier to record and complete. In each case, the benefit is not the screen itself but the wider reach of information and services. Technology becomes valuable when it lowers a genuine barrier.

However, a new channel can create a new gatekeeper. An online application that assumes fluent English, constant data access and the ability to upload documents may be simpler for the office but harder for the applicant. A disabled user may be excluded if a site cannot be read by assistive tools. An automated decision can also appear neutral while repeating biased data or leaving no clear path to challenge an error. Efficiency for the provider is therefore not proof of accessibility for the public.

The answer is not to reject technology or to preserve every paper process. It is to build alternatives and accountability into the system. Assisted help desks, clear language, offline routes for essential services and a human appeal channel can prevent a digital process from becoming a barrier. Designers should test a service with people who have different levels of literacy, income and physical access. Institutions must also explain how data are used and correct mistakes promptly, because a fast error can harm more people than a slow one.

Access must also be understood as a continuing condition rather than a one-time delivery of a device or an application. A service may be technically available but practically inaccessible when it consumes costly data, changes without warning or requires repeated authentication that a user cannot complete. Public systems should therefore measure successful completion and user understanding, not only registration or download. This approach does not make every service equally easy, but it makes exclusion visible early enough for a remedy.

The same discipline applies to private platforms that increasingly mediate ordinary life. Their convenience should not excuse confusing consent, unexplained recommendations or systems that leave a person unable to correct a damaging record. People need enough information and practical control to understand the choices they are making. This is not a demand that every user become a technical expert. It is a demand that powerful systems do not convert complexity into helplessness.

Some inequality would exist even without technology, and no application can solve unequal schooling, income or social power by itself. This is precisely why exaggerated promises are dangerous. A digital service should be judged by whether it expands a person’s real ability to learn, work or obtain a service, not by the number of downloads or transactions it records.

Technology is therefore neither an automatic equaliser nor an inevitable source of division. It is a tool whose social effect depends on access, design and accountability. When these conditions are taken seriously, digital systems can extend opportunity. When they are ignored, technology gives existing inequality a quicker and less visible form.

**Practice count:** 593 words (title excluded).  
**Annotation:** The thesis qualifies both sides; paragraphs 2–5 move from benefit to mechanism of exclusion to response; paragraph 6 limits technological claims.  
**Coaching score:** 87/100 — relevance/thesis 18, organisation 18, development/balance 22, grammar/vocabulary 22, conclusion/count discipline 7.
