## 1. Scope, central question and ownership firewall

Topic 20 explains Jahangir's reign (**1605-1627**) through five connected
problems:

1. accession and the early succession challenge;
2. consolidation in Mewar, Bengal and Kangra;
3. Nur Jahan's household authority and the disputed fixed-junta thesis;
4. the rebellions of Khurram and Mahabat Khan; and
5. Jahangir's governing, religious, artistic and autobiographical persona.

The exam-safe central proposition is:

> ⚠️ Jahangir preserved and extended much of Akbar's fiscal-military and
> composite political order, but succession rivalry, frontier strain and the
> increasing political weight of the imperial household made the later reign
> more contested. This is **consolidation under pressure**, not decline from
> 1605 and not sovereign replacement by Nur Jahan.

## 2. Master chronology

| Date | Event | Correct analytical use |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ 1599-1604 | Salim maintained a separate political base at Allahabad; coins and appointments expressed autonomy | accession background, not fixed personality |
| ✅ 1602 | Bir Singh Deo Bundela killed Abu'l Fazl in an action connected to Salim | pre-accession conflict; do not import full Topic 15 narrative |
| ✅ 1605 | Salim succeeded Akbar as Jahangir | institutional continuity required political settlement |
| ✅ 1606 | Khusrau rebelled; Guru Arjan was punished and died | succession, political suspicion, religion and source disagreement |
| ✅ 1608 | Islam Khan became governor of Bengal | beginning of decisive eastern consolidation |
| ✅ 1611 | Jahangir married Mihr-un-Nisa, later known as Nur Jahan | household network moved closer to sovereignty |
| ✅ 1613-15 | Khurram's pressure helped produce the Mewar settlement | force plus honour-sensitive accommodation |
| ✅ 1615-19 | Sir Thomas Roe represented James I at Jahangir's court | commercial diplomacy within Mughal sovereignty |
| ✅ 1620 | Kangra fort surrendered; Khurram's Deccan standing was high | consolidation remained uneven |
| ✅ 1621 | Khusrau died at Burhanpur while in Khurram's custody; Itimad-ud-Daulah died late in the year/early 1622 in narrative sequencing | succession and household balance sharpened |
| ✅ 1622 | Safavids took Qandahar; Khurram refused immediate compliance with the relief command and conflict escalated | bounded external trigger for internal crisis |
| ✅ 1622-25/early 1626 | Khurram's rebellion distracted the empire | multi-causal princely rebellion |
| ✅ 1626 | Mahabat Khan seized Jahangir near the Jhelum; Nur Jahan helped reverse the coup | emperor's custody was not control of the state |
| ✅ 1627 | Jahangir died near Lahore; Asaf Khan secured Khurram's succession and Nur Jahan retired | bridge to Topic 21, not its full narrative |

**Chronology traps**

- Khusrau's revolt and Guru Arjan's death belong to **1606**, not 1605.
- Nur Jahan's marriage in 1611 did not instantly create a proven permanent
  coalition.
- Her most active crisis politics belongs chiefly **after 1622**.
- Khurram's rebellion is not identical with the later war of succession of
  1657-58.

## 3. Accession: continuity required political work

✅ Salim was Akbar's surviving eldest son, but succession was not
constitutionally automatic. His earlier Allahabad establishment, autonomous
appointments, coinage and connection to Abu'l Fazl's killing had damaged trust.
Raja Man Singh and Mirza Aziz Koka briefly considered Khusrau, but the proposal
did not acquire decisive noble or Akbarian support.

✅ Jahangir did not conduct a general purge of Khusrau, Man Singh or Aziz Koka
at accession. He nevertheless remained wary of some established Akbar-era
nobles and promoted trusted associates such as Shaikh Farid Bukhari. Mirza
Ghiyas Beg, later Itimad-ud-Daulah, entered the high financial-administrative
structure through service as well as kinship.

⚠️ Accession should therefore be written as a bargain:

`dynastic claim + noble recognition + rewards/offices + public justice language
+ continued revenue-military machinery`.

This was neither modern hereditary law nor a free election by nobles.

## 4. Twelve orders and the chain of justice

✅ The *Jahangirnama* records early regulations commonly called Jahangir's
twelve orders. They address such matters as unofficial cesses, merchant
property, inheritance, sarais, forcible seizure of houses or peasant land,
hospitals and mutilation as punishment.

Use three evidentiary levels:

1. **textual proclamation** — the memoir records a benevolent programme;
2. **administrative transmission** — an order could be sent to officials; and
3. **local implementation** — requires separate evidence and cannot be assumed
   empire-wide.

✅ Jahangir's **zanjir-i-adl**, the chain of justice with bells at the Agra
riverfront/fort setting, projected direct royal accessibility.

⚠️ It was a symbol and possible petition route in a ruler-centred monarchy.
It was not a modern independent judiciary, equality before impersonal law or
proof that every subject could bypass local power.

✅ Satish Chandra also records a case in which an enquiry into an outrage
connected with Muqarrab Khan's establishment led to punishment and reduction
of the governor's mansab. ⚠️ One exemplary case proves the ideal and an
intervention, not uniform justice.

## 5. Khusrau's revolt and Guru Arjan: event before communal story

### Khusrau, 1606

✅ Six months after accession, Khusrau escaped with a small following and moved
toward Lahore. Support grew on the route, but Lahore's governor Dilawar Khan
kept the city closed. Jahangir defeated him near Bhairowal and captured him
while he attempted to move toward Afghanistan.

✅ The response included severe punishment of followers and penalties against
persons believed to have assisted or blessed the rebel. Khusrau was later
blinded after another alleged conspiracy and remained a focus of intrigue
until his death in 1621.

⚠️ The correct causal frame is:

`unsettled accession + princely claim + mobile followers + noble/regional
calculation + Punjab-Lahore routes + exemplary imperial punishment`.

Do not turn the revolt into a Hindu-Muslim or Sikh-Muslim war.

### Guru Arjan, 1606

✅ Jahangir's memoir represents Guru Arjan as a religious leader with Hindu and
Muslim followers and alleges that he blessed or materially assisted Khusrau.
The imperial narrative treats this as politically culpable.

✅ Sikh traditions and Jesuit/other testimony preserve different emphases,
including martyrdom, fine and torture. Satish Chandra notes disagreement over
whether the original order was direct execution or a heavy fine whose coercive
realisation caused death.

⚠️ A defensible answer keeps four dimensions separate:

- political suspicion after rebellion;
- Jahangir's hostile religious language;
- fine, confiscation and punishment;
- Sikh community memory and later consequences.

The death was a major turning point in Mughal-Sikh relations. It did not
instantly produce the later fully militarised Sikh conflict; that requires the
chronology of Guru Hargobind and subsequent Gurus.

## 6. Territorial consolidation: Mewar, Bengal and Kangra

### Mewar, 1613-15

✅ Jahangir intensified an inherited conflict by operating from Ajmer and
entrusting Prince Khurram with renewed pressure. The 1615 settlement recognised
Mughal suzerainty.

✅ Core terms and practices:

- Rana Amar Singh was not compelled to attend the Mughal court personally;
- a son or brother was expected to serve;
- Karan Singh was received with honour and integrated through mansab/service;
- the Rana house retained Mewar rather than being displaced by direct
  provincial annexation; and
- rebuilding Chittor's fortifications was restricted in the settlement
  tradition.

⚠️ The mechanism was:

`Mughal military pressure + Mewar's exhaustion + protection of Sisodia honour
+ unequal service relationship = durable accommodation`.

It was not equality, annexation or generosity without coercive leverage.

### Bengal and the eastern field

✅ Akbar had weakened Afghan power but not fully settled eastern Bengal.
Jahangir appointed **Islam Khan** in 1608. Islam Khan shifted the provincial
centre to **Dacca**, won over several zamindars, defeated the Barah-Bhuiyan
confederacy associated with Musa Khan and then broke Usman Khan's resistance.

✅ Jessore, Sylhet, Cachar and Kamrup entered the expanding Mughal field, while
an attack on the Ahoms failed. Defeated chiefs were later treated selectively
with conciliation; some estates were restored, and Afghans entered the Mughal
nobility in larger numbers.

⚠️ This was layered consolidation through campaign, alliance, headquarters,
release and service. It was not one-date conquest of all Bengal and Assam.

### Kangra, 1620

✅ A 1615 attempt failed. In 1620 a campaign under Raja Bikramajit Baghela
secured the formidable Kangra fort; a Mughal commander and faujdar were placed
there. Jahangir visited in 1622, had the khutba read, ordered a mosque and
performed sovereignty through religious-political symbolism.

✅ Chamba's submission followed in this changed hill balance.

⚠️ Kangra proves fort-level strategic and symbolic success, not effortless
administrative control of every Himalayan polity.

### Bounded Deccan context

✅ Jahangir-period pressure under Khurram checked Malik Ambar and helped produce
a 1620 settlement, but Jahangir deliberately did not pursue unlimited
annexation. Khurram's later rebellion enabled Deccani recovery.

The complete campaign, treaty and state-system narrative remains Topic 18.

## 7. English commercial diplomacy: Hawkins and Roe

### William Hawkins and the maritime setting

✅ Hawkins reached Jahangir's court as an English East India Company
representative seeking commercial access in the Surat world. His personal
access did not produce secure, unlimited privileges.

✅ The English naval success against Portuguese forces near
**Swally/Suvali in 1612** altered bargaining conditions on the Gujarat coast.

⚠️ This did not transfer Mughal sovereignty, make England an equal imperial
ally or predetermine British conquest.

### Sir Thomas Roe, 1615-19

✅ Roe was the ambassador of **James I** at Jahangir's court. His journal
records audiences, gifts, rank disputes, delays and the commercial objectives
of the English Company.

✅ The 1618 farman/permissions should be described as limited trading
facilities and confirmation of the English position, especially around Surat,
on Mughal terms.

They did **not** grant:

- territorial sovereignty;
- an empire-wide exclusive monopoly;
- a private territorial state;
- immunity from every local authority; or
- a constitutional beginning of British rule.

⚠️ Roe and Hawkins are encounter sources with commercial and European
assumptions. Compare them with farmans, factory records and local practice.

## 8. Nur Jahan: biography, authority and evidence

### Biography without romance

✅ Mihr-un-Nisa was born into the migrant family of Mirza Ghiyas Beg. She first
married Ali Quli Istajlu, known as Sher Afghan. After his death in a clash in
Bengal, she lived at the imperial court and married Jahangir in 1611.

✅ Later chroniclers' claim that Jahangir engineered Sher Afghan's death
because of an earlier romance is not accepted by Satish Chandra as secure
history.

### Household connections

| Person | Connection and political relevance |
|---|---|
| Ghiyas Beg / Itimad-ud-Daulah | father; experienced official and wazir |
| Asmat Begum | mother; part of the household network |
| Asaf Khan | brother; major noble and father of Arjumand Banu |
| Arjumand Banu / Mumtaz Mahal | Asaf Khan's daughter; married Khurram |
| Ladli Begum | Nur Jahan's daughter from Sher Afghan; married Shahryar |

Kinship created channels of access and succession politics. It did not make
every relative's interest identical.

### Forms of authority

✅ Evidence associates Nur Jahan with:

- command of the imperial household;
- reception of messengers and petitioners;
- influence over patronage and appointments;
- farmans issued in her name;
- coin legends using **Badshah Begum** and linked authority;
- hunting and travel with Jahangir;
- architectural, garden, dress and charitable patronage; and
- active political manoeuvre after 1622.

⚠️ Farmans and coins establish unusually public and formalised female
authority. They do not prove that she made every decision, monopolised every
promotion or constitutionally replaced Jahangir.

⚠️ Gender interpretation must be specific: she was an exceptional power-holder
working through a patriarchal imperial household. Her career did not abolish
patriarchy or create general political equality for women.

## 9. The disputed “Nur Jahan junta”

### Beni Prasad's thesis

⚠️ Beni Prasad proposed a stable junta of Nur Jahan, Itimad-ud-Daulah, Asaf
Khan and Khurram, dominant after 1611. In this model, its favour became the
principal route to promotion, court politics divided into two camps, and Nur
Jahan later turned from Khurram to Shahryar.

### Nurul Hasan and Satish Chandra's critique

✅/⚠️ The critique stresses:

- promotions remained distributed among several noble families;
- Mahabat Khan and other nobles retained favour outside the supposed bloc;
- no secure contemporary evidence proves a stable Nur Jahan-Khurram alliance
  for 1611-20;
- Khurram's rise followed his Mewar and Deccan performance, not only Nur
  Jahan's patronage; and
- political, service, regional and household alignments cut across a simple
  Persian-versus-old-nobility division.

✅ Irfan Habib's evidence for the family's extensive offices and provincial
governorships confirms real concentration of family influence. ⚠️ It still
does not prove that the entire nobility divided into two permanent camps.

### Best periodisation

1. **1611-21:** strong household influence and family advancement, but fluid
   alignments and continuing Jahangirian decision-making.
2. **After 1622:** Itimad-ud-Daulah's death, Jahangir's declining health,
   Qandahar, Khurram's rebellion and Mahabat's challenge pushed Nur Jahan into
   active crisis politics.

The fixed-junta model should be set aside, not Nur Jahan's power.

## 10. Khurram's rebellion, 1622-25/26

✅ Khurram's Mewar and Deccan successes, unprecedented rank, title **Shah
Jahan**, Hisar-Firuza jagir and proximity to the throne made him the strongest
princely contender. He obtained custody of Khusrau, who died at Burhanpur in
1621.

✅ The 1622 Qandahar emergency required a commander. Khurram feared a long
absence from court and demanded command, control of Punjab and security for his
family. Conflict also arose over jagirs, troops and Shahryar's elevation.

The causal matrix is:

`succession anxiety + strong Deccan military base + Qandahar command dispute +
jagir/resource bargaining + Shahryar-Ladli marriage + emperor-prince
correspondence + shifting noble support`.

✅ Khurram moved toward Agra but was defeated at Bilochpur in 1623. He then
moved through eastern India and the Deccan, winning temporary control in parts
of Orissa, Bengal and Bihar before further defeat by Mahabat Khan. He finally
submitted and sent Dara and Aurangzeb as hostages; Balaghat was assigned to him.

✅ Consequences included distraction for several years, weakened coordination
over Qandahar, Deccani recovery and sharper succession politics.

⚠️ “Nur Jahan caused the rebellion” is a monocausal trap. Her crisis politics
and support for Shahryar mattered, but so did the emperor, frontier command,
princely resources and Khurram's own ambition.

## 11. Mahabat Khan's coup de main, 1626

✅ Mahabat Khan had led the imperial response to Khurram. His later transfer,
separation from Prince Parvez, demand to submit accounts and elephants, and
summons to court created a grievance and fear environment.

✅ With a trusted Rajput force he seized Jahangir while the royal camp was
crossing the **Jhelum** on the road to Kabul. Nur Jahan's first armed effort
failed; she then entered the captive camp, manoeuvred politically and helped
detach nobles from Mahabat.

✅ Within months Mahabat's position collapsed. He had the emperor's person but
not stable control of the treasury, administration, broad nobility or a rival
legitimacy.

⚠️ Structural lesson:

`capture of embodied sovereign -> immediate command leverage`

but

`no bureaucracy + no durable coalition + no fiscal base + unpopular coercive
force -> no control of empire`.

The episode shows vulnerability in personalised monarchy, not completed Mughal
decline.

## 12. Jahangir as ruler: administration, nobility and religion

### Ruling class and consolidation

✅ Jahangir broadly retained Akbar's composite nobility. He raised major Rajput
rulers beyond the Kachhwahas, inducted Afghans such as Khan-i-Jahan Lodi into
high office and began enrolling leading Maratha sardars.

⚠️ Court and household politics changed access to the ruler, but the mansab,
jagir, provincial and revenue structures did not disappear. Their later
Shah-Jahan-period evolution belongs to Topic 21.

### Religious policy: continuity with contextual coercion

✅ Jahangir continued much of **sulh-i-kul**:

- he did not restore jizya;
- he prohibited forcible conversion in the Jahangiri rules;
- he celebrated or attended multiple Hindu and Persianate festivals;
- he supported temples, brahmans and religious establishments;
- Christians retained worship and church-building space;
- he met Muslim saints and Hindu thinkers, notably the Vedantin **Jadrup**;
- his memoir praises a realm in which different faiths could coexist.

✅ Yet coercive and exclusionary episodes also occurred:

- Guru Arjan's punishment and death;
- jihad language and temple destruction in parts of the Mewar campaign;
- Islamic sovereignty ritual at Kangra;
- destruction of a Varaha image at Pushkar, while other temples survived and
  Pushkar brahmans received a grant;
- a 1617 anti-Jain order in Gujarat which inscriptional/Jain evidence suggests
  was not implemented uniformly; and
- imprisonment of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi for a period.

⚠️ Therefore neither “perfect tolerance” nor “religious bigotry” is adequate.
Distinguish declaration, political context, locality, implementation and
reversal.

## 13. Art, naturalism and ruler-specific culture

✅ Jahangir was a connoisseur of painting, gardens, architecture, animals and
plants. His memoir records close natural observation; **Ustad Mansur** is
associated with precise studies of flora and fauna.

✅ Nur Jahan's patronage and the tomb of Itimad-ud-Daulah connect household
memory, white marble, inlay and architectural transition.

⚠️ For Topic 20, art matters as:

- imperial self-fashioning;
- connoisseurship and attribution;
- natural-history observation;
- household patronage; and
- a means of binding elite taste to the court.

The complete Mughal painting, music, architecture, literature, science and
technology survey remains Topic 24. A painting is a crafted representation,
not a photograph of political reality.

## 14. The *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri/Jahangirnama* and the source ladder

| Source class | What it can establish | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| *Jahangirnama/Tuzuk* | imperial chronology, persona, observations, stated orders and justifications | self-fashioning; silence; authorship/continuation layers; implementation not automatic |
| Mutamad Khan's *Iqbalnama* and Persian chronicles | court sequence, offices, rival explanation | patronage and hindsight |
| farmans, seals and coins | named formal authority and public titulature | cannot measure total decision-making or uniform reach |
| paintings | iconography, hierarchy, atelier practice, desired image | representation, not unmediated event |
| tombs, inscriptions, gardens and forts | material patronage, place, form and claim | motive and empire-wide effect need corroboration |
| Roe, Hawkins and factory records | commercial diplomacy, protocol and European observation | interested merchant/ambassador lens and limited access |
| Sikh traditions and Jesuit evidence | community memory and alternative testimony | genre, chronology and transmission require criticism |

✅ Jahangir wrote much of his memoir, while later portions involved
continuation/redaction. Always identify the portion and edition before making
an authorship claim.

⚠️ The governing method is:

`claim -> named source/date/person/place -> what it proves -> corroboration ->
what it cannot prove`.

## 15. Historiographical balance sheet

| Interpretation | Value | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| weak pleasure-loving ruler | notices alcohol, opium, health and access politics | ignores continued decisions, campaigns, settlements and institutional continuity |
| Nur Jahan as sovereign in all but name | takes farmans, coins and crisis action seriously | turns exceptional influence into total replacement |
| fixed Nur Jahan junta | highlights household kinship and family advancement | lacks proof of a stable 1611-20 monopoly or two permanent camps |
| simple Akbarian continuity | explains composite nobility, broad religious policy and consolidation | understates Guru Arjan, campaign coercion, Qandahar and succession stress |
| decline from 1605 | gives later crises a long pre-history | confuses serious strain with post-1707 imperial decline |

**Graded verdict**

> ⚠️ Jahangir remained a governing emperor whose reign achieved negotiated and
> territorial consolidation. Nur Jahan exercised exceptional formal and
> household authority, particularly in the post-1622 crisis, but neither a
> permanent junta nor sovereign replacement is demonstrated. The reign's
> failures reveal the structural risks of princely armies, household access and
> frontier overstretch without proving institutional collapse.

## 17. Examiner-grade answer architecture

### 10 marks: consolidation under pressure

1. define continuity without assuming stability;
2. use Mewar 1615 and one of Bengal/Kangra;
3. explain the mechanism, not merely the event;
4. qualify with Qandahar/Deccan/succession; and
5. conclude “uneven consolidation, not decline”.

### 15 marks: Nur Jahan and faction

1. reject the ceremonial-versus-sovereign binary;
2. use farman/coin/household/patronage evidence;
3. state Beni Prasad's junta thesis fairly;
4. use Nurul Hasan/Satish Chandra's promotion and alliance critique;
5. periodise 1611-21 and post-1622; and
6. conclude exceptional authority within Jahangirian sovereignty.

### 20 marks: crisis politics

1. accession and Khusrau;
2. inherited state and princely military bases;
3. Qandahar-command and resource crisis;
4. Khurram's multi-causal rebellion;
5. Mahabat's capture-versus-control distinction;
6. Nur Jahan's recovery role;
7. source and gender bias; and
8. graded verdict on state capacity.

Every paragraph should follow:

`claim -> named evidence -> causal analysis -> qualification/source limit`.

## 18. Prelims traps and rapid recall

- Jahangir = **Prince Salim**, reign **1605-1627**.
- Khusrau revolt and Guru Arjan episode = **1606**.
- Islam Khan to Bengal = **1608**; Dacca became the strategic provincial
  centre.
- Nur Jahan marriage = **1611**; fixed junta is a later disputed model.
- Mewar settlement and Roe's embassy begin = **1615**.
- Kangra = **1620**.
- Qandahar loss = **1622**, but full external-policy analysis belongs Topic 19.
- Khurram rebellion = **1622-25/early 1626**, not 1657-58.
- Mahabat Khan's Jhelum coup = **1626**.
- Chain of justice = ruler-centred justice symbol, not modern judicial review.
- Mewar = suzerainty plus honour-sensitive accommodation, not annexation.
- Roe = commercial facilities, not territory or sovereignty.
- Nur Jahan's coins/farmans = formal public authority, not proof of total rule.
- Jahangir's religious practice = broad continuity plus local coercive episodes.
- *Tuzuk/Jahangirnama* = indispensable royal memoir requiring source criticism.
- Final formula: **continuity + consolidation + household authority + succession
  contest + source limits**.
