## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — Muhammad bin Tughlaq: ambitious state-building, weak execution.**

✅ Muhammad bin Tughlaq is presented by Satish Chandra as a remarkable ruler: learned in religion, philosophy and sciences, open to Muslim mystics, Hindu yogis and Jain saints, and willing to appoint officials by merit rather than only noble birth.
- ⚠️ Satish Chandra uses the label "ill-starred idealist" to capture ambition
  and repeated failure, but each policy must be tested separately through intent,
  capacity, timing, ecology and sources rather than reduced to personality.
- ✅ **Daulatabad:** Deogir was renamed Daulatabad and used as a second capital to improve control over south India; officials, leading men and some Sufis were pressured to move, but Delhi was not emptied.
- ✅ **Token currency:** copper and brass coins were assigned the value of precious-metal currency; the scheme failed because unrestricted private imitation and weak verification destroyed confidence.
- ✅ **Agrarian policy:** in the doab, over-assessment, artificial prices and famine produced distress; later the **diwan-i-amir-i-Kohi** tried to extend cultivation through loans, but officials misused funds.

**Foundation — Firuz Shah: benevolence plus long-term weakness.**

✅ Firuz Shah Tughlaq tried to prevent further break-up by appeasing nobles, soldiers and theologians, while avoiding hard-to-control regions such as the Deccan.
- ✅ He failed to recover Bengal but retained a large north Indian Sultanate; his reign was remembered for peace and quiet development rather than military brilliance.
- ✅ He made offices and **iqtas** more hereditary, stopped harsh audit-torture of nobles, and extended heredity to soldiers — measures that bought peace but weakened central control.
- ✅ He built canals, founded towns such as Hissar-Firuzah and Firuzabad, set up hospitals, gave dowries to poor girls, and created a public works department.
- ⚠️ Afif reports a slave establishment of about **180,000**, including karkhana
  workers and an armed/personal corps. Treat this as a chronicler's large
  administrative claim, not an independently audited census; the corps later
  became a factional problem.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ Muhammad bin Tughlaq ruled **1325–51**; Firuz Shah ruled **1351–88**.
- ✅ Daulatabad was **Deogir**, not a new city; it was intended as a second capital for southern control.
- ✅ Delhi was **not completely emptied** during the Daulatabad move.
- ✅ Token currency failed mainly because forged coins flooded the market.
- ✅ **Diwan-i-amir-i-Kohi** was the agricultural development department.
- ✅ Firuz dug canals, founded towns, built hospitals and promoted public works.
- ✅ Firuz made offices and iqtas hereditary, weakening the Sultanate's central machinery.
- ✅ Timur's invasion came after Firuz's death, when the Sultanate was already weakened.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: Muhammad bin Tughlaq was not simply "mad"; Satish Chandra treats him as intelligent and innovative, but administratively impatient.

- ❌ Daulatabad was made the only capital. → It functioned as a **second capital**; Delhi remained populated.
- ❌ Token currency failed because paper money was unknown in the world. → Token currency existed elsewhere; failure in India came from **forgery and weak enforcement**.
- ❌ Firuz was strong because his reign was peaceful. → Peace came partly by **appeasing elites**, which weakened the centre.
- ❌ Firuz's welfare measures made the state modern. → They were benevolent but socially limited and tied to medieval privilege.

- ⚠️ Muhammad bin Tughlaq was not uniquely "impractical" — Alauddin's doab policy was equally harsh, but succeeded because of his coercive apparatus.
- ⚠️ Firuz's "welfare" was limited: jizyah was imposed, Sufis punished, and "welfare" meant noble/soldier appeasement more than peasant relief.
- ⚠️ Regional breakaways (Bengal 1338, Deccan 1347) had structural causes independent of Muhammad's policies.

## 9. Semantic-completeness repair: accession, sources and imperial scale

### 9.1 Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq

Ghiyasuddin rose from the north-western military/frontier establishment and
overthrew Khusrau Khan in 1320. His short reign combined restored legitimacy,
moderation in revenue demands, cultivation concerns, Bengal/Warangal and
frontier campaigns, and Tughlaqabad's fortified kingship.

His death in 1325 after the collapse of a pavilion at Afghanpur generated
accusations against prince Juna Khan/Muhammad. Barani, Ibn Battuta and later
traditions differ; accident, negligence and conspiracy cannot be resolved as
audited fact.

### 9.2 Source hierarchy

| Source | Historical value | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Ziya Barani | policies, elites, experiments, political ideals | later aristocratic/normative account; not eyewitness to every phase |
| Ibn Battuta | eyewitness court, roads, gifts, punishments and urban observations under Muhammad | traveller's literary conventions, marvels and personal position |
| Isami | Deccan displacement, Daulatabad and political critique | wrote from grievance/exile and retrospective memory |
| Shams-i Siraj Afif | Firuz's works, administration, slaves and benevolence | celebratory later-Tughlaq lens and large-number claims |
| Firuz's *Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi* | ruler's own preferred account of works/religious policy | self-representation, not neutral audit |
| Inscriptions/coins | titles, dates, minting and building acts | uneven survival and limited motive |
| Architecture/archaeology | cities, forts, canals, pillars and construction phases | later repair/reuse complicates attribution |
| Later chronicles | memory and long-term evaluation | chronological distance and inherited stereotypes |

Separate policy intent, source report, implementation evidence, regional effect
and later stereotype.

### 9.3 Muhammad bin Tughlaq's political geography

The inherited Sultanate stretched from Punjab/Ganga regions toward Gujarat,
Malwa and the Deccan, but direct control, tribute and campaign claims differed.
Distance, communications, plague, local elites, revenue capacity and rebellion
made an all-India centralized system fragile.

## 10. Daulatabad: phases, groups and reversal

Deogir/Daulatabad offered a central location for north-south communication and
control. It was developed as a **second capital**, not a permanent replacement
for an emptied Delhi.

| Phase | Policy/action | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| strategic development | court presence, road/communications and southern supervision | chronology of individual orders varies |
| pressured migration | royal household, officials, nobles, ulama/Sufis, merchants and artisans affected | the entire Delhi population was not deported |
| coercive implementation | hardship, surveillance and forced movement reported | Isami and later narratives magnify suffering for literary/political purposes |
| reversal/return | Delhi retained mint/population; return became possible | Daulatabad remained an important Deccan urban-cultural centre |
| political loss of purpose | Ma'bar, Warangal and other southern regions broke away | failure was tied to regional fragmentation, not geography alone |

The project combined rational strategic objectives with coercion, logistics and
changing political conditions.
Source exaggeration about an entirely deserted Delhi must not replace the
phased evidence.

## 11. Token currency: credibility, not fiat analogy

Muhammad issued copper/brass token coins assigned a value linked to precious
metal currency.

```text
need for monetary/fiscal flexibility
        |
state-declared token value
        |
weak mint monopoly / authentication
        |
private counterfeiting
        |
confidence and revenue collapse
        |
withdrawal and redemption at treasury cost
```

- The tokens were metal, not paper money.
- Modern fiat currency rests on institutions, legal tender, controlled issue,
  authentication and confidence; the analogy is therefore misleading if direct.
- Counterfeiting, tax payment with bad coins and inability to distinguish valid
  issues undermined the scheme.
- Redemption reportedly burdened the treasury; exact heaps/quantities are
  chronicler imagery, not audited statistics.
- Geographic circulation and implementation cannot be assumed uniform.
- Failure was not solely "bad implementation": fiscal context, trust, minting
  technology, enforcement and political instability interacted.

## 12. Doab revenue crisis and agricultural repair

### 12.1 Assessment and famine

- Revenue demand in the Ganga-Yamuna Doab was raised/reassessed using standard
  yields and official prices rather than actual harvest conditions.
- Harsh collection and official conduct aggravated rural distress.
- Famine/climatic scarcity overlapped with policy, rebellion and migration.
- Chronology matters: tax pressure and famine effects developed over phases,
  while relief followed severe crisis.

### 12.2 Relief and Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi

Muhammad established the **Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi** (often shortened Diwan-i Kohi)
to extend cultivation, improve crops and provide **sondhar** loans for seeds,
wells and inputs.
It was an agriculture-development experiment, not merely famine charity.

The programme shows agricultural ambition but suffered from:

- unsuitable/uncertain land selection;
- corruption or misuse by officials;
- monitoring and recovery problems;
- ecological and time constraints;
- limited demonstrated scale.

Relief camps and assistance should be analysed alongside, not used to erase, the
earlier revenue crisis.

## 13. Khurasan and Qarachil: projects must be separated

| Project | Intention/action | Outcome/limit |
|---|---|---|
| Khurasan project | planned western campaign amid perceived opportunity; recruited/paid a large army | campaign was not launched as envisaged; recruitment burdened treasury |
| Qarachil expedition | actual Himalayan campaign connected with hill/frontier objectives | severe losses from terrain, climate, supply and command; not an invasion of China |

Planning, recruitment and execution are different historical acts. The fiscal
cost of maintaining/disbanding troops and the impact on treasury must be kept
separate from imagined world-conquest narratives.

## 14. Southern policy, rebellion and contraction

| Region | Transition under Muhammad | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Devagiri/Daulatabad | major second-capital/military-administrative centre | later Bahmani/Deccan history has separate owners |
| Warangal | incorporated after Khalji-era tribute; later rebellion/loss | local chiefs and distance mattered |
| Kampili | conquest and political-religious consequences | not identical with all Karnataka |
| Ma'bar/Madurai | independence and Sultanate formation in the far south | direct Delhi control contracted |
| Vijayanagara | emerged in the fourteenth-century southern reconfiguration | Topic 09 |
| Bahmani state, 1347 | Deccan breakaway under Ala al-Din Bahman Shah | Topic 09 |

Bengal, Gujarat, Sindh and the Deccan saw rebellions or provincial autonomy.
Plague, army losses, fiscal pressure, communication distance, local elite
interests and policy failure interacted. No single rebellion explains
contraction.

## 15. Muhammad's intellectual and religious profile

Muhammad was learned in philosophy, logic, medicine/sciences and religious
debate, and interacted with ulama, Sufis, Jain teachers and Hindu yogis.

- Barani criticized him as a rationalist who would not accept claims merely on
  authority.
- His religious/intellectual openness coexisted with harsh punishment,
  suspicion and political violence.
- Caliphal recognition and Islamic legitimacy still mattered.
- Conflict with some ulama/Sufis did not make him uniformly anti-religious.

"Mad king," "mad genius," "ahead of his time" and "complete failure" are
stereotypes. Each decision requires an objective-capacity-outcome test.

### Ibn Battuta

Ibn Battuta personally experienced Muhammad's court and provides vivid
eyewitness evidence on gifts, punishments, routes, officials, Delhi and royal
conduct. His *Rihla* also follows travel-literature conventions, emphasizes the
marvellous and was composed/narrated after travel. Vividness is not statistical
representativeness.

## 16. Firuz Shah: accession and state bargain

Firuz succeeded during the 1351 Sindh/Thatta campaign after Muhammad's death.
His legitimacy depended on noble/military selection, Tughlaq lineage claims,
religious support and successful return to the core.

His model exchanged coercive centralization for conciliation:

- restored/confirmed noble positions;
- reduced harsh audit/punishment;
- used high-value iqtas and hereditary claims;
- sought peace and development in the north Indian core;
- accepted limits to reconquering distant provinces.

Bengal campaigns, Jajnagar/Nagarkot and Thatta show mixed military results.
Firuz was neither inactive nor an effective all-India reconqueror.

## 17. Revenue, heredity and military consequences

| Policy | Short-term effect | Long-term risk |
|---|---|---|
| hereditary iqtas/offices | reassured nobles and officials | reduced central transfer/audit leverage |
| hereditary soldiers' village assignments (*wajh*) | stabilized military households | weakened cash review, mobility and effectiveness |
| reduced coercive audit | conciliation | inflated/entrenched assignments |
| irrigation/cultivation | expanded output in benefited areas | regional concentration and maintenance needs |

Hereditary iqta did not become European feudal private ownership by one decree.
Revenue assignment, office, landholding and sovereignty remained distinct.

## 18. Canals, public works and urban foundations

Firuz is associated with canals linking river/water systems to Hissar and
Haryana tracts, gardens, reservoirs, repairs and towns such as Hissar-Firuzah
and Firuzabad/Firuz Shah Kotla.

- Benefiting villages were associated with a water charge
  (**haqq-i sharb**), reported as a share/levy in the sources.
- Canals supported cultivation, settlement and revenue where gradients,
  maintenance and local labour permitted.
- Dar al-Shifa/hospital, madrasas and educational patronage expanded courtly
  welfare/learning.
- **Diwan-i Khairat** is associated with charity/dowries for needy Muslim women.
- Employment-office claims and registers appear in narrative sources but should
  not be inflated into a modern labour ministry.
- Public works were significant but concentrated and source-celebrated; do not
  invent precise totals of canals, towns or gardens.

## 19. Slaves, taxation and religious policy

### 19.1 Bandagan

Firuz maintained a large establishment of slaves (**bandagan**) with a separate
**Diwan-i Bandagan**. Slaves served in households, workshops, administration
and armed/personal roles.

Afif's reported total is not an audited census. Coerced service, training,
patronage and political faction must all be considered. After Firuz, the corps
became one element in succession conflict.

### 19.2 Taxation and religion

- Firuz emphasized taxes he presented as authorized by Islamic law and removed
  some cesses, although practice and local burdens require caution.
- Jizya was treated as a distinct levy, and sources associate his reign with
  extending it to Brahmans who had previously claimed exemption.
- Ulama influence and sharper orthodoxy grew, but neither Firuz's policy nor the
  ulama formed a single unchanging programme.
- Temple actions, punishments and reported conversions require case-specific
  chronology; "uniform persecution" and "complete tolerance" are both false.
- Sanskrit works were translated, older monuments conserved/reused and Ashokan
  pillars transported to Delhi, demonstrating that orthodoxy coexisted with
  antiquarian, political and cultural appropriation.

## 21. Comparison: Muhammad and Firuz

| Axis | Muhammad bin Tughlaq | Firuz Shah |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | wide centralization, mobility and experimentation | conciliation, core development and stability |
| Administrative capacity | ambitious regulation, weak verification/communication in key projects | lower coercive audit, stronger elite entrenchment |
| Agrarian policy | Doab crisis followed by agricultural loans | irrigation/canal expansion and water levy |
| Military | large recruitment and wide campaigns | reduced distant ambition and hereditary arrangements |
| Religion | intellectually wide contacts plus harsh state violence | sharper orthodoxy plus translations/public works |
| Outcome | rebellions and territorial contraction | long peace but weakened post-reign centre |

Neither genius/failure nor benevolent/fanatical binaries are sufficient.

## 22. Post-1388 instability and bounded Timur context

Firuz's death was followed by competing heirs, nobles, slaves and provincial
power. Hereditary offices, military weakening, fiscal fragmentation and
territorial autonomy interacted. Timur's 1398 invasion belongs to Topic 06 and
was a devastating later rupture, not the sole cause of decline.
