## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — Sultanate administration: centralised but negotiated.**

✅ The Delhi Sultanate became a powerful, highly centralised state, at times controlling territory as far south as Madurai; even after disintegration, its administration influenced later regional states and the Mughals.
- ✅ The **sultan** held supreme political, military and legal authority, acted as commander-in-chief, and could hear appeals against officials.
- ✅ Succession had no settled rule: all sons had claims, nomination needed noble acceptance, and military strength often decided outcomes.
- ✅ Key departments included **wazir** (revenue/income-expenditure), **diwan-i-arz** (military recruitment/equipment/pay), **diwan-i-risalat** (religious grants), and **diwan-i-insha** (state correspondence).
- ✅ **Barids** acted as intelligence agents; **karkhanas** supplied and sometimes manufactured royal goods; Firuz also created public works and slave departments.
- ⚠️ The precise function assigned to **diwan-i-risalat** varies across modern reconstructions; this kit follows Satish Chandra's association with religious matters/grants, while **diwan-i-insha** securely handled royal correspondence.
- ✅ An **iqta** assigned revenue and administrative responsibility, not ownership of land; the centre could transfer a muqti and demand accounts and the surplus above authorised expenditure.

**Foundation — economy and society: towns, trade, inequality.**

✅ With Sultanate consolidation, communications and a sound currency based on the silver **tanka** and copper currency (called **dirham** in the assigned source; **jital** is also used for copper units) helped trade grow; towns and town life expanded.
- ✅ Ibn Battuta called Delhi the largest city in the eastern Islamic world and said Daulatabad equalled Delhi in size.
- ✅ Important towns included Lahore, Multan, Kara, Lakhnauti, Anhilwara/Patan and Cambay.
- ✅ India produced rice, sugarcane, wheat, oilseeds, sesame, indigo and cotton; village industries included oil-pressing, jaggery, weaving and dyeing.
- ✅ Wealthy merchants included Gujaratis, Marwaris, Jains, Bohras, Multanis and Khurasanis; Indian textiles reached the Red Sea, Persian Gulf and China.
- ✅ Society was unequal: nobles and wealthy merchants lived ostentatiously, while peasants, artisans and labourers often faced hardship.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ **Wazir** = revenue/income-expenditure; **diwan-i-arz** = military department.
- ✅ **Ariz-i-mamalik** recruited, equipped and paid the army; the sultan remained commander-in-chief.
- ✅ **Diwan-i-insha** handled state correspondence.
- ✅ **Barids** were intelligence agents.
- ✅ **Iqtas** were tracts assigned to nobles; holders were called **muqtis** or **walis**.
- ✅ Below provinces were **shiqs** and **parganas**; the pargana was headed by the **amil**.
- ✅ Cambay and Sonargaon were famous for textiles; Bengal for raw silk/fine cotton; Gujarat for textiles and gold/silver work.
- ✅ The Turks popularised/improved paper-making, spinning wheel, cotton-carder's bow, improved loom, iron stirrup and lime mortar use.
- ✅ Alauddin Khalji introduced **dagh** (horse-branding) and **chehra** (descriptive roll) for closer army inspection; Sher Shah revived/adapted these controls, and Akbar later systematised them within the mansabdari framework.
- ✅ **Fanam** was a small gold coin of south Indian/coastal circulation (especially Vijayanagara-Malabar trade), not a standard Delhi Sultanate revenue-office or tanka-jital term.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: The Sultanate state was centralised at the top but relied heavily on local elites and older village structures.

- ❌ Iqta was identical to later Mughal jagir in every respect. → It was an earlier Sultanate assignment with its own controls and evolution.
- ❌ The sultan delegated supreme military command to the ariz. → The **sultan** remained commander-in-chief.
- ❌ All Hindus were excluded from administration. → Local machinery remained largely in Hindu hands, and Hindus served as managers/soldiers/officials.
- ❌ Sultanate towns were only military camps. → Many began around garrisons but grew into commercial and cultural centres.

## 7. Mains angles

- ⚠️ The Sultanate administration combined central authority with dependence on local intermediaries.
- ⚠️ Urbanisation under the Sultanate was driven by garrisons, ruling-class consumption, trade, crafts and currency.
- ⚠️ Sultanate society should be studied through inequality: elite consumption coexisted with peasant and artisan hardship.

### 8.3 Mains engine — technological changes during the Sultanate

**Thesis:** "The Sultanate period witnessed significant technological diffusion — particularly in textiles, construction, agriculture and metallurgy — that transformed urban production, trade capacity and military power, though benefits were unevenly distributed."

| Mechanism | Evidence and effect | Social/economic consequence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ✅ Paper-making became better known and more widely used under the Turks | Enabled cheaper record-keeping, correspondence, revenue accounts and manuscript culture | Bureaucracy, scholarship and mercantile bookkeeping could expand more easily | Gains were concentrated among literate urban groups |
| 2 | ✅ Spinning wheel and cotton-carder's bow increased yarn preparation and spinning efficiency | Raised textile output and supported village-based production chains | Drew more women into spinning and low-value household industry | Employment widened, but much of it remained poorly paid and subordinate |
| 3 | ✅ Iron stirrup improved cavalry balance and shock-effect | Strengthened mounted warfare and helped Turkish military superiority | Military technology underpinned conquest and state formation | Advantage depended on horse supply, training and command, not stirrup alone |
| 4 | ✅ Improved loom technology supported finer cloth production | Increased craft specialisation and export competitiveness in textiles | Helped urban workshops and long-distance trade | Benefits flowed more to merchants and state-linked towns than to peasant households |
| 5 | ✅ Lime-mortar and the true arch enabled larger domes, stronger vaulting and durable construction | Expanded monumental architecture, urban construction and some hydraulic works | Shows how technology served imperial display and city-building | Monumental gains did not automatically improve rural welfare |
| 6 | ✅ Persian wheel and allied water-lifting devices, where attested, widened irrigation possibilities | Helped lift water more efficiently than simple manual methods | Could extend cultivation and support garden/agricultural intensification | Adoption was regionally uneven and depended on local capital and water conditions |

**Regional/social limits**
- ⚠️ Benefits were concentrated in towns, garrison centres and trade corridors rather than evenly across the countryside.
- ⚠️ Peasants remained the primary producers and saw little direct benefit from monumental construction technologies.
- ⚠️ The spinning wheel widened women's employment, but mostly in low-value domestic and village industry rather than high-status artisanal work.

**Verdict:** Technology was a necessary but insufficient condition for economic transformation; the Sultanate state directed many innovations toward military, urban and revenue goals rather than broad welfare.

### 8.4 Iqta–jagir comparison bank (corrected provenance)

✅ **Provenance correction:** **Dagh** (horse-branding) and **chehra** (descriptive roll of soldiers) were introduced for the standing army under **Alauddin Khalji** in the Sultanate period. **Sher Shah** later revived and adapted these controls, and **Akbar** systematised them within the mansab framework; he did **not** invent them.

| Axis | Sultanate iqta | Mughal jagir | Answer-use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenure | ✅ Transferable revenue assignment to a muqti/wali; tenure depended heavily on sultanic strength | ✅ Transferable jagir linked to a mansabdar; rotation became more systematic | Use to show continuity with greater Mughal regularisation |
| Revenue assignment method | ✅ Often a lump territorial assignment from which the holder met expenses and remitted surplus | ✅ Jagir valuation increasingly linked to measured/assessed jama within a more elaborate revenue system (zabt/dahsala where applicable) | Distinguishes rough assignment from more codified fiscal calculation |
| Fiscal-military obligation | ✅ Muqti maintained troops and order in broad terms; obligations were real but less finely graded | ✅ Mansab fixed more precise **zat/sawar** obligations, tying rank to cavalry requirement | Best comparative axis for 15-marker answers |
| Accountability | ✅ Audit and surplus remittance depended on an energetic centre; weak sultans lost control easily | ✅ Annual review, transfer and inspection operated through the mansab-jagir framework | Shows why Mughal control was more regular, though not perfect |

### 8.5 Counterpoints

- ⚠️ "Centralised" is relative: village-level governance (khut, muqaddam, patwari) remained largely unchanged and often in Hindu hands.
- ⚠️ Iqta ≠ jagir: temporal evolution matters; do not flatten Sultanate and Mughal systems into one.
- ⚠️ Economic prosperity was concentrated in trading towns and ports; rural producers often bore the heaviest burden.
- ⚠️ Technological change improved state capacity and urban production more than broad rural welfare.

### Analytical framework for a source question

- ✅ **Court/elite bias:** Most Persian chronicles were produced by literate courtly elites; peasants, lower castes and most women are marginal.
- ✅ **Patronage dependency:** Royal or noble patronage created systematic incentives to praise the patron and suppress failure.
- ✅ **Genre distinction matters:** Official histories (*Akbarnama*) ≠ private critiques (*Badauni*) ≠ memoirs (*Baburnama*, *Tuzuk*) ≠ Sufi malfuzat.
- ✅ **Cross-verification is essential:** Compare Abul Fazl with Badauni, or Barani with administrative outcomes, to expose bias.
- ✅ **What they cannot tell us well:** Everyday peasant experience, most women's lives, oral traditions, lower artisan conditions and much non-Muslim religious practice remain under-represented.
