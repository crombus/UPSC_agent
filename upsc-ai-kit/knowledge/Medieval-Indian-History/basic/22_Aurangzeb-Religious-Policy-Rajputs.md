- Topic 24 owns the full culture/society survey.

> ⚠️ Aurangzeb's personal orthodoxy altered the public legal-symbolic language

Analytical phases:

1. early pragmatic continuity and consolidation;

4. later wartime rule with continued recruitment, exceptions and uneven
   enforcement.

These are analytical phases, not airtight changes across every province.

## 3. Sharia, zawabit and sovereignty

✅ Aurangzeb was a pious Hanafi Muslim who sought greater conformity with
Islamic law. He patronised jurists, qazis, sadrs and religious institutions.

✅ **Zawabit** were ruler-made administrative regulations. The emperor selected
among opinions, appointed officials, allocated revenue and made political
exceptions.

⚠️ The state was neither an ulama-run theocracy nor a modern secular state:

`legal opinion -> imperial selection/order -> provincial/local administration
-> implementation, negotiation or non-enforcement`.

### Fatawa-i-Alamgiri

✅ The *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri* was an imperially sponsored collective compendium of
Hanafi jurisprudence.

It proves the political prestige of legal learning. It was not:

- a modern written constitution;
- a single criminal code automatically applied everywhere;
- proof that every official consulted it; or
- a replacement for imperial regulations and political judgement.

### Ulama

✅ Ulama, qazis, sadrs, imams and collectors benefited from patronage. Some
jizyah officials acquired influence; Satish Chandra notes corruption and
grasping conduct among some.

⚠️ They were not a homogeneous loyal bloc. Patronage, locality, doctrine and
interest divided them, and the policies did not reliably rally all Muslims.

## 4. Puritan and court-symbolic measures

Reported measures include action against intoxicants, gambling, prostitution
and practices considered un-Islamic; appointment of **muhtasibs**; changes to
court ceremonies such as jharoka darshan; regulation of Nauroz, music and coin
formulae; mosque repair; and patronage of Islamic officials.

For each claim ask:

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| exact date? | popular lists telescope decades |
| court practice or social order? | reach differs |
| order, chronicle approval or implementation record? | genre differs |
| which province/locality? | practice was uneven |
| exception or reversal? | a static reign-wide label is unsafe |

⚠️ Court symbolism could change the public idiom of sovereignty even without
uniform village enforcement. Uneven enforcement does not make the order
meaningless.

## 5. Jizyah, 1679

✅ Jizyah was a poll tax marking differentiated non-Muslim subjecthood in
classical Islamic law. It was not land revenue, customs duty or a tax on every
Hindu without exception.

✅ Standard categories distinguished wealth levels and exemptions. Women,
children, indigent persons, some dependants and persons unable to pay were
among exempt categories; administration and remissions varied. Avoid quoting a
rate unless the unit, category and source are secure.

✅ Revenue was placed in a separate treasury for needy Muslims/ulama rather
than simply relieving the general treasury. Satish Chandra cites a large
official total but also stresses collection corruption and political failure;
do not convert a court claim into exact realised revenue.

Why reimpose it?

- personal/legal orthodoxy;
- public assertion of Islamic rulership;
- alliance/patronage toward theologians;
- fiscal form; and
- political differentiation in a period of pressure.

Effects:

- visible reversal of Akbarian policy;
- alienation and mistrust among sections of Hindus;
- new collector-subject contact and opportunities for corruption;
- symbolic support for critics of imperial policy; but
- no proof that jizyah alone caused one rebellion or imperial decline.

✅ Crop-failure remissions occurred; in 1704 collection was suspended in the
south for the duration of war. ⚠️ Policy therefore had chronology and regional
variation.

## 6. Temple policy: order, practice, region and war

The formal legal distinction commonly protected old places of worship of
dhimmis while opposing new construction or political challenge. Actual Mughal
practice did not consistently follow one rule.

### Evidence categories

1. **general or provincial orders** against new/unauthorised temples;
2. **specific destruction or conversion** at Kashi, Mathura and elsewhere;
3. **wartime punishment** in Marwar-Mewar and rebellious localities;
4. **defacement/bricking up** rather than identical destruction in every case;
5. **grants, protection or non-interference** in other places; and
6. **restoration/complaint decisions** governed by property, law or politics.

✅ In 1669 an order targeted schools/temples associated in the court record
with objectionable teaching; Kashi Vishvanath and the Keshava Rai temple at
Mathura became major cases. During the 1679-80 Rajput war, old temples were
also destroyed in Jodhpur/Udaipur areas.

⚠️ Avoid both false universals:

- “all temples were destroyed throughout India”; and
- “only newly built or rebellious temples were ever affected”.

The safe method is `dated site -> source -> stated context -> actual action ->
regional reach -> counter-evidence`.

Temple destruction expressed real coercion and religious differentiation even
when political punishment also mattered.

## 7. Composite service and discrimination

✅ Hindu, Rajput and later Maratha mansabdars continued to serve and their
numbers could grow. Many Rajput houses—Amber, Bikaner, Bundi and Kota—remained
in Mughal service after 1679.

✅ Some discriminatory orders regarding appointments and customs were issued,
modified or evaded. Administrative need limited uniform exclusion.

⚠️ Continued inclusion does not prove equality or erase jizyah/temple
coercion. Discrimination does not prove that the state stopped recruiting
Hindus. The correct category is **differentiated incorporation**.

## 8. North Indian rebellions: do not flatten them

### Jats

✅ The Jats of the Mathura-Agra region were cultivators, zamindars and clan
communities. The c. 1669 rising associated with Gokula emerged from local
revenue, zamindari, official coercion and religious-political conditions.
Later resistance under Rajaram and Churaman shows recurrence, not one event.

### Satnamis, 1672

✅ A local clash near Narnaul escalated into mobilisation by the Satnami
community, which included cultivators and artisans. Imperial force suppressed
it.

### Bundelas

✅ Champat Rai and later Chhatrasal belonged to a Bundelkhand setting of local
chiefship, Mughal extraction, service and rebellion. Their chronology and
causes cannot be collapsed into jizyah alone.

⚠️ These movements differed in social base, place, leadership and duration.
They were neither modern national revolts nor purely religious/class
rebellions. Each shows how local state-society conflict could become armed
challenge.

## 9. Sikh context and Guru Tegh Bahadur

✅ Guru Tegh Bahadur became Guru in 1664, travelled in eastern India and
returned to Punjab in 1671. He was arrested and executed at Delhi in November
1675.

Source controls:

- no contemporary Persian narrative gives a complete transparent account;
- later Persian sources present law-and-order/rebellion claims;
- Sikh tradition centres defence of religious freedom and martyrdom;
- Kashmir Pandit tradition and later memory require chronology/genre care.

⚠️ Satish Chandra regards the punishment as unjustified. A defensible answer
recognises imperial security framing, religious coercion and Sikh martyr
memory without pretending that one late source resolves every detail.

✅ Guru Gobind Singh later founded the Khalsa in 1699. ⚠️ The 1675 execution
contributed to hardened relations but did not mechanically cause every later
institution or battle.

Detailed Sikh institutional history belongs its dedicated owner; Topic 22 owns
the Aurangzeb-period state-policy connection.

## 10. Rajput alliance before the breach

✅ Aurangzeb initially worked with Jai Singh, Jaswant Singh and other Rajput
rulers. Jai Singh shaped the 1665 Shivaji settlement; Jaswant retained major
service. Aurangzeb nevertheless showed growing reservations in promotion and
late-1670s Deccan use.

⚠️ The Rajput alliance was an exchange:

`watan authority + imperial mansab/jagir + honour/status + military service +
succession recognition`.

It was not equality, but it required credible Mughal guarantees.

## 11. Marwar crisis, 1678-79

✅ Jaswant Singh died at Jamrud in 1678. His widows later gave birth to sons;
Ajit Singh became the surviving claimant. Marwar was taken into **khalisa**
amid disputed succession, debt, withheld revenue and disorder.

✅ Khalisa administration during a minority or disputed succession had
precedents; it did not by itself prove a plan of permanent annexation.

The crisis escalated because:

- officials behaved as conquerors and searched property;
- Aurangzeb recognised **Inder Singh** for the tika;
- competing claims of the ranis and Rathor sardars were mishandled;
- temple demolition teams and the 1679 jizyah intensified fear;
- Durgadas and Rathor leaders carried Ajit Singh away and rejected imperial
  arrangements; and
- the Rathors restored Ajit's tika at Jodhpur.

⚠️ The immediate problem was succession, watan, debt, honour and imperial
overreach. Religion sharpened the legitimacy crisis; it was not the sole
trigger.

## 12. Mewar, Prince Akbar and the long settlement

✅ Rana Raj Singh supported Rathor resistance because Mughal occupation of
Marwar and interference in Rajput succession threatened Mewar's strategic and
political position. He was not simply acting through a close family relation
to Ajit.

✅ Terrain and Rathor-Mewar cooperation slowed Mughal operations. Prince Akbar,
sent to command, rebelled in January 1681 with Rathor support. Aurangzeb used
letters, intelligence and political manoeuvre to split the coalition; Akbar
fled toward the Deccan.

✅ Mewar reached a settlement after Rana Raj Singh's death, but Rathor war
continued intermittently. Ajit received recognition in 1698 without complete
restoration of Jodhpur.

✅ Other Rajput houses continued Mughal service.

⚠️ Military damage after 1681 was uneven, but prestige, trust and the
credibility of Mughal protection suffered at the moment Aurangzeb entered the
prolonged southern war.

## 13. Sources and historiography

| Source | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Maasir-i-Alamgiri and court chronicles | orders, campaigns and official idiom | patronage, selection and triumphal/legal framing |
| farmans, temple grants and local records | dated administrative action | one locality is not the empire |
| Waqa-i-Ajmer | Marwar-Mewar administration and events | official/local perspective |
| Jodhpur Hukumat-ri-Bahi | Rathor court/government evidence | dynastic/local perspective |
| Sikh traditions and later Persian texts | community memory and competing explanation | temporal distance and genre |
| inscriptions/material remains | survival, damage, repair, patronage | absence or survival alone does not prove motive |

Historiographical positions:

- communal monocause: correctly notices discrimination, but explains too much
  through one identity;
- statecraft-only revision: correctly notices succession/fiscal/strategic
  motives, but can understate religious meaning and coercion;
- balanced view: orthodoxy shaped choices and symbols; political contexts
  selected targets and timing; uneven administration shaped reach.

Satish Chandra calls Marwar-Mewar policy clumsy and blundering. Aurangzeb was
both constrained by circumstances and responsible for choices that worsened
them.

## 15. Answer architecture and rapid recall

### Religious policy

`orthodoxy -> sharia/zawabit -> dated measures -> jizyah/temple cases ->
continued incorporation -> implementation/reversal -> graded verdict`.

### Rajput policy

`alliance baseline -> Jaswant death -> khalisa precedent -> Inder Singh/
official overreach -> Ajit/Durgadas -> Mewar -> Prince Akbar -> continued
Rajput service -> legitimacy-cost verdict`.

### Traps

- Fatawa = legal compendium, not constitution.
- jizyah = poll tax and political signal, not land revenue.
- old/new temple distinction mattered formally but did not describe every act.
- continued Hindu service does not erase discrimination.
- Guru Tegh Bahadur cannot be explained by one transparent contemporary text.
- Jat, Satnami, Bundela and Sikh histories are not one communal rebellion.
- khalisa did not automatically mean permanent annexation.
- breach with Marwar-Mewar was not breach with all Rajputs.
- Topic 23 owns the Deccan-Maratha war and acute jagirdari crisis.

**Verdict:** differentiated incorporation survived, but sharper religious
hierarchy and mishandled Rajput succession damaged trust and widened the gap
between imperial coercive reach and political legitimacy.
