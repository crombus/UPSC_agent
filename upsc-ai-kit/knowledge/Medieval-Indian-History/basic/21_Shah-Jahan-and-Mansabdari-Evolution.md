## 3. Accession and early consolidation

✅ Khurram's succession was secured through Asaf Khan, the diwan, leading
nobles and the army after Jahangir's death. Rival princes and claimants were
eliminated. ⚠️ The episode demonstrates that Mughal succession was settled by
military-political coalition, not automatic primogeniture.

### Jujhar Singh Bundela

✅ Jujhar Singh submitted in 1628-29 after pressure, indemnity and partial jagir
confiscation, then served in the Deccan. His later attack on Gond ruler Prem
Narayan, seizure of wealth and renewed defiance produced another campaign.
Jujhar died while fleeing; territory was redistributed and members of his
family were coerced.

⚠️ The policy combined imperial extraction, punishment and reordering of local
authority. It should not be reduced to either neutral administration or a
religious war, although the destruction of Bir Singh Deo's Orchha temple and
mosque construction gave the victory an Islamic idiom.

### Khan Jahan Lodi

✅ The Afghan noble's rebellion emerged from mistrust, Deccan connections and
the monarchy-noble relationship. His defeat strengthened Shah Jahan's early
authority. The full Deccan sequence remains Topic 18.

### Zamindars and frontier chiefs

✅ Campaigns or pressure reached Gondwana, Palamau, Kumaon, Garhwal, Punjab
hills, Baltistan/Skardu and lower-Indus routes. Outcomes varied among indemnity,
submission, restored mansab, forts and thanas.

⚠️ Do not map-colour all these regions as uniformly annexed. The meaningful
question is what tribute, route security, fort possession or service obligation
was actually secured.

## 4. Bounded external and Deccan policy

### Deccan

✅ Ahmadnagar's Nizam Shahi dynasty ended after Daulatabad in 1633; the 1636
settlement made Bijapur and Golconda accept an unequal Mughal relationship.
Late operations in 1656-57 reopened the settlement.

⚠️ Shah Jahan did not annex the entire Deccan. Detailed treaties, Malik Ambar,
Shahji and southern operations belong Topic 18.

### Balkh and Qandahar

✅ Balkh in 1646-47 showed that field success could coexist with political and
logistical unsustainability. Qandahar was lost in 1649; Aurangzeb twice and
Dara once failed to recover it.

⚠️ These campaigns reveal the limits of long-distance supply, siege capacity
and durable control. Their complete diplomatic-military explanation belongs
Topic 19.

## 5. Imperial magnificence as political economy

✅ Shah Jahan used the Peacock Throne, Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid and
Shahjahanabad to materialise hierarchy, dynastic memory, order and imperial
wealth.

✅ The Peacock Throne was completed over seven years and first used in 1635.
Shahjahanabad began in 1638 and took about a decade in the assigned narrative.

✅ Shireen Moosvi's calculation, quoted by Satish Chandra, places Shah Jahan's
buildings and gardens at a little over Rs 289 lakhs over twenty-eight years.
The source concludes that construction was a significant khalisa expenditure
but not so excessive as to cripple imperial or military finance.

⚠️ Therefore:

- monuments were not costless;
- magnificence organised labour, materials, patronage and ceremonial power;
- expenditure alone cannot be declared the cause of fiscal crisis; and
- the full history of architectural forms belongs Topic 24/Indian Art and
  Culture.

## 6. What was the Mughal ruling class?

The ruling class was a **ranked imperial service elite**, not a fixed race,
religion or hereditary peerage. Mansab, office, jagir assignment, court access,
military service, kinship and demonstrated competence structured membership.

### Composition, 500 zat and above

Source-attributed Athar Ali series:

| Group | 1595 | 1621 | 1647-48 | 1656 | Safe inference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| princes | 4 | 4 | 4 | 8 | small but high-ranked dynastic layer |
| Iranis | 27 | 68 | 126 | 139 | strong growth and high-office presence |
| Turanis | 46 | 48 | 103 | 123 | remained major, proportion fell |
| Afghans | 4 | 15 | 26 | 34 | recovered from Akbar-era distrust |
| Indian Muslims | 14 | 35 | 65 | 59 | important Shaikhzada/administrative element |
| Rajputs | 20 | 34 | 73 | 87 | enduring military-political partners |
| Marathas | 0 | 1 | 10 | 12 | early but limited inclusion |
| other Hindus | 2 | 6 | 7 | 7 | administrative and service routes |
| total | 123 | 242 | 443 | 518 | elite numbers grew about 4.2 times |

Percentages and classifications are historian-made categories based on
available records. They do not prove fixed political blocs.

### Iranis and Turanis

✅ Their combined share fell from about **62.6%** in 1595 to **52.12%** in
1656, even while absolute numbers grew. Iranis held many central offices and
governorships.

⚠️ They should not be called foreign agents. Most were immigrants or
descendants incorporated into Mughal service and required to prove competence.

### Afghans and Indian Muslims

✅ Afghans gained under Jahangir and Shah Jahan but were less often placed in
some central administrative posts. Connections to landed Afghan society and
older political distrust mattered. Indian Muslims/Shaikhzadas continued to
advance; **Sadullah Khan** became Shah Jahan's celebrated wazir.

### Rajputs, Marathas and service communities

✅ Rajput prestige remained high, and Rajput contingents served in Deccan,
Balkh and Qandahar campaigns. Provincial governorships were fewer but not
absent. Late Mewar tension arose over Chittor's fortifications and sequestered
parganas.

✅ Habshis, Deccanis and Marathas were recruited as regional incorporation
continued, although a stable Maratha alliance was not achieved. Khatri,
Kayastha and some Brahman officials broadened the administrative base.

⚠️ Recruitment did not erase hierarchy, ethnic labels or faction. Faction
followed patronage, kinship, service, region and personal interest; it did not
mechanically equal Irani versus Turani or Hindu versus Muslim.

## 7. Mansab fundamentals

| Term | Meaning | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| **zat** | personal rank, status and salary grade | not troop count alone |
| **sawar** | cavalry obligation and its pay claim | not identical with zat |
| **jagir** | transferable assignment of revenue claim | not private ownership of soil |
| **jama** | assessed revenue on paper | not the same as realised **hasil** |
| **dagh** | branding/verification of horses | did not eliminate evasion |
| **chehra** | descriptive roll of troopers | record, not proof of constant readiness |
| **du-aspa sih-aspa** | doubled obligation/pay for a specified part of sawar rank | did not raise zat |
| **barawardi** | ordinary, non-du-aspa portion in the worked example | do not double the whole rank automatically |

✅ The mature dual zat-sawar system originated under Akbar. Du-aspa sih-aspa
is first discussed in the source under Jahangir. Shah Jahan systematised
contingent and payment adjustments rather than creating mansabdari from zero.

## 8. Salary reductions and effective contingents

✅ The source records progressive reductions in zat and sawar contract rates in
the early seventeenth century. A 1630 document suggests a broad zat reduction,
with lower mansabdars often facing sharper percentage cuts.

✅ Sawar payment figures were contract rates paid to mansabdars for hiring and
maintaining cavalry, not a uniform wage handed directly to every trooper.
Actual trooper pay varied with horses, quality and background.

### One-third, one-fourth and one-fifth rules

✅ Under Shah Jahan:

- a mansabdar posted where his jagir lay might muster about **one-third** of
  nominal sawar rank;
- one whose jagir lay elsewhere might muster **one-fourth**;
- Balkh-Badakhshan and perhaps Deccan service could use **one-fifth**.

✅ A 2000/2000 mansab with 1000 du-aspa sih-aspa and an outside-province jagir
could therefore yield a worked muster of 750: 250 ordinary plus 500 doubled.

⚠️ These ratios were systematised acknowledgements of actual practice and
fiscal conditions, not proof that nominal rank was meaningless.

## 9. The month-scale

Jagirs were graded by realised income relative to assessed jama:

`12-month -> 10-month -> 8-month -> 6-month -> 4-month`.

✅ Only princes and a few highest grandees might receive ten-month jagirs;
many mansabdars received eight-month assignments, generally not below four
months in North India. Deccan assignments could fall to three or four months
because jama was especially inflated.

✅ The month-scale applied to zat and sawar. The source's illustrative sawar
rates are:

| Jagir scale | Contract rate per sawar per month |
|---|---:|
| 12-month | Rs 40 |
| 8-month | Rs 30 |
| 6-month | Rs 25 |

The reduction was not exactly proportional because remount obligations also
declined.

⚠️ The month-scale was neither bonus nor simple fraud. It was an accounting and
service adjustment that recognised the jama-hasil gap while preserving the
rank system.

## 10. Revenue growth versus mansab growth

Source series:

| Year | Jama, crore dams | Index (1595-96 = 100) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1595-96 | about 516.251 | 100 |
| 1627 | about 630 | 122 |
| 1647-48 | about 880; about 862 excluding Balkh-Badakhshan | about 162 |
| 1656-57 | about 912 | 176 |

✅ Mansabdars of 500 zat and above increased from **123** to **518**, about
4.2 times, while the jama index rose to about 176.

⚠️ This created pressure because:

`more rank and salary claims + uneven hasil + limited productive jagirs +
costly service theatres -> salary cuts + reduced muster ratios + month-scale`.

The system still mobilised large armies and financed administration. The
**acute** jagirdari crisis of prolonged Deccan war remains Topic 23; the
post-1707 systemic decline remains Topic 25.

## 11. Administration and fiscal judgement

✅ Shah Jahan inherited a serious fiscal situation after Jahangir's generous
rank increases and contraction of khalisa. Qazwini's figures may exaggerate to
enhance Shah Jahan, so treat them as court-history claims.

✅ Expansion supplied new revenue assignments but also created new commanders,
garrisons, clients and service expectations. Deccan jama-hasil problems made
southern postings difficult.

⚠️ Administrative evolution was adaptive:

- salary rates were cut;
- actual contingent ratios were regularised;
- du-aspa targeted military obligation;
- month-scale aligned paper rank with expected revenue;
- khalisa and jagir shares were managed; and
- regional recruits widened the service elite.

Adaptation could preserve the system while transferring pressure onto
mansabdars, troopers, jagirdars and cultivators.

## 12. War of succession, 1657-58: endpoint

✅ Shah Jahan's illness in September 1657 generated rumours and military
preparation by Dara, Shuja, Aurangzeb and Murad. There was no accepted
primogeniture. Nomination, dynastic status, military resources, provincial
bases and noble/Rajput connections became decisive.

✅ Dara received extraordinary favour, rank and designation as heir; this
strengthened rather than ended his brothers' fears. Shuja and Murad crowned
themselves; Aurangzeb allied with Murad. Dharmat and Samugarh decided the main
military sequence; Aurangzeb confined Shah Jahan in Agra.

⚠️ Do not make the conflict a simple liberal Dara versus orthodox Aurangzeb
referendum. Religious language mattered, but nobles aligned through interests,
service ties, calculation and survival.

Topic 21 ends at the accession outcome. Aurangzeb's reign begins in Topic 22.

## 16. Rapid recall and traps

- 1627 death/succession transition; **1628 formal accession**.
- Mumtaz: standard **1631**, assigned OCR variant 1630.
- Shahjahanabad begun **1638**.
- ruling class = ranked composite service elite, not racial caste.
- zat = personal status/pay; sawar = cavalry obligation.
- jagir = revenue assignment, not land ownership.
- jama = assessment; hasil = realisation.
- du-aspa sih-aspa doubles specified sawar duty/pay, not zat.
- one-third/one-fourth/one-fifth = effective muster rules.
- month-scale reduces expected pay/obligation to realised jagir value.
- Deccan jagirs could be only 3-4 monthly.
- mansabdars 500 zat+ grew 123 -> 518; jama index 100 -> 176.
- architecture spending was significant, not proven fiscally ruinous.
- Balkh/Qandahar and Deccan are bounded cross-owner contexts.
- war of succession was military-political, not a two-ideology plebiscite.
- verdict: **visible apex + composite expansion + adaptive fiscal strain**.
