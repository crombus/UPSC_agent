## 2. Post-Bahmani regional setting

### 2.1 States and zones

The Bahmani break-up produced the Nizam Shahis of **Ahmadnagar**, Adil Shahis of **Bijapur**, Imad Shahis of **Berar**, Barid Shahis of **Bidar** and Qutb Shahis of **Golconda**. By the Mughal intervention phase, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda were the principal powers; Berar had been absorbed by Ahmadnagar, while Bidar remained a smaller contested state. **Khandesh**, ruled by the Faruqis, was not a Bahmani successor but was the Mughal gateway between Malwa/Gujarat and the Deccan.

| Strategic zone | Why it mattered |
|---|---|
| Khandesh–Burhanpur–Asirgarh | Narmada–Tapti approach, Agra–Surat traffic, army staging and access to the plateau |
| Berar–Balaghat | Northern foothold, revenue claim and corridor between Khandesh and Ahmadnagar |
| Ahmadnagar–Daulatabad–Khirki | Fortified political core and hinge between western Maharashtra, Telangana and routes south |
| Sholapur–Naldurg–Bidar | Recurrent successor-state rivalries and alliance bargaining |
| Karnataka/Coromandel | Post-1636 expansion, tribute, textile/indigo wealth and the later Mir Jumla dispute |
| Konkan and western ports | Horses, shipping, customs, Portuguese cartaz power and inland–maritime linkage |

### 2.2 A connected but frictional political economy

✅ The Vindhyas were not an absolute civilisational wall. Traders, pilgrims, Sufis, soldiers, diplomats and job-seeking elites had long moved between north and south. Tughlaq use of Daulatabad and later Malwa–Gujarat–Khandesh routes intensified connection.

⚠️ Connection did not remove friction. Plateau edges, long supply lines, monsoon rain, river crossings, fodder, fortified nodes and locally controlled countryside made **occupation easier than durable consolidation**.

## 3. Actors and factions: no communal flattening

| Group | Political role | Required qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Deccanis | Locally rooted Muslim service groups and court coalitions | Not one permanent party |
| Afaqis/gharibs | Immigrant elites, many from Iran/Khurasan | Migration or Shia association did not dictate every alliance |
| Habshis | African-origin military and political elites; Malik Ambar is the leading example | Not outsiders without local agency; not identical to one faction at all times |
| Afghans | Soldiers, nobles and landed networks | Could fight beside or against other Deccani groups |
| Marathas | Deshmukhs, landed families, cavalrymen, revenue intermediaries and diplomats | Before Shivaji they were not a single nationalist army or sovereign state |
| Deccani Brahmans | Local revenue records, diplomacy and administration | Their service crossed court boundaries |
| Mughal nobles | Princes, Rajputs, Iranis, Turanis, Afghans, Shaikhzadas and others | Mughal forces were also composite, not a northern religious bloc |

✅ Mores, Nimbalkars, Ghatges, Bhonsales and other Maratha families served different Deccani courts. **Bargirs/bargis** were mounted auxiliaries; **deshmukhs** were locally rooted landed/revenue power-holders. Service and jagir ties could change with political opportunity.

> 🔑 **Trap:** Deccani–afaqi, Sunni–Shia, Habshi–Afghan and Mughal–Deccan labels describe changing fields of patronage, office and survival. They are not timeless communal armies.

## 4. Vocabulary of political control

| Term | Meaning in this topic |
|---|---|
| Diplomacy/embassy | Demand, warning, intelligence-gathering or negotiation before/alongside force |
| Alliance | Temporary cooperation without necessarily accepting permanent subordination |
| Tribute/indemnity | Regular or one-time payment; not automatically direct annexation |
| Suzerainty/over-lordship | Acknowledged superior status, often through service, tribute, **khutba** or **sikka**, while the subordinate court survives |
| Protection | Mughal guarantee paired with hierarchy and payment; not equality |
| Arbitration | Emperor claims authority to settle disputes between Bijapur and Golconda |
| Occupation/garrison | Military possession of a fort or tract; countryside and revenue may remain insecure |
| Annexation/incorporation | Direct imperial claim and provincial administration |
| Durable control | Effective revenue, supply, local cooperation and law-and-order capacity; never infer it from a coloured map alone |

## 5. Why the Mughals moved south

1. ✅ **Imperial sovereignty:** Abu'l Fazl framed the subcontinent as one political field under Mughal over-lordship.
2. ✅ **Security and corridors:** Khandesh, Burhanpur and Asirgarh linked Malwa/Gujarat, Surat and the plateau.
3. ✅ **Deccani instability:** succession struggles and interstate rivalry created both danger and intervention openings.
4. ✅ **Revenue and commerce:** fertile tracts, inland routes, ports and later Karnataka textile/indigo wealth mattered.
5. ✅ **Portuguese context:** cartaz control, pilgrim shipping and pressure around Gujarat/Konkan encouraged wider leverage; Akbar knew that a land empire without a strong navy could not simply expel the Portuguese.
6. ⚠️ **Sectarian peace:** court conflicts concerned Akbar, but this cannot be turned into a civilising justification or the sole cause of conquest.

## 6. Akbar's phase: diplomacy, Ahmadnagar and an incomplete foothold

### 6.1 From Khandesh to the 1591 missions

✅ Early intervention in Khandesh followed Malwa politics. After Gujarat's conquest, Akbar gathered intelligence and used pressure, but Bengal, Bihar and the north-west delayed a full southern campaign. Khandesh's submission in 1576 offered a base, yet did not produce immediate conquest.

✅ In **1591**, four missions demanded acceptance of Mughal suzerainty. Khandesh complied clearly; Ahmadnagar rejected the demand sharply, while other courts offered courtesy and presents without submission. Diplomacy was coercive signalling, not proof of peaceful equality.

### 6.2 Chand Bibi and the Ahmadnagar crisis

✅ After Burhan Nizam Shah's death (1595) and Ibrahim's death in war with Bijapur, rival noble coalitions promoted competing claimants. Miyan Manju, leader of a Deccani coalition, invited Mughal help; Chand Bibi supported the infant **Bahadur** with Habshi backing and sought Bijapuri/Golconda assistance.

✅ Chand Bibi's four-month defence forced compromise in **1596**:

- Bahadur was recognised under her regency;
- Ahmadnagar ceded **Berar**;
- Mughal suzerainty was accepted.

⚠️ This was constrained statecraft, not either simple surrender or solitary heroism. A strong Deccani relief presence limited Mughal options; internal factions limited Chand Bibi's freedom.

### 6.3 Berar war, Ahmadnagar fort and Khandesh

✅ Bijapur, Golconda and Ahmadnagar contested Mughal possession of Berar. Their combined force was defeated in **1597**. Renewed attacks and factional conflict led to a second siege. Chand Bibi explored a subordinate arrangement for Bahadur but was accused of treachery and killed. The Mughals captured Ahmadnagar fort in **1600**.

✅ Akbar then secured **Asirgarh** and annexed **Khandesh** in **1601**. Burhanpur's position on the Agra–Surat route and as a Deccan gateway made this more than a prestige conquest.

⚠️ Yet a Nizam Shahi state survived under Murtaza II. Mughal possession of Ahmadnagar fort, Berar, Balaghat and Khandesh did not equal control over the remaining kingdom or countryside.

## 7. Malik Ambar and the resistance engine

### 7.1 Origin, office and coalition

✅ Malik Ambar was born in Ethiopia, passed through enslavement and commercial networks, entered Deccan service, rose among Habshi elites and became **Peshwa** of the restored Nizam Shahi regime.

His power joined:

```text
Nizam Shahi dynastic legitimacy
        +
Habshi, Afghan and Deccani soldiers
        +
Bijapuri support when interests aligned
        +
Maratha bargi cavalry and deshmukh networks
        +
forts, local intelligence and revenue
        =
mobile resistance without permanent interstate unity
```

### 7.2 Terrain, cavalry and logistics

✅ Maratha light cavalry used speed, dispersion, plunder, intelligence and the cutting of Mughal supplies. The method exploited long imperial communications and the gap between a besieging army's need for grain/fodder and its limited control over the surrounding countryside.

| Mughal strength | Deccani counter | Limit on both sides |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy cavalry and large composite armies | Avoid decisive battle; harass and isolate columns | Mobile troops could delay conquest but struggled to destroy secure imperial bases |
| Siege artillery and engineers | Fort network, provisioning and relief attempts | Artillery could take forts only if supply, time and coordination held |
| Burhanpur/Khandesh base | Attack Balaghat routes and communications | Gateway possession did not guarantee plateau control |
| Mansab/jagir mobilisation | Recruit Mughal allies and induce defections | Loyalty remained negotiable on both sides |
| Paper jama and territorial claim | Deny collection and disrupt cultivation | War reduced the revenue needed to finance war |

⚠️ “Guerrilla” is a useful analytical shorthand, not proof of an unchanged, uniquely Maratha doctrine. Campaigns mixed mobile cavalry, fort warfare, conventional battles, negotiation and defection.

### 7.3 Revenue and Khirki

✅ Later Marathi sources credit Malik Ambar with measurement, village boundaries, rates and replacement of **ijara** (revenue farming/contract) by a **zabti-type** settlement. Some documents mention chain measurement and progressive rates for newly cultivated land.

⚠️ Do not claim a uniform one-third assessment, a complete Todar Mal copy or empire-wide implementation. The evidence is later and incomplete. The secure argument is that fiscal reconstruction, local deshmukh relations and cultivation supported military resistance.

✅ Malik Ambar developed **Khirki** as a Nizam Shahi centre and is associated with its water system. Material remains help explain urban and logistical capacity; they do not by themselves prove every chronicle claim.

## 8. Jahangir-period campaigns and settlements

### 8.1 Reversal and failed coordination

✅ After Akbar's death and Khusrau's rebellion, Malik Ambar recovered territory. Bijapur supplied a fort, troops and a marriage alliance because Ahmadnagar could serve as a buffer. By **1610**, Mughal consolidation had largely collapsed outside secure bases.

✅ A 1611 two-pronged operation failed through rivalry and lack of coordination. This shows that numerical strength without unified command and supply discipline could not solve the Deccan problem.

### 8.2 1616–21: victories without unlimited annexation

✅ Khan-i-Khanan won over Habshi and Maratha nobles and defeated a combined Ahmadnagar–Bijapur–Golconda force in **1616**; Khirki was occupied and burned before withdrawal.

✅ Prince Khurram's pressure in **1617** compelled restoration of Balaghat and delivery of the Ahmadnagar fort key. Jahangir did **not** enlarge Akbar's annexations. Calling the Adil Shah *farzand* (“son”) and seeking accommodation reflected hierarchy plus restraint.

✅ Malik Ambar recovered ground again. In **1621**, another Mughal victory compelled restoration of territories, an additional belt near Ahmadnagar and an indemnity. Old rivalries then broke the united Deccani front.

✅ At **Bhaturi (1624)** Malik Ambar defeated Mughal–Adil Shahi forces and reached a late peak, but his conflicts with Bijapur also weakened sustained anti-Mughal cooperation.

⚠️ Jahangir's limited settlement was a deliberate attempt to avoid deeper commitments, not proof of either military impotence or permanent peace.

## 9. Maratha service networks before Shivaji

Topic 18 owns the **service-network bridge**, not the mature Maratha state.

- ✅ Maratha sardars served Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and the Mughals and could change allegiance.
- ✅ Khan-i-Khanan recruited figures such as Jagdev Rai, Babaji Kate, Udaji Ram and Bhonsale associates.
- ✅ **Jadhav Rao's** killing helped drive **Shahji Bhonsale** into Mughal service.
- ✅ Shah Jahan granted Shahji a mansab of **5000** and jagirs in the Poona region; transfer of those jagirs to Fath Khan contributed to Shahji's defection.
- ✅ Shahji later sustained a Nizam Shahi claimant with Bijapuri support and a large mobile force.

⚠️ This network supplied military skill, local knowledge and political confidence later used by Shivaji, but it did not mechanically or inevitably create the Maratha state.

## 10. Shah Jahan: extinction of Ahmadnagar and the 1636 settlement

### 10.1 Why policy hardened

✅ Khan-i-Jahan Lodi's rebellion and asylum with Ahmadnagar convinced Shah Jahan that an independent Nizam Shahi state would continue to challenge Mughal Berar–Balaghat claims. Unlike Akbar and Jahangir, he chose to extinguish the dynasty, while still avoiding immediate annexation of Bijapur and Golconda.

✅ Shah Jahan offered Bijapur roughly one-third of Ahmadnagar's territory for cooperation and invited Maratha sardars into Mughal service. This was diplomatic isolation plus military pressure.

### 10.2 Fath Khan, Daulatabad and continuing resistance

✅ In **1632**, Malik Ambar's son **Fath Khan** killed the Nizam Shah, installed a puppet and recognised Mughal sovereignty through khutba and sikka. He entered Mughal service.

✅ Mahabat Khan besieged **Daulatabad**, which surrendered in **1633**. The captive ruler was sent to Gwalior, ending the ruling Nizam Shahi line.

⚠️ Dynastic extinction did not end resistance. Shahji raised another claimant, recruited disbanded soldiers, received Bijapuri assistance and continued to cut supplies and hold territory.

### 10.3 The 1636 ahdnamas

#### Bijapur

Bijapur:

- recognised Mughal suzerainty;
- paid an indemnity of **twenty lakh rupees**;
- accepted Mughal arbitration in disputes with Golconda;
- agreed not to interfere with Mughal-protected Golconda;
- agreed to reduce Shahji to submission and, if employing him, keep him away from the Mughal frontier.

In return, Bijapur received a large share of former Ahmadnagar territory, described in the assigned Part II narrative as yielding about **twenty lakh huns** annually. Treat the rupee conversion in OCR editions cautiously.

#### Golconda

Golconda:

- inserted Shah Jahan's name in the **khutba** and removed the Iranian ruler's name;
- accepted loyalty and Mughal protection;
- redirected annual payment from Bijapur to the Mughal emperor, stated as **two lakh huns** in the assigned narrative.

### 10.4 What the settlement was—and was not

```text
Ahmadnagar dynastic extinction and partition
        ↓
Mughal direct provinces in the northern Deccan
        +
Bijapur and Golconda remain autonomous courts
        +
tribute + khutba + protection + arbitration + service conditions
        =
coercive suzerainty and buffer management, NOT total annexation
```

⚠️ Satish Chandra calls the treaties statesmanlike because they reduced direct costs and stabilised hierarchy. The qualification is essential: force created the bargain, the parties were unequal and compliance depended on continuing capacity.

## 11. Provincial incorporation and its limits

✅ Mughal gains were organised through the Deccan viceroyalty and provinces centred on **Khandesh, Berar, Ahmadnagar/Daulatabad and Telangana-related claims**, with Burhanpur as a major command and supply centre. Provincial names and boundaries changed; do not project a single timeless map onto 1601–57.

| Administrative instrument | Function | Structural limit |
|---|---|---|
| Viceroy/subadar and provincial diwan | Command, finance and imperial correspondence | Rival commanders and distance from court could weaken coordination |
| Mansab and jagir assignment | Pay and bind a composite service elite | Deccan jagirs often had inflated **jama** and poor **hasil** |
| Fort garrisons/thanas | Hold gateways and strategic nodes | Fort control could coexist with hostile countryside |
| Revenue assessment and local officials | Convert conquest into income | War, local bargaining, ecology and incomplete records reduced collection |
| Recruitment of Habshi, Deccani and Maratha elites | Obtain intelligence, cavalry and local roots | Defection remained possible when rewards or security changed |
| Treaty buffers | Lower direct administrative cost | Stronger Bijapur/Golconda expansion could upset the balance |

⚠️ Topic 21 owns the full mansabdari crisis. Topic 18's application is narrower: the Deccan was chronically deficit-prone; assessed income exceeded realisation, and subsidies from Malwa/Gujarat/Surat could be required. Territorial expansion did not automatically finance itself.

## 12. Aurangzeb's two Deccan viceroyalties—only to 1657

### 12.1 First tenure, 1636–44

✅ Aurangzeb became Deccan viceroy at about eighteen after the 1636 settlement and held the charge for roughly six years before leaving in 1644. His task was to supervise the newly arranged Mughal Deccan, manage forts/provinces and enforce the treaty order.

⚠️ Do not import his later imperial programme into this tenure. The evidence supports implementation and provincial experience, not a completed plan to annex Bijapur and Golconda in the 1630s.

### 12.2 Second tenure, 1652–57

✅ Reappointed in **1652**, Aurangzeb faced weak realisation from Deccan jagirs and disagreement with Shah Jahan over deficits. With diwan **Murshid Quli Khan**, he attempted to improve cultivation and revenue collection.

✅ Aurangzeb pressed for attacks on Golconda and Bijapur to obtain treasure and territory. His arguments combined fiscal need, treaty arrears, changed regional power and opportunities created by faction or succession.

⚠️ Court rivalry matters but cannot replace policy analysis. Aurangzeb accused Dara of frustrating annexation, yet the assigned source finds no conclusive proof that Dara determined Shah Jahan's final decisions.

## 13. Mir Jumla, Golconda and Bijapur, 1656–57

### 13.1 Why the 1636 order weakened

After 1636, Bijapur and Golconda expanded into the Karnataka/Coromandel field. Mughal diplomacy at first accepted or arbitrated this growth, including a **1646** sharing arrangement favouring Bijapur two-to-one. Expansion increased treasure and territory but also empowered nobles such as Shahji and **Mir Jumla**.

Mughal attitudes hardened because:

- the Mughal Deccan remained fiscally weak;
- Shah Jahan demanded compensation for “benevolent neutrality”;
- tribute and rupee–hun exchange disputes supplied a pretext;
- Shahji and Mir Jumla offered possible service and territorial openings;
- Bijapur's 1656 succession created vulnerability.

### 13.2 Golconda, 1656

✅ Mir Jumla, Golconda's leading noble with extensive Karnataka interests, broke with the Qutb Shahi court and entered the Mughal field. Aurangzeb sought full annexation; Shah Jahan's immediate objective was more limited compensation and submission.

✅ The settlement brought money and the **Ramgir** district to the Mughals. A further dispute arose over whether Mir Jumla's rich Karnataka/Coromandel jagir belonged to the Mughal emperor or Golconda. The area mattered for textile and indigo exports.

⚠️ Mir Jumla was not merely a passive “invitation” to conquest. His service, wealth and territorial claims changed the balance among emperor, viceroy and Qutb Shah.

### 13.3 Bijapur, 1657

✅ Muhammad Adil Shah's death in **1656**, arrears and Bijapur's conduct gave the Mughals a case for war. Shah Jahan authorised either annexation if feasible or a lesser settlement involving former Ahmadnagar territory, a large indemnity and recognition through khutba/sikka.

✅ The final **1657** terms stopped short of full annexation but compelled Bijapur to surrender Nizam Shahi lands received in 1636. Together with Golconda demands, this destroyed confidence in the earlier compact.

> 🔑 **End-point:** by 1657 the Mughals had reopened the annexation question without solving the cost of direct rule. That dilemma belongs to the background of Topic 23, not to a later-war narrative here.

## 14. War technology, forts and environment

| Dimension | Exam use |
|---|---|
| Artillery | Essential in major sieges such as Ahmadnagar, Asirgarh and Daulatabad; effectiveness depended on transport, powder, engineers and supply |
| Forts | Controlled corridors, stores and political legitimacy; a fort's fall did not automatically transfer all dependent countryside |
| Cavalry | Mughal mass and elite contingents versus Deccani/Maratha speed, reconnaissance and supply-cutting |
| Infantry | Garrison, siege and hill/fort functions; do not write a cavalry-only history |
| Monsoon | Rain, roads, rivers, disease, fodder and campaigning seasons affected tempo and supply |
| Geography | Plateau, ghats, Balaghat, Konkan and river basins created multiple theatres rather than one southward line |
| Logistics | Burhanpur, depots, grain, fodder, local intelligence and secure routes converted battlefield victory into staying power |

⚠️ Avoid technological determinism. Mughal artillery did not guarantee consolidation; Maratha mobility did not guarantee decisive victory. Outcomes arose from technology **plus** command, finance, alliance, terrain and time.

## 15. Vijayanagara, Portuguese and sea-commerce context

- ✅ Talikota (1565) and the surviving Vijayanagara/Rayal–Nayaka field explain why Bijapur and Golconda could expand south after 1636.
- ✅ Vijayanagara political survival and Nayaka principalities continued after the sack; do not write “Vijayanagara ended in 1565.”
- ✅ Portuguese cartaz power, Diu/Bassein/Goa positions, pilgrim shipping and horse/coastal commerce formed strategic context.
- ✅ Karnataka/Coromandel textiles and indigo sharpened the Mir Jumla/Golconda dispute.

⚠️ This is supporting context only. Full Vijayanagara–Portuguese ownership remains Topic 09; Topic 18 asks how maritime and southern resources affected Mughal–Deccan policy.

## 16. Sources and evidence limits

| Source class | Examples | What it can support | Main bias/limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mughal Persian chronicles | Abu'l Fazl's *Akbarnama*; Jahangir's *Tuzuk*; Shah Jahan-period chronicles such as Lahori's *Padshahnama* | Campaign sequence, imperial language, appointments, diplomacy | Court teleology, victory claims and hierarchy can compress resistance |
| Deccan court histories | Ferishta and other Nizam/Adil/Qutb Shahi narratives | Rival courts, boundaries, nobles and regional memory | Patronage, retrospective composition and court rivalry |
| Marathi records and later bakhars | Revenue/service traditions, deshmukhs and Maratha memory | Local offices, service networks and remembered settlement | Later testimony cannot prove uniform early-seventeenth-century practice |
| European accounts | Portuguese, Dutch and English commercial reports | Ports, trade, diplomacy, commodities and coastal conditions | Commercial/coastal lens and outsider misunderstanding |
| Treaties, farmans, letters | 1636 ahdnamas, imperial correspondence, Aurangzeb–Shah Jahan exchanges | Stated obligations, demands and fiscal disputes | Normative order is not proof of compliance |
| Inscriptions, khutba/sikka and coins | Titles, sovereign claims and named patrons | Public claims and chronology | Symbolic recognition does not map effective territorial control |
| Architecture and landscape | Asirgarh, Ahmadnagar, Daulatabad, Khirki/Aurangabad waterworks, Golconda/Bijapur forts | Route, defence, urban capacity and patronage | Surviving fabric cannot independently prove campaign motive or exact control |
| Paintings | Jahangir's symbolic image of shooting Malik Ambar | Imperial imagination and political representation | Not a literal event record |

### Source method

```text
claim
  → identify source and patronage
  → state what the evidence directly shows
  → compare rival/local/material evidence
  → separate nominal claim from implementation
  → give a qualified conclusion
```

## 17. Historiographical debates

1. **All-India sovereignty or expansionism?**
   Both matter. Universal over-lordship language justified intervention, but policy often preferred subordinate states to direct annexation.

2. **Malik Ambar: defender or destabiliser?**
   He preserved Nizam Shahi resistance and trained/confidently mobilised Maratha forces; his rivalry with Bijapur and repeated reopening of war also weakened a united front.

3. **Jahangir: failure or restraint?**
   Military victories followed by limited terms support deliberate restraint, though repeated reversals expose incomplete state capacity.

4. **1636: statesmanship or coercive pause?**
   It reduced direct costs and stabilised a hierarchy, but depended on force, tribute and enforceable trust.

5. **1656–57: Aurangzeb's aggression or Shah Jahan's reversal?**
   Aurangzeb pressed annexation, while Shah Jahan oscillated between full conquest and limited extraction. Satish Chandra places responsibility for reopening the dilemma substantially on Shah Jahan; Dara's decisive role is unproven.

6. **Maratha rise: inevitable outcome?**
   Service networks, local landed power and mobile warfare were enabling conditions, not an automatic national awakening or predetermined state.

## 19. Prelims facts and traps

- ✅ Khandesh/Faruqi was a gateway state, not one of the five Bahmani successors.
- ✅ Chand Bibi defended Ahmadnagar and accepted the 1596 Berar settlement for infant Bahadur.
- ✅ Ahmadnagar fort fell in 1600; Asirgarh/Khandesh in 1601.
- ✅ Malik Ambar was Ethiopian/Habshi and Nizam Shahi Peshwa, not a Maratha king.
- ✅ Standard chronology: Malik Ambar died in **1626**; one assigned OCR narrative prints 1627.
- ✅ Jahangir's 1617 restraint was deliberate; it was not total conquest.
- ✅ Daulatabad's 1633 fall ended the ruling Nizam Shahi line, but Shahji continued claimant resistance.
- ✅ The 1636 treaties preserved Bijapur and Golconda as subordinate but autonomous courts.
- ✅ Aurangzeb's Deccan viceroyalties were **1636–44** and **1652–57**.
- ✅ Mir Jumla's Karnataka interests were central to the 1656 Golconda dispute.
- ✅ The 1657 Bijapur treaty recovered former Nizam Shahi lands but did not annex the whole state.
- ❌ Khutba/sikka recognition = complete administration.
  ✅ It demonstrates a sovereign claim; effective control requires revenue, garrison and local compliance.
- ❌ “Mughals versus Muslim Deccan states” means no religious complexity.
  ✅ Alliances crossed ethnic, sectarian and religious lines.
- ❌ Mobile warfare made artillery irrelevant.
  ✅ Sieges and forts remained decisive; mobile forces attacked the logistics enabling them.
- ❌ Vijayanagara disappeared in 1565.
  ✅ The capital was sacked, but Aravidu/Rayal and Nayaka politics continued.

## 20. Executable answer architecture

### 20.1 10 marks / 150 words

**Likely demand:** “Examine why Mughal conquest did not produce durable control in the Deccan.”

1. **Opening (20–25 words):** define the Deccan as connected but frictional and distinguish conquest from consolidation.
2. **Three evidence paragraphs:**
   - Ahmadnagar fort/Asirgarh versus surviving Nizam Shahi countryside;
   - Malik Ambar's cavalry, supply-cutting and local coalition;
   - jagir/revenue deficit and changing allies.
3. **Qualification:** Mughal artillery, forts and diplomacy still produced major footholds.
4. **Verdict:** control remained negotiated and uneven.

**Compression spine:** `fort victory ≠ countryside → mobility/logistics → local elites/revenue → qualified capacity verdict`.

### 20.2 15 marks / 250 words

**Likely demand:** “Assess Malik Ambar's role in Mughal–Deccan relations.”

- **Thesis:** defender and state-builder, but not a solitary or permanently successful liberator.
- **Body I:** Ethiopian/Habshi origin, Peshwa, dynastic legitimacy and coalition.
- **Body II:** bargi mobility, terrain, supply and reversals by 1610.
- **Body III:** revenue reconstruction, Khirki and deshmukh relations—with source caution.
- **Counterpoint:** defeats in 1616/17/21, conflict with Bijapur, no permanent expulsion.
- **Legacy:** confidence/service bridge to later Maratha power without inevitability.
- **Verdict:** greatest significance lay in delaying consolidation and exposing its fiscal-logistical conditions.

### 20.3 20 marks / 250–300 words

**Likely demand:** “Trace the evolution of Mughal Deccan policy from Akbar to the settlements of 1657.”

| Answer block | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| Introduction | policy spectrum: diplomacy → selective annexation → limited settlement → renewed intervention |
| Akbar | 1591 missions; Chand Bibi/Berar 1596; Ahmadnagar 1600; Khandesh 1601; incomplete control |
| Jahangir | Malik Ambar; 1616–21 victories; deliberate restraint |
| Shah Jahan | Ahmadnagar extinction 1633; partition and 1636 ahdnamas |
| Administration | viceroyalty, forts, jagirs, jama–hasil deficit, local recruitment |
| Aurangzeb to 1657 | two viceroyalties; Murshid Quli Khan; Mir Jumla/Golconda 1656; Bijapur 1657 |
| Analytical comparison | suzerainty/tribute/arbitration versus annexation/durable control |
| Historiography | 1636 statesmanship; 1656–57 reversal; no proven decisive Dara conspiracy |
| Conclusion | policy adapted repeatedly but never resolved the cost–control dilemma |

> **Model conclusion:** Mughal power could win sieges, dictate treaties and recruit local elites, but durable Deccan rule required revenue, supply, trust and regional cooperation that imperial victories repeatedly failed to secure together.
