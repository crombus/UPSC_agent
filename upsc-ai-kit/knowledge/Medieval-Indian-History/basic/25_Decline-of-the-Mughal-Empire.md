## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — why the empire unravelled.**

✅ Satish Chandra frames the decline around the **"Responsibility of Aurangzeb"** debate — the long, exhausting **Deccan wars (1687–1707)** overstretched the empire, drained the treasury and tied down the army, while the emperor's absence from the north loosened control there.
- ✅ The empire was still **at the height of its power and prestige** during much of Aurangzeb's reign (even the Ottoman sultan sought his support, 1680) — so decline was **not simply "Muslim vs Hindu"** but structural and administrative.
- ✅ **Jagirdari/be-jagiri crisis:** jama-hasil mismatch, shortage of assignable productive lands, new entrants and corruption weakened the service system and intensified factional competition.
- ✅ Post-1707 emperors faced powerful noble coalitions, succession wars and struggles over the **wizarat**; "weak rulers" alone is not an adequate explanation.

**Foundation — the eighteenth-century aftermath.**

- ✅ **Regional states** emerged from both breakaway provinces and resistance movements: Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad retained nominal allegiance while exercising autonomy; Jats, Sikhs and Marathas built other regional formations.
- ✅ **Foreign invasions** exposed and deepened imperial weakness: **Nadir Shah (1739)** and **Ahmad Shah Durrani** repeatedly affected Delhi and Punjab.
- ✅ **Panipat (1761)** ended the Peshwa's bid for north Indian supremacy, but political contest and regional state-building continued; British ascendancy was not an automatic or immediate consequence.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: The Mughal decline was **multi-causal & structural** — don't reduce it to any single factor or to religion.

- ❌ The empire collapsed immediately after Aurangzeb's death. → It **lingered nominally to 1857**; real power fragmented over the 18th century.
- ❌ Nadir Shah conquered and ruled India. → He **raided (1739)**, looted Delhi, and left — he did not annex.
- ❌ Successor-state governors formally declared independence. → They stayed **nominally loyal** to Delhi while acting autonomously.
- ❌ Panipat III was Mughals vs Marathas. → It was **Marathas vs Ahmad Shah Abdali** (Afghan); the Mughal emperor was a bystander.

### 8.1 Thesis options

| Directive family | Qualified thesis |
|---|---|
| Critically examine decline | "Mughal decline was a convergence of structural crises — jagirdari malfunction, Deccan military exhaustion, noble factionalism, rural resistance and frontier exposure — not reducible to any single ruler's personality or religious policy." |
| Discuss jagirdari crisis | "Satish Chandra's jagirdari-crisis thesis argues that fixed or shrinking productive lands could not sustain a swelling nobility, producing be-jagiri, predatory extraction and political fragmentation — the empire's fiscal skeleton collapsed before its political shell." |
| Assess regional reordering | "The eighteenth century was political reorganisation, not civilisational collapse: Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, Marathas and Sikhs built functioning states with continuing trade and cultural patronage — British supremacy was contingent, not foreordained." |

### 8.2 Named evidence units

| # | Evidence | Claim it supports | Analytical function | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ✅ Deccan wars (1687–1707): 20-year campaign drained treasury, tied down army, swelled nobility with Deccani/Maratha entrants | Military exhaustion + fiscal drain = immediate cause of structural crisis | Connects a specific campaign to the abstract "decline" | Deccan was not the only cause; internal north Indian problems pre-existed |
| 2 | ✅ Jagirdari crisis: jama-hasil gap, be-jagiri, frequent transfers, new entrants | Fiscal-service system broke down structurally | Core of Satish Chandra's explanatory framework | Other historians emphasise agrarian/social factors alongside the fiscal mechanism |
| 3 | ✅ Sayyid Brothers (1713–20): king-makers who deposed Farrukhsiyar (1719) | Central authority had collapsed into noble faction-capture | Illustrates how weak rulers were symptoms, not causes, of decline | Sayyid period also saw jizyah abolition and Maratha bargaining — not purely negative |
| 4 | ✅ Nadir Shah (1739): sacked Delhi, took Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor | External shock exploited pre-existing internal weakness | Proves that imperial defence had failed; but does not explain why | Nadir Shah did not annex; his raid was an event, not a structural cause |
| 5 | ✅ Successor states: Bengal (Murshid Quli Khan), Awadh (Saadat Khan), Hyderabad (Nizam-ul-Mulk) | Regional state-formation continued political and economic functions | Reframes "decline" as devolution, not anarchy | These states remained nominally Mughal; formal break came much later |
| 6 | ✅ Panipat III (1761): Marathas vs Abdali; Peshwa's north Indian bid checked | No single successor could replace the Mughals | Demonstrates that the "successor" question remained unresolved until British intervention | Panipat III did not end Maratha power in the Deccan; it was a north Indian setback |

### 8.3 Counterpoints

- ⚠️ "Decline" is teleological: 18th-c. India had vigorous internal trade, regional cultural patronage and state-building. "Decline" applies to the centre, not to India.
- ⚠️ Aurangzeb's role is debated: Jadunath Sarkar's "bigotry" thesis vs Satish Chandra's structural approach — neither is universally accepted.
- ⚠️ British supremacy required specific military-fiscal innovations (subsidiary alliances, revenue management); it was not an automatic consequence of Mughal collapse.

### 8.4 Verdict scaffolding

- **10-mark:** Jagirdari crisis + Deccan wars (2–3 units); conclude: structural, not personal.
- **15-mark:** Add Sayyid Brothers + successor states + Nadir Shah; conclude: centre collapsed, regions reorganised.
- **20-mark:** Full 6-unit + historiographical debate (Sarkar vs Chandra vs Alam/Subrahmanyam) + non-inevitability of British rule; graded verdict.

## 9. Semantic-completeness synthesis

### 9.1 Period and ownership

Topic 25's core window is **1707-1761**:

- post-Aurangzeb succession and the imperial court;
- noble, jagirdari, zamindari and fiscal-military structures;
- provincial successor states and resistance-based regional powers;
- Marathas, Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas;
- Nadir Shah, Ahmad Shah Abdali and Panipat;
- economic/social continuity and change; and
- historiographical debate.

Aurangzeb's religious/Rajput and Deccan-Maratha narratives remain Topics
22-23. Detailed Mughal society/economy/culture remains Topic 24. Plassey
(1757) may appear as a boundary date, but Company conquest, Buxar (1764),
Diwani (1765) and colonial state formation belong **Modern Indian History Topic
01 onward**.

> ⚠️ “Decline” describes the contraction of Mughal central fiscal-military
> authority, not the sudden disappearance of the dynasty, Indian economy,
> regional states or cultural production.

### 9.2 Court chronology after 1707

| Reign/event | Core significance |
|---|---|
| ✅ Bahadur Shah I, 1707-12 | won succession; attempted conciliation with Rajputs/Marathas/Sikhs but lacked time and resources for durable settlements |
| ✅ Jahandar Shah, 1712-13 | depended heavily on Zulfiqar Khan; short reign exposed noble coalition politics |
| ✅ Farrukhsiyar, 1713-19 | raised by Sayyid Brothers; conflict with them ended in deposition and death |
| ✅ Rafi-ud-Darajat and Shah Jahan II, 1719 | brief Sayyid-backed emperors |
| ✅ Muhammad Shah, 1719-48 | Sayyids defeated in 1720; Nizam left the court; regional autonomy deepened; Nadir Shah struck in 1739 |
| ✅ Ahmad Shah Bahadur, 1748-54 | court factions and Abdali pressure intensified |
| ✅ Alamgir II, 1754-59 | Imad-ul-Mulk dominated; emperor was killed |
| ✅ Shah Jahan III, 1759-60 | brief puppet phase |
| ✅ Shah Alam II from 1759/60 | begins before Panipat, but Buxar/Diwani and later Company relations belong Modern History |

Succession conflict was not merely proof of bad personalities. Without
primogeniture, princes required provincial revenue, armies and noble/Rajput
coalitions; each war destroyed resources and redistributed office.

### 9.3 Emperor, wazir and noble factions

The emperor retained symbolic legitimacy, appointment power, titles and the
Mughal seal even when coercive command shrank. Wazirs and military grandees
sought to control:

- succession and access to the emperor;
- high mansabs and jagirs;
- provincial governorships;
- revenue, military command and court patronage.

✅ The **Sayyid Brothers**, Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali Khan, made and unmade
emperors, deposed Farrukhsiyar in 1719, modified religious policy and bargained
with Marathas. Their fall in 1720 did not restore an uncontested monarchy.

Labels such as Irani, Turani and Hindustani describe origin/service networks,
not permanent coherent parties. Personal patronage, kinship, office, provincial
base and policy divided factions.

### 9.4 Fiscal-military structures

The decline mechanism joined:

1. **mansab claims** — growing ranks and service expectations;
2. **jagir assignment** — transferable revenue claims;
3. **paibaqi** — land/revenue available or awaiting assignment;
4. **be-jagiri** — delay or shortage of suitable productive assignments;
5. **jama-hasil gap** — paper assessment exceeded realised revenue;
6. **frequent transfer** — encouraged short-term extraction;
7. **zamindar resistance/bargain** — collection required local cooperation;
8. **war disruption and chauth** — competing fiscal claims reduced hasil;
9. **thin contingents/corruption** — poor income weakened service; and
10. **noble faction** — competition for office/jagir fed succession politics.

Feedback:

`poor assignment -> weak contingent or harsh extraction -> peasant/zamindar
resistance and flight -> lower hasil -> noble indebtedness/faction -> unstable
transfer and further coercion`.

⚠️ Jagirdari crisis is a structural thesis, not the claim that no jagirs
existed or that agriculture ceased.

### 9.5 Aurangzeb's responsibility and inherited burden

✅ Twenty years of southern war tied down the emperor and army, lengthened
communications, expanded garrisons and incorporated new Deccani/Maratha
claimants. Northern administration and frontier defence suffered.

Aurangzeb's choices mattered:

- annexation removed Bijapur/Golconda buffers;
- failure to settle Marathas prolonged conflict;
- Rajput and religious policies damaged trust.

Yet he did not personally create every fiscal, agrarian or succession problem.
Earlier mansab growth, jama-hasil mismatch, local power and no primogeniture
were inherited structures.

### 9.6 Provincial successor states

These states combined autonomy with Mughal forms, titles and nominal loyalty.

| State | Principal founder/consolidator | Mechanism | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bengal | Murshid Quli Khan | revenue reorganisation, provincial control, court autonomy | remitted tribute and used Mughal offices/titles |
| Awadh | Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk | military-fiscal base, zamindar management, control of Ganga plain | nominal imperial service and Persianate court |
| Hyderabad | Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah | Deccan military/provincial base after leaving Delhi politics | Mughal mansab, administration and legitimacy vocabulary |

They did not initially issue modern declarations of independence. Regional
state formation was devolution and adaptation, not simply secession.

### 9.7 Jats, Sikhs and Rohillas

**Jats**

✅ Churaman and later Badan Singh/Suraj Mal built a Bharatpur-centred power from
zamindari, forts, cultivation and control of routes near Agra-Delhi. Jat state
formation was not only peasant protest or religious conflict.

**Sikhs**

✅ After Guru Gobind Singh and Banda Bahadur, repression and reorganisation
preceded the growth of **misls** and Dal Khalsa in the Punjab. Sikh power drew
on armed community, local bases and frontier conditions. Full later Sikh Empire
history lies beyond this topic.

**Rohillas**

✅ Afghan/Rohilla chiefs established power in Rohilkhand amid Mughal provincial
weakness and migration/service networks. They were one element in north
India's multipolar balance, not invaders simply replacing the empire.

### 9.8 Maratha bid for supremacy

After 1707, Shahu's release, conflict with Tarabai and the rise of the Peshwas
reshaped Maratha politics.

Key mechanisms:

- recognition of chauth and sardeshmukhi claims;
- Peshwa fiscal-military coordination;
- mobile armies and tributary arrangements;
- Holkar, Scindia, Gaekwad and Bhonsle houses;
- bargaining with Mughal emperors, nobles and regional states.

Maratha expansion often sought revenue and political recognition rather than
uniform direct administration. Confederacy widened reach but complicated
central command.

### 9.9 Nadir Shah, 1739

✅ Nadir Shah defeated Mughal forces at **Karnal**, entered Delhi and carried
away enormous wealth, including the Peacock Throne; the Koh-i-Noor entered his
possession.

He exploited:

- weak frontier intelligence and command;
- factional delay;
- poor coordination despite large forces; and
- fiscal-military deterioration.

⚠️ The raid was a devastating shock and revelation of weakness, not the
original cause of decline. Nadir did not remain to annex and govern India.

### 9.10 Ahmad Shah Abdali and Panipat

✅ Ahmad Shah Durrani/Abdali repeatedly invaded from 1748 to 1767, contesting
Punjab and north India. Mughal, Afghan, Rohilla, Awadh, Sikh, Jat and Maratha
interests interacted.

✅ At the **Third Battle of Panipat, 1761**, Abdali's coalition defeated the
Marathas. The Mughal emperor was not the principal belligerent.

Maratha difficulties included:

- extended supply and communication;
- uncertain regional alliances;
- hostile/neutral local powers;
- large non-combatant camp;
- Abdali-Rohilla coordination; and
- operational choices.

⚠️ Panipat checked Peshwa-led north Indian supremacy but did not end Maratha
power. Nor did it automatically establish British paramountcy.

### 9.11 Economy and society: continuity and change

Imperial political fragmentation did not mean uniform economic collapse.

Continuities:

- agriculture and cultivation expansion in several regions;
- internal trade, merchant credit and textile production;
- regional courts and urban consumption;
- Mughal revenue and administrative practices;
- Persianate and regional cultural patronage.

Changes and disruptions:

- warfare, multiple levies and insecure routes in some zones;
- shifting capitals and patronage centres;
- regionalisation of revenue and military markets;
- company influence at some ports;
- repeated north-west invasions.

Regional variation is essential. Bengal's commercial strength could coexist
with Delhi's political contraction.

### 9.12 Culture after imperial contraction

Artists, musicians, literati and craftsmen moved to Awadh, Hyderabad,
Rajasthan, Punjab, Bengal and Maratha courts. Muhammad Shah's Delhi also
remained culturally significant.

⚠️ Cultural decentralisation was not decay alone. It preserved Mughal forms
while generating regional styles and new patronage networks.

### 9.13 Historiographical debate

| Emphasis | Contribution | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| weak successors | notices court instability | makes symptom into cause |
| Jadunath Sarkar/Aurangzeb-centred | highlights Deccan overreach and political/religious choices | can personalise structural problems |
| Satish Chandra jagirdari crisis | links fiscal-service mechanism, nobility and war | must include agrarian/local power and regional variation |
| agrarian crisis/resistance | centres zamindars, peasants and extraction | cannot alone explain court/frontier chronology |
| regional reordering | shows successor-state and economic continuity | should not minimise violence and imperial contraction |
| colonial inevitability | links fragmentation to Company opportunity | teleological; Company victory required separate post-1757 institutions |

Best synthesis:

`inherited institutional strain + Aurangzeb-period choices/war + succession/
noble conflict + local fiscal resistance + regional state formation + external
shocks`.

No single factor is sufficient.

### 9.14 Source and quantification controls

Use court chronicles, newsletters, farmans, revenue documents, regional
records, Marathi/Persian/Sikh materials, European company records, travellers,
coins and material culture comparatively.

- court texts may protect patrons or blame rivals;
- company records privilege company transactions;
- jama is not hasil;
- plunder estimates and army sizes require attribution;
- later nationalist/communal narratives can flatten eighteenth-century
  coalitions;
- absence of Delhi control does not prove absence of governance.
