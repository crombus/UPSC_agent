# Medieval India: Eras, Dynasties and Distinctive Features - Last-Minute Revision Chart

> **Purpose:** One-place revision immediately before Prelims or GS-I.
> **Coverage:** Arab-Turkish contacts, Delhi Sultanate, regional kingdoms, Vijayanagara-Bahmani, Bhakti-Sufi traditions, Mughals, Marathas and the eighteenth-century transition.
> **Source base:** Every relevant Core and Advanced Medieval Indian History file, the Master Chronology and Satish Chandra.
> **Two-pass design:** The master chart gives the non-negotiable, exam-complete Core identity of every era. The optional Advanced layer adds source criticism, debates and nuance; skipping it must not affect the ability to answer Prelims or Mains.

---

## 1. The 60-second chronology ladder

```text
Arab trade and Sind contact
    -> Ghaznavid raids
    -> Ghurid conquest
    -> Mamluk consolidation
    -> Khalji military-fiscal centralisation
    -> Tughlaq expansion, experiments and decentralisation
    -> regional kingdoms + Vijayanagara-Bahmani
    -> Lodi-Afghan phase
    -> Babur and early Mughal conquest
    -> Sur administrative interregnum
    -> Akbar's integrated empire
    -> Jahangir-Shah Jahan consolidation and cultural zenith
    -> Aurangzeb's widest expansion and Deccan strain
    -> Maratha regional power
    -> jagirdari crisis + successor states
    -> Nadir Shah and Abdali
    -> Panipat III, 1761
```

> **Memory line:** Contact -> raid -> conquest -> Sultanate -> centralisation -> regionalisation -> Mughal integration -> overextension -> successor states.

## 2. Master era-identification chart

| Era / dynasty | Approximate date | Political signature | Administrative / economic marker | Cultural / religious marker | Fastest identification clue |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| **Arab-Turkish contact phase** | 8th-12th centuries | Arab Sind contact differs from later Turkish conquest; north India divided among regional Rajput powers | Arab maritime trade; Sind as scientific-cultural conduit; Turkish cavalry and Persianised political culture | Indian numerals and astronomy enter Arabic learning; early Muslim trading settlements and Sufis | Trade and knowledge contact before political conquest |
| **Ghaznavid-Ghurid invasions** | c. 1000-1206 | Mahmud's repeated raids and Punjab base; Muhammad Ghuri's conquest opens the Gangetic plain | Mobile cavalry, frontier bases and tribute/plunder | Al-Biruni and Persianate court context | Ghazni = raids; Ghur = durable conquest pathway |
| **Mamluk / Slave dynasty** | 1206-1290 | Aibak begins; **Iltutmish consolidates**; Raziya contests noble power; Balban strengthens monarchy | Iqta structure develops; Delhi becomes centre; spies and frontier defence | Qutb complex; Persian court culture; Balban's royal ceremonial | Iltutmish = real consolidator; Balban = stern kingship |
| **Khalji dynasty** | 1290-1320 | Wider ruling elite; Alauddin resists Mongols and expands into Rajasthan and the Deccan | **Market control + 50% doab revenue + standing army**; registered merchants and banjaras; state granaries | Siri and Alai Darwaza; cultural activity under Amir Khusrau | Mongol threat -> cheap army -> fixed prices |
| **Tughlaq dynasty** | 1320-1414 | Largest territorial reach followed by rapid breakaways | Muhammad bin Tughlaq: Daulatabad, token currency, doab assessment, Diwan-i-Amir-Kohi; Firuz: canals, towns, heredity and appeasement | Tughlaqabad and austere architecture; Firuz's public works | Ambitious experiment versus welfare-led decentralisation |
| **Sayyid-Lodi phase** | 1414-1526 | Weak Sayyids followed by Afghan Lodi attempt at consolidation | Afghan noble traditions; Sikandar's agrarian and urban measures; Ibrahim's noble conflict | Regional Afghan court culture | First Afghan dynasty; ends at Panipat I |
| **Regional Sultanates** | 14th-16th centuries | Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur and Kashmir flourish outside Delhi | Ports, textiles, regional revenue systems and balance-of-power politics | Bengal literature; Gujarat-Jain architectural synthesis; Jaunpur as "Shiraz of the East"; Zainul Abidin's tolerance | Delhi's decline produces regional prosperity, not a vacuum |
| **Ahom state** | 13th-19th centuries, bounded medieval extension | Tai-Ahom state formation in the Brahmaputra valley | **Paik** rotational labour-military organisation | **Buranjis**; royal moidams at Charaideo | Paik + Buranji + Charaideo |
| **Vijayanagara-Bahmani age** | c. 1336-1565 and beyond | Rival Deccan powers contest Tungabhadra doab, Krishna-Godavari and west-coast ports | Vijayanagara **amaram/nayaks**; Bahmani tarafs; imported horses and port control | Hampi; Bidar; Telugu/Sanskrit/Persian-Deccani culture; broad military-cultural borrowing | Doab + horses + ports, not a simple religious war |
| **Bhakti-Sufi age** | strongly visible c. 12th-17th centuries | Social-religious networks across political boundaries | Khanqah, silsilah and patronage; vernacular devotional communities | Chishti/Suhrawardi; Kabir, Nanak, Chaitanya, Meera and others; saguna/nirguna | Personal devotion, guru/pir, vernacular expression and ritual critique |
| **Sultanate architectural phase** | 13th-16th centuries | Monumental expression of new regimes and regional states | Lime mortar, true arch and dome; Indian craftsmen and local materials | Qutb complex, Alai Darwaza, Tughlaqabad; arabesque plus Indian motifs | Arch-dome technology + Indian craft synthesis |
| **Babur and early Mughal conquest** | 1526-1530 | Panipat I, Khanwa and Ghaghra establish Mughal power | Gunpowder, field artillery and tulughma-style battlefield tactics | Baburnama; Central Asian-Timurid outlook | Panipat I + artillery + Rana Sanga at Khanwa |
| **Humayun-Afghan struggle** | 1530-1540; 1555-56 | Fragile Mughal control; defeats at Chausa and Kanauj; exile and restoration | Weak consolidation and Afghan resurgence | Persian exile strengthens later cultural connections | Lost empire to Sher Shah, recovered it shortly before death |
| **Sur Empire / Sher Shah** | 1540-1555 | Short Afghan empire between Humayun's reigns | Roads and sarais; measurement, rai and patta; one-third share; rupee; dagh and chehra | Sasaram tomb and Afghan architectural transition | Administrative bridge from Sultanate to Akbar |
| **Akbar: conquest and integration** | 1556-1605 | Panipat II; Rajput alliance; Gujarat, Bengal and wider expansion | Mansabdari, jagir, dahsala/zabt, twelve subas, composite nobility | Fatehpur Sikri; translation projects; inclusive imperial culture | Conquest + elite integration, not conquest alone |
| **Akbar: religious-political synthesis** | 1563-1605 | Inclusive sovereignty supports empire-building | Pilgrim tax and jizyah removed; mahzar limits clerical monopoly | Ibadat Khana, **sulh-i-kul**, Tauhid-i-Ilahi | Sulh-i-kul = governing plural empire; not a new mass religion |
| **Jahangir** | 1605-1627 | Consolidation; Mewar settlement; Nur Jahan's exceptional influence | Continuity in mansabdari; English trading privileges at Surat | Naturalistic painting; memoirs; Itimad-ud-Daula | Mewar settlement + Nur Jahan + Thomas Roe |
| **Shah Jahan** | 1628-1658 | Imperial magnificence; Deccan settlement; Balkh/Qandahar setbacks | Mansabdari growth and **month-scale** reveal fiscal strain | Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid and Shahjahanabad | Architectural zenith plus early jagirdari strain |
| **Aurangzeb** | 1658-1707 | Widest territorial extent; Rajput breach; Bijapur-Golconda annexation; prolonged Maratha war | Jizyah restored; Deccan war, jama-hasil gap and jagirdari pressure | Orthodox turn, but statecraft still mixes sharia and imperial necessity | Maximum expansion becomes maximum overextension |
| **Shivaji and Maratha state formation** | c. 1645-1680; expansion after 1707 | Fort-centred regional state, mobile warfare, coronation and later confederacy | Ashtapradhan; chauth; sardeshmukhi; revenue assessment and navy | Maratha political identity with Deccani administrative inheritance | Forts + mobility + chauth, not mere raiding |
| **Later Mughals and successor states** | 1707-mid-18th century | Noble factions and autonomous Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad | Jagirdari/be-jagiri crisis; provincial revenue autonomy | Regional courts sustain Mughal-derived culture | Imperial contraction with regional state formation |
| **External shocks and Panipat III** | 1739-1761 | Nadir Shah sacks Delhi; Abdali invades; Maratha northern bid checked | Loot and frontier weakness intensify political fragmentation | Regional political cultures continue | Nadir Shah 1739; Panipat III = Marathas vs Abdali |

## 3. Dynasty-wise ruler and policy fingerprint

| Ruler / dynasty | One phrase to remember | Signature act / institution | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mahmud of Ghazni | Raider with Punjab base | Somnath raid, 1025; repeated expeditions | Do not call him founder of Delhi Sultanate |
| Muhammad Ghuri | Conquest pathway | Tarain II, 1192; Chandawar, 1194 | Tarain I was a Ghurid defeat |
| Aibak | Beginning | Independent rule from 1206; Qutb building phase | Iltutmish, not Aibak, was the main consolidator |
| Iltutmish | Consolidation | Delhi centre, iqta control, caliphal investiture | Investiture legitimised; it did not create his power |
| Raziya | Monarchy versus nobles and gender barrier | Personal rule and army leadership | Failure was not caused by gender alone |
| Balban | Royal authority | Spies, frontier defence, sijada and paibos | Consolidator, not great territorial conqueror |
| Alauddin Khalji | Military-fiscal state | Market controls, standing army, doab revenue, Malik Kafur | Not a welfare price-control scheme |
| Muhammad bin Tughlaq | Imagination beyond capacity | Daulatabad, token currency, agricultural loans | Intelligent experimenter, not simply "mad" |
| Firuz Shah Tughlaq | Welfare with decentralisation | Canals, towns, hospitals, hereditary offices/iqtas | Peaceful rule weakened central machinery |
| Mahmud Gawan | Bahmani centraliser | Eight tarafs, Goa-Dabhol, Bidar madrasa | Not merely a military commander |
| Krishnadeva Raya | Vijayanagara high point | Expansion, irrigation, literature and Portuguese diplomacy | Talikota occurred after his reign |
| Babur | Gunpowder conquest | Panipat I and Khanwa | Panipat I opponent was Ibrahim Lodi |
| Sher Shah | Administrative bridge | Road-sarai-revenue-currency package | Restored old routes; did not invent every road |
| Akbar | Integration | Mansabdari, Rajput alliance, dahsala and sulh-i-kul | Din-i-Ilahi was not a mass religion |
| Jahangir | Consolidation and court faction | Mewar settlement; Nur Jahan; Roe | Nur Jahan was not a formally crowned sovereign |
| Shah Jahan | Zenith and strain | Monumental architecture; month-scale | Magnificence did not mean absence of fiscal pressure |
| Aurangzeb | Expansion and overextension | Deccan annexations, jizyah, long Maratha war | Decline cannot be reduced to religion alone |
| Shivaji | Regional state-builder | Forts, Ashtapradhan, chauth, navy, coronation | Ashtapradhan was not a modern cabinet |

## 4. High-frequency administration comparison

### A. Sultanate, Sur and Mughal systems

| Dimension | Delhi Sultanate | Sher Shah | Akbar / Mughals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Territorial revenue assignment | **Iqta** to muqti/wali | Sarkar-shiq/pargana framework with strong local officials | **Jagir** to mansabdar; khalisa retained by crown |
| Nature of assignment | Revenue and administrative charge, transferable in principle; not ownership | Revenue administration tied to measurement, officials and zamindars | Right to collect assigned state revenue; no proprietary ownership |
| Army control | Ariz/diwan-i-arz; horse and troop inspection developed unevenly | **Dagh and chehra** | Mansab with **zat and sawar**; dagh and chehra |
| Revenue landmark | Alauddin: measurement and half produce in doab | Measurement, crop rates, patta, roughly one-third share | Zabt and dahsala; ten-year averages; one-third share converted to cash |
| Rural intermediaries | Khuts, muqaddams, chaudhris and local chiefs | Zamindars controlled but retained | Zamindars remain socially and militarily powerful |
| Core caution | Centralised top depended on local society | Important predecessor, not a complete Mughal blueprint | Administration varied by region; zabt did not cover every field |

### B. Terms that must never be mixed

| Term | Correct meaning |
|---|---|
| **Iqta** | Sultanate revenue-administrative assignment; holder = muqti/wali |
| **Khalisa** | Land/revenue reserved for direct crown collection |
| **Jagir** | Mughal assignment of state revenue to a mansabdar; not land ownership |
| **Mansab** | Rank fixing status, pay and service obligation |
| **Zat** | Personal rank and status/pay |
| **Sawar** | Cavalry obligation |
| **Dagh** | Branding of horses |
| **Chehra** | Descriptive roll of soldiers |
| **Zabt** | Measurement-based revenue assessment |
| **Dahsala** | Ten-year average method used in revenue calculation |
| **Batai / ghalla-bakhshi** | Physical crop-sharing |
| **Kankut** | Appraisement using measurement and crop estimate |
| **Nasaq** | Assessment substantially based on previous demand/payment |
| **Patta** | Written statement of cultivated area, crop and revenue demand |
| **Amaram** | Vijayanagara service-revenue territory under nayaks; not identical to transferable iqta |
| **Paik** | Ahom rotational labour-military service subject |

## 5. The distinctions UPSC most often tests

### A. Ghaznavid versus Ghurid

| Dimension | Ghaznavid | Ghurid |
|---|---|---|
| Main Indian pattern | Raids, plunder and Punjab frontier base | Territorial conquest and political implantation |
| Key ruler | Mahmud of Ghazni | Muhammad Ghuri / Muizzuddin |
| High-yield event | Somnath, 1025 | Tarain II, 1192; Chandawar, 1194 |
| Result | Did not establish Delhi Sultanate | Commanders laid basis of Sultanate |

### B. Alauddin Khalji versus Muhammad bin Tughlaq

| Dimension | Alauddin Khalji | Muhammad bin Tughlaq |
|---|---|---|
| Main challenge | Mongols and large standing army | Governing an over-expanded empire |
| Economic device | Coercive Delhi-centred price control | Token currency and ambitious fiscal experiments |
| Agrarian policy | Half produce in doab; intermediaries curbed | Heavy doab assessment; later cultivation loans |
| Why policy failed/ended | Depended heavily on ruler's surveillance and coercion | Forgery, over-assessment, famine and weak implementation |
| Safe verdict | Systematic military-fiscal centralisation | Bold ideas exceeding administrative capacity |

### C. Chishti versus Suhrawardi

| Feature | Chishti | Suhrawardi |
|---|---|---|
| Major centres | Ajmer, Delhi, Ajodhan | Multan and Punjab |
| State relationship | Preferred distance from rulers | More willing to accept grants and office |
| Ethos | Poverty, service, sama and mass contact | Organised order with greater material resources |
| Trap | Not primarily a conversion machine | Not uniformly anti-state |

### D. Vijayanagara versus Bahmani

| Dimension | Vijayanagara | Bahmani |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | c. 1336, Harihara-Bukka tradition | 1347, Alauddin Bahman Shah |
| Administrative marker | Amaram and hereditary nayaks | Tarafs; Mahmud Gawan's eight-taraf reform |
| High point | Deva Raya II; Krishnadeva Raya | Firuz Shah; Mahmud Gawan |
| Strategic interest | Tungabhadra, Krishna-Godavari, ports and horses | Same shared strategic geography |
| Decline marker | Talikota, 1565, but polity lingered | Deccani-Afaqi conflict and five successor states |

### E. Akbar versus Aurangzeb

| Dimension | Akbar | Aurangzeb |
|---|---|---|
| Integration strategy | Rajput partnership, composite nobility, sulh-i-kul | Continued multi-ethnic service but greater orthodoxy and political distrust |
| Tax-religion marker | Jizyah abolished, 1564 | Jizyah reimposed, 1679 |
| Territorial phase | Consolidation and durable integration | Widest expansion but prolonged Deccan overextension |
| Rajput relationship | High partnership and matrimonial-political alliance | Marwar-Mewar breach after Jaswant Singh's death |
| Safe comparison | Inclusive state-building | Orthodoxy plus statecraft failures; not religion as sole cause |

### F. Chauth versus sardeshmukhi

| Levy | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Chauth** | Approximately one-fourth claim/contribution from territories outside direct Maratha core control |
| **Sardeshmukhi** | Additional roughly ten-per-cent superior-deshmukh claim |

> **Trap:** Neither term should be described simply as a modern tax category or as proof of full territorial sovereignty in every area where it was collected.

## 6. Optional Advanced enrichment: deeper interpretation

| Era / issue | Core identification | Optional Advanced correction |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish conquest | Cavalry advantage and political fragmentation | Avoid "foreign superiority versus Indian inferiority." Frontier loss, military organisation, regional rivalry and contingent battles interacted. |
| Sultanate state | Centralised monarchy using iqta and departments | Central authority remained negotiated through local chiefs, village elites and variable provincial reach. |
| Alauddin's reforms | Military-fiscal market control | Barani is indispensable but normative and opinionated; policy description may exaggerate uniform implementation. |
| Muhammad bin Tughlaq | Failed experiments | Separate sound objective from weak sequencing, enforcement and political context; do not diagnose madness. |
| Regional kingdoms | Delhi fragments into autonomous powers | This was positive regional state formation with trade, language and architectural innovation, not only decline. |
| Vijayanagara-Bahmani | Rival Deccan empires | Conflict was strategic and commercial; both borrowed military and cultural practices across religious lines. |
| Bhakti-Sufi | Devotion and social critique | Similar vocabulary does not prove one-way borrowing. "Symbiosis" is safer; neither movement abolished caste or hierarchy immediately. |
| Sultanate architecture | Indo-Islamic synthesis | Arch and dome were not invented by Turks; Indian craftsmen, spolia, local materials and experimentation shaped new forms. |
| Mughal administration | Mansabdari and revenue integration | Systems evolved gradually and varied regionally; paper rank, jama and actual troop/revenue yield could diverge. |
| Akbar's syncretism | Sulh-i-kul and interfaith inquiry | Tauhid-i-Ilahi was an elite spiritual-political discipline, not a new mass religion or modern secularism. |
| Nur Jahan | Powerful queen and court faction | A fixed "Nur Jahan junta" operating unchanged from 1611 is disputed; her active high politics intensified after 1622. |
| Aurangzeb | Orthodoxy, Rajput breach and Deccan war | Balance sharia, secular regulations and political necessity. Neither blanket denial nor universal temple-destruction claims are safe. |
| Shivaji | Fort-centred Maratha state | His institutions adapted Deccani precedents; the state was not an exclusively communal structure. |
| Mughal decline | Deccan strain, factions and successor states | Use a multi-causal frame: jagirdari crisis, jama-hasil gap, zamindars, war, noble competition and regional reordering. |
| Eighteenth century | Imperial contraction | It was not simply a dark age: regional states, commerce and cultural production continued despite warfare. |

> **Firewall rule:** Core alone must support a complete Prelims decision and a complete Mains answer. Advanced may improve sophistication, but no indispensable fact, distinction or answer mechanism may exist only in Advanced.

## 7. Evidence-to-inference rules for medieval questions

| Evidence | Safe inference | Unsafe overreach |
|---|---|---|
| Court chronicle / tarikh | Royal politics, offices, campaigns and elite attitudes | Literal acceptance of every praise or moral judgement |
| Farman | Specific imperial order, privilege or policy | Proof of uniform implementation across the empire |
| Traveller account | Observed markets, court, routes, customs and urban features | Treating one visitor's description as universal social reality |
| Revenue manual | Normative system and administrative categories | Assuming every village followed the model identically |
| Monument | Patronage, technology, style and political symbolism | Exact social tolerance or oppression from architecture alone |
| Coin | Sovereignty, titles, economy and iconography | Complete picture of all exchange or political control |
| Hagiography | Saintly memory, devotional network and values | Exact chronology or verified meeting of every named saint |
| Prashasti / panegyric | Political claims and royal ideology | Treating every conquest claim as permanent annexation |
| Temple or mosque destruction record | A specific act in a particular context | Universal policy across every reign and region |
| Grant or jagir record | Transfer of revenue rights and elite relationship | Ownership of the soil in the modern proprietary sense |

> **Elimination rule:** Medieval evidence is strongest for named institutions, orders, patronage and political claims. It requires caution when used to infer uniform implementation, motives, communal behaviour or the condition of the entire population.
