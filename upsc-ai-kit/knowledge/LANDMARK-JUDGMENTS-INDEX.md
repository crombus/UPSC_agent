# UPSC LANDMARK JUDGMENTS - QUICK REVISION INDEX

> **Format:** case and year -> controlling holding -> best answer use -> present caveat.
> A case must not be quoted through an overruled proposition. Decision year, not filing year,
> is used.

## 1. Constitutional amendment and basic structure

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Shankari Prasad v Union of India** | 1951 | Constitutional amendment was not "law" under Art 13; Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights | Overruled by *Golak Nath*, then broadly restored subject to basic structure |
| **Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan** | 1965 | Reaffirmed Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights | Superseded by later basic-structure doctrine |
| **I. C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab** | 1967 | Parliament could not abridge Fundamental Rights; introduced prospective overruling | Amendment-power holding overruled by *Kesavananda* |
| **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala** | 1973 | Parliament may amend any provision but cannot damage the Constitution's basic structure | Governing doctrine; list of basic features is not closed |
| **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain** | 1975 | Free and fair elections, democracy, rule of law and judicial review are basic features | Struck the election-immunity core of the 39th Amendment |
| **Minerva Mills v Union of India** | 1980 | Limited amending power and FR-DPSP harmony are basic features | Invalidated the 42nd Amendment's unlimited-power clauses |
| **Waman Rao v Union of India** | 1981 | Ninth Schedule insertions after 24 April 1973 face basic-structure review | Pre-*Kesavananda* insertions remain protected by this cutoff |
| **I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu** | 2007 | Post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws can be invalidated when core Fundamental Rights/basic structure are damaged | Ninth Schedule is not blanket immunity |
| **SCAORA v Union of India / NJAC Case** | 2015 | Judicial independence is basic structure; 99th Amendment and NJAC Act invalid | Collegium revived; reform debate remains open |
| **Anjum Kadari v Union of India** | 2024 | Basic-structure doctrine is a test for constitutional amendments, not a free-standing ground to invalidate ordinary legislation | Ordinary law must be tied to violated constitutional provisions |

## 2. Constitutional structure, federalism and Governors

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **In re Berubari Union** | 1960 | Cession of Indian territory requires constitutional amendment; Art 3 alone is insufficient | Its view that Preamble was not part of Constitution was rejected in *Kesavananda* |
| **State of West Bengal v Union of India** | 1963 | India is not a compact of sovereign States; Union could acquire State property within competence | Use with later recognition of federalism as basic structure |
| **Shamsher Singh v State of Punjab** | 1974 | President and Governor normally act on ministerial aid and advice | Discretion is exceptional, not a parallel executive authority |
| **S. R. Bommai v Union of India** | 1994 | Art 356 is reviewable; majority should ordinarily be floor-tested; federalism and secularism are basic features | Court may restore an unlawfully dismissed government |
| **Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India** | 2006 | Bihar Assembly dissolution based on speculative Governor reports was unconstitutional | Assembly was not restored because elections had begun |
| **B. P. Singhal v Union of India** | 2010 | Governors hold office during pleasure but cannot be removed arbitrarily or for mere ideological disagreement | Review is limited; reasons need not initially be disclosed |
| **Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker** | 2016 | Governor ordinarily cannot summon or set Assembly agenda without aid and advice | Speaker-removal-notice rule was referred for reconsideration in *Subhash Desai* |
| **GNCTD v Union of India** | 2018 | Delhi LG is generally bound by aid and advice and cannot refer every disagreement | Public order, police and land remain excluded fields |
| **Shivraj Singh Chouhan v Speaker** | 2020 | Constitutional courts may order a prompt floor test when objective facts create genuine majority doubt | Does not authorise manufactured instability |
| **GNCTD v Union of India (Services)** | 2023 | Delhi government controls services connected with transferred subjects | Practical effect qualified by Parliament's 2023 legislation and pending challenges |
| **Subhash Desai v Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra** | 2023 | Governor cannot use an internal party dispute to call a floor test without objective material | Government not restored because CM resigned before test |
| **State of Punjab v Principal Secretary to Governor** | 2023 | Governor cannot question a constitutionally summoned session and indefinitely obstruct Bills | Use with later Constitution Bench guidance on Arts 200-201 |
| **Tamil Nadu Governor case and 2025 Presidential Reference** | 2025 | No pocket veto; prolonged inaction is reviewable, but courts cannot create general fixed timelines or deemed assent | Quote the later Constitution Bench advisory position with the case-specific order |

## 3. Parliament, legislatures, elections and ordinances

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Ram Jawaya Kapur v State of Punjab** | 1955 | Executive may act within legislative competence without prior legislation, but not against law or rights | Foundation of parliamentary executive power |
| **Mohinder Singh Gill v CEC** | 1978 | ECI has broad residual Art 324 power where law is silent; official orders stand on recorded reasons | ECI cannot contradict legislation or fairness |
| **D. C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar** | 1987 | Repeated ordinance re-promulgation to bypass legislature is a fraud on the Constitution | Strengthened by *Krishna Kumar Singh* |
| **Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu** | 1992 | Tenth Schedule valid; Speaker acts as tribunal and final decision is judicially reviewable | Paragraph 7 invalid for want of State ratification |
| **Kuldip Nayar v Union of India** | 2006 | Open ballot and removal of State-domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha elections upheld | Rajya Sabha federalism does not require member residence |
| **Raja Ram Pal v Speaker, Lok Sabha** | 2007 | Houses may expel members, but privilege is subject to limited review for illegality/unconstitutionality | Arts 122/212 protect procedural irregularity, not substantive illegality |
| **Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar** | 2017 | Ordinance is conditional legislative power; laying is mandatory and routine re-promulgation unconstitutional | Expired ordinances normally create no enduring rights |
| **Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Speaker** | 2020 | Speaker should ordinarily decide defection petitions within three months | Independent tribunal was suggested, not created |
| **Sita Soren v Union of India** | 2024 | MPs/MLAs have no legislative-privilege immunity from bribery prosecution | Overruled *P. V. Narasimha Rao* on bribery immunity |
| **Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms** | 2002 | Voters have an Art 19(1)(a) right to candidate criminal, financial and educational information | Basis of candidate-affidavit disclosure |
| **PUCL v Union of India (Disclosure)** | 2003 | Parliament could not nullify the constitutional right to candidate information through inadequate disclosure | Distinguish from 2013 NOTA case |
| **PUCL v Union of India (NOTA)** | 2013 | Ballot secrecy includes the choice to reject all candidates; NOTA introduced | NOTA does not currently compel re-election |
| **Lily Thomas v Union of India** | 2013 | Sitting legislators lose office immediately upon a disqualifying conviction | A stay of conviction, not merely sentence, can prevent disqualification |
| **Public Interest Foundation v Union of India** | 2018 | Court declined to invent charge-framing disqualification but ordered prominent criminal-case disclosure | Legislative reform remains necessary |
| **Anoop Baranwal v Union of India** | 2023 | Interim CEC/EC appointment committee included PM, LoP and CJI to protect independence | Superseded prospectively by the 2023 appointments law, whose validity is separately contested |
| **Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India (Electoral Bonds)** | 2024 | Anonymous electoral bonds violated voters' right to information; unlimited corporate funding change was arbitrary | Scheme discontinued; not a ban on every corporate donation |

## 4. Judiciary, appointments and tribunals

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **S. P. Gupta v Union of India / First Judges Case** | 1981 | Executive primacy in judicial appointments | Overruled on appointments by Second Judges Case; survives for PIL standing |
| **Second Judges Case / SCAORA v Union of India** | 1993 | Judicial primacy through a CJI-led collegium | Modified by Third Judges Case |
| **Third Judges Case / Special Reference No. 1** | 1998 | Collegium requires institutional consultation with senior-most judges | Governing collegium structure |
| **S. P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India** | 1987 | Tribunals could substitute for High Courts if equally effective and independent | Substitute theory overruled by *L. Chandra Kumar* |
| **L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India** | 1997 | Judicial review under Arts 32 and 226/227 is basic structure; tribunals are supplemental | Tribunal decisions remain reviewable by High Courts |
| **R. Gandhi / Madras Bar Association** | 2010 | Court functions may move to tribunals only with adequate judicial independence and competence | Foundation of later tribunal cases |
| **Madras Bar Association v Union of India** | 2014 | National Tax Tribunal invalid for transferring core High Court work to an insufficiently independent body | Tribunalisation itself was not prohibited |
| **Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank** | 2019 | Tribunal Rules invalidated for independence defects; Finance Act Money Bill issue referred | Money Bill issue not finally settled there |
| **Madras Bar Association line** | 2020-21 | Short tenure, restrictive age and executive-dominated appointments undermined tribunal independence | Check each operative statutory provision separately |

## 5. Equality, caste and reservation

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan** | 1951 | Communal seat allocation violated Arts 15(1), 29(2); DPSPs could not override FRs | Led to First Amendment and Art 15(4) |
| **E. P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu** | 1974 | Equality and arbitrariness are antithetical | Foundation of non-arbitrariness under Art 14 |
| **State of Kerala v N. M. Thomas** | 1976 | Substantive equality permits affirmative measures; Art 16(4) is an enabling equality provision | Reservation is not simply an exception to equality |
| **Indra Sawhney v Union of India** | 1992 | Upheld 27% OBC quota, creamy-layer exclusion and ordinary 50% ceiling; no promotion quota under then Art 16(4) | Promotion position later changed by amendments |
| **State of Karnataka v Appa Balu Ingale** | 1995 | Enforced equal access and condemned untouchability under Arts 14, 15 and 17 | Art 17 has transformative and horizontal force |
| **M. Nagaraj v Union of India** | 2006 | Promotion-reservation amendments valid, subject to data on backwardness, inadequate representation and efficiency | Backwardness-data requirement removed by *Jarnail Singh* |
| **Jarnail Singh v Lachhmi Narain Gupta** | 2018 | No need to prove SC/ST backwardness; cadre-wise inadequate-representation data and efficiency safeguards continue | Does not create automatic promotion reservation |
| **Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India** | 2022 | Upheld 103rd Amendment and EWS reservation by 3:2 | Majority treated ordinary 50% ceiling as inapplicable to EWS |
| **State of Punjab v Davinder Singh** | 2024 | States may sub-classify SCs for fair distribution if supported by evidence | Creamy-layer observations were not a single binding formula |
| **Safai Karamchari Andolan v Union of India** | 2014 | Ordered eradication of manual scavenging, rehabilitation and compensation for sewer deaths | Implementation gap remains central |

## 6. Speech, press, internet and information

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Romesh Thappar v State of Madras** | 1950 | Political discussion is foundational; circulation ban failed under then Art 19(2) | Art 19(2) was widened by First Amendment |
| **Sakal Papers v Union of India** | 1962 | Price-page regulation could not indirectly reduce newspaper circulation | Press freedom forms part of Art 19(1)(a) |
| **Bennett Coleman v Union of India** | 1972 | Newsprint controls judged by their effect on pages, circulation and expression | Effect test, not merely stated policy object |
| **State of U. P. v Raj Narain** | 1975 | Citizens possess a democratic right to know public acts | Subject to lawful privilege and RTI exemptions |
| **S. Rangarajan v P. Jagjivan Ram** | 1989 | Speech cannot be suppressed because opponents threaten violence; danger must be proximate | Rejects a heckler's veto |
| **Shreya Singhal v Union of India** | 2015 | IT Act s 66A invalid for vagueness/chilling effect; distinguished discussion, advocacy and incitement | Did not strike s 69A; intermediary liability was read down |
| **Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India** | 2020 | Speech/trade through internet are protected; shutdown orders must be published, temporary, necessary and proportionate | Did not declare internet access an independent FR |
| **Kaushal Kishor v State of Uttar Pradesh** | 2023 | Art 19(2) grounds are exhaustive; majority recognised some horizontal operation of Arts 19/21 | State is not automatically liable for every ministerial remark |
| **CBSE v Aditya Bandopadhyay** | 2011 | Evaluated answer scripts are information accessible under RTI | RTI does not compel creation of explanations or new records |
| **CPIO, Supreme Court v Subhash Chandra Agarwal** | 2019 | CJI's office is a public authority; privacy, fiduciary interests and public interest require balancing | Disclosure is contextual, not automatic |

## 7. Life, liberty, privacy and criminal procedure

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras** | 1950 | Treated rights as separate silos and Art 21 procedure largely as enacted law | Rejected by *R. C. Cooper*, *Maneka* and *Puttaswamy* |
| **Kharak Singh v State of U. P.** | 1963 | Invalidated domiciliary night visits; majority denied a general privacy right | Anti-privacy proposition overruled by *Puttaswamy* |
| **ADM Jabalpur v Shivakant Shukla** | 1976 | Emergency majority denied habeas corpus during suspension of Art 21 enforcement | Expressly overruled by *Puttaswamy*; Arts 20-21 now non-suspendable |
| **Maneka Gandhi v Union of India** | 1978 | Art 21 procedure must be just, fair and reasonable; Arts 14, 19 and 21 are interconnected | Core substantive-due-process authority |
| **Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar** | 1979-80 | Speedy trial and legal aid are components of Art 21 | Foundational undertrial-prisoner case |
| **Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration** | 1978-80 | Prisoners retain rights except those necessarily curtailed; torture/arbitrary solitary confinement prohibited | Prison walls do not exclude Fundamental Rights |
| **D. K. Basu v State of West Bengal** | 1997 | Mandatory arrest and detention safeguards against custodial abuse | Reflected substantially in criminal-procedure law |
| **Selvi v State of Karnataka** | 2010 | Involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and BEAP violate self-incrimination and mental privacy | Voluntary use requires safeguards |
| **K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India** | 2017 | Privacy is intrinsic to liberty and Part III; intrusion requires legality, legitimate aim, proportionality and safeguards | Overruled contrary privacy/emergency propositions |
| **Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)** | 2018 | Aadhaar framework largely upheld for targeted welfare subject to limits; private mandatory use provisions curtailed | Must be read with later legislation and data-protection law |
| **Common Cause v Union of India (Passive Euthanasia)** | 2018 | Right to die with dignity permits passive euthanasia and advance directives under safeguards | Procedure simplified by Supreme Court in 2023 |
| **Kolkata Municipal Corporation v Bimal Kumar Shah** | 2024 | Art 300A deprivation requires fair legal process including notice, hearing, reasons and compensation framework | Property remains a constitutional, not Fundamental, Right |
| **In Re: Demolition of Structures** | 2024 | Executive demolition cannot punish accused persons/families; prescribed nationwide due-process safeguards | Exceptions include specified public encroachments and court-ordered demolition |

## 8. Religion, secularism and minority education

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Shirur Mutt case** | 1954 | Religious denominations manage religious affairs; secular administration may be regulated; developed essential-practices doctrine | Essential-religious-practices doctrine remains contested |
| **Bijoe Emmanuel v State of Kerala** | 1986 | Respectful refusal to sing anthem on sincere conscience grounds protected | Protects conscience, not disrespect |
| **Shayara Bano v Union of India** | 2017 | Invalidated instant triple talaq through a fractured 3:2 majority | Not a unanimous Art 14 judgment; followed by 2019 statute |
| **Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala** | 2018 | Majority invalidated Sabarimala women's exclusion on equality, dignity and religious-freedom grounds | Not stayed or overruled; broader review/reference remains pending |
| **Ahmedabad St Xavier's College v State of Gujarat** | 1974 | Minority institutions possess administrative autonomy, subject to excellence and anti-maladministration regulation | Autonomy is not immunity |
| **T. M. A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka** | 2002 | Minority status is State-wise; educational autonomy is subject to fairness, merit and anti-profiteering regulation | Clarified by later cases |
| **P. A. Inamdar v State of Maharashtra** | 2005 | State could not impose reservation or appropriate seats in unaided professional institutions | Art 15(5) later enabled special provisions but exempted minority institutions |
| **Pramati Educational Trust v Union of India** | 2014 | RTE obligations cannot be imposed where they abrogate minority-institution rights | Governing minority exception to RTE framework |

## 9. Gender, sexuality, family and autonomy

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Vishaka v State of Rajasthan** | 1997 | Workplace sexual harassment violates Arts 14, 15, 19 and 21; binding guidelines filled legislative vacuum | Institutional framework superseded by POSH Act, 2013 |
| **NALSA v Union of India** | 2014 | Recognised right to self-identified gender and protection under Arts 14-16, 19 and 21 | Later law must be read consistently with dignity and autonomy |

## 10. Education, health, livelihood and disability

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |

| **Unni Krishnan v State of Andhra Pradesh** | 1993 | Free education up to age 14 derived from Art 21 | Admission scheme overruled; rights reasoning led to Art 21A |
| **Society for Unaided Private Schools v Union of India** | 2012 | Upheld RTE 25% entry-level obligation for non-minority unaided schools | Minority institutions exempted by *Pramati* |
| **Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation** | 1985 | Livelihood is part of Art 21; eviction requires lawful and fair procedure | Did not create an absolute right to encroach |

## 11. Administrative law, accountability and anti-corruption

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **State of Orissa v Binapani Dei** | 1967 | Administrative decisions with civil consequences must follow fair hearing | Natural justice is contextual |
| **A. K. Kraipak v Union of India** | 1969 | Administrative/quasi-judicial distinction is thin; reasonable apprehension of bias invalidates decision-making | Core conflict-of-interest case |
| **Union of India v Tulsiram Patel** | 1985 | Natural justice may be excluded in constitutionally permitted exceptional circumstances | Exception remains reviewable |
| **S. N. Mukherjee v Union of India** | 1990 | Administrative/quasi-judicial authorities ordinarily must record reasons | Reasoned orders enable review |
| **Shrilekha Vidyarthi v State of U. P.** | 1991 | State contractual action remains subject to Art 14 non-arbitrariness | Review does not convert courts into contract managers |
| **Tata Cellular v Union of India** | 1994 | Courts review tender decision-making for illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, not commercial merits | Foundation of procurement review |
| **Vineet Narain v Union of India** | 1997 | Institutional safeguards, CVC supervision and tenure protections required for anti-corruption investigation | Many directions later became statutory |
| **Centre for PIL v Union of India / P. J. Thomas case** | 2011 | CVC appointment invalid because selection ignored institutional integrity | Court reviews selection legality, not candidate preference |
| **Subramanian Swamy v Manmohan Singh** | 2012 | Sanction request to prosecute should ordinarily be decided within three months, extendable for legal advice | Sanction differs from investigation approval |
| **T. S. R. Subramanian v Union of India** | 2013 | Civil servants should not obey oral orders; directions should be recorded; Civil Services Boards/minimum tenure encouraged | Implementation remains uneven |
| **Subramanian Swamy v Director, CBI** | 2014 | Special prior protection for senior officers under DSPE Act s 6A violated Art 14 | Distinguish current PC Act s 17A |
| **Prakash Singh v Union of India** | 2006 onward | Seven police-reform directives on security commissions, tenure, separation, boards and complaints authorities | Compliance remains incomplete |
| **State of West Bengal v CPDR** | 2010 | Constitutional courts may order CBI investigation without State consent | Exceptional power, not routine federal bypass |
| **Alok Kumar Verma v Union of India** | 2019 | CBI Director could not be effectively removed/divested without statutory high-powered committee | Protects procedure, not immunity |

## 12. Economy, taxation, property and insolvency

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **R. C. Cooper v Union of India** | 1970 | Rights review examines effect, not merely object; bank-nationalisation compensation could not be illusory | Property framework later amended; effect test survives |
| **R. K. Garg v Union of India** | 1981 | Economic legislation receives wider experimental latitude but remains constitutionally reviewable | Deference is not immunity |
| **Govind Saran Ganga Saran v CST** | 1985 | Valid tax needs clear taxable event, person, rate and measure | Core Art 265 tax-certainty rule |
| **McDowell v CTO and Azadi Bachao Andolan** | 1985, 2003 | Colourable evasion lacks protection, but legitimate tax planning within statute remains lawful | GAAR now supplies statutory anti-avoidance rules |
| **Jindal Stainless v State of Haryana** | 2016 | Non-discriminatory tax is not automatically a trade restriction; compensatory-tax test rejected | Important pre-GST fiscal-federalism doctrine |
| **Swiss Ribbons v Union of India** | 2019 | Upheld IBC; insolvency aims at resolution/value maximisation and CoC has commercial primacy | Mandatory statutory protections remain reviewable |
| **Essar Steel v Satish Kumar Gupta** | 2019 | CoC commercial wisdom generally non-justiciable beyond statutory review grounds | CoC cannot violate mandatory law |
| **Internet and Mobile Association v RBI** | 2020 | RBI had regulatory power but virtual-currency banking restriction was disproportionate on evidence | Did not legalise cryptocurrency |
| **Mohit Minerals v Union of India** | 2022 | GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding; ocean-freight levy invalid | Enacted GST law remains binding unless invalidated |
| **Mineral Area Development Authority v SAIL** | 2024 | Mineral royalty is not a tax; States retain distinct taxing powers under List II | Overruled contrary *India Cement* statement |
| **K. T. Plantation v State of Karnataka** | 2011 | Art 300A deprivation requires valid law, public purpose and non-arbitrariness; compensation cannot be illusory | Market value is not invariably mandatory |

## 13. Environment and natural resources

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **M. C. Mehta v Union of India / Oleum Gas Leak** | 1987 | Created absolute liability for hazardous enterprises without *Rylands* exceptions | Compensation relates to capacity and harm |
| **M. C. Mehta v Union of India / Ganga Tanneries** | 1988 | Polluting industries may be closed if they cannot install treatment; inability to afford compliance is no defence | Environment-development enforcement |
| **Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar** | 1991 | Pollution-free water and air form part of Art 21; PIL cannot serve private vendetta | Rights plus PIL bona fides |
| **Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India** | 1996 | Polluter pays includes ecological restoration costs | State may recover remediation expenditure |
| **Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v Union of India** | 1996 | Precautionary principle and polluter pays are part of Indian environmental law | Does not mean automatic prohibition of all risk |
| **M. C. Mehta v Kamal Nath** | 1997 | State holds rivers, forests and similar resources under public-trust doctrine | Regulated private use is not automatically barred |
| **T. N. Godavarman v Union of India** | 1997 onward | Forest includes dictionary meaning irrespective of ownership/notification; continuing judicial supervision | Read with later statutory forest changes |
| **A. P. Pollution Control Board v M. V. Nayudu** | 1999 | Precaution may shift safety burden to developer; courts need scientific expertise | Contextual, not universal burden reversal |
| **Narmada Bachao Andolan v Union of India** | 2000 | Majority allowed staged project continuation with expert appraisal and rehabilitation conditions | Includes strong dissent; not automatic priority for megaprojects |
| **Lafarge Umiam Mining v Union of India** | 2011 | Environmental decisions require informed, transparent and institutionally sound appraisal | Court reviewed process rather than substituting merits |
| **Goa Foundation v Union of India** | 2014 | Applied intergenerational equity and constrained illegal/excessive mining | Later auctions/laws affect operational status |
| **Common Cause v Union of India (Illegal Mining)** | 2017 | Illegal mining attracted recovery of mineral value under statutory framework | Do not universalise the exact recovery formula |
| **Hanuman Laxman Aroskar v Union of India** | 2019-20 | EIA requires candour, disclosure and genuine application of mind | Project later allowed with added safeguards |
| **Alembic Pharmaceuticals v Rohit Prajapati** | 2020 | Routine ex-post-facto environmental clearance conflicts with environmental rule of law | Court did not automatically close all industries |
| **M. K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India** | 2024-25 | Recognised right against adverse climate effects under Arts 14/21 and required expert balancing of renewables with biodiversity | Remedial scope continues to evolve |

## 14. Labour, social security and livelihood

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India** | 1982 | Work below statutory minimum wage can be forced labour under Art 23 | Economic compulsion can negate real choice |
| **Randhir Singh v Union of India** | 1982 | Equal pay for equal work is enforceable through Arts 14 and 16 where work is substantially equal | Job title alone is insufficient |
| **D. S. Nakara v Union of India** | 1983 | Pension is social welfare, not bounty; arbitrary cutoff among homogeneous pensioners invalid | Does not invalidate every pension cutoff |
| **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India** | 1984 | State must identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labour; dignity under Art 21 | Also foundational PIL case |

## 15. Internal security and surveillance

| Case | Year | Controlling holding | Use / present caveat |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Khudiram Das v State of West Bengal** | 1975 | Preventive-detention satisfaction is reviewable for mala fides, irrelevant material and non-application of mind | Courts do not ordinarily reassess sufficiency |
| **Icchu Devi Choraria v Union of India** | 1980 | Preventive-detention safeguards require strict compliance; State bears burden of showing legality | Procedure is substantive protection |
| **Kartar Singh v State of Punjab** | 1994 | TADA largely upheld with strict safeguards for confessions, prosecution and fairness | TADA lapsed; use by analogy only |
| **PUCL v Union of India / Telephone Tapping** | 1997 | Telephone tapping implicates privacy and speech; procedural safeguards required | Read with *Puttaswamy* and current interception rules |
| **Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v Union of India** | 1997 | AFSPA upheld subject to necessity, proportional use of force and procedural safeguards | Does not create immunity for unlawful force |
| **Nandini Sundar v State of Chhattisgarh** | 2011 | Arming poorly trained civilian auxiliaries in counter-insurgency violated equality and life/liberty | Development and rule of law are security necessities |
| **EEVFAM v Union of India** | 2016-17 | Excessive-force and alleged fake-encounter cases require investigation even in disturbed areas | AFSPA does not provide blanket immunity |
| **NIA v Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali** | 2019 | UAPA bail court conducts limited prima facie review and does not deeply test prosecution evidence at that stage | Must be read with later liberty/delay cases |
| **Union of India v K. A. Najeeb** | 2021 | Constitutional courts may grant bail despite statutory restrictions when prolonged trial delay defeats liberty | Not a general dilution of UAPA threshold |
| **Vernon Gonsalves v State of Maharashtra** | 2023 | Mere possession/association is insufficient without statutory ingredients and reliable prima facie material | Refines, but does not overrule, *Watali* |

## 16. Ten indispensable case chains

| Theme | Chain |
|---|---|
| Amendment power | `Shankari -> Golak Nath -> 24th Amendment -> Kesavananda -> Minerva -> Coelho` |
| Due process | `Gopalan -> R. C. Cooper -> Maneka -> Puttaswamy` |
| Privacy | `Kharak Singh dissent -> PUCL tapping -> Puttaswamy -> data-protection framework` |
| Reservation | `Champakam -> N. M. Thomas -> Indra Sawhney -> Nagaraj -> Jarnail -> Janhit/Davinder Singh` |
| Article 356 | `Bommai -> Rameshwar Prasad -> modern Governor/floor-test cases` |

## Source discipline

- Use the Supreme Court judgment/SCR portals for the controlling text.
- Distinguish majority holdings, concurrences, dissents and observations.
- State when a proposition was overruled, narrowed, legislatively superseded or remains under review.
- Do not cite a later implementation event as part of the original judgment.
- For current litigation, use the latest final order rather than a hearing-stage observation.
