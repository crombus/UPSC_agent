## 1. Snapshot

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Article | ✅ **368** (Part XX) |
| Nature | ✅ Neither too flexible (Britain) nor too rigid (USA) — a **synthesis** |
| Power type | ✅ **Constituent power** of Parliament |
| Limit | ✅ Cannot alter the **basic structure** (*Kesavananda*, 1973) |

## 2. Core idea

✅ Only **Parliament** can initiate an amendment (not states). Bill passed by **special majority**;
federal provisions also need **half the states** to ratify. President **must** give assent
(24th Amdt made this compulsory). Parliament can amend **any** part — including FR — **but not** the
basic structure.

## 3. Three ways to amend

| Way | Majority | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Simple majority** (outside Art 368) | Members present & voting | New states, citizenship, legislative councils, 5th/6th Schedule |
| ✅ **Special majority** | Total-membership + 2/3 present & voting | FR, DPSP, most provisions |
| ✅ **Special majority + half states** | Above + ratification by ½ states | President's election, SC/HC, Centre-state powers, **GST Council**, 7th Schedule |

> 🔑 Trap: state ratification is by **simple** majority, and only **half** the states (USA = 3/4).
> **No joint sitting** for amendment deadlocks.

## 4. Basic Structure — the case sequence ⭐

| Case | Year | What it said |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Shankari Prasad | 1951 | Parliament **can** amend FR |
| ✅ Golak Nath | 1967 | Parliament **cannot** amend FR |
| ✅ 24th Amendment | 1971 | Parliament **can** amend FR (reversed Golak Nath) |
| ✅ **Kesavananda Bharati** | 1973 | Can amend FR **but not basic structure** (13-judge bench) |
| ✅ Minerva Mills | 1980 | Judicial review & limited amending power = basic features |

## 5. Some basic-structure elements

✅ Supremacy of Constitution · secularism · federalism · separation of powers · **judicial review** ·
rule of law · free & fair elections · independence of judiciary · FR-DPSP balance.

## 6. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ Amendment power = **Art 368**, Part XX; only **Parliament** initiates.
- ✅ **Special majority** = total membership + 2/3 present & voting.
- ✅ **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** = basic-structure doctrine (13 judges).
- ✅ President **must** assent to amendment bills (24th Amdt, 1971).
- ✅ **No joint sitting** for constitutional amendment bills.

### 8.0 Direct Mains demands owned by this Core file

**2019 GS-II Q12 — “Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot
be enlarged into absolute power.” In the light of this statement explain whether Parliament under
Article 368 can destroy the Basic Structure by expanding its amending power. · 15 marks · 250 words.**
1. ✅ Locate the power: **Art 368 (Part XX)** — Parliament amends by **addition, variation or repeal**, exercising **constituent power**.
2. ✅ Procedure: initiated only in Parliament; **special majority** in each House (majority of total membership + two-thirds present & voting); federal provisions also need ratification by **half the States**; President **must assent** (24th Amendment, 1971).
3. ✅ The FR-amendability question: *Shankari Prasad* (1951, can amend FR) → *Golak Nath* (1967, cannot) → **24th Amendment (1971)** restoring the power.
4. ✅ The turning point: *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973, 13-judge Bench) — Parliament can amend any part **but cannot damage the basic structure**.
5. ✅ Reinforcement: *Minerva Mills* (1980) — judicial review and **limited amending power** are themselves basic features; the 42nd Amendment's attempt at unlimited power was struck down.
6. ⚠️ Explain the significance: the doctrine reconciles constitutional flexibility with a non-amendable core, so amendment is possible but constitutionalism is protected.
7. Verdict: ⚠️ Art 368 confers wide but **not unlimited** power — constituent power stops at the basic structure.

**2025 GS-II Q12 — "Procedural and substantive limits on Parliament's amending power" · Examine · 15 marks · 250 words.**
1. ✅ Frame the two kinds of limit: **procedural** (how the power must be exercised) and **substantive** (what can never be altered).
2. ✅ Procedural limits: amendment **only in Parliament** (not State legislatures); **special majority**; **no joint sitting** to break a deadlock; State ratification (simple majority of half the States) for federal provisions; **mandatory Presidential assent** (24th Amendment).
3. ✅ Substantive limit: the **basic-structure doctrine** (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) — constituent power cannot alter the Constitution's essential features.
4. ✅ Evidence that the substantive limit is real: *Indira Nehru Gandhi* (1975), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *I.R. Coelho* (2007, Ninth-Schedule laws post-1973 reviewable), *NJAC* (2015, judicial independence).
5. ⚠️ Examine the tension: critics (and the 2023–24 Dhankhar debate) argue the substantive limit dilutes parliamentary sovereignty; the reply is that Indian Parliament is not sovereign — it is bound by the Constitution and judicial review.
6. Verdict: ⚠️ the amending power is hedged by both procedure and an implied substantive ceiling — a limited constituent power under constitutional supremacy.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- *Article 368 was designed as a synthesis — neither as rigid as the American amendment process nor as flexible as the British — and the basic-structure doctrine added the one limit the framers left implicit.*
- *The amending power is constituent, not merely legislative, yet* Kesavananda *established that even constituent power is bounded: Parliament may change the Constitution but not its identity.*
- *The genius and the controversy of the doctrine are the same feature — an un-enumerated, judicially evolving ceiling that protects constitutionalism while giving the Court the last word.*
- *India's amendment federalism is asymmetric: most of the Constitution bends to Parliament's special majority, but the federal core bends only with the consent of half the States.*

### 8.4 Bank A — Procedure and the three amendment routes

| Route | Majority / consent | Provisions covered | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ Simple majority (outside Art 368) | Members present & voting | New States, citizenship, legislative councils, 5th/6th Schedule, delimitation, UTs | **Not** counted as Art 368 amendments |
| ✅ Special majority | Total-membership majority + 2/3 present & voting | FR, DPSP and most of the Constitution | "Total membership" = full strength regardless of vacancies |
| ✅ Special majority + State ratification | Above + **half the State legislatures** by simple majority | Federal provisions (see the list below) | Ratification by **simple** majority; no time limit; consent cannot be forced |

- ✅ **The exact federal list (needs State ratification):** (1) Articles **54 and 55**; (2)
  Articles **73 and 162**; (3) **Article 241**; (4) **Article 279A**; (5) Chapter IV of Part V;
  (6) Chapter V of Part VI; (7) Chapter I of Part XI; (8) any of the **Seventh Schedule** Lists;
  (9) representation of States in Parliament; and (10) **Article 368 itself**.
- ✅ **Procedural discipline:** initiated **only in Parliament**; **each House separately** (no joint sitting); **President must assent** — cannot withhold or return (24th Amendment, 1971).

**📰 Worked example — the two limbs of the special majority, tested on the floor (Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026):**

> ⚠️ **Label first:** this is a **defeated Bill**. It is evidence of *how Art 368(2) operates*, not law,
> not precedent and not a forecast. Full evidence unit: `Parliament.md` §7.4.

| Step | What happened (✅ documented) | What it proves about Art 368 |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | ✅ Introduced in the **Lok Sabha on 16 April 2026** (with the Delimitation Bill, 2026 and the UT Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026); the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** proposed amending **Art 81** to raise the Lok Sabha ceiling from **550 to 850** (up to **815** States + **35** UTs) and letting Parliament fix the census basis for delimitation | ✅ An amendment Bill may be introduced in **either House**, needs **no prior State consent**, and — unlike a Money Bill — is not confined to the Lok Sabha |
| The division | ✅ Voted **17 April 2026**: **298 for : 230 against**, **528 present and voting** | ✅ **Art 368(2)** demands **two** simultaneous limbs — a **majority of the total membership of the House** *and* **two-thirds of the members present and voting** |
| Limb 1 | ✅ 298 exceeds a majority of the total membership (543 elected) | ✅ Satisfied — this is the same threshold an ordinary Bill or a confidence vote clears |
| Limb 2 | ✅ Two-thirds of **528** present and voting ≈ **352**; the Bill polled 298 | ❌ **Failed** — the amendment fell here |
| Consequence | ✅ The Bill was **defeated in the first House**; it never reached the Rajya Sabha, and the linked ordinary Bills **did not pass** | ✅ Defeat in one House ends the amendment; there is **no joint sitting** to break an Art 368 deadlock, and dependent ordinary legislation falls with it |

- **Why this is the sharpest available illustration:** ⚠️ it shows in one division that **a governing majority is not an amending majority**. The most common error in amendment answers is to treat "special majority" as a single number; the 2026 division separates the two limbs cleanly. It also shows that the *entrenchment* design works as intended: the Constitution's rigidity is supplied not by an elaborate convention (as in the USA) but by an **arithmetic that ordinary politics rarely reaches**.
- ⚠️ **Limits on use:** do **not** write that the House "rejected delimitation" (it rejected an amendment Bill), do **not** cite the 850 ceiling as law, do **not** project seat distributions, and do **not** treat the defeat as a precedent binding any future Parliament — a defeated Bill creates **no** legal consequence beyond its own failure. ⚠️ On **State ratification**: the Bill **failed in the Lok Sabha**, so the ratification stage **never arose** and the House never had to decide the question. Whether the proposed changes would have engaged the **representation-of-States** limb of the **Art 368 proviso** is **not settled** — do **not** assert either that ratification was required or that it was not, absent an authoritative legislative or judicial resolution.

### 8.7 Constituent power, incentives and consequences

- ✅ **Mechanism:** Art 368 is **constituent power**, distinct from ordinary legislative power; but *Kesavananda*/*Minerva Mills* subject its exercise to **judicial review** on basic-structure grounds.
- ⚠️ **Incentive effect:** a government with a special majority can reshape most of the Constitution cheaply, so the substantive ceiling is the main check against majoritarian over-reach; it raises the political cost of altering the constitutional core.
- ⚠️ **Who gains/loses:** the doctrine empowers the judiciary as the final arbiter of constitutional identity and constrains transient parliamentary majorities — the core of the "legislature vs judiciary" tension.
- ⚠️ **Comparative note:** ✅ Indian amendment is a **synthesis** — **neither as rigid as the USA** (three-fourths of States) **nor as flexible as the UK** (ordinary legislation); the full India–UK–USA comparison is owned by **`Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md`**.

### 8.8 Criticism, counter-argument and current status

- ✅ **Criticism of the procedure:** no special amending body (unlike the US convention); States cannot initiate amendments; State consent needed only from **half** (vs three-fourths in the USA); no time-frame for ratification; no joint sitting for deadlock; process resembles ordinary legislation but for the special majority.
- ⚠️ **Criticism of the doctrine:** it is judicially created and un-enumerated, giving unelected judges the final word — the **judicial-overreach** argument.
- ⚠️ **Counter-argument:** unlike the UK, Indian Parliament is **not sovereign**; it is a creature of the Constitution, and judicial review (itself a basic feature) is the guarantee of constitutionalism.
- 📰 **Current status (checked 28 August 2026):** the defeated **131st Amendment Bill, 2026**
  illustrates the two simultaneous special-majority limbs; Gazette **S.O. 1922(E), 16 April 2026**
  commenced the 106th Amendment while Article 334A still postpones operational reservation;
  *Anjum Kadari* (2024 INSC 831) confines the free-standing basic-structure test to constitutional
  amendments. The 2023 Dhankhar debate remains analytical context, not a change in law.

### 8.9 Verdict scaffolds

- **Procedure demands:** ⚠️ *Article 368 balances flexibility and rigidity through graded majorities, reserving the federal core for the States' consent while leaving the rest to Parliament's special majority.*
- **Basic-structure demands:** ⚠️ *The doctrine is best defended as the guardian of constitutional identity, not judicial overreach: it does not forbid amendment, only the destruction of the Constitution's essential features.*
- **Limits demands:** ⚠️ *Parliament's amending power is hemmed in on both sides — procedural discipline in its exercise and a substantive ceiling in its reach — so it is a limited constituent power under constitutional supremacy.*

Standard confusion pairs:
- ✅ Simple majority (outside Art 368) vs special majority (Art 368) vs special majority + ratification.
- ✅ State ratification by **simple** majority of **half** the States (India) vs **three-fourths** (USA).
- ✅ **No joint sitting** for amendment bills (unlike ordinary bills under Art 108).
- ✅ Amendment bill — President **must assent**; ordinary bill — President has options (assent/withhold/return).
- ✅ Basic structure was propounded in **Kesavananda (1973)**, not *Golak Nath* (which only made FR unamendable).

### 8.11 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- Do **not** state amendments can be initiated in State legislatures — they can be initiated **only in Parliament** (Art 368); States may only pass a resolution regarding creation/abolition of a legislative council.
- Do **not** state a joint sitting resolves an amendment-bill deadlock — there is **no joint-sitting provision** for amendment bills.
- Do **not** state States ratify by special majority or three-fourths — ratification is by **simple majority of not less than half** the State legislatures.
- Do **not** state the President can withhold or return an amendment bill — the **24th Amendment (1971)** made assent mandatory.
- Do **not** state the basic structure was laid down in *Golak Nath* — it was propounded in **_Kesavananda Bharati_ (1973)**; *Golak Nath* only held FR unamendable.
- Do **not** state all Ninth-Schedule laws are immune from review — laws added **after 24 April 1973** are open to basic-structure review (*I.R. Coelho* 2007).
- Do **not** present the list of basic-structure elements as closed/exhaustive — it is **illustrative and judicially evolving**.
- Do **not** misstate the *Kesavananda* Bench strength — it was a **13-judge** Bench.
- ⚠️ Do **not** cite the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** as law, as a precedent or as a forecast. ### Semantic-completeness ownership and PYQ control

- **Power and routes:** Article 368 in Part XX confers constituent power to amend
  by addition, variation or repeal. Constitution-affecting changes authorised by
  provisions such as Articles 2-4, 11 and 169 may use simple-majority ordinary
  legislation outside Article 368; most amendments require the Article 368 special
  majority; federal subjects additionally require State ratification.
- **Exact procedure:** an amendment Bill may originate in either House and may be
  introduced by a minister or private member without prior Presidential
  recommendation. Each House separately must pass it by a majority of its total
  membership and two-thirds of members present and voting; there is no joint
  sitting. Where the proviso applies, not less than half the State legislatures
  ratify by simple majority. The President shall assent.
- **Exact federal proviso:** Articles 54-55, Articles 73 and 162, Article 241,
  Article 279A, Chapter IV of Part V, Chapter V of Part VI, Chapter I of Part XI,
  any Seventh Schedule List, representation of States in Parliament and Article
  368 itself require the additional ratification route.
- **Doctrine chronology:** Shankari Prasad (1951), Sajjan Singh (decision 1964,
  reporting 1965), Golak Nath (1967), 24th Amendment (1971), Kesavananda Bharati
  (24 April 1973), Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao
  (decision 1980, reporting 1981), S.R. Bommai (1994), L. Chandra Kumar (1997),
  I.R. Coelho (2007) and NJAC (2015) must retain their distinct holdings.
- **Basic structure:** the list is illustrative and case-attributed, not a closed
  list announced wholesale in Kesavananda. Limited amending power and judicial
  review prevent constitutional destruction while allowing broad amendment.
- **Ninth Schedule:** Waman Rao fixes 24 April 1973 as the cut-off; I.R. Coelho
  subjects later insertions to basic-structure review through the impact on
  Fundamental Rights. Article 31B is not absolute immunity.
- **Ordinary-law boundary:** Anjum Kadari, 2024 INSC 831, rejects basic structure
  as a free-standing ground to invalidate ordinary legislation; the challenger
  must identify legislative incompetence, Part III or another constitutional defect.
- **Current controls:** the 131st Amendment Bill, 2026 was defeated and is not law
  or precedent. S.O. 1922(E), 16 April 2026 commenced the 106th Amendment, while
  Article 334A still requires the post-commencement census figures and delimitation
  before reservation becomes operational.
- **Four-ledger/PYQ control:** procedure, case holdings, dates, amendments,
  elements, criticisms, current instruments and every routed 2018-2026 demand
  were checked; unavailable historical keys remain labelled and no status is inferred.
