## Core idea
The **Election Commission of India (ECI)** conducts free & fair elections to **Parliament, State Legislatures,
and the President/Vice-President offices** (NOT local bodies — those are the State Election Commission). The CEC
and two ECs have **equal powers** and decide differences by **majority**.

> 🔑 **EC powers = ABC** — **A**dministrative · **A**dvisory · **Quasi-Judicial**.
> 🔑 **Removal asymmetry:** CEC removed like an SC judge; **other ECs only on the CEC's recommendation**.

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ Appointed by the **President**; term **6 yr / 65 yr**.
- ✅ Voting age lowered **21 → 18** by the **61st Amendment (1988)**.
- ✅ **Art 326** = universal adult suffrage; **Art 329** = bar on court interference (election petitions only).
- ✅ Governed by **RPA 1950 & 1951**.
- ✅ Reforms: **NOTA (2013), VVPAT, exit-poll ban, NRI voting**.

- **2018 · GS-II · Q1** — Demand (neutral rendering): *the EVM controversy and the ECI's task of ensuring trustworthy elections.* Directive: **What are the challenges** (enumerate + evaluate) · **10 marks · 150 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Thesis** — the challenge to "trustworthy elections" is wider than the EVM: it is a four-front problem of *machine trust, money power, criminalisation and neutral administration*. (2) **Machine trust** — EVM–VVPAT integrity; ✅ *Subramanian Swamy v. ECI (2013)* directed a paper trail (VVPAT), and ✅ *ADR v. ECI (26 Apr 2024)* upheld the EVM–VVPAT system, refused 100% count and a return to ballot paper, and added two safeguards (sealed Symbol Loading Units for 45 days; verification of burnt-memory microcontrollers on a runner-up's request). (3) **Money power** — ✅ the electoral-bonds scheme was struck down (**15 Feb 2024**) as violating the voter's right to information under **Art 19(1)(a)**. (4) **Criminalisation & disclosure** — ✅ *ADR (2002)* and ✅ *PUCL (2003)* on candidate-antecedent disclosure; ✅ *Lily Thomas (2013)* on immediate disqualification of convicted legislators. (5) **Administration & neutrality** — **Art 324** powers, the **Model Code**, and the appointment reform after *Anoop Baranwal (2023)*. (6) **Verdict** — the ECI's constitutional mandate is intact; the residual deficits are funding transparency, criminalisation and appointment independence, not the voting machine.
- **2019 · GS-II · Q11** — Demand (neutral rendering): *the grounds on which a people's representative is disqualified under the RPA 1951, and the remedies available.* Directive: **On what grounds … and mention the remedies** · **15 marks · 250 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Frame** — disqualification flows from two layers: the **constitutional** grounds in **Art 102 (Parliament)/Art 191 (State legislature)** (office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, non-citizenship, and "any law made by Parliament"), plus the **Tenth Schedule** for defection, and the **statutory** grounds in the **RPA 1951**. (2) **Statutory grounds** — **Section 8** (conviction for listed offences; two-year-plus sentence → disqualified from conviction and six years after release), **8A** (corrupt practices), **9** (dismissal for corruption/disloyalty), **9A** (government contracts), **10** (office under a government company), **10A** (failure to lodge election-expense accounts), and **11A** (disqualification arising out of conviction and corrupt practices). (3) **Turning-point** — ✅ *Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)* struck down **Section 8(4)**, ending the sitting-member protection, so disqualification is now immediate on conviction. (4) **Remedies** — **Section 11** (ECI may remove or reduce the disqualification period); an appellate **stay of the conviction** (not merely the sentence) suspends disqualification; **Art 103/192** reference of a sitting member's disqualification to the President/Governor, who acts on the **ECI's binding opinion**; and the **election-petition** route (Section 80 onward) to a High Court with appeal to the Supreme Court under **Section 116A**. (5) **Verdict** — the grounds are broad and the remedies real but slow; the reform frontier is time-bound adjudication.
- **2020 · GS-II · Q1** — Demand (neutral rendering): *simplifying the disqualification of a candidate found guilty of corrupt practices under the RPA.* Directive: **Comment** (measured evaluation) · **10 marks · 150 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Position** — the case for simplification is strong, but only within due-process limits. (2) **Present chain (why it is slow)** — a corrupt practice under **Section 123** must be *proved in an election petition* before the High Court, which under **Section 99** names the persons guilty; disqualification then follows under **Section 8A** by the President's order on the **ECI's opinion** — a multi-forum, multi-year process. (3) **Problem** — petitions routinely outlast the House's term, so the sanction rarely bites; **Art 324** gives the ECI no power to disqualify for corrupt practice on its own. (4) **Reform options** — special election benches with a statutory time-limit (the RPA's own directive for a six-month disposal is unmet), empowering the ECI, and disclosure-based deterrence (*ADR 2002*). (5) **Caution** — simplification must not dilute the standard of proof, since a corrupt-practice finding carries a six-year civic disqualification. (6) **Verdict** — simplify the *procedure and timeline*, not the *evidentiary threshold*.
- **2022 · GS-II · Q11** — Demand (neutral rendering): *how election disputes for Parliament or a State Legislature are resolved under the RPA 1951.* Directive: **Discuss and refer to case laws** · **15 marks · 250 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Constitutional bar** — **Art 329(b)**: an election to Parliament or a State legislature can be called in question **only by an election petition** presented as the law provides — no writ jurisdiction during the poll. (2) **Case anchor** — ✅ *N. P. Ponnuswami v. Returning Officer (1952)* held Art 329(b) is an **exclusive** remedy that bars interference with the electoral process midway. (3) **Petition mechanism** — **Section 80** (only by election petition), **80A** (the **High Court** tries it), **81** (filed within 45 days), **83** (contents, including full particulars of any corrupt practice), **86** (dismissal for non-compliance), **98** (High Court's final orders), **99** (naming those guilty of corrupt practices), **100** (grounds to declare an election void), and **116A** (appeal to the **Supreme Court** within 30 days). (4) **Basic-structure anchor** — ✅ *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)* struck down the 39th Amendment's attempt to place the PM's election beyond judicial scrutiny, holding **free and fair elections and judicial review** part of the basic structure. (5) **Verdict** — the design keeps courts out during the poll but guarantees a full judicial remedy after it; the weakness is delay, not access.
- **2022 · GS-II · Q15** — Demand (neutral rendering): *the Election Commission and the evolution of the Model Code of Conduct.* Directive: **Discuss** · **15 marks · 250 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Origin** — ✅ the MCC grew from a State-level code (Kerala, 1960) into an inter-party consensus that Laxmikant dates to **1968**, elaborated by the ECI over successive elections. (2) **Legal character** — ⚠️ it is **non-statutory**, enforced through the ECI's **Art 324** plenary supervision and political consensus, operative from the **announcement of the schedule** until the poll process is complete. (3) **Overlap with law** — parts of the same conduct are separately punishable: bribery, undue influence and communal appeals under **Section 123**/electoral-offence provisions of the RPA and offences now under the **BNS 2023**. (4) **The statutory-backing debate** — a Parliamentary Standing Committee recommended making the MCC legally enforceable; the ECI has resisted, preferring the **speed and flexibility** that statutory litigation would lose. (5) **Verdict** — the MCC's authority rests on moral-plus-Art-324 enforcement; converting it into a statute risks trading swift ECI action for slow court proceedings.

- **2025 · GS-II · Q1** — Demand (neutral rendering): *the meaning of "corrupt practices" under the RPA 1951 and whether disproportionate assets amount to "undue influence".* Directive: **Discuss and analyse** · **10 marks · 150 words**.
  - **Core routing supersedes the older `advanced/27_Election-Commission.md` pointer for this demand.**
  - **Executable route:** (1) **Define** — **Section 123** lists corrupt practices: bribery (123(1)), **undue influence (123(2))**, appeals on religion/race/caste/community/language (123(3)), promotion of enmity, false statements, hiring vehicles, excess expenditure and obtaining a public servant's assistance. (2) **Undue influence** — 123(2) is **any direct or indirect interference with the free exercise of the electoral right**; its target is the *voter's* freedom. (3) **Analyse the asset question** — disproportionate assets concern the *candidate's own enrichment* and are an offence under anti-corruption/income-tax law; they do **not**, without proof of voter coercion or inducement, satisfy 123(2). (4) **Correct route** — the constitutional response is **disclosure**: ✅ *ADR (2002)* and *PUCL (2003)* established the voter's right to know antecedents, and later jurisprudence required disclosure of income sources of a candidate and associates. (5) **Verdict** — unexplained enrichment is a transparency and anti-corruption problem, not automatically a corrupt practice; the fix is fuller disclosure and investigation, not stretching "undue influence".

### 5.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The ECI's constitutional strength lies not in its removal safeguards but in Article 324's plenary supervisory power; the real deficit exposed after* Anoop Baranwal *is the appointment process, not the conduct of the poll.*
- *Disqualification law after* Lily Thomas *is substantively strong but procedurally slow: the grounds bite immediately on conviction, yet corrupt-practice findings arrive after the House has dissolved.*
- *Article 329(b) trades mid-poll judicial access for a guaranteed post-poll remedy — a deliberate design choice that protects the electoral timetable at the cost of speed.*
- *The Model Code works precisely because it is non-statutory: its authority is swift Art 324 enforcement, which statutory litigation would slow rather than strengthen.*
- *"One nation, one election" is a governance-efficiency argument colliding with a federal-autonomy argument; until Article 82A is enacted it is a Bill and cannot be treated as settled reform.*
- *The ECI's quasi-judicial and advisory roles are narrower than its administrative role: it can decide symbol disputes and advise on disqualification, but it cannot itself disqualify for a corrupt practice — that power sits with the courts and the President under Art 103/192.*

### 5.4 Bank A — The ECI as a constitutional body (legal source → appointment → tenure → removal → powers → limits)

| Attribute | Position | Analytical point / limit |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Legal source | **Art 324** creates the EC; **Arts 325–329** govern elections; conduct is regulated by **RPA 1950 and RPA 1951** | The body is constitutional; its *procedure* is statutory — a Bill can reshape procedure but not abolish Art 324 |
| ✅ Composition | **CEC + such other ECs** as the President fixes; three-member (CEC + 2 ECs) permanently since **Oct 1993**; decisions by **majority**, equal powers | The Constitution does **not** fix the number — it is set by the President/law, a flexibility point |
| ✅ Appointment | By the **President**; since the **CEC and Other ECs (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023** on a committee of **PM + a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM + LoP in the Lok Sabha** | ⚠️ The 2023 Act replaced the CJI (whom *Anoop Baranwal* had put on the panel **only "until a law is made by Parliament"**) with a Minister, giving the executive a 2:1 weight. 📰 **Status verified 28 Aug 2026:** challenge **live and undecided**; the official 22 March 2024 order declined interim interference, and no later official stay or merits verdict was located |
| ✅ Tenure | **6 years or 65 years**, whichever is earlier | ⚠️ The Constitution is **silent** on the term; it is fixed by law — do not call six years a constitutional term |
| ✅ Removal | **CEC** removable **only like a Supreme Court judge** (address of both Houses by special majority on proved misbehaviour/incapacity); **other ECs and Regional Commissioners** only on the **CEC's recommendation** | This is the single most examined asymmetry: the two ECs have **weaker** protection than the CEC |
| ✅ Powers | **Administrative** (rolls, schedule, symbols, MCC, cancelling rigged polls), **Advisory** (advising the President/Governor on MP/MLA disqualifications), **Quasi-judicial** (party/symbol disputes) | The ECI **cannot itself disqualify** for a corrupt practice — that runs through the election petition and Art 103/192 |
| ✅ Independence safeguards | CEC's service conditions cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment; expenditure charged; Art 324 plenary "superintendence, direction and control" | ⚠️ Laxmikant's flagged gaps: **no prescribed qualifications, no security of tenure in the Constitution, no bar on post-retirement office** |

- **Case anchor for Art 324's reach:** ✅ *Mohinder Singh Gill v. CEC (1978)* read Art 324 as a **reservoir of power** to act where the law is silent, subject to natural justice — the basis for the ECI's MCC and residual authority.

### 5.5 Bank B — Disqualification grounds and remedies (RPA 1951)

- **Claim → grounds are two-layered.** ✅ **Constitutional** grounds sit in **Art 102/191** (office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, foreign citizenship/allegiance, "any law made by Parliament") plus the **Tenth Schedule** (defection). → **Mechanism:** the "law made by Parliament" clause imports the RPA 1951. → **Limit:** the constitutional list is short; the operative detail is statutory.

| RPA 1951 section | Ground | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Section 8** | Conviction for listed offences; a sentence of **two years or more** disqualifies from conviction and for six years after release | 8(1)–(3) live; **8(4) is dead** after *Lily Thomas (2013)* |
| ✅ **Section 8A** | Disqualification on the ground of **corrupt practices** | Decided by the **President** on the **ECI's opinion** |
| ✅ **Section 9** | Dismissal from government service for **corruption or disloyalty** | |
| ✅ **Section 9A** | Subsisting **government contracts** | |
| ✅ **Section 10** | Holding an **office under a government company** | |
| ✅ **Section 10A** | Failure to **lodge election-expense accounts** in time | ECI may disqualify for up to three years |
| ✅ **Section 11A** | Disqualification arising out of **conviction and corrupt practices** | Links the sanction to the disqualification period |
| ✅ **Section 11** | ECI's power to **remove or reduce** a disqualification | This is itself a remedy |

- **Turning-point:** ✅ *Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)* held **Section 8(4)** (which had shielded sitting members for three months and during appeal) **ultra vires Art 102/191**, so a convicted legislator is disqualified **immediately**. → **Significance:** removed the incumbency shield. → **Limit:** an appellate court's **stay of the conviction itself** (not merely the sentence) suspends the disqualification.
- **Remedies bank (for "mention the remedies"):** ✅ Section 11 (ECI relief) · ✅ appellate stay of conviction · ✅ **Art 103/192** reference of a sitting member's disqualification to the President/Governor on the **ECI's binding opinion** · ✅ the **election-petition** route (below) with appeal to the Supreme Court under **Section 116A**.

### 5.6 Bank C — Corrupt practices (Section 123) and the disproportionate-assets analysis

- **Claim → Section 123 is a closed statutory list, not a general morality clause.** ✅ It covers **bribery (123(1))**, **undue influence (123(2))**, appeals on **religion/race/caste/community/language (123(3))**, promotion of enmity, publication of false statements about a candidate, hiring/procuring vehicles, incurring expenditure beyond the ceiling, and obtaining the assistance of government servants. → **Mechanism:** a corrupt practice voids the election under **Section 100** when proved in an election petition and can attract disqualification under **Section 8A**. → **Limit:** proof requires a **clear election nexus** and a high standard.
- **Undue influence specifically:** ✅ **123(2)** targets **direct or indirect interference with the free exercise of an electoral right** — its object is the **voter's** freedom, not the candidate's wealth.
- **Disproportionate assets → not automatically undue influence.** ⚠️ Unexplained enrichment is a **candidate-side** anti-corruption/income-tax offence; without proof that it **coerced or induced voters**, it does not satisfy 123(2). → **Correct constitutional route:** ✅ *Union of India v. ADR (2002)* and *PUCL v. Union of India (2003)* created the voter's **right to know** a candidate's criminal, financial and educational antecedents under **Art 19(1)(a)**; disclosure and investigation, not a strained reading of "undue influence", is the remedy.

### 5.7 Bank D — Election disputes: the exclusive petition route

- **Claim → the Constitution channels every election dispute into one route.** ✅ **Art 329(b)**: an election to Parliament or a State legislature may be **called in question only by an election petition**. → **Case:** ✅ *N. P. Ponnuswami v. Returning Officer (1952)* held this an **exclusive bar** on judicial interference during the poll. → **Limit:** no writ lies to stop or correct the process midway; the remedy is post-result.

| Section | Function in the dispute |
|---|---|
| ✅ **80 / 80A** | Dispute only by election petition; tried by the **High Court** |
| ✅ **81** | Petition filed within **45 days** of the result |
| ✅ **83** | Must contain full particulars, including of any corrupt practice |
| ✅ **86** | High Court dismisses a petition that does not comply |
| ✅ **98** | High Court's final orders (dismiss / void / void-and-declare-another-elected) |
| ✅ **99** | Naming persons proved guilty of corrupt practices |
| ✅ **100** | Grounds on which an election is declared void |
| ✅ **116A** | Appeal to the **Supreme Court** within 30 days |

- **Basic-structure anchor:** ✅ *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)* struck down the **39th Amendment**'s attempt to immunise the Prime Minister's election, affirming **free and fair elections and judicial review** as basic features.

### 5.8 Bank E — The Model Code of Conduct (routed by 2022 Q15)

- **Claim → the MCC binds by consensus and Art 324, not by statute.** ✅ It began as a State-level code (Kerala, 1960), became an inter-party consensus Laxmikant dates to **1968**, and was progressively elaborated by the ECI. → **Mechanism:** the ECI enforces it under **Art 324** "superintendence, direction and control". → **Limit:** ⚠️ it is **non-statutory**, so its force is administrative and moral, not penal in itself.

| MCC aspect | Position | Legal character / caution |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Trigger and duration | In force from the **announcement of the schedule** until the poll process is complete | Not tied to the notification of the poll alone |
| ✅ Coverage | General conduct, meetings and processions, polling day, the party in power, and election manifestos | A code of *conduct*, not an offence list |
| ⚠️ Overlap with statute | Hate speech, bribery and communal appeals are separately punishable | Under **Section 123**/electoral-offence provisions of the RPA and the **BNS 2023** |
| ⚠️ Enforcement tools | Censure, transfer of officials, deferral of new schemes, direction to prosecute | Fast because it avoids the courts |
| ⚠️ Statutory-backing debate | A Parliamentary Standing Committee urged statutory status; the ECI resists | Statutory backing would gain enforceability but lose **speed and flexibility** |

- **Trap:** ⚠️ "the MCC is a law" is wrong; but "MCC breaches are never punishable" is equally wrong — the *same conduct* may attract the RPA or the BNS.

### 5.10 Mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- **Why the design centralises adjudication in the High Court:** ⚠️ routing disputes to a court (Section 80A) rather than the ECI keeps the *conduct* of the poll and the *judging* of it separate, protecting the ECI's neutrality — but it also **imports judicial delay**, so petitions often outlive the House.
- **Why the appointment change matters institutionally:** ⚠️ the **2023 Act**'s executive-weighted panel improves accountability-to-the-elected-government but weakens the *appearance* of independence that *Anoop Baranwal* sought; the consequence is a live legitimacy debate around every appointment made under it.
- **Why the MCC is enforced administratively:** ⚠️ Art 324 lets the ECI **censure, transfer officials, defer schemes or order prosecution** within days, an incentive structure that a statutory MCC — dependent on courts — would blunt.
- **Consequence of the electoral-bonds ruling:** ⚠️ striking the scheme restored disclosure but left an **unfilled gap** in a transparent funding mechanism, keeping political-finance reform on the agenda.

### 5.11 Criticism, counter-argument, variation and implementation constraints

- **Independence critique:** ⚠️ the Constitution's silence on qualifications, tenure security and post-retirement office (Laxmikant) plus the 2023 appointment formula leave the ECI's independence resting on convention and Art 324, not hard guarantees.
- **Counter (the office is resilient):** ⚠️ *Mohinder Singh Gill* and the ECI's record of enforcing the MCC and cancelling rigged polls show real operational autonomy irrespective of the appointment route.
- **Enforcement-gap critique:** ⚠️ disqualification for corrupt practice is strong on paper but slow in fact; the RPA's six-month disposal norm for election petitions is routinely missed.
- **Implementation constraint on reform:** ⚠️ ONOE, state funding and a statutory MCC each demand constitutional amendment or heavy institutional capacity; none is a mere administrative fix, which is why committees recommend and Bills stall.

### 5.12 Verdict scaffolds

- **Independence verdict:** "The ECI's authority is anchored in Article 324's plenary power; after *Anoop Baranwal* and the 2023 Act, the unresolved question is not whether it can run an election but who chooses those who run it."
- **Disqualification verdict:** "Since *Lily Thomas* the law disqualifies the convicted at once; the remaining injustice is temporal — corrupt-practice findings that arrive after the term they were meant to void."
- **Disputes verdict:** "Article 329(b) and *Ponnuswami* keep the courts out of the polling booth and inside the petition; the design is sound, its delay is the defect."

### 5.13 Prelims close-option distinctions

| Confusion pair | Correct discrimination |
|---|---|
| ECI vs State Election Commission | The ECI runs **Parliament/State-legislature/President–VP** polls; **panchayat/municipality** polls belong to the **SEC (Art 243K/243ZA)** |
| CEC vs other ECs removal | CEC like a **Supreme Court judge**; other ECs only on the **CEC's recommendation** — not identical |
| Term source | 6 years/65 years is fixed by **law**, not the Constitution |
| Anoop Baranwal panel | The CJI was on the panel **only "until a law is made"**; the **2023 Act** replaced him with a Union Minister |
| RPA 1950 vs 1951 | **1950** = seat allotment, delimitation, rolls, election machinery; **1951** = actual conduct, qualifications/disqualifications, corrupt practices, **disputes** |
| Voting age | Lowered **21 → 18** by the **61st Amendment (1988)**, not from 1950 |
| **Delimitation Commissions till December 2023** | ✅ **Four** have been constituted so far — under the Acts of **1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002**; the Delimitation Commission is a **statutory** body whose orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court (**Art 329(a)**) |
| All-India commissions vs J&K 2020–2022 | ✅ The "four till Dec 2023" fact refers to all-India commissions under 1952/1962/1972/2002 Acts. The later **J&K-specific** commission was constituted in 2020 under the post-reorganisation statutory framework and completed its final order in 2022 |
| Aadhaar and electoral rolls | ✅ The 2021 Act permits Aadhaar to establish identity/authenticate roll entries, but non-furnishing for a prescribed **sufficient cause** cannot by itself deny inclusion or delete an entry; alternate documents are governed by rules |
| Electoral-roll qualifying dates | ✅ After the 2021 amendment: **1 January, 1 April, 1 July and 1 October**, not only 1 January |
| **Contesting from multiple Lok Sabha constituencies** | ✅ **Section 33(7), RPA 1951** caps a candidate at **two** constituencies; if elected from both, one seat must be vacated (**Section 70**); before the **1996** amendment there was no cap |

| Electoral bonds | Struck down **15 Feb 2024**; the scheme is **void**, not merely suspended |
| Election-petition forum | Filed before the **High Court** (Section 80A), **not** the ECI; appeal lies to the **Supreme Court** (Section 116A) |
| Art 324 vs Art 329 | **Art 324** = the ECI's plenary superintendence; **Art 329(b)** = the bar on courts during the poll and the exclusive petition route afterwards |
| Model Code of Conduct status | The MCC is **not statutory**; specific breaches are punishable under the **RPA 1951/IPC**, but the code itself rests on ECI **moral/convention** authority |
| One Nation One Election status | Introduced as the **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** (proposing **Art 82A**) and referred to a **JPC** — a **Bill, not law** |

### 5.14 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- ✅ Art 324 creates the EC; Arts 325–329 govern elections; do not attribute the disqualification/dispute detail to the Constitution — it is in the **RPA 1951**.
- ✅ Three-member ECI is a **1993** settlement; the Constitution fixes neither the number nor the term.
- ✅ The **Election Laws (Amendment) Act 2021** and its operational rules must be kept separate:
  the Act creates the Aadhaar/qualifying-date authority and safeguards; rules/forms prescribe the
  implementation and alternate documents. Do not write "mandatory Aadhaar voting".
- ✅ The **J&K Delimitation Commission's 2022 final order** is a completed, bounded exercise. Its
  final constituency allocation/order must be distinguished from any separate recommendations,
  which did not become law merely because the Commission proposed them.
- ✅ *Lily Thomas (2013)* struck **Section 8(4)** only — do not say it struck Section 8 itself.
- ✅ *ADR v. ECI (26 Apr 2024)* **upheld** EVMs and **refused** 100% VVPAT counting; do not report it as ordering a full count or a return to ballot paper. The two safeguards (sealed SLUs for 45 days; runner-up microcontroller checks) are the operative directions.
- ✅ The electoral-bonds judgment (**15 Feb 2024**) rests on **Art 19(1)(a)** right to information; state it as an exact holding, not a general "transparency" remark.
- ⚠️ **One Nation One Election is a Bill, not law.** 📰 **Verified 13 August 2026:** the **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** (proposing **Art 82A**) and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2024** were introduced on **17 Dec 2024** and referred to a **Joint Committee chaired by P.P. Chaudhary**, whose term was **extended on 30 July 2026 to report by the Winter Session 2026**. Permitted: "introduced, referred to a Joint Committee, still under examination". **Prohibited:** calling it enacted, stating **Art 82A** as part of the Constitution, asserting a report has been presented, or forecasting an implementation year (2029/2034 figures circulating in commentary are **projections, not commitments**). Re-verify before dating.
- 📰 **CEC/EC appointments challenge — dated status, verified 28 August 2026.** The challenge to section 7 of the 2023 Act remains live. The official Supreme Court order of **22 March 2024** declined interim interference and treated observations as tentative; no later official stay or merits judgment was located. Permitted: *"the Act operates while the constitutional challenge remains pending."* Prohibited: "struck down", "upheld", "stayed" or "the CJI has been restored". *Anoop Baranwal* created the CJI-inclusive arrangement only **until Parliament made a law**.
- ⚠️ The Model Code is **non-statutory**; do not cite it as a statute, though parts of the same conduct are punishable under the RPA and the BNS 2023.
- ✅ The election-petition sections (**80–100, 116A**) and the disqualification sections (**8–11A**) are all in the **RPA 1951**, not the RPA 1950 — never cite the RPA 1950 for disputes or disqualifications.
