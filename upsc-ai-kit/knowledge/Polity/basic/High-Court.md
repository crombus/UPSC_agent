## Core idea
✅ The **High Court** is the top of a state's judiciary and the protector of Fundamental Rights within the
state. India has **one integrated judiciary**: SC → HCs → subordinate courts. The **7th Amdt (1956)** allows a
**common HC** for two or more states.

> 🔑 **HC writ power (Art 226) > SC (Art 32):** HC issues writs for FRs **AND any other purpose**. *Chandra
> Kumar (1997): HC writ jurisdiction = **basic structure**.*
> 🔑 **Qualification:** citizen + **10 yrs judicial office OR 10 yrs HC advocate** (no "distinguished jurist"
> option, unlike SC).
> 🔑 **Two stages:** the HC Chief Justice initiates a recommendation after consulting
> two seniormost HC colleagues; the Supreme Court collegium then considers it. HC-judge
> transfers use a broader consultation process.

## Subordinate courts (3 tiers)
✅ **District Judge = Sessions Judge** (top) → Subordinate Judge / Chief Judicial Magistrate → Munsiff /
Judicial Magistrate. District judges appointed by the **Governor in consultation with the HC** (Art 233). A
**death sentence needs HC confirmation**.

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ HC judge takes oath before the **Governor**; can be **transferred** to another HC.
- ✅ HC has **supervisory jurisdiction (Art 227)** over all courts/tribunals (except military).
- ✅ **NALSA** (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987) → free legal aid + **Lok Adalats** (Art 39A).
  For the authority ladder and the ordinary/Permanent Lok Adalat, Family Court and Gram Nyayalaya
  distinctions, use `Lok-Adalats-and-Other-Courts.md`.

### 6.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The High Court, not the Supreme Court, is the citizen's first and widest constitutional court — Article 226 reaches further in purpose than Article 32, which is why the framers gave the States a court that protects both fundamental and ordinary legal rights.*
- *The High Court's writ and superintendence powers make it the working guardian of the rule of law in the States, but a power the Constitution protects as basic structure is being hollowed out in practice by vacancies and pendency.*
- *Control over the district judiciary (Article 235) is the constitutional guarantee of a locally independent judiciary; the All India Judicial Service debate is therefore a contest between national uniformity and federal judicial autonomy.*
- *Judicial independence at the High Court level is secured by design — appointment, tenure and transfer safeguards — but delivered only where the State and Union fill vacancies and fund the courts.*
- *Article 226's discretion is its discipline: because the High Court may decline relief where an adequate alternative remedy exists, the writ court remains a guardian of last resort rather than a substitute for the ordinary legal process.*

### 6.4 Bank A — Writ jurisdiction and the Article 32 vs 226 distinction

- **Claim:** the High Court's writ power is wider in purpose than the Supreme Court's. **Provision:** **Art 226** empowers a HC to issue the five writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights **"and for any other purpose"** (i.e., ordinary legal rights too); **Art 32** is confined to **Fundamental Rights**. **Mechanism:** a litigant with a non-FR legal grievance can reach the HC but not the SC under Art 32. **Limitation:** ⚠️ "wider" refers to **purpose**, not reach — Art 32 runs across all of India while Art 226 is territorial.
- **Claim:** territorial reach was deliberately expanded. **Provision:** **Art 226(2)** (inserted by the **15th Amendment, 1963**) lets a HC issue writs to a government/authority/person **outside** its territory if the **cause of action arises wholly or in part within** its jurisdiction. **Mechanism:** prevents a defendant escaping a HC merely by sitting elsewhere. **Limitation:** ⚠️ still bounded by where the cause of action arises — not a national writ.
- **Claim:** the writ power cannot be abolished. **Case:** *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)* held that judicial review under **Art 226/227** (HC) and **Art 32** (SC) is part of the **basic structure** and cannot be excluded, even for tribunals. **Mechanism:** tribunal orders remain reviewable by a HC Division Bench. **Limitation:** ⚠️ review tests legality/jurisdiction, not the merits afresh.
- **Claim:** the "writ jurisdiction" is a defined set of five writs, each with a distinct target. **Provision (Art 226, mirroring Art 32):** the HC issues **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto**. **Mechanism:** each remedy answers a different illegality (see table). **Limitation:** ⚠️ they are the *named* writs plus "directions/orders" — the HC is not confined to their technical English form.

| Writ | Issued to secure |
|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | Production of a detained person; tests the **legality of detention** |
| **Mandamus** | Command to a public authority to **do its legal duty** (not against a private person) |
| **Prohibition** | Stops a lower court/tribunal **exceeding jurisdiction** (before the order) |
| **Certiorari** | **Quashes** an order already passed **without/in excess of jurisdiction** |
| **Quo warranto** | Challenges a person's **holding of a public office** unlawfully |

- **Claim:** the HC is more than a writ court. **Provision:** it is a **court of record (Art 215)** with contempt power, exercises **appellate** civil/criminal jurisdiction over subordinate courts, and four chartered HCs (**Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi**) retain **original civil** jurisdiction alongside writ, admiralty, contempt and election matters. **Mechanism:** combines original, appellate and supervisory roles in one institution. **Limitation:** ⚠️ original civil jurisdiction is the exception, not the norm across HCs.
- **Claim:** the HC's writ discretion is a self-imposed discipline. **Doctrine:** under **Art 226** a HC may **decline** relief where an **adequate alternative remedy** (statutory appeal/tribunal) exists — a rule of practice, not a bar; it does **not** apply where a fundamental right is violated, a fair procedure is denied, or an order is wholly without jurisdiction. **Mechanism:** channels routine disputes to the statutory ladder while keeping the constitutional door open for rights and jurisdictional failures. **Limitation:** ⚠️ the rule is discretionary — the HC can always entertain a writ in a fit case.

| Axis | **Art 32 (Supreme Court)** | **Art 226 (High Court)** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Itself a Fundamental Right** (Ambedkar: "heart and soul") | A **constitutional power**, not a FR |
| Purpose | Only **FR** enforcement | FRs **and any other purpose** (wider) |
| Discretion | The remedy is a right; SC cannot ordinarily refuse | HC has **discretion** (e.g., alternative-remedy rule) |
| Territorial reach | **Whole of India** | HC's territory (extended by the 15th Amdt where the cause of action arises) |

### 6.5 Bank B — Superintendence, withdrawal and control over the district judiciary

- **Claim:** the HC supervises every court and tribunal beneath it. **Provision:** **Art 227** — power of superintendence (administrative and judicial) over all courts and tribunals in its territory, **except** those under a law relating to the Armed Forces; it can be exercised **suo motu**. **Mechanism:** keeps subordinate forums within their jurisdiction. **Limitation:** ⚠️ a supervisory, not appellate, power — used sparingly, not to re-decide facts.
- **Claim:** the HC can pull up constitutional questions itself. **Provision:** **Art 228** — the HC may **withdraw** a case from a subordinate court if it involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**, decide that question (or the whole case) and return it. **Mechanism:** keeps constitutional interpretation with the HC. **Limitation:** ⚠️ confined to constitutional-interpretation questions.
- **Claim:** the district judiciary is controlled by the HC, not the executive. **Provisions:** **Art 233** — district judges appointed by the **Governor in consultation with the HC**; **Art 234** — other judicial-service recruits appointed by the Governor per rules made in consultation with the **SPSC and the HC**; **Art 235** — **control** (posting, promotion, leave) over the subordinate judiciary is **vested in the High Court**. **Mechanism:** insulates lower courts from executive control. **Limitation:** ✅ a **death sentence still requires HC confirmation** — district autonomy is bounded upward.
- **Claim:** the subordinate judiciary is a defined three-tier structure below the HC. **Provision/design (Art 233–237):** the **District Judge = Sessions Judge** sits at the top of the district (original + appellate, civil + criminal); below are the **Subordinate/Civil Judge (Senior Division) / Chief Judicial Magistrate**, then the **Munsiff / Civil Judge (Junior Division) / Judicial Magistrate**; metros add **City Civil Courts** and **Metropolitan Magistrates**. **Mechanism:** a graded hierarchy channels appeals upward to the HC. **Limitation:** ⚠️ "**district judge**" is defined widely in **Art 236** (includes additional/joint/assistant district judges, sessions judges, chief judge of a small-cause court, etc.) — do not read it narrowly.

| Tier | Civil side | Criminal side |
|---|---|---|
| Top | **District Judge** | **Sessions Judge** (same person; capital sentence needs **HC confirmation**) |
| Middle | Civil Judge (Senior Division) | Chief Judicial Magistrate |
| Lowest | Civil Judge (Junior Division) / Munsiff | Judicial Magistrate |

### 6.6 Bank C — Appointment, transfer and the common High Court

- **Claim:** HC judges are appointed on the collegium's advice, with narrower qualifications than the SC. **Provisions:** **Art 217** — appointment by the **President** after consulting the **CJI, the Governor and (except for the CJ) the HC Chief Justice**; qualifications = citizen + **10 years' judicial office OR 10 years as an HC advocate** (**no "distinguished jurist" option**, unlike the SC); retirement at **62**; oath before the **Governor (Art 219)**. **Mechanism:** the SC collegium (**CJI + 2 senior-most**) clears HC names. **Limitation:** ⚠️ the collegium is a judicial creation, not Article text.
- **Claim:** transfer is a safeguard that is also a pressure point. **Provision:** **Art 222** — the President may **transfer** a judge from one HC to another after consulting the CJI (through the collegium); the judge's **consent is not constitutionally required**. **Mechanism:** allows redistribution and, historically, contested "punitive" transfers. **Limitation:** ⚠️ transfers must be in the public interest, not punishment — an independence concern flagged since *Sankalchand Sheth (1977)* and *S.P. Gupta (1981)*.
- **Claim:** one High Court may serve more than one State. **Provision:** **Art 231** (inserted by the **7th Amendment, 1956**) allows a **common High Court** for two or more States or a State and a UT. **Mechanism:** e.g., the **Punjab & Haryana HC** and the **Bombay HC** (Maharashtra, Goa and UTs). **Limitation:** ✅ there are **25 High Courts**, so "one HC per State" is wrong.
- **Claim:** the Constitution provides for continuity when the Bench is short-staffed. **Provisions:** **Art 223** — the President appoints an **acting Chief Justice** when the office is vacant or the CJ is absent; **Art 224** — **additional judges** (for a temporary rise in business) and **acting judges** (for an absent permanent judge), holding office up to age **62**; **Art 224A** — the CJ may, with the President's prior consent, request a **retired HC judge** to **sit and act** in the HC. **Mechanism:** manages workload and vacancies without diluting the appointment safeguards. **Limitation:** ⚠️ these are stop-gaps; an Art 224A retiree is not deemed a serving HC judge.

### 6.7 Mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- **Why Art 226 is the busier court:** because it covers ordinary legal rights and reaches the litigant locally, the HC absorbs the bulk of constitutional and administrative-law litigation; the SC largely hears appeals/SLPs above it.
- **Why control sits with the HC:** vesting posting, promotion and discipline of the district judiciary in the HC (Art 235) removes the executive's leverage over the judges who try most cases — the structural core of independence at the ground level.
- **Consequence:** the HC is simultaneously a rights court, a supervisory authority and the administrative head of the State judiciary; overload in any of these roles (especially pendency) degrades all three.
- **Incentive tension in transfers:** ⚠️ the transfer power (Art 222) can rationalise workloads and break local entrenchment, but the same lever can be perceived as pressure on an inconvenient judge — which is why the collegium, not the executive alone, controls it.
- **Why superintendence is used sparingly:** ⚠️ Art 227 lets the HC keep every subordinate forum within jurisdiction, but if used to re-decide facts it would collapse the appeal structure — so the self-restraint is functional, not merely stylistic.

### 6.8 Criticism, counter-argument, variation and implementation constraints

- **All India Judicial Service (AIJS) debate:** **Art 312** lets Parliament create an AIJS on a Rajya Sabha resolution, but **not for any post below district judge (Art 312(3))**. ⚠️ *For:* national merit recruitment, faster filling of vacancies, uniform standards, diversity. *Against:* it erodes HC control (Art 235) and the State role (Art 233/234), raises language and local-law barriers, and several States and HCs oppose it — so there is **no consensus**.
- **Pendency and vacancies:** 📰 a large share of India's pending cases sits in the High Courts, and HC judge vacancies persist against sanctioned strength (verify current figures before citing). ⚠️ The reply to "tribunalise or expand AIJS" is that the binding constraint is appointments, infrastructure and case management, not a shortage of forums.
- **Access-to-justice constraint:** ✅ **NALSA/SLSA/DLSA** under the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** and **Lok Adalats** (Art 39A) widen access, but they supplement rather than substitute the HC's constitutional role.
- **Independence-in-practice variation:** ⚠️ transfer policy, collegium delays and State cooperation on infrastructure vary, so the same constitutional design delivers unevenly across HCs.
- **Subordinate-judiciary constraint:** ⚠️ even where Art 235 vests control in the HC, actual recruitment depends on the **State Public Service Commission and State finances (Art 233/234)**, so filling district vacancies is a shared responsibility the HC cannot discharge alone.

### 6.9 Verdict scaffolds

- **Writ-power verdict:** "Article 226 makes the High Court the wider of the two constitutional courts in purpose, and *L. Chandra Kumar* makes that power un-removable; the High Court is therefore the everyday guardian of both fundamental and ordinary legal rights."
- **Independence verdict:** "By vesting control of the district judiciary in the High Court (Article 235) and protecting judges through appointment, tenure and transfer safeguards, the Constitution builds independence into the State judiciary — but the guarantee is only as strong as the vacancies that are filled and the courts that are funded."
- **AIJS verdict:** "The All India Judicial Service promises uniform quality but tests the federal balance of Articles 233–235; it is worth pursuing only in a form that preserves High Court control and accommodates local language and law."
- **Access verdict:** "The High Court's writ jurisdiction guarantees the *right* to a remedy, but NALSA, Lok Adalats and the clearing of vacancies determine the *reality* of access — the constitutional promise and its delivery must be judged together."
- **Superintendence verdict:** "Articles 227 and 235 make the High Court the head, not merely the apex, of the State judiciary; its authority over subordinate courts is what turns a hierarchy of courts into a single, independent judicial system."

### 6.10 Prelims close-option distinctions

| Confusion pair | Correct discrimination |
|---|---|
| Art 32 vs Art 226 | Art 32 = a **FR**, only for FRs, all-India; Art 226 = a **power**, for FRs **and other purposes**, territorial (extended by the 15th Amdt) — **Art 226 is wider in purpose** |
| Art 226 vs Art 227 | **226** = issue writs (needs a petition); **227** = superintendence over courts/tribunals (can be **suo motu**), excludes Armed-Forces tribunals |
| HC vs SC retirement age | HC judge retires at **62**; SC judge at **65** |
| "Distinguished jurist" | An eligibility route for the **SC only**, **not** the HC |
| Oath before whom | HC judge takes oath before the **Governor (Art 219)**; the President appoints |
| District judge appointment | **Art 233** — by the **Governor in consultation with the HC**; not by the HC alone and not by the SPSC |
| Control over subordinate courts | **Art 235** vests it in the **High Court**, not the State executive |
| Number of High Courts | **25**, but some are **common** to two+ States/UTs (Art 231) — not one per State |
| Art 228 | **Withdrawal** of a case involving a **substantial constitutional-interpretation question** — not a general appeal power |
| Common HC source | **Art 231**, inserted by the **7th Amendment, 1956** |
| Additional / acting / retired judges | **Art 224** additional & acting judges; **Art 223** acting Chief Justice; **Art 224A** retired judge invited to sit — do not confuse the three |
| Transfer of a HC judge | **Art 222** — by the **President** (through the CJI-led collegium); the judge's **consent is not required** |
| Writ against whom | **Mandamus** never lies against a **private individual**; **habeas corpus** can run even against a **private** detainer |
| Prohibition vs certiorari | **Prohibition** stops proceedings **midway**; **certiorari** **quashes** a completed order — both target **jurisdictional** error |
| HC judge removal | Same route as an SC judge — by the **President after a Parliamentary address (Art 217(1)(b) r/w Art 124(4) procedure and the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968)**; no separate lower standard |

### 6.11 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- ✅ **25 High Courts**; strength is **not fixed** (the President determines the number of judges); HC judges retire at **62**.
- ✅ **Art 226 is wider than Art 32 in purpose** (FRs + other purposes) but **narrower in territorial reach**; the 15th Amendment (1963) extended Art 226 to where the cause of action arises.
- ✅ Judicial review under **Art 226/227 is basic structure** (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997) — it cannot be excluded by statute or by creating a tribunal.
- ⚠️/📰 Do **not** state live HC vacancy counts or pendency totals as settled facts — mark them 📰 and re-verify.
- ✅ The **AIJS cannot cover posts below district judge (Art 312(3))**; it is a **proposal**, not an existing service — do not describe it as operational.
- ⚠️ Distinguish Constitution text (Art 214–237) from judicial interpretation (collegium, basic structure, transfer norms) and statute (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987); the collegium and the Memorandum of Procedure are not in the constitutional text.
- ⚠️ Case law must be an exact proposition: *L. Chandra Kumar* protected HC **review of tribunals**; it did not abolish tribunals.
- ⚠️ Do not confuse the **five writs**: *mandamus* commands a duty, *certiorari* quashes a completed order, *prohibition* stops proceedings midway, *habeas corpus* tests detention, *quo warranto* challenges an office-holder — mixing these is a common Prelims trap.
