## 2. Core idea

✅ DPSP are **ideals/instructions to the State** while making laws and policies. They aim to build a
**welfare state** and secure **social & economic democracy** (whereas FRs secure **political
democracy**). Not legally enforceable, but morally & politically binding — the real force is
**public opinion**.

## 3. The 3 categories (Laxmikant's classification)

| Category | Idea | Example articles |
|---|---|---|
| 🟥 Socialistic | social-economic justice, welfare | ✅ 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47 |
| 🟩 Gandhian | rural reconstruction | ✅ 40 (panchayats), 43 (cottage industry), 43B (co-ops), 46, 47 (prohibition), 48 (cow slaughter) |
| 🟦 Liberal-Intellectual | modern liberal state | ✅ 44 (UCC), 45, 48 (agri), 48A (environment), 49, 50 (separate judiciary), 51 (world peace) |

## 5. Amendments that added DPSP

- ✅ **42nd (1976):** substituted/recast Art 39(f), and inserted Arts 39A (legal aid), 43A (workers in management) and 48A (environment).
- ✅ **44th (1978):** Art 38 — minimise inequalities.
- ✅ **86th (2002):** Art 45 changed → elementary education became FR (Art 21A).
- ✅ **97th (2011):** Art 43B — co-operative societies.

## 7. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ DPSP = Articles **36–51**, Part IV; borrowed from **Ireland**.
- ✅ **Non-justiciable** (Art 37); no court enforcement.
- ✅ Art **44** = UCC; Art **40** = panchayats; Art **50** = separate judiciary.
- ✅ **Present position:** Neither Part III nor Part IV enjoys abstract, unlimited supremacy. Parliament may
  amend rights to pursue DPSP without damaging the **basic structure**, while the harmony and balance between
  the two Parts is itself basic structure (*Minerva Mills*, 1980). The surviving Art 31C shield is the specific
  exception for genuine Art 39(b)/(c) laws against Arts 14 and 19.

➡️ The full case-law spine (Champakam → Golaknath → Kesavananda → Minerva Mills), the named critics,
Art 31C and the directives outside Part IV are all held **in this file** (§8.4–§8.10).

### 8.0 Direct Mains demands owned by this Core file

**2023 GS-II Q2 — "Entitlement to free legal aid and the role of NALSA" · Assess · 10 marks · 150 words.**
1. ✅ Constitutional entitlement: **Art 39A** (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976) directs the State to secure **equal justice and free legal aid**; ⚠️ it is also read into **Art 21** as part of a fair procedure (*Hussainara Khatoon* 1979; *M.H. Hoskot* 1978).
2. ✅ Statutory architecture: the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** created a four-tier structure — **NALSA** (apex) → State Legal Services Authorities → District Legal Services Authorities → Taluk committees.
3. ✅ NALSA's role: frame policy, provide free legal aid to eligible persons, and organise **Lok Adalats** (statutory, with civil-court status and **binding, non-appealable awards**), legal-awareness camps and legal-aid clinics.
4. ⚠️ Assess — achievements: mass dispute disposal through Lok Adalats, wide eligibility (SC/ST, women, children, persons with disability, persons in custody, low-income litigants), and decentralised reach.
5. ⚠️ Assess — limits: uneven quality of assigned counsel, low awareness, under-utilisation, and persistent pendency despite the machinery.
6. Verdict: ⚠️ free legal aid converts a non-justiciable DPSP into an operational entitlement, but delivery still lags the promise; the priority is quality and awareness, not new institutions.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The DPSP are non-justiciable but not non-binding: Art 37 makes them "fundamental in the governance of the country", so their real sanction is political accountability rather than judicial enforcement.*
- *The history of Part IV is the history of its reconciliation with Part III — from the subordination of Champakam to the "two wheels of a chariot" balance of Minerva Mills, harmony rather than hierarchy is the settled constitutional position.*
- *DPSP are best read as a directional compass, not a delivery guarantee: implementation is real (legal aid, panchayats, RTE, environment) but uneven, which is the honest answer to any "how far realised" question.*
- *Free legal aid shows the DPSP working at their best — a Part IV directive (Art 39A) reinforced by Art 21 and operationalised by statute (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987) into an actual entitlement.*

| Case / Amendment | Year | Exact proposition | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|

| ✅ 24th & 25th Amendments | 1971 | 24th: Parliament can amend FR; 25th inserted **Art 31C** immunising laws for Art 39(b)/(c) from Art 14/19 | — |
| ✅ *Kesavananda Bharati* | 1973 | Upheld the first part of Art 31C but struck the clause ousting judicial review (a basic feature) | — |
| ✅ 42nd Amendment | 1976 | Extended Art 31C protection to **all** DPSP | Struck down in part below |
| ✅ *Minerva Mills* | 1980 | The 42nd-Amdt extension is void; FR–DPSP **balance** is a basic feature ("two wheels of a chariot") | But Art 39(b)/(c) protection survives |
| ✅ *Unni Krishnan* | 1993 | Read the right to education into Art 21 in the light of Arts 41, 45, 46 (DPSP illuminating a FR) | Later given textual form by Art 21A (86th Amdt) |

  inconsistent ordinary law, but Parliament may amend rights to pursue DPSP without damaging basic structure;
  the surviving Art 31C specifically protects genuine Art 39(b)/(c) laws against Arts 14 and 19.

### 8.6 Constitutional mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- ✅ **Mechanism:** DPSP are non-justiciable (Art 37) but bind the State politically; courts use them to interpret ambiguous statutes and to judge whether a restriction on a FR is "reasonable" under Art 14/19.
- ⚠️ **Incentive:** because the sanction is electoral, governments implement DPSP selectively — visible welfare directives (education, legal aid, panchayats) advance faster than contested ones (UCC, prohibition).
- ⚠️ **Who gains/loses:** land reform, legal aid and reservation-linked directives redistribute toward the poor and backward classes; property and business interests bore the cost, which is why Art 31C and the Ninth Schedule were needed to protect implementing laws.
- ⚠️ **Consequence:** the DPSP turned the Indian State from a "police state" into a **welfare state** in aspiration, and gave courts a text to expand socio-economic rights through Art 21.

### 8.7 Criticism, counter-argument and implementation constraints

- ✅ **No legal force:** critics called them "pious aspirations" (Ivor Jennings) and a "cheque payable when the bank permits" (K.T. Shah); counter — Art 37 gives them governance primacy and interpretive force.
- ✅ **Illogically arranged / conservative:** N. Srinivasan (poor classification); Jennings (19th-century philosophy).
- ✅ **Constitutional conflict:** K. Santhanam — Centre–state and executive tensions.
- ⚠️ **Resource and federal constraints:** delivery depends on State finances and capacity, so the same directive yields very different outcomes across States.
- ⚠️ **Contested directives:** UCC (Art 44) collides with legal pluralism; prohibition (Art 47) and cow-slaughter (Art 48) attract political and rights-based objections.

> ⚠️ **Ownership note:** Art 44 is a Directive Principle, so this Core file owns the constitutional
> architecture of the UCC demand. The **social and community-impact** dimension is developed in
> `../../Indian-Society/` and `../../Social-Justice/`; the **religion-rights half** (Art 25–28) is in

| Step in the argument | Named anchor | Mechanism / significance | Limitation or status caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ The directive itself | **Art 44** — the State "shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India" | Placed among the **liberal-intellectual** directives; it addresses **personal law** (marriage, divorce, maintenance, succession, adoption, guardianship), not criminal or contract law, which are already uniform | ✅ Non-justiciable under **Art 37** — no court can compel Parliament to enact a UCC |
| ✅ The constitutional tension | **Art 25–26** freedom of religion vs **Art 14/15** equality | Personal laws that differentiate by sex face an equality challenge; the defence invokes religious freedom and the **Art 29–30** cultural guarantee | ⚠️ Art 25 is itself **subject to public order, morality, health and to the other provisions of Part III**, and **Art 25(2)(b)** expressly permits social-welfare and reform legislation — so the religious-freedom defence is textually bounded |
| ✅ The judicial prompts | **Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum (1985)**, **Sarla Mudgal (1995)**, **John Vallamattom (2003)** | In each the Court decided the case on the statute before it and **additionally observed** that Art 44 remained unimplemented | ⚠️ These are **observations urging the legislature**, not directions to enact a UCC — do **not** write that the Supreme Court "ordered" or "directed" a UCC |
| ✅ Reform without a UCC | **Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)** set aside instant triple talaq; the **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019** followed | Shows that **targeted statutory reform inside a personal-law system** can deliver the equality outcome a UCC is asked to deliver | ⚠️ *Shayara Bano* invalidated a **practice**; it did not rule on the constitutionality of personal-law systems as a whole |
| ✅ Codification precedent | The **Hindu Code** statutes of the mid-1950s (marriage, succession, minority and guardianship, adoption and maintenance) | Proves that codification of one community's personal law is constitutionally and politically feasible, and is the standard example of a "step towards Art 44" | ⚠️ It was **community-specific codification**, not a uniform code — do not present it as a partial UCC |
| ✅ Existing uniform option | The **Special Marriage Act, 1954** | Supplies a secular civil-marriage route to persons who satisfy the Act's statutory conditions; it is not accurately described as a route open only to, or automatically available to, “any two citizens” | ⚠️ It is **opt-in** and does not itself create a comprehensive UCC |
| ✅ Sub-national implementation | **Goa** has a common family-law regime with exceptions; **Uttarakhand's UCC Act, 2024 commenced on 27 January 2025** | Personal law lies in **Concurrent List Entry 5**, so both Parliament and State legislatures can legislate subject to constitutional/federal rules | ⚠️ Do not call Goa perfectly uniform or treat one State model as the constitutionally required national design |
| 📰 Uttarakhand's **2026 amendment — current instrument** | The Governor promulgated an Amendment Ordinance on 27 January 2026; the official UCC portal now publishes **The Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand (Amendment) Act, 2026**, GO dated **7 April 2026** | The succession from Ordinance to Act is a clean Art 213 illustration and shows post-enactment correction in a new codification | ✅ Cite the operative **Amendment Act**, not the Ordinance as the current instrument. Use the official Act/Gazette for fine detail; do not invent litigation outcomes or statistics |
| ⚠️ Law Commission position | The **21st Law Commission's consultation paper (2018)** took the view that a uniform civil code was **not necessary or desirable at that stage** and favoured reforming discriminatory practices within each personal law; the **22nd Law Commission** reopened public consultation on the subject | Gives the answer a non-partisan institutional voice on both sides | ⚠️ A Law Commission paper is **advice, not law**; do not attribute a final recommendation to the 22nd Commission unless verified |

- **Two-sided verdict scaffold (use for any "Critically examine"/"Discuss" UCC demand):** ⚠️ *Article 44 states a constitutional goal that equality under Articles 14 and 15 independently supports; but because it is non-justiciable and touches identity, the constitutionally safer route India has actually travelled is reform-within-personal-law — Shah Bano to Shayara Bano to the 2019 Act — and a uniform code will be legitimate only when it is arrived at through consultation rather than imposed as a majoritarian settlement.*
- 🔑 **Answer discipline:** separate **(i)** what Art 44 says, **(ii)** what the courts have actually held versus merely observed, **(iii)** what has already been achieved by targeted reform, and **(iv)** what remains contested. Answers that collapse these four registers lose marks even when the facts are right.

### 8.8 Verdict scaffolds

- **Justiciability demands:** ⚠️ *DPSP are non-justiciable by design, but Art 37 makes them the moral and political charter of governance; their weakness in court is the price of flexibility for a resource-scarce State.*
- **FR–DPSP conflict demands:** ⚠️ *The journey from Champakam to Minerva Mills settles the matter — FR retain primacy, yet the balance between Parts III and IV is itself a basic feature that neither can be sacrificed to.*
- **Implementation demands:** ⚠️ *The welfare-state goal is partly realised — legal aid, panchayats, RTE and environmental protection are concrete gains — but uneven delivery means the DPSP remain a compass the State has followed unevenly.*

### 8.10 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- Do **not** state DPSP are justiciable or enforceable by courts — **Art 37** makes them non-justiciable.
- Do **not** state DPSP were borrowed from the USA — they are borrowed from **Ireland** (Ireland from Spain).
- Do **not** state *Champakam* gave DPSP primacy — it gave **FR** primacy over DPSP.
- Do **not** state *Minerva Mills* struck down all of Art 31C — it struck only the **42nd-Amendment extension** to all DPSP; the **Art 39(b)/(c)** protection survives.
- Do **not** state NALSA is a constitutional body — it is **statutory** (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987).
- Do **not** state a single national income ceiling for legal-aid eligibility — the ceiling is **notified by the appropriate government** and varies by State.
- 📰 On Art 39(b): as of the *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* judgment (**5 November 2024**), not every privately owned asset is automatically a "material resource of the community"; the inquiry is contextual.
- 📰 **UCC status discipline, dated 28 August 2026 (§8.7A):** Uttarakhand's UCC commenced on
  **27 January 2025**. The 27 January 2026 Amendment Ordinance was succeeded by the officially published
  **Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand (Amendment) Act, 2026**, GO dated **7 April 2026**. Cite the Act as the
  current amending instrument, and do not invent a litigation outcome, penalty figure or implementation statistic.
- ⚠️ Do **not** write that any court has "ordered" or "directed" a Uniform Civil Code; the judicial record consists of **observations urging the legislature**, not mandamus.
- 📰 Status as of **28 August 2026**: the Part III–Part IV harmony/basic-structure formulation and the
  surviving Art 31C shield remain controlling; recheck for any later ruling on Art 31C.
