## Core idea
The **anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule, 52nd Amdt 1985)** disqualifies legislators who **switch parties** or
**defy the party whip**. The **91st Amendment (2003)** deleted the "split" defence and capped the **Council of
Ministers at 15%**. The **Speaker** decides, but courts can review (**Kihoto Hollohan**).

> 🔑 **Only MERGER (at least 2/3 of the legislature party) survives** — the split
> (1/3) exception was deleted in 2003.
> 🔑 Party member disqualified for: **voluntarily leaving** the party OR **voting against the whip** (not condoned in 15 days).
> 🔑 **Nominated** member: may join a party **within 6 months**; **Independent**: disqualified if he joins **any** party.

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ 52nd Amdt amended Arts **101, 102, 190, 191**.
- ✅ **Kihoto Hollohan:** Speaker acts as a **tribunal**; decision open to **judicial review**.
- ✅ 91st Amdt: **15% CoM cap** (states min 12); defectors barred from ministership.

## 📰 Current link
📰 **Speaker's delay** in deciding petitions (Maharashtra Shiv Sena split, 2022) is the big criticism. **Keisham
Meghachandra (2020):** SC urged an **independent tribunal** and a ~3-month decision limit. "Resign-and-recontest"
loophole used to topple governments (Karnataka/MP).

### 5.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The Tenth Schedule cured retail defection and legalised wholesale defection: by deleting the one-third split and keeping the two-thirds merger, it made the group defection the surviving and now preferred mode.*
- *The law's design flaw is the adjudicator, not the grounds: vesting a partisan Speaker with an untimed, final-on-its-face power converted disqualification into an instrument of delay.*
- *Anti-defection traded one evil for another — it bought government stability at the price of the individual legislator's freedom of conscience, which is why the whip's reach is the real reform question.*
- *Judicial review rescued the law from unaccountability but cannot fix its timing: courts can review a decision, and even a non-decision, but cannot make a reluctant Speaker decide on time — only an independent tribunal can.*
- *The "resign-and-recontest" model shows the law's outer limit: it disciplines the vote inside the House but cannot stop engineered mass resignations that topple a government from outside it.*

### 5.4 Bank A — Origin, grounds and the surviving exception

| Claim | Named provision / anchor | Mechanism / significance | Limitation / status caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| The law is a 1985 constitutional insertion, not an ordinary statute | ✅ **52nd Amendment Act, 1985** added the **Tenth Schedule** and amended **Arts 101, 102, 190, 191** | Made defection a ground of **disqualification** from the House | It is part of the **Constitution** (Tenth Schedule), not a stand-alone Act — do not call it merely a "law passed by Parliament" |
| Party members are disqualified on two grounds | ✅ **Tenth Schedule, para 2(1)** — (a) **voluntarily giving up** party membership, or (b) **voting/abstaining against the party whip** without prior permission and not condoned within **15 days** | Binds the elected member to the party on whose ticket they won | Ground (b) applies to a **whipped** vote, not to every vote; a member "belongs to" the party that set them up as a candidate |
| Independents and nominated members have distinct rules | ✅ para 2(2) — an **independent** is disqualified if he **joins any party**; ✅ para 2(3) — a **nominated** member is disqualified if he joins a party **after 6 months** | Different entry rules reflect their different mandates | A nominated member **may** join a party within 6 months without penalty; an independent **may not** join at all |
| Only the merger exception survives | ✅ **para 4** — no disqualification if the original party **merges** and **at least 2/3 of the legislature party** agree; ✅ **para 5** — a presiding officer who gives up party membership on taking the chair is protected | Protects genuine group realignment and the dignity of the chair | ✅ The **one-third "split" exception (old para 3) was DELETED by the 91st Amendment (2003)** — do not cite "split" as a live defence |

### 5.5 Bank B — Deciding authority and judicial review

- **Claim — the presiding officer, not a court or the ECI, decides.** ✅ *Provision:* **para 6** — the **Speaker/Chairman** decides disqualification, and the decision is declared **final**. *Mechanism:* keeps the question within the House. *Limitation:* finality is **not** absolute (see below).
- **Claim — the ouster of courts was struck down; the Speaker is a reviewable tribunal.** ✅ *Case:* ***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)*** (five-judge Bench) — invalidated **para 7** (which barred judicial review) for want of the **State ratification** required by Art 368(2), and held the Speaker acts as a **tribunal** whose decision is subject to **judicial review** on grounds of *mala fides, perversity, or violation of natural justice*. *Limitation:* review is generally available **after** the decision, not by a pre-emptive (*quia timet*) petition.
- **Claim — "voluntarily giving up membership" is wider than resignation.** ✅ *Case:* ***Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994)*** — the phrase is **not** synonymous with formal resignation; it can be **inferred from conduct** (e.g., publicly acting against the party). *Significance:* a member cannot dodge disqualification merely by not resigning on paper.
- **Claim — even the Speaker's *inaction* is reviewable.** ✅ *Case:* ***Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya (2007)*** — where the Speaker **fails or refuses to decide** a petition within a reasonable time, the Court can intervene; delay cannot be used to defeat the Tenth Schedule. *Significance:* pinned accountability on omission, not just decision.
- **Claim — delay is now bounded by a judicial norm.** ✅ *Case:* ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Assembly (2020)*** — petitions should ordinarily be decided within a **reasonable period of three months**, and Parliament should consider an **independent tribunal** headed by a retired judge. *Limitation:* this is a **recommendation/norm**, not an enforceable statutory deadline.
- **Claim — the whip belongs to the *political party*, not the legislature party.** ✅ *Case:* ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (Constitution Bench, 11 May 2023)** — arising from the 2022 Shiv Sena split. Its Tenth-Schedule propositions: (i) the **Speaker must recognise the Whip and the Leader appointed by the *political party***, not by a faction of the **legislature party** — the Tenth Schedule's whole premise is party discipline, so allowing a breakaway legislature-party bloc to appoint the whip would defeat it; (ii) the Speaker deciding disqualification must determine **who the real political party is** by reference to the party's own constitution and leadership structure, not merely by counting legislators; (iii) disqualification petitions must be decided within a **reasonable period**; (iv) the **Election Commission is not barred** from deciding a symbol dispute under the Symbols Order merely because disqualification petitions are pending — the two proceedings run on **different tracks**. *Significance:* the sharpest modern statement that the Tenth Schedule protects the **party**, not the **numbers**. *Limitation:* ⚠️ the Court expressly **referred to a larger (seven-judge) Bench** the *Nabam Rebia* (2016) question — whether a Speaker facing a **notice for his own removal** may adjudicate disqualification — so that point is **open and undecided**; ⚠️ do **not** state that the Court disqualified anyone or restored any government (it did neither), and do not treat the Symbols-Order holding as settling the merits of any symbol dispute.

### 5.6 Bank C — The 91st Amendment and the office bar

| Claim | Named provision / anchor | Mechanism / significance | Limitation / status caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 91st Amendment tightened the law in three ways | ✅ **91st Amendment Act, 2003** — deleted the split exception, capped the Council of Ministers, and barred defectors from office | Removed the largest loophole and cut the reward for defection | Do not confuse the 52nd (created the law) with the 91st (tightened it) |
| Ministries are size-capped | ✅ CoM (incl. PM/CM) **≤ 15%** of the House; States have a **minimum of 12** ministers | Curbs the use of ministerial berths to buy defectors | The cap is 15%, **not** 10% |
| A disqualified defector is barred from office | ✅ a member disqualified on defection **cannot be a Minister** or hold a **remunerative political post** until re-elected or the term ends | Blocks the "defect-then-reward" route | The bar runs until re-election, not merely to the next session |

- **Reform lineage (state the source and status):** ✅ the **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)**, **Law Commission 170th Report (1999)** and **NCRWC (2002)** recommended vesting the decision in the **President/Governor on the ECI's advice** and limiting the whip to **confidence and money votes**; only the **91st Amendment (2003)** was enacted, while the tribunal and whip-limiting proposals **remain proposals**, not law.

### 5.7 Mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- **Why wholesale replaced retail defection:** with the one-third split gone and the two-thirds merger retained, the incentive shifted from individual crossings to **engineering a two-thirds group** — the law rechannelled defection rather than ending it.
- **Why the Speaker's power invites delay:** the same officer belongs to a party and decides petitions that can save or sink that party's government, so **sitting on a petition** until after a floor test became a rational partisan strategy — the harm *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Keisham Meghachandra* targeted.
- **Why the whip chills dissent:** because ground (b) disqualifies a member for defying a whip, the individual legislator's independent judgement is subordinated to the party command — stabilising governments but hollowing intra-party democracy and deliberation.
- **Consequence — the "resign-and-recontest" workaround:** members **resign** their seats (rather than defect) to reduce the House total and engineer a favourable floor-test majority, then recontest — a route the Tenth Schedule does not reach (Karnataka 2019, Madhya Pradesh 2020).

### 5.8 Criticism, counter-argument, variation and implementation constraints

- **Core critique:** ⚠️ "the law banned retail defection but legalised wholesale defection" — the surviving merger exception and the resignation route let group defections and engineered collapses continue.
- **Adjudicator critique:** ⚠️ a partisan Speaker with an untimed, finality-clad power is the structural defect; *Kihoto* made the decision reviewable but could not make it timely, which *Keisham* then tried to bound.
- **Liberty counter-argument:** ⚠️ the whip's breadth harms the legislator's freedom of conscience and reduces MPs/MLAs to vote-fodder; the Goswami and Law Commission fix (whip only for confidence/money votes) is the standard answer.
- **Counter (in defence of the law):** ⚠️ it did curb the 1960s–70s "**Aaya Ram Gaya Ram**" retail defections and gave governments stability; the failure is of the exceptions and the adjudicator, not of the principle.
- **Implementation constraint:** ⚠️ the tribunal and whip-limiting reforms are **recommendations, not enacted law**; no statutory deadline binds the Speaker, so timely adjudication still depends on judicial nudging.

### 5.9 Verdict scaffolds

- **Effectiveness verdict:** "The Tenth Schedule succeeded against retail defection and failed against wholesale and engineered defection; its principle is sound, but the merger exception, the resignation route and a partisan adjudicator leave the mischief only half-cured."
- **Adjudicator verdict:** "The decisive reform is not new grounds but a new decider: shifting disqualification from a party Speaker to an independent tribunal — as *Keisham Meghachandra* urged — is the change that would make the law timely and impartial."
- **Liberty verdict:** "Anti-defection bought stability with the coin of conscience; confining the whip to confidence and money votes, as the Goswami Committee proposed, would restore intra-party democracy without reopening the door to opportunistic defection."

### 5.11 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- ✅ The **Tenth Schedule** was inserted by the **52nd Amendment (1985)** and amended **Arts 101, 102, 190, 191**; do not place it in the Ninth Schedule.
- ✅ Only the **merger (2/3)** exception survives; the **split (1/3)** exception was **deleted by the 91st Amendment (2003)**.
- ✅ Nominated member — may join a party **within 6 months** freely; **independent** — disqualified on joining **any** party.
- ✅ ***Kihoto Hollohan* (1992)** struck down para 7's court-ouster and held the Speaker a **reviewable tribunal**; ✅ ***Ravi S. Naik* (1994)** — conduct can amount to "voluntarily giving up membership"; ✅ ***Rajendra Singh Rana* (2007)** — the Speaker's **inaction** is reviewable; ✅ ***Keisham Meghachandra* (2020)** — decide within **~3 months**, independent tribunal **suggested**.
- ✅ 91st Amendment — CoM cap **15%** (States min 12) and a **ministerial-office bar** on defectors; do not state 10%.
- ⚠️ The **independent tribunal** and the **whip-limiting** proposals are **recommendations (Goswami 1990, Law Commission 170th 1999, NCRWC 2002), not enacted law** — a recommendation is not a provision.
- ⚠️/📰 The **"resign-and-recontest"** route and **Speaker-delay** controversies (e.g., Maharashtra 2022) are **political practice/litigation**, not settled constitutional holdings — present them as criticism, not as text, and verify the status of any specific pending case before dating it.
