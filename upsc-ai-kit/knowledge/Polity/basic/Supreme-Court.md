## Core idea
✅ The Supreme Court is the **guardian of the Constitution** and **guarantor of Fundamental Rights**. India
has **one integrated judiciary** enforcing both Central and state laws (unlike the US double system).

> 🔑 **Qualification:** Citizen + **5 yrs HC judge / 10 yrs HC advocate / distinguished jurist** — **no minimum age.**

## The Collegium (must remember) ⭐
✅ Judges appointed by the **President** but effectively selected through the **collegium**. For Supreme
Court appointments it is **CJI + 4 seniormost judges**, settled by the **1998 Presidential Reference
(Third Judges Case)**. The **NJAC (99th Amdt 2014)** was **struck down in 2015** (Fourth Judges Case)
→ collegium restored.

## Five jurisdictions
✅ **Original** (federal disputes, exclusive) · **Writ** (only for FRs, Art 32) · **Appellate** (incl. **SLP,
Art 136**) · **Advisory** (Art 143, *not binding*) · **Court of Record** (Art 129, contempt power).

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ **Judicial Review** — power to strike down laws violating the Constitution; part of the **basic structure**.
- ✅ HC's writ power (Art 226) is **wider** than SC's (Art 32).
- ✅ **No SC judge ever removed** (Ramaswami 1993 survived).
- ✅ **PIL** introduced early 1980s by **Justices Krishna Iyer & Bhagwati** (relaxed *locus standi*).
- ✅ SC law is **binding on all courts** (Art 141); it can review its own judgments.

### 7.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The Supreme Court is India's most powerful constitutional actor precisely because it is bounded — its authority to review even constitutional amendments (Kesavananda, 1973) is legitimate only while it respects the separation of powers it enforces.*
- *Judicial independence is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of democracy; without accountability it becomes an unreviewable power, which is why the collegium's opacity is the weakest link in an otherwise strong design.*
- *PIL democratised access to justice but concentrated power in the judiciary; its legitimacy now depends on the Court distinguishing rights-enforcement from policy-making.*
- *The collegium and the US confirmation model represent opposite trade-offs — insulation versus accountability — so the reform question is not which to copy but how to add transparency to insulation.*
- *The constitutionalisation of the environment converted Directive Principles into enforceable Article 21 guarantees, but enforcement still runs through regulators the Court cannot replace.*
- *Article 142 is the paradox of the Court's power — the instrument that lets it "do complete justice" is also the one with the fewest textual limits, so its legitimacy rests on self-restraint rather than on the words of the Constitution.*

### 7.4 Bank A — Appointment, the collegium and the NJAC

- **Claim:** the appointment power was constitutionally the executive's but is now judicially controlled. **Provision / cases:** **Art 124** (SC) and **Art 217** (HC) require "consultation"; the *First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981)* read this as **executive primacy**; the *Second Judges Case (1993)* reversed it — "consultation" means **concurrence** and created the **collegium**; the *Third Judges Case (1998)* fixed it at **CJI + 4 senior-most** SC judges. **Mechanism:** the collegium recommends and the President appoints. **Limitation:** ⚠️ the collegium is a **judicial creation, not written into the Constitution**.
- **Claim:** Parliament's attempt to reclaim a role failed. **Provision / case:** the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** and the **NJAC Act, 2014** created a six-member commission (CJI + 2 senior SC judges + Union Law Minister + 2 eminent persons). **Mechanism / why struck down:** in *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)*, by a **4:1** majority, the Court held that the Law Minister's presence and the eminent persons' veto compromised the **independence of the judiciary** — part of the **basic structure** — and struck it down, reviving the collegium. **Status caution:** 📰 the revised **Memorandum of Procedure** has remained unfinalised amid Centre–collegium disagreement (verify latest status before dating it).
- **Claim:** appointment of the CJI runs on a seniority convention, not a rule in the text. **Provision / practice:** ⚠️ the senior-most SC judge is appointed CJI by convention; it was broken by the supersessions of **1973 (A.N. Ray)** and **1977 (M.H. Beg)** and restored after the *Second Judges Case (1993)*. **Mechanism:** convention shields the office from executive favour. **Limitation:** ⚠️ a convention, not Article text — do not cite it as a constitutional mandate.

| Appointment axis | India (collegium) | USA |
|---|---|---|
| Formal appointer | President (**Art 124**) on the collegium's binding recommendation | President (**Art II §2**) |
| Decisive check | Judiciary (CJI + senior judges) | Legislature (**Senate confirmation**) |
| Tenure | Retirement at **65** (SC) | Life tenure — "during good behaviour" (**Art III**) |
| Transparency | Low (no public hearing/criteria) | High (public Senate hearings) |
| Main risk | Opacity, nepotism perception | Politicisation, partisan gridlock |

### 7.5 Bank B — Independence, removal and accountability

- **Claim:** independence is engineered by several provisions, not one. **Provisions:** security of tenure to **65** (**Art 124**), salary charged on the **Consolidated Fund** (**Art 125 / 146**, non-votable), **Art 121** (no parliamentary discussion of a judge's conduct except on a removal motion), **Art 124(7)** (no practice after retirement), **Art 50** (separation of the judiciary from the executive), contempt power (**Art 129**). **Mechanism:** each removes a lever of executive or legislative pressure. **Limitation:** ⚠️ independence can shade into non-accountability.
- **Claim:** removal is deliberately near-impossible. **Provision / procedure:** **Art 124(4)** — removal by presidential order after an address by **each House** supported by a **special majority** (a majority of the total membership plus two-thirds of members present and voting) on **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**; the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** requires a motion signed by **100 LS / 50 RS** members → a three-member inquiry committee. **Mechanism:** protects tenure. **Limitation:** ✅ **no judge has ever been removed** (*V. Ramaswami*, 1993, survived — the motion fell in the Lok Sabha).
- **Claim:** the accountability tools between doing nothing and impeachment are thin. **Instruments:** the **in-house procedure** (adopted 1999) and the **Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (1997)** are **non-statutory**; the **Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill, 2010 lapsed**. **Mechanism:** there is no graded statutory sanction short of removal. **Limitation:** ⚠️/📰 a persistent accountability gap; a Bill is never law.
- **Claim:** financial and administrative autonomy underwrite independence. **Provisions:** **Art 125** — salaries fixed by Parliament, and allowances/rights cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment (save a financial emergency); **Art 146(3)** — the Court's administrative expenses are **charged on the Consolidated Fund** (non-votable). **Mechanism:** the purse is placed beyond the government's annual leverage. **Limitation:** ⚠️ autonomy over money does not by itself resolve the appointments and accountability debates.

### 7.6 Bank C — Jurisdictions, precedent and complete justice

- **Claim:** the Court's reach is plural and defined article-by-article. **Provisions (the five jurisdictions):** **Original** (**Art 131**, federal disputes; excludes inter-State water disputes → Art 262), **Writ** (**Art 32**, only for FRs), **Appellate** (**Art 132–134, 134A**; **SLP under Art 136**), **Advisory** (**Art 143**, opinion **not binding**), **Review** (**Art 137**) — plus the judge-made **curative petition** (*Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra*, 2002). **Mechanism:** each performs a distinct function. **Limitation:** ⚠️ advisory opinions do not bind; the curative petition is an extraordinary last remedy.
- **Claim:** the Court's word is law for other courts, and it can do "complete justice". **Provisions:** **Art 141** (law declared binding on all courts, though the SC is not bound by itself); **Art 142** (any order for complete justice); **Art 144** (all civil and judicial authorities act in aid of the SC). **Mechanism:** Art 142 has resolved matters such as the Bhopal / Union Carbide settlement. **Limitation:** ⚠️ Art 142 is criticised as an unbounded discretion that can trespass on the legislative and executive domain.

**The limits of Article 142 — the anchors that turn a criticism into an argument:**

| Claim | Named case / event | Exact proposition | Use / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Art 142 **cannot override a fundamental right** | ✅ ***Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner* (1963)**, Constitution Bench | An order under Art 142 must be consistent with the **fundamental rights** guaranteed by Part III; the power cannot be exercised in contravention of them | ⚠️ The earliest and cleanest textual limit — use it first, because it shows the limit is **constitutional**, not merely prudential |
| Art 142 **cannot supplant substantive law** | ✅ ***Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India* (1998)**, Constitution Bench | Art 142 is a **residuary power supplementary** to existing law; it cannot be used to **supplant substantive law applicable to a case**, to ignore statutory provisions dealing with the subject, or to build a new edifice where none exists — there the Court declined to use Art 142 to debar an advocate from practice as a punishment for contempt, that being the statutory domain of the Bar Council | ⚠️ **This is the single most quotable limit.** Its formula — *supplement, not supplant* — is the phrase to deploy in any "judicial overreach" answer |
| The limit is **live, not historical** | 📰 ✅ **Presidential Reference under Art 143 on assent to Bills — opinion of 20 November 2025 (2025 INSC 1333)** | The Court held that it **cannot fix rigid timelines** for the Governor or the President to act on Bills and **cannot grant "deemed assent" under Art 142**, since doing so would substitute the Court's will for that of a constitutional authority and disturb the separation of powers; prolonged **unexplained inaction** nonetheless remains reviewable | ⚠️ It is an **advisory opinion under Art 143** and is therefore **not binding as a judgment**, though it is authoritative and is treated as controlling in this folder; it also effectively **limits the deemed-assent route** taken in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor* (**8 April 2025**). Cross-file: `Governor-and-CM.md` §6.12. **Prohibited:** stating that the earlier judgment was "overruled" (an advisory opinion does not overrule), or that Art 142 has been curtailed generally |
| The honest verdict on Art 142 | ⚠️ Analytical | Art 142's limits are **judicially self-imposed and inconsistently applied**: the Court has both refused to use it against statutory schemes (*SCBA*) and used it expansively (the Union Carbide settlement; dissolution-of-marriage-on-irretrievable-breakdown orders). Its discipline therefore rests on **self-restraint plus the two hard limits above** | ⚠️ This is the balanced position an examiner rewards — neither "Art 142 is unlimited" nor "Art 142 is well-bounded". Do **not** assert that any specific Art 142 order was "unconstitutional" |
- **Claim:** the Court is a court of record with contempt power. **Provision:** **Art 129** plus the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (civil vs criminal contempt; truth a defence since the 2006 amendment if bona fide and in the public interest; punishment up to **6 months** and/or a **₹2,000** fine). **Mechanism:** protects the administration of justice. **Limitation:** ⚠️ can chill legitimate criticism.
- **Claim:** the appellate and advisory doors have defined scope. **Provisions:** **Art 132–134** cover constitutional, civil and criminal appeals; **Art 136 (SLP)** is a **discretionary, plenary** power to hear an appeal from **any court or tribunal except a military tribunal**; **Art 143** lets the President seek an opinion, which the Court may decline (except on a pre-Constitution treaty dispute) and which does **not bind**. **Mechanism:** SLP is the Court's residual gateway to correct injustice. **Limitation:** ⚠️ SLP is not a regular right of appeal; it is exercised sparingly.

- **Claim:** a court of record can also refer questions to a larger Bench. **Provision:** **Art 145** empowers the SC to make **rules of procedure** (with presidential approval); **Art 145(3)** requires a **minimum of five judges** (a **Constitution Bench**) to decide a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution** or an **Art 143** reference. **Mechanism:** major constitutional questions are decided by a larger, more authoritative bench. **Limitation:** ⚠️ most matters are heard by 2–3 judge benches; the five-judge floor applies only to substantial constitutional questions.

### 7.7 Bank D — Judicial review, PIL and judicial legislation

- **Claim:** judicial review is not a single article but a basic feature. **Provisions / case:** it flows from **Art 13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 246, 251, 372**; it is a **basic-structure** element (*Kesavananda*, 1973; reaffirmed in *L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997). **Mechanism:** it strikes down ultra vires laws and executive action; even Ninth Schedule laws added after **24 April 1973** are reviewable (*I.R. Coelho*, 2007). **Limitation:** ⚠️ it tests legality, not policy wisdom.
- **Claim:** PIL relaxed standing to open the Court to the poor. **Case / mechanism:** *S.P. Gupta (1981)* relaxed *locus standi*; epistolary jurisdiction treats letters as petitions; continuing mandamus keeps a case alive to monitor compliance (*Vineet Narain*, 1998). **Limitation:** ⚠️ the risk of frivolous or "publicity-interest" litigation and docket overload.
- **Claim:** judicial law-making is legitimate only as gap-filling. **Case:** *Vishaka (1997)* laid down binding guidelines "until Parliament legislates", later superseded by the **2013 Act**. **Mechanism:** Art 141 gave the guidelines the force of law. **Limitation:** ⚠️ court norms must yield to statute; permanent law-making violates the separation of powers.
- **Claim:** activism is legitimate up to the point it becomes governance. **Mechanism:** post-Emergency judges used PIL and Art 32/226 to fill executive/legislative failure; ⚠️ critics label the anthem-in-cinemas order (2016) and the highway-liquor ban (2016) as overreach. **Limitation:** the reply is that restraint (*deference to elected branches*) and activism are two poles of one duty; the line is crossed when the Court administers rather than adjudicates.
- **Claim:** even constitutional amendments are reviewable — the deepest source of the Court's power. **Cases (exact propositions):** *Kesavananda Bharati (1973)* — Parliament may amend under Art 368 but **cannot alter the basic structure**; *Minerva Mills (1980)* — judicial review and the balance between FRs and DPSPs are themselves basic features; *I.R. Coelho (2007)* — Ninth Schedule laws added after **24 April 1973** are testable against the basic structure. **Limitation:** ⚠️ "basic structure" is judge-identified and not an exhaustive list.

### 7.9 Mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- **Why the collegium behaves as it does:** having won primacy through the Judges cases, the judiciary's incentive is to guard insulation; the cost is that opacity and the absence of a secretariat slow appointments and invite the nepotism charge — which in turn fuels periodic executive attempts (NJAC) to reclaim a role.
- **Why PIL expanded power:** relaxed standing plus Art 32/226 access plus Art 141/142 remedial reach gave the Court both the door (who can sue) and the tools (what it can order); continuing mandamus converts a one-off ruling into ongoing administration.
- **Consequence:** India's Court can strike down constitutional amendments (basic structure) — rare among democracies — so its power is structural, not merely rhetorical; but it has "neither purse nor sword" and depends on executive compliance, which is the real limit.
- **Why removal never succeeds:** Art 124(4) sets a political threshold (a special majority in both Houses) that a governing party rarely reaches alone and an opposition rarely carries against government resistance; the mechanism protects tenure but leaves misconduct short of removal effectively unsanctioned.
- **Court–Parliament dialogue:** ⚠️ major judgments have triggered constitutional responses — the *Golak Nath*/*Kesavananda* line prompted the 24th and 42nd Amendments, and the collegium cases prompted the 99th Amendment — so judicial power operates within an ongoing institutional dialogue, not a one-way command.

### 7.10 Criticism, counter-argument, variation and implementation constraints

- **Overreach critique:** ⚠️ critics cite the order making the national anthem mandatory in cinemas (2016, later made non-mandatory) and the ban on liquor sales within 500 m of highways (2016, later relaxed) as instances where the Court entered policy; the reply is that these were rights-linked and course-corrected.
- **Collegium critique vs reply:** ⚠️ opacity, delay and nepotism (critique) vs insulation from executive capture and basic-structure protection (reply) — the reform consensus is transparency, not a return to executive primacy.
- **Accountability constraint:** ⚠️/📰 the only hard tool is Art 124(4); the in-house procedure is non-statutory and the 2010 Bill lapsed, so a graded mechanism is still missing.
- **Capacity constraint:** 📰 large pendency and judicial vacancies weaken even a powerful Court's delivery (verify current figures before citing).
- **Variation:** ⚠️ the activism/restraint balance shifts with the era and the bench — assertive in the post-Emergency PIL decades, more restrained on economic-policy questions — so "the judiciary" is not a single, constant actor.
- **Implementation constraint on PIL:** ⚠️ continuing-mandamus cases (forests, pollution, prison reform) can outrun the Court's capacity to monitor compliance, converting a remedy into open-ended administration that depends on executive follow-through the Court cannot compel.

### 7.11 Verdict scaffolds

- **Independence verdict:** "Judicial independence is the precondition of every other constitutional guarantee, but it earns legitimacy only when matched by transparent appointments and a credible accountability mechanism — the one reform the current design still lacks."
- **Collegium/comparison verdict:** "The collegium protects independence and the US model protects accountability; India's task is not to import Senate confirmation but to give the collegium the transparency and secretariat that would make its insulation trustworthy."
- **PIL/power verdict:** "PIL made the Supreme Court one of the world's most powerful courts, but its authority is constitutional, not sovereign — it endures only while the Court keeps rights-enforcement distinct from governance."
- **Environmental verdict:** "By reading Articles 48A and 51A(g) into Article 21, the Court constitutionalised the environment; the achievement is real, its limit is that enforcement still runs through regulators the Court can supervise but not replace."
- **Judicial-legislation verdict:** "Vishaka shows judicial legislation at its most defensible — an interim, rights-protecting norm under Article 141 that stepped aside the moment Parliament acted in 2013; it is a bridge over a legislative gap, not a permanent seat in the legislature."
- **Constitutional-morality verdict:** "Constitutional morality asks the judiciary to hold others to constitutional values while accepting the same discipline itself — independence in deciding, accountability in how it decides — which is why the missing graded accountability mechanism, not the collegium alone, is the reform that matters."

- **Representation verdict:** "Greater representation of women in the higher judiciary is desirable not as tokenism but because a bench that mirrors the society it judges strengthens both the legitimacy and the perspective of constitutional adjudication; the constraint lies in the collegium's opaque, seniority-bound talent pool, so diversity and appointment reform are the same project."

### 7.12 Prelims close-option distinctions (answering the routed objective demands)

| Confusion / routed demand | Correct discrimination |
|---|---|
| **Writ of Prohibition** (2024 Q83) | Issued by SC/HC to a **lower court or tribunal** to stop it exceeding or acting without jurisdiction; **preventive** (before a decision) — unlike **certiorari**, which **quashes** a decision already made; lies only against judicial/quasi-judicial bodies, not against a legislature or a purely administrative act |
| **Impeachment / removal of SC judges** (2019 Q46) | **Art 124(4)** + **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**; **special majority** in each House on **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**; motion by **100 LS / 50 RS** members; ✅ never successful |
| **Original jurisdiction scope** (support for 2019 Q52/Q81) | **Art 131** covers only **federal disputes** (Centre–State, State–State); it **excludes** inter-State water disputes (Art 262 → tribunals), Finance Commission matters and purely political questions |
| **Article 142** (2019 Q52) | Power to pass any order for **complete justice**; it is **supplementary, not supplanting** — it cannot override a fundamental right (*Prem Chand Garg*, 1963) or displace the substantive statutory law governing a case (*Supreme Court Bar Association*, 1998), and it cannot be used to grant "deemed assent" to Bills (Art 143 opinion, **20 Nov 2025**). It is **not** a source of unlimited legislative power |
| **HC jurisdiction vs SC review** (2019 Q81) | HC **Art 226** covers FRs **and** other legal rights (**wider in purpose**); SC **Art 32** is only for FRs but **all-India in reach**; SC hears appeals/SLP (Art 132–136) from HCs |
| **Retired SC judges and court powers** (2021 Q88) | **Art 128** — the CJI may (with consent and the President's approval) request a **retired SC judge** (or a retired HC judge qualified for the SC) to **sit and act** in the SC; **Art 224A** — a retired HC judge may sit in a HC; **Art 124(7)** — a retired SC judge **cannot plead or act** before any court/authority in India |
| **Contempt of Courts** (2022 Q11) | **Art 129/215** make the SC/HC courts of record with **inherent** contempt power; the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** defines civil vs criminal contempt but does **not** curtail the constitutional power |
| **Advocates / Bar Council** (2022 Q12) | The **Advocates Act, 1961** makes the **Bar Council of India** a **statutory** body regulating legal education and the profession; State Bar Councils enrol advocates — this is a statutory, not a constitutional, scheme |
| **The five writs contrasted** (support for 2024 Q83) | **Habeas corpus** (produce the detained person), **mandamus** (command a public duty — not against a private individual or the President/Governor personally), **prohibition** (stop a lower court/tribunal before decision), **certiorari** (quash a decision already made), **quo warranto** (challenge a usurped public office); both SC (Art 32) and HC (Art 226) issue all five |

### 7.13 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- ✅ Strength is **34** (1 CJI + 33), raised from 31 in **2019**; SC judges retire at **65**, HC judges at **62**.
- ✅ A **Constitution Bench (Art 145(3)) is a minimum of five judges** for a substantial constitutional question or an Art 143 reference — not the whole Court, and not merely a "larger bench."
- ✅ The **collegium is not in the Constitution** — it is the product of the Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges cases; the **NJAC / 99th Amendment was struck down in 2015**.
- ⚠️/📰 Do **not** state live vacancy counts, pendency totals or the current number/percentage of women judges as settled facts — mark them 📰 and re-verify.
- ✅ The advisory opinion under **Art 143 is not binding**; **inter-State water disputes are excluded** from original jurisdiction (Art 262).
- ⚠️ Case law must be an exact proposition: *M.C. Mehta (1987)* = **absolute** (not strict) liability; *Vishaka* guidelines were **interim** and are now **superseded by the 2013 Act**.
- ✅ The **Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill, 2010 lapsed** — never cite it as law; the in-house procedure and the Restatement of Values are **non-statutory**.
- ⚠️ Distinguish Constitution text (Art 124–147) from judicial interpretation (collegium, basic structure), statute (Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968, Contempt of Courts Act 1971) and administrative practice (Memorandum of Procedure).
- ✅ The CJI-by-seniority rule is a **convention**, not Article text (broken 1973/1977, restored after 1993) — do not present it as a constitutional command.
- ⚠️ *Kesavananda* (1973) **did not** bar amendment of Fundamental Rights; it barred alteration of the **basic structure** — a distinction Prelims and Mains both test.
- ✅ **Article 142 limits (§7.6):** the correct formula is **"supplementary, not supplanting"** (*Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India*, **1998**); an Art 142 order must also be consistent with **fundamental rights** (*Prem Chand Garg*, **1963**); and the **Art 143 opinion of 20 November 2025** held the Court **cannot grant "deemed assent"** to Bills or fix rigid timelines for the Governor/President, though unexplained inaction remains reviewable. ⚠️ An **advisory opinion does not "overrule"** a judgment — describe the 20 Nov 2025 position as **controlling and later**, not as an overruling of *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor* (8 Apr 2025). ⚠️ Do **not** assert that Art 142 has been curtailed generally, and do not label any individual Art 142 order "unconstitutional".
