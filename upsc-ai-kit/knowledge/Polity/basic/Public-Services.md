## Core idea
India has **three tiers** of services — **All-India (IAS/IPS/IFoS), Central, and State**. AIS officers serve **both**
Centre and states but are **recruited by the Centre**. Civil servants hold office at the **pleasure** of the
President/Governor (**Art 310**), but **Art 311** protects them from arbitrary dismissal.

> 🔑 **Art 312 = RAJYA SABHA** resolution (2/3 present & voting) to create a new AIS — protects **states**.
> 🔑 **Art 311's two shields:** (1) no dismissal by a **subordinate** authority; (2) **inquiry + hearing** required.
> 🔑 3 exceptions to the inquiry: **criminal conviction · not practicable · security of State**.

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ **ICS→IAS, IP→IPS** in 1947; **IFoS** = third AIS (1966).
- ✅ **All-India Services Act, 1951** governs the AIS; up to **33⅓%** senior posts by state-service promotion.
- ✅ **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** — enabled by 42nd Amdt but **never created**; post ≥ District Judge.
- ✅ **42nd Amdt (1976)** removed the second-stage (punishment) hearing under Art 311.

### 5.2 Qualified thesis options

- *The Indian civil service holds office at the pleasure of the executive (Art 310) but not at its mercy: Article 311, the express constitutional exceptions and judicial review convert an inherited royal prerogative into a rule-bound, reviewable power.*
- *Article 311 is a genuine safeguard against arbitrary dismissal, but its three exceptions and the 42nd Amendment's removal of the second hearing show a deliberate balance — security of tenure that yields to conviction, impracticability and the security of the State.*
- *The All-India Services are the constitutional keystone of Indian unity: recruited by the Centre yet serving the States, they can be created only through a Rajya Sabha resolution, making them a federal bargain rather than a central imposition.*
- *The real threat to the services is not dismissal but the transfer: without the assured tenure and Civil Services Boards that* T.S.R. Subramanian *sought, security of tenure on paper coexists with politicised postings in practice.*
- *Civil-service independence and democratic accountability are not opposites to be traded off but conditions to be reconciled — tenure secures integrity, while political direction and judicial review secure answerability.*

### 5.4 Bank A — Part XIV architecture and recruitment (Claim → provision → mechanism → limitation)

- ✅ **Claim:** the services have a dedicated constitutional Part. **Provision:** **Part XIV, Articles 308–323** — "Services under the Union and the States": Arts **308–314** cover the services proper; **Arts 315–323** cover the **Public Service Commissions** (owned by `basic/UPSC-and-SPSC.md`). **Mechanism:** it constitutionalises the permanent executive. **Limitation:** cross-link the PSC half; do not re-derive it here.
- ✅ **Claim:** recruitment and service conditions are set by law, with a rule-making fallback. **Provision:** **Art 309** — the appropriate **legislature** may regulate **recruitment and conditions of service**; **until it does, the President or the Governor may make rules**. **Mechanism:** it lets administration run through executive rules pending legislation. **Limitation:** ✅ such rules are **subordinate** to any Act the legislature later passes and to the other Articles of Part XIV.
- ✅ **Claim:** Part XIV's remaining service Articles complete the scheme. **Provision:** **Art 312A** (inserted by the **28th Amendment, 1972**) lets **Parliament vary or revoke** the service conditions of the former **Secretary of State (ex-ICS) officers**; **Art 313** is a **transitional** provision continuing existing service laws until superseded; **Art 314** (the original protection for ex-Crown-service officers) was **repealed by the 28th Amendment, 1972**. **Mechanism:** they wound down colonial-era service guarantees. **Limitation:** ✅ mainly of historical-completeness value — cite for a "Part XIV scheme" question, not as live everyday law.
- ✅/⚠️ **Claim:** service disputes have a dedicated constitutional adjudication track. **Provision:** **Art 323A** (inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**) empowers Parliament to set up **Administrative Tribunals** for recruitment and service matters, enacted as the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** (the **Central Administrative Tribunal**). **Case (exact proposition):** *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (**1997**) held that tribunal decisions remain subject to **judicial review by a Division Bench of the High Court** under Art 226/227 — tribunals **supplement**, not supplant, the constitutional courts. **Limitation:** ⚠️ adjudicatory depth is shared with the Tribunals/Judiciary files — cite the head here, cross-link the detail.

### 5.5 Bank B — The doctrine of pleasure and its Indian limits (Art 310)

- ✅ **Claim:** tenure is at pleasure, inherited from English law. **Provision:** **Art 310** — except as expressly provided, a civil servant holds office **during the pleasure** of the **President** (Union) or **Governor** (State). **Mechanism:** it enables removal without a contractual cause of action. **Limitation — the Indian restrictions:** ⚠️ the pleasure is **not absolute** — it is subject to (a) the **Art 311** procedural safeguards; (b) **express constitutional exceptions** where tenure is *not* at pleasure (e.g., **Supreme Court and High Court judges, the CAG, the Chief Election Commissioner, the Chairman/members of the UPSC** — removable only by their special constitutional processes); and (c) **judicial review** for mala fides and violation of Part III/311. So the English "unfettered pleasure" is **constitutionally fettered** in India.

### 5.6 Bank C — Article 311 safeguards, exceptions, the 42nd Amendment and case law

- ✅ **Claim:** Art 311 gives two procedural safeguards, to civilians only. **Provision:** **Art 311** — (1) no dismissal or removal by an authority **subordinate** to the appointing authority; (2) no **dismissal, removal or reduction in rank except after an inquiry** in which the person has a **reasonable opportunity of being heard**. **Mechanism:** it imports natural justice into disciplinary action. **Limitation:** ✅ it protects members of **civil services / civil posts only** — **defence personnel are excluded**.
- ✅ **Claim:** the inquiry can be dispensed with in exactly three situations. **Provision:** the **second proviso to Art 311(2)** — the inquiry is excluded where (i) the person is **dismissed on conviction on a criminal charge**; (ii) the authority is satisfied, **for reasons recorded in writing**, that holding an inquiry is **not reasonably practicable**; or (iii) the **President/Governor** is satisfied that in the **interest of the security of the State** an inquiry is not expedient. **Mechanism:** it balances fairness against conviction, disorder and security. **Limitation:** ⚠️ the "not practicable" ground is subject to **limited judicial review**; the "security of State" satisfaction is largely subjective.
- ✅ **Claim:** the 42nd Amendment narrowed the safeguard. **Provision:** the **42nd Amendment (1976)** removed the **second-stage opportunity** (the earlier right to make a representation against the **proposed penalty**); now the single reasonable opportunity is at the **inquiry** stage. **Mechanism:** it streamlined disciplinary action. **Limitation:** ⚠️ critics see it as a dilution of fair-hearing rights.
- ✅/⚠️ **Leading propositions (case law, exact):** *Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India* (**1958**) laid down the **"motive vs foundation"** test — a termination is "punishment" attracting Art 311 only when the **misconduct is the foundation** of the order, not merely its motive (so bona fide termination of a probationer/temporary servant is outside Art 311). *Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel* (**1985**, Constitution Bench) **upheld the three exceptions** to the inquiry and held that where an exception applies, natural justice is **excluded to that extent**, subject to limited review. **Use/caution:** cite these as **exact propositions**, not name-drops.
- ✅/⚠️ **Claim:** a fair inquiry includes seeing the report against you. **Case (exact proposition):** *Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar* (**1993**, Constitution Bench) held that where the inquiry officer's report is **against** the delinquent, a **copy must be furnished** before the disciplinary authority imposes a penalty, as part of the **reasonable opportunity** under Art 311(2); non-supply vitiates the order only where it causes **prejudice**. **Use:** cite for the *content* of natural justice in disciplinary law.
- ✅ **Claim:** the safeguard's scope is civil service *or* civil post. **Provision:** Art 311 protects a person **holding a civil post** under the Union/State even if not a member of an organised service; **defence personnel, workmen and purely contractual engagements** fall outside — pin the scope before applying the safeguard.

### 5.7 Bank D — All-India Services and the federal safeguard (Art 312)

- ✅ **Claim:** creating a new AIS needs the States' House to consent. **Provision:** **Art 312** — Parliament may create a **new All-India Service** (including an **All-India Judicial Service**) only if the **Rajya Sabha** first passes a resolution, supported by **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**, that it is necessary in the **national interest**. **Mechanism:** it makes the AIS a **federal bargain** — the States' House guards State interests. **Limitation:** ✅ existing AIS (IAS, IPS, IFoS) predate this and rest on the **All-India Services Act, 1951**.
- ✅ **Claim:** AIS are jointly controlled — the "unity" instrument. **Mechanism:** officers are **recruited and trained by the Centre** but serve the **States**; **ultimate control lies with the Centre**, immediate control with the States (cross-link `basic/Centre-State-Relations.md`). **Significance:** they knit the Union and States administratively. **Limitation:** ⚠️ this dual control is itself a source of Centre–State friction.
- ✅/⚠️ **Claim:** the AIJS is enabled but not created. **Provision:** **Art 312** expressly allows an **All-India Judicial Service** (post **not below District Judge**) by **ordinary law** — **no Art 368 amendment needed** — yet **no such law has been made**. **Debate:** the Centre favours it to fill vacancies and standardise; many **States and High Courts resist** on federalism, language and control grounds. **Status:** ⚠️ a **live proposal**, not law.
- ✅ **Claim:** the existing AIS run on a 1951 statute and a joint-cadre system. **Provision:** the **All-India Services Act, 1951** and the service rules govern the **IAS, IPS and IFoS**; up to **33⅓%** of senior posts are filled by **promotion from State services** through a UPSC-chaired committee, and **cadre allocation, deputation and empanelment** are managed by the **cadre-controlling ministry** (e.g., DoPT for the IAS). **Mechanism:** it operationalises the "recruited by the Centre, serving the States" design. **Limitation:** ⚠️ deputation and empanelment are recurring **Centre–State flashpoints** over control of officers (cross-link `basic/Centre-State-Relations.md`).
- ✅/📰 **Claim:** the deputation lever is rule-governed and contested. **Provision:** the **IAS (Cadre) Rules, 1954** govern cadre allocation and **Centre–State deputation**; 📰 the Union's **2021 proposal** to amend these rules — to enable central deputation of officers even without State concurrence in specified situations — drew formal objections from several States as a threat to cadre control. **Use:** cite as a **live federal flashpoint / proposal**, and verify its current status before asserting it is in force.

### 5.8 Bank E — Security of tenure, transfers and cadre control

- ✅/⚠️ **Claim:** the operative threat to neutrality is the transfer, and the Court has tried to address it. **Case (exact proposition):** *T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India* (**2013**) directed that (i) **Civil Services Boards** be constituted at the Union and State levels to advise on **postings and transfers**; (ii) civil servants be given a **minimum assured tenure**; and (iii) officers should **not act on oral instructions** — directions should be recorded in writing. **Mechanism:** it aimed to insulate postings from arbitrary political interference. **Status caution:** 📰 implementation has been **partial and uneven** — several States have not fully constituted CSBs or guaranteed fixed tenure; treat these as **judicial directions imperfectly implemented**, not settled practice.
- ⚠️ **Cadre-control frame:** the Centre (through the cadre-controlling ministry, e.g. DoPT for the IAS) manages cadre allocation, deputation and empanelment; this is the lever behind Centre–State tussles over officers.
- ⚠️/✅ **Reform pedigree:** the **Hota Committee (2004)** and the **Second ARC (10th Report, *Refurbishing of Personnel Administration*, 2008)** recommended **fixed minimum tenure** and insulation of postings — the same fixes *T.S.R. Subramanian* (2013) later judicialised; cite them as the **policy lineage** behind the tenure demand, keeping deeper reform detail in the Governance folder.

### 5.9 Mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- ⚠️ **Why the pleasure doctrine survives:** the executive needs to remove the manifestly unfit and disloyal; the Constitution keeps the *power* but fetters its *exercise*.
- ⚠️ **Why security of tenure matters:** a servant who can be dismissed or transferred at will has an incentive to please the political master, not the law — hence Art 311 and the demand for fixed tenure.
- ⚠️ **Consequences of weak transfer-protection:** frequent, politicised transfers undermine institutional memory and honest administration even where formal dismissal protection is strong — the tenure problem, not the dismissal problem, is the live one.
- ⚠️ **Perverse incentive of empanelment/deputation:** because the Centre controls empanelment and choice postings, ambitious officers face an incentive to align with the political executive of the day — the reason reformers press for **rule-bound, board-vetted** postings rather than discretionary ones.

### 5.10 Criticism, counter-argument and the accountability–independence tension

- **Independence case:** ⚠️ Art 311 and assured tenure protect **integrity and impartiality**, letting officers resist illegal orders.
- **Accountability counter:** ⚠️ over-strong protection can **shield inefficiency and misconduct**; disciplinary action becomes slow and litigation-prone — hence the 42nd Amendment's streamlining and reform demands.
- **The calibrated answer:** ⚠️ the goal is **neutral competence** — insulated enough to be honest, answerable enough to be responsive; the instruments are Art 311 (against arbitrariness), CSBs and fixed tenure (against politicised transfers), and judicial review (against mala fides).
- **Variation:** ⚠️ the AIJS and lateral-entry debates show that reform is contested along **federal** and **reservation** lines, not merely on efficiency.
- **Mis-targeted-protection critique:** ⚠️ disciplinary proceedings are slow and heavily litigated, so misconduct is under-punished while honest officers stay under-protected against politicised transfers — the safeguard is **strong on dismissal, weak on tenure**, i.e. mis-aimed.

### 5.11 Verdict scaffolds

- **Pleasure verdict:** "India retained the pleasure doctrine but disarmed its arbitrariness — Art 311, the constitutional exceptions and judicial review turn a royal prerogative into a rule-of-law power."
- **AIS verdict:** "The All-India Services are a federal instrument of unity created only with the States' consent through the Rajya Sabha — administrative integration by bargain, not by fiat."
- **Reform verdict:** "The security the services need is less against dismissal than against the transfer; the unfinished task is to give effect to assured tenure and Civil Services Boards, not to weaken Article 311."
- **Federal-personnel verdict:** "The All-India Services are shared property; when the Centre reaches for the deputation lever without the States' consent it strains the very bargain — Art 312 and the 1951 Act — that makes those services an instrument of union rather than of central command."

### 5.13 Factual-risk and current-status controls

- ✅ The **pleasure doctrine (Art 310) is limited** by Art 311, constitutional exceptions and judicial review — never state it as absolute.
- ✅ **Art 311's three exceptions** are exact (conviction / not practicable-with-reasons / security of State); the **42nd Amendment** removed only the **second** opportunity.
- ✅ **Art 312 = Rajya Sabha** 2/3 present-and-voting; the **AIJS** is **enabled but not created** and needs only **ordinary law**.
- ✅/📰 *T.S.R. Subramanian* (2013) directions (CSB, fixed tenure, written instructions) are **judicial directions, partially implemented** — state the status, do not claim they are universally in force.
- ⚠️ Keep **civil-service reform** (lateral entry, Mission Karmayogi, Citizens' Charter, e-governance) in the **Governance** folder; cite as cross-links, do not develop as constitutional law.
- ⚠️ Cite *Dhingra* (1958) and *Tulsiram Patel* (1985) as **exact propositions**, not as decorative names.
- ✅ **Defence personnel** are outside Art 311; do not extend the safeguard to them.
- ✅ **Art 323A Administrative Tribunals** were enabled by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**; *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) preserved **High Court judicial review** — never state that tribunals oust the constitutional courts.
- 📰 The **IAS (Cadre) Rules** deputation-amendment (2021) is a **proposal**, not settled law — verify its status before asserting it is in force.
- ⚠️ Cite the **Hota Committee (2004)** and **Second ARC (2008)** as **recommendations**; fixed tenure and CSBs are **judicially directed and partly adopted**, not universally enacted.
- ✅ Distinguish a **civil post** (protected by Art 311) from **defence or purely contractual** engagements (outside it); scope errors are a common trap.
