| ✅ Articles | **5–11, Part II** |
| ✅ Constitution covers | only **who were citizens on 26 Jan 1950** |

| ✅ Type | **Single citizenship** (like Canada) — no state citizenship |
| ✅ Dual citizenship | India does not recognise it under the Citizenship Act; **Art 9** addresses voluntary foreign citizenship at commencement. OCI is NOT dual citizenship |

## Core idea
✅ Constitution gives **no permanent/elaborate** rules — it just names the four categories of people who
were citizens at commencement and hands the rest to **Parliament (Art 11)**, which passed the
**Citizenship Act 1955**.

## Acquisition — 5 ways
> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **"BeD RaIN"** = **B**irth, **D**escent, **R**egistration, **N**aturalisation,
> **I**ncorporation of territory.

✅ **Birth** rule tightened over time: 1950-1987 (any parent's nationality OK) → 1987 (either parent a
citizen) → **2004 (both parents, or one citizen + other not an illegal migrant)**.

## Loss — 3 ways
> 🔑 **Mnemonic:** **"R-T-D"** = **R**enunciation (voluntary), **T**ermination (took foreign
> citizenship), **D**eprivation (Centre cancels — fraud/disloyalty).

## Single citizenship — exceptions
✅ Uniform rights everywhere, **except**: (1) Art **16(3)** permits Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for specified public employment; (2) state concessions to residents (fees); (3) Art 19 curbs in **tribal areas**; (4) *till
2019* J&K permanent residents under **Art 35A** (abolished 2019).

## Must-Know Facts
- ✅ Articles **15, 16, 19 and 29** are textually citizen-specific; Article **30** speaks of minorities rather than citizens. Voting and specified offices additionally require citizenship under their own provisions.
- ✅ Article **22(3)(a)** excludes enemy aliens from clauses **22(1) and 22(2)**; avoid saying every protection in Article 22 is unavailable.
- ✅ **OCI** cardholders **cannot** vote, be MP/MLA, President/VP, or SC/HC judge.
- ✅ **Assam Accord / NRC cut-off = 24 March 1971** (Citizenship Amdt Act 1985).
- ✅ **L.M. Singhvi Committee** → led to OCI (2003); PIO+OCI merged in **2015**.

## 📰 Current link — CAA 2019 ⭐
📰 ✅ **Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019:** creates a facilitated application route for **6 communities (Hindu, Sikh,
Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, Christian)** from **Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan** who entered India **by
31 Dec 2014**. 📰 **Rules notified March 2024; first certificates issued May 2024.** ⚠️ Challenged in
SC on Art 14 grounds. It does **not automatically confer citizenship**; statutory
eligibility, application and scrutiny remain necessary.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- *India chose single citizenship to build fraternity in a plural federation, yet it tolerates residence- and region-based distinctions — through Articles 16(3), 19(5) and 371 — so that national unity does not erase local protection.*
- *The CAA 2019 debate turns on a single question: whether fast-tracking six named communities from three neighbours is a reasonable classification of persecuted minorities or an impermissible religious test under Article 14 — a question still before the Supreme Court.*
- *Citizenship, domicile and residence are three different statuses: only citizenship confers political rights, and conflating it with residence is the commonest error in both Prelims options and public debate.*
- *OCI is diaspora engagement without enfranchisement: a statutory card, not a second citizenship, while Article 9 addresses the commencement routes and Section 9 of the Citizenship Act controls later voluntary foreign citizenship.*

### 8.4 Bank A — Constitutional scheme (Arts 5–11) and statutory modes

- **Claim:** the Constitution fixes only who were citizens in 1950 and delegates the rest. **Evidence:** ✅ **Arts 5–11 (Part II)** identify the four classes who became citizens at **commencement (26 Jan 1950)**; ✅ **Art 9** bars **dual citizenship** (voluntary foreign citizenship = not an Indian citizen); ✅ **Art 11** empowers **Parliament** over all citizenship matters → the **Citizenship Act 1955**. **Significance:** ⚠️ citizenship is therefore mainly **statutory, not a Fundamental Right**. **Caution:** ✅ do not call citizenship a Fundamental Right.
- **Claim:** acquisition and loss are statutory and time-conditioned. **Evidence:** ✅ acquisition by **Birth, Descent, Registration, Naturalisation, Incorporation of territory** (🔑 "BeD RaIN"); ✅ birth rule tightened — 1950–1987 any parent; from **1987** either parent a citizen; from **2004** both parents (or one citizen + other not an illegal migrant); ✅ naturalisation needs **12 months' continuous residence + 11 of the preceding 14 years**. **Significance:** ⚠️ "born in India = citizen" is true only for **1950–1987**. **Caution:** ✅ loss is by **Renunciation** (voluntary), **Termination** (automatic on acquiring foreign citizenship) and **Deprivation** (compulsory, by the Centre).

### 8.5 Bank B — Single citizenship and its exceptions (routed anchors)

- **Claim:** single citizenship gives uniform rights, but residence-based distinctions survive. **Evidence:** ✅ India (like Canada) has **single citizenship** — no separate state citizenship. Exceptions: ✅ **Art 16(3)** lets Parliament prescribe residence requirements for specified classes of public employment; ✅ **Art 19(5)** permits restrictions on movement/residence to protect **Scheduled Tribes**; ✅ **Art 371/371-A to 371-J** give region-specific safeguards (e.g., **371-A Nagaland**, **371-G Mizoram** protect customary law/land; **371-D** local-cadre posts in AP/Telangana). **Significance:** ⚠️ uniformity of citizenship coexists with regional protection. **Caution:** ✅ these are constitutional special provisions, **not** separate citizenships.
- **Claim:** the reason residence benefits are lawful is a precise Art 15 point. **Evidence:** ✅ **Art 15(1)** bars State discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex and **place of birth** — but **not residence**. **Significance:** ⚠️ States may therefore favour **residents/domiciles** (e.g., fee concessions) without breaching equal citizenship, because "residence" is not a prohibited ground. **Caution:** ✅ "**place of birth**" ≠ "**residence**" — discrimination on place of birth remains barred.
- **Claim:** the largest single-citizenship exception has been removed. **Evidence:** ✅ till 2019, **Art 35A** (1954 Presidential Order under Art 370) protected J&K "permanent residents" in jobs/land; 📰 abrogated in **2019**, and the **Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in December 2023** (*In re Article 370*). **Significance:** ⚠️ J&K residents now stand under the general constitutional scheme, with statutory domicile rules. **Caution:** ✅ do **not** describe Art 35A permanent-resident privileges as current.

### 8.6 Bank C — OCI, CAA 2019 and the domicile distinction

- **Claim:** OCI is statutory engagement, not citizenship. **Evidence:** ✅ **L.M. Singhvi Committee** → **Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2003** created OCI; **2015 Amendment** merged **PIO + OCI**; ✅ OCI cardholders **cannot vote, contest, hold constitutional office (President/VP/judge/MP/MLA) or claim public-employment equality**. **Significance:** ⚠️ OCI is **not dual citizenship** (Art 9 bars it). **Caution:** ✅ OCI is a **statutory** status, cancellable for fraud/disaffection.
- **Claim:** the CAA 2019 is a religion-specified facilitation, and its validity is contested. **Evidence:** ✅ it offers a **facilitated naturalisation route (residence cut to 5 years)** for **Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, Christian** migrants from **Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan** who entered **on or before 31 December 2014**; 📰 **rules notified March 2024, first certificates issued May 2024**. **Significance:** ⚠️ **criticism** — a religion-based classification excluding Muslims allegedly violates **Art 14** and secularism, with NRC-linkage fears; ⚠️ **defence** — it is an under-inclusive facilitation for persecuted minorities of three neighbours, strips **no** citizen's status, and grants **no automatic citizenship** (statutory scrutiny remains). **Caution:** 📰 a constitutional challenge (Art 14) was **pending** before the Supreme Court on the record held here (status as of 13 August 2026); do not state it has been struck down or finally upheld.
- **Claim:** citizenship, domicile and residence are three distinct statuses. **Evidence:** ✅ **Citizenship** = full legal membership (Arts 5–11 + 1955 Act), carrying **political rights**; ⚠️ **Domicile** = the legal system a person treats as permanent home (India has a single **domicile of India**; "domicile in India" was a condition for Art 5 citizenship at commencement); ⚠️ **Residence** = factual ordinary residence, the basis for **electoral registration** in a constituency and for residence-based state benefits. **Significance:** ⚠️ only citizenship confers the vote and eligibility for office; residence/domicile do not. **Caution:** ✅ do not equate long residence or domicile with citizenship.

### 8.7 Mechanism, incentives and consequences

- ⚠️ **Fraternity mechanism:** single citizenship removes inter-state citizenship barriers, incentivising a national labour market and identity; the safety valve is the Art 16(3)/371 residence protections for vulnerable regions.
- ⚠️ **Who gains, who loses:** single citizenship benefits inter-state migrants and national integration, while the **residence and tribal-area exceptions** (Arts 16(3), 19(5), 371) protect locals and vulnerable regions from being outcompeted.
- ⚠️ **CAA design consequence:** by keying eligibility to religion + country + date, the Act creates an intelligible differentia its defenders call reasonable and its critics call a religious test — the crux of the Art 14 litigation.

### 8.8 Criticism, counter-argument and variation

- ⚠️ **OCI critique:** OCI expands diaspora ties and soft power but withholds political rights, which some see as second-class status and others as the necessary consequence of Art 9.
- ⚠️ **Counter-argument on the CAA:** defenders frame it as a narrow humanitarian facilitation for persecuted minorities of three theocratic-majority neighbours; critics say using **religion** as a criterion breaches Art 14 secularism — the dispute the Supreme Court must resolve.
- ⚠️ **Regional variation:** citizenship politics differ sharply — **Assam** turns on the NRC and the 1971 cut-off, while the CAA debate is nationwide; the same statutory framework produces different flashpoints by region.

### 8.9 Verdict scaffolds

- **Single-citizenship verdict:** "Single citizenship builds one nation without erasing regional protection: uniform political membership coexists with residence- and tribe-based safeguards under Articles 16(3), 19(5) and 371."
- **CAA verdict:** "The CAA's fate rests on Article 14: whether persecuted-minority facilitation is a reasonable classification or an impermissible religious criterion — a question the Supreme Court, not the statute's title, will settle."
- **Distinction verdict:** "Citizenship is membership, domicile is legal home, residence is a fact of location; only the first carries the vote, and conflating them is the error the Constitution's single-citizenship design guards against."

### 8.10 Prelims close-option distinctions (answers the routed objective demand)

- **Routed 2021 Prelims GS-I Q89 — citizenship, domicile and deprivation:** ✅ **Deprivation** is **compulsory loss ordered by the Central Government** — for fraud in registration/naturalisation, disloyalty/disaffection to the Constitution, unlawful trading/communicating with the enemy in war, a **2-year imprisonment within 5 years** of registration/naturalisation, or **7 years' continuous residence abroad**; it applies to **registered/naturalised** citizens. ✅ **Domicile in India** was a condition for citizenship at commencement (Art 5); India has **single citizenship** with no separate state citizenship.
- **Renunciation vs Termination vs Deprivation:** ✅ Renunciation = **voluntary declaration**; Termination = **automatic** on acquiring foreign citizenship; Deprivation = **compulsory by the Centre**.
- **OCI vs dual citizenship:** ✅ OCI is **not** dual citizenship (Art 9 bars it); OCI cannot vote or hold constitutional office.
- **NRC vs CAA cut-offs:** ✅ **Assam NRC = 24 March 1971** (Assam Accord); ✅ **CAA = 31 December 2014**.
- **Citizenship as a right:** ✅ citizenship is **not a Fundamental Right**; it is governed by ordinary law under **Art 11**.
