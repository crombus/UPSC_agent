## 2. What & Why (core idea)

✅ Fundamental Rights (FRs) protect persons against unconstitutional State action, but their
**beneficiaries differ**: some are citizen-only while others protect every person. They promote
**political democracy**, prevent authoritarian rule, and act as **limitations on the State** —
aiming at "a government of laws and not of men."

## 3. The Six Fundamental Rights

| # | Right | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Right to Equality | ✅ 14–18 |
| 2 | Right to Freedom | ✅ 19–22 |
| 3 | Right against Exploitation | ✅ 23–24 |
| 4 | Right to Freedom of Religion | ✅ 25–28 |
| 5 | Cultural & Educational Rights | ✅ 29–30 |
| 6 | Right to Constitutional Remedies | ✅ 32 |

> ✅ The **7th** — Right to Property (Art 31) — was **removed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978**.
> It is now a **legal right** under **Article 300-A** (Part XII).

> 🔑 Mnemonic: **"E-F-E-R-C-C"** → Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Religion,
> Cultural/educational, Constitutional remedies.

## 4. Key Features (simplified)

- ✅ Some FRs apply only to **citizens**, others to **all persons** (incl. foreigners, companies).
- ✅ **Not absolute** — the State can impose **reasonable restrictions** (courts decide reasonableness).
- ✅ Mostly available against the **State**; a few also against **private individuals**.
- ✅ **Justiciable** — you can move the courts directly; the **Supreme Court** guarantees them (Art 32).
- ✅ Parliament may amend Part III only through the constitutional-amendment process and
  **cannot damage the basic structure**; ordinary law remains reviewable under Article 13.

**2021 GS-II Q13 — "Right to Equality in the Constitutions of the USA and India" · Analyze the distinguishing features · 15 marks · 250 words.**
1. ✅ Anchor both systems: India, **Art 14** fuses *equality before law* (British/Dicey, negative) and *equal protection of laws* (American, positive); the USA relies on the single **Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment**.
2. ✅ India writes **protective discrimination into the text** — Art 15(3) (women/children), Art 15(4)/(5) (SEBC/SC/ST, education), Art 16(4) (backward-class reservation); the US Constitution has no such express clauses.
3. ✅ India's distinctive equality Articles have **no US analogue** — Art 17 (abolition of untouchability, enforceable against private persons) and Art 18 (abolition of titles).
4. ⚠️ Standard of review differs: India applies **reasonable classification** (intelligible differentia + rational nexus) and the **arbitrariness test** (*E.P. Royappa* 1974, *Maneka Gandhi* 1978); US courts apply **tiered scrutiny** developed judicially, not textually.
5. ⚠️ Enforcement: India guarantees the remedy itself as a fundamental right (Art 32); US equal-protection claims run through ordinary judicial review.
6. Verdict: ⚠️ both secure formal equality, but India's is a **substantive, group-conscious equality embedded in the text**, whereas the US model turns on a single judicially-elaborated clause.

**2022 GS-II Q2 — "Right of movement and residence and its limits" · Comment · 10 marks · 150 words.**
1. ✅ Source: **Art 19(1)(d)** freedom of movement and **Art 19(1)(e)** freedom of residence and settlement — **citizens only, against the State**.
2. ✅ Scope: movement is **internal** (throughout the territory of India); the foreign-travel dimension is protected under **Art 21** (*Maneka Gandhi* 1978), not Art 19(1)(d).
3. ✅ Limits: **Art 19(5)** permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public or for the **protection of any Scheduled Tribe**.
4. ⚠️ Illustrations of limits: restricted-area/tribal-area permits, public-health quarantine, movement curbs for public order — each tested for reasonableness and proportionality under Art 19(5) read with Art 14.
5. Verdict: ⚠️ mobility is integral to citizenship and national unity but is qualified, not absolute; ST-protection and general-public limits are constitutionally sanctioned.

**2023 GS-II Q11 — "Constitution as a living instrument and the expanding right to life and liberty" · Illustrate · 15 marks · 250 words.**
1. ✅ Text: **Art 21** protects life and personal liberty for **all persons**, deprivable only by procedure established by law.
2. ✅ *A.K. Gopalan* (1950): narrow reading — protection only against arbitrary **executive** action.
3. ✅ *Maneka Gandhi* (1978): the turn — procedure must be **fair, just and reasonable** (a due-process *standard* read into "procedure established by law", not American substantive due process); life means life with **human dignity**.
4. ✅ Illustrate the expansion: privacy (*K.S. Puttaswamy* 2017), clean environment, livelihood, shelter, health, speedy trial, free legal aid, against handcuffing/solitary confinement.
5. ⚠️ "Living instrument": the Court reads Art 21 as an evolving guarantee, so new facets are recognised without textual amendment.
6. ⚠️ Caution: expansion is **judicial interpretation**, not a new Article; over-expansion risks blurring justiciable limits.
7. Verdict: ⚠️ Art 21 is the clearest proof of the Constitution as a living document — a short clause becoming the charter of dignity.

**2023 GS-II Q12 — "Constitutional perspectives of gender justice, with provisions and case law" · Explain · 15 marks · 250 words** (Polity owns the constitutional half).
1. ✅ Formal equality: Art 14 (equality before law), Art 15(1) (no discrimination on grounds only of sex), Art 16(1)/(2) (equal opportunity in public employment).
2. ✅ Substantive/protective equality: **Art 15(3)** permits special provisions for women and children — enabling protective and affirmative measures.
3. ✅ Dignity duty: **Art 51A(e)** directs citizens to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
4. ✅ Case law: *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) — workplace sexual-harassment guidelines from Arts 14, 19(1)(g), 21; *Indian Young Lawyers Association* (Sabarimala, 2018) — access tested against equality and constitutional morality.
5. ⚠️ Art 21 dignity/autonomy jurisprudence underpins bodily autonomy and choice.
6. Cross-link: ⚠️ the socio-economic half (schemes, data) is owned by **Social-Justice**; Polity supplies the constitutional engine.
7. Verdict: ⚠️ the Constitution moves from formal non-discrimination to substantive, dignity-based gender justice.

**2019 GS-II Q5 — "What France can learn from India's approach to secularism" · 10 marks** (the religion-rights half; the comparison is owned by `basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md`).
1. ✅ Engine: **Art 25** (conscience; profess, practice, propagate — all persons), **Art 26** (denominations manage religious affairs), **Art 27** (no tax to promote a religion), **Art 28** (religious instruction in State institutions).
2. ✅ India's model is **positive/principled-distance secularism**: the State may regulate secular activity and **reform** — Art 25(2) allows throwing open Hindu institutions to all classes, and Art 17 abolishes untouchability.
3. ⚠️ Contrast with French **laïcité** (strict separation excluding religion from the public sphere); India permits equal engagement, not exclusion.
4. ⚠️ What France could draw: accommodation of religious plurality and reform-from-within rather than prohibition.
5. Cross-link: ⚠️ the full India–France comparison is owned by **`basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md`**; this file supplies the Art 25–28 rights engine.
6. Verdict: ⚠️ Indian secularism protects religious freedom while retaining a constitutional reform mandate.

**2024 GS-II Q12 — "Right to privacy under Article 21; DNA testing for paternity" · Explain · 15 marks · 250 words** (already routed to this owning topic).
1. ✅ Anchor: privacy is a fundamental right under **Art 21** (and Part III) — *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017), nine-judge Bench.
2. ⚠️ DNA/paternity testing pits a party's right to **identity and truth** against the other party's **privacy and bodily integrity**.
3. ⚠️ Courts apply a **proportionality / eminent-need test** — a DNA test is not ordered routinely; there must be a strong prima facie case and no less-intrusive alternative.
4. ✅ The **statutory presumption of legitimacy** protects a child born during a valid marriage; it is rebutted only by strong evidence.
5. Verdict: ⚠️ privacy is not absolute — it yields to a compelling, proportionate and lawful need, but never to routine compulsion.

### 7.2 Qualified thesis options

- *Fundamental Rights are not a static list but a judicially administered floor of dignity: the text has changed little since 1950, yet the content has expanded through Art 21 from mere protection against executive illegality to a positive charter of privacy, environment and livelihood.*
- *India's equality code is distinctive precisely because it is group-conscious: reasonable classification and protective discrimination are not exceptions to equality but its constitutional method.*
- *The strength of Part III lies less in the rights than in Art 32 — a right to the remedy itself — which is why Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" and the Court treats it as a basic feature.*
- *Rights in India are qualified, not absolute: every freedom in Art 19 carries its own restriction clause, so the real constitutional question is always the reasonableness and proportionality of the limit.*
- *The 44th Amendment's protection of Arts 20 and 21 even during Emergency is the constitutional lesson of 1975–77 — that some guarantees must survive the State's own claim of necessity.*

| Claim | Provision / case | Mechanism | Limitation / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protection against conviction is absolute in three ways | ✅ Art 20: no ex-post-facto law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination | Non-suspendable even in Emergency (with Art 21) | Ex-post-facto bars conviction not trial; self-incrimination excludes specimen signature/blood |

### 7.5A Bank B1 — **Article 19(1)(a) / 19(2): the free-speech and digital-rights evidence bank**

> Purpose: free speech is the single most-asked Part III family and the one most often answered with
> adjectives instead of authorities. Every unit below is **claim → named case/statute → significance →
> limit or dated status**. Statuses are stated as of **13 August 2026** and each carries a
> re-verification instruction.

**B1.1 — The foundational pair and the birth of "reasonable restrictions" (1950–51)**

| Claim | Case / statute | Significance | Limit / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-censorship and circulation bans on the press fall within Art 19(1)(a), and a State law authorising them for "public safety and public order" could not be saved by the **original** Art 19(2) | ✅ ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950)** — ban on circulation of the journal *Cross Roads* in Madras struck down | Established that (i) **freedom of circulation is part of freedom of speech**, and (ii) unless a restriction fits an **enumerated** ground in Art 19(2), it fails | ⚠️ The original Art 19(2) did **not** list "public order"; the Court held that grounds relating to security of the State could not be stretched to cover ordinary public order. Do **not** say the Court "struck down public-order restrictions as such" |
| A pre-censorship order on a newspaper on grounds of public safety/public order was equally bad under the unamended Art 19(2) | ✅ ***Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* (1950)** — pre-censorship of *Organiser* under the East Punjab Public Safety Act struck down | Companion case to *Romesh Thappar*; together they establish that **prior restraint on the press is presumptively unconstitutional** in India | ⚠️ Both are **1950** decisions on the **pre-amendment** text; **Fazl Ali J. dissented** in both. Do not cite them as authority on the *present* Art 19(2) wording |
| Parliament's response was to **widen** Art 19(2), not to overrule the cases | ✅ **Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951** — added **public order**, **friendly relations with foreign States** and **incitement to an offence**, and inserted the word **"reasonable"** before "restrictions" | The decisive structural point: the First Amendment simultaneously **expanded the grounds** and **subjected every restriction to a reasonableness test enforceable by courts** | ⚠️ Do **not** describe the First Amendment as purely restrictive — inserting "reasonable" created the judicial control that *Shreya Singhal* later used. Also do not attribute "sovereignty and integrity of India" to 1951: that ground came with the **16th Amendment (1963)** |

> 🔑 **The eight grounds of Art 19(2), exactly:** sovereignty and integrity of India · security of the
> State · friendly relations with foreign States · public order · decency or morality · contempt of
> court · defamation · incitement to an offence. Restrictions must be **reasonable** and imposed **by
> law** (a Constitution-permitted ground does not licence an executive order without legal authority).

**B1.2 — Sedition: the narrowing, the freeze, and the successor provision**

| Claim | Case / statute | Significance | Limit / dated status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sedition survived constitutional challenge **only because it was read down** | ✅ ***Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962)**, five-judge Bench, upholding **IPC s.124A** | Confined the offence to words or acts amounting to **incitement to violence or to public disorder**; strong criticism of the government, however harsh, is **not** sedition. Classic illustration of **reading down** to save a provision under Art 19(2) "public order" | ⚠️ *Kedar Nath* **upheld** s.124A — never write that it struck it down. Its protection is a **construction**, not a textual amendment, so misuse at the FIR stage was never cured |
| The provision was placed in abeyance rather than decided | ✅ ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India*** — by an interim order of **11 May 2022** the Supreme Court directed that **s.124A be kept in abeyance**: no fresh FIRs, and pending proceedings held in suspension, while the Union reconsidered the provision | A rare instance of a penal provision being **suspended by interim order** pending a constitutional decision — powerful evidence of the **chilling-effect** reasoning without a final holding | 📰 **Dated status (13 Aug 2026):** the constitutional challenge is **still undecided**; the abeyance has been **relaxed in part**, with reporting that pending trials/appeals may proceed **where the accused does not object**, the freeze otherwise continuing. ⚠️ Do **not** say s.124A was "struck down", "repealed by the Court", or "finally decided". Re-verify on the Supreme Court portal before writing any date |
| The successor provision is **not merely a renamed s.124A** | ✅ **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, s.152** ("acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"), in force with the BNS from **1 July 2024**; the IPC stands repealed | The **ingredients differ**: s.124A turned on exciting *disaffection/hatred/contempt* towards the Government established by law; **s.152 targets exciting secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities or encouraging feelings of separatist activity, endangering sovereignty/unity/integrity** — and expressly extends to electronic communication and the **use of financial means**. Punishment is also more severe | ⚠️ The two most common errors are opposite and both fatal: "sedition has simply been renamed" and "sedition has been abolished". The accurate formulation is **repeal-and-replace with different ingredients and a wider medium clause**. 📰 s.152 is itself under constitutional challenge on **vagueness/overbreadth** grounds — record it as **challenged and undecided**, not as struck down |

**B1.3 — Vagueness, overbreadth and the chilling effect (the doctrinal core)**

| Claim | Case / statute | Significance | Limit / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| A speech-restricting penal provision that is **vague and overbroad** is unconstitutional even if some applications would be legitimate | ✅ ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015)** struck down **s.66A, Information Technology Act, 2000** in its entirety | The single most usable Indian authority on free speech. It (i) imported **vagueness and overbreadth** as independent vices; (ii) named the **chilling effect** as a constitutional harm; (iii) held that terms such as "grossly offensive" and "annoyance" have **no manageable standard**; (iv) held that Art 19(2) grounds are **exhaustive** — "offensiveness" is not among them | ⚠️ s.66A was struck down for **vagueness/overbreadth**, not because "the internet is a fundamental right". Note the recorded scandal of **continued registration of cases under a struck-down section**, which the Court has repeatedly had to address — good evidence that declaring a law void is not self-executing |
| The Court separated three activities and protected two of them | ✅ *Shreya Singhal*'s **discussion / advocacy / incitement** distinction | **Discussion** and **advocacy** — however unpopular — are protected; only when speech reaches **incitement** may Art 19(2) be invoked. This is the test to deploy in any hate-speech, sedition or "anti-national speech" answer | ⚠️ The line is drawn at **incitement**, not at offence, hurt sentiment or unpopularity |
| Intermediary liability and blocking were treated differently | ✅ *Shreya Singhal* **read down s.79** IT Act and the Intermediary Guidelines (actual knowledge = a court order or a government notification), and **upheld s.69A** with its procedural safeguards | Shows the Court's method: strike the vague penal provision, **preserve** the narrowly-drawn blocking power with safeguards | ⚠️ Do **not** say the Court struck down internet blocking — **s.69A was upheld**. 📰 The **IT Rules, 2021** and later amendments are separate instruments under challenge; do not merge them into *Shreya Singhal* |

**B1.4 — Digital access, shutdowns and proportionality**

| Claim | Case / statute | Significance | Limit / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| The **medium** is protected because the freedom is | ✅ ***Anuradha Bhasin v. Do **not** quote shutdown counts or "world's highest" claims without a dated official source |

**B1.5 — Reputation, defamation and the competing right**

| Claim | Case / statute | Significance | Limit / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Criminal** defamation is constitutionally valid | ✅ ***Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016)** upheld **IPC ss.499–500** (now carried forward as **BNS, 2023, s.356**) | Reasoned that **reputation is an intrinsic part of the right to life under Art 21**, and that Art 19(2) expressly names **defamation** as a ground; free speech and reputation must be **balanced**, not ranked | ⚠️ The strongest counter-argument — that criminal process is disproportionate where a civil remedy exists, and that s.499's exceptions place the burden on the speaker — must be stated for balance in a "Critically examine" answer. ⚠️ Update the section number: the IPC is repealed; the offence now sits in the **BNS** |
| Free speech is limited by other **fundamental rights**, not only by Art 19(2) grounds | ✅ *Subramanian Swamy* uses **Art 21 (reputation)**; ✅ the **fraternity/dignity** language of the Preamble is often paired with it | Gives an answer its second axis: speech is bounded **horizontally** by others' dignity, not merely **vertically** by State interests | ⚠️ Do not convert this into a general "balancing" licence — Art 19(2) grounds remain **exhaustive**, and any restriction must still be **by law** and **reasonable** |

**B1.6 — Press freedom, prior restraint and the hate-speech boundary (state only what is supportable)**

- ✅ **There is no separate "freedom of the press" Article.** Press freedom is derived from **Art 19(1)(a)** and is therefore subject to the same Art 19(2) grounds — a settled proposition and a frequent Prelims discriminator. ⚠️ Do not cite a non-existent press Article, and do not claim journalists hold rights beyond those of any citizen.
- ✅ **Prior restraint is presumptively suspect** in India: *Romesh Thappar* and *Brij Bhushan* (1950) struck at pre-censorship and circulation bans, and *Shreya Singhal* (2015) refused to let vague standards operate as a de facto prior restraint through the chilling effect. ⚠️ Do **not** state that prior restraint is *absolutely* barred — pre-censorship of films is constitutionally permitted, cinema being treated as a distinct medium under **Art 19(2)** with the **Cinematograph Act, 1952** and the certification regime.
- ✅ **The right to know** and the citizen's access to information are read into Art 19(1)(a) — the constitutional foundation on which the **Right to Information Act, 2005** was built (see `CIC-and-SIC.md`). ⚠️ Keep the Act's later amendments and the **DPDP Act, 2023 s.44(3)** issue in that file, dated, rather than asserting them here.
- ⚠️ **Hate speech — the honest position.** India has **no single statutory definition of "hate speech"**; the ground actually invoked under Art 19(2) is **public order** (or, where applicable, decency/morality or incitement to an offence), and the operative test remains *Shreya Singhal*'s **incitement** threshold. Named statutory anchors that may safely be cited are the **BNS, 2023** provisions on promoting enmity between groups and on statements conducive to public mischief (**IPC ss.153A and 505 in the repealed Code**), and **s.123(3)/(3A) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951** (corrupt practice — appeals on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language). **Do not** invent a "hate speech law", do not assert Law Commission recommendations as enacted, and do not attribute a definition to any judgment.

**B1.7 — Answer structures for the free-speech family**

| Marks | Architecture | Evidence load |
|---:|---|---|
| **10** | **Thesis** (speech is protected by a *test*, not by a slogan) → **the Art 19(2) architecture** (eight enumerated grounds + "reasonable" + "by law") → **one narrowing authority** (*Kedar Nath* reading down, or *Shreya Singhal* on vagueness) → **one limit or live status** → **verdict** | 3 units; at least **1 Article + 1 case** |
| **15** | **Thesis** → **the 1950–51 sequence** (*Romesh Thappar*/*Brij Bhushan* → First Amendment adds grounds *and* "reasonable") → **the doctrinal core** (*Shreya Singhal*: vagueness, overbreadth, chilling effect, discussion–advocacy–incitement) → **the medium** (*Anuradha Bhasin*: publication, proportionality, no indefinite shutdown) → **the counterweight** (*Subramanian Swamy*: Art 21 reputation) → **qualified verdict** | 5–6 units; **≥ 4 named cases**, ≥ 1 dated status |
| **20** | **Thesis** → **text and structure** (19(1)(a)/19(2), exhaustive grounds, restriction must be by law) → **the historical settlement** (1950 cases → First Amendment 1951 → 16th Amendment 1963) → **the criminal-law axis** (*Kedar Nath* read-down → *Vombatkere* abeyance and its 2026 status → BNS s.152's **different ingredients**, itself challenged) → **the digital axis** (*Shreya Singhal* s.66A struck / s.69A upheld / s.79 read down; *Anuradha Bhasin* proportionality and review) → **the competing-rights axis** (*Subramanian Swamy*, Art 21 reputation; the hate-speech honest position) → **institutional gap** (declared-void provisions still being used; shutdown practice; opaque orders) → **graded verdict** | 7–9 units + one explicit paragraph on **what the doctrine has *not* fixed** |

- **Qualified thesis options (free speech):**
  - *India protects speech through a structured test rather than an absolute guarantee: Article 19(2)'s grounds are exhaustive and every restriction must be both "by law" and "reasonable" — which is why the decisive Indian battles have been fought over vagueness and procedure, not over the existence of limits.*
  - *The First Amendment of 1951 is misread as a purely restrictive amendment: by inserting the word "reasonable" it created the judicial control that later allowed Section 66A to be struck down.*
  - *India's free-speech deficit is now less a problem of bad text than of unreviewable process — abeyant provisions, secret orders and prosecutions under struck-down sections show that the doctrine is ahead of the administration.*
  - *The shift from IPC s.124A to BNS s.152 was a re-drafting, not an abolition: the ingredients changed, the medium widened, and the constitutional question simply moved to a new provision.*

- **Novel-hypothetical route (use when the question is unfamiliar — e.g. a new law on "online misinformation", AI-generated content, deepfakes, or a platform-blocking order):** ⚠️ this is a **method**, not a set of facts; assert **no** provisions of any instrument you have not verified.
  1. **Classify the restriction:** is it *penal* (criminalising speech), *preventive* (blocking/suspension), or *structural* (licensing, intermediary duties)? Each attracts a different line of authority.
  2. **Locate the Art 19(2) ground actually available** — and say so explicitly. If the ground is "offensiveness", "fake news" or "annoyance" as such, **it is not in Art 19(2)** and *Shreya Singhal* is decisive.
  3. **Apply vagueness/overbreadth:** is there a manageable standard? Would a reasonable citizen know what is prohibited? Is protected **discussion and advocacy** swept in along with **incitement**?
  4. **Apply proportionality** (*Anuradha Bhasin*): legitimate aim → rational connection → necessity/least restrictive means → balancing; and test **duration, review and publication** of any order.
  5. **Check the procedural safeguards** the Court has previously required — a **published** order, a **reasoned** order, **periodic review**, and an **available challenge** (the *Bhasin* triad); and the *Shreya Singhal* s.79 rule that an intermediary's obligation is triggered by a **court order or government notification**, not private complaint.
  6. **Name the competing right** if one exists (reputation/dignity under Art 21 after *Subramanian Swamy*; privacy under *Puttaswamy*) and balance rather than rank.
  7. **State the limit of your own answer:** if the instrument's status is undecided, say **"challenged and undecided as of [date]"** — an examiner rewards a dated abstention and penalises an invented holding.

### 7.5B Bank B2 — **Article 21: the named substantive anchors** (each with its proposition and its limit)

> "expanded". This bank supplies the **named holdings** an examiner looks for. Format:
> **claim → case → exact proposition → limit.** This is paper-affecting, not decorative — an Art 21

**B2.1 — The dignity core**

| Claim | Case | Exact proposition | Limit / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life means **more than animal existence** | ✅ ***Francis Coralie Mullin v. **B2.2 — Livelihood, shelter and the socio-economic reading**

| Claim | Case | Exact proposition | Limit / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Livelihood** is part of the right to life | ✅ ***Olga Tellis v. **B2.3 — Custody, arrest and the prison**

| Claim | Case | Exact proposition | Limit / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Custodial violence assaults Art 21, and courts may lay down **binding safeguards** | ✅ ***D.K. Basu v. | **Prisoners retain fundamental rights** | ✅ ***Sunil Batra v. | **Speedy trial** is part of Art 21 | ✅ ***Hussainara Khatoon v. | **Free legal aid** is part of Art 21 | ✅ ***M.H. Hoskot v. **B2.4 — End-of-life, health, environment, education, privacy**

| Claim | Case | Exact proposition | Limit / dated status |
|---|---|---|---|
| The right to life includes the **right to die with dignity** | ✅ ***Common Cause (A Registered Society) v. Union of India* (2018)**, five-judge Bench | Recognised **passive euthanasia** — withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment for a terminally ill patient or one in a permanent vegetative state — and the validity of an **advance medical directive ("living will")** as part of the Art 21 right to die with dignity, subject to procedural safeguards | ⚠️ **Active euthanasia and assisted suicide remain unlawful** — never blur the two. 📰 **Dated status (13 Aug 2026):** the 2018 procedural safeguards proved unworkable and were **modified/simplified by a Constitution-Bench order in January 2023** (streamlining the medical-board mechanism and removing the judicial-magistrate countersignature). Cite the **holding** as settled and the **procedure** as modified; re-verify the current steps before listing them |
| **Emergency medical care** is within Art 21 | ✅ ***Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal* (1996)** | A government hospital's failure to provide **timely emergency medical treatment** violates Art 21; preservation of life is a constitutional obligation of the State and compensation may be awarded | ⚠️ It obliges the State to provide **emergency** care; it does **not** create a general enforceable right to health services on demand. Do not cite health-expenditure figures here |
| A **pollution-free environment** is within Art 21 | ✅ ***Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar* (1991)**, with the ✅ ***M.C. Mehta*** line (Oleum gas leak, Ganga pollution, vehicular pollution) | The right to life includes the **right to enjoyment of pollution-free water and air**; Art 21 is read with **Arts 48A and 51A(g)** and enforced through Art 32 | ⚠️ *Subhash Kumar* also **warned against PIL used for personal or oblique motives** — a useful balancing unit. Environmental doctrine (precautionary principle, polluter pays, public trust, absolute liability) belongs to the Environment folder; keep only the **Art 21 hook** here |
| **Education** moved from interpretation to text | ✅ ***Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1993)** read education into Art 21; ✅ the **86th Amendment (2002)** inserted **Art 21A** and recast Art 45; the **RTE Act, 2009** followed | The clearest illustration of the full cycle **judicial interpretation → constitutional text → statute** | ⚠️ After 2002 the correct citation for the 6–14 right is **Art 21A**, not Art 21; and Art 21A is a **fundamental right**, not a directive |
| **Privacy** is within Art 21 | ✅ ***K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (24 August 2017)**, nine-judge Bench | Privacy is intrinsic to life and personal liberty under Art 21 and to the freedoms in Part III; any restriction must satisfy **legality, legitimate aim and proportionality** with procedural safeguards | ⚠️ Privacy is **not a new Article**. The proportionality test it settled is the same one applied in *Anuradha Bhasin* (§7.5A) — cross-cite them instead of treating them as separate doctrines |

- **The unifying mechanism (state this, don't just list cases):** ⚠️ Art 21's expansion works through **three moves** — (i) *Maneka* converted "procedure established by law" into a **reasonableness review**; (ii) courts then read **non-justiciable DPSP content** (livelihood, health, environment, legal aid, education) into the justiciable right, so Part IV is enforced **through** Part III; and (iii) **Art 32 remedies** — guidelines, continuing mandamus and public-law compensation — supplied enforcement the text never mentions.
- **The limit that earns marks:** ⚠️ every anchor above is **remedial rather than distributive**. Art 21 can compel **fair procedure, safeguards, emergency response and compensation**; it cannot by itself allocate housing, appoint doctors or fund schools. The honest verdict is that Art 21 has made the State **answerable for deprivation**, not **liable to deliver** — which is precisely why the DPSP-to-statute route in `Directive-Principles.md` still matters.

**The Essential Religious Practices (ERP) doctrine — stated precisely, because most answers state it wrongly:**

| Step | Named case | Exact proposition | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Origin | ✅ ***Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* (1954)** | The Court distinguished **religious** matters, which Art 26(b) protects, from the **secular** activities associated with religion, which the State may regulate, and held that what constitutes an **essential part of a religion is to be decided with reference to the doctrines of that religion itself** | ⚠️ This is the doctrine's textual root; do not attribute the doctrine's origin to a later case |
| Consequence | ⚠️ Judicial practice since | Courts, not religious authorities, end up deciding **which** practice is essential, and only an essential practice gets Art 25/26 protection | ⚠️ The criticism — that a secular court is made an arbiter of theology — is a legitimate and examinable objection, not a fringe view |
| Application | ✅ ***Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (Sabarimala, 2018)** | The majority held the exclusion of women of a particular age group unconstitutional and found the practice was not an essential religious practice of a separate religious denomination | 📰 The review proceedings led to a **reference of broader questions to a larger bench**. A **nine-judge Constitution Bench heard the reference in April–May 2026 and has reserved its opinion; no verdict had been pronounced as of 13 August 2026.** Write the 2018 holding as decided **and** record the reference as reserved — do **not** state or predict the outcome, and do **not** describe the 2018 decision as overruled |
| Boundary | ✅ Art 25(2)(b) | Even a genuinely religious practice yields to laws providing for **social welfare and reform** and for throwing open Hindu religious institutions to all classes and sections of Hindus | ⚠️ This is why the "religious freedom" defence is weaker in India than in a strict-separation system |

- 🔑 **Answer discipline:** ERP questions are won by separating **(i)** the constitutional text, **(ii)** the judicially created test, **(iii)** the specific holding in a named case, and **(iv)** the criticism of judicial competence — a single fused paragraph reads as opinion.

### 7.7 Bank D — Enforcement engine and saving provisions (Art 12, 13, 32, 31A/B/C, 33–35, 358/359)

**Who is bound; what is void:**

| Claim | Provision / case | Mechanism | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| "State" is defined broadly | ✅ Art 12; ⚠️ instrumentality/agency test (*Ajay Hasia* 1981) | Includes government, legislatures, local & "other authorities" that are instrumentalities of the State | A body under deep and pervasive State control is "State"; a purely private body is not |

- ⚠️ **Horizontality:** several FRs already bind private persons — Art 15(2), 17, 23, 24. 📰 In *Kaushal Kishor v. ### 7.8 Constitutional mechanism, institutional incentives and consequences

- ✅ **Judicial review is the operating mechanism** (Art 13 + Art 32/226): a citizen can move the Supreme Court directly, which makes rights self-executing rather than dependent on legislative goodwill.
- ⚠️ **Incentive effect:** because Art 32 guarantees the remedy, the cost of unconstitutional State action is immediate invalidation — this disciplines executive and legislative behaviour and channels rights-expansion through the courts.
- ⚠️ **Who gains / who loses:** protective discrimination (Art 15(3)–(5), 16(4)) redistributes opportunity toward backward classes and women, while the 50% ceiling and creamy-layer rule guard against over-reach; disciplined-forces personnel (Art 33) trade some rights for institutional cohesion.
- ⚠️ **Consequence:** the Art 21 route has made the Supreme Court a major policy actor (environment, privacy, custodial rights) — both the strength and the standing criticism of the Indian rights model.

### 7.9 Criticism, counter-argument, variation and implementation constraints

- ⚠️ **Judicial over-expansion:** reading ever more into Art 21 risks converting policy questions into rights and straining the separation of powers.
- ⚠️ **Reasonableness is open-ended:** "reasonable restrictions" and "public order" give the State wide latitude; the balance shifts case by case.
- ⚠️ **Preventive detention is a structural anomaly:** Art 22 permits detention without trial inside a rights chapter — a criticised feature, aggravated by the un-enforced 44th-Amendment reduction.
- ⚠️ **Federal/administrative variation:** enforcement quality varies across States (legal aid, custodial safeguards); the text is uniform but delivery is not.
- ⚠️ **Emergency lesson:** the pre-1978 position allowed even Art 21 to be suspended; the 44th Amendment corrected this, showing the rights framework is itself a product of political experience.

### 7.10 Verdict scaffolds

- **Equality / reservation demands:** ⚠️ *India's equality guarantee is substantive and group-conscious; reservation is a constitutional method of equality, disciplined by the 50% ceiling, creamy-layer exclusion and the efficiency caveat of Art 335 — not a departure from Art 14.*
- **Life & liberty demands:** ⚠️ *Art 21 is the Constitution's living core; from* Gopalan *to* Maneka *to* Puttaswamy *it has grown by interpretation, and the task now is to expand dignity without dissolving the line between rights and policy.*
- **Emergency / suspension demands:** ⚠️ *The suspension architecture is deliberately asymmetric — Art 19 yields, but Arts 20 and 21 do not — and the 44th Amendment makes that non-derogable core the settled constitutional answer to 1975–77.*
