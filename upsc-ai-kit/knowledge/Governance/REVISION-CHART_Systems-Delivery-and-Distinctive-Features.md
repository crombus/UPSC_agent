# Governance: Systems, Delivery and Distinctive Features - Last-Minute Revision Chart

> **Purpose:** One-place revision immediately before GS-II Mains, with selective Prelims utility.
> **Coverage:** Good governance, policy design, development actors, civil society, e-governance, digital public infrastructure, citizen-centric administration, accountability, civil services, administrative reform, regulators, local governance, public finance, participation, evaluation and sports governance.
> **Two-pass design:** Core is independently answer-complete. Advanced adds institutional design, trade-offs and deeper evaluation; skipping it must not affect the paper outcome.
> **Status rule:** Distinguish recommendation, policy, scheme, executive instruction, enacted law, commenced provision, constituted institution, operational platform and evaluated outcome.

## 2. Universal governance-answer framework

| Step | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Define | What exact governance failure or objective is involved? |
| Locate | Does the failure arise in design, capacity, delivery, accountability or learning? |
| Assign | Which level and actor has authority and responsibility? |
| Diagnose | Is the constraint legal, fiscal, administrative, technological, behavioural or political? |
| Include | Who is excluded by geography, disability, language, gender, identity, cost or digital design? |
| Balance | Which trade-off is involved: speed/consultation, autonomy/accountability, efficiency/equity or data/privacy? |
| Reform | What institutional correction, safeguard and feedback mechanism are required? |
| Measure | Which output, outcome and impact indicators would establish success? |

### L-A-C-E-F-I test

`Legality/status -> Accessibility -> Capacity -> Enforceability -> Federal fit -> Inclusion/privacy`

Use this test for a portal, charter, regulator, grievance system, scheme, social audit, public-private arrangement or reform proposal.

## 3. Government, governance and good governance

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning | Do not confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| Government | Formal institutions possessing public authority | Governance, which includes processes and non-state actors |
| Governance | Processes through which collective decisions are made, implemented and reviewed | Administration alone |
| Good governance | Effective, lawful, accountable, transparent, responsive, inclusive and participatory exercise of authority | Mere efficiency |
| Public administration | Organisation and performance of executive work | The entire governance ecosystem |
| Development administration | Administration directed toward socio-economic transformation | Routine regulatory administration |
| Governmentality/capacity | Ability to make credible decisions and implement them | Size of government |

### Good-governance balance

| Value | Without balancing value |
|---|---|
| Efficiency | May produce exclusion or procedural unfairness |
| Participation | May become slow, unequal or captured |
| Transparency | May reveal failure without creating remedy |
| Autonomy | May weaken democratic accountability |
| Standardisation | May ignore local context |
| Discretion | May enable responsiveness or arbitrariness |
| Technology | May reduce friction or scale exclusion |

> **Core verdict:** Good governance is not maximisation of one value; it is an institutional balance that converts lawful authority into inclusive outcomes with correction mechanisms.

## 4. Five recurring governance gaps

| Gap | Identification marker | Typical correction |
|---|---|---|
| Design gap | Wrong diagnosis, unclear goal, one-size-fits-all rule | Evidence, consultation, pilots and explicit theory of change |
| Capacity gap | Staff, skill, finance, technology or coordination deficit | Functional devolution, training, staffing and interoperable systems |
| Delivery gap | Delay, discretion, last-mile exclusion or poor quality | Process redesign, service standards, assisted access and local capacity |
| Accountability gap | Diffused responsibility, opacity or weak remedy | Named responsibility, audit, appeal, consequence and disclosure |
| Learning gap | Outputs counted but outcomes ignored | Independent evaluation, data-quality checks and policy correction |

```text
good intention
    - accurate diagnosis
    - feasible design
    - capable institution
    - accessible delivery
    - enforceable accountability
    - credible evaluation
    =
implementation gap
```

## 5. Policy design and implementation

### Policy cycle

`problem recognition -> agenda setting -> formulation -> adoption -> financing -> implementation -> monitoring -> evaluation -> redesign/termination`

| Design question | Failure if ignored |
|---|---|
| What is the public problem? | Solution searches for a problem |
| Who is affected and how? | Targeting and inclusion errors |
| What causal mechanism changes behaviour/outcome? | Activity without impact |
| Which institution has authority and capacity? | Unfunded or unowned mandate |
| How do Union, state and local roles interact? | Federal coordination failure |
| What incentives face frontline workers and beneficiaries? | Gaming, burden shifting or low uptake |
| How will grievances and exceptions be handled? | Rigid exclusion |
| What evidence will trigger correction or exit? | Permanent but ineffective programme |

### Common implementation failures

| Failure | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Principal-agent problem | Implementer incentives diverge from policy objective |
| Information asymmetry | Centre cannot observe local conditions or service quality |
| Street-level discretion | Frontline decisions reshape the actual policy |
| Silo administration | Interdependent tasks remain divided across departments |
| Unfunded mandate | Responsibility transferred without resources |
| Target fixation | Measurable output displaces real objective |
| Exclusion error | Eligible person is denied |
| Inclusion error | Ineligible person receives benefit |

> **Mains line:** Implementation is not a mechanical final stage; frontline institutions, incentives and citizen capability partly remake the policy itself.

## 6. Development processes and the development industry

| Actor | Contribution | Governance risk |
|---|---|---|
| State | Scale, legitimacy, taxation and rights framework | Bureaucratic rigidity or weak local fit |
| NGO | Innovation, mobilisation, specialised reach and monitoring | Weak disclosure, donor dependence or limited scale |
| SHG/community organisation | Trust, peer support, savings, credit and collective agency | Elite capture, unpaid burden or unequal voice |
| Charity/trust | Flexible finance and public-purpose activity | Paternalism and weak accountability |
| Donor/development agency | Finance, expertise and knowledge transfer | Donor-driven priorities and fragmented projects |
| Private enterprise/social enterprise | Technology, management and capital | Profit bias, cream-skimming and accountability gaps |
| Cooperative | Member ownership and pooled economic action | Politicisation or managerial weakness |

### Evaluate any development intervention

`legitimacy + community ownership + additionality + equity + financial sustainability + disclosure + grievance + measurable outcome`

> **Trap:** Non-state does not mean non-public, anti-state or automatically participatory. Judge the actor by authority, funding, accountability and community control.

## 7. NGOs, SHGs, pressure groups and civil society

| Institution | Primary character | Distinctive contribution |
|---|---|---|
| NGO | Formally organised non-government body | Service, advocacy, research or mobilisation |
| SHG | Small affinity-based mutual-support group | Savings-credit plus collective agency |
| Pressure group | Seeks to influence public policy without normally seeking government office | Interest articulation |
| Social movement | Sustained collective action around social change | Agenda formation and norm change |
| Community-based organisation | Locally rooted membership institution | Local knowledge and participation |
| Trade union/professional association | Organised occupational interest | Bargaining, standards and representation |

### Social-capital chain

```text
regular association
 -> trust and information
 -> collective savings/action
 -> bargaining power
 -> access to institutions/markets
 -> agency and empowerment
```

### Civil-society answer balance

| Contribution | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| Last-mile reach | Public disclosure and outcome verification |
| Voice for marginal groups | Internal representation and inclusion |
| Innovation | Evidence before scaling |
| State accountability | Lawful, proportionate regulation |
| Service partnership | Clear responsibility; state must not abandon rights obligation |

> **Core verdict:** Democratic governance requires an autonomous civil society, but autonomy and public accountability should reinforce—not cancel—each other.

## 8. E-governance and user-centricity

### Transformation ladder

| Stage | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Computerisation | Digital tools added to an existing office |
| Digitisation | Analogue record converted into digital form |
| Digitalisation | Workflow redesigned using digital technology |
| Integration | Departments and databases exchange authorised information |
| Transformation | Institution, incentives and service journey redesigned around outcomes |

### Interaction models

| Model | Example logic |
|---|---|
| G2C | Public service, certificate, grievance or benefit |
| G2B | Licence, tax, procurement or compliance |
| G2G | Interdepartmental data/workflow |
| G2E | Personnel, training and internal administration |

### User-centric design test

`discoverable -> understandable -> accessible -> multilingual -> assisted -> low-document -> consent-aware -> trackable -> appealable`

| E-governance promise | Failure mode |
|---|---|
| Lower transaction cost | Citizens bear device, travel, authentication or agent cost |
| Reduced discretion | Algorithmic rigidity creates unreviewable exclusion |
| Faster service | Back-end delay survives behind a front-end portal |
| Transparency | Dashboard lacks responsibility or remedy |
| Scale | Design defect also scales |
| Data integration | Function creep, breach or surveillance risk |

> **Trap:** An online service is not automatically user-centric. Judge the whole service journey, including assisted and offline alternatives.

## 9. Digital public infrastructure and data governance

### DPI architecture

```text
IDENTITY / AUTHENTICATION
          +
PAYMENT / TRANSACTION RAIL
          +
DATA OR DOCUMENT EXCHANGE
          +
OPEN STANDARDS AND GOVERNANCE
          ->
multiple public and private services
```

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| DPI | Shared digital building blocks enabling multiple services |
| Platform | Technical and institutional environment connecting participants |
| Interoperability | Systems exchange and use information through agreed standards |
| Data minimisation | Collect only what is necessary for the stated purpose |
| Purpose limitation | Use data only for a specified legitimate purpose |
| Consent | Informed and specific permission where legally relevant |
| Privacy by design | Safeguards embedded before deployment |
| Federated design | Data/functions remain distributed rather than held in one repository |

### DPI evaluation

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Inclusion | Can people without devices, literacy, connectivity or reliable biometrics access service? |
| Competition | Do open standards prevent lock-in and gatekeeping? |
| Security | Are authentication, encryption, access control and breach response adequate? |
| Privacy | Are collection, sharing, retention and deletion lawful and proportionate? |
| Accountability | Can a person discover, challenge and correct a harmful decision? |
| Federalism | Are state and local capacities and legitimate variation accommodated? |

> **Status discipline:** A data-protection Act, its commencement provisions, notified rules, an appointed board and operational enforcement are separate stages.

## 10. Citizen-centric administration

### Service-quality chain

`service standard -> public commitment -> accessible delivery -> tracking -> grievance -> appeal -> correction -> compensation/consequence where provided`

| Tool | Distinctive role |
|---|---|
| Citizens' Charter | Public commitment on service, standard, time and responsibility |
| Sevottam | Framework linking charter, grievance redress and service capability |
| Right-to-Public-Services law | Statutory time-bound delivery and remedies where state law provides |
| CPGRAMS | Administrative grievance registration, routing, tracking, feedback and appeal architecture |
| Facilitation centre/CSC | Assisted access interface |

### Why charters fail

- vague standards and no measurable timeline;
- no named responsible officer;
- weak consultation and public awareness;
- no link to internal process capacity;
- no effective escalation, remedy or consequence;
- copied charter not adapted to local service;
- no periodic review using grievance data.

> **Trap:** A Citizens' Charter is ordinarily an administrative commitment, not by itself a universally enforceable statutory right.

## 11. Transparency, accountability and remedy

### Accountability chain

```text
TRANSPARENCY
information becomes visible
        ->
ANSWERABILITY
authority explains decision/performance
        ->
ENFORCEABILITY
correction, remedy or consequence
        ->
LEARNING
system changes to prevent recurrence
```

| Mechanism | Primary function | Key distinction |
|---|---|---|
| RTI | Citizen access to information plus proactive disclosure | Disclosure is not grievance adjudication |
| CAG audit | Financial, compliance and performance audit | Audit findings feed legislative accountability |
| CVC | Central vigilance supervision/advice within statutory mandate | Not a criminal court |
| Lokpal | Corruption complaints against covered public servants | Not a general service-grievance portal |
| CIC/SIC | RTI complaints and second appeals | Not general anti-corruption bodies |
| Departmental grievance | Administrative correction of service failure | Distinct from judicial remedy |
| Social audit | Community verification, public hearing and follow-up | Not the same as departmental inspection |
| Whistle-blowing protection | Protects disclosure of wrongdoing under applicable framework | Enactment and commencement must be separated |

### RTI answer dimensions

`democratic participation + exposure of arbitrariness + better records + accountability`

balanced against

`privacy + legitimate confidentiality + institutional capacity + safety + misuse claims`

> **Core rule:** Transparency is necessary but insufficient; accountability additionally requires an identifiable duty-holder, a forum, an answer and a corrective consequence.

## 12. Social audit, inspection and financial audit

| Mechanism | Who examines? | Main object | Public participation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal inspection | Department/supervisor | Compliance and administration | Usually limited |
| Financial audit | Auditor | Accounts, legality and financial propriety | Indirect |
| Performance audit | Auditor | Economy, efficiency and effectiveness | Indirect |
| Evaluation | Internal/external evaluator | Outputs, outcomes and causal impact | Variable |
| Social audit | Community/beneficiaries through institutional process | Records versus lived implementation | Central |

### Effective social-audit chain

`record access -> independent facilitation -> community verification -> public hearing -> named findings -> time-bound recovery/correction -> action-taken disclosure`

> **Trap:** A public meeting alone is not a social audit. Records, verification, independence and follow-up are indispensable.

## 13. Civil services in a democracy

| Democratic role | Institutional requirement |
|---|---|
| Continuity | Professional, stable administration |
| Political responsiveness | Loyalty to lawful elected policy, not partisan interest |
| Rule of law | Objective procedure and reasoned decisions |
| Welfare delivery | Field capacity and citizen orientation |
| Federal coordination | Union-state-local cooperation |
| Crisis response | Preparedness, discretion and accountability |
| Policy advice | Evidence, candour and institutional memory |

### Recurring tensions

| Tension | Balanced position |
|---|---|
| Neutrality vs commitment | Commitment to Constitution and lawful government, not party |
| Generalist vs specialist | Integrate coordination skill with domain expertise |
| Permanence vs performance | Protect impartiality while enforcing accountability |
| Discretion vs control | Permit reasoned flexibility with records, review and remedy |
| Central standards vs local context | Common capability with contextual implementation |
| Lateral entry vs career service | Expertise plus transparent selection, conflict safeguards and institutional integration |

### Mission Karmayogi logic

`rule-based personnel administration -> role-based competency development -> continuous learning -> improved public-service outcomes`

> **Mains line:** Civil-service reform must align recruitment, posting, training, performance, specialisation, tenure and ethics; training alone cannot repair distorted incentives.

## 14. Administrative reform and Second ARC

### Reform sequence

```text
DIAGNOSIS
 -> recommendation
 -> government decision
 -> legal/administrative change
 -> resources and ownership
 -> implementation
 -> behavioural adoption
 -> outcome evaluation
```

| Reform family | Typical instruments |
|---|---|
| Structural | Departmental reorganisation, delegation and coordination |
| Procedural | Simplification, single-window service and reduced layers |
| Personnel | Recruitment, tenure, competencies and performance |
| Ethical/accountability | Codes, vigilance, disclosure and grievance |
| Technological | Workflow redesign and interoperable platforms |
| Participatory | Consultation, decentralisation and social accountability |

### Second ARC memory clusters

| Cluster | Major themes |
|---|---|
| Ethical government | Ethics, corruption, transparency and crisis of confidence |
| Citizen-state interface | Citizen-centric administration, e-governance and RTI |
| Personnel/state capacity | Civil services and organisational structure |
| Decentralisation | Local governance and social capital |
| Public order/security | Public order, terrorism, conflict and crisis management |
| Financial management | Budgeting, expenditure and accountability |

> **Trap:** ARC recommendation is an analytical source, not proof that a reform is current law or has been implemented.

## 15. Regulatory governance

### Why regulators exist

`technical complexity + natural monopoly/network effects + information asymmetry + need for credible rules -> specialised regulator`

| Design dimension | Independence safeguard | Accountability safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Appointment | Transparent criteria and plural selection | Published process and eligibility |
| Tenure/removal | Fixed tenure and specified grounds | Reviewable removal process |
| Finance | Predictable resources | Audit and legislative scrutiny |
| Rule-making | Technical expertise | Consultation and reasoned publication |
| Enforcement | Investigative capacity | Due process and proportionality |
| Adjudication | Decisional separation | Appeal or judicial review |
| Post-tenure | Cooling-off/conflict rules | Disclosure |

### Regulatory failure modes

- capture by regulated industry or political executive;
- revolving door and conflict of interest;
- overlapping jurisdiction and forum shopping;
- opaque subordinate legislation;
- over-regulation that suppresses entry and innovation;
- under-regulation that socialises risk;
- weak consumer representation;
- accountability deficit without effective appeal.

> **Core verdict:** Independence means insulation from improper influence, not freedom from law, reasons, audit, legislative scrutiny or judicial review.

## 16. Local governance and service delivery

### Devolution test: 3F + functionary accountability

| Requirement | Core question |
|---|---|
| Functions | Is the activity clearly assigned? |
| Funds | Are predictable own and transferred revenues available? |
| Functionaries | Does the local body control adequate staff? |
| Functionality | Can it plan, procure, maintain assets and use data? |
| Accountability | Are Gram Sabha/ward, audit and grievance mechanisms real? |

### Subsidiarity

`Assign a function to the lowest level capable of performing it effectively, while higher levels provide standards, finance, equalisation and support.`

| Local advantage | Local risk |
|---|---|
| Context knowledge | Elite capture |
| Citizen proximity | Weak technical capacity |
| Integrated local planning | Fragmented line departments |
| Faster feedback | Inadequate own revenue |
| Democratic participation | Unfunded mandates |

### Service-delivery chain

`activity mapping -> local plan -> finance -> staff/procurement -> delivery -> maintenance -> citizen feedback -> audit`

> **Trap:** Constitutional status does not automatically produce operational devolution. Examine state law, activity mapping, finances, staff control and actual practice.

## 17. Public finance as a delivery system

| Tool | Function | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Authorises and allocates public resources | Incremental inputs detached from outcomes |
| Treasury/PFMS architecture | Tracks sanction, release and expenditure | Data quality and process burden |
| Direct Benefit Transfer | Transfers benefit to identified recipient/account | Exclusion from identification, authentication or banking failure |
| Single Nodal Agency architecture | Tracks and rationalises scheme fund flow | Transition and coordination complexity |
| Just-in-time fund release | Reduces idle balances and improves cash management | Frontline delay if controls are poorly calibrated |
| Outcome budget/framework | Connects expenditure with outputs/outcomes | Indicator gaming and weak attribution |
| Procurement rules | Secure competition, value and integrity | Delay, collusion or lowest-cost quality failure |

### Finance-to-outcome chain

`authorisation -> release -> receipt -> procurement/transfer -> utilisation -> asset/service -> output -> outcome -> audit/evaluation`

> **Trap:** Expenditure is neither output nor outcome. A high utilisation rate can coexist with poor quality, exclusion or no durable impact.

## 18. Participatory governance

### Participation ladder

| Level | Citizen role |
|---|---|
| Information | Receives facts and decisions |
| Consultation | Gives views |
| Involvement | Participates during design/implementation |
| Collaboration | Shares deliberation or implementation |
| Empowerment | Holds delegated decision authority/control |

### Participation-quality test

`early enough + representative + informed + accessible + deliberative + linked to decision + feedback on acceptance/rejection`

| Mechanism | Best use |
|---|---|
| Gram Sabha/ward forum | Local planning, scrutiny and accountability |
| Public consultation | Rules, policy and projects |
| Social audit | Verification of implementation |
| Participatory budgeting | Citizen input into priorities/allocation |
| Resident/user committee | Service feedback and co-production |
| SHG/federation | Collective agency and livelihood/service interface |

> **Trap:** Attendance is not empowerment. Participation can be tokenistic when agenda, information, time and final authority remain closed.

## 19. Monitoring, evaluation and outcomes

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Input | Money, staff, time or material used |
| Activity | Action performed |
| Output | Immediate deliverable produced |
| Outcome | Change in access, behaviour, quality or condition |
| Impact | Longer-term change attributable partly or wholly to intervention |
| Monitoring | Continuous tracking of implementation |
| Evaluation | Periodic systematic assessment of relevance, design, implementation or results |
| Audit | Examination against financial, legal, compliance or performance criteria |

### Results chain example

`funds -> teachers recruited/trained -> classes delivered -> attendance/learning improves -> life opportunities expand`

### Indicator-quality test

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Validity | Does it measure the intended concept? |
| Reliability | Would measurement be consistent? |
| Disaggregation | Can inequality by sex, region, identity or disability be seen? |
| Verifiability | Can source data be independently checked? |
| Timeliness | Is it available when correction is possible? |
| Behavioural effect | Will it encourage gaming or neglect of unmeasured work? |

> **Core rule:** Monitoring asks whether implementation is on track; evaluation asks whether the intervention works, for whom, why and at what cost.

## 20. Sports governance as an application

| Layer | Governance question |
|---|---|
| International federation | Who makes sport-specific rules and recognises events? |
| National federation | How are teams, recognition and domestic administration managed? |
| Event organiser | Who controls entry, scheduling, commercial rights and venue rules? |
| Anti-doping system | Which testing, adjudication and appeal rules apply? |
| Athlete selection | Are criteria transparent, prospective and reviewable? |
| Dispute resolution | Is an internal appeal, tribunal, court or international forum available? |
| Integrity | How are conflicts, betting, manipulation, harassment and safeguarding handled? |

### Tournament question rule

Separate:

`governing body -> tournament organiser -> eligibility/qualification rule -> current champion/host/date`

The first three are institutional architecture; the last may be volatile current affairs.

> **Governance lesson:** Sports bodies may exercise monopoly-like rule-making power even when they are not conventional state departments; transparency, due process, athlete voice and effective remedies remain central.

## 21. High-frequency comparison grid

| Pair | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Transparency vs accountability | Visibility vs answerability plus correction/consequence |
| Grievance vs appeal | Complaint about failure vs challenge to a decision before higher forum |
| Audit vs evaluation | Compliance/performance examination vs assessment of design/results/causality |
| Output vs outcome | Immediate deliverable vs resulting change |
| Consultation vs participation | Seeking views vs broader involvement or shared authority |
| Decentralisation vs delegation | Redistribution across levels vs transfer by superior that may remain revocable |
| Deconcentration vs devolution | Administrative field dispersal vs authority to autonomous lower government |
| Digitisation vs transformation | Convert records vs redesign institutions and service journey |
| DBT vs subsidy reform | Transfer channel vs broader question of entitlement, pricing and targeting |
| Regulator vs ministry | Specialised arm's-length oversight vs political-executive policy responsibility |
| NGO vs pressure group | Organisational form/functions vs influence-oriented interest articulation |
| Citizen Charter vs RTS law | Administrative service commitment vs statutory enforceability where enacted |
| Social audit vs public hearing | Full verification-accountability process vs one stage/forum |
| Neutrality vs anonymity | Non-partisanship vs convention of ministerial responsibility shielding routine official visibility |

## 22. UPSC traps and safe corrections

| Trap | Safe correction |
|---|---|
| "Technology removes discretion." | It redistributes discretion into code, data, exceptions and system design. |
| "Transparency ensures accountability." | Add answerability, forum, remedy and consequence. |
| "Decentralisation always improves delivery." | Test 3Fs, capacity, capture and accountability. |
| "NGOs are either anti-state or substitutes for the state." | They can partner, innovate, mobilise and monitor; rights obligations remain public. |
| "A high expenditure rate proves success." | Separate expenditure, output, outcome and impact. |
| "Consultation means consent." | Consultation informs decision; it does not always transfer authority. |
| "Independent regulator is unaccountable." | Independence and structured accountability must coexist. |
| "Portal availability means service accessibility." | Test language, disability, connectivity, assisted access and remedy. |
| "Act enacted means fully operational." | Check commencement, rules, body, appointments and implementation. |
| "ARC recommendation describes current law." | Verify adoption and legal/administrative status separately. |
| "Civil-service reform equals training." | Include incentives, tenure, staffing, specialisation, process and accountability. |
| "Public-private partnership transfers state responsibility." | Contracting does not eliminate public accountability for public purpose. |

## 23. Reusable Mains answer skeletons

### A. "Why has a governance mechanism not achieved its potential?"

1. Define its intended function.
2. Show design weaknesses.
3. Add capacity and coordination constraints.
4. Explain exclusion and frontline incentives.
5. Identify accountability and remedy gaps.
6. Recommend redesign, safeguards and measurable outcomes.

### B. "Discuss the role of an institution/actor"

1. Constitutional/democratic relevance.
2. Distinct contributions.
3. Indian mechanism or example.
4. Limitations and capture/accountability risks.
5. Balanced reform preserving autonomy and public purpose.

### C. "Technology can transform governance. Critically examine."

1. Efficiency, integration, transparency and scale.
2. Back-end process reform rather than portalisation.
3. Digital divide, privacy, security and algorithmic exclusion.
4. Assisted access, open standards, audit, grievance and human review.
5. End with rights-preserving digital transformation.

### D. "Decentralisation/participation improves development."

1. Subsidiarity and local information.
2. Participation, ownership and accountability gains.
3. Weak 3Fs, capture, capacity and coordination.
4. Activity mapping, fiscal equalisation, staff, transparency and social audit.

### E. "Reform civil services/regulators."

1. State the democratic or market-governance function.
2. Diagnose incentive and institutional-design failures.
3. Balance autonomy with answerability.
4. Reform appointment, tenure, capability, disclosure, review and performance.

## 24. Five-minute active recall

1. Recite the five governance gaps.
2. Draw the policy cycle and results chain.
3. Explain why transparency is not accountability.
4. Distinguish output, outcome and impact.
5. Apply L-A-C-E-F-I to one portal or scheme.
6. State the 3F test and two local-governance risks.
7. Give three reasons Citizens' Charters fail.
8. Distinguish digitisation, digitalisation and transformation.
9. Give one contribution and one safeguard for NGOs, SHGs and regulators.
10. Explain why ARC recommendations, enacted law and operational reform are different.
11. State one efficiency-inclusion and one autonomy-accountability trade-off.
12. End one answer with a measurable reform rather than a slogan.

## 25. Optional Advanced enrichment

Use the Advanced files only after the Core chart is secure:

- institutional economics: principal-agent problems, transaction costs and credible commitment;
- behavioural public policy: bounded rationality, default design and administrative burden;
- co-production: citizens contribute information, labour, monitoring or service capability;
- collaborative governance: state, market and civil society jointly deliberate and implement;
- adaptive governance: pilots, feedback and reversible correction under uncertainty;
- regulatory theory: capture, responsive regulation and risk-based supervision;
- data governance: federated architecture, algorithmic accountability and purpose limitation;
- evaluation depth: counterfactual reasoning, attribution, external validity and unintended effects.

> **Firewall:** These concepts sharpen analysis but must never replace the Core requirements of legality, accessibility, capacity, accountability, federal fit, inclusion and measurable outcomes.
