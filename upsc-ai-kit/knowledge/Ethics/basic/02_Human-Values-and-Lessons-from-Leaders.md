## 1. Visual foundation

```text
SOURCES OF VALUES              ->   VALUE                ->   ADMINISTRATIVE TRANSLATION
Family (early conditioning)         Truth/Honesty              Accurate reporting, no fudging
Education/teachers                  Compassion                 Empathy in service delivery
Religion/culture                    Courage                    Whistleblowing, standing up to pressure
Personal role models/leaders        Self-discipline             Resisting temptation under discretion
Life experience/struggle            Duty (nishkama karma)        Devotion to task without craving credit
```

**Core proposition:** ⚠️ Values are not innate; they are transmitted through family, education,
religion/culture and lived example, then tested and reaffirmed (or corroded) by institutional
incentives — the same institutions-plus-values synthesis taught in `01`.

## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Values** | Enduring beliefs about what is desirable/worthwhile, guiding choice across situations (contrast with `03` attitude, which is object-specific). |
| ⚠️ **Value education** | Deliberate cultivation of values through family, schooling and institutional training (e.g., foundation-course modules, Mission Karmayogi behavioural competencies). |
| ⚠️ **Exemplar-based learning** | Learning ethics through the lives of leaders/reformers rather than abstract rules alone — the method GS-IV quotation-questions test directly. |
| ✅ **Nishkama karma** | Bhagavad Gita's ideal of dutiful action without attachment to its fruit — a recurring Mains "duty and detachment" theme (2025 GS-IV Q5(a) quotation on devotion to duty). |

## 3. Leaders, teachings and their administrative lesson

| Leader/thinker | Teaching (verbatim where tested) | Administrative lesson |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Mahatma Gandhi | "Seven Social Sins" — ARC Box 2.8 reproduces the list and sources it to Gandhi in *Young India*, 1925: politics without principles; wealth without work; leisure without conscience; knowledge without character; commerce without morality; science without humanity; worship without sacrifice. ⚠️ The original *Young India* note of 22 October 1925 reads "**pleasure** without conscience" (the ARC's "leisure" is a variant), and Gandhi introduced the list as sent to him by a correspondent rather than composed by him | Warns against decoupling means from ethical restraint — power/knowledge/wealth must stay tied to conscience and character. |
| ✅ Swami Vivekananda | "Learn everything that is good from others, but bring it in, and in your own way absorb it; do not become others" (2024 GS-IV Q3(a) — traceable to *Complete Works*, Vol. III, "The Common Bases of Hinduism", Lahore, 1897). "The strength of a society is not in its laws, but in the morality of its people" (2025 GS-IV Q3(c) — ⚠️ set by UPSC as Vivekananda's, but **not traceable** to the *Complete Works*; answer the idea, do not assert the provenance) | Selective, self-respecting learning from other systems; law is necessary but not sufficient — societal morality is the deeper safeguard. |
| ✅ Sardar Patel | "Faith is of no avail in the absence of strength. Faith and strength, both are essential to accomplish any great work" (2024 GS-IV Q3(b) — ⚠️ no primary speech/letter source traced; treat as UPSC's attribution) | Conviction (values) must be paired with capacity/resolve to achieve results — echoes the values-plus-institutions synthesis. |
| ✅ Thiruvalluvar | "Those who in trouble untroubled are, will trouble trouble itself" (2025 GS-IV Q3(a)). ⚠️ This is a modern paraphrase, not a named historic translation, of **Tirukkural 623** (Chapter 63, *Iṭukkaṇ Aḻiyāmai* — not being distressed by adversity); G.U. Pope renders it "Who griefs confront with meek, ungrieving heart, / From them griefs, put to grief, depart" | Equanimity under adversity — a civil servant's composure itself defuses a crisis. |
| ✅ William James | "The greatest discovery of my generation is that a human being can alter his life by altering his attitudes" (2025 GS-IV Q3(b)). ⚠️ Widely attributed to James but **not traceable** to his writings; popularised by Norman Vincent Peale (1952). A genuine James line on the same theme: "Believe that life is worth living, and your belief will help create the fact" (1895) | Attitude is malleable and self-authored — links directly to `03` (attitude change). |
| ✅ Mahavir | Ahimsa (non-violence), Satya, Asteya, Brahmacharya, Aparigraha (the five *Mahavratas*), with Anekantavada (many-sidedness of truth) and Syadvada (conditional predication) as the associated epistemological doctrines — tested in 2025 GS-IV Q4(b) | Anekantavada supports listening to multiple stakeholder viewpoints before deciding; Aparigraha cautions against accumulation/nepotism in office. |
| ✅ Kautilya | ARC Annexure-I(2) quotes the *Arthashastra*: "Just as it is not possible not to taste honey or poison placed on the surface of the tongue, even so it is not possible for one dealing with the money of the king not to taste the money in however small a quantity"; and "It is possible to know even the path of birds flying in the sky, but not the ways of officers moving with their intentions concealed" (Kangle's numbering: 2.9.32 and 2.9.34) | One of the earliest articulations that opportunity/discretion — not only character — breeds corruption; see `06` for full treatment. |

## 4. Indian applications and examples

- ⚠️ A district officer citing Vivekananda's "learn but do not become others" while adapting an
  e-governance model (from Estonia or Singapore) to local conditions rather than copying it wholesale.
- ⚠️ A civil servant applying Thiruvalluvar's equanimity teaching while managing a disaster response
  despite personal grief (the exact tension tested in the 2025 GS-IV Q7 case study on DC "Vijay").
- ⚠️ Gandhi's "commerce without morality" warning applied to a procurement officer resisting
  under-invoicing pressure from a favoured contractor.

## 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ ARC **Box 2.8** reproduces Gandhi's Seven Social Sins and sources them to *Young India*, 1925,
  in this order: politics without principles; wealth without work; **leisure** without conscience;
  knowledge without character; commerce without morality; science without humanity; worship
  without sacrifice. ⚠️ The *Young India* note of **22 October 1925** (Collected Works, Vol. XXVIII)
  reads "**pleasure** without conscience", and Gandhi introduced the list as one his correspondent
  wanted readers to know — cite it as a list Gandhi published and endorsed, not one he is documented
  to have composed.
- ✅ Mahavir's five great vows (*Mahavratas*): Ahimsa, Satya, Asteya, Brahmacharya, Aparigraha;
  Anekantavada and Syadvada are the associated Jain doctrines of the many-sidedness of truth and
  conditional predication. ⚠️ Jain tradition contrasts Parshvanatha's four vows with Mahavir's
  five — brahmacharya is the vow Mahavir's tradition *separately enumerated*, having earlier been
  understood within aparigraha.
- ✅ Kautilya's *Arthashastra* is the classical Indian source the ARC quotes (Annexure-I(2)) for the
  structural, opportunity-driven — rather than purely moral — origin of official corruption.
- ⚠️ Value transmission channels commonly tested: family, school/teachers, religion, peer group,
  media, and direct exemplar/role-model learning.

## 6. UPSC traps

- ❌ Quotation questions only test memorisation of the quote. -> They test your ability to translate
  the quote into a governance/administrative lesson relevant to "the present context" (the standard
  phrasing in 2024-2025 papers).
- ❌ Values are fixed and unchangeable once formed. -> William James's own quoted teaching (2025
  GS-IV Q3(b)) explicitly holds that attitudes/values can be altered by conscious effort.
- ❌ Anekantavada means "no view is ever correct." -> It means truth is many-sided and no single
  perspective is complete — administratively, it supports multi-stakeholder consultation, not moral
  relativism or indecision.
- ❌ Every quotation UPSC prints is an authenticated saying of the named thinker. -> The paper only
  proves UPSC's attribution. The Vivekananda "learn everything that is good" line (2024) is
  traceable to the *Complete Works*; the Sardar Patel line (2024), the William James line (2025)
  and the Vivekananda "strength of a society" line (2025) are **not** traceable to primary
  sources. Answer the idea in the "present context" — never invent a citation for the quotation.

## 7. PYQ application

- ✅ 2024 GS-IV Q3(a)-(c): three verbatim quotations (Vivekananda, Sardar Patel, Kant) each required
  "what does it convey to you in the present context" — the standard exam format for this topic.
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q3(a)-(c): three verbatim quotations (Thiruvalluvar, William James, Vivekananda),
  same format.
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q4(b): "major teachings of Mahavir" and "their relevance in the contemporary world."
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q5(a): quotation on devotion to duty ("one who is devoted to one's duty attains
  highest perfection in life") analysed via "sense of responsibility and personal fulfilment."

## 8. Mains angles

- ⚠️ Always structure a quotation answer as: (1) restate the core idea in one line, (2) explain the
  thinker's context briefly, (3) translate into a specific administrative/governance lesson, (4) give
  one concrete Indian example, (5) close with a one-line normative takeaway.
- ⚠️ Do not spend words on biography; examiners reward application, not history.

> **Answer thesis:** A civil servant's values are inherited from family, education and culture, but
> made administratively usable only when consciously translated — through exemplars like Gandhi,
> Vivekananda, Kautilya and Mahavir — into specific standards of conduct under discretion and
> pressure.

## 9. Probable questions

- ⚠️ **Prelims:** Match the thinker to the correct teaching (Gandhi's seven sins, Mahavir's vows,
  Kautilya's warning on discretion).
- ⚠️ **Mains (10 marks):** "Learn everything that is good from others... do not become others."
  Explain this in the present context of civil service reform borrowing from global best practices.
- ⚠️ **Mains (15 marks):** How do Mahavir's Anekantavada and Aparigraha remain relevant to a modern
  civil servant handling multi-stakeholder disputes and public resources?
