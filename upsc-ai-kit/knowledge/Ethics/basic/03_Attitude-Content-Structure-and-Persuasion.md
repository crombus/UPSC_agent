## 1. Visual foundation

```text
ATTITUDE = evaluative disposition toward an object/person/idea

        COGNITIVE           AFFECTIVE            BEHAVIOURAL
      (belief/thought)   -> (feeling/emotion) -> (action tendency)
            |                    |                     |
            +------ the "ABC" (Rosenberg-Hovland) structure of attitude -----+
                                    |
                                    v
                    PERSUASION (source, message, audience; channel added later)
                                    |
                                    v
                    ATTITUDE CHANGE  <-->  COGNITIVE DISSONANCE
              (William James: "alter life by altering attitudes")
```

**Core proposition:** ✅ Attitude has three components — cognitive, affective, behavioural (the ABC
model) — and is distinct from **value** (broad, enduring, cross-situational) and from **belief**
(a single cognitive claim); attitude is the evaluative stance toward a *specific* object.

## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Attitude** | A relatively stable evaluative disposition (positive/negative) toward a specific object, person, event or idea, comprising cognitive, affective and behavioural components. |
| ✅ **Cognitive component** | Beliefs/knowledge/information the person holds about the attitude object. |
| ✅ **Affective component** | The emotional charge (like/dislike, comfort/discomfort) attached to the object. |
| ✅ **Behavioural (conative) component** | The predisposition/intention to act in a particular way toward the object. |
| ✅ **Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger, 1957) | Psychological discomfort from holding two inconsistent cognitions/attitudes, or from acting against one's own attitude — resolved by changing the attitude, the behaviour, or rationalising the gap. |
| ⚠️ **Attitude vs value vs belief vs aptitude** | Value = general, enduring, cross-situational standard (e.g., honesty); attitude = evaluative stance toward one specific object (e.g., "I dislike this particular policy"); belief = a single factual/causal claim a person holds true; aptitude = the trainable capacity to perform a role or task well (see `04`). |

## 3. How attitudes form and change (mechanism)

1. Attitudes form through **direct experience, social learning (family/peers/media), and
   reinforcement** — repeated positive/negative outcomes attach evaluative charge to an object.
2. **Persuasion** changes attitude via the classical Hovland-Yale ("Yale attitude change") levers:
   the *communicator's* credibility and expertise, the *message's* content/framing, and the
   *audience's* predispositions — with the *channel/medium* added by later communication research.
3. **Cognitive dissonance** is a powerful internal driver of attitude change: when behaviour and
   attitude conflict (e.g., a civil servant who values transparency is asked to withhold routine
   information), the discomfort pushes either attitude change, behaviour change, or self-justifying
   rationalisation — the last being an ethical risk in administration.
4. Attitudes influence but do **not mechanically determine** behaviour — situational constraints,
   social pressure and institutional incentives can produce behaviour inconsistent with a held
   attitude (the attitude-behaviour gap).
5. ✅ William James's teaching (2025 GS-IV Q3(b), verbatim: "a human being can alter his life by
   altering his attitudes") captures the *malleability* of attitude — it is not fixed temperament
   but a chosen, revisable stance.

## 4. Indian administrative applications and examples

- ⚠️ A field officer's negative attitude toward a beneficiary community (formed through biased
  social conditioning) can distort service delivery even where rules are neutral — training must
  target the affective, not only the cognitive, component.
- ⚠️ Persuasion principles explain why *credible source* + *clear message* + *appropriate channel*
  (e.g., a respected local leader delivering a vaccination message via a trusted local-language
  channel) shifts public attitude more than a purely top-down circular.
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q1(a): social media's "ethical dilemmas" in the "present digital age" is fundamentally
  an attitude/persuasion-at-scale problem — algorithmic amplification changes which messages reach
  which audiences, reshaping public attitudes outside institutional control.

## 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ The ABC model of attitude (cognitive, affective, behavioural) is associated with Rosenberg and
  Hovland.
- ✅ Cognitive dissonance theory was proposed by Leon Festinger (1957).
- ✅ The Hovland-Yale ("Yale attitude change") approach identifies the **communicator/source**, the
  **message** and the **audience/recipient** as the core persuasion variables; the *channel/medium*
  is a standard addition from wider communication research (Lasswell's formula), not part of the
  original Yale triad.
- ⚠️ Attitude is specific-object-directed; value is general and cross-situational; **aptitude** is
  neither — it is the capacity/disposition to perform a role well (see `04`). Keeping the three
  apart is a frequently tested distinction.

## 6. UPSC traps

- ❌ Attitude and value mean the same thing. -> Value is a broad, enduring standard; attitude is a
  specific evaluative stance toward a particular object — they are related but distinct levels.
- ❌ A positive attitude guarantees consistent ethical behaviour. -> Situational pressures and
  institutional incentives can produce an attitude-behaviour gap; attitude is necessary but not
  sufficient for ethical conduct.
- ❌ Persuasion is inherently unethical manipulation. -> Persuasion is a neutral influence process;
  its ethical status depends on the truthfulness of the message and the respect for audience
  autonomy, not on the act of persuading itself.

## 8. Mains angles

- ⚠️ Structure attitude-change answers as: define attitude (ABC) -> identify which component is
  weakest/targeted -> apply a persuasion lever (credible source, message framing, channel choice) ->
  give an Indian governance example -> note the attitude-behaviour gap as a limitation.
- ⚠️ For social-media/technology questions, explicitly note that persuasion at algorithmic scale
  changes the *audience* variable of the Hovland-Yale model — reach and targeting are now automated.

> **Answer thesis:** Attitude is a specific, three-component evaluative stance that is changeable
> through targeted persuasion, but its translation into consistent ethical behaviour requires
> institutional reinforcement, not persuasion alone.

## 10. Selectable named evidence/application units

> Pick 1-2 units per answer; each follows claim -> named evidence/example -> significance -> limitation.

1. *Claim:* Persuasion by a credible, trusted messenger shifts attitude faster than top-down
   circulars. *Evidence:* Pulse Polio and COVID-19 vaccination communication campaigns using local
   respected figures/ASHA workers in vernacular language. *Significance:* demonstrates the
   Hovland-Yale "source credibility" variable operating at national scale. *Limitation:* credibility
   gains can be undone quickly by a single visible institutional failure (adverse event, corruption
   story), showing attitude change is fragile, not permanent.
2. *Claim:* Algorithmic/social-media persuasion changes the "audience" variable at unprecedented
   scale. *Evidence:* ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q1(a)'s framing of social media's "ethical dilemmas" in the
   "present digital age." *Significance:* shows attitude/persuasion theory must now account for
   automated targeting, not just message content. *Limitation:* regulatory response (content
   moderation, algorithmic transparency mandates) is still evolving and unevenly enforced.
3. *Claim:* Cognitive dissonance can produce ethical rationalisation risk in office. *Evidence:*
   an official who values transparency but is directed to withhold a routine file under an RTI
   Act, 2005 exemption may resolve the discomfort by convincing themselves the exemption is broader
   than it is. *Significance:* shows dissonance-resolution can drift toward self-justifying
   misconduct if not checked. *Limitation:* the same dissonance can equally resolve toward
   principled objection/escalation (see `10`) — the direction is not determined by the theory alone.
4. *Claim:* Attitude change requires targeting the affective, not only the cognitive, component.
   *Evidence:* foundation-course and Mission Karmayogi behavioural-competency modules (see `04`)
   designed around simulated citizen interaction, not lecture-only instruction. *Significance:*
   reflects deliberate design against the ABC model's affective gap. *Limitation:* training effects
   on long-held attitudes (e.g., caste or gender bias) are difficult to measure and can fade without
   reinforcement.
5. *Claim:* The attitude-behaviour gap explains why declared values do not guarantee conduct.
   *Evidence:* an officer expressing pro-transparency attitudes in training who nonetheless resists
   RTI disclosure under peer/superior pressure in practice. *Significance:* shows institutional
   incentive design (not attitude alone) must close the gap. *Limitation:* attributing every gap to
   "situational pressure" can become an excuse — genuine attitude change must still be assessed.
6. *Claim:* Attitude is malleable across a career, not fixed at entry. *Evidence:* William James's
   teaching (2025 GS-IV Q3(b)) applied to induction/refresher training design for long-serving
   officials showing signs of cynicism or burnout. *Significance:* justifies periodic, not only
   entry-level, values training. *Limitation:* repeated training without accompanying institutional
   reform (workload, incentive structures) risks becoming a box-ticking exercise.

## 11. Moral attitude vs political attitude (official syllabus clause)

> ✅ The official GS-IV syllabus clause reads: "Attitude: content, structure, function; its
> influence and relation with thought and behaviour; **moral and political attitudes**; social
> influence and persuasion." Sections 1-10 above cover the general ABC structure; this section
> closes the moral-vs-political distinction the clause names explicitly.

| Dimension | ⚠️ Moral attitude | ⚠️ Political attitude |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | An evaluative stance toward what is right/wrong, just/unjust, honest/dishonest — grounded in ethical reasoning (duty, consequence, virtue — see `08`) and applied to conduct generally. | An evaluative stance toward a specific party, ideology, policy programme or contest for power — grounded in political preference/affiliation. |
| **Object** | Conduct, character, and the rightness of an act or rule (e.g., "bribery is wrong regardless of who benefits"). | A particular political actor, platform or outcome (e.g., preferring one party's manifesto or leader over another's). |
| **Scope** | General and portable across contexts — a moral judgment about honesty applies whether the actor is from one's own party or the opposition. | Context-bound to the contest itself — a political attitude is, by design, partisan and does not claim universal applicability. |
| **Source** | Ethical reasoning, socialisation into values (family, education, religion/philosophy — see `02`), and professional codes (see `09`, `16`). | Political socialisation, ideological identification, group/party loyalty, and electoral competition. |
| **Civil-servant obligation** | Must be actively retained and exercised — a civil servant is expected to judge right from wrong (corruption, discrimination, procedural unfairness) and act on that judgment regardless of who is affected. | Must be **bracketed/suspended in official conduct** — Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, Rule 5 restricts a government servant's participation in politics and demonstrations, and Rule 7 restricts canvassing in elections, operationalising the constitutional expectation (Article 309-linked service rules) of political neutrality/non-partisanship. |

**Core proposition:** ⚠️ A civil servant is required to *retain* a moral attitude (an active,
reasoned sense of right and wrong that informs every decision) while *bracketing* a personal
political attitude in official conduct — because moral judgment is what impartial, lawful
administration requires, whereas an official who lets *political* preference (for a party, leader
or programme) visibly steer a decision compromises the non-partisanship that lets the same officer
serve any elected government of the day (see `04`'s "non-partisanship" as a foundational value, and
`09`'s Nolan "objectivity"/"selflessness" principles).

### Why the two cannot be treated alike

1. **Function:** a moral attitude is the very instrument of ethical decision-making in office (e.g.,
   refusing an improper instruction because it is wrong); a political attitude, if allowed to
   operate inside official decisions, substitutes partisan preference for the public interest the
   office exists to serve.
2. **Institutional design:** the political-neutrality/non-partisanship obligation (Conduct Rules;
   the "permanent executive" convention under a rotating political executive) exists precisely so
   that a civil servant's *political* attitude never determines who receives a benefit, a licence or
   a posting — while the *moral* attitude (impartiality, honesty, rule-of-law fidelity) is exactly
   what makes that neutrality trustworthy rather than merely mechanical compliance.
3. **Behavioural translation:** a moral attitude should visibly translate into behaviour (reporting
   corruption, applying rules impartially); a political attitude translating into official
   behaviour (favouring a party worker's file, canvassing on duty) is itself the misconduct the
   Conduct Rules exist to prevent.

### Limits — what non-partisanship does not require

- ⚠️ **Policy awareness is not partisanship.** A civil servant is expected to understand the
  political executive's policy priorities, brief ministers candidly (including with unwelcome
  advice — the "constructive dissent" duty, see `09`), and implement lawfully enacted policy
  faithfully — none of this is "political attitude" in the sense the neutrality obligation
  restricts; it becomes partisan only when the officer's *personal* preference for a party/leader,
  rather than the law and the record, visibly drives a discretionary decision.
- ⚠️ **Private political belief is not itself misconduct.** The Conduct Rules restrict *public*
  political activity, canvassing and organised political association by a serving official, not
  the mere private holding of a political opinion or the exercise of one's vote — the obligation is
  behavioural non-partisanship in office, not enforced political blankness of mind.
- ⚠️ **Moral objection to a lawful policy is not "political attitude" either.** An officer who
  raises a reasoned ethical/legal objection to an instruction on file (Sec. 8-level file-noting
  practice, see `22`) is exercising moral judgment about propriety/legality, not political
  preference — provided the objection is framed in terms of law, rules and public interest rather
  than partisan alignment.

### Evidence units

1. *Claim:* Non-partisanship is a codified conduct obligation, not an informal norm. *Evidence:*
   Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, Rule 5 (participation in politics/demonstrations)
   and Rule 7 (election canvassing restrictions). *Significance:* gives a named, citable legal
   anchor for "bracketing political attitude" answers. *Limitation:* the Rules restrict *conduct*,
   not private belief — over-claiming a duty of political "blankness" of mind is itself a trap.
2. *Claim:* Moral judgment must survive political change even as political attitude is bracketed.
   *Evidence:* the "permanent executive" convention — the same civil servant serves successive,
   differently aligned elected governments. *Significance:* shows why moral attitude (impartial,
   rule-bound judgment) is the constant, while political attitude is deliberately made irrelevant to
   official duty. *Limitation:* the line blurs in practice when policy implementation itself is
   contested as partisan — the test remains whether the officer's *personal* party preference, not
   the lawfully enacted policy, is driving the decision.

## 12. Executable directive decoding and answer architecture

| Directive word | What is tested | Structural move |
|---|---|---|
| **Examine** | Multi-dimensional analytical treatment | ABC model -> which component is the point of intervention -> persuasion lever applied -> Indian example -> limitation |
| **Discuss** | Balanced argument with a position | State the phenomenon -> supporting mechanism -> counter-consideration -> reasoned position |
| **Suggest** | A specific, actionable proposal | Diagnose the attitude problem precisely -> name the lever (source/message/channel) -> propose one concrete measure -> note its limitation |

**10-mark architecture (~150 words):** define attitude via the ABC model -> identify which
component is engaged by the question's scenario -> apply a persuasion/attitude-change lever -> give
one Indian governance example (from Section 9) -> note the attitude-behaviour gap as a limitation ->
one-line conclusion.

**Counterpoint and reasoned verdict (template):** "Persuasion/attitude-change through [named lever]
addresses [X], but the attitude-behaviour gap means [Y] institutional safeguard is also required for
the shift to translate into consistent ethical conduct."
