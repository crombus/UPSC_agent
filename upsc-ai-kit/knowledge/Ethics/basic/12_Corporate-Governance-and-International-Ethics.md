## 1. Visual foundation

   ethics as external PR/compliance            ethics as how business is actually conducted,
   layer bolted onto profit-seeking             embedded in every decision, not an add-on
                    |
                    v
        CORPORATE GOVERNANCE = structures/processes ensuring
        a company is directed and controlled ethically and
        accountably (board oversight, audit, disclosure)
                    |
                    v
        CSR (Companies Act 2013, s.135) = a STATUTORY,
        ring-fenced expenditure obligation on qualifying companies —
        DISTINCT FROM, not identical to, corporate ethics

**Core proposition:** ✅ ARC 1.17 — governance ethics must extend "across the board," including a
deliberate "paradigm shift from the pejorative 'business ethics' to 'ethics in business'" — ethics
should describe *how* business is done throughout, not a separate reputational function.

## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Ethics in business vs "business ethics"** (ARC 1.17) | ARC explicitly favours "ethics in business" — ethical conduct integrated into ordinary commercial decision-making — over "business ethics" as a bolt-on compliance department. |
| ⚠️ **Corporate ethics vs CSR** | Corporate ethics concerns the *means* by which a business operates (honest accounting, fair labour practices, non-bribery, environmental compliance); CSR under Section 135 is a statutory expenditure/transfer regime for qualifying companies, with discretion confined to lawful policy and project choices. It does not substitute for ethical core operations. |
| ✅ **Serious Fraud Office model** (ARC Box 3.2; ARC 3.7.9 citing the Naresh Chandra Committee, 2002) | A dedicated, multi-disciplinary investigative body (law, accountancy, investigation expertise) for complex corporate/financial fraud, modelled on the UK's Serious Fraud Office (constituted under the Criminal Justice Act 1987; operational from April 1988). |
| ✅ **UNCAC (UN Convention against Corruption, 2003)** | The principal global anti-corruption treaty, adopted by UNGA resolution 58/4 on 31 October 2003 and in force from 14 December 2005. It requires States Parties to take preventive, criminalisation, cooperation and asset-recovery measures; Articles 6 and 8 concern preventive bodies and public-official codes. **India signed on 9 December 2005 and ratified on 9 May 2011.** |

## 3. Mechanism: how corporate and international ethics interlock with public administration

1. **Regulatory design** (Companies Act, SEBI norms, competition law) sets external constraints
   within which corporate ethics must operate — but law alone cannot guarantee ethical conduct
   (mirroring the public-sector law-insufficiency point in `10`).
2. ✅ ARC Box 3.5 (printed alongside para 3.6.1) records that after the collapse of Enron and
   WorldCom the US Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, "granting sweeping protection to
   whistleblowers in publicly traded companies", with retaliation punishable by up to 10 years'
   imprisonment — ⚠️ precisely, that ten-year penalty attaches to 18 U.S.C. §1513(e), inserted by
   SOX s.1107; SOX s.806 (18 U.S.C. §1514A) is the separate *civil* whistleblower remedy. This
   shows that corporate ethics failures trigger the same whistleblower-protection logic as
   public-sector corruption (see `19`).
3. ✅ ARC 3.7.9 records that the Naresh Chandra Committee recommended in 2002 a **Corporate Serious
   Fraud Office (CSFO)** in the then Department of Company Affairs — a multi-disciplinary team that
   "not only uncovers the fraud, but is able to direct and supervise prosecutions", with a task
   force for each case and oversight by a committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary. ⚠️ The
   committee's formal name was the **High Level Committee on Corporate Audit and Governance**
   (appointed 21 August 2002); ARC 3.7.9 refers to it as the committee on "Corporate Audit and
   Finance".
4. **International instruments** (UNCAC, OECD-ADB Anti-Corruption Action Plan, US Foreign Corrupt
   Practices Act) extend anti-corruption norms across borders, recognising that multinational
   business and cross-border bribery require coordinated, not merely national, response.
5. ⚠️ 2024 GS-IV Q7 (tech company, AI/data-centre GHG emissions) is primarily owned by Topics
   `13` and `22`; Topic `12` supplies a corporate-governance cross-link on stakeholder/shareholder
   duties, board accountability, core-business ethics and greenwashing.

## 4. Indian applications and examples

- ✅ Companies Act, 2013, Section 135 mandates CSR spending of at least 2% of average net profits of
  the three immediately preceding financial years for qualifying companies — ⚠️ this is a
  *statutory CSR mandate*, distinct from, and not a substitute for, the company's underlying ethical
  conduct in its core business (labour, environment, anti-bribery practices).
- ✅ ARC 1.14: the ADB-OECD Anti-Corruption Action Plan for Asia and the Pacific, "signed by the
  Government of India" (India endorsed it on 30 November 2001), commits to inter-regional
  cooperation on corruption prevention — relevant to any question on India's international
  anti-corruption commitments. ⚠️ Do not confuse it with the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, 1997, to
  which India is **not** a party.
- ⚠️ A data-centre operator (as in 2024 GS-IV Q7) facing rising GHG emissions from AI-driven
  compute demand must balance shareholder-value obligations against environmental/ethical duties —
  a direct test of corporate-ethics-meets-environmental-ethics reasoning (see `13`).

## 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ UNCAC was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 31 October 2003 (resolution 58/4), opened for
  signature at Merida in December 2003 and entered into force on 14 December 2005. **India signed
  on 9 December 2005 and ratified on 9 May 2011.**
- ✅ The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (US, 2002) followed the Enron and WorldCom collapses; its s.806 created
  a civil whistleblower remedy (18 U.S.C. §1514A) and its s.1107 created the criminal retaliation
  offence (18 U.S.C. §1513(e)) carrying up to ten years' imprisonment.
- ✅ Companies Act, 2013, Section 135 applies to a company that, in the immediately preceding
  financial year, had net worth of ₹500 crore or more, **or** turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more,
  **or** net profit of ₹5 crore or more; the spend is at least 2% of average net profits of the
  three immediately preceding financial years, on Schedule VII activities.
- ✅ The Naresh Chandra Committee (High Level Committee on Corporate Audit and Governance, 2002)
  recommended a Corporate Serious Fraud Office in India; the **Serious Fraud Investigation Office
  (SFIO)** now exists under Section 211 of the Companies Act, 2013 (notified by S.O. 2005(E) of
  21 July 2015), with power of arrest under Section 212(8).

## 6. UPSC traps

- ❌ CSR spending is the same as corporate ethics. -> CSR is a specific statutory/discretionary
  spend obligation; corporate ethics concerns the integrity of the company's core operations
  (accounting, labour, environment, anti-bribery) — a company can meet CSR requirements while still
  behaving unethically in its main business.
- ❌ "Business ethics" and "ethics in business" mean the same thing to the ARC. -> ARC 1.17
  explicitly prefers "ethics in business" precisely to reject the narrower, bolt-on connotation of
  "business ethics."
- ❌ India has no international anti-corruption commitments. -> ARC 1.14-1.15 cites UNCAC, the
  ADB-OECD Anti-Corruption Action Plan, and World Bank anti-corruption funding conditions as
  commitments India has engaged with.

### Business ethics vs corporate ethics vs CSR — three distinct layers

| Layer | What it covers | Governing question |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **"Ethics in business"** (ARC 1.17's preferred term) | How *every* ordinary commercial decision is made — accounting, labour treatment, procurement, pricing, environmental compliance | "Is this specific transaction/decision honest and lawful?" |
| ⚠️ **Corporate governance** | The structures ensuring the company is *directed and controlled* accountably — board composition, audit committee independence, related-party-transaction disclosure | "Is the company's internal decision-making structure itself trustworthy and accountable?" |
| ✅ **CSR (Companies Act, 2013, s.135)** | A *statutory, ring-fenced spend* obligation on qualifying companies, directed at Schedule VII social/environmental activities | "Has the company met its mandated minimum social spend?" |

- ⚠️ **The core doctrinal point (repeatedly tested):** a company can satisfy CSR to the last rupee
  while its *core business* remains unethical (mis-selling, labour exploitation, environmental
  non-compliance in its main operations) — CSR spend is not, and was never designed to be, a proxy
  for core-business ethics.

### Section 135 architecture, with threshold and status caution

- ✅ **Applicability threshold** (any one of three, in the immediately preceding financial year): net
  worth ≥ ₹500 crore, **or** turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore, **or** net profit ≥ ₹5 crore.
- ✅ **Spend obligation:** at least 2% of average net profit of the three immediately preceding
  financial years, on Schedule VII activities, through a board-approved CSR policy.
- ✅ **Unspent-fund architecture:** Section 135(5)-(6), inserted through the Companies (Amendment)
  Act, 2019 and brought into force from 22 January 2021, creates the transfer regime. Amount not
  linked to an ongoing project must go to a specified Schedule VII fund within six months of the
  financial year-end. Ongoing-project amount goes to an **Unspent CSR Account** within 30 days,
  must be spent within three financial years, and any residual amount must then be transferred to
  the specified fund within 30 days. The 2021 CSR Rules define and operationalise "ongoing project."
- ✅ **Impact assessment (mandate introduced by the Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021;
  cost cap revised by the 2022 amendment):** the 2021 amendment first introduced the requirement
  that companies with average CSR obligation of ₹10 crore or more in the three preceding financial
  years must conduct an independent impact assessment of their CSR projects (with a project outlay
  of ₹1 crore or more, completed at least one year before the assessment) through an independent
  agency; the **2022 amendment** did not create the impact-assessment mandate itself but revised
  the cap on the assessment's own permissible cost to 2% of total CSR expenditure for the year or
  ₹50 lakh, whichever is **higher**. ⚠️ Do not attribute the impact-assessment requirement itself to
  the 2022 amendment — that amendment's contribution was the cost-cap revision, not the mandate's
  introduction.
- ⚠️ **Threshold/status caution:** CSR rules have been amended multiple times (2021, 2022) on
  implementing-agency eligibility, the CSR-2 reporting form, and unspent-fund mechanics — verify the
  current text of the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014 (as amended) and any MCA circular before
  citing a specific numeric threshold in a live/administrative context, since rule-level detail is
  more amendment-prone than the Section 135 statutory thresholds themselves.
- ✅ **Dated 2026 application:** MCA notifications G.S.R. 415(E) and 416(E) of 27 May 2026 added
  subscription to eligible zero-coupon zero-principal instruments on the Social Stock Exchange to
  Schedule VII and created a capped route under Rule 4A. This is an additional CSR route, not a
  change to Section 135's applicability thresholds or 2% base obligation.

### Stakeholder responsibility and the greenwashing/box-ticking limit

- ⚠️ **Stakeholder theory (contrast with pure shareholder-primacy):** corporate governance ethics
  increasingly requires accounting for employees, consumers, local communities and the environment
  as legitimate stakeholders whose interests constrain profit-maximisation, not merely shareholders
  to whom fiduciary duty is owed narrowly.
- ⚠️ **Greenwashing/box-ticking risk:** a company can technically satisfy CSR's 2%-spend requirement
  through low-impact, easily-measured activities (a one-off donation, a branded plantation drive)
  while its core operations continue high-emission or exploitative practices — this is precisely why
  the Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021's impact-assessment requirement exists, and why
  "ethics in business" (Section 2/10 above) must be assessed *separately* from CSR compliance.
- ⚠️ **GS-IV-safe verdict:** CSR is a valuable, statutorily enforceable floor for social spend, but a
  "critically examine CSR" question should explicitly flag its greenwashing/box-ticking vulnerability
  and argue that CSR compliance must be paired with, not substituted for, core-business ethics
  (accounting integrity, labour standards, environmental compliance) and governance-structure
  integrity (independent boards, related-party-transaction scrutiny).

## 11. War ethics and international aid ethics (historical demand)

> ⚠️ 2022 GS-IV Q5(a) (historical demand, routed here): illustrate the ethical issues in the
> Russia-Ukraine war. ⚠️ 2023 GS-IV Q1(b) (historical demand, routed here): comment on ethics in
> contemporary international aid. The Russia-Ukraine conflict began with Russia's full-scale
> invasion in **February 2022**; any present conflict, ceasefire, territorial, casualty or
> negotiation status must be independently re-verified immediately before use. The ethical
> framework below does not depend on a time-sensitive battlefield claim.

### War ethics framework (Just War tradition, applied neutrally)

| Principle | Meaning | Application to an active conflict |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Sovereignty** | A state's territorial integrity and political independence are protected under the UN Charter (Article 2(4)'s prohibition on the use of force against territorial integrity); a war of aggression against a sovereign state is the paradigm violation. | The core *jus ad bellum* (justice of resorting to war) question in any inter-state conflict — was force used in self-defence/UN-authorised action, or in violation of another state's sovereignty. |
| ⚠️ **Legitimate (competent) authority** | *Jus ad bellum.* Force may be resorted to only by a public authority entitled to act for the political community, not by a faction, private army or self-appointed champion. In the modern legal register this is the UN Charter's allocation: Article 51 self-defence by a State against armed attack, or Security Council authorisation. | Separates a State's defensive war from a rebellion, a militia campaign or a private punitive expedition, however sincere the actors' motives. The single criterion most often omitted by candidates, and the one that decides the 2026 Q3(c) quotation (Sec. 11A). |
| ⚠️ **Right intention** | *Jus ad bellum.* The war must be actually waged for the just cause avowed — restoring a just peace — and not for annexation, plunder, prestige, domestic political consolidation or revenge. Intention is assessed by conduct (war aims, terms offered, treatment of the defeated), not by declaration. | Right intention is a **necessary but not sufficient** condition: a sincerely intended war that lacks just cause, authority or last resort remains unjust. This is the exact analytical move a "just intent" quotation demands. |
| ⚠️ **Last resort** | *Jus ad bellum.* Force is permissible only when peaceful alternatives — negotiation, mediation, arbitration, sanctions, third-party good offices — have been exhausted or are manifestly futile. | Converts the ethical test from a state of mind into a **checkable record**: what was offered, what was refused, what was concealed. Concealing an available settlement (as Worcester does in *Henry IV*) destroys the last-resort claim irrespective of intention. |
| ⚠️ **Reasonable prospect of success and proportionality of ends** | *Jus ad bellum.* The anticipated good of the war must be proportionate to its total expected destruction, and the resort to force must not be futile. | Blocks the "heroic but hopeless" justification that treats willingness to die as itself a moral warrant — precisely Hotspur's register. |
| ⚠️ **Civilian protection (distinction)** | International humanitarian law (the Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols) requires distinguishing combatants from civilians and civilian infrastructure, and prohibits deliberately targeting the latter. | The most frequently invoked *jus in bello* (justice in the conduct of war) principle — strikes on civilian energy grids, hospitals or residential areas raise direct IHL concerns regardless of which side commits them. ⚠️ **Treaty-status caution:** India is a party to the four Geneva Conventions of 1949 but is **not** a party to Additional Protocols I and II of 1977; write "Geneva Conventions and customary IHL" rather than asserting Indian treaty obligation under AP I. |
| ⚠️ **Proportionality** | Even a lawful military objective must not cause civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete military advantage anticipated. | Used to assess whether a specific strike, even against a legitimate military target, was ethically/legally defensible given its collateral civilian cost. |
| ⚠️ **Humanitarian neutrality** | Humanitarian aid organisations (ICRC and others) operate on the principles of neutrality, impartiality and independence, treating all affected civilians without regard to which side they support. | Neutrality is what allows aid access to both sides of a conflict — politicising aid delivery (favouring one side's civilian population) undermines this access-enabling principle. |
| ⚠️ **Doctrine of double effect** | A scholastic test (associated with Aquinas) for acts with both a good and a foreseen bad effect. Four conditions: (i) the act itself is not intrinsically wrong; (ii) the bad effect is **foreseen but not intended**, as neither the end nor the means; (iii) the good effect is not *produced by* the bad effect; (iv) there is proportionate reason for permitting the bad effect. | The bridge between "intention" talk and "consequence" talk. It explains why incidental civilian death in a proportionate strike on a military objective is treated differently from terror bombing — and why it **cannot** launder a case where the harm is the very mechanism of the intended good. ⚠️ Double effect excuses *side-effects*, never *means*. |

- ⚠️ **GS-IV-safe answer posture:** name the specific principle(s) engaged by the scenario in the
  question (sovereignty breach, a specific IHL/proportionality concern, or a neutrality question)
  rather than issuing a general condemnation of "war is bad" — the exam rewards precise application
  of a named ethical/legal framework, and a balanced, evidence-anchored treatment over a one-sided
  political stance.

### 11A. Right intention, legitimate authority, last resort and double effect

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q3(c) (10 marks, ~150 words).** The 2026 paper sets the
> line "the arms are fair when the intent of bearing them is just" for ethical examination. **This
> sub-section is the owner.** Read it with Sec. 11B (the rights floor that intention cannot
> displace), Sec. 11C (interest versus ethics) and Sec. 11D (the ethics of *supplying* arms, which
> is a different question from the ethics of *using* them). 📰 No current affairs are required for
> this demand; it is decidable on static doctrine alone.

**⚠️ Attribution caution — the line is a rebel's self-justification, not an authorial verdict.**

✅ Verified text (Folger Shakespeare Library edition, *Henry IV, Part 1*, Act 5, scene 2):
"Now, for our consciences, the arms are fair / When the intent of bearing them is just."

- ✅ The speaker is **Hotspur (Henry Percy)**, rallying his own troops immediately before the
  Battle of Shrewsbury — that is, a **rebel commander addressing an army raised against his
  reigning king**.
- ✅ In the *same scene*, Worcester has just decided to suppress from Hotspur "the liberal and kind
  offer of the King," and instructs Vernon to conceal it. Hotspur therefore forms his sincere
  judgment of "just intent" on **deliberately falsified information**, with a peaceful settlement
  available and hidden from him.
- ⚠️ Two consequences for an answer. (i) **Attribution:** the line is a dramatic character's
  self-exculpation; presenting it as "Shakespeare's ethical position that intention sanctifies
  war" is an attribution error, and an examiner who knows the play will read it as one. (ii)
  **Substance:** the play's own dramatic irony *is* the ethical argument — sincerity of intention
  is shown to be worthless where the cause lacks legitimate authority, where the facts grounding
  the intention were manipulated, and where a non-violent alternative existed. The quotation is
  best treated as the **thesis to be tested**, not the conclusion to be endorsed.

**Comparison chart — where "just intent" is decisive, and where it is not**

| Question asked | Does right intention settle it? | Why | Named test to apply instead/alongside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the agent **blameworthy** (culpability, mens rea)? | Largely yes | Moral appraisal of a *person* tracks what she meant, knew and could have known. | Virtue appraisal; culpable ignorance — did she take reasonable care to check the facts? |
| Is the **act right** (permissibility)? | No | Rightness depends on cause, authority, last resort, proportion and conduct — all of which are external to the agent's state of mind. | Full *jus ad bellum* checklist (Sec. 11 table). |
| May this **belligerent party** resort to force at all? | No | Authority is institutional, not psychological. A sincere faction is still a faction. | Legitimate authority; UN Charter Art. 2(4) and Art. 51. |
| May this **target** be struck? | No | *Jus in bello* applies symmetrically to both sides regardless of the justice of their cause. | Distinction; proportionality in attack; military necessity; double effect. |
| Is **foreseen civilian harm** excusable? | Only partly | Intention distinguishes side-effect from means, but proportionality still binds. | Doctrine of double effect, all four conditions (Sec. 11 table). |
| Should the officer/State **be believed**? | No | Intention is unverifiable in itself; it is evidenced by war aims, terms offered, and treatment of the defeated. | Conduct-based evidence; published terms; record of alternatives refused. |

**Three arguments for the quotation, and the reply to each**

| Argument for "just intent makes arms fair" | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Deontological:** on a Kantian reading the moral worth of an act lies in the maxim/will, not the outcome; a soldier who fights from duty acts well. | Kant's own test is **universalisability and respect for persons as ends**, not sincerity. A maxim of "take up arms whenever I privately judge my cause just" cannot be willed universally — it licenses every rebel, militia and aggressor equally. Good will constrains, it does not authorise. |
| **Agent-centred/virtue:** we praise the honest soldier and blame the cynical one even when the outcomes coincide. | True but **category-shifted**: this establishes a judgment about the *agent*, not about the *act*. Virtue ethics itself requires *phronesis* (practical wisdom) — and wisdom includes checking the facts and the authority, which Hotspur conspicuously fails to do. |
| **Practical/epistemic:** in war, consequences are unknowable, so intention is the only workable standard. | Consequences are uncertain, not unknowable; that is why Just War supplies **procedural** criteria (declared authority, exhausted alternatives, proportionality assessment, reasonable prospect) which are auditable *ex ante*. Uncertainty argues for restraint and process, not for subjectivism. |

⚠️ **Symmetry objection (the strongest one, and the one to state explicitly):** if sincere intent
sufficed, then **both sides** of nearly every war would be fighting justly, since each believes its
cause just. A criterion that cannot distinguish an aggressor from a defender has failed as a
criterion. This is also why IHL binds both belligerents **irrespective** of the justice of their
cause — the equal application of *jus in bello*.

❌ **UPSC traps on this demand**

- ❌ "Shakespeare/the quotation proves that good intention justifies war." -> It is Hotspur's line,
  spoken by a deceived rebel; the play frames it ironically. Cite the speaker.
- ❌ "Right intention is the same as just cause." -> Just cause is about the **objective wrong** being
  responded to; right intention is about **why the agent actually acts**. A just cause pursued for
  annexation fails right intention; a sincere motive without a just cause fails just cause.
- ❌ "Double effect permits any harm the agent did not desire." -> It requires all four conditions,
  including that the good effect is not *produced through* the harm, plus proportionate reason.
- ❌ "Ends-justify-means consequentialism is the opposite of this quotation." -> Both are single-
  criterion positions; the Just War tradition is deliberately **multi-criterion and conjunctive** —
  every criterion must be satisfied, so failing one is fatal even if the others hold.

**10-mark spine (~150 words, "examine/comment"):** state the claim and immediately place the
quotation with its speaker and dramatic context (one line) -> concede the genuine truth in it
(right intention is a *necessary* condition; culpability tracks intention) -> show it is not
*sufficient* by naming the three criteria it cannot supply — legitimate authority, just cause, last
resort -> deploy the symmetry objection in one sentence -> add the *jus in bello* point that
conduct is judged independently of cause -> one Indian-register line (Article 51 of the
Constitution directs the State to promote international peace and security and respect for
international law and treaty obligations; India's declared doctrine of restraint and no-first-use
rests on institutional commitment, not on asserted sincerity) -> verdict: **intention purifies the
agent, procedure legitimises the act; ethics in the use of force requires both.**

### International aid ethics

| Concept | Meaning | Ethical tension |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Donor conditionality** | Aid tied to policy conditions (governance reform, economic liberalisation, human-rights benchmarks) set by the donor state/institution. | Can improve accountability for aid use, but risks compromising the recipient state's sovereignty and self-determined development path — the central donor-ethics dilemma. |
| ⚠️ **Ownership** | The principle (echoing the 2005 Paris Declaration on Aid Effectiveness) that recipient countries should lead and own their own development strategies rather than have them dictated externally. | Genuine ownership requires donors to accept recipient-led priorities even where they diverge from donor preference — frequently honoured only partially in practice. |
| ⚠️ **Dependency risk** | Long-term aid reliance can weaken domestic institution-building and revenue mobilisation, or distort local markets (e.g., food-aid undercutting local agricultural prices). | Requires aid design (time-bound, capacity-building-oriented) that avoids substituting for, rather than building, domestic capacity. |
| ⚠️ **Accountability** | Both donor and recipient are accountable — donors for effective, non-corrupt disbursement; recipients for transparent utilisation. | Weak accountability on either side (donor-driven graft, recipient diversion of aid) undermines the ethical basis for continued aid. |

- ⚠️ **Balanced verdict:** aid ethics requires holding donor conditionality's accountability benefit
  against its sovereignty cost, and valuing recipient ownership without ignoring legitimate donor
  concern for effective, non-corrupt utilisation — a one-sided "aid is always paternalistic" or "aid
  conditions are always justified" answer misses the genuine tension GS-IV is testing.
- ⚠️ **Non-refoulement boundary:** Article 33 of the Refugee Convention expresses the prohibition
  on returning a refugee to threatened persecution. India is not a party to the 1951 Convention or
  1967 Protocol. In Indian answers, use *NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh* for Article 21
  protection of non-citizens and treat *Mohammad Salimullah* as an interim order, not a final
  settlement of customary-law non-refoulement.
- ⚠️ Cross-link: `10_Sources-of-Ethical-Guidance-Laws-Rules-Conscience.md` treats sovereignty-vs-
  intervention tensions at the individual-conscience level (Snowden); this file's contribution is
  the state/institutional-level international-ethics register.

## 11B. Security-rights balancing: limitation, derogation and proportionality

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q2(b) (10 marks, ~150 words):** how national security is
> to be balanced with human rights. **This section is the owner.** It also supplies the rights
> floor for any Section-B case involving surveillance, preventive detention, restriction orders or
> emergency powers (2026 Q8 and Q11 route through here for the *legal floor*, while
> `13_Emerging-Ethics-Technology-AI-and-Environment.md` Sec. 11C owns the algorithmic-surveillance
> mechanism and `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md` owns the case architecture).
> 📰 Current affairs are optional decoration here; the demand is decidable on settled law.

**The two-layer grammar (the single most examinable distinction).**

| Layer | What it is | Legal register | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Limitation** | The ordinary, permanent qualification of a right in normal times (reasonable restrictions on speech, movement, assembly). | Constitution, Art. 19(2)-(6); *Puttaswamy* proportionality for privacy. | Available every day; must still be **prescribed by law**, pursue a **legitimate aim**, be **necessary** and **proportionate**, with procedural safeguards. |
| ⚠️ **Derogation** | The temporary suspension of a treaty obligation during a proclaimed emergency threatening the life of the nation. | ICCPR Art. 4; in the Indian Constitution, Part XVIII (Art. 352-360) and the Art. 359 suspension route. | Exceptional, time-bound, notified, non-discriminatory — and **barred altogether** for a non-derogable core. |

- ✅ **ICCPR Article 4(1) (verbatim, OHCHR text):** derogation is permitted only "In time of public
  emergency which threatens the life of the nation and the existence of which is officially
  proclaimed", only "to the extent strictly required by the exigencies of the situation", only if
  consistent with other international-law obligations, and only if the measures "do not involve
  discrimination solely on the ground of race, colour, sex, language, religion or social origin".
- ✅ **ICCPR Article 4(2) — the non-derogable core (verbatim list):** "No derogation from articles 6,
  7, 8 (paragraphs I and 2), 11, 15, 16 and 18 may be made under this provision." In substance:
  the right to life (6), freedom from torture and from non-consensual medical or scientific
  experimentation (7), freedom from slavery and servitude (8(1)-(2)), no imprisonment for inability
  to fulfil a contract (11), no retrospective criminal law (15), recognition as a person before the
  law (16), and freedom of thought, conscience and religion (18).
- ✅ **ICCPR Article 4(3):** a derogating State must **immediately notify** other States Parties
  through the UN Secretary-General, stating the provisions derogated from and the reasons, and must
  notify again when derogation ends. ⚠️ India acceded to the ICCPR in 1979; verify the precise date
  and any reservation text before quoting it.
- ⚠️ **Siracusa Principles (1984)** — an expert, **non-binding** interpretive statement on limitation
  and derogation clauses in the ICCPR. Its examinable content is the rule that a limitation must be
  prescribed by law, respond to a pressing public or social need, pursue a legitimate aim, and be
  strictly necessary and proportionate, with the burden of justification on the State and with no
  limitation applied in a discriminatory manner. ⚠️ Cite it as persuasive scholarship, never as a
  treaty; do not attribute numbered paragraphs to it unless you have verified them.

**The Indian floor — what security may never displace**

| Instrument | Operative content | Answer use |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ Article 21 | No deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law, read after *Maneka Gandhi* as requiring a **fair, just and reasonable** procedure. | The residual guarantee that survives every security justification. |
| ⚠️ Article 22(3)-(7) | Preventive detention is constitutionally contemplated but **conditioned**: the Art. 22(1)-(2) rights of counsel and production before a magistrate do not apply; detention beyond the constitutionally specified period requires an **Advisory Board** opinion; grounds must be communicated and representation permitted, subject to the "public interest" exception on disclosure. | The precise hook for any case involving preventive detention. ⚠️ Do not state the maximum period or Board composition from memory — verify the current text and the applicable statute before writing numbers. |
| ✅ *Puttaswamy* (2017) proportionality | Legality -> legitimate aim -> rational connection -> necessity/least restrictive means -> proportionality *stricto sensu* -> procedural safeguards. | The workhorse test; full treatment at `13_Emerging-Ethics-Technology-AI-and-Environment.md` Sec. 11A. |
| ⚠️ *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) | Restriction orders (including internet suspension and orders restricting movement) must be **published** so they can be challenged, are subject to **judicial review**, must satisfy proportionality and use the **least restrictive** effective measure, and indefinite restriction is impermissible — periodic review is required. | The single best authority for "security measures must be auditable, temporary and reviewable". ⚠️ Verify the operative directions before quoting paragraph numbers. |
| ⚠️ NHRC and statutory oversight | Independent post-facto scrutiny of custodial and security-operation complaints. | Names an institutional, not merely moral, check. |

**Comparison chart — three postures a State can take**

| Posture | Claim | Characteristic failure | Ethical verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Security absolutism** | Rights are a peacetime luxury; survival comes first. | Permanent emergency; normalisation of exception; the security apparatus becomes unaccountable. | Rejected: it makes the State's own justification (protecting persons) self-defeating, since it sacrifices the very persons it claims to protect. |
| ⚠️ **Rights absolutism** | No right may ever yield to a security claim. | Ignores that the State's protective duty is itself a rights-duty owed to potential victims. | Rejected: conflicts between rights are real, and refusing to weigh them is not neutrality. |
| ⚠️ **Structured proportionality (the defensible middle)** | Rights may be limited, never nullified, and only through a disciplined, reviewable procedure; a non-derogable core is absolute. | Harder to administer; requires record-keeping, publication and review capacity. | Adopted: it is the only posture that keeps the trade-off **visible, bounded, temporary and correctable**. |

**Objections and replies**

- *Objection:* "Proportionality is elastic — any government can assert necessity."
  *Reply:* which is why the test is **procedural as well as substantive**: written order by a
  competent authority, stated aim, stated duration, publication, least-restrictive alternative
  considered on record, periodic review, and independent post-facto audit. Elasticity is disciplined
  by evidence requirements, not by rhetoric.
- *Objection:* "Emergencies are exactly when procedure is unaffordable."
  *Reply:* ICCPR Art. 4 concedes the emergency and still imposes proclamation, strict necessity,
  non-discrimination and notification. Emergency changes the *threshold*, not the *existence*, of
  justification.
- *Objection:* "Secrecy defeats review."
  *Reply:* graduated disclosure — the operative order and its legal basis are published even where
  underlying intelligence is withheld; a designated reviewing authority sees the full record.
- *Objection:* "Human-rights criticism aids the adversary."
  *Reply:* the opposite is the stronger empirical claim — indiscriminate measures produce
  alienation, recruitment and evidentiary failure. Rights compliance is a **counter-insurgency
  asset**, not a concession.

❌ **Traps**

- ❌ "National security is a complete answer to a rights claim." -> It is a **legitimate aim**, which
  is only the *second* step of a six-step test; it does not supply necessity or proportionality.
- ❌ "Derogation means rights are suspended." -> Article 4(2)'s core is non-derogable, and every
  derogation must still be proclaimed, strictly necessary and non-discriminatory.
- ❌ "Preventive detention is unconstitutional in India." -> It is constitutionally contemplated by
  Article 22(3)-(7); the ethical and legal argument is about **safeguards and proportionality**, not
  about existence.
- ❌ "Balancing means splitting the difference." -> Proportionality is not a compromise; it asks
  whether a **less restrictive measure would achieve the same aim**, and if so, mandates it.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** define the two-layer grammar (limitation vs derogation) in one line
-> state the non-derogable core with ICCPR Art. 4(2) named -> lay out *Puttaswamy*'s six steps as
the operative Indian test -> one named illustration of auditability (publication and periodic review
of restriction orders, *Anuradha Bhasin*) -> concede the State's genuine protective duty to
potential victims -> verdict: **security and rights are not rival goods to be traded but a single
question — is this specific measure the least restrictive effective means, for the shortest
necessary time, on a reviewable record?**

**20-mark/case-study integration:** treat legality and the non-derogable core as **hard thresholds**
(an option that breaches them is eliminated, not discounted), and proportionality, non-discrimination
and contestability as **weighted checks** among surviving options — the hard-threshold/weighted
distinction is owned by `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md`.

## 11C. Ethics and national interest: realism, idealism and the ethical middle

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q5(b) (10 marks, ~150 words):** whether ethics is
> subjugated to strategic interests in international relations. **This section is the owner**; it
> also supplies the normative frame for Sec. 11D's arms-transfer decision.

**Comparison chart — four traditions on the ethics/interest relation**

| Tradition | Core claim | Named anchor | What it gets right | Limitation to state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Classical realism** | States act on interest defined in terms of power; moral principles cannot be applied to state action in their abstract universal form but must be filtered through the concrete circumstances of time and place; **prudence** is the supreme political virtue. | Hans Morgenthau, *Politics Among Nations* (1948), the six principles — especially the fourth. | Takes seriously the leader's **fiduciary duty** to those she protects, and the real costs of moralism. | ⚠️ **Morgenthau is not an amoralist.** He requires moral principles to be *mediated by consequences*, not abandoned. An answer that reads realism as "ethics has no place" misreads the text and loses marks. |
| ⚠️ **Power-political scepticism** | Justice exists only between equals; "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must". | Thucydides, the Melian Dialogue; Kautilya's *mātsya-nyāya* (the law of the fish) and *maṇḍala* theory in the *Arthaśāstra*. | Describes anarchy and self-help honestly. | It is a **description of a predicament**, not a justification; Thucydides presents the Melian episode as a warning, and Kautilya's *rājadharma* imposes duties on the king he does not permit him to shed. |
| ⚠️ **Liberal/cosmopolitan idealism** | Law, institutions, republican government and trade progressively domesticate anarchy; duties run to persons, not only to states. | Kant, *Perpetual Peace* (1795); UN Charter; UDHR/ICCPR; R2P as affirmed in the 2005 World Summit Outcome (paras 138-139). | Explains treaty-making, IHL and the cost that reputational failure imposes. | Risks moral overreach and selective enforcement; R2P's third pillar is contested in practice. |
| ⚠️ **Constructivism** | Interests are not fixed by anarchy; they are constituted by norms and identity — "anarchy is what states make of it" (Wendt). | Norm-cascade accounts of the landmine and chemical-weapons taboos. | Explains how ethical claims *become* interests over time. | Descriptive strength, weak decision-guidance for a specific dilemma. |

**The decisive concept for the "subjugated" framing: dirty hands.**

⚠️ Michael Walzer, "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" (1973): the political leader may
sometimes be obliged to do what is genuinely wrong in order to discharge her office, **and the
moral residue does not disappear**. The ethical minimum is therefore to **acknowledge the wrong,
bear its cost, and refuse to redefine it as right**. This gives a precise answer to the question:
ethics is *not* subjugated when the trade-off is admitted, bounded, reviewed and compensated; it
*is* subjugated when the trade-off is renamed a virtue.

**Three normative replies to "ethics is subjugated to strategy"**

1. ⚠️ **Prudence is not amorality.** Morgenthau's fourth principle demands that universal moral
   principles be *applied through* circumstance, and treats prudence — weighing the consequences of
   alternative political actions — as a moral requirement, not an escape from morality.
2. ⚠️ **Interest convergence.** Reliability, treaty compliance, humanitarian record and predictability
   are *strategic assets*: they buy coalition access, basing and technology partnerships, and reduce
   the cost of future bargaining. Ethical consistency is the **long-horizon component of interest**,
   not its rival.
3. ⚠️ **Institutional embedding.** Treaty law, IHL, export-control regimes, review mechanisms and
   parliamentary/judicial oversight make ethical breach **cost-bearing**, converting a moral claim
   into a calculable one.

**Indian register (use one, not all):** Panchsheel (1954) and its five principles; non-alignment and
its successor doctrine of **strategic autonomy**, which refuses the ethics/interest binary rather
than resolving it in one direction; ✅ **Article 51** of the Constitution (Directive Principles),
which directs the State to endeavour to promote international peace and security, maintain just and
honourable relations between nations, foster respect for international law and treaty obligations,
and encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration; humanitarian assistance and
disaster-relief deployments; *Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam* as a declared civilisational framing. 📰 A
current summit theme or a recent relief operation may be named in **one clause** as illustration —
it must never carry the argument.

❌ **Traps**

- ❌ "Realism says ethics is irrelevant." -> Morgenthau's fourth and sixth principles say the opposite;
  realism relocates ethics into prudence and consequences.
- ❌ "Panchsheel/NAM proves India subordinates interest to ethics." -> Both were also instruments of
  interest (autonomy, room for manoeuvre); presenting them as pure altruism is as one-sided as
  denying their normative content.
- ❌ "Because states sometimes act unethically, ethics has no causal role." -> Breach does not
  disprove a norm; it is parasitic on it — states expend effort justifying, denying or concealing
  breaches precisely because the norm constrains.
- ❌ Writing the answer as a list of countries behaving badly. -> The directive tests a **conceptual
  relation**; carry it with one tradition, one counter-tradition, one concept (dirty hands) and one
  Indian anchor.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** concede the descriptive claim with one structural reason (anarchy,
self-help, the leader's fiduciary duty to her own citizens) -> distinguish **subordination** from
**mediation** using Morgenthau's fourth principle -> give the interest-convergence reply in one
sentence -> give the institutional-embedding reply with one named instrument -> deploy Walzer's
dirty hands as the test that separates an honest trade-off from a laundered one -> one Indian anchor
(Article 51 / strategic autonomy) -> verdict: **ethics is not external to national interest; where
it is treated as external, the interest is being measured over too short a horizon.**

## 11D. Arms-transfer ethics and export due diligence

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section B, Q12 (20 marks, ~250 words):** the head of a defence firm's
> weapon-sales department must choose between two buyer countries, with the question itself stating
> that "due diligence at each stage was pivotal". **This section is the substantive owner**; the
> eight-element case architecture, the hard-threshold/weighted-check distinction and the steelman
> and residual-risk elements remain owned by `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md`.
> It also owns the **historical** 2020 GS-IV Q8 demand (ATGM export to a friendly nation; ethical
> issues for a responsible arms-exporting nation; five ethical factors in an arms-sale decision),
> which is already listed in this file's 2018-2023 PYQ block but had no substantive module until now.
> ⚠️ **Scope note:** Sec. 11 and 11A concern the ethics of *fighting* (*jus ad bellum* / *jus in
> bello*). This section concerns the ethics of **supplying** — a distinct question with its own
> law, its own actors and its own failure modes.

### 11D.1 Why supplying is a distinct moral question

| Concept | Meaning | Why it matters in the answer |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Complicity** | Knowingly providing material assistance that makes another's wrongful act possible or easier, without sharing its intention. | The supplier's culpability is **its own**, graded by foreseeability and by the contribution made — not derived from the buyer's guilt. |
| ⚠️ **Dual-use** | An item with both legitimate civilian/defensive and illegitimate offensive or proliferation applications. | Defeats the "it is only equipment" defence; the ethical object of assessment is the **foreseeable use**, not the label. |
| ⚠️ **Diversion** | The item reaching an end-user or end-use other than the declared one — by re-transfer, capture, resale or deliberate onward supply to a proxy. | The dominant real-world risk, and the reason due diligence must be **staged and continuing**, not a single pre-sale judgement. |
| ⚠️ **Irreversibility** | Once transferred, a weapon system cannot be recalled, and its effects persist across governments and decades. | Raises the evidentiary standard: uncertainty should count **against** the transfer, not for it. |
| ⚠️ **Opportunity-cost justice** | A buyer whose military budget crowds out health, education and infrastructure imposes the cost of the purchase on its own poorest. | The distinctive *development* dimension; it is the eighth EU criterion (below), and is easy marks because most candidates omit it. |

### 11D.2 The Indian legal and regulatory architecture (the anchors that score)

- ✅ **SCOMET** — Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies — is India's
  dual-use and munitions export-control list, notified as **Appendix 3 to Schedule 2 of the ITC (HS)
  Classification of Export and Import Items**, administered by the **DGFT** (Ministry of Commerce
  and Industry) under the **Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992**. Policy and
  procedure sit in the SCOMET chapter of the Foreign Trade Policy and Handbook of Procedures.
- ✅ **Inter-Ministerial Working Group (IMWG)** in DGFT (HQ), with representatives of other
  Ministries/Departments, decides SCOMET authorisation applications. Its published general criteria
  are directly usable as an answer framework: credentials of the end-user and credibility of the
  declared end-use; integrity of the chain of transmission from supplier to end-user; the potential
  of the item (including the **timing** of its export) to contribute to end-uses not in conformity
  with India's national security or foreign-policy objectives, global non-proliferation objectives,
  or India's treaty obligations; **assessed risk that the exported items will fall into the hands of
  terrorists, terrorist groups and non-State actors**; the recipient State's own export-control
  measures; the capabilities and objectives of the recipient State's weapons and delivery-system
  programmes; assessment of end-use(s); and the applicability of the control lists of the NSG, MTCR,
  Australia Group and Wassenaar Arrangement.
- ✅ **End-Use-cum-End-User Certificate (EUC)** — the operative due-diligence instrument. The
  prescribed certification requires that: the item "will be used only for stated purpose and that
  such use will not be changed, nor items modified or replicated without consent of Government of
  India"; "neither the items nor replicas nor derivatives thereof will be **re-transferred** without
  consent of Government of India"; and the "**end-user shall facilitate such verifications as are
  required by Government of India**". The EUC must name the item, the importer and **all entities in
  the supply chain**, the specific end-use, and the purchase order/contract. The Government may
  require additional formal assurances on end-use and non-transfer **from the recipient State**, and
  may stipulate additional end-use conditions in the authorisation based on proliferation concerns.
- ✅ **Post-shipment obligations** — the SCOMET procedure requires exporters to submit post-shipment
  details/reports in the prescribed form within prescribed timelines, with consequences for failure;
  re-export or re-transfer down the chain requires fresh application and fresh EUCs from each link,
  assessed on end-use/end-user verification. This is what makes Indian practice a **staged**
  obligation: pre-licence assessment -> licence conditions -> EUC and State assurances -> shipment ->
  post-shipment verification -> control over onward transfer.
- ✅ **Catch-all controls** — if an exporter is notified by DGFT, or knows or has reason to believe,
  that a **non-listed** item has a potential risk of use in or diversion to weapons of mass
  destruction, a missile system, or military use "including by terrorists and non-State actors", a
  SCOMET authorisation must be sought. ⚠️ This is the decisive rebuttal to "the item is not on any
  list, so the sale is clean": knowledge, not listing, triggers the duty.
- ⚠️ **Weapons of Mass Destruction and their Delivery Systems (Prohibition of Unlawful Activities)
  Act, 2005**, amended in **2022** to prohibit the **financing** of prohibited activities and to
  empower freezing/seizure of related assets — India's principal statutory implementation of the
  **UN Security Council Resolution 1540 (2004)** obligation to prevent non-State actors from
  acquiring WMD and their delivery systems. ⚠️ Verify section numbers before citing them.
- ⚠️ **Defence exports specifically** are additionally routed through the Department of Defence
  Production/MoD authorisation framework for munitions-list items, with open general export licences
  for specified low-sensitivity categories. ⚠️ The categories and thresholds change; do not quote
  them from memory.
- ✅ **Article 51** (Directive Principles) — promotion of international peace and security, respect
  for international law and treaty obligations — is the constitutional value-anchor for an Indian
  answer on export restraint.

**⚠️ India's regime-membership map — the cautions that separate a precise answer from a wrong one**

| Regime / treaty | India's status | Answer caution |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **MTCR** | Member (2016) | Membership is an **adherence commitment**, implemented domestically through SCOMET; it is not a treaty obligation enforceable against India internationally. |
| ⚠️ **Wassenaar Arrangement** | Participating State (2017) | Politically binding, consensus-based; controls conventional arms and dual-use goods. Not a treaty. |
| ⚠️ **Australia Group** | Participant (2018) | Chemical/biological proliferation controls. Not a treaty. |
| ⚠️ **Nuclear Suppliers Group** | **Not** a member | Do not list India as an NSG member; India operates under the 2008 NSG waiver arrangement, which is a different thing. |
| ⚠️ **Arms Trade Treaty (ATT)** | **Not** a party | The ATT is therefore a **normative reference**, not an Indian legal obligation. India's stated concerns have included the balance of the text, the adequacy of provisions against transfers to non-State actors, and recognition of the right of self-defence. ⚠️ State this precisely; claiming India is ATT-bound is a factual error. |
| ⚠️ **UN Security Council arms embargoes / UNSCR 1540** | **Binding** | Embargo compliance and the bar on transfers reaching non-State actors are **hard thresholds** in any case-study option set — an option that breaches them is eliminated, not merely down-weighted. |
| ⚠️ **NPT** | **Not** a party | Relevant where the buyer is described as a non-NPT nuclear-weapon possessor: India cannot invoke the NPT as its own obligation, so the argument must run through **MTCR Category I restraint, UNSCR 1540, diversion risk and regional-stability reasoning** instead. |

- ✅ **MTCR Category I — the dispositive rule for long-range missile demands.** Category I covers
  complete rocket and unmanned-aerial-vehicle systems (ballistic missiles, space launch vehicles,
  sounding rockets, cruise missiles, target and reconnaissance drones) **capable of delivering a
  payload of at least 500 kg to a range of at least 300 km**, their major complete subsystems
  (rocket stages, engines, guidance sets, re-entry vehicles), related software and technology, and
  specially designed production facilities. The MTCR Guidelines state that "particular restraint
  will be exercised in the consideration of Category I transfers **regardless of their purpose**,
  and there will be a **strong presumption to deny** such transfers"; the transfer of Category I
  **production facilities is not authorised**; and other Category I items are authorised "only on
  rare occasions" and only where the government obtains **binding government-to-government
  undertakings** and assumes responsibility for ensuring the stated end-use. ⚠️ Note the asymmetry
  a good answer exploits: restraint applies **regardless of purpose**, so a buyer's benign stated
  intention does not relieve the presumption.

### 11D.3 The eight-criterion assessment grid (foreign comparator, use as a template)

⚠️ **Flag it as foreign.** The **EU Common Position 2008/944/CFSP** (as amended in 2019) is not
binding on India, but its eight criteria are the most complete published grid for a licensing
decision and map directly onto an exam fact pattern. Verified content:

| # | Criterion | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | International obligations and commitments | Compliance with UN/EU/OSCE arms embargoes and with non-proliferation and arms-control treaty obligations; denial if approval would be inconsistent with them. |
| 2 | Human rights and IHL in the country of final destination | **Deny** where there is a **clear risk** the equipment might be used for internal repression, or in the commission of **serious violations of IHL**; special caution where serious human-rights violations have been established by competent UN, EU or Council of Europe bodies. |
| 3 | Internal situation of the destination country | **Deny** where the transfer would provoke or prolong armed conflict, or aggravate existing tensions. |
| 4 | Preservation of regional peace, security and stability | **Deny** where there is a clear risk of aggressive use against another country or of asserting a territorial claim by force; consider the likelihood of armed conflict with a neighbour and effects on regional stability. |
| 5 | National security of the supplier and of friendly/allied states | Effect on the supplier's own defence interests and risk of use against its own or allied forces — while expressly **not allowed to override** the human-rights and regional-stability criteria. |
| 6 | Behaviour of the buyer towards the international community | Record on **support for or encouragement of terrorism** and international organised crime; compliance with international commitments including the non-use of force and IHL; commitment to non-proliferation and arms control. |
| 7 | Risk of diversion or undesirable re-export | Technical capability to use the equipment; capability to apply effective export controls; record on respecting re-export consent provisions; **risk of diversion to terrorist organisations or individual terrorists**; risk of reverse engineering. |
| 8 | Compatibility with the recipient's technical and economic capacity | Whether the purchase is compatible with the buyer's economy, "taking into account the desirability that states should meet their legitimate security and defence needs with the **least diversion of human and economic resources for armaments**". |

⚠️ In an Indian answer, present criteria 1-8 as an **assessment structure** and route the *authority*
through SCOMET/IMWG, MTCR restraint, UNSC embargoes and UNSCR 1540 — never as though India were
applying an EU instrument.

### 11D.4 Staged due diligence — the answer to "due diligence at each stage"

```text
STAGE 1  PRE-ENQUIRY SCREENING
         classify item (SCOMET category / munitions list / MTCR Cat I?)
         screen buyer State, intermediaries, brokers, freight forwarders
                    |
                    v
STAGE 2  RISK ASSESSMENT
         embargo check -> non-State-actor/proxy-support check ->
         human rights & IHL record -> regional stability ->
         diversion & re-export capability -> economic-compatibility check
                    |
                    v
STAGE 3  AUTHORISATION
         DGFT/IMWG application; inter-ministerial clearance;
         additional end-use conditions stipulated in the authorisation
                    |
                    v
STAGE 4  CONTRACTUAL CONDITIONING
         EUC naming every entity in the supply chain + specific end-use;
         no-modification / no-replication clause; NO-RE-TRANSFER undertaking;
         State-level assurance where required; verification-facilitation clause
                    |
                    v
STAGE 5  SHIPMENT CONTROL
         consignee verification; transit/transhipment routing; brokering controls
                    |
                    v
STAGE 6  POST-SHIPMENT VERIFICATION
         prescribed post-shipment reports within prescribed timelines;
         on-site/end-use verification as the EUC permits
                    |
                    v
STAGE 7  CONTINUING OVERSIGHT
         consent control over any re-export/re-transfer (fresh EUCs from each link);
         suspension/revocation on adverse information; sanction for non-reporting
```

⚠️ **The ethical point of the diagram:** due diligence is a **continuing obligation with retained
control**, not a one-time judgement at the point of sale. A supplier who cannot verify, cannot
condition, and cannot stop onward transfer has not done due diligence — it has done paperwork.
⚠️ Vocabulary bridge: the **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011)** supply the
generic language — identify, prevent, mitigate and **account for** adverse human-rights impacts,
including those to which an enterprise's operations **contribute** through business relationships —
which is why "we only sold it" is not a UNGP-compliant position.

### 11D.5 The replaceability defence, and why it fails

⚠️ The strongest argument a case study will plant is: *"If we refuse, the buyer will simply purchase
elsewhere; our refusal changes nothing and costs us the order."* Four replies, in ascending force:

1. **Agency:** moral responsibility attaches to **one's own act**. That another would have done the
   wrong does not convert my doing of it into a non-wrong; it is at most a claim about consequences,
   and it is offered as a claim about permissibility.
2. **Proves too much:** if substitutability excused participation, it would excuse the bribe-taker,
   the polluting contractor and the trafficker equally — every wrong in a competitive market becomes
   permissible. A principle that licenses everything distinguishes nothing.
3. **Empirically weak:** substitution is rarely perfect. Alternative suppliers differ in
   capability, price, delivery time, interoperability, spares, training and political cost;
   collective restraint is the entire mechanism by which export-control regimes work, and each
   participant's compliance is what makes the regime non-empty.
4. **Self-defeating for the supplier:** a reputation for selling into diversion risk raises the cost
   of every future technology partnership, co-development agreement and regime standing — the
   interest-convergence point of Sec. 11C applied to a firm.

⚠️ **Concession to make the answer credible:** the argument does have a real kernel — where refusal
is *certainly* futile and the item is genuinely non-critical, refusal is symbolic rather than
protective. The right response is not to accept the sale but to **shift the action**: refuse, record
the reasons, and raise the diversion concern in the regime's information-exchange channels so that
restraint becomes collective rather than unilateral.

### 11D.6 Decision grid for a two-buyer arms-sale case

| Signal in the fact pattern | Ethical/legal reading | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer **supports armed groups abroad** | Direct diversion-to-non-State-actor risk; engages UNSCR 1540 and IMWG criterion on terrorists/non-State actors; EU criteria 6 and 7. | **Hard threshold** — eliminates the option, not merely down-weights it. |
| Item is a **long-range missile system** | Engages MTCR Category I strong presumption of denial **regardless of purpose**. | **Hard threshold**, rebuttable only on the Guidelines' own rare-occasion conditions. |
| Buyer is a **non-NPT possessor of nuclear weapons and delivery systems** | Proliferation-significance and regional-stability concern; India cannot invoke NPT obligation, so argue through MTCR restraint, UNSCR 1540 and stability. | High weight. |
| Buyer's **military budget crowds out health/education/infrastructure** | EU criterion 8 economic-compatibility concern; opportunity-cost justice toward the buyer's own population. | Genuine but **weighted**, never a standalone veto — a sovereign State may set its own budget. |
| **Advance payment / immediate revenue / "shopping elsewhere"** | Commercial pressure and the replaceability defence. | Relevant to firm viability; **not** a permissibility argument (Sec. 11D.5). |
| Buyer is **threatened by a neighbour**; seeks defensive capability | UN Charter Art. 51 self-defence; legitimate security need. | Supports transfer — but must still clear diversion, IHL-record and regional-stability checks. |
| Buyer offers **long-term R&D partnership / co-development**, is non-aligned | Strategic and industrial benefit; also improves verification access and end-use visibility. | Supports transfer; strengthens post-shipment control. |
| **Alliance, basing and grant relationship with a third power; FTA leverage** | Strategic benefit to the supplier State — but EU criterion 5 expressly bars supplier self-interest from overriding human-rights and regional-stability criteria. | Weighted, and **capped**. |

⚠️ **Verdict logic to write:** eliminate any option failing a hard threshold; among survivors, choose
the transfer that is **capability-appropriate, condition-bearable and verifiable**, i.e. the buyer
who will accept a full EUC with non-re-transfer and verification-facilitation clauses, State-level
assurance, staged delivery, and post-shipment verification. Where the profitable option fails a hard
threshold, the correct institutional move is **a criteria-based, recorded, communicated denial** —
communicated in process terms ("does not meet end-use and diversion criteria"), not moral terms —
with a **conditional future channel** left open if the buyer's situation changes.

### 11D.7 Objections, replies and traps

- *Objection:* "Arms exports are a sovereign commercial matter; ethics belongs to war, not trade."
  *Reply:* the transfer decision is where the supplier's own causal contribution is made and where
  it still retains control; once delivered, control is gone. **The point of sale is the last point
  of choice.**
- *Objection:* "Denying a friendly, threatened State is itself unethical — it leaves a victim
  undefended."
  *Reply:* correct, and this is why **blanket restraint is not the answer**. The grid distinguishes
  a legitimate self-defence supply from a capability supply into proliferation or proxy risk; a
  responsible exporter's default is conditional approval with verification, not refusal.
- *Objection:* "Export controls are Western instruments used to keep India out of markets."
  *Reply:* India's own SCOMET/IMWG framework applies substantially equivalent criteria as a matter
  of **national policy and security**, not external imposition; and India's defence-export ambitions
  make a credible control record an enabling asset, not a constraint.
- *Objection:* "Post-shipment verification is unenforceable abroad."
  *Reply:* it is enforced through **consent architecture** — the EUC obliges the end-user to
  facilitate verification, re-transfer requires fresh Indian consent, and non-compliance carries
  authorisation consequences for future business.

❌ **Traps**

- ❌ "India is bound by the Arms Trade Treaty." -> India is **not** a party; the ATT is a normative
  reference only.
- ❌ "India is an NSG member." -> It is not.
- ❌ "MTCR bans all missile exports." -> It creates a **strong presumption of denial** for Category I
  (≥500 kg payload to ≥300 km) and a licensing/restraint regime for Category II; production
  facilities for Category I are not authorised.
- ❌ "If the item is not on the SCOMET list, no authorisation is needed." -> **Catch-all controls**
  apply where the exporter is notified, or knows or has reason to believe, that a non-listed item
  risks WMD/missile/military use including by terrorists and non-State actors.
- ❌ "An end-user certificate settles the matter." -> An EUC is a **conditioning instrument**, not a
  guarantee; its value depends on chain-of-supply naming, verification-facilitation, re-transfer
  consent and actual post-shipment follow-through.
- ❌ Writing the answer as pure realpolitik ("sell to the highest bidder") or pure pacifism ("never
  export arms"). -> Both bypass the question, which is about **due diligence at each stage**.
- ❌ Treating the buyer's poverty/military-spending profile as a **veto**. -> It is EU criterion 8, a
  weighted development consideration, not a threshold.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** define the supplier's distinct duty (complicity, not participation)
-> name the Indian architecture in one clause (SCOMET under the FT(D&R) Act, 1992; DGFT/IMWG
authorisation; EUC with non-re-transfer and verification clauses; post-shipment reporting) -> name
the hard floor (UNSC embargoes, UNSCR 1540/WMD Act non-State-actor bar, MTCR Category I presumption
of denial) -> one weighted consideration (diversion risk or economic compatibility) -> rebut
replaceability in one sentence -> verdict: **due diligence is continuing control, not a signature.**

**15-mark spine (~250 words):** add the eight-criterion grid as the assessment structure, the
regime-membership cautions, and one objection-reply pair (legitimate self-defence supply vs proxy
risk).

**20-mark case-study integration:** build the answer on `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md`'s eight elements. Use this
section for Element 3 (options must include at least one *conditional-approval* option, not only
sell/refuse), Element 5 (hard thresholds = embargo, non-State-actor diversion, MTCR Category I;
weighted checks = human-rights/IHL record, regional stability, economic compatibility, long-term
strategic value), Element 6 (decision with the verdict logic of Sec. 11D.6), Element 7

denial communicated in process terms), and Element 8 (residual risk = diversion despite the EUC;
mitigation = staged delivery, serialised tracking, verification visits, consent-controlled
re-transfer, suspension clause, and regime information-exchange).

📰 **Optional current anchor (one clause maximum, never a substitute for the above):** India's
declared defence-export growth and its stated ambition to be a net defence exporter may be named to
show why a credible export-control record is now an Indian *interest*. ⚠️ Do not quote export-value

## 12. Selectable named evidence/application units

> Pick 1-2 units per answer; each follows claim -> named evidence/example -> significance -> limitation.

1. *Claim:* CSR compliance does not guarantee core-business ethics. *Evidence:* Section 10's
   business-ethics/CSR/corporate-governance three-layer table. *Significance:* prevents a common
   scoring error of treating CSR spend as a proxy for ethical conduct. *Limitation:* the distinction,
   while doctrinally clean, still requires a concrete named example in the answer to earn full marks.
2. *Claim:* Impact assessment is the institutional response to CSR's greenwashing risk. *Evidence:*
   the Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021's mandatory impact assessment for ≥ ₹10 crore
   CSR obligors, with its assessment-cost cap revised by the 2022 amendment (Section 10).
   *Significance:* shows a real regulatory correction, not just a theoretical caution.
   *Limitation:* impact assessment applies only above the ₹10 crore threshold — smaller CSR
   obligors remain unaudited for actual social impact.
3. *Claim:* Neutrality is the precondition for humanitarian access in an active conflict. *Evidence:*
   ICRC's neutrality-impartiality-independence operating principles (Section 11). *Significance:*
   explains why politicised aid delivery undermines its own access. *Limitation:* neutrality can be
   exploited by a belligerent party to shield itself from accountability if applied without any
   accompanying human-rights monitoring.
4. *Claim:* Donor conditionality trades sovereignty for accountability. *Evidence:* the Paris
   Declaration on Aid Effectiveness's "ownership" principle set against typical governance-linked aid
   conditions (Section 11). *Significance:* gives a named framework for balancing the two.
   *Limitation:* "ownership" can be invoked by a recipient government to resist legitimate
   anti-corruption conditions, not only illegitimate donor overreach.
5. *Claim:* The Serious Fraud Investigation Office institutionalises the Naresh Chandra Committee's
   2002 recommendation. *Evidence:* SFIO under Companies Act s.211-212 (Section 5 above).
   *Significance:* shows a two-decade lag between recommendation and institutional realisation, a
   useful "reform takes time but does arrive" evidence point. *Limitation:* SFIO's arrest power
   (s.212(8)) has faced judicial scrutiny on procedural safeguards in specific cases — verify current
   case law before citing a blanket claim of unchecked power.
6. *Claim:* Sincerity of intention cannot by itself make a resort to force permissible. *Evidence:*
   *Henry IV, Part 1*, Act 5 scene 2 — the "arms are fair when the intent is just" line is spoken by
   **Hotspur**, a rebel whose judgement rests on Worcester's deliberate concealment of the King's
   pardon offer (Sec. 11A). *Significance:* converts a quotation-based question into a doctrinal one
   by showing that legitimate authority and last resort are independently required.
   *Limitation:* the point is literary-analytical; it must be discharged in one or two lines and
   then handed over to the Just War criteria, or the answer becomes a book review.
7. *Claim:* A supplier's end-use control is contractual and continuing, not a one-time signature.
   *Evidence:* India's SCOMET End-Use-cum-End-User Certificate, which requires the end-user to use
   the item only for the stated purpose without modification or replication, **not to re-transfer**
   items, replicas or derivatives without the Government of India's consent, and to **facilitate such
   verifications as are required by** the Government, with all supply-chain entities named and
   post-shipment reporting prescribed (Sec. 11D.2). *Significance:* gives a named, Indian,
   verifiable instrument in place of a general appeal to "responsible exports".
   *Limitation:* the certificate's force depends on the exporting State's willingness to act on
   adverse information and on the end-user State's cooperation with verification.
8. *Claim:* "Someone else would have sold it anyway" is not a permissibility argument.
   *Evidence:* the four-step rebuttal in Sec. 11D.5 — agency, proves-too-much, imperfect
   substitutability, and the supplier's own long-run regime standing. *Significance:* dismantles the
   strongest defence a case study will plant, in one compact move. *Limitation:* where refusal is
   genuinely futile, the honest position is that refusal is symbolic; the answer must then shift to
   collective restraint through regime information-exchange rather than pretend unilateral impact.
9. *Claim:* Security measures must be auditable, bounded and reviewable rather than merely
   justified. *Evidence:* ICCPR Article 4's proclamation, strict-necessity, non-discrimination and
   notification conditions plus its non-derogable core (Articles 6, 7, 8(1)-(2), 11, 15, 16 and 18),
   read with the Indian requirement that restriction orders be published, proportionate and
   periodically reviewed (Sec. 11B). *Significance:* replaces a vague "balance" with a checkable
   procedure. *Limitation:* ICCPR obligations bind India as a matter of treaty commitment but are
   enforced domestically through constitutional standards, not directly — say so rather than
   implying direct enforceability.
