## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **AI ethics in administration** | The set of concerns (bias, reliability, opacity/explainability, accountability, human oversight and contestability) raised when algorithmic tools inform administrative or judicial decisions. |
| ⚠️ **Algorithmic accountability** | The principle that a human/institution must remain answerable for an AI-assisted decision's outcome — accountability cannot be "delegated" to the algorithm itself. |
| ✅ **Precautionary principle** (environmental ethics) | Where an activity raises threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. ⚠️ Rio Declaration **Principle 15** (1992), which is its classic statement, actually uses the words "precautionary **approach**". |
| ⚠️ **Intergenerational justice** | The ethical obligation to consider the interests/rights of future generations (who cannot participate in present decision-making) when making resource-use and environmental decisions today. |
| ✅ **Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)** | Rio Declaration Principle 7 and UNFCCC Articles 3.1 and 4.1: all states share responsibility for addressing climate change, but responsibility is differentiated by historical contribution and "respective capabilities". The Paris Agreement (Article 2(2)) expresses it as **CBDR-RC**, "in the light of different national circumstances". |
| ⚠️ **Feedback loop ("dirty data")** | A self-reinforcing cycle in which a predictive system trained on *past enforcement records* directs resources to the areas or groups already over-policed; the resulting new records confirm the prediction. The system learns **where the police went**, not where crime occurred. |
| ⚠️ **Proxy discrimination** | Discrimination achieved without using a protected attribute, because a permitted variable (postal code, locality, surname, occupation, phone model, electricity consumption) correlates strongly with caste, religion, class or gender. Deleting the protected field does **not** remove the effect. |
| ⚠️ **Place-based vs person-based prediction** | *Place-based* forecasts where and when an incident is likely, and directs patrols. *Person-based* scores named individuals for future offending and places them on lists. The second attaches a consequence to a person for an act not committed — a far higher ethical threshold. |
| ⚠️ **Anticipatory guilt** | Treating a statistical likelihood of future wrongdoing as a present ground for adverse treatment. It inverts the presumption of innocence, which attaches to acts, not to profiles. |
| ⚠️ **Automation bias** | The documented human tendency to over-trust a machine-generated recommendation and to under-weigh contrary evidence, which hollows out "human in the loop" safeguards into rubber-stamping. |
| ⚠️ **Function creep** | The gradual expansion of a system's use beyond the purpose for which it was authorised and consented to (a pandemic contact-tracing tool repurposed for law enforcement; an attendance database used for benefit denial). |
| ⚠️ **Contestability** | The practical capacity of an affected person to learn that an automated system produced an adverse outcome, obtain reasons in intelligible form, submit counter-evidence, and obtain human re-decision. Distinct from mere "explainability", which is a property of the model, not a right of the person. |
| ⚠️ **Academic/professional integrity in AI-assisted work** | The requirement that a named human author or officer retains, and can demonstrate, **originating and verifying judgement** over work produced with AI assistance — including responsibility for accuracy, originality and attribution — because accountability cannot be transferred to a tool. |
| ⚠️ **Free, prior and informed consent (FPIC)** | The standard that a community affected by a project on its land must consent **before** the decision is finalised, without coercion, and on the basis of full disclosure of impacts. In India its statutory reflections are the Gram Sabha's role under FRA 2006 and PESA 1996 and the consent provisions of RFCTLARR 2013. |

## 3. Mechanism: how emerging-ethics dilemmas arise and are resolved

1. **Technology/environmental change outpaces existing rules** — an AI tool or a new industrial
   process may operate in a genuine regulatory gap, forcing ethical reasoning to substitute for
   settled law temporarily.
2. **Apply the deontological floor** — does the technology/action violate a non-negotiable right
   (privacy, non-discrimination, ecological safety) regardless of its efficiency/profitability gain?
3. **Apply consequentialist analysis with a long time-horizon** — climate and AI-bias harms often
   manifest slowly and diffusely, requiring analysts to resist discounting future/dispersed harm
   simply because it is less immediately visible than present benefit.
4. **Apply the precautionary principle** where scientific uncertainty is high but potential harm is
   serious/irreversible (environmental clearance, novel AI deployment in high-stakes decisions).
5. ✅ 2024 GS-IV Q1(a): "application of AI as a dependable source of input for administrative
   rational decision-making is a debatable issue" — requires "critical examination... from the
   ethical point of view," directly testing algorithmic accountability and human oversight.
6. ✅ 2024 GS-IV Q2(b): "global warming and climate change are the outcomes of human greed in the
   name of development" — tests intergenerational justice and the ethics of consumption/growth.
7. ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q2(b): environmental clearance disputes in "ecologically sensitive border areas,"
   examined against "national security" — tests the precautionary principle against strategic/
   security imperatives, a genuine multi-value dilemma, not a simple pro-environment answer.

## 4. Indian applications and examples

- ⚠️ An AI-based welfare-eligibility screening tool must retain human review for edge cases and
  publish its selection criteria to avoid opaque, unaccountable exclusion of genuine beneficiaries.
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q2(b)'s border-area environmental-clearance dilemma requires balancing ecological
  protection, local community rights, and legitimate national-security infrastructure needs —
  resolvable only through transparent, case-specific environmental impact assessment, not a
  blanket rule either way.
- ⚠️ A data-centre operator's AI-driven emissions growth (see `12`) requires verifiable renewable-
  energy transition commitments, not offsetting through opaque carbon-credit purchases alone.

## 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ The precautionary principle is a foundational concept in environmental ethics and law; its
  classic international statement is Rio Declaration **Principle 15** (1992), which speaks of the
  "precautionary approach", and it is embedded in India's environmental jurisprudence through
  *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996).
- ✅ CBDR appears in Rio Declaration Principle 7 and UNFCCC Articles 3.1 and 4.1 (1992); the Paris
  Agreement's Article 2(2) uses the fuller CBDR-RC formulation.
- ⚠️ "Algorithmic accountability" and "explainability" are standard terms in current AI-governance
  discourse, distinguishing a transparent, auditable AI system from an opaque "black box".

## 6. UPSC traps

- ❌ AI-based decision-making is inherently more objective/unbiased than human decision-making. ->
  AI systems can encode and amplify the biases present in their training data; "objectivity" claims
  require independent scrutiny, not assumption.
- ❌ The precautionary principle means all development must stop wherever any risk exists. -> It
  means proportionate protective measures are justified despite scientific uncertainty for serious/
  irreversible risks — not an absolute prohibition on all development.
- ❌ Climate change is purely a technical/scientific problem with no ethical dimension. -> 2024
  GS-IV Q2(b) explicitly frames it as an outcome of "human greed... in the name of development,"
  requiring an ethical, not merely technical, response.
- ❌ A system that improves public order is thereby ethically validated. -> Effectiveness is a
  **necessary but not sufficient** condition. A measure that works must still be lawful,
  proportionate, non-discriminatory in effect, contestable and bounded in purpose; an efficient
  instrument of unequal treatment is more dangerous, not less (Sec. 11C).
- ❌ Removing caste/religion fields from a dataset makes an algorithm non-discriminatory. -> **Proxy
  discrimination**: locality, surname, occupation and consumption variables reconstruct the
  protected attribute. Fairness must be tested on **outcomes across groups**, not on the absence of
  a field.
- ❌ "There is a human in the loop, so accountability is preserved." -> Only if the human has the
  information, time, authority and incentive to disagree. **Automation bias** plus production
  pressure converts oversight into ratification; the safeguard must be designed (reasons recorded
  for overrides *and* for acceptances, override rates monitored), not asserted.
- ❌ Using AI to draft a report, note or paper is inherently dishonest. -> The offence is **undisclosed
  substitution of judgement and unverified output**, not assistance as such (Sec. 11B).
- ❌ Consent of the district administration or a project proponent's public meeting satisfies the
  rights of a forest community. -> The FRA/PESA architecture vests the decision in the **Gram Sabha**,
  and FRA s.4(5) bars eviction before the recognition and verification of rights is complete
  (Sec. 14F).

## 11A. Data/privacy ethics anchor: Puttaswamy and the DPDP Act, 2023

> ⚠️ This section is written to be answer-complete for a 10/15-mark question or for the data/privacy
> dimension of a Section-B case study; full information-governance and DPDP statutory mechanics are
> owned by `15_Transparency-RTI-and-Information-Sharing.md` and detailed sectoral coverage is in
> `Science-and-Technology/basic/12_Data-Protection-DPDP-Act-and-Cybersecurity.md` — this file's
> contribution is the ethical framework (consent, purpose limitation, proportionality) that those
> files' statutory detail operationalises.

- ✅ **Constitutional anchor:** *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India*, (2017) 10 SCC 1
  — a nine-judge Supreme Court bench (24 August 2017) unanimously held that privacy is a fundamental
  right intrinsic to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms in Part III.
  ⚠️ For an administrative answer, test a State restriction through legality, a legitimate aim,
  rational connection, necessity or least-restrictive means, proportionate balancing, and
  procedural safeguards. The exact doctrinal formulation developed across the privacy and Aadhaar
  judgments; do not compress proportionality into a mere statement that the State has a useful aim.
- ✅ **Statutory anchor:** the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) is India's first
  dedicated personal-data-protection statute, built around **consent-based processing, purpose
  limitation** (data used only for the purpose it was collected/consented for), **data
  minimisation**, and a **Data Protection Board of India** for enforcement.
- 📰 **Commencement/status as at 29 August 2026:** G.S.R. 843(E), 13 November 2025, commenced only
  specified provisions immediately. The Consent Manager tranche begins on 13 November 2026. Core
  provisions concerning notice, consent, Data Principal rights, Data Fiduciary and Significant Data
  Fiduciary duties, transfer restrictions, Board proceedings and penalties begin on 13 May 2027.
  Thus *Puttaswamy* and the underlying ethical duties of dignity, autonomy, purpose limitation,
  minimisation and security apply now, while most DPDP operational duties are enacted and notified
  but not yet in force. Do not describe the Act as fully operational or those future duties as
  presently enforceable.
- ⚠️ **Ethics-law distinction (exam-critical):** Puttaswamy is a *constitutional* holding (privacy as
  a fundamental right, subject to a proportionality test on any restriction); the DPDP Act is the
  *ordinary legislative* mechanism operationalising informational privacy for personal-data
  processing specifically. An administrator's **ethical** duty to protect a citizen's data (dignity,
  autonomy, trust) exists independently of, and precedes, the *legal* compliance duty created by a
  statute's specific, phased provisions — a case study should not treat "the Act is not yet fully in
  force" as a reason the underlying ethical duty is any weaker.
- ⚠️ **Evidence unit:** *Claim:* consent and purpose limitation are the operational ethical core of
  data-privacy administration. *Evidence:* the DPDP Act's consent/purpose-limitation/data-
  minimisation design, read with Puttaswamy's proportionality standard for any state infringement of
  privacy. *Significance:* gives a two-layer test (is the state aim legitimate and proportionate;
  is citizen consent/purpose genuinely respected) for any administrative data-use dilemma.
  *Limitation:* consent can become a formality (long, unread terms) rather than genuine informed
  consent — an ethical safeguard (plain-language notice, granular consent) is still needed beyond
  bare statutory compliance.
- ⚠️ **Limits:** proportionality review is fact-sensitive and contested case-by-case; the DPDP Act's
  phased commencement means some safeguards a candidate might assume are "already enforceable" are
  not yet operative — always separate the constitutional principle (settled since 2017) from the
  statute's live commencement status (verify before use).
- ❌ **UPSC trap:** "The DPDP Act, 2023 has fully replaced Puttaswamy as the source of India's
  privacy law." -> Puttaswamy remains the constitutional foundation (privacy as a fundamental right,
  with a proportionality test); the DPDP Act is a subordinate statutory mechanism for personal-data
  processing specifically, phased into force, and does not itself amend or supersede the
  constitutional right.
- ✅ Cross-link: `15_Transparency-RTI-and-Information-Sharing.md` for the DPDP Act's direct amendment
  of RTI Section 8(1)(j) and the transparency-versus-privacy trade-off in information governance;
  `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md` Sec. 14.13 for a fully worked eight-element
  data/privacy case study built on this anchor.

## 11B. Professional and academic integrity in AI-assisted work

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q1(a) (10 marks, ~150 words):** the ethical issues raised
> when AI tools are used to produce academic or professional work. **This section is the owner.**
> Read it with Sec. 11A (data/privacy) and with
> `16_Codes-of-Ethics-and-Codes-of-Conduct.md` for the code-of-conduct register.
> 📰 No current anchor is required; the demand is decidable on professional-ethics doctrine.

**The governing idea: judgement is non-delegable.**

⚠️ An author, researcher or officer occupies a position of trust in which others rely on her
**independent verification** of what she puts her name to. A tool may accelerate search, drafting,
translation, summarisation or code generation; it cannot assume the **answerability** that is the
whole point of authorship or of an official signature. The ethical question is therefore never
"was a machine used?" but "**whose judgement stands behind this, and can that person account for
every claim in it?**"

**Comparison chart — the four distinct wrongs (do not merge them)**

| Wrong | What is violated | Why it is wrong even if undetected | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Misattribution of authorship** | Truthfulness about provenance; the reader's reliance interest. | The value of a byline is a claim about who is answerable; a false claim defrauds the reader even where the text is good. | Would the reader change how she weighs this work if she knew how it was produced? |
| ⚠️ **Abdication of verification** | Duty of care; competence. | Generative systems produce fluent, confident output that may be incorrect, incomplete or biased; passing it on unverified transfers risk to the reader or the citizen. | Can the author independently evidence each factual and legal claim from a primary source? |
| ⚠️ **Undisclosed substitution of effort where effort is the thing assessed** | The integrity of an evaluative system (examinations, research training, selection). | An assessment measures a candidate's capacity; substituting the tool's capacity corrupts the measurement and unfairly disadvantages honest peers. | Is the process itself what is being certified, or only the product? |
| ⚠️ **Plagiarism and derivative appropriation** | Others' intellectual property and credit. | AI output may reproduce or closely paraphrase training material without attribution. | Has similarity been checked and sources cited independently of the tool? |

⚠️ Keeping these four apart is the single highest-value move in this answer: most candidates collapse
all AI use into "plagiarism", which is only one of the four and often not the operative one.

**Named professional anchors**

- ✅ **ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) authorship criteria** — all four
  must be met: (i) substantial contributions to conception or design, or to acquisition, analysis or
  interpretation of data; (ii) drafting the work or reviewing it critically for important
  intellectual content; (iii) final approval of the version to be published; and (iv) **"Agreement
  to be accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy
  or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved."**
- ✅ **ICMJE on AI (verbatim, Recommendations):** "Chatbots (such as ChatGPT) **should not be listed
  as authors because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the
  work**, and these responsibilities are required for authorship… humans are responsible for any
  submitted material that included the use of AI-assisted technologies. **Authors should carefully
  review and edit the result because AI can generate authoritative-sounding output that can be
  incorrect, incomplete, or biased.** Authors should not list AI and AI-assisted technologies as an
  author or co-author, nor cite AI as an author." Authors are directed to **disclose** the use of
  AI-assisted technologies in the cover letter and in the manuscript.
- ⚠️ **UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational
  Institutions) Regulations, 2018** — establishes institutional academic-integrity machinery
  (Academic Integrity Panels, mandatory similarity checks, graded penalties by similarity band).
  ⚠️ **Two cautions.** First, **do not quote the similarity-percentage bands or penalty tiers from
  memory** — verify the gazette text before using any number. Second, the 2018 Regulations address
  **plagiarism/textual similarity**; they do not of themselves define undisclosed generative-AI use
  as an offence, so an answer should argue by analogy and principle, not assert a binding national
  AI rule that has not been verified to exist.
- ⚠️ **UNESCO, *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research* (2023)** — cite at title level
  for the propositions of human-centred use, age-appropriate access, capacity building and
  institutional disclosure policies. ⚠️ Do not quote it; verify wording before attributing any
  sentence to it.
- ⚠️ **In the Indian administrative register**, the analogue is the rule that a file noting, an
  inspection report or a reasoned order is the **officer's own** application of mind; an AI-drafted
  note adopted without verification is a failure of the duty to apply mind, not merely a stylistic
  matter. Route the conduct-rule framing through `16_Codes-of-Ethics-and-Codes-of-Conduct.md`.

**Permissible vs impermissible — the line an examiner is looking for**

| Generally defensible with disclosure | Generally indefensible |
|---|---|
| Literature search, retrieval and de-duplication, with every source read and verified by the author. | Presenting AI-generated text as one's own original composition where originality is what is assessed. |
| Language editing, translation, readability improvement, formatting. | Accepting AI-generated citations, statutes, case names or figures without checking them against the primary source. |
| Code generation that the author reads, tests and can explain line by line. | Listing an AI system as an author, or citing it as one. |
| Generating counter-arguments or outlines the author then independently evaluates. | Uploading confidential case files, personal data or unpublished third-party material into a public tool (a separate **confidentiality** breach — Sec. 11A). |
| Statistical or image analysis using disclosed, validated, reproducible tools. | Using AI to fabricate or "beautify" data, images or results. |
| Drafting routine correspondence from officer-supplied facts, then verified and signed. | Deciding a citizen's entitlement, disciplinary case or evaluation on unverified machine output. |

**Objections and replies**

- *Objection:* "A calculator, a spell-checker and a search engine are also tools; this is moral panic."
  *Reply:* the difference is **generativity and plausibility**. A calculator does not invent a
  citation that looks real. Generative output must be treated as a **hypothesis requiring
  verification**, not as a retrieved fact, and it can substitute for the very reasoning being
  assessed.
- *Objection:* "Disclosure is impractical and stigmatising."
  *Reply:* professional practice already handles this through routine, non-stigmatising
  acknowledgement (statistical support, editorial assistance, funding). A one-line methods
  disclosure normalises use and makes deception, not use, the offence.
- *Objection:* "Detection tools are unreliable, so rules are unenforceable."
  *Reply:* enforceability is better served by **process and viva-style accountability** — requiring
  the author to explain, defend and evidence the work — than by detection arms races. Design
  assessments so that unverifiable substitution has no payoff.
- *Objection:* "Restricting AI disadvantages students without elite writing training."
  *Reply:* a genuine equity point, which argues for **supervised, disclosed, skill-building use**
  and institutional access, not for a silent free-for-all that advantages whoever is most willing
  to conceal.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** frame the issue as answerability, not tool-use -> separate the four
distinct wrongs in one sentence each (misattribution, non-verification, substituted effort,
plagiarism) -> name ICMJE's rule that a chatbot cannot be an author because it cannot be
**accountable** -> give the permissible/impermissible line with two concrete examples -> name the
Indian institutional anchors with the verification caution (UGC 2018 for plagiarism machinery;
conduct-rules duty to apply mind) -> add the equity and confidentiality dimensions in one clause
each -> remedy: mandatory disclosure, author-verification of every claim, assessment redesign
toward defence of work, and institution-level policy -> verdict: **the tool may draft; only a named
human can be answerable, and integrity is the maintenance of that answerability.**

## 11C. Predictive policing, algorithmic discrimination and surveillance proportionality

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section B, Q8 (20 marks, ~250 words):** the case of an AI-enabled
> predictive-policing/surveillance deployment that appears to improve public order while generating
> discriminatory effects. **This section owns the substantive mechanism and the evaluation grid.**
> The eight-element case architecture, the hard-threshold vs weighted-check distinction, and the
> steelman/residual-risk elements remain owned by
> `22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md`; the treaty-law limitation/derogation floor is
> in `12_Corporate-Governance-and-International-Ethics.md` Sec. 11B; the Indian privacy test is in
> Sec. 11A above.

### 11C.1 The five mechanisms that generate the harm

| Mechanism | How it operates | What it defeats |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Feedback loop / dirty data** | The model is trained on arrest, FIR and stop records — artefacts of **past deployment**, not of underlying offending. It predicts more incidents where police already patrolled; patrols intensify; more records are generated; the prediction "verifies" itself. | The claim that the system is evidence-driven. Rising detection in a targeted locality is **not** evidence that the prediction was correct. |
| ⚠️ **Proxy discrimination** | Locality, surname, occupation, migration status, housing type and consumption data reconstruct caste, religion and class even when those fields are excluded. | The "we removed protected attributes" defence. |
| ⚠️ **Base-rate and threshold effects** | Even an accurate classifier applied to a low-prevalence event produces a large majority of false positives, concentrated in the over-sampled group. | "The model is 95% accurate, so it is fair." Accuracy says nothing about the distribution of errors. |
| ⚠️ **Opacity** | Proprietary or complex models yield outputs without intelligible reasons, so neither the officer nor the affected person can identify the operative ground. | Natural justice: the affected person cannot answer a case she cannot see. |
| ⚠️ **Automation bias plus production pressure** | Officers under target pressure accept machine recommendations as authoritative; disagreement costs time and invites second-guessing. | The "human in the loop" safeguard, unless it is deliberately engineered. |

### 11C.2 Place-based vs person-based — the decisive distinction

| | **Place-based** (hot-spot forecasting) | **Person-based** (individual risk scoring/lists) |
|---|---|---|
| What is predicted | Where and when an incident is more likely | Whether a named individual will offend or re-offend |
| Adverse consequence attaches to | An area, in the form of resource allocation | **A person**, in the form of stops, visits, listing, surveillance, opposition to bail |
| Presumption of innocence | Not directly engaged; patrol allocation is an ordinary command decision | **Directly engaged** — a burden is imposed for an act not committed (**anticipatory guilt**) |
| Equality analysis | Risk of spatial proxy discrimination against marginalised settlements | Risk of both proxy discrimination and stigmatic labelling that is self-fulfilling |
| Ethical threshold | Manageable with transparency, rotation, outcome monitoring and a bar on using the output as suspicion | **Very high**; requires independent factual grounds beyond the score, express legal basis, notice and contestation |
| ⚠️ Comparator flag | — | ⚠️ **EU AI Act Article 5(1)(d)** (foreign, illustrative only, not applicable in India) prohibits AI systems "for making risk assessments of natural persons in order to assess or predict the risk of a natural person committing a criminal offence, **based solely on the profiling of a natural person or on assessing their personality traits and characteristics**", while expressly not applying to systems that **support human assessment already based on objective and verifiable facts directly linked to a criminal activity**. Cite as a comparator that names the exact line; never as Indian law. |

⚠️ **The usable rule extracted from that comparator, stated in Indian terms:** an algorithmic score
may **direct attention and allocate resources**; it may never **constitute suspicion**, supply the
grounds for a coercive action, or be recorded as a reason in an order. The officer must be able to
state facts that would have justified the action **without** the score.

### 11C.3 Surveillance proportionality — extending the Sec. 11A ladder

| *Puttaswamy* step | What a surveillance/prediction deployment must show | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|
| **Legality** | An identifiable statutory or rule-based authorisation for *this* collection and *this* use — not a general executive decision or a procurement contract. | "The tender was approved" offered as the legal basis. |
| **Legitimate aim** | Prevention or detection of specified offences, stated in advance. | Aim stated as "public order" at large, which licenses anything. |
| **Rational connection** | Evidence, independent of the system's own outputs, that it reduces the targeted harm. | Self-referential validation: more arrests in the targeted area treated as proof of accuracy. |
| **Necessity / least restrictive means** | Demonstrated inadequacy of less intrusive alternatives (targeted investigation, lighting, beat restructuring, community policing, victim-centred measures). | The technology is adopted first and justified afterwards. |
| **Proportionality *stricto sensu*** | The aggregate intrusion and the distribution of error burdens are justified by the benefit — assessed **group-wise**, not in aggregate. | Benefits accrue to the majority, error costs concentrate on one locality or community. |
| **Procedural safeguards** | Purpose limitation, retention limits, access logs, audit trail, independent oversight, published impact assessment, and a **contestation route**. | Data retained indefinitely; no logs; no complaint mechanism. |

- ⚠️ **DPDP Act, 2023 caution for this case type:** the Act permits the Union Government to exempt
  notified State instrumentalities from its provisions on specified grounds (a s.17(2)(a)-type
  power). ⚠️ Verify the exact provision and the terms of any notification before citing it. The
  ethical consequence to state is precise: **where the statutory data-protection floor can be
  disapplied for State security agencies, the burden of justification shifts onto constitutional
  proportionality and institutional design, and "we complied with DPDP" ceases to be an answer.**
- ⚠️ **Function creep** is the characteristic longitudinal failure: a system authorised for traffic
  management or missing-person tracing is progressively used for protest monitoring, benefit
  verification or immigration checks. The control is **purpose limitation written into the
  authorisation plus logged, reviewed access**, not assurance.

### 11C.4 Contestability architecture (what to actually recommend)

```text
BEFORE DEPLOYMENT
  published purpose + legal basis + retention/access policy
  independent pre-deployment impact assessment (equality + privacy)
  disparate-impact testing on group-wise error rates, not aggregate accuracy
            |
            v
IN OPERATION
  score MAY allocate patrols; score MAY NOT constitute suspicion
  officer records independent grounds for every coercive action
  override rates and acceptance rates BOTH logged and reviewed
  rotation/decay of hot-spot designation to break the feedback loop
            |
            v
AFTER AN ADVERSE OUTCOME
  notice that an automated system was used
  reasons in intelligible, non-technical form
  right to submit counter-evidence
  human re-decision by a different officer
  independent grievance channel + periodic published audit
            |
            v
PERIODIC
  sunset clause + renewal only on evidence of effect
  external audit of disparate impact, function creep and data access
```

⚠️ **Why this is the right recommendation set:** it does not require abandoning the technology, it
attacks each named mechanism directly (rotation breaks the feedback loop; group-wise error testing
exposes proxy discrimination; the "may not constitute suspicion" rule blocks anticipatory guilt;
logged override *and acceptance* rates expose automation bias; sunset and access audit control
function creep), and every element is institutionally implementable.

### 11C.5 Objections, replies and traps

- *Objection:* "It works — crime figures fell. Ethics should not obstruct effectiveness."
  *Reply:* effectiveness is necessary, not sufficient (Sec. 6 trap). Further, the measured fall may
  be **an artefact of the feedback loop**; and an effective instrument of unequal treatment is more
  dangerous than an ineffective one because it scales.
- *Objection:* "Police have always used experience and intuition to allocate patrols; this only
  formalises it."
  *Reply:* three differences change the ethics — **scale** (whole populations rather than a beat),
  **durability** (records persist and follow a person), and **false objectivity** (a number
  launders a discretionary judgement and insulates it from challenge). Formalisation is an
  opportunity for auditability only if audit is actually built in.
- *Objection:* "Over-represented groups in the data really do have higher offending rates."
  *Reply:* the data record **enforcement**, not offending; and even where a differential exists,
  imposing a burden on an individual because of a group statistic is precisely what equality before
  the law forbids. The individual is not the average.
- *Objection:* "Transparency would let offenders game the system."
  *Reply:* graduated disclosure — publish the purpose, legal basis, categories of data, evaluation
  results and audit findings, while withholding operational parameters. Opacity about *whether the
  system discriminates* is never operationally necessary.

❌ **Traps:** treating the whole question as "privacy vs security" and omitting **equality** (the
sharper ground here); recommending "ban AI in policing" (unimplementable and not what the directive
asks); asserting that the EU AI Act binds India; claiming DPDP compliance settles the matter;
proposing "human in the loop" without designing against automation bias; using accuracy figures
without group-wise error breakdown.

**20-mark case integration:** hard thresholds = express legal basis, the bar on a score constituting
suspicion or grounds for a coercive order, and non-discrimination in effect; weighted checks =
necessity, retention, oversight capacity, community trust and cost. Options must include a
**conditional continuation** (place-based only, with the contestability architecture above), not
merely "continue" or "scrap". Residual risk = feedback-loop persistence and function creep;
mitigation = rotation, sunset, external audit and published disparate-impact reporting.

## 11D. Research ethics and bioethics in administration

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q2(a) (10 marks, ~150 words):** the ethical issues in
> conducting research or administering an unproven intervention involving **vulnerable groups**.
> **This section is the owner.** It also supports any Section-B case in which an official is
> pressed to roll out an untested treatment, pilot or technology on a captive population.

**The five foundational instruments (name one or two, never list all five)**

| Instrument | Year | Distinctive contribution |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Nuremberg Code** | 1947 | Voluntary consent of the human subject is **absolutely essential**; the subject must be free to withdraw; the experiment must yield fruitful results unprocurable by other means. |
| ✅ **WMA Declaration of Helsinki** | 1964, as amended by the 75th WMA General Assembly, **Helsinki, October 2024** | The working professional standard: independent ethics-committee review, informed consent, special protection for those in situations of vulnerability, and the strict confinement of unproven interventions (below). |
| ⚠️ **Belmont Report** | 1979 | The three-principle grammar used in almost every syllabus: **respect for persons, beneficence, justice** — with justice understood as the fair distribution of research burdens and benefits. |
| ⚠️ **CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines** | latest revision 2016 | Operational guidance for research in low-resource settings, including the duty of responsiveness to the host community's health needs and post-trial access. |
| ⚠️ **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants** (2017) and the **New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019** | 2017 / 2019 | India's own standard-setting and registration/compensation architecture, including ethics-committee registration and compensation for trial-related injury. ⚠️ Verify rule numbers and compensation formulae before citing them. |

**✅ Verified Helsinki (2024) provisions to quote**

- **Vulnerability (¶¶19-20):** research with individuals, groups or communities "in situations of
  particular vulnerability" is "only justified if it is **responsive to their health needs and
  priorities** and the individual, group, or community stands to **benefit** from the resulting
  knowledge, practices, or interventions"; they should be included only when the research cannot be
  carried out in a less vulnerable group, or when their exclusion would perpetuate or worsen
  disparities.
- **Independent review (¶23):** the protocol must go to a research ethics committee that is
  "transparent in its functioning, must be adequately qualified and independent", must be "able to
  resist undue influence", and must have the right to **monitor, recommend changes to, withdraw
  approval for, and suspend** ongoing research.
- **Consent (¶25):** "individuals capable of giving informed consent **may not be enrolled in
  research unless they freely agree**", even where it is appropriate to consult family members or
  community representatives — community assent does not replace individual consent.
- **Dependent relationships (¶27):** where the potential participant "is in a **dependent
  relationship** with the physician or may consent under duress", consent "must be sought by an
  appropriately qualified individual who is **completely independent** of this relationship".
- **Unproven interventions (¶37) — the decisive provision:** an unproven intervention may be used
  for "an individual patient" only "because approved options are inadequate or ineffective and
  enrollment in a clinical trial is not possible"; the physician "must first seek expert advice,
  weigh possible risks, burdens, and benefits, and obtain informed consent"; and — the line that
  decides most exam fact patterns — "**These interventions must never be undertaken to circumvent
  the protections for research participants set forth in this Declaration.**"

**Comparison chart — four routes, and which one the facts actually describe**

| Route | Trigger | Consent standard | Oversight | Permissible scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Standard care** | Approved intervention, established evidence | Ordinary clinical consent | Professional regulation | Population |
| ✅ **Clinical research** | Knowledge-generating intent | Full informed consent, documented; vulnerability safeguards; independent consent-taker where dependency exists | **Independent ethics committee** with monitoring and suspension powers; registration | Only as per protocol |
| ✅ **Compassionate/unproven use (Helsinki ¶37)** | An **individual** patient; approved options inadequate; trial enrolment not possible | Informed consent **plus** prior expert advice | Case-by-case, recorded | **Individual only — never a programme** |
| ⚠️ **Emergency public-health measure** | Declared emergency; imminent, serious population risk | Statutorily authorised; must still meet proportionality | Statutory authority + review | Population, but time-bound, reviewed, and never research by another name |

⚠️ **The examinable move:** a "pilot" that enrols a captive or dependent population (prisoners,
institutionalised persons, patients dependent on the administering officer, residents of a scheme
whose benefits are conditional) in an untested intervention is **research wearing the clothes of
service delivery**. Helsinki ¶37's final sentence forbids exactly that re-labelling.

**Vulnerability taxonomy (use two or three, with the specific safeguard)**

| Source of vulnerability | Example | Specific safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Capacity-based** | Children; persons with cognitive impairment | Legally authorised representative **plus** the participant's assent; strict risk ceiling |
| ⚠️ **Dependency-based** | Patients of the investigating doctor; employees; students; prisoners | Helsinki ¶27 — consent taken by a person **wholly independent** of the relationship |
| ⚠️ **Deprivation-based** | Persons for whom the trial is the only route to care or to an incidental benefit | Avoid **undue inducement**: reimbursement of costs, never payment that overwhelms deliberation; assured standard of care regardless of participation |
| ⚠️ **Structural/social** | Scheduled Tribes, manual scavengers, trafficked persons, undocumented migrants | Community engagement **and** individual consent; responsiveness to the group's own health priorities (¶¶19-20); local-language, non-technical disclosure with comprehension checks |
| ⚠️ **Situational** | Disaster- or conflict-affected populations | Independent monitoring; no bundling of research participation with relief entitlement |

**Objections and replies**

- *Objection:* "Strict rules delay life-saving research and thereby cost lives."
  *Reply:* correct as a cost, and it is why Helsinki permits individual compassionate use and why
  regulators operate expedited review, rolling submission and adaptive designs. The answer is
  **faster legitimate process**, not suspended process — and a population rollout of an unproven
  intervention that fails produces harm at scale plus a durable collapse of trust that suppresses
  future participation.
- *Objection:* "The community leaders consented on behalf of the village."
  *Reply:* Helsinki ¶25 is explicit — community consultation is appropriate, but individuals capable
  of consent may not be enrolled unless they **freely agree**. Community assent is a precondition,
  never a substitute.
- *Objection:* "Participants are poor; payment is the only realistic way to recruit."
  *Reply:* payment that eliminates the practical option of refusal is **undue inducement**, which
  converts consent into a transaction. Reimburse costs and time; do not price risk.
- *Objection:* "This is a service pilot, not research."
  *Reply:* the test is **intent to generate generalisable knowledge and the use of an unvalidated
  intervention**, not the label on the file. If either is present, ethics-committee review is owed.

❌ **Traps:** treating consent alone as sufficient (independent review, scientific validity, risk
ceiling and justice in participant selection are separate requirements); citing Helsinki as a legal
instrument (it is a professional standard — Indian legal force runs through the NDCT Rules, 2019 and
ICMR guidance); recommending "take community consent" without individual consent; forgetting
**post-trial access** and compensation for research-related injury; framing vulnerability as a
property of persons rather than of **situations and relationships**.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** identify which of the four routes the facts actually describe ->
state the three-principle grammar (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) -> apply the
vulnerability rule: participation justified only if responsive to the group's own needs, with
benefit to them, and only where the research cannot be done in a less vulnerable group -> name the
two structural safeguards (independent ethics-committee review with power to suspend; consent taken
by someone independent of any dependency) -> quote Helsinki ¶37's bar on using compassionate use to
circumvent participant protections -> add the Indian anchors (ICMR 2017, NDCT Rules 2019,
compensation and post-trial access) -> verdict: **urgency changes the speed of review, never its
existence; and the vulnerable are protected by structure, not by good intentions.**

## 12. Selectable named evidence/application units

> Pick 1-2 units per answer; each follows claim -> named evidence/example -> significance -> limitation.

1. *Claim:* Algorithmic accountability requires a human decision-maker to remain answerable.
   *Evidence:* India's AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY, 5 November 2025) "accountability" sutra.
   *Significance:* gives a current, named institutional anchor for "human-in-the-loop" answers.
   *Limitation:* guidelines are advisory, not a binding statute — do not claim India has enacted a
   comprehensive AI law.
2. *Claim:* The precautionary principle justifies proportionate, not absolute, caution. *Evidence:*
   *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) embedding the principle in Indian
   environmental jurisprudence. *Significance:* shows the principle already has domestic judicial
   force, not only an international-declaration status. *Limitation:* proportionate application
   still requires case-specific scientific assessment — it is not a blanket development veto.
3. *Claim:* CBDR anchors India's climate-negotiation position without denying its own mitigation
   responsibility. *Evidence:* UNFCCC Articles 3.1/4.1 and Paris Agreement Article 2(2)'s CBDR-RC
   formulation. *Significance:* supplies a named, defensible framework for equity-based climate
   answers. *Limitation:* CBDR is increasingly contested by developed countries seeking universal
   mitigation commitments — note the framework is a negotiating position, not a settled consensus.
4. *Claim:* Environmental clearance in border areas requires balancing, not a one-sided choice.
   *Evidence:* ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q2(b)'s framing against national security. *Significance:* models a
   genuine multi-value tension rather than a simple pro-environment stance. *Limitation:* the
   "balance" still requires a case-specific, transparent EIA process to be more than a rhetorical
   compromise.
5. *Claim:* Accountability for AI-assisted work cannot be transferred to the tool. *Evidence:* the
   ICMJE rule that chatbots "should not be listed as authors because they cannot be responsible for
   the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the work", coupled with its fourth authorship
   criterion of "agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work" (Sec. 11B).
   *Significance:* converts a vague integrity appeal into a named professional standard with a
   stated reason. *Limitation:* ICMJE governs biomedical journals; extend it to administration by
   analogy and say that you are doing so.
6. *Claim:* An algorithmic prediction may direct attention but may never constitute suspicion.
   *Evidence:* the place-based/person-based distinction and the ⚠️ EU AI Act Article 5(1)(d) line
   between profiling-based risk assessment (prohibited) and support for human assessment "already
   based on objective and verifiable facts directly linked to a criminal activity" (Sec. 11C.2).
   *Significance:* supplies a precise, implementable operating rule instead of a general call for
   caution. *Limitation:* the EU instrument is a foreign comparator with no application in India —
   flag it as illustrative and route the Indian authority through *Puttaswamy* proportionality.
7. *Claim:* An unproven intervention may be used for an individual, never rolled out to a
   population. *Evidence:* Declaration of Helsinki (2024) ¶37, which permits unproven intervention
   for "an individual patient" where approved options are inadequate and trial enrolment is
   impossible, and states that such interventions "must never be undertaken to circumvent the
   protections for research participants set forth in this Declaration" (Sec. 11D).
   *Significance:* decides, in one sentence, any case in which a "pilot" is proposed on a captive
   or dependent population. *Limitation:* Helsinki is a professional standard; Indian legal force
   runs through the NDCT Rules, 2019 and ICMR guidance, so cite the domestic instrument for
   enforceability.
8. *Claim:* Eviction of forest dwellers before their rights are recognised is unlawful, not merely
   unfair. *Evidence:* FRA 2006 s.4(5) — "no member of a forest dwelling Scheduled Tribe or other
   traditional forest dweller shall be evicted or removed from forest land under his occupation till
   the recognition and verification procedure is complete" — read with the Gram Sabha's decisional
   competence affirmed in the *Niyamgiri* case, (2013) 6 SCC 476 (Sec. 14F). *Significance:* moves
   the answer from sympathy to a hard legal threshold. *Limitation:* the bar operates until
   recognition is complete; it is not a permanent veto on acquisition, so the rest of the argument
   must be carried by consent, valuation and rehabilitation-first sequencing.

## 14. Environmental ethics framework: orientations, principles and the Vellore Citizens case

> ⚠️ This section closes the environmental-ethics dimension that Sections 1-13 apply but do not
> name systematically — the three normative *orientations* one can take toward nature, and the
> operative *principles* (sustainable development, intergenerational equity, precaution, polluter-
> pays, environmental justice) GS-IV expects an answer to invoke by name.

### 14A. Three normative orientations toward nature

| Orientation | Core claim | Administrative implication | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Anthropocentric** | Nature has value chiefly/only insofar as it serves human interests (resources, health, aesthetics, future human welfare). | Justifies environmental protection *instrumentally* — e.g., protecting a wetland because it recharges groundwater humans need. | Can under-protect a species/ecosystem with no direct, provable human-use value. |
| ⚠️ **Biocentric** | All living organisms have inherent worth, not merely instrumental value to humans; human interests do not automatically override other species' interests. | Justifies protection of a species *for its own sake* (e.g., an endangered endemic species with no known human-use value). | Can be practically difficult to apply when human survival/livelihood needs directly conflict with a non-human organism's interest. |
| ⚠️ **Ecocentric** | Value attaches to the ecosystem/whole (species, habitats, ecological processes) as an interdependent system, not to individual organisms or humans alone. | Justifies protecting ecological processes and habitats as a unit (e.g., a river's entire flow regime, not just the fish in it). | Can be invoked to block *any* human use of an ecosystem, including sustainable/traditional community use, if applied without proportionality. |

- ⚠️ **Stewardship caution:** a "stewardship" framing (humans as trustees, not owners, of nature —
  echoing Gandhian trusteeship, see `06`) is a workable *administrative* middle path between pure
  anthropocentrism and pure ecocentrism, but it still centres human responsibility/agency — do not
  present it as identical to biocentrism/ecocentrism in an answer that specifically asks the three
  orientations to be distinguished.
- ⚠️ **Deep-ecology caution:** "deep ecology" (Arne Naess's term for a biocentric/ecocentric position
  that rejects anthropocentrism as the root cause of the environmental crisis) is a genuine named
  philosophical position, but a GS-IV answer should present it as *one* school of environmental
  thought, not as the uncontested correct one — India's own constitutional and statutory framework
  (Article 48A/51A(g), the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986) is textually anthropocentric-cum-
  stewardship in structure (protecting the environment "for the sake of" present and future
  generations), not deep-ecological in the strict Naess sense; over-claiming a single orientation as
  "correct" is itself a trap (Sec. 14C).

### 14B. Operative principles

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Sustainable development** | Development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs" (Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future*, 1987) — the standard bridging concept between development and ecological limits. |
| ⚠️ **Intergenerational equity** | The ethical obligation to leave future generations no worse off in their capacity to meet their needs than the present generation — the temporal extension of "public interest" beyond those currently living (see `08` on long-time-horizon consequentialism). |
| ✅ **Precautionary approach/principle** | Where an activity threatens serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty is not a reason to postpone cost-effective preventive measures (Rio Declaration Principle 15, 1992 — see Section 2 above for the "approach" vs "principle" wording caution). |
| ✅ **Polluter-pays principle** | The party causing pollution bears the cost of remedying/compensating the resulting environmental harm, rather than the cost falling on the state/public exchequer or affected citizens by default — applied by the Supreme Court in *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (below) as part of Indian environmental law. |
| ⚠️ **Environmental justice** | The claim that environmental burdens (pollution exposure, displacement, resource loss) and benefits (clean environment, compensation, resettlement) must be distributed fairly, with particular attention to disproportionate burdens on the poor, tribal and marginalised communities who are least able to bear or contest them. |

### 14C. *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* — significance and limits

- ✅ **Citation and holding:** (1996) 5 SCC 647, decided 28 August 1996. Leather tanneries in Tamil
  Nadu's Vellore district discharged untreated effluent into the Palar river, contaminating
  agricultural land and drinking water. The Supreme Court held that **sustainable development**, the
  **precautionary principle** and the **polluter-pays principle** are part of the environmental law
  of India and part of Indian environmental jurisprudence. It directed closure of non-complying
  tanneries pending effluent-treatment compliance and required an authority mechanism under the
  Environment (Protection) Act to assess compensation for ecological damage and affected persons.
- ⚠️ **What it correctly stands for:** it is the leading Indian judicial anchor for treating
  sustainable development, precaution and polluter-pays as *justiciable* legal principles (not
  merely policy aspirations) enforceable via Article 21 and the Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction —
  a genuinely strong, citable precedent for "environmental principles have legal force in India"
  answers.
- ⚠️ **Its limits — do not over-claim:** (a) it is a **specific-industry, specific-river** judgment
  (tannery effluent on the Palar) resolved through case-specific directions and a compensation-
  authority mechanism, not a general environmental code covering every future dispute; (b) its
  practical setting now includes the specialised **National Green Tribunal** (constituted 2010),
  which applies sustainable development, precaution and polluter-pays under NGT Act Section 20;
  the NGT does not displace the Supreme Court's constitutional jurisdiction or erase *Vellore*;
  (c) the judgment's balancing of industry/livelihood against ecological harm
  is itself an anthropocentric-leaning application (protecting human health/agriculture from
  pollution) rather than an ecocentric one — a nuance worth naming if a question specifically tests
  the orientations of Section 14A; (d) it does not, by itself, resolve every precautionary-principle
  application (e.g., novel AI deployment, Section 1-9 above) — it is the domestic *environmental*
  anchor specifically, not a general-purpose precautionary-principle authority for all emerging-
  ethics domains.

### 14D. UPSC traps (environmental ethics)

- ❌ Ecocentrism is the only defensible environmental-ethics position and anthropocentrism is
  simply wrong. -> GS-IV rewards naming the tension between orientations and applying the one the
  scenario calls for, not declaring one orientation universally correct; India's own legal
  framework is predominantly a sustainable-development/stewardship synthesis, not strict deep
  ecology.
- ❌ Sustainable development means environment always yields to development, or always overrides
  it. -> Sustainable development is precisely the attempt to hold *both* present development needs
  and future-generation ecological capacity together — treating it as a one-sided override in
  either direction misstates the Brundtland definition.
- ❌ *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* is a general environmental statute/code. -> It is a Supreme
  Court judgment resolving a specific tannery-pollution dispute that established precaution and
  polluter-pays as part of Indian law; ongoing enforcement now runs substantially through the NGT.
- ❌ The precautionary principle and the polluter-pays principle are the same thing. -> Precaution
  governs *ex ante* decision-making under scientific uncertainty (should the activity proceed at
  all, and with what safeguards); polluter-pays governs *ex post* liability for harm actually
  caused — a strong answer keeps the two analytically separate even when citing the same case for
  both.

### 14E. 10/20-mark architecture integration (environmental ethics)

**10-mark architecture (~150 words):** name the specific principle/orientation the question tests
(precaution, polluter-pays, intergenerational equity, or an anthropocentric/biocentric/ecocentric
framing) -> define it precisely -> cite *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) as the domestic
judicial anchor where relevant, stating its actual holding, not a generalised claim -> one Indian
example/limitation -> one-line conclusion naming the safeguard (NGT enforcement, EIA process, or a
named institutional review).

**20-mark/case-study architecture (~250 words):** facts/stakeholders (including future-generation
and non-human/ecological stakeholders explicitly, per Sec. 14A) -> constraints (scientific
uncertainty, development pressure, livelihood dependence) -> options weighted through the
precautionary and polluter-pays principles, not a blanket "protect environment" or "allow
development" position -> decision naming which orientation/principle was decisive and why -> the
`22`-style implementation/communication and residual-risk elements, with the residual ecological/
compliance risk named explicitly rather than assumed resolved.

## 14F. Displacement, consent and the rights of forest-dwelling communities

> ✅ **Route note — 2026 GS-IV Section A, Q1(b) (10 marks, ~150 words):** the ethical challenges in
> displacing forest-dwelling communities for a development or conservation project. **This section
> is the owner.** Read it with Sec. 14A-14C (orientations toward nature, precaution, sustainable
> development) and with `09_Public-Service-Values-Status-and-Ethical-Dilemmas.md` for the officer's

### 14F.1 The seven-part ethical-challenge taxonomy (the structure that scores)

⚠️ Most answers on displacement produce an undifferentiated list of hardships. Separating the
*kinds* of wrong is what converts description into ethical analysis.

| # | Ethical challenge | What exactly is wronged | Named illustration or anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Consent and agency** | The community is treated as an object of planning rather than an author of the decision affecting its own life. | FPIC; Gram Sabha competence under FRA 2006 and PESA 1996; RFCTLARR consent thresholds. |
| 2 | **Distributive injustice** | Costs (land, habitat, livelihood, burial and sacred sites) fall on one community; benefits (power, minerals, revenue, urban water) accrue to distant others. | The classic "benefit-sharing deficit" of large projects. |
| 3 | **Procedural injustice** | Notice in an unfamiliar language, hearings at inaccessible times and places, technical documents, no independent advice, no appeal that is practically available. | Social Impact Assessment public hearing under RFCTLARR s.4. |
| 4 | **Recognitional injustice** | The community's own valuation of the forest — as ancestral, sacred, medicinal, identity-constituting — is not merely outweighed but **not registered as a value at all**, because compensation is computed only in market terms. | *Niyamgiri*: religious and cultural claims to a sacred hill treated as justiciable community rights, not sentiment. |
| 5 | **Cultural and epistemic loss** | Traditional ecological knowledge, language, seasonal practice and customary institutions do not survive relocation; the loss is irreversible and non-compensable. | Intergenerational and non-instrumental value (Sec. 14A). |
| 6 | **Intergenerational and ecological harm** | Both the displaced community's descendants and the ecosystem's non-human interests are affected by an irreversible change. | Precautionary principle; intergenerational justice (Sec. 2, Sec. 14B). |
| 7 | **Conservation-induced displacement** | The same wrongs committed in the **name of protecting nature** — eviction from a protected area — which is ethically harder because the aim is itself a genuine good and the community is frequently the ecosystem's most effective steward. | FRA's own premise that historical injustice was done to forest dwellers whose rights went unrecorded. |

### 14F.2 The Indian rights architecture — what an officer is actually bound by

- ✅ **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act,
  2006 — s.4(5) (verbatim):** "Save as otherwise provided, **no member of a forest dwelling
  Scheduled Tribe or other traditional forest dweller shall be evicted or removed from forest land
  under his occupation till the recognition and verification procedure is complete.**" ⚠️ Quote it
  exactly; a paraphrase that adds "under this Act" after "procedure" is a misquotation.
- ⚠️ **FRA architecture beyond s.4(5):** s.3(1) recognises both **individual** forest rights (to hold
  and live on forest land for habitation or self-cultivation) and **community** forest rights
  (including rights over minor forest produce, grazing and community forest resources); s.5
  empowers the **Gram Sabha** to protect wildlife, forest, biodiversity, catchment areas and the
  community's cultural and natural heritage; s.6 makes the **Gram Sabha the initiating authority**
  for determining the nature and extent of rights. ⚠️ Cite these at heading level; verify the exact
  wording before quoting a sub-clause.
- ✅ **PESA (Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act), 1996** — mandates **consultation
  with the Gram Sabha or Panchayat at the appropriate level before land acquisition** in Scheduled
  Areas and before resettlement of affected persons. ⚠️ Note the honest distinction: PESA's
  land-acquisition provision is framed as **consultation**, which is weaker than consent — say so
  rather than overstating it.
- ⚠️ **RFCTLARR (Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and
  Resettlement) Act, 2013** — prior consent of at least **80% of affected families** for acquisition
  for private companies and **70%** for public-private-partnership projects, the consent process to
  be carried out **along with the Social Impact Assessment**; a mandatory SIA with public hearing;
  and, in Scheduled Areas, prior consent of the Gram Sabha/Panchayats/Autonomous District Councils.
  ⚠️ Verify the section numbers and any State amendment before citing them; the 80/70 figures should
  be written as statutory thresholds, not as observed practice.
- ⚠️ ***Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment & Forest* (the Niyamgiri case),
  (2013) 6 SCC 476, decided 18 April 2013** — the Supreme Court held that the **Gram Sabhas of the
  affected villages** were the competent forum to decide the community's religious, cultural and
  community forest-rights claims over the Niyamgiri hills, directed that they be convened and their
  decision communicated to the Ministry, which would then take the final decision. ⚠️ All the Gram
  Sabhas consulted subsequently rejected the proposal. **Significance:** it converts consultation
  into a **decision-making competence** located in the community itself, and treats a sacred
  landscape as a legally cognisable interest rather than sentiment. ⚠️ Verify the directions and any
  subsequent proceedings before quoting timelines.
- ⚠️ **UNDRIP (2007)** Articles 10, 19 and 32(2) express the FPIC standard internationally
  (no forcible removal without FPIC; consultation in good faith to obtain FPIC before adopting
  measures or approving projects affecting indigenous lands). ⚠️ It is a **General Assembly
  declaration**, not a treaty; and India's position is that it does not recognise a distinct
  "indigenous peoples" category domestically. Cite it as a normative standard, never as an Indian
  obligation.
- ⚠️ **Constitutional frame:** Article 21 (life and livelihood), Article 244 with the Fifth and Sixth
  Schedules, Articles 15(4) and 46 (protective and promotional duties), and Articles 48A and 51A(g)
  (environmental duties of the State and of citizens).

### 14F.3 Ethical evaluation grid for a displacement proposal

| Test | Question to put to the file | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Necessity** | Is displacement genuinely unavoidable, or is it merely the cheapest alignment? Has a no-displacement or reduced-displacement alternative been costed? | Alternatives analysis absent or pro-forma. |
| ⚠️ **Prior recognition** | Have FRA claims been recognised and verified **before** any eviction step? | Eviction proposed while claims are pending — a direct s.4(5) breach. |
| ⚠️ **Consent competence** | Has the **Gram Sabha** decided, in a properly convened meeting with quorum, adequate notice, local language and a recorded resolution? | A "public meeting" chaired by the proponent or the sub-divisional office substituted for a Gram Sabha resolution. |
| ⚠️ **Completeness of valuation** | Does the compensation register common-property resources, minor forest produce, grazing, sacred sites and the loss of a functioning economy — or only titled land? | Market-value-only computation; landless forest-dependent households invisible. |
| ⚠️ **Restoration, not transfer, of livelihood** | Is the package land-for-land where feasible, with skills, market access and a transition period — or a lump sum? | Cash settlement with no follow-through; documented post-displacement impoverishment. |
| ⚠️ **Sequencing** | Is rehabilitation **completed before** dispossession, or promised after? | "R&R will follow" — the single most common ethical failure in practice. |
| ⚠️ **Benefit-sharing** | Does the affected community share in the project's ongoing benefits (employment, revenue share, electricity, road, water), or only in a one-time payment? | Costs permanent, benefits one-off. |
| ⚠️ **Grievance and reversibility** | Is there an accessible, independent grievance route, and a stop/alter mechanism if the SIA's assumptions fail? | No appeal available in practice. |

### 14F.4 Objections and replies

- *Objection:* "The greater good of millions must outweigh the interests of a few thousand."
  *Reply:* three moves. (i) The aggregate calculation is usually **misspecified** — it prices titled
  land and omits common-property resources, cultural loss and the long tail of impoverishment.
  (ii) Even a correct aggregate does not license **using** a community as a mere means; a
  utilitarian gain does not extinguish a rights claim, it must **satisfy** it through consent,
  compensation and benefit-sharing. (iii) Historical pattern matters: the same communities have
  borne this cost repeatedly, so each new project compounds a **cumulative** injustice.
- *Objection:* "Communities are not competent to assess complex technical projects."
  *Reply:* paternalism of this form has no limit and is empirically weak — Gram Sabhas in *Niyamgiri*
  gave reasoned decisions. The answer to complexity is **independent technical advice, in the local
  language, funded but not controlled by the proponent**.
- *Objection:* "Conservation requires inviolate areas free of human habitation."
  *Reply:* the premise is contested; long-resident communities are often effective stewards, and
  FRA s.5 expressly vests protective duties in the Gram Sabha. Where relocation is genuinely
  necessary for a critical wildlife habitat, the ethical standard is **voluntary, informed,
  fully-compensated relocation with completed rehabilitation first**, not eviction.
- *Objection:* "Consent thresholds make projects impossible."
  *Reply:* they make **badly designed** projects difficult. Consent is also a risk-management
  instrument: projects that acquire genuine consent face fewer stoppages, less litigation and lower
  long-run cost.

❌ **Traps:** treating compensation as the whole of the ethical question (it addresses distribution
only, not consent, recognition or procedure); writing "tribals oppose development" as though the
opposition were irrational; asserting that FRA gives an absolute veto (it bars eviction before
recognition and vests decision competence in the Gram Sabha — argue the specific provision rather
than a slogan); confusing PESA **consultation** with RFCTLARR **consent**; describing UNDRIP as
binding on India; omitting the conservation-induced displacement case, which the word "forest" in
the question usually signals.

**10-mark spine (~150 words):** open by naming the conflict precisely (a legitimate public purpose
against a rights-bearing community, not "development vs environment") -> deploy three or four heads
of the taxonomy (consent, distributive, recognitional, intergenerational) with one line each ->
anchor in law with FRA s.4(5) quoted and the Gram Sabha's competence affirmed in *Niyamgiri* ->
name one honest limitation (PESA is consultation, not consent) -> state the officer's operative
standard: necessity-tested, rights-recognised-first, Gram-Sabha-decided, fully valued including
common-property and cultural loss, rehabilitation **completed before** dispossession, with
continuing benefit-sharing -> verdict: **displacement is ethically survivable only when the affected
community is a co-author of the decision and a co-beneficiary of the project; otherwise it is a
transfer of cost from the powerful to the powerless.**

- ⚠️ **Attribution/context caution:** the phrase commonly rendered "war is the continuation of
  policy [or "politics"] by other means" is a paraphrase of Carl von Clausewitz's *On War*
  (*Vom Kriege*, published posthumously in 1832 by his widow, Marie von Clausewitz); Clausewitz
  wrote in German, and English renderings vary between "policy" and "politics" depending on the
  translator (the standard modern Howard-Paret translation uses "policy") — an answer should treat
  this as a paraphrased translation of a 19th-century Prussian military-theory text, not a verbatim
  English quotation with one fixed canonical wording, and should not present it as an ethics
  treatise in the first instance — it is strategic theory that GS-IV repurposes for an ethics
  question about the *justification* of war as an instrument of state policy.
- ⚠️ **What an ethics answer must add beyond the attribution:** Clausewitz's claim is a
  *descriptive/strategic* one (war is an extension of political intercourse, not a UN-Charter-era
  ethical claim about when war is justified) — a GS-IV answer should pair it with the *normative*
  Just War framework (sovereignty/*jus ad bellum*, civilian protection, proportionality,
  humanitarian neutrality) fully set out in `12`'s Section 11, rather than treating the quotation
  alone as a complete ethical position. This file's contribution is the attribution caution and this
  explicit cross-link; `12` remains the substantive owner for the ethical-framework content the
  question requires.
