**Core proposition:** ✅ ARC 2.2.6 — a Code of Ethics covers "broad guiding principles," while a
Code of Conduct must "in a precise and unambiguous manner, stipulate a list of acceptable and
unacceptable behaviour and action" — the two are complementary, not interchangeable.

## 2. Essential definitions

| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Code of Ethics** | Aspirational, principle-based standard (e.g., the seven Nolan principles) guiding *how* an official should aspire to act, without necessarily specifying sanctions. |
| ✅ **Code of Conduct** | Precise, rule-based, sanctionable list of specific dos and don'ts (e.g., gift limits, business-interest restrictions, disclosure deadlines). |
| ✅ **Ministers' Code of Conduct (India)** | Existing Government of India code (ARC 2.4.2) covering asset/liability disclosure, divestment from business interests, restrictions on gifts (symbolic gifts retained; non-symbolic gifts above ₹5,000 referred to the Toshakhana), and rules on foreign employment of family members. |
| ✅ **Rajya Sabha/Lok Sabha Committees on Ethics** | Parliamentary bodies overseeing legislators' "moral and ethical conduct," maintaining a Register of Members' Interests and recommending sanctions for breaches (ARC 2.5.2-2.5.3). |
| ✅ **Civil servants' Code of Conduct (CCS/AIS Conduct Rules)** | A third, statutory tier distinct from the Ministers'/legislators' codes above: the **Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964** (for central-government employees) and the **All India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1968** (for IAS/IPS/IFoS officers) — precise, sanctionable rules enforced through the CCS (CCA) Rules disciplinary machinery, not merely aspirational. ⚠️ Exact rule-number citations should be verified against the current DoPT/state-cadre-authority consolidated text before use in a Mains answer, since both sets of rules have been amended multiple times (notably 2014). |

## 3. Mechanism: how India's dual-code architecture is meant to work

1. **Code of Ethics** should be based on the Nolan-type seven principles (see `09`) and cover the
   Minister-civil servant relationship in broad terms (ARC 2.4.3).
2. **Code of Conduct** operationalises the aspirational principles into specific, checkable rules —
   e.g., ARC 2.4.2's Ministers' Code requires disclosure of assets/liabilities to the PM/CM, divestment
   from businesses supplying goods/services to government, and restrictions on accepting valuable
   gifts.
3. ✅ ARC 2.4.4-2.4.5's recommendations: create a Code of Ethics *in addition to* the existing Code
   of Conduct; set up dedicated monitoring units in the PM's/CM's offices, empowered to receive
   public complaints; make the PM/CM duty-bound to ensure observance, "even in the case of coalition
   governments where the Ministers may belong to different parties"; submit an annual report on
   observance — with specific violations and action taken — to the appropriate legislature; and put
   the Code of Ethics, the Code of Conduct and the annual report in the public domain.
4. **Legislators** have their own parallel structure: the Rajya Sabha's Code of Conduct (ARC
   2.5.2.2) and the Lok Sabha Ethics Committee's general ethical principles (ARC 2.5.3.1) both
   emphasise subordinating private interest to public trust, non-acceptance of fees/gifts for
   parliamentary acts, and maintaining high standards of morality, dignity and decency.
5. ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q5(a) confirms this dual-code gap persists in the current exam's own framing: Code
   of Conduct "already in operation," Code of Ethics "not yet put in place" — asking candidates to
   "suggest a suitable model for code of ethics."
6. **Civil servants have their own statutory Code of Conduct, running in parallel to the
   Ministers'/legislators' codes above.** ✅ Rule 3 of the **CCS (Conduct) Rules, 1964** and the
   substantively identical Rule 3 of the **AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968** require every government
   servant to "at all times maintain absolute integrity and devotion to duty" and "do nothing which
   is unbecoming of a Government servant." Amendments since 2014 (⚠️ verify the exact rule
   sub-clause before quoting numbers in an answer) added explicit requirements to maintain **high
   ethical standards, political neutrality, accountability and transparency, responsiveness to the
   public** (especially weaker sections), and to take decisions "solely in public interest" while
   avoiding any private interest that conflicts, or appears to conflict, with official duty.
   ✅ Rule 3(3) additionally requires that oral directions from a superior ordinarily **not** be
   acted upon unless confirmed in writing, and obliges the government servant who receives an oral
   instruction to seek written confirmation as soon as possible — the superior is then duty-bound to
   confirm it in writing; this is the standard, current-rule-grounded response to "my superior gave
   me a verbal order to do something improper" case-study prompts (compare `21`'s honest-official
   protections and `09`'s dilemma framing).
7. **A Code of Ethics for civil servants must be built as a distinct, aspirational layer above
   these enforceable Conduct Rules — not a restatement of them.** ⚠️ A defensible model: (a) anchor
   it in the seven Nolan principles (`09`) and the constitutional oath, stated as *dispositions*
   ("I aspire to...") rather than sanctionable clauses; (b) make it civil-service-specific by adding
   duties the Conduct Rules do not fully capture in aspirational language — courage in tendering
   unwelcome advice, empathy toward the vulnerable citizen, and stewardship of public resources for
   future generations; (c) keep it non-justiciable and complementary, exactly as ARC 2.4.4-2.4.5
   recommends for the Ministers' Code of Ethics — with the CCS/AIS Conduct Rules and CCS (CCA)
   Rules remaining the separate, sanctionable enforcement layer for actual breaches.

## 4. Indian applications and examples

- ✅ ARC 2.4.2's gift rule: symbolic gifts (sword of honour, ceremonial robes) may be retained;
  non-symbolic gifts valued over ₹5,000 must be assessed by the Toshakhana, with the Minister able
  to purchase the item by paying the difference above ₹5,000.
- ✅ ARC 2.5.2.2's Rajya Sabha Code of Conduct requires Members to resolve any conflict between
  personal interest and public trust by subordinating the former, and forbids accepting fees for
  votes, bills, resolutions or questions.
- ✅ The Mudgal case (1951) and the "cash-for-questions" case (2005) — both examined in `23` —
  illustrate the Code of Conduct's *enforcement* dimension (ten Lok Sabha Members and one Rajya
  Sabha Member expelled in the 2005 case), distinct from the aspirational Code of Ethics.
- ✅ The CCS/AIS Conduct Rules' conflict-of-interest architecture, at a safe conceptual level (⚠️
  cite the current consolidated rule text before quoting a specific sub-rule number in an answer):
  gifts from a person/firm having official dealings with the government servant must ordinarily be
  refused, and acceptance beyond a modest, prescribed threshold requires reporting to/sanction from
  the prescribed authority; engaging in private trade, employment or investment that could conflict
  with official duty requires prior permission; and a government servant with a private interest
  (e.g., a relative bidding for a contract they administer) is expected to declare it and recuse
  from the decision rather than participate — the same disclose-and-recuse logic examined in `22`'s
  conflict-of-interest case studies (e.g., 2024 GS-IV Q10, Sneha's brother's procurement bid).

### Model Code of Ethics for civil servants — aspirational, not a restatement of the Conduct Rules

| Layer | Nature | Example content |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Code of Ethics (proposed)** | Aspirational, non-justiciable, principle-based | Nolan's seven principles (`09`); constitutional oath; "public office as public trust"; courage to render unwelcome advice; empathy for the vulnerable; inter-generational stewardship of public resources |
| ✅ **CCS/AIS Conduct Rules (existing)** | Enforceable, sanctionable, rule-based | Rule 3's integrity/devotion-to-duty/political-neutrality obligations; Rule 3(3) oral-instruction safeguard; gift/private-interest/conflict-of-interest restrictions; enforced via CCS (CCA) Rules disciplinary proceedings |

⚠️ The two layers must stay distinct: collapsing the aspirational Code of Ethics into a mere
restatement of the enforceable Conduct Rules (or vice versa) reproduces exactly the confusion ARC
2.2.6 warns against for Ministers, now at the civil-servant tier.

## 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ ARC 2.2.6: Code of Ethics = broad principles; Code of Conduct = precise, sanctionable rules.
- ✅ The Rajya Sabha's Committee on Ethics was first constituted by the Chairman on **4 March 1997**
  (ARC 2.5.2.1); the Lok Sabha's Committee on Ethics is recorded by the ARC (2.5.3.1) from the
  Thirteenth Lok Sabha onward. ⚠️ ARC 2.5.3.2 notes that, as per available information, only a few
  State Legislatures — Andhra Pradesh among them — had then adopted comparable arrangements.
- ✅ ARC 2.4.2's Ministers' Code of Conduct fixes the Toshakhana gift-valuation threshold at ₹5,000:
  a gift below that value may be retained; above it, the Minister may purchase the item from the
  Toshakhana by paying the difference over ₹5,000.
- ⚠️ India, per the ARC's 2007 diagnosis and the 2025 GS-IV Q5(a) framing, still lacks a
  comprehensive Code of Ethics for Ministers, even though a Code of Conduct exists.
- ✅ Rule 3 of the CCS (Conduct) Rules, 1964/AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968 requires "absolute integrity
  and devotion to duty"; post-2014 amendments add political neutrality, accountability,
  transparency and public responsiveness as explicit obligations for every civil servant.
- ✅ Rule 3(3)-type provisions require a government servant who receives an oral direction to seek
  written confirmation as soon as possible, and place a corresponding duty on the superior to
  confirm it in writing — the standard, rule-grounded answer to an "improper verbal order" prompt.

## 6. UPSC traps

- ❌ Code of Ethics and Code of Conduct are simply two names for the same document. -> ARC 2.2.6
  explicitly distinguishes them by function: aspirational principle vs precise, sanctionable rule.
- ❌ India has neither a Code of Conduct nor a Code of Ethics for Ministers. -> A Code of Conduct
  for Ministers already exists (ARC 2.4.2); it is the Code of Ethics that remains largely
  unimplemented, per both the ARC (2007) and the 2025 GS-IV paper's own framing.
- ❌ Parliamentary Ethics Committees can unilaterally punish a Member without House approval. -> The
  Committees recommend sanctions (e.g., expulsion); the House itself must accept/adopt the
  recommendation for it to take effect (ARC 2.5.7.2-2.5.7.3).
- ❌ India has no enforceable Code of Conduct at all for civil servants, only for Ministers and
  legislators. -> The CCS (Conduct) Rules, 1964 and AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968 are a third,
  independently enforceable statutory Code of Conduct tier for civil servants, backed by CCS (CCA)
  Rules disciplinary proceedings — the implementation gap the ARC and 2025 GS-IV Q5(a) identify is
  specifically the missing aspirational Code of Ethics, not an absence of any conduct rules.
- ❌ A civil servant must instantly obey any oral instruction from a superior, proper or improper.
  -> Rule 3(3)-type provisions entitle (and in effect require) the government servant to seek
  written confirmation of an oral direction, placing a reciprocal duty on the superior to confirm
  it — this is the documented, current-rule response, not resignation or silent compliance.

## 7. PYQ application

- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q5(a): "Code of Conduct... already in operation... Code of Ethics is not yet put in
  place. Suggest a suitable model for code of ethics to maintain integrity, probity and transparency
  in governance" — the single most directly source-matching PYQ for this topic.
- ⚠️ Any question on parliamentary conduct/discipline should reference the Rajya Sabha/Lok Sabha
  Ethics Committees and cite a concrete precedent (Mudgal, cash-for-questions — see `23`).

## 8. Mains angles

- ⚠️ For 2025 GS-IV Q5(a)-type questions, propose a Code of Ethics model explicitly built on the
  seven Nolan principles (`09`), covering the Minister-civil-servant relationship, with a dedicated
  monitoring unit and annual public reporting — precisely the ARC's own 2.4.5 recommendation,
  updated for current institutional context.
- ⚠️ Always state clearly which existing Indian Code of Conduct provisions already exist, before
  proposing new Code of Ethics elements — this shows awareness of the current implementation gap
  rather than reinventing an already-existing wheel.

> **Answer thesis:** India already has enforceable Codes of Conduct at all three tiers — Ministers
> (ARC 2.4.2), legislators (Rajya Sabha/Lok Sabha Ethics Committees) and civil servants (CCS/AIS
> Conduct Rules) — but a shared, aspirational Code of Ethics built on Nolan-type principles, which
> the ARC recommended in 2007 as the complement to each of these rule-based codes, remains
> substantially unimplemented, a persisting and currently examined gap.
