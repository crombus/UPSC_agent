## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — constitutional deadlock pushed nationalism from dominion status to complete independence.**

- ✅ The **Simon Commission** was appointed in **1927** and reached India in **1928** without any Indian member.
- ✅ The boycott slogan **"Simon Go Back"** turned the Commission into a nationwide anti-imperialist issue across parties and regions.
- ✅ **Lala Lajpat Rai** was injured in the Lahore lathi-charge and died in **November 1928**.
- ✅ The **Nehru Report (1928)**, prepared by a committee chaired by **Motilal Nehru**, proposed dominion status, fundamental rights and joint electorates.
- ✅ **Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929)** rejected the Nehru Report; Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Bose demanded full independence.

**Foundation — Civil Disobedience converted Purna Swaraj into mass action.**

- ✅ The **Lahore Congress (December 1929)** adopted **Purna Swaraj**; Jawaharlal Nehru presided and the tricolour was hoisted at Ravi.
- ✅ **26 January 1930** was observed as the first nationalist **Independence Day**.
- ✅ Gandhi launched the **Dandi Salt March** on **12 March 1930** and broke the salt law at Dandi on **6 April 1930**.
- ✅ CDM spread through salt satyagraha, foreign cloth/liquor boycott, no-tax campaigns, forest-law defiance, Dharasana, Peshawar and Sholapur.
- ✅ The **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931)** suspended CDM and enabled Congress participation in the Second Round Table Conference.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Radicalisation-by-exclusion thesis:** *The all-white Simon Commission and the failure of the Nehru Report's compromise together destroyed the dominion-status middle ground, so that Purna Swaraj became not a radical faction's demand but the only remaining Congress position.*
- **Symbol thesis:** *Salt was chosen because it was the one colonial exaction that touched every Indian irrespective of class, region, caste or gender, and because breaking the salt law was simultaneously trivial in economic effect and total in symbolic meaning — an offence anyone could commit and no government could ignore.*
- **Qualified-success thesis:** *Civil Disobedience did not win independence or major constitutional concession, but it achieved what the RTCs proved: no constitutional settlement for India could be made without the Congress, and Britain now had to negotiate rather than merely legislate.*
- **Ambivalent-settlement thesis:** *The Poona Pact preserved the political unity of the Hindu social order at the cost of denying the Depressed Classes the separate political voice they had been granted, and both the preservation and the cost are real; the Pact's reserved seats were a compromise, not a resolution.*

### 8.4 Bank A — Constitutional deadlock, 1927–29

- **Claim:** Each British move produced a nationalist counter-move that narrowed the space for compromise.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Simon Commission** was appointed in **1927** and arrived in **1928** with **no Indian member**, producing the all-party boycott and the slogan **"Simon Go Back"**; ✅ **Lala Lajpat Rai** died in **November 1928** after the Lahore lathi-charge; ✅ the **Nehru Report (1928)**, from a committee chaired by **Motilal Nehru**, proposed **dominion status, fundamental rights and joint electorates**; ✅ **Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929)** rejected it and demanded minority safeguards, while **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Bose** demanded full independence.
- **Significance:** The Nehru Report is the pivot — it was the nationalist movement's own constitution-making attempt, and its failure to satisfy either the Muslim League or the Congress left shows that the communal and the radical challenges arrived simultaneously.
- **Limit/caution:** The Nehru Report proposed **dominion status**, not independence; do not conflate it with Purna Swaraj.

### 8.5 Bank B — Purna Swaraj and the salt symbol

- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Lahore Congress (December 1929)** adopted **Purna Swaraj** with **Jawaharlal Nehru** presiding and the tricolour hoisted at the Ravi; ✅ **26 January 1930** was observed as the first nationalist **Independence Day**; ✅ Gandhi launched the **Dandi Salt March on 12 March 1930**, walking with **78 chosen followers** nearly 200 miles from **Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi**, and broke the salt law on **6 April 1930**.
- **Gandhi's own framing (repository-verified):** ✅ He declared that British rule had brought "moral, material, cultural and spiritual ruination" to the country, that he regarded that rule as a curse, that he was out to destroy that system of government, and that **"Sedition has become my religion. Ours is a non-violent battle."** — ⚠️ **this is the correct location of that statement**, which is sometimes wrongly associated with the Non-Cooperation period (see the correction note in `basic/20`).
- **Significance:** The choice of salt converted a constitutional demand into an act that any Indian could personally perform, which is why participation extended so far beyond the Congress's organised membership.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not extend the "78 followers" figure to the wider march, which grew continuously; and do not describe salt manufacture as economically significant — its power was symbolic and legal.

### 8.6 Bank C — Forms, geography and participants of CDM

| Form of defiance | Where it was strongest | Analytical point |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Salt satyagraha | Coastal Gujarat, Bombay, Bengal, Madras coast | Universal, low-cost, legally clean act of defiance |
| ✅ Boycott and picketing of foreign cloth and liquor | Urban centres across India | ✅ Women participated on a wide scale, leaving domestic seclusion, picketing shops and marching in processions |
| ✅ No-tax and no-rent campaigns | Gujarat, United Provinces | Directly attacked colonial revenue — the most threatening form |
| ✅ Forest-law defiance | Central Provinces, Maharashtra, tribal tracts | Linked colonial ecological restriction to national struggle |
| ✅ Dharasana, Peshawar, Sholapur | Gujarat, NWFP, Bombay Presidency | Repression made visible; Sholapur saw near-collapse of authority |
| ✅ NWFP mobilisation | Frontier province | ✅ **Khudai Khidmatgars ("Servants of God"), the Red Shirts** — so called from the colour of their shirts — under **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan**, "the Frontier Gandhi", pledged to non-violence — the movement's deepest penetration of a Muslim-majority region |
| ✅ Army disaffection | Peshawar | ✅ **Two platoons of Garhwali soldiers refused to fire on demonstrators**, facing court martial and long imprisonment — evidence that nationalism was reaching the army, the regime's chief instrument |

**Why the Frontier in 1930 and not in 1920–22 (a standard "why here, why now" demand):**
- ✅ **Prior organisational work, not sudden conversion.** Bipan Chandra records that **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan "had been active for several years in the area, and it was his mass work which lay behind the formation of the band of non-violent revolutionaries, the Khudai Khidmatgars"**. The organisation that carried the Frontier in 1930 simply did not exist during Non-Cooperation; the Khilafat appeal of 1920–22 had reached the province as a sentiment, not as a structure.
- ✅ **A local material grievance to attach the national campaign to.** Government repression in the province was directed against the Khudai Khidmatgars **and the peasants they led against the Government's policy of extracting revenue through cruel methods and torture** — so Civil Disobedience in the NWFP had an agrarian content that the 1920–22 mobilisation lacked.
- ✅ **A demonstrable tipping event.** On **23 April 1930** the arrest of Congress leaders in the NWFP produced "a mass demonstration of unprecedented magnitude in Peshawar", during which **the city was virtually in the hands of the crowd for more than a week**, and it was there that the Garhwali soldiers refused to fire.
- ✅ **The state treated it as the most serious theatre.** **Ghaffar Khan was arrested on 24 December 1931 and Peshawar district was occupied by the army** — a level of coercion applied to no other province.
- ⚠️ **Analytical point:** the Frontier case shows that mass movements penetrate where a *local* organisation with a *local* grievance already exists to receive the national call. The same logic explains Bardoli in 1928 and Awadh in 1921 (`basic/20`, `basic/23`).
- ⚠️ **Caution:** do not attribute the Frontier's participation to religious identity or to "Pathan martial character"; the verified explanation is organisational and agrarian.

- **Analytical use:** the Garhwali refusal and the Khudai Khidmatgars are the two highest-value evidence units in this bank, because they answer the "how deep did it go?" question with specific, verified facts rather than adjectives.

### 8.7 Bank D — Negotiation: Gandhi–Irwin and the Round Table Conferences (routed demand)

- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Gandhi–Irwin Pact was signed on 5 March 1931**; under it Congress agreed to **suspend Civil Disobedience** and to **participate in the Second Round Table Conference**, while the government undertook to withdraw the emergency ordinances and release political prisoners not convicted of violence, with concessions on the making of salt for personal use in coastal areas and on peaceful picketing.
- **What was not conceded:** ⚠️ the Congress demand for a public inquiry into police excesses was refused, and ✅ the Pact did not save **Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru**, executed on 23 March 1931 — a fact that made the Pact deeply unpopular with the movement's radical wing.
- **RTC sequence:** ✅ **First RTC (1930)** — Congress absent; **Second RTC (1931)** — Gandhi attended as the sole Congress representative; ✅ **Third RTC (November–December 1932)** was "largely formal and unimportant", with **only 46 of 112 delegates attending**; ⚠️ a **White Paper in March 1933** carried the process toward the 1935 Act (`basic/24`).
- **Significance:** The RTC sequence is the strongest evidence for the qualified-success thesis: Britain convened a conference without the Congress and achieved nothing, convened one with it and still conceded nothing, and then legislated unilaterally — proving both the Congress's indispensability and the limits of negotiation without leverage.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not list Gandhi–Irwin terms beyond those above without verified support; the analytically important point is the asymmetry — Congress suspended a movement in exchange for procedural undertakings.

### 8.8 Bank E — Communal Award, Poona Pact and the Harijan turn (routed demand)

- **Evidence:** ✅ Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald announced the Communal Award in August 1932**; it apportioned representation among communities and **extended separate electorates to the untouchables**. ✅ Gandhi, then in **Yeravda jail**, saw in it a motive to divide Hindu society, arguing that separate electorates would politically separate the untouchables and permanently block their integration, and **decided to fast unto death** to reverse the arrangement. ✅ Some Depressed Classes leaders such as **M.C. Rajah** favoured joint electorates, but the most influential, **Dr B.R. Ambedkar**, saw separate electorates as the only hope of securing political representation for the untouchables. ✅ Gandhi, though opposed to separate electorates, **was not averse to reserved seats**, and Ambedkar ultimately agreed once **the number of reserved seats was increased and a two-tier election system was recommended**. ✅ This became the basis of the **Poona Pact of September 1932**, which the government subsequently accepted.
- **The Harijan turn (routed demand — "event after which Gandhi took up Harijan upliftment"):** ⚠️ It was **after this Communal Award crisis and the Poona Pact settlement of September 1932** that the removal of untouchability moved from being one item of the constructive programme to the central preoccupation of Gandhi's public work; ✅ the weekly **Harijan** subsequently became, alongside *Young India*, one of his principal communication vehicles.
- **Significance:** The episode is the clearest instance in which a constitutional device forced a social question to the centre of national politics, and it permanently defined the terms — reservation within joint electorates — on which Indian representation for Scheduled Castes has operated since.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ Do not present the Pact as a meeting of minds. Ambedkar's agreement was made under the pressure of a fast that risked mass reprisal; state the coercive context honestly and note that the disagreement over political separateness persisted. ⚠️ Do not name the anti-untouchability organisation Gandhi founded or its date without verified support.

### 8.9 Comparative engine — NCM (1920–22) versus CDM (1930–34)

| Dimension | Non-Cooperation | Civil Disobedience |
|---|---|---|
| Goal stated | Swaraj within a year; Khilafat redress | **Purna Swaraj** |
| Core method | Withdrawal of cooperation (boycott) | **Breaking the law** (salt, forest, tax) |
| Muslim participation | High, via Khilafat | Much lower, except NWFP under the Khudai Khidmatgars |
| Women's role | Present | ✅ Wide and prominent — picketing, processions, satyagraha |
| Government response | Relatively restrained until 1921–22 | Mass arrests, ordinances, sustained repression |
| Ending | Unilateral withdrawal after Chauri Chaura | Negotiated suspension (1931), resumption, withdrawal (1934) |

### 8.10 Counter-evidence, balance and variation

- **Successes to state:** all-India spread, deep rural penetration, unprecedented women's participation, penetration of the NWFP and evidence of army disaffection, and the demonstrated necessity of Congress in any settlement.
- **Limits to state:** no constitutional gain proportionate to the sacrifice; Muslim participation outside NWFP was limited; business support was mixed; and the movement was suspended, resumed and finally wound down without a decisive victory.
- **Regional variation:** Gujarat's no-tax campaign, Bengal's revolutionary overlap, the Frontier's Khudai Khidmatgars, the Central Provinces' forest satyagraha and Sholapur's near-insurrection are five different movements under one name.
- ⚠️ Business and capitalist support was ambivalent — traders and cloth importers contributed most to boycott while mill-owners were cautious; do not present Indian capital as uniformly behind the movement.

### 8.11 Verdict scaffolds

- **Deadlock verdict:** "Simon and the Nehru Report between them destroyed the dominion-status compromise from opposite directions — Britain by excluding Indians, and Indian politics by failing to agree among themselves."
- **CDM verdict:** "Civil Disobedience won no constitution and settled one question permanently: after 1931 the British could still refuse the Congress, and they could no longer ignore it."
- **Poona Pact verdict:** "The Pact secured joint electorates and conceded reserved seats — preserving a single Hindu political community at the price of the separate political voice the Award had recognised."

### 8.12 Factual-risk cautions

- Simon appointed **1927**, arrived **1928**; Lajpat Rai died **November 1928**; Nehru Report **1928**; Fourteen Points **1929**.
- The Nehru Report proposed **dominion status**, not Purna Swaraj.
- Lahore Congress **December 1929**; Independence Day pledge **26 January 1930**; Dandi March **12 March – 6 April 1930**.
- Congress was **absent** from the First RTC; Gandhi attended the **Second**; the **Third** saw only **46 of 112** delegates attend.
- Gandhi–Irwin Pact = **5 March 1931**; it did **not** prevent the executions of **23 March 1931**.
- The **Communal Award (August 1932)** granted separate electorates to the Depressed Classes; the **Poona Pact (September 1932)** replaced them with **reserved seats in joint electorates**.
- Do not attribute the Poona Pact's seat numbers or the exact terms of the two-tier election system without verified support.
