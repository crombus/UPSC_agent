## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — the Company changed from merchant to ruler.**

- ✅ After **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)**, the East India Company was no longer only a trading body; with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)** it controlled revenue and had to be regulated as a political power.
- ✅ The early constitutional acts were Parliament's response to Company misrule, financial crisis and the need to make a private corporation answerable for governing Indian territories.
- ⚠️ The period from **1773 to 1853** is best read as a constitutional ladder: each act corrected defects of the previous one while increasing Crown/Parliamentary control and centralising Indian administration.
- ✅ The **Regulating Act, 1773** made the Governor of Bengal the **Governor-General of Bengal**; **Warren Hastings** became the first holder of this office.

**Foundation — centralisation and law-making grew step by step.**

- ✅ The **Regulating Act** placed Bombay and Madras under Bengal in matters of war and peace, created a Council of four, and set up the **Supreme Court at Calcutta**.
- ✅ **Pitt's India Act, 1784** introduced a Board of Control representing the British Crown while the Company Court of Directors retained commercial administration — a classic system of **dual control**.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1833** converted the Governor-General of Bengal into the **Governor-General of India**; **William Bentinck** was the first Governor-General of India.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1853** separated legislative from executive work in the Governor-General's Council and introduced open competitive recruitment for the civil service.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ **1773** created the office of **Governor-General of Bengal**, not Governor-General of India.
- ✅ **Warren Hastings** was the first Governor-General of Bengal under the Regulating Act.
- ✅ The **Supreme Court at Calcutta** was established in **1774**.
- ✅ **Pitt's India Act, 1784** created the **Board of Control** and began Crown supervision through dual control.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1813** ended the Company's Indian trade monopoly except **tea and China trade**.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1813** sanctioned **₹1 lakh** annually for education and allowed Christian missionaries.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1833** made **William Bentinck** the first **Governor-General of India**.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1833** added a **Law Member** to the Council; **Macaulay** became the first Law Member.
- ✅ The **Charter Act, 1853** introduced open competitive examination for civil service recruitment.
- ⚠️ The acts show a shift from Company autonomy to parliamentary sovereignty and eventual Crown rule in 1858.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: **Governor-General of Bengal (1773)** is not the same as **Governor-General of India (1833)**.

- ❌ Regulating Act made Warren Hastings Governor-General of India. → ✅ It made him **Governor-General of Bengal**.
- ❌ Pitt's India Act ended Company rule. → ✅ It created **dual control**; Company rule continued.
- ❌ Charter Act 1813 ended all Company monopoly. → ✅ It ended Indian trade monopoly but kept **tea and China trade**.
- ❌ Charter Act 1833 introduced competitive civil service exam. → ✅ It spoke of open recruitment; the **1853 Act** introduced competition.
- ❌ Supreme Court at Calcutta was created by Pitt's India Act. → ✅ It was created under the **Regulating Act framework** and began in **1774**.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Accountability-under-pressure thesis:** *Parliament regulated the Company not to protect Indians but to control a corporation whose Indian revenue, patronage and debts had become a British political and fiscal problem; Indian benefit, where it occurred, was incidental.*
- **Centralisation thesis:** *Between 1773 and 1853 the direction of travel is unambiguous — from three presidencies to one Government of India, from Company autonomy to parliamentary sovereignty, and from personal discretion to codified law.*
- **Free-trade thesis:** *The constitutional acts are unintelligible without British commercial interest: 1813 and 1833 dismantled a chartered monopoly precisely when British industry needed an open Indian market.*
- **Foundation thesis:** *The pre-1858 acts created the durable machinery of British India — a supreme executive, a legislating council, a codifying law commission and a competitively recruited service — which the Crown inherited in 1858 rather than replaced.*

### 8.4 Named evidence units (claim → evidence → significance → limit)

**A. Regulating Act, 1773 — the accountability opening**
- **Claim:** The first intervention treated Company misgovernment as a British constitutional problem.
- **Evidence:** It created the office of **Governor-General of Bengal** (first holder Warren Hastings), a Council of four, subordinated Bombay and Madras in matters of war and peace, and provided the framework under which the **Supreme Court at Calcutta** began work in 1774.
- **Significance:** It established the two principles that all later acts elaborated — a single supreme Indian executive and an external check on it.
- **Limit/caution:** It worked badly in practice; the Council could outvote the Governor-General and Supreme Court jurisdiction clashed with Company authority, which the Amending Act of 1781 tried to settle.

**B. Pitt's India Act, 1784 — dual control**
- **Claim:** Britain chose supervision over takeover.
- **Evidence:** A **Board of Control** representing the Crown was placed over political and revenue matters while the **Court of Directors** retained commercial administration and patronage.
- **Significance:** This is the classic case of divided sovereignty — the Crown governed policy without owning the liabilities, and the Company kept profit and patronage without final political authority.
- **Limit/caution:** Dual control did not end Company rule; answers that say it did are wrong.

**C. Charter Act, 1813 — the free-trade turn (routed Prelims demand)**
- **Claim:** 1813 marks the point at which British industrial interest overrode chartered monopoly.
- **Evidence:** ✅ It ended the Company's monopoly of Indian trade **except for tea and the China trade**; it sanctioned **₹1 lakh annually** for education and the encouragement of learning; and it permitted Christian missionaries to enter Company territory. It also reasserted Crown sovereignty over the Company's Indian possessions.
- **Significance:** Each provision opened India further — to British manufactures, to English-language education and to missionary activity — so 1813 is simultaneously an economic, cultural and constitutional turning point and the hinge between this owner and both `basic/07` and `basic/09`.
- **Limit/caution:** The ₹1 lakh was an annual sanction, modest in scale, and its purpose was contested between Orientalists and Anglicists until 1835; do not present it as the founding of modern Indian education.

**D. Charter Act, 1833 — all-India government and codification (routed Prelims demand)**
- **Claim:** 1833 created the Government of India as a single legislating authority.
- **Evidence:** The **Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India** — **William Bentinck** being the first — a **Law Member** was added to the Council with **Macaulay** as the first holder, a **Law Commission** was provided for, and the Company ceased to be a trading body in India, becoming a purely administrative agency.
- **Significance:** Legislative power was centralised and law-making detached from local presidency practice, producing the codification drive that later yielded the great Indian codes.
- **Limit/caution:** The Act's non-discrimination language did not open real power to Indians; the gap between the promise and the practice is itself examinable.

**E. Charter Act, 1853 — bureaucratic professionalisation**
- **Claim:** The colonial state moved from patronage to examination-based bureaucracy while remaining unrepresentative.
- **Evidence:** It separated legislative from executive functions within the Governor-General's Council, expanded the legislative council and introduced **open competitive examination** for civil-service recruitment.
- **Significance:** This produced the permanent, examined, career civil service that outlasted the Company, the Crown and 1947 — the single most durable institutional legacy of the period.
- **Limit/caution:** "Open" competition was held in Britain at an age limit that made Indian entry practically very difficult; note the formal-versus-effective distinction rather than calling it Indianisation.

**F. The limits — no Indian participation**
- **Claim:** Constitutional development before 1858 was development of British control, not of Indian representation.
- **Evidence:** No Indian sat in the legislative or executive councils created by these acts; law-making was by officials, for revenue, order and commerce.
- **Significance:** Supplies the essential balancing paragraph and explains why Indian constitutional demand begins only after 1858 with the Councils Acts.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not project later nationalist categories backwards; Indian political demand in this period was largely petitionary and elite.

### 8.5 Structural direction (use for "trace"/"assess the direction" demands)

```text
1773  fragmented presidencies       -> single supreme executive (GG of Bengal)
1784  company autonomy              -> Crown supervision (Board of Control)
1786  divided council               -> strong personal executive authority
1813  chartered monopoly            -> open British trade + education + missions
1833  presidency legislation        -> all-India legislature + codification
1853  patronage recruitment         -> competitive bureaucracy + legislative separation
1858  company agency                -> direct Crown rule (continues in basic/12)
```

### 8.6 Counter-evidence, variation and caution

- **Against a "progress" narrative:** each act increased efficiency of control, not liberty; the same machinery that codified law also perfected extraction.
- **Against a purely British-motive reading:** the 1781 amendment and the Law Commission also responded to genuine administrative and jurisdictional chaos in India.
- **Variation:** Bengal, Madras and Bombay retained distinct revenue and judicial practice well past 1833; "centralisation" was legal before it was administrative.
- ⚠️ The characterisation of 1813 as a "free-trade" act should note that free trade ran one way — British goods entered India far more easily than Indian goods entered Britain (developed in `basic/07`).

### 8.7 Verdict scaffolds

- **Trajectory verdict:** "The acts of 1773–1853 did not democratise Indian government; they nationalised it — transferring control from a corporation to the British state while leaving Indians outside both."
- **Institutional verdict:** "1858 changed the sovereign, not the structure; the executive, the council, the codes and the covenanted service were all in place before the Crown arrived."
- **Economic-linkage verdict:** "Constitutional and economic policy moved together: the same decade that opened Indian markets to Lancashire also opened Indian government to Parliament."

### 8.8 Factual-risk cautions

- **Governor-General of Bengal (1773)** and **Governor-General of India (1833)** are different offices — the commonest error in this topic.
- Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General of Bengal; William Bentinck the first Governor-General of India.
- The 1813 Act ended the Indian trade monopoly but retained tea and the China trade.
- The 1833 Act spoke of non-discrimination in office; the **1853** Act introduced competitive examination.
- Pitt's India Act did not end Company rule.
- Do not attribute the Supreme Court at Calcutta to Pitt's India Act; it belongs to the 1773 framework and began in 1774.
