## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — INA transformed defeat into political energy.**

- ✅ The **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)** first emerged from Indian prisoners of war under Mohan Singh and Rash Behari Bose with Japanese support.
- ✅ **Subhas Chandra Bose** revived and led the INA in 1943, giving the slogans **"Give me blood and I will give you freedom"** and **"Dilli Chalo"**.
- ✅ The **Rani of Jhansi Regiment**, associated with **Lakshmi Sahgal**, symbolised women's participation in militant nationalism.
- ✅ Though INA's military campaign failed at **Imphal-Kohima (1944)**, its trials at the Red Fort turned its soldiers into national symbols.

**Foundation — post-war unrest showed the Raj's coercive base was weakening.**

- ✅ The INA trials of **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon** created Hindu-Muslim-Sikh unity in public sympathy.
- ✅ The **RIN ratings' revolt (February 1946)** began at HMIS Talwar in Bombay and spread to ships and shore establishments. It was serious but short-lived, urban-centred and not a coordinated national military revolution.
- ✅ The **Cabinet Mission (1946)** rejected Pakistan but proposed a weak centre, provincial groups, provinces, a Constituent Assembly and an Interim Government.
- ✅ **Direct Action Day (16 August 1946)** marked the collapse of constitutional trust into mass communal violence.

**Foundation — the negotiations of 1944–45 failed before the elections settled who could negotiate.**

- ✅ In **April 1944 C. Rajagopalachari** proposed a formula for a post-war commission to demarcate contiguous Muslim-majority districts where a **plebiscite of the adult population** would decide on Pakistan, with agreement on essential services and implementation deferred until after the full transfer of power. ✅ **Gandhi opened talks with Jinnah on this basis in July 1944**; the **Gandhi–Jinnah talks broke down in September 1944**.
- ✅ **Wavell**, who had replaced Linlithgow as Viceroy in 1943, convened the **Simla Conference (25 June – 14 July 1945)** to form an **entirely Indian Executive Council** with only the Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief as British members, equal representation for caste Hindus and Muslims and separate representation for the Scheduled Castes.
- ✅ Simla **collapsed on Jinnah's demand for parity and his claim that the League alone had the right to nominate all the Muslim members**; the Congress refused, because conceding it would have meant admitting that the Congress was a party of caste Hindus only — at a time when **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad was Congress president**.
- ✅ In the **elections of the winter of 1945–46** the **Congress won over 90 per cent of the general seats** in the provincial elections (including **23 of the 36 labour seats**) while the **Muslim League made a similar sweep of the Muslim constituencies** — taking, across India, **74.7 per cent of the votes cast in Muslim constituencies** against the Congress's **80.9 per cent in the general constituencies**.

- ✅ **RIN Mutiny** occurred in **February 1946** and began in Bombay among naval ratings.

- ✅ Cabinet Mission **rejected Pakistan** but proposed **grouping of provinces**.
- ✅ **Direct Action Day** was called by the Muslim League on **16 August 1946**.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: The **INA trials** and **RIN Mutiny** mattered politically even though neither defeated the British militarily.

- ❌ INA won India's independence on the battlefield. → INA's military campaign failed, but its trials created huge patriotic mobilisation.
- ❌ RIN Mutiny was a Congress-led movement. → It was a naval ratings' revolt; national parties did not fully own it.
- ❌ INA/RIN alone forced immediate British withdrawal. → They intensified the post-war crisis, but long-term loss of legitimacy, mass nationalism, British weakness and constitutional calculations also mattered.
- ❌ Cabinet Mission accepted the demand for Pakistan. → It **rejected Pakistan** but proposed provincial groupings.
- ❌ The C.R. Formula was a Congress offer of Pakistan on Jinnah's terms. → It offered a **plebiscite in contiguous Muslim-majority districts after the transfer of power**, with continuing joint arrangements for essential services — which is precisely why Jinnah rejected it.
- ❌ The Simla Conference failed because the Congress refused an Indian Executive Council. → The Congress accepted the framework; it refused the League's claim to a **monopoly of Muslim nominations**, which would have conceded that the Congress spoke only for caste Hindus.
- ❌ The 1945–46 elections were a referendum in which Indians voted for Partition. → They were **separate-electorate elections on a heavily restricted franchise**; the League itself presented them as a plebiscite for Pakistan, but that is a **party claim**, not the constitutional character of the poll.
- ❌ Cabinet Mission created the Constitution of India. → It proposed the **Constituent Assembly** framework; the Assembly drafted the Constitution later.
- ❌ Direct Action Day was part of the Cabinet Mission proposal. → It was a Muslim League political call after constitutional deadlock.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Coercion-base thesis:** *The decisive change between 1942 and 1946 was not in nationalist strength but in the reliability of the instruments of rule: once the loyalty of Indian soldiers, sailors, police and officials could no longer be assumed, the coercive foundation on which the Raj had always rested became unusable.*
- **Symbolic-politics thesis:** *The INA's importance was almost entirely political rather than military — it lost its campaign and won its trial, turning captured officers into cross-communal national symbols and forcing the government to abandon the prosecutions.*
- **Overdetermination thesis:** *No single factor explains 1947: post-war British exhaustion and a Labour government, an economically depleted empire, the international climate of decolonisation, the impossibility of governing without Indian collaboration, and continuous mass pressure all converged — an answer weighting only one of these is incomplete.*
- **Ambiguity-collapse thesis:** *The Cabinet Mission failed not because its scheme was unworkable but because its central compromise — provincial grouping — was deliberately ambiguous, and the two parties accepted it on incompatible readings, so the plan destroyed the trust it was meant to create.* ⚠️ **Qualification:** This thesis accurately explains the July 1946 rupture but does not fully account for the final breakdown — by December 1946 HMG had resolved the ambiguity in the League's favour, yet the League still refused to join the Constituent Assembly and the Interim Government remained ungovernable (see §8.8 endgame chain).
- **Negotiation-sequence thesis:** *Between 1942 and 1946 the constitutional question changed from "how much self-government" to "how many states", and every scheme after 1944 failed at the same point — the mechanism by which the League's claim to speak for Indian Muslims would be recognised: the C.R. Formula offered a deferred plebiscite, Simla offered parity in an executive, and the Cabinet Mission offered a group of provinces, and Jinnah rejected each because none conceded the League a monopoly of Muslim representation in advance.*
- **Mandate-not-inevitability thesis:** *The 1945–46 elections transformed the Pakistan demand from an elite assertion into a constitutionally certified mandate and thereby destroyed the Congress's argument that the League did not represent Muslims — but a mandate on separate electorates and a restricted franchise is not a plebiscite on a defined territory, and unity remained constitutionally available for another year.*

### 8.4 Bank A — The INA (routed demand on officers and organisation)

- **Claim:** The INA's political effect was produced by its defeat, not despite it.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)** first emerged from Indian prisoners of war under **Mohan Singh** and **Rash Behari Bose** with Japanese support; ✅ the first experiment collapsed by early 1943 when Mohan Singh pressed for autonomy and allied status rather than subsidiary-force status and was removed and arrested; ✅ **Subhas Chandra Bose** — who had resigned the Congress presidency after Tripuri (1939), founded the **Forward Bloc**, and **left Calcutta in disguise on the night of 16–17 January 1941**, travelling via Kabul to Berlin — reached Southeast Asia in **May 1943**, **revived and reorganised the INA**, and in **October 1943 proclaimed the Provisional Government of Azad Hind (Free India) at Singapore**, recognised by Japan and later by eight other governments, becoming supreme commander with the slogans **"Dilli Chalo"** and **"Give me blood and I will give you freedom"**; ✅ the **Rani of Jhansi Regiment**, associated with **Lakshmi Sahgal**, symbolised women's participation in militant nationalism; ✅ **Andaman and Nicobar were renamed Shaheed and Swaraj** under the Azad Hind setup; ✅ the **Imphal campaign launched on 8 March 1944** failed at **Imphal–Kohima**; ✅ Bose was officially reported to have died in an air crash in **August 1945**; ✅ the Red Fort trials of **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon** — a Muslim, a Hindu and a Sikh tried together — created cross-communal public sympathy, and ✅ the defence was conducted by **Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, K.N. Katju, Jawaharlal Nehru and Asaf Ali**, with the Congress organising an **INA Relief and Enquiry Committee**.
- **Significance:** Bose's contribution was to convert a body of prisoners into a **claimant government with an army, a cabinet, foreign recognition and a territorial ambition**, which is why the trials could not be treated as ordinary courts-martial: the composition of the accused made them an unintended demonstration of national unity at precisely the moment when communal politics was hardening, and the government's eventual retreat from the prosecutions showed that it could no longer enforce military discipline against national opinion. ✅ The Commander-in-Chief himself concluded that "general opinion in the Army is in favour of leniency", and the Governor of the North-West Frontier Province urged abandoning the trials because the issue was becoming "more and more purely Indian versus British".
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ The INA's association with Japanese support was politically contested at the time and remains an interpretive question; state the fact of Japanese backing without endorsing or condemning the alliance — the local sources themselves record "Japanese duplicity" and the Japanese refusal to treat the INA as an allied rather than subsidiary force. Do not claim the INA defeated British forces. ⚠️ Recruitment of **around forty thousand men by 1945** is attested in Bandyopadhyay and may be used **with attribution**; do not state casualty figures, and **do not assert any account of Bose's death beyond the official report of the August 1945 air crash**. ⚠️ The Congress took up the INA cause after the fact and made it an election plank; **S. Gopal** read that as "a touch of escapism", and ⚠️ historians differ on whether the INA agitation produced any direct change in imperial policy, though **P.S. Gupta** has suggested it contributed to the decision to send the Cabinet Mission. Attribute these as positions; do not assert them as settled fact.

### 8.5 Bank B — Service disaffection and the RIN revolt

- **Claim:** The 1946 mutinies mattered because of what they implied, not what they achieved.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **RIN ratings' revolt of February 1946** began at **HMIS Talwar in Bombay** and spread to ships and shore establishments; ✅ it was **serious but short-lived, urban-centred and not a coordinated national military revolution**.
- **Significance:** For a regime that had reorganised its army after 1857 precisely to prevent Indian solidarity in the ranks (`basic/08`, `basic/11`), a naval revolt supported by urban crowds was the failure of the empire's principal security doctrine.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ National parties did not fully own the revolt and it was settled quickly; do not present it as a nationalist-directed insurrection or as the immediate cause of withdrawal.

**Comparison — 1857 and 1946 (high-value contrast):**

| | 1857 | 1946 |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | Bengal army sepoys, regionally concentrated | Naval ratings across establishments, urban support |
| Politics | Restorationist — Mughal, Peshwa, taluqdari | Nationalist — INA symbols, tricolour, freedom slogans |
| Civilian link | Deep in Awadh and the Doab; absent elsewhere | Urban strikes and crowds; no rural base |
| Duration and outcome | Prolonged; suppressed by force | Days; settled by negotiation and persuasion |
| Effect on British policy | Restructured the empire to continue | Convinced the government that continuation was unaffordable |

### 8.6 Bank C — The failed negotiations of 1944–45 (C.R. Formula, Gandhi–Jinnah, Wavell Plan and Simla)

- **Claim:** The 1944–45 negotiations failed on a single recurring point — not on the *quantum* of self-government but on **who was entitled to represent Indian Muslims** — and that is why the electoral verdict of 1945–46 mattered so much.

**C.R. Formula and the Gandhi–Jinnah talks, 1944**

- **Evidence:** ✅ In **April 1944 C. Rajagopalachari** proposed that a **post-war commission demarcate the contiguous districts in which Muslims were in absolute majority**; there a **plebiscite of the adult population** would decide whether they preferred Pakistan; in the event of partition there would be **mutual agreement to run certain essential services such as defence and communications**; **border districts could choose either sovereign state**; and **implementation would wait until after the full transfer of power**. ✅ In **July 1944 Gandhi proposed talks with Jinnah on the basis of the "Rajaji formula"**, which in substance amounted to an acceptance of the Pakistan demand; ✅ the **Gandhi–Jinnah talks of September 1944 broke down**.
- **Why it failed:** ✅ In Gandhi's own account the talks failed on a **difference of perspective**: he regarded separation as a division *within the family* and therefore wished to retain elements of partnership, whereas **Jinnah wanted complete dissolution with sovereignty**. ⚠️ Three concrete objections follow from the formula's own terms: the plebiscite would be confined to districts of absolute Muslim majority and so yielded a **truncated territory** rather than whole provinces; implementation was deferred until *after* the British left, which gave the League no guarantee; and it made Pakistan a matter of **district-by-district popular consent rather than of recognition of the Muslims as a nation**, which was Jinnah's substantive demand.
- **Significance:** ⚠️ The formula is the analytical hinge of 1944: it establishes that the Congress leadership was already willing to contemplate partition three years before it happened, so the 1947 acceptance cannot be presented as a sudden capitulation. It also proves that the obstacle was **not the principle of separation but its mechanism and its scale**.
- **Limitation:** ⚠️ Rajagopalachari's initiative did not carry the Congress organisation, and Gandhi negotiated in a personal capacity while the Congress Working Committee was imprisoned after 1942 — do not describe the formula as an official Congress offer. ⚠️ **Ayesha Jalal's** position, recorded in the local sources, is that at **no point between 1940 and the arrival of the Cabinet Mission in 1946 did Jinnah or the League coherently define the Pakistan demand**; the vagueness was itself politically useful for mass mobilisation. Use this to explain why every scheme aimed at a defined territory failed.

**Wavell Plan and the Simla Conference, June–July 1945**

- **Evidence:** ✅ **Wavell**, who had replaced **Linlithgow** as Viceroy in **1943**, judged that "India after the war will become a running sore which will sap the strength of the British empire" and would be **ungovernable by force** because ruthless repression would not be acceptable to the British public; ✅ during his **March 1945** visit to London he convinced **Churchill** of the desirability of a **Congress–League coalition government** as a pre-emptive measure against the post-war crisis he predicted. ✅ He therefore convened a conference at Simla to form an **entirely Indian Executive Council with the Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief as the only British members**, with **equal representation for caste Hindus and Muslims**, **separate representation for the Scheduled Castes**, and the door open to discussion of a new constitution. ✅ The **Simla Conference of 25 June – 14 July 1945** broke down on **Jinnah's demand for parity and his claim of an exclusive right for the Muslim League to nominate all the Muslim members of the council**; ✅ the **Congress refused**, because accepting it would have amounted to admitting that the Congress was a party of caste Hindus only — ✅ and **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad was Congress president at that very moment**. ✅ Wavell called off the conference, since a coalition without the League would not work.
- **Significance:** Simla is the clearest demonstration that the **veto had passed to the League**: the British were prepared to Indianise the executive immediately, and a single party's claim to a representational monopoly was enough to stop it. ⚠️ It also establishes the direct link to 1946 — ✅ **Attlee's statement of 15 March 1946** that "a minority will not be allowed to place a veto on the progress of the majority" was, in Bipan Chandra's formulation, "a far cry from Wavell's allowing Jinnah to wreck the Simla Conference in June–July 1945 by his insistence on nominating all Muslims". The British decision in 1946 to proceed with the Congress rather than wait for the League is intelligible only against Simla.
- **Limitation:** ⚠️ Simla failed on **representation, not on the constitutional substance**; nothing at Simla decided partition, and the conference met before any electoral test of either party's claim — which is exactly the deficiency the 1945–46 elections then removed. ⚠️ Do not present Wavell as a partitionist: his purpose was a coalition to hold India together within a post-imperial Commonwealth relationship.

### 8.7 Bank D — The elections of 1945–46 and the transformation of bargaining power

- **Claim:** The elections converted the Pakistan demand from a contested elite assertion into a constitutionally certified mandate, and thereby changed what could be negotiated — without settling what Pakistan would territorially be.

| Evidence | Source-supported figure |
|---|---|
| ✅ Congress in the provincial elections | **Over 90 per cent of the general seats**, including **23 of the 36 labour seats** |
| ✅ Congress share of votes in general constituencies (all-India) | **80.9 per cent**; majorities in **every province except Bengal, Sind and Punjab** |
| ✅ Muslim League share of votes in Muslim constituencies (all-India) | **74.7 per cent**; a sweep of the Muslim constituencies |
| ✅ Bengal | League took **93 per cent of Muslim votes** and **119 of the 250 assembly seats** |
| ✅ Punjab (175 seats) | **Muslim League 75** (almost sweeping the rural Muslim constituencies), **Congress 51**, **Akalis 22**, **Unionist Party 18** — yet Khizr Hyat Khan Tiwana still formed a coalition with the Congress and the Akalis, so the League was **kept out of office in the province it most needed** |
| ✅ Other parties | Communist Party **8 seats**; Hindu Mahasabha **3**; Ambedkar's All-India Scheduled Castes Federation **2 of the 151 seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes** |
| ✅ Franchise | The electorate was **heavily restricted — about 10 per cent of the population** |

- **How this changed negotiating power:**
  1. ⚠️ **It destroyed the Congress's central factual claim.** Until 1946 the Congress could argue that the League was an elite body that did not represent Muslims — the argument that had justified refusing Jinnah's demand at Simla. After a 74.7 per cent sweep of Muslim constituencies the argument was no longer available in that form, and the League became, in the sources' phrase, the **only constitutionally legitimated representative of the Indian Muslims**.
  2. ⚠️ **It made the Pakistan claim harder to treat as mere elite assertion.** The League had converted a demand articulated by landed notables in 1940 into a mass mandate by mobilising professionals, business groups, students, the Punjab Muslim Students Federation, and the **ulama, pirs and sajjada nishins** whose fatwas made support for Pakistan a matter of individual religious obligation. ✅ The Congress president **Azad** later complained that the **Bengal** campaign in particular had been turned into "a religious crusade" — a criticism of the campaign's idiom that must be reported as Azad's judgement, not asserted as a general description of Muslim voters' motives.
  3. ⚠️ **It equally strengthened the Congress**, which emerged with an overwhelming general-constituency mandate and with the **INA agitation and the 1942 repression** as its two main campaign planks. The outcome was therefore not one victor but **two mandates in two separate electorates** — which is precisely the structural problem the Cabinet Mission then had to solve.
  4. ⚠️ **It marginalised every third force** — the Communists, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Scheduled Castes Federation and the Unionists — removing the intermediary groupings through which a compromise might otherwise have been brokered.

- **Qualification — what the verdict did *not* establish (essential for balance):**
  - ✅ The poll was fought on **separate electorates** and a **franchise of about a tenth of the population**; it was not universal-suffrage voting and not a territorial referendum.
  - ⚠️ It was **not a plebiscite on a fully specified territorial Partition**: no boundary, no constitutional structure and no population arrangement was on the ballot. ✅ **Anita Inder Singh's** position — that the League "**presented** the elections as a plebiscite for Pakistan" — is a statement about the League's *framing*, and must be reported as such rather than as the character of the election.
  - ✅ **Ayesha Jalal's** argument that the demand was never coherently defined before 1946 cuts directly against reading the result as a mandate for a known outcome.
  - ⚠️ It **did not erase Muslim dissent or regional complexity**: ✅ nationalist Muslims contested — the sources note that only in constituencies where nationalist Muslims stood did Congress candidates need to canvass; ✅ **Azad** led the Congress; ✅ the League still won **only 75 of 175 seats in Punjab** and was excluded from office there by a Congress–Akali–Unionist coalition; ✅ in Bengal 119 of 250 seats was a plurality achieved in a province the League would in fact see divided; and ✅ regional Muslim parties such as **Fazlul Huq's Krishak Praja Party** and the **Unionists** had been marginalised by political pressure rather than by any uniform Muslim opinion.

- **Link forward — from the verdict to the Mission, the Interim Government and the breakdown:**
  - ⚠️ The election result is the reason the Cabinet Mission's compromise took the **grouping** form at all: two certified mandates in mutually exclusive electorates could be reconciled only by a scheme that gave the League a bloc without giving it a state. ✅ The Mission's sections — **A** (Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, C.P., Orissa), **B** (Punjab, NWFP, Sind) and **C** (Bengal and Assam) — were to meet separately to decide group constitutions under a common centre controlling **defence, foreign affairs and communications**.
  - ✅ The Mission was **ambivalent on whether grouping was compulsory or optional** — it declared **grouping optional but sections compulsory**, a contradiction it "deliberately quibbled about". ✅ The **League announced acceptance on 6 June 1946** in so far as the basis of Pakistan was implied by compulsory grouping; ✅ **Nehru's AICC speech of 7 July 1946** asserted that the Congress was "not bound by a single thing except that we have decided to go into the Constituent Assembly"; ✅ **Jinnah withdrew the League's acceptance on 29 July 1946**.
  - ✅ **Wavell preferred to wait for the League**, as he had at Simla a year earlier; **His Majesty's Government insisted that Congress cooperation was vital**, and the **Interim Government was formed on 2 September 1946 with Congress members alone and Nehru as de facto head** — against the League's insistence that every settlement be acceptable to it. ⚠️ Note the order: the League had already forged the weapon of **Direct Action** after withdrawing on 29 July, and the violence broke out in **Calcutta on 16 August 1946**, *before* the Congress-only Interim Government took office on 2 September — so Direct Action is the League's response to the withdrawal of its own acceptance and to the prospect of exclusion, not a reaction to an existing Congress ministry.
  - **Analytical payoff:** the chain **election → two mandates → grouping compromise → incompatible interpretations → League's withdrawal and Direct Action → Congress-only Interim Government** is the single most efficient causal spine available for any "why did 1945–46 fail" or "was Partition inevitable" question, because each link is dated and named.

### 8.8 Bank E — The Cabinet Mission and the constitutional endgame

- **Claim:** The Mission was a serious unity plan defeated by an ambiguity at its centre.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Cabinet Mission (1946)** **rejected Pakistan** but proposed a **weak centre**, **grouping of provinces**, provinces, a **Constituent Assembly** and an **Interim Government**.
- **Significance:** The scheme addressed the League's substantive fear — Hindu-majority domination — without conceding partition, which is why it is rightly called the last serious attempt at unity. Its failure turned on whether grouping was **compulsory** (the League's reading, making a Muslim bloc effective) or **optional** (the Congress's reading, making it a shell); once each side had publicly stated an incompatible interpretation, neither could retreat.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ The Mission proposed the **Constituent Assembly framework**; it did **not** write the Constitution. ✅ **Direct Action Day** was a Muslim League political call after the constitutional deadlock, not a part of the Mission's proposals.

**Post-2 September 1946 endgame evidence chain** (source-verified from *India's Struggle for Independence*):

| Date | Event | Analytical significance |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **26 Oct 1946** | Muslim League enters the Interim Government | Jinnah's own framing: entry is a continuation of the Pakistan struggle by other means — not a concession to the Mission's unity logic. The League had accepted *neither* the grouping provision in its constitutional sense *nor* the Constituent Assembly, and had not abandoned Direct Action as a political weapon. |
| ✅ **6 Dec 1946** | HMG formally accepts the League interpretation that grouping is compulsory and provinces cannot opt out before the group constitution is settled | The British concede the League's most critical interpretive demand — yet the League *still* does not enter the Constituent Assembly. This breaks the core logic of the ambiguity-collapse thesis as a complete explanation: by December 1946 the ambiguity had been resolved in the League's favour, but the final failure was not reversed. |
| ✅ **9 Dec 1946** | Constituent Assembly meets for the first time | The League boycotts and demands that the Assembly be dissolved. Nine provinces proceed; the framework of a united India still nominally survives, but without the League's participation it has no political foundation. |
| ✅ **5 Feb 1947** | Nine Interim Government members from the Congress side formally demand the resignation of the League ministers | The Interim Government has become ungovernable from within; the only remaining question is how and when power is transferred. |
| ✅ **20 Feb 1947** | Attlee announces that Britain will transfer power not later than June 1948 | The announcement shifts the question from *whether* to partition to *how to partition quickly*, driving the acceleration to the 3 June Plan. |

**Counterpoint against a pure-ambiguity thesis:**

The ambiguity-collapse thesis — that the Mission failed because its grouping clause was deliberately vague and each party accepted a different plan — **accurately explains the July 1946 rupture** (Jinnah's withdrawal of 29 July after Nehru's AICC speech of 7 July). It does not fully explain the **final failure after December 1946**. By 6 December HMG had conceded the League's own interpretation of compulsory grouping; the ambiguity that destroyed the July settlement was formally resolved in the League's favour. Yet the League still did not join the Constituent Assembly, and the Interim Government became ungovernable in the following weeks. The sequence 6 Dec 1946 → 9 Dec 1946 boycott → 5 Feb 1947 demand for resignations → 20 Feb 1947 Attlee announcement shows that what ended the Cabinet Mission framework was not (only) textual ambiguity but the **irreconcilability of sovereign claims**: the League's goal was recognition of the Muslims as a separate nation with a separate constitutional track, not resolution of a textual dispute.

⚠️ **Qualification rule for exam answers:** ambiguity is a **proximate cause of the July rupture** and a necessary but not sufficient explanation of the complete breakdown. Any verdict that makes ambiguity the *sole or decisive* cause should add: *"Even after the British resolved the ambiguity in the League's favour in December 1946, the League refused to enter the Constituent Assembly and the Interim Government collapsed — which indicates that the deeper obstacle was not textual ambiguity but incompatible constitutional goals."*

**Cabinet Mission (1946) compared with the Nehru Report (1928) — two failed settlements:**

| | ✅ **Nehru Report, 1928** (`basic/22`) | ✅ **Cabinet Mission, 1946** |
|---|---|---|
| Author | Indians themselves — a committee chaired by **Motilal Nehru** | The British government's mission |
| Objective sought | **Dominion status**, fundamental rights, **joint electorates** | A united India with a **weak centre**, **grouping of provinces**, a Constituent Assembly and an Interim Government; **Pakistan rejected** |
| Concession offered to Muslim opinion | Rights guarantees in place of separate electorates — i.e. it asked the League to give up the mechanism it had held since 1909 | Grouping — i.e. it offered the League a bloc of provinces without conceding partition |
| Why it failed | **Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929)** rejected it; the Congress left, led by Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Bose, simultaneously rejected dominion status as too little — it satisfied neither flank | The grouping clause was **ambiguous**: compulsory on the League's reading and optional on the Congress's, so each accepted a different plan; once both interpretations were public, neither could retreat |
| What the failure cost | The dominion-status middle ground collapsed; **Purna Swaraj** followed at Lahore in 1929 | The united-India middle ground collapsed; **Direct Action Day**, communal violence and partition followed |

- ⚠️ **The comparative argument:** both attempts failed at the same point — the *mechanism of minority security*. In 1928 the Congress asked the League to trade a guaranteed political instrument for a promised legal guarantee, and in 1946 the British tried to supply an instrument so vaguely defined that both sides could claim it. Between the two dates the League's demand had escalated from **safeguards within India** to **states outside it**, so the 1946 failure was final in a way the 1928 failure was not.
- ⚠️ **Difference in stakes:** in 1928 rejection cost a constitutional scheme; in 1946 rejection cost the constitutional option itself, because by then the British were leaving and the alternative to agreement was not further negotiation but division (`basic/27`).

### 8.9 Bank F — Weighting the causes of British withdrawal

| Factor | Weight and reasoning |
|---|---|
| Collapse of the collaboration base | ⚠️ Highest explanatory weight — services, police and armed forces could no longer be relied upon; the Raj had always governed through Indians |
| Post-war British exhaustion | ⚠️ High — economic depletion, a Labour government, demobilisation pressure and competing commitments |
| Continuous mass nationalism | ⚠️ High cumulative weight — 1920–22, 1930–34 and 1942 had made governance by consent impossible |
| INA and RIN episodes | ⚠️ Moderate but sharply timed — they crystallised the loyalty question at the decisive moment |
| International context | ⚠️ Moderate — the post-war climate of decolonisation and American pressure |
| Constitutional calculation | ⚠️ Moderate — a negotiated transfer preserved British interests better than a forced one |

- **Analytical use:** any "why did the British leave" answer should rank factors explicitly. ✅ Note the file's own caution — INA and RIN alone did not force withdrawal; long-term loss of legitimacy, mass nationalism, British weakness and constitutional calculation all mattered.

### 8.10 Counter-evidence, balance and caution

- **Against a nationalist-triumph reading:** the transfer was negotiated, on British legislative terms and a British timetable, with partition as the price.
- **Against a purely British-exhaustion reading:** exhaustion explains willingness to leave, not the impossibility of staying; the collaboration collapse supplies that.
- **Against a purely INA/RIN reading:** both episodes were brief and contained; their significance is evidentiary — they revealed a condition rather than creating it.
- **Against reading the 1945–46 elections as making Partition inevitable:** both parties accepted the Cabinet Mission scheme in some form *after* the elections, in June–July 1946; the option of a united India therefore survived the verdict by more than a year and closed only when the grouping interpretations were made irreconcilable and the Interim Government was constituted without the League.
- **Against reading the elections as irrelevant:** the alternative to the League's mandate was not a united India but a continued dispute over representation which Simla had already shown to be unresolvable; after 1946 no scheme could exclude the League, and every subsequent proposal had to be one the League could accept.
- ⚠️ The relative weight of these factors is genuinely contested among historians; an answer that acknowledges the contest scores higher than one that asserts a single cause.

### 8.11 Verdict scaffolds

- **Withdrawal verdict:** "The British left because they could no longer find enough Indians willing to help them stay — every other factor operated through that one."
- **INA verdict:** "The INA lost every battle it fought and won the only trial that mattered."
- **Cabinet Mission verdict:** "The Mission's plan was the last arrangement under which one India was constitutionally possible; it failed because its central compromise had to mean different things to different parties in order to be accepted at all — and even when the British resolved that ambiguity in the League's favour in December 1946, the League still refused the Constituent Assembly, which indicates that the deeper obstacle was not the text but the incompatibility of sovereign claims."
- **Negotiations verdict (1942–47):** "Between Cripps and Mountbatten the British moved from offering India a constitution to asking Indians how many constitutions they wanted; every intervening scheme — the C.R. Formula, Simla, the Cabinet Mission — foundered on the same question of who could speak for Muslims, and the 1945–46 elections answered it in a way that made the Cabinet Mission's ambiguity unsustainable."
- **Elections verdict:** "The elections of 1945–46 made Pakistan unrefusable without making it defined: they proved that the League represented the Muslim electorate, but they were fought on separate electorates by a tenth of the population, and they left the territory, the constitution and the fate of Punjab and Bengal entirely open."

### 8.12 Factual-risk cautions

- INA origins: **Mohan Singh** and **Rash Behari Bose** with Japanese support; **Subhas Chandra Bose** reached Southeast Asia in **May 1943**, reorganised the INA and proclaimed the **Provisional Government of Azad Hind in October 1943 at Singapore**; **Imphal campaign launched 8 March 1944**; Red Fort trials of **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon**.
- Do not confuse the **first INA (1942, Mohan Singh)** with **Bose's reorganised INA (1943)** — the first experiment had effectively collapsed by early 1943 before Bose arrived.
- **C.R. Formula = April 1944, C. Rajagopalachari**; **Gandhi–Jinnah talks = September 1944**. Rajagopalachari's proposal was **not an official Congress offer**, and the plebiscite was to be held **only in contiguous Muslim-majority districts, after the transfer of power**.
- **Simla Conference = 25 June – 14 July 1945**, convened by **Wavell**, who succeeded **Linlithgow in 1943**. It failed on **Jinnah's claim that the League alone could nominate all Muslim members** of the Executive Council — not on the Congress rejecting an Indian executive.
- Elections were held in the **winter of 1945–46**; use only the figures in §8.7. ⚠️ **Do not state seat totals for the central legislature elections** — the source books held here supply the provincial results and the all-India vote shares, not central-assembly seat counts. Do not convert a vote share into a seat share or vice versa.
- Do not describe the 1945–46 elections as a referendum, a plebiscite or a universal-franchise verdict; the electorate was about **10 per cent of the population** and voting was by **separate electorates**.
- Cabinet Mission sections: **A** (Madras, Bombay, U.P., Bihar, C.P., Orissa), **B** (Punjab, NWFP, Sind), **C** (Bengal, Assam); the centre was to control **defence, foreign affairs and communications**. The Mission declared **grouping optional but sections compulsory**.
- Sequence discipline: League acceptance **6 June 1946** → Nehru's AICC speech **7 July 1946** → Jinnah's withdrawal **29 July 1946** → Interim Government **2 September 1946** (Congress only) → **Direct Action Day, 16 August 1946** preceded the Interim Government's formation, so do not present it as a response to it.
- **Two different Attlee statements — do not conflate.** ✅ **15 March 1946**: "a minority will not be allowed to place a veto on the progress of the majority" — the statement that marked the British departure from the Simla posture and preceded the Cabinet Mission. ✅ **20 February 1947** (`basic/27`): the announcement that Britain would transfer power not later than June 1948. The first is about the League's veto; the second sets the withdrawal deadline.
- RIN revolt = **February 1946**, beginning at **HMIS Talwar, Bombay**.
- The Cabinet Mission **rejected Pakistan**; **Direct Action Day** was a League call after deadlock, not a Mission proposal.
- The Mission proposed the Constituent Assembly; the Assembly, not the Mission, drafted the Constitution.
- Do not state INA strength, casualty figures or the number of ships involved in the RIN revolt.
- Do not claim any single event "forced" British withdrawal.
