## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — Company expansion used war plus diplomacy.**

- ✅ After Bengal, the Company used revenue, armies and alliances to defeat Mysore, Marathas and Sikhs.
- ✅ Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan was a major southern opponent because it tried to modernise its army and resist Company control.
- ✅ The Maratha confederacy was powerful but divided among the Peshwa, Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad.
- ✅ Sikh power remained strong under Ranjit Singh, but after his death internal weakness and Anglo-Sikh wars led to annexation.

**Foundation — two instruments must never be confused.**

- ✅ **Subsidiary Alliance** was associated with Lord Wellesley from **1798**; Indian states accepted British troops, paid subsidy, kept a Resident and gave up independent diplomacy.
- ✅ **Doctrine of Lapse** was associated with Lord Dalhousie; states without a natural heir could be annexed by refusing adoption rights.
- ✅ Awadh was annexed in **1856** on alleged misgovernance, not by Doctrine of Lapse.
- ⚠️ These devices converted Indian rulers into dependent allies before outright annexation.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: Subsidiary Alliance = Wellesley; Doctrine of Lapse = Dalhousie. Do not mix authors, dates or mechanisms.

- ❌ Awadh was annexed by Doctrine of Lapse. → Awadh was annexed in **1856** for alleged misgovernance.
- ❌ Tipu died in the Third Anglo-Mysore War. → Tipu died in the **Fourth** war in **1799**.
- ❌ Treaty of Mangalore ended the First Anglo-Mysore War. → It ended the **Second** war.
- ❌ Treaty of Salbai ended the Second Anglo-Maratha War. → It ended the **First** war.
- ❌ Ranjit Singh fought the Anglo-Sikh Wars. → The wars occurred **after his death**.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Fiscal-military thesis:** *British expansion succeeded because Bengal's revenue financed a permanent, drilled, salaried sepoy army with secure supply; Indian states with comparable courage lacked comparable fiscal continuity.*
- **Serial-defeat thesis:** *Indian powers were not weaker than the Company in aggregate; they were defeated in sequence because no durable anti-British coalition survived contact with British diplomacy.*
- **Indirect-rule thesis:** *Subsidiary Alliance was more destructive than annexation: it disarmed the Indian state, drained its treasury for a foreign garrison and removed its diplomatic independence while leaving the ruler nominally responsible for the resulting misgovernment.*
- **Anti-teleological thesis:** *Expansion was neither planned from 1757 nor accidental; it proceeded through repeated local decisions in which security, revenue and commercial advantage were interpreted expansively by governors-general on the spot.*

### 8.4 Named evidence units (claim → evidence → significance → limit)

**A. Subsidiary Alliance — the provisions (routed Prelims demand)**
- **Claim:** The alliance was a complete instrument of subordination disguised as protection.
- **Evidence:** ✅ Systematised by Wellesley from **1798**, with Hyderabad the first major acceptor, its core terms were: the state accepted a British force stationed in its territory; it paid for that force in cash or by ceding revenue-yielding territory; it accepted a British **Resident** at court; it could not employ Europeans without British consent; and it could not conduct relations or make war with other Indian states without British approval. In return the British promised protection and formal non-interference in internal administration. Acceptors included Hyderabad (1798), Mysore's restored Wodeyars (1799), Awadh (1801) and the Peshwa by the Treaty of Bassein (1802), followed by Maratha chiefs.
- **Significance:** Each clause removed one attribute of sovereignty — army, treasury, diplomacy and court autonomy — so the state remained but the capacity to resist did not.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ The technique had precedents before Wellesley, including French subsidiary practice and earlier Company arrangements; Wellesley's contribution was systematic and aggressive application, not invention.

**B. Doctrine of Lapse and title extinction**
- **Claim:** Dalhousie converted succession law into an annexation instrument.
- **Evidence:** Refusal to recognise adopted heirs led to annexations including Satara (1848), Jhansi (annexed in 1854 after the 1853 succession dispute) and Nagpur (1854); Awadh was taken separately in **1856** on the ground of alleged misgovernance.
- **Significance:** Lapse struck at the ruling houses' core legitimating practice — adoption — and so created a class of dispossessed princely claimants with a direct personal grievance.
- **Limit/caution:** Not every succession was refused, and Awadh must never be attributed to Lapse; the two grounds are separately examinable.

**C. Mysore — the most systematic resistance**
- **Claim:** Mysore came closest to matching the Company because it attacked the capability gap directly.
- **Evidence:** Four Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–69, 1780–84, 1790–92, 1799); Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan reorganised the army along European lines and sought foreign alliances; Tipu died defending Seringapatam in 1799 and the Wodeyars were restored under subsidiary terms.
- **Significance:** Shows the defeat of Indian states was not caused by unwillingness to modernise; Mysore modernised and still lost because it fought coalitions of the Company with the Nizam and the Marathas.
- **Limit/caution:** Treaty sequence matters — Madras (1769), Mangalore (1784), Seringapatam (1792); do not attach the wrong treaty to the wrong war.

**D. Marathas — reach without cohesion**
- **Claim:** The confederate structure that gave Maratha power its extent also made it defeatable in parts.
- **Evidence:** Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–82 ending in the Treaty of Salbai; 1803–05 under Wellesley including Assaye, after which Scindia and Bhonsle submitted; 1817–18 ending the confederacy with Baji Rao II deposed and pensioned at Bithur).
- **Significance:** The First War produced a negotiated peace; by the Second the Company could isolate individual chiefs; by the Third it faced no united Maratha command at all — the trajectory of fragmentation is the argument.
- **Limit/caution:** Salbai closed the **First** war, not the second; misplacing it is a common and costly error.

**E. Sikhs — late consolidation, late defeat**
- **Claim:** Punjab survived longest because it was the one Indian state with a comparable standing army and a settled succession while Ranjit Singh lived.
- **Evidence:** Sikh power under Ranjit Singh remained intact through the period of Maratha collapse; the Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–46, 1848–49) came only after his death, ending in the annexation of Punjab in 1849; Sindh had been annexed by Napier in 1843.
- **Significance:** The correlation between Ranjit Singh's death, court instability and defeat is the strongest available evidence that internal cohesion, not battlefield quality, decided outcomes.
- **Limit/caution:** Ranjit Singh himself fought no Anglo-Sikh War; the wars are posthumous.

**F. Dispossession as the pre-1857 grievance bank**
- **Claim:** Expansion created identifiable categories of losers whose grievances converged in 1857.
- **Evidence:** Displaced ruling houses (Jhansi, Nagpur, Satara), the Awadh taluqdars and the province's disbanded soldiery after 1856, and pensioned or demoted princely families.
- **Significance:** Gives any "causes of 1857" answer a concrete political-economy base instead of a list of resentments.
- **Limit/caution:** Annexation grievance is one strand among several; it does not by itself explain the geography or the social spread of 1857.

### 8.5 Expansion mechanism (for "how" questions)

```text
Bengal revenue (post-1765)
  -> permanent salaried sepoy army + European officer corps + artillery train
  -> war against one Indian state at a time, with Indian allies recruited against it
  -> victory financed by indemnity or ceded territory
  -> ceded territory raises revenue -> larger army
  -> subsidiary treaty disarms surviving states without administrative cost
  -> Lapse and misgovernance doctrines absorb the residual states
```

### 8.6 Counter-evidence, variation and caution

- **Against inevitability:** the Company suffered reverses (notably in the Second Anglo-Mysore War) and negotiated rather than dictated the Treaty of Salbai; expansion was contested.
- **Against uniformity:** the northwest frontier and Punjab were absorbed on strategic-frontier logic, Awadh on alleged misgovernance and central India through Lapse — the *grounds* differed and answers should say so.
- **Against a purely military reading:** the Resident system, indemnity finance and control of succession did as much political work as any battle.
- ⚠️ Avoid describing expansion as a single deliberate "plan of conquest"; the pattern is consistent, the intention was contested even within the Company and in Parliament.

### 8.7 Verdict scaffolds

- **Instrument verdict:** "Subsidiary Alliance annexed sovereignty before Lapse annexed territory; the second was possible because the first had already succeeded."
- **Comparative verdict:** "Mysore lacked allies, the Marathas lacked unity and the Sikhs lacked time; the Company lacked none of the three."
- **1857-linkage verdict:** "Annexation did not cause the Revolt, but it supplied it with leaders who had already lost everything the British could take."

### 8.8 Factual-risk cautions

- Subsidiary Alliance = Wellesley (from 1798); Doctrine of Lapse = Dalhousie — never interchange them.
- Awadh (1856) was annexed on alleged misgovernance, not by Lapse.
- Tipu Sultan died in the **Fourth** Anglo-Mysore War (1799).
- Mangalore closed the Second, Seringapatam the Third, Madras the First Anglo-Mysore War.
- Salbai closed the First Anglo-Maratha War.
- Punjab was annexed in 1849, Sindh in 1843 — different wars, different agents.
- Do not state subsidy amounts or troop strengths for any subsidiary treaty.
