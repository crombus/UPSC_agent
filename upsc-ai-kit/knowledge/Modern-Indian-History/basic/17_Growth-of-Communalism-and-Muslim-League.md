## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — communalism is modern politics, not ancient religiosity.**

- ✅ Bipan Chandra treats communalism as a modern ideology that converts religious identity into claims about shared secular and political interests; this is a source interpretation, not a synonym for religion.
- ✅ Communalism is not the same as religion, religious reform or personal religiosity.
- ✅ Its core claim is that followers of one religion share common political, economic and social interests that are separate from other religious communities.
- ⚠️ This makes communalism a political ideology of identity-based representation, not a timeless Hindu-Muslim hatred.

**Foundation — colonial institutions and middle-class competition deepened communal politics.**

- ✅ Roots included uneven modern development, colonial divide-and-rule, separate electorates, use of religion in politics and competition among educated middle classes for jobs and representation.
- ✅ The All-India Muslim League was founded in **1906** at **Dhaka** by Muslim elites including Aga Khan and Nawab Salimullah.
- ✅ Morley-Minto reforms of **1909** gave separate electorates to Muslims, strengthening communal representation in constitutional politics.
- ✅ The Lucknow Pact of **1916** temporarily united Congress and League, but it accepted separate electorates as the basis of agreement.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ **All-India Muslim League** was founded in **1906** at **Dhaka**.
- ✅ Key League-associated names include **Aga Khan** and **Nawab Salimullah**.
- ⚠️ The **Simla Deputation (Oct 1906)** sought separate Muslim representation and immediately preceded the League's foundation.
- ✅ **Separate electorates for Muslims** were introduced by the **Morley-Minto reforms / Indian Councils Act 1909**.
- ⚠️ **Hindu Mahasabha** emerged in **1915** and **RSS** was founded in **1925**; their placement under "communalism" here follows Bipan Chandra's analytical framework.
- ✅ **Lucknow Pact 1916** represented temporary Congress-League cooperation and accepted separate electorates.
- ⚠️ **Savarkar's Hindutva** appeared in **1923** as part of later Hindu communal ideology.
- ✅ **Lahore Resolution 1940** spoke of "independent states" in Muslim-majority zones and did not itself use the word "Pakistan"; it belongs to the later Pakistan-demand trajectory.
- ⚠️ Communalism should be defined as political use of religious identity, not religion itself.

## 5. UPSC Traps

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Modern-ideology thesis:** *Communalism was a product of modern politics, not of ancient antagonism: it required a colonial state that counted and represented people by religion, a competitive market for education and office, and a print public in which community claims could be made.*
- **Institutionalisation thesis:** *Separate electorates did not create communal sentiment; they converted it into a permanent structural interest by making religious identity the basis of political existence — after 1909 a Muslim politician's constituency was, by law, only Muslims.*
- **Elite-competition thesis:** *The clearest social root was competition among the educated middle classes of different communities for a scarce supply of government posts, professional openings and legislative seats created by the colonial economy itself.*
- **Nationalist-limitation thesis:** *The national movement fought communalism as a political rival while conceding its premises in negotiation — most importantly at Lucknow in 1916 — which is why every episode of unity rested on the very framework it needed to dismantle.*

### 8.4 Definitional engine (use this before any narrative)

```text
Stage 1: Believers of one religion share common SECULAR (political/economic) interests.
Stage 2: Those interests DIFFER from the interests of other religious communities.
Stage 3: Those differing interests are mutually HOSTILE and irreconcilable.
```

- **Analytical use:** most nineteenth-century religious reform stops at nothing beyond religious identity; the political ideology begins at stage 1 and becomes dangerous at stage 3. Locating a movement on this ladder is a higher-order answer than calling it "communal".
- **What communalism is not:** religion, religiosity, religious reform, or communal *riot*. ✅ Bipan Chandra's framework treats it as a modern political ideology; ⚠️ note that this is a source interpretation, and say so.

### 8.5 Named evidence units (claim → evidence → significance → limit)

**A. Colonial policy and institutional design**
- **Claim:** The state made religion a unit of political accounting.
- **Evidence:** ✅ Divide-and-rule practice, official patronage, and above all the introduction of **separate electorates for Muslims by the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909**; ⚠️ the **Simla Deputation (October 1906)**, led by the Aga Khan, had petitioned Minto for separate representation shortly before the League's foundation.
- **Significance:** Separate electorates created a self-reinforcing political incentive — communal appeals now had an electoral payoff and inter-communal appeals had none.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ Do not claim the government "invented" the Muslim League or scripted the Simla Deputation; the documented claim is the concession and its consequences, not a proven conspiracy.

**B. Organisational milestones**
- **Claim:** Communal politics acquired durable organisational form on both sides.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **All-India Muslim League** was founded in **1906 at Dhaka**, with Aga Khan and Nawab Salimullah among its leaders; ⚠️ the **Hindu Mahasabha** emerged in **1915**, **Savarkar's *Hindutva*** appeared in **1923** and the **RSS** was founded in **1925** — placed here under communal politics following Bipan Chandra's analytical framework.
- **Significance:** Once organisations exist, communal claims acquire continuity across events and survive individual leaders — the difference between sentiment and politics.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ These classifications follow one interpretive framework; present them as such, and do not equate the internal character of these organisations with one another.

**C. Socio-economic root — elite competition**
- **Claim:** The material basis was competition for the scarce opportunities the colonial economy provided.
- **Evidence:** ✅ Uneven modern development between and within communities; competition among educated middle classes for jobs, education and legislative representation.
- **Significance:** This explains the timing — communal politics grew as education and office-seeking expanded, not as religiosity intensified — and it explains why communalism was strongest among the professional classes rather than among the rural poor.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not reduce the phenomenon to economics alone; ideology and colonial design were independent contributors.

**D. Lucknow 1916 — unity on communal terms**
- **Claim:** The movement's greatest early success against communal division was also a concession to it.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Lucknow Pact (1916)** produced Congress–League cooperation on a joint reform demand while **Congress accepted separate electorates** (with weightage — see `basic/18`).
- **Significance:** The Pact proves both that inter-communal political agreement was possible and that its available currency was communal representation — the central paradox of the topic.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ Whether the bargain was a necessary tactical step or a strategic error is disputed among historians; present both readings.

**E. The 1919 extension**
- **Claim:** After 1909 the communal-representation principle expanded rather than contracted.
- **Evidence:** ✅ Under the Montagu–Chelmsford scheme, communal representation and reservation were **retained and considerably extended**: in addition to Muslims, **Sikhs were granted separate electorates**, seats were **reserved for non-Brahmans in Madras**, and the **"depressed classes" were offered nominated seats** at all levels — despite theoretical criticism of the separate-electorate principle in the Montagu–Chelmsford Report itself.
- **Significance:** Demonstrates that the logic was generalisable to any identity the state chose to recognise — the strongest possible evidence for the institutionalisation thesis, and the background to the later Communal Award and Poona Pact (`basic/22`).
- **Limit/caution:** Nominated seats and reserved seats are not the same as separate electorates; keep the three mechanisms distinct.

**F. Why nationalism could not defeat it**
- **Claim:** The failure was structural and strategic together.
- **Evidence:** ⚠️ The movement's dominant idiom drew on Hindu cultural symbolism (`basic/15`); it negotiated within a communal electoral framework it could not unilaterally abolish; and it treated communalism episodically, as a problem of unity at moments of agitation, rather than continuously, as an ideology requiring sustained counter-argument.
- **Significance:** Gives the "critically examine" answers a real explanation rather than a lament.
- **Limit/caution:** Concede genuine achievements — Lucknow, Khilafat–Non-Cooperation (`basic/20`) and consistent formal secularism in Congress doctrine.

### 8.6 Causal mechanism

```text
Colonial census, enumeration and representation by community
   + uneven access to English education between and within communities
   + scarce government posts and professional openings
   -> community-based claim-making by educated elites
   -> Simla Deputation 1906 -> League 1906 -> separate electorates 1909
   -> electoral incentive rewards communal appeal, penalises cross-communal appeal
   -> extension in 1919 to Sikhs, non-Brahmans, depressed classes
   -> every constitutional negotiation must now be conducted in communal units
   -> 1932 Communal Award, 1940 Lahore Resolution, 1947 Partition (basic/22, 27)
```

### 8.7 Counter-evidence, balance and variation

- **Against inevitability:** communal organisations remained electorally weak for long periods; the Justice Party's collapse and the League's poor showing before 1937 show that communal politics needed specific conditions to succeed.
- **Regional variation:** communal politics developed very differently in Bengal and Punjab (where it intersected with agrarian class divisions) from the United Provinces (where it was primarily a service-and-office competition).
- **Intra-community diversity:** Muslim opinion was never unified — Deobandi, nationalist-Muslim, regional and class currents opposed the League; the same is true of Hindu opinion.
- ⚠️ Never write an answer that assigns collective agency or blame to a religious community; the analytical subjects are ideologies, organisations and elites.

### 8.8 Verdict scaffolds

- **Conceptual verdict:** "Communalism was born in the modern colonial public sphere, not in medieval history; it is a claim about interests made in the language of faith."
- **Institutional verdict:** "Separate electorates converted a competitive elite ideology into a permanent constitutional structure, and no later negotiation could be conducted outside it."
- **Nationalist verdict:** "The movement repeatedly achieved communal unity and never achieved communal transformation, because it fought communal organisations without dismantling the framework that made them rational."

### 8.9 Factual-risk cautions

- Muslim League = **1906, Dhaka**; separate electorates = **1909, Morley–Minto** — never 1919 or the Lucknow Pact.
- The Lucknow Pact **accepted** separate electorates; it did not introduce them.
- The **Lahore Resolution (1940)** spoke of "independent states" in Muslim-majority zones and did **not** itself use the word "Pakistan".
- The Simla Deputation is ⚠️-tagged in this file; do not assert an official conspiracy behind it.
- Hindu Mahasabha (1915), *Hindutva* (1923) and RSS (1925) are placed under this heading on Bipan Chandra's framework; flag the interpretive basis.
- Do not use communal-riot narratives, casualty figures or population percentages as evidence.
