## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — the empire declined from strength, not sudden collapse.**

- ✅ Aurangzeb's death in **1707** was followed by wars of succession and a rapid weakening of central authority; weak successors could not hold the vast empire together.
- ✅ **Bahadur Shah I (1707–12)** followed a policy of compromise with Marathas, Rajputs and Sikhs, but reversed nothing fundamental; after him a series of weak rulers depended on powerful nobles.
- ✅ The **Sayyid Brothers** (Abdullah Khan & Husain Ali) became "**king-makers**" — raising and deposing emperors (Farrukhsiyar) — showing that the throne had become a prize for court factions.
- ✅ During **Muhammad Shah's long reign (1719–48)**, court factionalism continued and provinces such as **Hyderabad, Bengal and Awadh** became effectively autonomous. His sobriquet "Rangila" should not substitute for structural explanation.

**Foundation — external shocks exposed the hollowness.**

- ✅ **Nadir Shah** of Persia invaded in **1739**, defeated the Mughals at **Karnal**, sacked Delhi and took away enormous plunder including the **Peacock Throne** and the **Kohinoor** — a shattering blow to Mughal prestige.
- ✅ **Ahmad Shah Abdali** (Nadir Shah's successor) invaded India repeatedly between **1748 and 1761**, further draining the empire and culminating in the **Third Battle of Panipat (1761)**.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ **Aurangzeb died in 1707**; decline dates from the wars of succession that followed.
- ✅ The **Sayyid Brothers** (Abdullah Khan, Husain Ali) were the "**king-makers**".
- ✅ **Nadir Shah** defeated the Mughals at **Karnal (1739)**, sacked Delhi, took the **Peacock Throne & Kohinoor**.
- ✅ **Muhammad Shah (1719–48)** — during his reign Hyderabad, Bengal and Awadh became autonomous in practice.
- ✅ **Ahmad Shah Abdali** invaded India several times, **1748–1761**.
- ⚠️ The three great regional powers rising from the ruins: **Marathas, Sikhs, Jats**, plus autonomous provinces.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: The Mughal decline was **multi-causal and gradual** — do not reduce it to a single cause (e.g., "only Aurangzeb" or "only foreign invasions").

- ❌ The empire collapsed immediately in 1707. → It **fragmented gradually** over decades; the emperor remained a symbolic figure into the 19th century.
- ❌ Nadir Shah established Persian rule in India. → He **plundered and left**; he did not annex the empire.
- ❌ The Sayyid Brothers were emperors. → They were **powerful nobles/king-makers**, not rulers.
- ❌ Awadh, Bengal and Hyderabad formally declared independence. → They became **autonomous in practice** while nominally acknowledging the emperor.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Structural-primacy thesis:** *Mughal decline was primarily a crisis of the empire's own fiscal-military institutions — the mismatch between mansabdar claims and revenue-yielding jagirs — with weak succession and foreign invasion acting as accelerants rather than originators.*
- **Two-level thesis (best for "dark age" wordings):** *The eighteenth century was a decline of the imperial centre but simultaneously a redistribution of political and fiscal power to the regions; "decline" is accurate for Delhi and misleading for Bengal, Awadh or the Maratha domains.*
- **Legitimacy-survival thesis:** *Mughal power collapsed long before Mughal legitimacy did; successor states dismantled imperial authority while continuing to use imperial sanction, which is why fragmentation looked orderly rather than anarchic.*
- **Opportunity thesis (for British-ascendancy wordings):** *Decline did not invite conquest automatically; it created a competitive multi-state field in which an externally financed, naval-backed trading company could convert alliance into sovereignty.*

### 8.4 Named evidence units (claim → evidence → significance → limit)

**A. Fiscal-institutional core (jagirdari crisis)**
- **Claim:** The empire's failure was institutional before it was personal.
- **Evidence:** Growth in the number of mansabdars outran the supply of revenue-yielding jagirs, producing the scramble known as *be-jagiri*.
- **Significance:** Nobles competed for assignments rather than serving the centre, so the reward system that had bound the elite to the emperor became the mechanism of its fragmentation.
- **Limit/caution:** The precise scale of the shortfall is debated; present it as a structural mismatch identified by Satish Chandra and endorsed in Bipan Chandra's account, not as a quantified statistic.

**B. Court factionalism and the collapse of succession**
- **Claim:** Once the throne became a prize contested by noble blocs, imperial authority became derivative.
- **Evidence:** The nobility divided into Turani, Irani, Afghan and Hindustani groupings; the Sayyid Brothers (Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali) raised and deposed Farrukhsiyar and were called "king-makers"; four emperors were enthroned within 1719.
- **Significance:** Sovereignty passed from the emperor to whoever controlled the guard and the treasury, which is the operational meaning of "decline".
- **Limit/caution:** Faction was a symptom of the jagir scramble as much as an independent cause; do not present it as the root.

**C. Provincial autonomy with retained legitimacy**
- **Claim:** Fragmentation took a legalistic rather than secessionist form.
- **Evidence:** Murshid Quli Khan in Bengal, Saadat Khan in Awadh and Nizam-ul-Mulk in Hyderabad (1724) founded effectively hereditary regimes while continuing to strike coin and have the *khutba* read in the emperor's name. The same weakened centre issued the **1717 farman** under Farrukhsiyar, granting the English East India Company duty-free trade privileges in Bengal.
- **Significance:** Explains why the empire survived as a symbolic order into the nineteenth century, why later powers including the Company sought Mughal sanction for their gains, and why an imperial concession made from weakness in 1717 became the fiscal grievance that produced the Anglo-Bengal conflict of the 1750s (`basic/03`, `basic/04`).
- **Limit/caution:** Autonomy was not uniform — Awadh's and Bengal's paths differed in their relations with the centre and with zamindars. The 1717 farman was a trade privilege, not a grant of territory or sovereignty.

**D. Deccan over-stretch**
- **Claim:** Imperial expansion itself generated the fiscal and military exhaustion that broke the system.
- **Evidence:** Aurangzeb's prolonged Deccan campaigns absorbed treasury and army for decades without extinguishing Maratha resistance.
- **Significance:** Conquest added claimants to jagirs faster than it added assignable revenue, tightening the very crisis described in unit A.
- **Limit/caution:** Attributing decline principally to Aurangzeb's religious policy is a contested older reading associated with Jadunath Sarkar; do not present it as settled.

**E. External shock as revealer, not originator**
- **Claim:** Invasion measured the collapse rather than producing it.
- **Evidence:** Nadir Shah's victory at Karnal (1739), the sack of Delhi and the removal of the Peacock Throne and Kohinoor; Abdali's repeated invasions between 1748 and 1761.
- **Significance:** A state that could be defeated in a single field engagement and looted at its capital had already lost the fiscal-military capacity to defend itself.
- **Limit/caution:** The shock was nonetheless real — the loss of prestige and bullion accelerated provincial disengagement; avoid dismissing invasion as merely "symptomatic".

**F. Regional vitality (counter-evidence)**
- **Claim:** Central collapse coexisted with regional consolidation.
- **Evidence:** Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan and Alivardi Khan maintained a strong revenue administration and commercial output; Marathas, Sikhs and Jats built new military-fiscal orders.
- **Significance:** Supports the two-level thesis and blocks the "uniform decadence" answer.
- **Limit/caution:** Regional prosperity was uneven and often war-disrupted; do not overstate it into a claim that nothing was lost.

### 8.5 Causal mechanism (use when the question asks *how*, not *what*)

```text
Deccan over-stretch + rising elite numbers
        -> claims on jagirs exceed assignable revenue (be-jagiri)
        -> nobles maximise short-term extraction from peasants/zamindars
        -> agrarian resistance + falling remittance to centre
        -> centre cannot pay/discipline nobility
        -> factional capture of succession (Sayyid Brothers, 1719)
        -> governors convert office into hereditary autonomy
        -> centre defenceless against Karnal (1739) and Abdali
        -> multi-state competitive field open to Company intervention
```

### 8.7 Verdict scaffolds

- **Weighting verdict:** "Foreign invasion was decisive in timing but not in causation: Karnal was possible only because the jagir-agrarian crisis had already hollowed the fiscal-military core."
- **Two-level verdict:** "The century was a decline of the Mughal centre and a transformation of Indian regional polity; a single label misdescribes both."
- **Continuity verdict:** "What survived was the Mughal form — office titles, revenue vocabulary, coinage and khutba; what disappeared was the centre's capacity to enforce them."
- **Forward-linking verdict:** "Decline supplied the opportunity for British ascendancy; it did not supply the outcome, which required the Company's naval reach, credit and Bengal's revenue."

### 8.8 Factual-risk cautions

- Do not date the "fall" of the empire to 1707; the dynasty formally continued until 1857 and the answer should say authority, not existence, collapsed.
- Do not state a figure for Nadir Shah's plunder; describe it qualitatively.
- Do not claim provincial governors declared independence — they retained Mughal legitimacy formally.
- Do not make Aurangzeb's religious policy the stated cause; present it as a contested and insufficient explanation.
- Keep the *be-jagiri* concept distinct from ordinary corruption: it is a structural shortfall of assignable revenue.
- Panipat III (1761) belongs to the Maratha-Abdali contest; it is a consequence of the fragmented order, not a cause of Mughal decline.
