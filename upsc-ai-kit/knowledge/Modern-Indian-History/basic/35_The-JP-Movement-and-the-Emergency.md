## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — an economic and political crisis produced a mass movement.**

- ✅ By 1973 **inflation, unemployment, food scarcity, drought and the oil shock** had eroded Indira Gandhi's popularity.
- ✅ Student agitations in **Gujarat (Nav Nirman)** and **Bihar** grew into a nationwide protest under **Jayaprakash Narayan (JP)**, who called for "**Total Revolution (Sampoorna Kranti)**."
- ✅ The **1974 railway strike** and the movement's momentum challenged the government directly.
- ⚠️ The movement drew on **students, the middle class, traders and the intelligentsia** rather than the rural or urban poor.

**Foundation — the Emergency (1975–77) suspended democracy, then democracy reasserted itself.**

- ✅ The **Allahabad High Court judgment (12 June 1975)** unseating Indira triggered the national Emergency: the proclamation was dated **25 June 1975** under Article 352 and publicly announced on **26 June**.
- ✅ Civil liberties were suspended; the press was censored; opposition leaders were jailed under **MISA**; the **42nd Amendment** expanded central power.
- ✅ **Sanjay Gandhi** became an extra-constitutional power centre; **compulsory sterilisation** and slum demolitions were the era's worst excesses.
- ✅ In the **1977 elections**, the electorate defeated Congress and ended the Emergency — a verdict for civil liberties.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: The **Allahabad High Court verdict (June 1975)**, not the JP movement alone, was the immediate trigger for the Emergency.

- ❌ The Emergency was declared under Article 356. → It was a **national emergency under Article 352**.
- ❌ JP led a movement of workers and peasants. → It was led mainly by **students, the middle class and traders**.
- ❌ The 44th Amendment was passed during the Emergency. → The **42nd Amendment (1976)** was; the **44th (1978)** later reversed many of its provisions.
- ❌ Indira was unseated by the Supreme Court in 1975. → It was the **Allahabad High Court**; the SC later granted a conditional stay.
- ❌ The Emergency was ended by the courts. → It was ended by the **1977 electoral verdict**.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Convergence thesis:** *The Emergency was produced by the convergence of three separate crises — an economic crisis of inflation, scarcity and unemployment; a political crisis of mass agitation that the government could not absorb; and a legal crisis created by a court judgment against the Prime Minister personally — none of which alone would have sufficed.*
- **Personalisation thesis:** *The Emergency was possible because the consolidation of 1969–71 had removed every institutional check within the ruling party (`basic/34`); a leader with a plebiscitary mandate and no internal opposition could treat a judicial verdict against herself as a national emergency.*
- **Split-verdict thesis:** *Indian democracy both failed and passed the test: the institutional safeguards — Parliament, the ruling party, much of the press and, in the detention cases, the higher judiciary — did not prevent the suspension of liberties, while the electorate reversed it decisively at the first opportunity.*
- **Middle-class-movement thesis:** *The JP movement's strength and its limitation were the same fact — it mobilised students, the middle class, traders and the intelligentsia with unprecedented moral authority, and it never acquired a rural or working-class base, which is why it could destabilise a government and not sustain one after 1977.*

### 8.4 Named evidence units (claim → evidence → significance → limit)

**A. The economic crisis**
- **Evidence:** ✅ **Two failed monsoons in 1972–73**, drought and inflation, deepened by the **1973 oil shock**; ✅ by 1973 **inflation, unemployment, food scarcity, drought and the oil shock** had eroded Indira Gandhi's popularity.
- **Significance:** The "Garibi Hatao" mandate of 1971 (`basic/34`) was won on a promise of redistribution; its economic failure within two years is what converted a landslide into a legitimacy crisis.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not cite inflation rates or output figures without verified support.

**B. The movement**
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **Nav Nirman** student agitation began in **Gujarat in January 1974**; ✅ the **Bihar student movement erupted in March 1974** and **Jayaprakash Narayan (JP)** took leadership, calling for "**Total Revolution (Sampoorna Kranti)**"; ✅ an all-India **railway strike of 22 days** followed in **May 1974**; ✅ on **25 June 1975** JP's Delhi rally announced countrywide civil disobedience.
- **Significance:** JP's authority came from outside electoral politics — a Gandhian and socialist veteran who had refused office — which gave the movement a moral standing the opposition parties lacked and made it far more threatening than their combined strength.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ The movement drew on **students, the middle class, traders and the intelligentsia** rather than the rural or urban poor; ⚠️ its call for the police and administration to disobey unlawful orders was constitutionally contested and was used by the government to justify its response — state this without endorsing either side.

**C. The judicial trigger and the proclamation**
- **Evidence:** ✅ On **12 June 1975** the **Allahabad High Court** (Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha), on **Raj Narain's** petition, set aside Indira Gandhi's election; ✅ the **Emergency proclamation was dated 25 June 1975** under **Article 352** and was **publicly announced on 26 June**; ✅ opposition leaders were arrested under **MISA**.
- **Significance:** The trigger was personal rather than national, which is the decisive analytical fact — a threat to one office-holder's seat was converted into a proclamation of internal emergency.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ It was the **Allahabad High Court**, not the Supreme Court, which later granted a conditional stay; ✅ the proclamation was under **Article 352** (national emergency), not Article 356. ✅ Keep the dating precise: **dated 25 June, announced 26 June**.

**D. The Emergency regime**
- **Evidence:** ✅ Civil liberties were suspended, the press censored and opposition leaders jailed under **MISA**; ✅ the **42nd Amendment (1976)** expanded central and executive power; ✅ **Sanjay Gandhi** became an extra-constitutional power centre, and **compulsory sterilisation** and **slum demolitions** were the era's worst excesses.
- **Significance:** The extra-constitutional character of Sanjay Gandhi's authority is the key institutional point — the excesses were committed by a power centre with no office, no accountability and no legal existence, which is what unaccountable emergency power produces.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ Do not state sterilisation or demolition numbers; the figures are contested and vary widely by source.

**E. The reversal**
- **Evidence:** ✅ In the **March 1977 elections** the electorate defeated the Congress and the Emergency ended — a verdict for civil liberties; ✅ the **44th Amendment (1978)** later reversed many of the 42nd Amendment's provisions.
- **Significance:** The reversal was electoral, not judicial or institutional, which is why the "test passed" verdict must specify *who* passed it — the voters, and not the institutions designed to prevent such a situation.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ The 44th Amendment's constitutional content belongs to the Polity owner; record here only that the reversal was legislated after 1977.

### 8.5 Causal mechanism

```text
1971 landslide + personalised leadership + weakened party organisation (basic/34)
   -> 1972-73 monsoon failure, inflation, oil shock -> mandate discredited
   -> student agitations (Gujarat Jan 1974, Bihar Mar 1974) -> JP supplies moral leadership
   -> railway strike May 1974 -> the state's economic lifeline challenged
   -> Allahabad HC judgment 12 June 1975 -> the leader's own position at stake
   -> JP's 25 June rally announces civil disobedience -> the two crises fuse
   -> proclamation dated 25 June, announced 26 June -> MISA, censorship, 42nd Amendment
   -> excesses under an extra-constitutional power centre
   -> March 1977 election -> electoral reversal
```

### 8.6 Counter-evidence, balance and caution

- **What held:** the electoral machinery itself, which produced a free verdict in 1977; sections of the press and judiciary that resisted; and the absence of any attempt to abolish elections permanently.
- **What failed:** Parliament, the ruling party, the constitutional amendment process and, in the detention jurisprudence, the apex court's protection of personal liberty.
- **Regional variation:** the Emergency's excesses were experienced very unevenly, with northern India — particularly the sterilisation and demolition campaigns — far more severely affected, which is reflected in the 1977 electoral geography.

### 8.7 Verdict scaffolds

- **Test verdict:** "The Emergency was a test the institutions largely failed and the electorate decisively passed — which is why 1977 is remembered as a democratic vindication rather than an institutional one."
- **Movement verdict:** "JP gave the opposition what it could not generate for itself — moral authority — and could not give it what it most needed, a social base beyond the towns."
- **Causal verdict:** "An economic failure produced a political challenge, a court judgment made it personal, and a leadership without internal checks answered a threat to itself with a proclamation about the nation."

### 8.8 Factual-risk cautions

- Nav Nirman = **Gujarat, January 1974**; Bihar movement = **March 1974**; railway strike = **May 1974 (22 days)**.
- Allahabad High Court judgment = **12 June 1975**, Justice **Jagmohanlal Sinha**, on **Raj Narain's** petition.
- Proclamation **dated 25 June 1975** under **Article 352**, **publicly announced 26 June** — the standard chronology trap.
- Detentions were under **MISA**; the **42nd Amendment is 1976**; the **44th Amendment is 1978** and came *after* the Emergency.
- The Emergency was ended by the **1977 electoral verdict**, not by the courts.
- Do not quantify sterilisations, demolitions or detentions.
