## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — the 1930s widened nationalism from political freedom to social and economic justice.**

- ✅ The rise of the **Left** in the 1930s reflected the influence of socialist ideas on leaders such as **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose**.
- ✅ The **Congress Socialist Party (1934)** was formed within Congress by leaders including **Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Acharya Narendra Dev**, **Ram Manohar Lohia** and **Minoo Masani**.
- ✅ Communist groups, **M.N. Roy**, the **CPI formation point at Kanpur (1925)** and conspiracy cases such as **Peshawar**, **Kanpur** and **Meerut** marked the growth and repression of communist politics.
- ✅ The **Karachi resolution (1931)** on Fundamental Rights and Economic Programme and the **Faizpur Congress (1936)** agrarian emphasis show how social-economic demands entered Congress politics.

**Foundation — peasants, workers and princely-state subjects created pressure from below.**

- ✅ The **AITUC (1920)** became an early all-India labour platform, with Lala Lajpat Rai as its first president.
- ✅ Textile and railway strikes indicated that industrial workers were becoming politically visible in the freedom struggle.
- ✅ Peasant movements included **Awadh Kisan mobilisation (Baba Ramchandra)**, **Eka (1921–22)**, **Moplah (1921)** and **Bardoli (1928)**. Moplah combined agrarian and anti-colonial grievances but also acquired a grave communal dimension; it must not be romanticised as a purely class movement.
- ✅ The **All India Kisan Sabha (1936)** brought agrarian issues onto an organised national platform.
- ✅ In the late 1930s–40s, **Bakasht** struggles in Bihar, the **Warli Adivasi movement (1945)**, **Tebhaga (1946)** and **Telangana (from 1946)** connected tenancy, rent, forced labour and land questions with left organisation.
- ✅ The **All India States Peoples' Conference (1927)** coordinated struggles for rights in princely states.
- ✅ Subhas Bose's **Haripura (1938)** and **Tripuri (1939)** presidency episodes highlighted tensions between left-radical planning and the Gandhian-majority leadership.

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)

- ✅ **AITUC** was founded in **1920**; **Lala Lajpat Rai** was its first president.
- ✅ **Bardoli Satyagraha (1928)** was a Gujarat revenue struggle led by **Vallabhbhai Patel**, who earned the title **Sardar**.
- ✅ **Congress Socialist Party** was formed in **1934** within Congress.
- ✅ CSP leaders included **Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Acharya Narendra Dev**, **Ram Manohar Lohia** and **Minoo Masani**.
- ✅ **All India Kisan Sabha** was formed in **1936**, with leaders such as **Sahajanand Saraswati** and **N.G. Ranga**.
- ✅ **All India States Peoples' Conference** was founded in **1927** for struggles in princely states.
- ✅ **Tebhaga (1946)** demanded two-thirds of produce for Bengal sharecroppers; **Telangana** began in 1946 against landlord oppression under the Hyderabad state.
- ✅ **Subhas Chandra Bose** was Congress president at **Haripura (1938)** and again at **Tripuri (1939)** before resigning.
- ✅ **Forward Bloc** was formed by Bose in **1939**.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: Do not merge all left, labour and peasant bodies into one organisation; their dates and social bases differ.

- ❌ AITUC was founded in 1936. → ✅ AITUC was founded in **1920**.
- ❌ AIKS was founded in 1920. → ✅ All India Kisan Sabha was formed in **1936**.
- ❌ Bardoli was a mill-worker strike. → ✅ Bardoli was a **revenue/peasant satyagraha** in Gujarat.
- ❌ Moplah was either only agrarian or only communal. → It began in an agrarian and anti-colonial setting but included communal violence; both dimensions must be stated.
- ❌ Patel became "Sardar" after Independence. → ✅ The title became associated with him after **Bardoli (1928)**.
- ❌ Forward Bloc was formed before the Tripuri crisis. → ✅ Bose formed it in **1939** after resigning from Congress presidentship.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Agenda-widening thesis:** *The Left, peasant and labour movements of the 1930s converted swaraj from a transfer-of-power demand into a social question, forcing the Congress to specify what independence would mean for the tenant, the worker and the untouchable — the Karachi resolution and the Faizpur agrarian emphasis are the institutional proof.*
- **Two-track thesis:** *Popular mobilisation ran on two tracks that met only intermittently: Congress-directed campaigns with disciplined organisation, and autonomous class movements with their own leaders, demands and timing; the movement's strength lay in the overlap and its limits in the gap.*
- **Deepening-democracy thesis:** *States' Peoples' movements made the freedom struggle genuinely all-India by extending it into the third of the subcontinent that paramountcy had insulated from nationalist politics — which is why princely integration in 1947 had a political base and not merely a diplomatic one.*
- **Structural-conflict thesis:** *The Tripuri crisis was not a clash of personalities but the point at which the Congress had to decide whether it was a coalition seeking independence or a party with a social programme; the Gandhian leadership chose the first, and Bose's exit is the price of that choice.*

### 8.4 Bank A — The Left inside and outside Congress

- **Claim:** Socialism entered Indian nationalism through two distinct doors, one inside the Congress and one outside it.
- **Evidence:** ✅ Socialist ideas influenced **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose**; the **Congress Socialist Party (1934)** was formed inside the Congress by **Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, Ram Manohar Lohia** and **Minoo Masani**; outside it, **M.N. Roy**, the **CPI formation point at Kanpur (1925)** and the **Peshawar, Kanpur and Meerut conspiracy cases (Meerut, 1929–33)** mark both the growth and the repression of communist politics.
- **Significance:** The CSP's decision to work inside the Congress rather than against it is the single most consequential organisational choice of the Indian Left — it kept the national movement united and it subordinated class politics to the anti-colonial front.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not treat the CSP and the CPI as one current; their relationship to the Congress and to the 1942 movement differed sharply.

### 8.5 Bank B — Peasant movements by phase

| Phase | Movement | Grievance and form | Analytical point |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ 1920–22 | **Awadh Kisan Sabha** (Baba Ramchandra), **Eka (1921–22)**, **Moplah/Mappila (1921)** | Rent above recorded rates, *thekedar* oppression, tenancy insecurity | Autonomous initiative during a Congress campaign (see `basic/20`) |
| ✅ 1928 | **Bardoli Satyagraha** | Revenue enhancement; disciplined no-tax satyagraha under **Vallabhbhai Patel**, given the title **Sardar** by the women of Bardoli | The model of Congress-directed agrarian mobilisation |
| ✅ 1930–34 | No-tax and no-rent campaigns within CDM | Revenue and rent refusal | Agrarian grievance harnessed to the national campaign (`basic/22`) |
| ✅ 1936 onward | **All India Kisan Sabha** (**Sahajanand Saraswati**, **N.G. Ranga**); **Faizpur Congress, 1936** | Organised national agrarian platform; agrarian question inside Congress | Institutionalisation of the peasant question |
| ✅ Late 1930s–47 | **Bakasht** (Bihar), **Warli** (1945), **Tebhaga** (1946), **Telangana** (from 1946) | Sharecropping shares, forced labour, landlord power, land redistribution | Radicalisation as the colonial state weakened |

**Bardoli's organisational anatomy (high-value evidence):** ✅ Patel divided the taluqa into **thirteen workers' camps (*chhavanis*)**, each under an experienced leader; **one hundred political workers** drawn from across the province, assisted by about **1,500 volunteers**, many of them students, formed the movement's army; a publications bureau brought out the daily ***Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika*** carrying reports, speeches and pictures of *jabti* (confiscation) proceedings; and an **intelligence wing** identified wavering peasants, shadowed them, and gathered advance information on confiscation moves so that villagers could lock their houses or move to neighbouring Baroda.
- **Significance:** This is the most detailed available demonstration of what "disciplined satyagraha" actually meant operationally, and it converts Bardoli from a name into an argument about organisation.
- **Limit/caution:** Bardoli was a single taluqa of relatively prosperous peasant proprietors; do not generalise its methods or its success to Indian agrarian politics as a whole.

**Tebhaga and Telangana:** ✅ **Tebhaga (1946)** demanded two-thirds of the produce for Bengal sharecroppers; ✅ **Telangana (from 1946)** was directed against landlord oppression within the Hyderabad state. ⚠️ Both connect agrarian radicalism to the transfer-of-power moment and carry directly into `basic/28` and `basic/38`.

**Awadh and Gujarat compared, 1920–30 (a standard "why did these regions diverge?" demand):**

| | ✅ Awadh / United Provinces | ✅ Gujarat |
|---|---|---|
| Agrarian structure | Taluqdari — a powerful landlord class restored by the British after 1857 (`basic/11`), with tenants facing rent enhancement, *thekedar* rent-farming and eviction | Ryotwari-type peasant proprietorship — cultivators held directly from the state, so the antagonist was the **revenue demand**, not a landlord |
| Consequent target | The **landlord and his agents**; demands were about rent, ejection and forced labour | The **government**; demands were about revenue assessment and remission |
| Leadership | Local and low-caste grassroots leaders — **Baba Ramchandra**, and in the Eka movement **Madari Pasi** — who resisted Congress discipline | Congress leaders of the same social world as the cultivators — **Vallabhbhai Patel** at **Bardoli (1928)**, building on **Kheda (1918)** (`basic/19`) |
| Relation to the national movement | ✅ **Autonomous**: Eka's contact with the nationalists **diminished** and it went its own way; suppressed by **March 1922** | ✅ **Integrated**: a disciplined Congress campaign with camps, volunteers, a daily paper and an intelligence wing |
| Outcome | Repression; the agrarian question re-emerged only through the AIKS from 1936 | A model of satyagraha that fed directly into Civil Disobedience's no-tax campaigns (`basic/22`) |

- ⚠️ **The explanatory variable is the land system.** Where an Indian landlord stood between the peasant and the state, agrarian conflict was directed inward and the Congress — which included landholding supporters — could not lead it without splitting. Where the cultivator faced the state directly, agrarian and national grievance were the same grievance, and Congress leadership was natural. This single argument answers most "why did agrarian politics differ across regions?" questions and links this owner to `basic/07`.

### 8.6 Bank C — Labour

- **Claim:** Industrial workers became a political constituency in their own right, distinct from both Congress and the Left.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **AITUC (1920)** became the first all-India labour platform, with **Lala Lajpat Rai** as first president; ✅ textile and railway strikes made industrial workers politically visible; ✅ the **Karachi resolution (1931)** on Fundamental Rights and Economic Programme brought labour protection into Congress doctrine.
- **Significance:** Karachi is the pivot — it committed the Congress on paper to a social programme, which is what later made the Congress ministries' labour and tenancy legislation both possible and disappointing.
- **Limit/caution:** Trade unionism was concentrated in a few industrial centres; do not describe the working class as a national political force in this period.

### 8.7 Bank D — Tribal resistance and the Munda *ulgulan* (routed demand)

- **Claim:** Tribal movements were not pre-political survivals; by the late nineteenth century they had acquired an explicitly anti-colonial political horizon.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The rebellion (***ulgulan***) of the Munda tribesmen led by **Birsa Munda** occurred in **1899–1900**. For over thirty years Munda *sardars* had struggled against the destruction of their **system of common landholding** by the intrusion of **jagirdars, thikadars (revenue farmers) and merchant moneylenders** — the outsiders known as ***dikus***. ✅ Birsa, born in a poor share-cropper household in **1874**, had a vision in **1895**, declared himself a divine messenger with healing powers, and mobilised Munda peasants across Chota Nagpur, speaking of a lost golden age (*satjug*) and the present dark age (*kaljug*). ✅ On **Christmas Eve 1899** he proclaimed a rebellion to establish Munda rule, gathering a force of about **6,000 Mundas** armed with swords, spears, battle-axes and bows; ✅ he was **captured in early February 1900 and died in jail in June**.
- **Significance:** ✅ What was new, as Bandyopadhyay stresses, was the **political awareness** — Birsa's aim was not merely to drive out the *dikus* but "to put an end to the British Raj" and to establish a Birsa Raj and a Birsaite religion. That connection between local land alienation and the colonial state as a whole is what distinguishes late-nineteenth-century tribal movements from earlier localised risings.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ Note the movement's own discrimination — the **non-tribal poor were not to be attacked** — which shows a targeted rather than indiscriminate hostility. Do not present tribal revolt as generic "primitive rebellion"; and keep Birsa's *ulgulan* (1899–1900) distinct from the Santhal Hool (1855–56, `basic/07` and `basic/11`).

**Related tribal risings to name alongside the *ulgulan*:** ✅ the **Kols of Chhotanagpur rebelled from 1820 to 1837**, thousands being massacred before British authority could be re-imposed; ✅ the **hill tribesmen of Rampa in coastal Andhra revolted in March 1879** against **the depredations of the government-supported mansabdar and the new restrictive forest regulations**, and the authorities had to mobilise **regiments of infantry, a squadron of cavalry and two companies of sappers and miners** before rebels "numbering several thousands" were defeated by the end of **1880**; ✅ the **Santhal Hool of 1855–56** under **Sido and Kanhu** (`basic/07`, `basic/11`).
- **Analytical use:** Rampa is the most useful of these for a comparison answer because it names both drivers in one case — a **state-backed intermediary** and **forest regulation** — and because the scale of the force required exposes how seriously the colonial state treated "primitive" revolt.

> **Evidence owner — colonial forest policy:** the legislative chain that produced the forest grievance in every case above (**1806** naval-timber interest → **railway sleepers** → **Forest Department 1864** → **Government Forests Act 1865** → **Indian Forest Act 1878** with its **reserved / protected / unclassified** classification and complete state monopoly → **c. 1900, about 20 per cent of India's land area** under forest administration), together with the restriction of **jhum/podu**, subsistence hunting and pastoral access, and the princely-state versus directly-administered variation (Tehri Garhwal versus Kumaun), is stored once in **`basic/07` §8.8**. Draw the mechanism from there and use this file for the movements themselves; do not duplicate the statutory detail here.

**Nineteenth-century resistance compared with post-1947 policy (for "compare the two eras" demands):**

| | ✅ Colonial era (Kol 1820–37, Santhal 1855–56, Rampa 1879, Munda 1899–1900) | ✅ Post-1947 (see `basic/31`) |
|---|---|---|
| Driver | Land alienation to *dikus*, revenue farmers and moneylenders; forest regulation; state-backed intermediaries | Displacement by development, continued land alienation, forest-access restriction |
| Form of claim | Armed revolt with a messianic-religious idiom; by 1899 an explicit anti-Raj political horizon | Electoral and constitutional claims; autonomy, statehood, scheduled-area protection |
| State response | Military suppression, then **administrative exceptionalism** — the Santhal Parganas as a separate unit recognising tribal distinctiveness (`basic/07`) | Constitutional exceptionalism — **Fifth and Sixth Schedules**, autonomous councils, statehood |
| Underlying theory | Tribal areas as ungovernable by ordinary law; exclusion as a security device | **Integration without imposition** — development along the tribes' own genius |
| Continuity to name | Both eras answer tribal grievance with a **special legal regime** rather than by removing the economic cause | Autonomy granted; land alienation and displacement persist |

- ⚠️ **The analytical continuity is the highest-value point:** colonial "excluded areas" and republican "scheduled areas" are different in purpose and similar in method — both isolate tribal territory under a distinct legal regime instead of resolving the land and forest questions that generated the grievance. That single sentence carries a 15-mark comparison.

### 8.8 Bank E — States' Peoples' movements and the Congress's internal conflict

- **Claim:** Paramountcy insulated a third of India from nationalist politics until the movement built its own instrument to reach inside.
- **Evidence:** ✅ The **All India States Peoples' Conference (1927)** coordinated struggles for rights within princely states.
- **Significance:** AISPC created the political cadre and legitimacy on which the integration of 1947 depended; without it, accession would have been a purely diplomatic transaction between the Government of India and the princes (see `basic/28`).
- **Limit/caution:** The Congress long avoided direct intervention in princely states to preserve its constitutional position; describe the relationship as a cautious and shifting one.

**The Tripuri crisis (routed demand — Forward Bloc and 1939 alignments):** ✅ **Subhas Chandra Bose** was Congress president at **Haripura (1938)** and was re-elected at **Tripuri (1939)** against the wishes of the Gandhian leadership; ✅ following the crisis he **resigned and formed the Forward Bloc in 1939**.
- **Significance:** The crisis is best read as an institutional test of whether the Congress presidency carried independent authority against the Working Committee and the Gandhian core; the answer — that it did not — settled the Congress's internal constitution for the endgame.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ The dispute combined ideology (planning, a more confrontational war strategy) with organisational control; do not reduce it to either factor alone, and do not project Bose's later wartime course backwards onto 1939.

### 8.9 Counter-evidence, balance and variation

- **Congress achievement to concede:** it absorbed socialist, peasant and labour demands into a common national programme without fragmenting — an outcome few anti-colonial movements achieved.
- **Congress limitation to state:** its social programme stopped short of challenging landlordism directly, and its ministries' agrarian legislation was cautious (`basic/24`).
- **Autonomy of movements:** Eka, Moplah, Warli, Tebhaga and Telangana each had leaders, demands and timing of their own; several were suppressed by or after the transfer of power, not only by the colonial state.
- ⚠️ **Moplah/Mappila must not be romanticised** — it combined agrarian and anti-colonial grievance with grave communal violence; both dimensions must appear in any answer.

### 8.10 Verdict scaffolds

- **Agenda verdict:** "The Left did not capture the national movement; it changed what the national movement had to promise, and that promise became the agenda of the republic."
- **Autonomy verdict:** "Popular movements were neither Congress creations nor Congress rivals; they were parallel mobilisations whose partial convergence gave Indian nationalism its mass character."
- **Princely-states verdict:** "The States' Peoples' movements turned princely India from a diplomatic problem into a political one, which is precisely why its integration succeeded so quickly in 1947."
- **Tribal verdict:** "By 1899 tribal resistance had learned to name the Raj as its enemy; the *ulgulan* is the point at which land alienation became an anti-colonial politics."

### 8.11 Factual-risk cautions

- AITUC = **1920**; CPI/Kanpur = **1925**; AISPC = **1927**; Bardoli = **1928**; Meerut case = **1929–33**; Karachi = **1931**; CSP = **1934**; AIKS and Faizpur = **1936**; Haripura = **1938**; Tripuri and Forward Bloc = **1939**; Warli = **1945**; Tebhaga = **1946**; Telangana = from **1946**.
- The **AIKS** is 1936 and the **AITUC** 1920 — the commonest date confusion in this topic.
- Patel's title **Sardar** dates from **Bardoli (1928)**, not from 1947.
- Birsa Munda's *ulgulan* = **1899–1900**, Chota Nagpur, against *dikus* and the Raj; he died in **jail in June 1900**.
- Do not assign membership figures to the CSP, CPI, AITUC or AIKS.
- The Bardoli organisational figures (**thirteen camps, 100 workers, about 1,500 volunteers**) are the ones attested in the repository's sources.
