## 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Foundation — a planned, mixed economy gave way to liberalisation.**

- ✅ India built a **mixed economy** — the **IPRs of 1948 and 1956** and the **Mahalanobis Second Plan (1956)** gave the **public sector** the "commanding heights," especially heavy industry.
- ✅ Growth (~4% a year to the mid-1960s) far exceeded the colonial rate, but a maze of **licences and controls** ("licence-permit raj") throttled efficiency.
- ✅ A **balance-of-payments crisis in 1991** forced **liberalisation** under **Narasimha Rao** and **Manmohan Singh**.
- ⚠️ The model shifted from **state-led import substitution** toward a more open, market-oriented economy.

**Foundation — land, caste, gender and communal questions defined social change.**

- ✅ **Land reforms** abolished **zamindari** (largely by 1956) and made about **20 million superior tenants** owners. Tenancy and ceiling results were uneven: records were unreliable, evasion and eviction were common, and **Bhoodan** could not substitute for structural reform.
- ✅ The **Green Revolution** raised foodgrain output and was initially concentrated in north-western wheat regions. Chandra argues that it later spread, that the technology was broadly scale-neutral and that early predictions of general class polarisation were overstated; critics continue to stress unequal access, regional lags and ecological costs.
- ✅ **Caste**: untouchability was abolished, reservations extended, and from **Ambedkar** to the **Dalit Panthers**, **BSP** and **Mandal (1990)**, caste became central to politics.
- ✅ **Women**: the **Hindu-law reforms of 1955–56** substantially expanded rights in marriage, succession, guardianship, adoption and maintenance, but did not create complete legal or social equality; a new women's movement (1970s–90s) took up dowry, rape and domestic violence; **communalism** revived from the 1960s and turned endemic after the late 1970s.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: **Zamindari abolition largely succeeded**, but **tenancy and ceiling reforms largely failed** — don't lump all land reform together.

- ❌ All land reforms succeeded equally. → **Zamindari abolition** worked; **tenancy/ceiling** reform mostly failed.
- ❌ Bhoodan redistributed most donated land. → Much Bhoodan land was **never effectively distributed**.
- ❌ The Green Revolution either benefited all regions equally or permanently widened inequality everywhere. → It was initially concentrated; Chandra stresses later diffusion and small-farmer viability, while other scholarship identifies persistent regional, class and ecological inequalities.
- ❌ Naxalbari was in the 1970s. → It was **1967**; CPI(ML) came in **1969**.
- ❌ Reforms began under Rajiv Gandhi. → **Liberalisation began in 1991** under Narasimha Rao.

### 8.2 Qualified thesis options

- **Design-and-deficit thesis:** *India's planned economy achieved precisely what it prioritised — a heavy-industrial base, self-reliance and growth far above the colonial rate — and failed at what it did not: agriculture until the mid-1960s, employment, literacy and efficiency, so the model's failures were consequences of its choices rather than of its execution.*
- **Disaggregated-land-reform thesis:** *Land reform must be assessed component by component: zamindari abolition largely succeeded because it had powerful political backing and a clear target; tenancy and ceiling reform largely failed because they required the state to act against the very rural classes on whose support it depended.*
- **Contested-Green-Revolution thesis:** *The Green Revolution ended India's food-import dependence and generated a genuine scholarly dispute about its distributive effects; the defensible position states the output achievement, notes the initial regional and class concentration, records both the diffusion argument and the inequality critique, and adds the ecological cost.*
- **Law-versus-society thesis:** *On caste and gender the Indian state legislated ahead of social change — untouchability abolished, reservations extended and women's rights substantially expanded through the Hindu-law reforms — but formal equality remained incomplete and unevenly enforced; that gap helped turn caste and gender into political movements rather than merely legal categories.*

### 8.4 Bank A — The economic model and its break

- **Evidence:** ✅ The **IPRs of 1948 and 1956** and the **Mahalanobis Second Plan (1956)** gave the **public sector the "commanding heights"**, especially in heavy industry; ✅ growth of about **4 per cent a year to the mid-1960s** far exceeded the colonial rate, while a maze of **licences and controls** — the "licence-permit raj" — throttled efficiency; ✅ a **balance-of-payments crisis in 1991** forced **liberalisation** under **Narasimha Rao** and **Manmohan Singh**.
- **Significance:** The juxtaposition is the answer to "was planning a failure?" — it delivered a structural transformation that colonial India had been denied, and it built in an allocative inefficiency that eventually made the model unsustainable.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ The ~4 per cent figure is the source's and applies **to the mid-1960s**; do not extend it to later decades or substitute other growth rates. Detailed reform content belongs to the Economy owner.

### 8.5 Bank B — Land reform, disaggregated

| Component | Outcome | Evidence | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ Zamindari abolition | **Largely succeeded** (largely complete by 1956) | About **20 million superior tenants** became owners | Clear target, strong political will, and the intermediaries were a politically weak class |
| ✅ Tenancy reform | **Largely failed** | Unreliable records, widespread evasion and eviction | Oral and concealed tenancies were poorly recorded; the beneficiaries were politically weak and the losers were not |
| ✅ Ceiling legislation | **Largely failed** | Evasion through benami and family partition | The rural classes it targeted were the state's own political base |
| ✅ **Bhoodan** | **Could not substitute for structural reform** | Vinoba Bhave; began at **Pochampalli, 1951**; much donated land was **never effectively distributed** | Voluntary donation cannot reallocate land at scale |

- **Analytical use:** the table *is* the answer to the "zamindari succeeded where tenancy failed" demand — the explanatory variable is the political weight of the class being expropriated.
- **Limit/caution:** ✅ Tenancy-area estimates are rough because oral and concealed tenancies were poorly recorded; state this rather than quoting precise percentages.

### 8.6 Bank C — Agriculture and agrarian struggle

- **Green Revolution:** ✅ The **HYV package** from the **mid-1960s** was initially concentrated in **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh** and in wheat. ✅ **Chandra argues** that it later spread to rice regions and other states, that the technology was broadly **scale-neutral**, and that early predictions of general class polarisation were overstated; ✅ **critics continue to stress unequal access, regional lags and ecological costs**.
- **Significance:** Presenting both positions is what a "critically examine" answer requires; asserting either alone is a factual-discipline failure in this topic.
- **Agrarian struggles:** ✅ **Telangana** (`basic/23`); ✅ the **Naxalbari uprising (1967)** launching the Naxalite movement, with **CPI(ML) formed in 1969**; ✅ the farmers' movements of the 1980s — **Nasik (Sharad Joshi)** in 1980 and **Sisauli (Mahendra Singh Tikait)** in 1986.
- **Significance of the sequence:** the class content changes completely across the three — landless and tenant struggles in the 1940s–60s, and *market* demands (prices, inputs, credit) from surplus-producing farmers in the 1980s. That shift is itself evidence of what the Green Revolution did to rural class structure.
- **Limit/caution:** Do not merge Naxalbari-type struggles with the 1980s farmers' movements; they represent opposed rural interests.

### 8.7 Bank D — Caste and gender

- **Caste:** ✅ Untouchability was abolished and reservations extended; ✅ the political trajectory runs from **Ambedkar** through the **Dalit Panthers** and the **BSP** to **Mandal (1990)** (`basic/37`).
- **Gender:** ✅ The proposed **Hindu Code** was enacted through the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955**, and the **Hindu Succession**, **Hindu Minority and Guardianship**, and **Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Acts, 1956**; these substantially expanded women's rights within Hindu personal law but did not create complete equality. ✅ A new **women's movement from the 1970s to the 1990s** took up dowry, rape and domestic violence.
- **Communalism:** ✅ Communalism **revived from the 1960s and turned endemic after the late 1970s** (`basic/36`, `basic/37`).
- **Significance:** The common pattern across all three is the law-versus-society gap — formal equality arrived early and social transformation did not follow, which converted each into a political movement demanding enforcement and representation rather than merely legislation.
- **Limit/caution:** ⚠️ Do not import contemporary statistics, scheme names or case law; the Social Justice, Polity and Indian Society owners hold those. Keep the treatment historical and analytical.

### 8.9 Bank E — Two long threads for synthesis questions

**Thread 1 — self-reliance as an idea, 1905 to 1991:**

| Phase | Content of "self-reliance" | Institutional form | What changed it |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Swadeshi, 1905** (`basic/15`) | Boycott of foreign goods plus **indigenous enterprise and national education** | Swadeshi stores and ventures; National Council of Education (1906) | Repression; undercapitalisation |
| ✅ **Gandhian, 1920–47** (`basic/20`, `basic/22`) | **Khadi and charkha** — self-reliance as village production and moral discipline, not import substitution | Constructive programme; national schools | Independence transferred the argument from society to the state |
| ✅ **Nationalist economics, 1928–39** (`basic/22`, `basic/24`) | Fiscal and monetary autonomy — **protective tariffs**, a **lower rupee–sterling ratio**, coastal shipping reserved for Indian companies (Gandhi's eleven points, 1930) | Congress economic demands; the ministries' limited scope | Independence made these achievable by legislation |
| ✅ **Planning, 1948–66** (`basic/32`, and §8.4 above) | Self-reliance as **capital-goods capacity** — the ability to make the machines that make machines | **IPR 1948/1956**; **Mahalanobis Second Plan (1956)**; public-sector commanding heights | The mid-1960s crisis: war, drought, food aid and the **1966 devaluation** (`basic/34`) |
| ✅ **Deepened autarky, 1966–80** | Self-reliance as **insulation** — controls, licensing, restriction of foreign capital | The "licence-permit raj"; bank nationalisation (1969) | Growing inefficiency; the accumulating cost of the control regime |
| ✅ **Food self-reliance, mid-1960s onward** | Self-reliance re-defined as **food security** rather than industrial autarky | **Green Revolution** HYV package | Succeeded — and thereby removed the argument that autarky was existential |
| ✅ **1991 onward** (`basic/37`) | Self-reliance re-defined as **competitiveness and external solvency** rather than self-sufficiency | Liberalisation under **Rao and Manmohan Singh** | The **balance-of-payments crisis** |

- ⚠️ **The argument that carries a 20-mark answer:** the *word* is continuous from 1905 to 1991 and its *content* is not. It moves from village production, to fiscal autonomy, to capital-goods capacity, to insulation, and finally to competitiveness — and each redefinition followed a demonstrated failure of the previous one. Treating "self-reliance" as one unchanging idea is the error the question is testing.

**Thread 2 — agrarian distress and regime change, 1857 to 1977:**

| Moment | Agrarian distress | Political consequence |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **1857** (`basic/11`) | Taluqdar dispossession in Awadh, over-assessment on owner-cultivators, land auctions in the NWP | The most sustained civil participation in the Revolt; afterwards the taluqdars were **restored** and became the Raj's rural base |
| ✅ **1859–1900** (`basic/07`) | Planter coercion, rent enhancement, moneylender debt after the cotton collapse | **Indigo (1859–60)**, **Pabna (1873 on)**, **Deccan riots (1875)** → the **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879** — distress produced remedial legislation, not regime change |
| ✅ **1917–22** (`basic/19`, `basic/20`) | *Tinkathia* obligations, revenue in a failed harvest, rent above recorded rates | **Champaran, Kheda**, the **Awadh Kisan Sabha** and **Eka** — agrarian grievance became the entry point for mass nationalism |
| ✅ **1928–34** (`basic/22`, `basic/23`) | Revenue enhancement; depression-era price collapse | **Bardoli (1928)**; **no-tax and no-rent campaigns** inside Civil Disobedience |
| ✅ **1946–47** (`basic/23`) | Sharecropping shares, forced labour, landlord power | **Tebhaga** and **Telangana** at the moment of transfer — agrarian radicalism shaping the new state's land agenda |
| ✅ **1965–67** (`basic/34`) | Two harvest failures, food-aid dependence, devaluation | The **1967 election** — the Congress lost eight states; the end of one-party dominance |
| ✅ **1972–77** (`basic/35`) | Failed monsoons, drought, inflation, the oil shock | The **JP movement**, the **Emergency**, and the **1977 defeat** of the Congress |

- ⚠️ **The pattern to state:** agrarian distress rarely produced regime change on its own; it produced regime change when it coincided with a **political crisis of legitimacy** — 1857 (annexation), 1967 (post-Nehru succession and devaluation) and 1975–77 (a discredited mandate). Where distress occurred without such a crisis, it produced remedial legislation (1879) or localised revolt instead. That distinction is the analytical payoff of the whole thread.
- ⚠️ **The mirror pattern:** the state's standard answer to agrarian distress across all three regimes — colonial, Nehruvian and post-1967 — was **relief and technology rather than redistribution** (the 1879 Act, the Green Revolution, procurement and price support), which is why §8.5's land-reform verdict and this thread reach the same conclusion.

### 8.10 Counter-evidence, balance and caution

- **Achievements:** an industrial base, food self-sufficiency, an end to famine mortality, universal franchise sustained, legal abolition of untouchability, substantial expansion of women's rights within Hindu personal law, and abolition of intermediary tenures.
- **Failures:** literacy, employment, tenancy and ceiling reform, regional and class inequality, ecological cost, and endemic communalism after the late 1970s.
- **Variation:** Punjab/Haryana against eastern India in agriculture; the south against the Hindi belt in caste mobilisation; Kerala against the national average in social indicators.
- ⚠️ Every quantitative claim in this owner is source-attributed; carry the attribution into the answer and do not round or update figures.

### 8.11 Verdict scaffolds

- **Model verdict:** "Planning built the economy India needed to have and could not build the economy India needed to become; 1991 was the correction of a success, not the repudiation of a failure."
- **Land verdict:** "Land reform worked exactly as far as the state's political base allowed and stopped precisely where it began — which is why intermediaries disappeared and landlords did not."
- **Synthesis verdict:** "Independent India transformed its economy substantially, its politics profoundly and its social hierarchies least of all; the third failure is why the first two produced movements rather than contentment."
