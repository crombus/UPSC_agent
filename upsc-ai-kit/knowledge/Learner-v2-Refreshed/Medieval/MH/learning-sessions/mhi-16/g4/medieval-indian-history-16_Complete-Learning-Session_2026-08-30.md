### SESSION 1 — PART I — ORIENTATION: A STATE MADE THROUGH STAGES, NOT FROM ZERO

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Akbar's government evolved in stages from Sultanate and Sur precedents, connecting ruler-centred authority, differentiated offices, ranked service, verification and revenue records without becoming a modern bureaucracy.

**Technical definition:** The institutional sequence moved from the regency lesson and stronger fiscal specialisation to numerical mansabs, dagh, twelve subas, dahsala and the late zat-sawar distinction, with each device serving a distinct administrative problem.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Akbar did not create a finished administration at accession; he progressively coordinated inherited offices, rank, records and revenue practices into a larger but still personal and uneven imperial system.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **institutional stages**
- **ruler-centred authority**
- **administrative precedents**
- **numerical mansab**
- **dahsala**
- **zat-sawar**

**How to use them:** Define the institutional stages through administrative precedents; then show how ruler-centred authority coordinated numerical mansab, dahsala and zat-sawar before qualifying hierarchy, mediation and uneven reach.

**Learning objective.** Explain why Akbar's government is best read as a ruler-centred,
evolving system of rank, record and revenue rather than as an invention-from-zero or a
modern bureaucracy. The package follows the repository's basic and advanced Topic 16
files, Satish Chandra Part II OCR evidence, and the cross-links to Topics 07, 14, 15,
17, 21 and 25. It deliberately does not import the later jagirdari crisis as if it were
already complete in Akbar's lifetime.

**Core thesis.** Akbar inherited administrative languages and techniques from the Delhi
Sultanate and the Sur interregnum, then reorganised, specialised, replicated and
systematised them for an expanding empire. A mansab linked status, remuneration and
service. A revenue system converted knowledge of fields, yields and prices into a fiscal
claim. Provincial duplication and centrally appointed officers made the court more
informed. None of those arrangements dissolved hierarchy, local negotiation, coercion
or dependence on the emperor's supervision.

The chronology matters. In 1556 Bairam Khan's powerful *wakil* showed the political
danger of a regent who could overshadow a young sovereign. The diwan's fiscal importance
strengthened from the 1560s; a numerical mansab hierarchy is generally associated with
1567; Akbar's Mughal-frame *dagh* measure dates to his eighteenth regnal year, 1573-74;
revenue experiments culminated in the 1580 adoption of dahsala and division into twelve
subas; and the explicit *zat-sawar* distinction arrived only in 1595-96. This sequence
forbids the tempting but wrong answer that every component appeared in a finished form at
accession.

> **FACT:** A chronological explanation earns more than a static list: it can show
> response to regency, expansion, fiscal experimentation and the need to verify noble
> service.

| Claim | Evidence anchor | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Akbar built on precedents | Sultanate iqta practice; Sur measurement and controls | Precedent is not proof of institutional sameness |
| He systematised offices | Separate diwan, mir bakhshi and provincial counterparts | Separation remained accountable to the ruler |
| He extended information | records, musters, revenue returns and news | Records register norms as well as practice |

**Bounded modern analogy.** A cadastral survey, payroll audit or personnel grade can
clarify one function—measurement, verification or ranking. It must stop there. Mughal
revenue assessment did not create modern registered property title; mansabs were not a
meritocratic civil service; subas were not federal provinces; and the court lacked
democratic accountability.

### SESSION 2 — PART II — KINGSHIP AND CENTRAL GOVERNMENT

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** PART II — Kingship and central government comprises PART II, Kingship and central government as its core connected dimensions.

**Technical definition:** Technically, PART II — Kingship and central government is analysed by relating PART II to Kingship, then testing the relationship through central government and Wakil.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> PART II — Kingship and central government comprises PART II, Kingship and central government as its core connected dimensions.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **PART II**
- **Kingship**
- **central government**
- **Wakil**
- **prestige, residual coordinating role**
- **reduced after the regency lesson**

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through PART II; define Kingship, connect central government with Wakil to explain the mechanism, and use prestige, residual coordinating role for the decisive comparison or qualification.

Akbar was the sovereign source of appointment, justice, revenue, military command and
patronage. Courtly routines—darbar, access, petitions and, where securely grounded,
jharoka darshan—could make authority visible. Their political value was personal
communication and display, not a written constitution. The fuller discussion of
religious ideology, *sulh-i-kul* and the Ibadat Khana belongs to Topic 17; here the
relevant point is that imperial authority authorised offices and resolved conflicts among
them.

The *wakil* illustrates this ruler-centred correction. Bairam Khan acted as a powerful
regent in Akbar's early reign. Later, the office kept ceremonial prestige but was
prevented from becoming a permanent all-powerful wazir. This was not a separation-of-
powers doctrine. It was a monarch's lesson that one officer should not monopolise access,
decision and patronage.

The diwan or diwan-i-ala headed financial administration: income and expenditure,
accounts, scrutiny of khalisa, jagir and inam flows, and supervision of revenue
paperwork. It is misleading to write “Todar Mal invented revenue administration.”
Muzaffar Khan Turbati, Todar Mal and Shah Mansur represent a team of fiscal expertise;
their changing roles also reveal that policy was tested and revised. The diwan was not a
finance minister in a cabinet answerable to a legislature. It was a specialist office
whose competence increased the emperor's capacity to audit.

The mir bakhshi connected the emperor to the service elite. He handled presentation and
recommendations of mansabdars, muster-related records and military payment machinery; in
the source tradition he is also associated with intelligence/news and high appointments.
The safe formulation is functional: he was a nodal officer for rank, military personnel
and records. Calling him a modern defence minister falsely implies a separate department
with autonomous command.

The mir saman or khan-i-saman directed the imperial household and karkhanas. Stores,
workshops and supply were not decorative palace details: they supported the movable
material world of court, campaign and gift. The sadr-us-sudur dealt with grants and
connected religious/charitable functions; qazi-ul-quzat represented the highest judicial
office. Their functions could overlap with political authority and grants, and the
concentration of sadr power was curbed. Avoid presenting either as an independent modern
welfare ministry or judiciary.

| Office | Primary working field | Check or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Wakil | prestige, residual coordinating role | reduced after the regency lesson |
| Diwan | accounts, revenue, fiscal scrutiny | ruler and parallel officers remained decisive |
| Mir bakhshi | mansabs, presentation, muster records | actual contingents still needed inspection |
| Mir saman | household, karkhanas, stores | served imperial rather than public-private separation |
| Sadr/qazi | grants and judicial-religious functions | authority was not constitutionally autonomous |

> **TRAP:** “Checks and balances” is usable only with the qualifier *ruler-centred*.
> It meant office differentiation, central appointment and cross-reporting—not modern
> separation of powers.

### SESSION 3 — PART III — PROVINCE, DISTRICT, PARGANA AND VILLAGE

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** The territorial ladder is pargana, then sarkar, then suba.

**Technical definition:** The hierarchy was suba - sarkar - pargana - village.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The hierarchy was suba - sarkar - pargana - village.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **PART III**
- **Province**
- **district**
- **pargana**
- **village**
- **suba - sarkar - pargana - village**

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through PART III; define Province, connect district with pargana to explain the mechanism, and use village for the decisive comparison or qualification.

In 1580 the empire was divided into twelve subas. At the provincial level a subadar
(also described in relevant contexts as sipahsalar) was joined by a provincial diwan,
bakhshi, sadr-qazi and reporting personnel. The design mattered: a governor did not
independently own the fiscal apparatus, while the fiscal officer was not a military
sovereign. Centrally appointed offices and separate reports were meant to restrain
provincial consolidation. The practical strength of a subadar could nevertheless exceed
a formal diagram; distance, local alliances and campaign conditions qualified the check.

The hierarchy was **suba -> sarkar -> pargana -> village**. It is a territorial scale,
not a single chain of command in which every office did the same work. At a sarkar,
faujdar and amil-guzar/amalguzar conventions need careful use: the former relates to
coercive/military order, the latter to revenue work. A faujdar's jurisdiction was not
always coterminous with a sarkar; terminology and reach could vary. At pargana level,
shiqdar, amil, qanungo, potdar/treasurer and karkun/clerk appear in the record cluster.
Their work made local assessment, memory, accounts and custody administratively visible.

Village government retained intermediaries. Muqaddams or patels, patwaris, chaudhuris,
zamindars and customary bodies could mediate between cultivators and the fiscal machine.
Panchayat and customary autonomy should therefore be stated as context-specific and
source-grounded, not as a claim that the Mughal state was absent. Conversely, the claim
that the state directly reached every cultivator in identical fashion is equally false.
State demand travelled through records, local knowledge and unequal bargaining power.

**Assignments are not ownership.** Khalisa referred to revenues reserved for the crown;
jagir to a state revenue assignment often used to meet a mansabdar's remuneration; and
madad-i-maash/inam to grant arrangements. A jagir did not normally confer ownership of
land, a hereditary feudal estate, or a sovereign province. A jagirdar and a zamindar
occupied different positions: the former was commonly a transferable imperial assignee;
the latter had local landed claims, social standing, often armed following, and a
mediating role in collection.

### SESSION 4 — PART IV — MANSABDARI: ONE HIERARCHY, SEVERAL STATE PROBLEMS

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** PART IV — Mansabdari: one hierarchy, several state problems comprises PART IV, Mansabdari and one hierarchy as its core connected dimensions.

**Technical definition:** The system created a single civil-military service world in which an officer's place in the courtly hierarchy, entitlement and expected usefulness were linked.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> This does not erase social hierarchy, family advantage or faction.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **PART IV**
- **Mansabdari**
- **one hierarchy**
- **several state problems**
- **Why one institution solved multiple problems**
- **1595-96**

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through PART IV; define Mansabdari, connect one hierarchy with several state problems to explain the mechanism, and use Why one institution solved multiple problems for the decisive comparison or qualification.

A *mansab* was a graded imperial rank. It conveyed status, salary/remuneration and
service obligation; it did not describe merely a cavalry quota. The system created a
single civil-military service world in which an officer's place in the courtly hierarchy,
entitlement and expected usefulness were linked. Ranks began at 10. High ranks of 5000
and, exceptionally under Akbar, 7000 must be handled as markers of elevation rather than
evidence of one fixed table operating unchanged across the reign.

Mansabdari also helped incorporate a composite ruling class: Turani, Irani, Indian
Muslim, Rajput, Afghan and other elements could be placed in a common ranked framework.
It supplied a language of imperial honour and a channel for promotion, demotion,
patronage and surveillance. This does not erase social hierarchy, family advantage or
faction. It is therefore wrong both to call every mansab a hereditary feudal title and to
call the system a modern, impersonal meritocracy.

The explicit two-part rank came late, in Akbar's fortieth regnal year (1595-96). *Zat*
indicated personal rank, status and related pay; *sawar* indicated the cavalry obligation
associated with the rank. This distinction was introduced to clarify dimensions of
service and should not be projected unchanged to the early 1560s. Classifications of
first, second and third class mansabdars are technical and have source-specific
interpretations; an answer should explain the basic distinction before using such labels.

Payment could be cash or, often, a jagir assignment. The assessment figure (*jama*) and,
where source-supported, dami valuation helped calculate an assignment; actual receipt
(*hasil*) could fall below the paper figure. The jagir did not turn the assignee into the
owner of every field or into a provincial sovereign. Transfers, review, promotion and
dependency on imperial appointment discouraged a durable territorial base, but short
tenure could encourage quick extraction. That tension is a seed of later problems, not a
licence to narrate the full seventeenth-century jagirdari crisis here.

**Why one institution solved multiple problems:** a rank made diverse nobles comparable;
a salary/service link attached privilege to obligation; jagir or cash payment enabled
remuneration; and musters offered a way to test a claimed military resource. This is the
integrative insight a Mains answer should foreground.

### SESSION 5 — PART V — DAGH, CHEHRA, ARMY AND INFORMATION

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** Dagh was horse branding and chehra a descriptive roll of soldiers.

**Technical definition:** The imperial military was not a modern uniform national standing army.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The imperial military was not a modern uniform national standing army.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **PART V**
- **Dagh**
- **chehra**
- **army**
- **information**
- **1573-74**

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through PART V; define Dagh, connect chehra with army to explain the mechanism, and use information for the decisive comparison or qualification.

*Dagh* was horse branding and *chehra* a descriptive roll of soldiers. Their provenance
is a high-value factual trap. Alauddin Khalji introduced/used these forms of control;
Sher Shah revived or adapted them; Akbar systematised or reintroduced them within the
Mughal framework. Akbar did not invent them from nothing. The owner source places the
Akbar-era introduction of dagh in the eighteenth regnal year, 1573-74. Its purpose was
to narrow the gap between paper claims and actual horses/men, deter substitution and give
the court a record for inspection.

Inspection had costs and effects. Nobles could resist a device that made their claims more
visible; officers could harass those being verified; evasion and imperfect compliance
persisted. Thus dagh and chehra demonstrate both administrative intent and the limits of
enforcing paperwork. Writing that they “completely eliminated corruption” is as wrong as
calling them irrelevant.

The imperial military was not a modern uniform national standing army. It combined
mansabdar contingents with ahadis and, where source-grounded, artillery, infantry,
elephants and centrally maintained elements. Cavalry remained central. The mir bakhshi's
records linked rank and military personnel, but direct imperial control was mediated by
officers, supply, review and local conditions.

News writers such as waqia-navis, and relevant sawanih/secret reporting terminology when
context permits, indicate that information was an administrative resource. The safe
connection is that the court sought reports and the mir bakhshi's field included
intelligence/news in the source account. Avoid inventing a single modern intelligence
agency with uniform reach. Justice and policing likewise mixed emperor, qazi, faujdar,
kotwal and local institutions; they did not deliver uniform rule of law in the modern
sense. Coercion, hierarchy, discretion and local mediation qualify any simple
centralisation narrative.

bargaining power could alter the experienced burden. A prudent answer uses the term
“predictability” rather than “welfare” as the primary policy objective.

Local intermediaries make this ethical and political complexity visible. A patwari or
qanungo could retain local knowledge essential to a record; a muqaddam, chaudhuri or
zamindar could mediate demand, mobilise people or resist it. Their agency means the
imperial state did not simply stand outside village society, but it also means a
cultivator did not meet an abstract rational state alone. Social power and local armed
capacity shaped how a formal demand was realised.

The same caution disciplines modern analogies. Today’s land-record systems may be used to
clarify measurement, map-making or audit as a problem of state capacity. They cannot make
Mughal assessment equivalent to a legally conclusive property title, an elected
government's welfare delivery, or an accountable tax administration. The relevant
comparison is narrow and functional: both need information; their legal worlds and
accountability structures are radically different.

**Balanced Mains line:** “Dahsala increased the legibility and predictability of the
imperial claim, but did not dissolve the coercion, market risk and layered mediation
through which that claim reached cultivators.” This answers fiscal capacity and peasant
experience without inventing a universal welfare outcome.

A complete account of Akbar's government must distinguish an institution's intended
design from its operating conditions. Design begins at the court: the emperor appoints
and ranks officials; offices receive differentiated fields; manuals and record systems
make resources visible; provincial counterparts reproduce a pattern. Operation begins
where a decision meets distance, climate, crop, local knowledge, military pressure and
the interests of men who collect, record or command. This distinction is central to every
limitation in the topic.

Take provincial administration. A centrally appointed subadar, diwan and bakhshi embody
a deliberate refusal to put all civil, fiscal and military leverage in one pair of hands.
But appointments travel through patronage, a provincial governor might have campaign
experience and local connections, and reporting depended on the willingness and capacity
of subordinates. Formal parallelism did not guarantee equal leverage. The right claim is
that the arrangement created channels for supervision and rivalry, not that it produced
automatic compliance.

Take fiscal assessment. The jarib, crop-rate practice and ten-year averaging represent a
serious attempt to make fields and claims legible. Yet land could be difficult to measure,
productivity could vary, prices could move, and cultivation could be interrupted. The
persistence of batai, kankut and nasaq gives concrete form to that problem. Alternatives
were not necessarily evidence that officials ignored policy; they could be rational
responses to information and terrain. Nevertheless, alternative methods could also leave
more scope for negotiation, discretionary estimation and unequal pressure.

Take military service. Dagh and chehra show that the court knew a number in a mansab
record was not a sufficient indicator of fighting capacity. Branding and descriptive rolls
sought a test. The very need for a test tells the historian that nominal and actual
strength could diverge. Inspection cannot be assessed simply as “success” or “failure”:
it raises the cost of deception, produces records for punishment or correction, but also
creates compliance costs, official opportunities for harassment and incentives to evade.

Take village society. Pargana staff, patwaris, chaudhuris, muqaddams and zamindars
provided the state with information and routes of collection. Their inclusion made
imperial reach possible; their position also made direct uniform state contact unlikely.
A cultivator's encounter with revenue may have been shaped by the local intermediary,
harvest and market as much as by a norm in the Ain. One must not write either that Mughal
administration bypassed villages or that villages sat outside the imperial system.

“could check,” “is associated with,”** and **“was limited by”** where sources describe a
rule or institutional intention. Reserve **“universally ensured,” “completely eliminated,”
“everywhere applied,”** and **“automatically produced”** for claims supported by evidence

letting one exceptional year dominate the calculation. This is a genuine gain in fiscal
intelligibility.

At the provincial level, that claim has to become a monitored demand. A provincial diwan
and subordinate record staff make the assessment part of a chain of accounts. The
subadar's wider political-military authority gives the state coercive presence, but the
separation of offices means that fiscal language does not originate only in the governor's
command. If a jagir supports a mansabdar, the chain also connects the apparent value of an
assignment to the wider salary and service hierarchy. A high figure on paper may support
a high claim; it remains necessary to ask what can actually be received.

At pargana and village levels, the requirement meets the archive of local knowledge:
field histories, crop conditions, people who know boundaries, prior payments and social
obligations. Qanungos, patwaris, amils, chaudhuris, muqaddams and zamindars do not all
perform identical tasks, but together they make the fiscal process socially embedded.
Their involvement can prevent a distant office from working blindly; it can also make
collection dependent on unequal local power. A state whose record needs intermediaries
cannot be reduced to direct bureaucracy.

At realisation, harvest, price, transport and bargaining determine whether the scheduled
**jama** becomes **hasil**. Crop sharing through batai may be more workable in one setting;
kankut or nasaq may lower the cost of a full fresh measurement elsewhere. A remission in
crop failure can be fiscally rational because abandoned cultivation harms future returns.
But fiscal rationality does not settle the distributive question for cultivators. The
burden can still be sharpened by cesses, monetisation pressure, local exactions or
coercion. This is why a well-written conclusion says that predictability may coexist with
extraction.

At the point of feedback, reports and accounts return upward. They can reveal a shortfall,
justify adjustment, expose an official or leave a problem concealed. The same feedback
logic applies to mansab: a rank, a listed salary and a supposed contingent acquire value
only when record, muster and inspection relate them to actual people and resources. Thus
revenue administration, nobility management and military control are connected by a
broader state practice: the court repeatedly turns social resources into ranked,
recorded and reviewable claims.

This layered case study supplies a compact answer to the question, “How did Akbar's state
reach the countryside?” It did so neither by direct contact with every peasant nor by
surrendering authority to local society. It created procedures and offices that worked
through intermediaries, then attempted to supervise the result. The quality of that
supervision varied; its aspiration and institutional traces are nevertheless historically
significant.

#### A historian's checklist before accepting a strong claim

When a source gives a precise total, first identify the unit. Is it a mansab rank, an
assessed jama, a realised hasil, a suba count, a category of land, or a description of
horses? Numbers travel badly across categories. A high mansab is not automatically an
actual number of fighting men after zat-sawar; a jama is not a cash balance after hasil;
a territorial hierarchy does not establish a faujdar's identical jurisdiction everywhere.
Defining the unit is often the difference between using evidence and merely displaying it.

Next identify the temporal layer. The 1567 numerical hierarchy, 1573-74 dagh, 1580
dahsala/subas and 1595-96 zat-sawar do not describe one simultaneous institutional
moment. A source compiled late in the reign may give a mature pattern, while a question
may ask about an early practice. This is why an answer should use dates as arguments:
“The later distinction clarifies an earlier ambiguity” is stronger than “the system was
always dual-ranked.”

Then identify the geographic layer. A rate or method in an identified zabt zone cannot
automatically become an all-India rule. Claims about Lahore-to-Allahabad, Malwa and
Gujarat need the companion observation that methods varied elsewhere. The map of a state
is not the map of equal administrative depth. Forested, hilly, frontier and regionally
distinct areas can preserve different relations of measurement, crop sharing, estimate
and local authority.

Finally identify the voice. Abu'l Fazl's account can be especially informative about
imperial categories and justifications because it participates in the court's
administrative-literary project. It must be read against the possibility of idealisation.
Badauni's criticism can reveal resistance or disagreement, but it must be read against
his own position. A farman establishes a command, not necessarily the final local result;
a coin indicates a circulation or claim, not the entire agrarian structure. Triangulation
is not an optional “source criticism” sentence appended at the end—it is how claims about
capacity remain proportionate to evidence.

**Use this final test before writing:** Have I named the institution? Have I stated its
mechanism? Have I dated it correctly? Have I specified the scale? Have I separated norm
from practice? Have I admitted one concrete limit? If all six answers are yes, the
response is likely both fact-safe and analytical.

#### Synthesis: why the pieces belong in one topic

Mansabdari and dahsala are often revised as separate chapters because one appears
military and the other agrarian. Akbar's state made them interdependent. A graded service
elite needed dependable remuneration; remuneration required a fiscal claim; a fiscal
claim depended on records and local mediation; and the capacity to use a service elite in
war depended on knowing whether paper cavalry existed in fact. Diwan, mir bakhshi,
provincial officers, record personnel and household supply should therefore be seen as
connecting nodes rather than isolated facts.

This synthesis also explains the central position of the emperor. He was not merely the
ceremonial summit above self-running departments. Appointment, promotion, transfer,
grant, audit and dispute settlement acquired force through sovereign patronage. That
personal centre could coordinate a diverse empire, but it meant the system relied on
supervision, information and court politics rather than an impersonal constitutional
routine. “Centralisation” in this context is a process of making resources reportable to
the centre, not the disappearance of regional variation.

The final conceptual formula is therefore: **imperial authority + differentiated offices
+ ranked elite + revenue information + verification + local mediation = substantial but
uneven early modern state capacity.** Every term after the plus sign also carries a limit:
officers can compete, rank can be nominal, assessments can exceed receipts, inspections
can be evaded, and intermediaries can bargain. A conclusion that preserves this double
movement is more accurate than a celebration of administrative “perfection.”

#### Q1. Which statement best captures the relationship between zabti and dahsala?

- A. separate religion from all administration.

- D. make the diwan a hereditary office.
**Answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The answer follows the reduced effective power of the wakil after Bairam Khan. The plausible but wrong fiscal alternative mistakes a regency lesson for a rule about the diwan.

#### Q3. Which officer is most closely connected with mansab recommendations, presentation and muster records?
- A. Diwan
- B. Sadr-us-sudur
- C. Mir bakhshi
- D. Mir saman
**Answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Mir bakhshi is the rank-and-muster node. Diwan is the closest distractor because finance could overlap with payment, but he was not the primary officer for presentation and military record.

- A. the subadar owned all land in the suba.

- B. sarkar -> pargana -> suba.

- D. suba -> sarkar -> pargana.

- A. a hereditary sovereign province.

**Explanation:** A revenue assignment is the key discriminator. The ownership distractor is tempting because revenue rights are substantial, but they are not private title.

- A. religious grant from judicial office.
- B. a suba from a sarkar.

**Answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Zat relates to personal status/pay and sawar to cavalry obligation. The cash/crop option is a revenue-method distinction, not a mansab distinction.

**Explanation:** Continuity through Alauddin, Sher Shah and Akbar is the required correction. The closest distractor erases precedent by assigning invention solely to Akbar.

- A. test claimed cavalry resources against actual horses and personnel.
- B. fix village boundaries permanently.
- C. replace all cash payments with grain.
- D. convert jagirs into property titles.
**Answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Dagh and chehra target fictitious musters and substitution. The jagir distractor imports a property issue unrelated to verification.

average of produce and prices, it supported cash assessment. **Batai/ghalla-bakhshi** divided
crop in a fixed proportion, fitting circumstances where realised output mattered but requiring
supervision and division. **Kankut** combined field measurement with inspection/appraisal of
standing crop, while **nasaq** substantially relied on prior payment/assessment and estimate,
avoiding a full fresh survey. Polaj, parati/parauti, chachar and banjar helped classify
cultivation/fallow history; they should not be confused with a simple fertility scale.

**Fiscal reach.** Measurement, jarib standardisation, local dastur and provincial/central
diwan records increased the state's ability to formulate a predictable claim. Cash demand
connected assessment to coin and markets, and the system is associated with Lahore-to-
Allahabad, Malwa and Gujarat. It financed court, campaign, service and household and checked
officials through documentation.

**Limits.** A paper **jama** could differ from realised **hasil**. Zabt was not universal:
difficult tracts, regional price/productivity variation and administrative capacity kept
alternatives alive. Zamindars and village intermediaries mediated local reach; cesses, price
risks, transport and coercive collection complicate welfare claims. Remission did not erase
extraction.

**Conclusion.** The multiplicity of methods is not evidence of failure; it is evidence of
pragmatic adaptation. It also marks the boundary of a state whose record capacity remained
uneven.

#### Mains 8. [15 marks | 200 words] Assess the place of zamindars and local intermediaries in Akbar's revenue administration.
**Directive alignment:** Assess agency and constraint; avoid both disappearance and

**Introduction.** Akbar's fiscal system increased official measurement and record-making, but

**Roles.** At village and pargana levels, muqaddams/patels, patwaris, chaudhuris, qanungos,

information, mobilisation and negotiation.

#### Mains 9. [20 marks | 250 words] Trace continuity and change from Sultanate and Sur practices to Akbar's administration.
**Directive alignment:** Trace a process across periods while avoiding “first invention” and

#### Mains 10. [20 marks | 250 words] How should Ain-i-Akbari be used to evaluate Akbar's state capacity? Give a balanced verdict.
**Directive alignment:** Evaluate a source as evidence and use it to reach—not assume—a
verdict on state capacity.

**Introduction.** The *Ain-i-Akbari*, part of Abu'l Fazl's Akbarnama project, is indispensable
for administrative vocabulary, norms and compiled data, but it is an imperial compendium and
cannot be read as a modern census or a transparent record of universal practice.

**What it reveals.** Its categories illuminate the state imagined and claimed by Akbar's
court: mansab rank, office, suba organisation, revenue terminology, household resources and
fiscal order. Read alongside details such as the diwan's accounts, mir bakhshi's personnel
records, dahsala calculation and provincial differentiation, it supports an argument for
considerable early modern state capacity: rulers sought legible fields, ranked service,
inspected horses and collected reports.

**Source criticism.** Abu'l Fazl wrote within a patronage relationship and presented an ideal
imperial order; precision of figures does not by itself prove implementation in every village.
Norm must be distinguished from practice, and administrative efficiency from justice.
**Badauni** can reveal hostile/sectarian courtly reaction when relevant, but his opposition
is not a neutral social survey. Farmans, surviving revenue records, coins, inscriptions and
later historians can corroborate particular orders, transactions, circulation or local
variation, each with survival and genre limits.

**Balanced state-capacity verdict.** Akbar's system linked records, specialised offices,
mansab, inspection, provincial replication and fiscal calculation more systematically than a
loose personal retinue. Yet reach was uneven: alternatives to zabt, local zamindars,
jama-hasil gaps, inspection evasion and coercive collection demonstrate limits. Capacity was
real precisely because the court tried to manage these problems, not because its normative
text made them disappear.

**Conclusion.** Use the Ain as a rich map of imperial administrative aspiration and
repertoire, triangulated against other evidence; then a qualified, not triumphalist, account
of Mughal state capacity follows.

**Why this earns marks:** It makes source criticism operational, names complementary evidence,
and turns “capacity” into mechanisms and limitations rather than praise of figures.

### 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Deeper — Akbar's state used checks, balances and expertise.**

- ✅ Part II argues that Akbar inherited Sultanate structures but reorganised departments so their functions did not encroach on each other and yet balanced one another.
- ✅ The all-powerful *vakil* was reduced: Bairam Khan's dominance convinced Akbar not to allow a second over-mighty minister.
- ✅ The *diwan* became a specialist in income/expenditure and revenue; the *mir bakhshi* handled mansabs, military presentation, intelligence and high appointments.
- ✅ Provincial government repeated the centre's checks: subah governor, diwan, bakhshi, sadr-qazi and news-writer were appointed from the centre.

**Deeper — dahsala and mansabdari were evolving systems.**

- ✅ The dahsala was implemented by a team including Muzaffar Khan, Todar Mal and Shah Mansur; popular memory over-associates it with Todar Mal alone.
- ✅ Mansabdari was unique because it made a graded single service combining civil and military responsibilities; rank fixed status, salary and retainers.
- ✅ The dagh system was introduced to reduce the gap between paper cavalry and actual mustered troops, but it caused noble resistance and administrative harassment.
- ✅ The dual **zat-sawar** rank was introduced late to distinguish personal status/pay from actual cavalry obligation.
- ✅ Chandra, citing later research, cautions that *zabt* probably did not cover the whole land of any province; crop-sharing, appraisement and estimate remained part of the working system.

## CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### FINAL CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES — State & Government under Akbar

#### 1. Chronology that prevents static answers
- **1556:** accession; Bairam Khan's powerful wakil-regency is the warning against an over-mighty coordinator.
- **1565:** diwan strengthens; fiscal work is a team and department story, not a single-hero story.
- **c. 1567:** numerical mansab hierarchy associated with the eleventh regnal year.
- **1573-74 (18th regnal year):** Akbar's Mughal-frame dagh measure; not first invention of dagh/chehra.
- **1580:** twelve subas and dahsala implementation; do not make either a finished all-India blueprint.
- **1595-96 (40th regnal year):** explicit zat-sawar distinction; do not project it unchanged backward.

#### 2. State form: centralised, personal, differentiated
- Emperor: appointment, justice, revenue, military command and patronage remain concentrated at the centre.
- Vakil: Bairam Khan lesson -> prestige survives; effective monopoly is curbed.
- Diwan/diwan-i-ala: income-expenditure, accounts, khalisa-jagir-inam scrutiny; name **Muzaffar Khan Turbati + Todar Mal + Shah Mansur**.
- Mir bakhshi: mansab presentation/recommendation, muster/personnel record, associated news/intelligence; not a modern defence minister.
- Mir saman/khan-i-saman: household, stores and karkhanas = material state capacity.
- Sadr-us-sudur/qazi-ul-quzat: grants and judicial-religious functions; no modern autonomous welfare/judicial analogy.
- Formula: **office differentiation + central appointment + cross-reporting -> ruler-centred check**, not separation of powers.

#### 3. Administrative ladder and local mediation
- **Suba -> sarkar -> pargana -> village**; ascending territorial size = **pargana -> sarkar -> suba**.
- 1580: twelve subas; provincial subadar, diwan, bakhshi, sadr-qazi and reporting apparatus balance one another.
- Sarkar: distinguish faujdar's coercive/order field from amil-guzar/amalguzar revenue work; jurisdiction may vary.
- Pargana: shiqdar, amil, qanungo, potdar and karkun form a record/collection cluster.
- Village: muqaddam/patel, patwari, chaudhuri, zamindar and custom; neither direct-uniform state reach nor romantic autonomy.
- Khalisa = crown revenue; jagir = assigned state revenue; madad-i-maash/inam = grant. **Jagir != ownership.**
- Jagirdar = generally transferable assignee; zamindar = local claimant/intermediary with social/armed resource.

#### 4. Mansabdari: remember the triad
- **Mansab = rank + remuneration + service obligation**, a single civil-military imperial service.
- Ranks begin at 10; high 5000 and exceptional 7000 under Akbar; no single rigid all-reign table.
- Composite elite: Turani, Irani, Indian Muslim, Rajput, Afghan and others; integration does not equal equality.
- **Zat = personal rank/status/pay; sawar = cavalry obligation**, explicit in 1595-96.
- Cash and jagir payment coexist; jama/dami valuation is not equivalent to realised hasil.
- Transfer, promotion, demotion and assignment create dependence; elite family advantage and hierarchy persist.
- **Not hereditary feudal title; not modern merit civil service.**

#### 5. Dagh, chehra, army and information
- Provenance mnemonic: **Alauddin used -> Sher Shah renewed -> Akbar systematised**.
- Dagh = brand horse; chehra = describe soldier; goal = reduce fictitious muster/substitution.
- Limit: resistance, harassment, inspection cost and paper-versus-actual troop gap.
- Army: mansabdar contingents + ahadis + artillery/infantry/elephants where relevant; cavalry central; not modern standing army.
- News writers and records improve oversight, but no claim of one uniform modern intelligence agency.

#### 6. Revenue logic and terminology
- Objective: predictable fiscal claim, military/court/household finance, knowledge of cultivation, control of officials/intermediaries; **not welfare-only**.
- Sher Shah precedent -> Akbar experiments -> local circles/records -> 1580 dahsala.
- **Zabti = measurement-based assessment. Dahsala = ten-year produce-price averaging.**
- Dahsala: theoretical one-third of average produce in cash; **not a permanent ten-year settlement**.
- Jarib with bamboo/iron rings = standardising measurement evidence; do not assign universal modern bigha equivalence.
- Polaj/parati(ch)/chachar/banjar = cultivation/fallow history, not fertility-only ladder.
- Batai = crop share; kankut = measurement + standing crop estimate; nasaq = previous-demand estimate.
- Safe reach: Lahore to Allahabad, Malwa, Gujarat. Alternatives prove zabt was not universal.
- Jama = assessed demand; hasil = realised receipt. Cash nexus is uneven, not complete commercialisation.
- Remission/taccavi may support cultivation; cesses, transport, price risk and coercion qualify benevolence.

#### 7. Comparisons and limits
- Iqta vs jagir: both revenue-service assignments; compare tenure, audit, scale and rank relation—avoid identity/invention claims.
- Sher Shah vs Akbar: continuity plus refinement; not mere copying, not Akbar-first.
- Capacity = rank + record + inspection + revenue + provincial replication + household supply + news.
- Limits = local intermediaries, uneven reach, corruption, nominal contingents, cash-price risk, coercion, jama-hasil gaps, transfer incentive.
- Later jagirdari crisis belongs chiefly to Topics 21/25; here identify only structural seeds.

#### 8. Source discipline and PYQ route
- Ain-i-Akbari: administrative compendium/data/ideal order in Akbarnama project; useful for categories/norms, not census-like proof of universal practice.
- Abu'l Fazl: imperial ideology/praise; Badauni: hostile/sectarian lens; triangulate with farmans, records, coins, inscriptions and historians.
- 2021 Prelims GS-I Q45 direct/owned: exact local-paper wording and option order reproduced above; **(a) inferred, high confidence, not officially verified**.
- 2019 jagirdar-zamindar is Topic 24 adjacent; 2025 religious syncretism is Topic 17 adjacent.

#### 9. Twenty-second recall
1. 1580 = twelve subas + dahsala.
2. 1573-74 = Akbar's dagh; 1595-96 = zat-sawar.
3. Pargana -> sarkar -> suba.
4. Jagir is revenue assignment, not land title.
5. Zabt measures; dahsala averages.
6. One-third is theoretical claim; realised burden can differ.
7. Team, not Todar Mal alone.
8. Ruler-centred checks, not constitutional separation.
9. Ain gives norms/ideal order; test practice.
10. Best verdict: systematised but uneven early modern imperial capacity.

#### 10. Last-minute answer compression
- For an **administration** question, use: Bairam-wakil correction -> diwan/mir bakhshi functions -> twelve subas -> ruler-centred qualification.
- For a **mansabdari** question, use: rank-salary-service -> composite elite -> 1595-96 zat/sawar -> dagh/chehra -> jagir-transfer tension.
- For a **revenue** question, use: Sur precedent/team -> zabti versus dahsala -> jarib/rates -> alternatives -> jama-hasil/local mediation.
- For a **sources** question, use: Ain category/norm -> Abu'l Fazl patronage -> Badauni's situated dissent -> corroborating traces -> norm-practice verdict.
- Never write “Akbar invented dagh/chehra,” “dahsala was a permanent ten-year settlement,” “jagir was land ownership,” “zabt was universal,” or “subas were federal provinces.”
- Named evidence should perform a job: 1580 dates reorganisation; 1595-96 dates refinement; jarib shows measurement; jama-hasil shows fiscal gap; a zamindar shows mediated reach.

#### 11. Three one-line verdicts
- **State:** “Centralisation was a ruler-centred increase in reportability, not the creation of impersonal constitutional administration.”
- **Mansab:** “The mansab joined honour, remuneration and military obligation, making elite integration an instrument of command.”
- **Dahsala:** “The ten-year average made a fiscal claim more legible, while alternative methods and local risk marked the boundary of uniform implementation.”

#### 12. Final caution
- State capacity is not a synonym for a modern state. In this topic it means the court's enlarged
  ability to classify, rank, record, inspect, assign and collect through people whose cooperation
  and local knowledge it could never fully replace.
