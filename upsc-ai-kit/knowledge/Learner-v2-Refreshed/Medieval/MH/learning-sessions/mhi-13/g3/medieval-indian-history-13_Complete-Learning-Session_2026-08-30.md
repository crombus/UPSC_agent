### 01. Scope, source order and the central problem

[FACT] This package follows the Topic 13 basic and advanced files first; then Topic 12 on Babur, Topic 08 on Gujarat–Malwa–Mewar, Topic 14's forward boundary, the master chronology, the answer-worthiness audit and the local PYQ routing ledgers. It then uses searchable pages from Satish Chandra, *Medieval India: From Sultanat to the Mughals, Part II*, chapter on Humayun and the Afghans. The local-text page references are used as a source base, not copied as an authority beyond their reach.

[ANALYSIS] The topic asks why a dynasty that won Panipat and Khanwa could lose north India within a decade, and why it could return in 1555. The productive answer is not a biography of a supposedly feckless emperor. It is a political problem: imperial reach was asserted faster than revenue, administration, noble commitment and communications could sustain it, while Afghan and regional competitors retained real capacity.

[LIMIT] This does not make decline automatic. Humayun exercised choices; Sher Khan made choices; Gujarat, Mewar, Bengal, the brothers and the imperial centre were distinct fields. A careful answer balances structure with agency and does not turn a later monument, popular legend or a compressed chronicle into proof of every event.

[FACT] Kamran already held Kabul and Qandahar at Babur's death. He then occupied Lahore and held Punjab up to the Sutlej, seeking confirmation from Humayun. Humayun accepted his possession while Afghan activity and Bahadur Shah's ambitions demanded attention elsewhere; khutba and sikka continued in Humayun's name. Askari and Hindal held assigned territories. The source warns that the brothers were broadly loyal until the Bengal episode; Hindal's independent move at Agra and elite defections belong to the later crisis. After Chausa, Kamran's 10,000 hardened troops were not placed under Humayun's command.

[ANALYSIS] Kamran's control represented a de facto partition but not an explanation that can be retrofitted onto every event. It had a double effect. It narrowed Humayun's direct reach and later denied him a crucial reinforcement; it also spared him some immediate north-western burdens when Qandahar faced Iranian pressure. This is exactly why an answer must name its time period.

Askari's role similarly changes: he was entrusted with Gujarat, and the inability of his command to create cohesion is evidence of an elite-administration problem, not merely personal betrayal. Hindal's later action at Agra mattered because a Bengal expedition had already stretched the rear. The brothers' choices interacted with state capacity, battlefield confidence and noble calculation.

> MEMORY: **K-A-H** = **K**amran's Punjab hinge; **A**skari's Gujarat test; **H**indal's Bengal-period rear crisis. Do not turn the mnemonic into a timeless guilt list.

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through The early Afghan phase; define Dadrah/Daurah, connect Chunar with In to explain the mechanism, and use Humayun for the decisive comparison or qualification.

[FACT] In the source sequence, Humayun besieged Kalinjar, then moved against Chunar after learning that Sher Khan had acquired it. Sher Khan was absent, and a settlement followed: service under his son, a contingent and payment were offered. Humayun accepted because he did not then contemplate an attack on Bihar and did not regard Sher Khan as the central danger. At Dadrah on the Gomti, Humayun defeated Afghan forces led in the eastern field; the strategic core is consolidation against east-U.P. resistance.

[ANALYSIS] Chunar was called the gateway to Bihar because it sat in an approach system—not because one fort controlled the entire east by itself. A fort could affect land movement, river-side communications, provisioning and the security of a force moving from the Gangetic core toward Bihar and Bengal. The first settlement was therefore not absurd: Humayun had several fronts and an immediate interest in keeping Sher Khan apart from other Afghan rebels. Its later failure became visible only when Sher Khan's regional power increased.

[LIMIT] Retrospective knowledge makes every compromise look foolish. A fair answer says the calculation underestimated a future rival, but avoids claiming that the complete later Sher Shah was already obvious in 1531–32. That distinction is especially important in a source field with a disputed chronology.

### SESSION 7 — GUJARAT AND MALWA, 1535–37: VICTORY WITHOUT DURABLE SETTLEMENT

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** The Gujarat episode is a laboratory in the difference between campaign and administration.

**Technical definition:** But 'removed danger' is not identical with 'held Gujarat'.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> The Gujarat episode is a laboratory in the difference between campaign and administration.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **Gujarat**
- **Malwa**
- **victory without durable settlement**
- **Supply pressure at Mandsor avoided an artillery trap**
- **Mandu and Champaner showed operational reach**
- **Ahmedabad demonstrated a ten-month campaign's momentum**

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through Gujarat; define Malwa, connect victory without durable settlement with Supply pressure at Mandsor avoided an artillery trap to explain the mechanism, and use Mandu and Champaner showed operational reach for the decisive comparison or qualification.

[FACT] Humayun marched from Agra in early 1535, moved through eastern Malwa and held a strategically advantageous position near Ujjain/Mandsor rather than directly attacking the Chittor siege. Bahadur Shah's defended camp used carts, ditches and artillery; Humayun avoided a frontal attack and cut supplies. Bahadur Shah withdrew; Mandu fell, then Champaner and Ahmedabad. The campaign was rapid and displayed real drive. Yet after assigning Askari overall responsibility and dividing Gujarat among nobles, Mughal authority collapsed. Bahadur Shah recovered, Askari withdrew, and Malwa too was lost by February 1537.

[ANALYSIS] The Gujarat episode is a laboratory in the difference between campaign and administration. A commander can defeat an opponent, seize fortresses and distribute treasure; none of these actions creates a revenue system, local coalition or co-ordinated hierarchy. Many Mughal nobles had relocated to the Delhi–Agra area and did not wish to reside in Gujarat, which they treated as foreign. Askari's capacity, mutual suspicion, regional reassertion and Humayun's slow intervention combined. The failure is multi-causal, not a no-effort story.

| Military success | Administrative failure |
|---|---|
| Supply pressure at Mandsor avoided an artillery trap | Revenue was not gathered in time and command lacked cohesion |
| Mandu and Champaner showed operational reach | Begs did not acquire a stake in long-term settlement |

[ANALYSIS] A chronological narrative gains explanatory force only when it identifies the choices available at a particular moment. #### Mains 1. Examine the proposition that Humayun inherited an unfinished conquest.

**10 marks | 150 words | Original UPSC-style practice**

**Model solution**

**Claim.** Humayun inherited a conquest rather than a settled empire. Babur's victories had created a Delhi–Agra core, but the state that passed in December 1530 remained financially and administratively thin.

**Named evidence and analysis.** First, the local Satish Chandra account notes stretched finances after Babur had exhausted the Lodi treasure and sought a share from assigned revenues. That evidence matters because war, rewards and office-holding required a dependable fiscal base; a victory treasury was not a routine revenue system. Second, the administration relied heavily on begs holding large assignments. Such delegation could mobilise men, yet it also made local authority and collection dependent on followers whose interests were not automatically identical with the centre. Third, eastern Afghans remained politically active from east Uttar Pradesh to Bihar, while Sher Khan had access to Chunar. This shows that Panipat and Ghaghra had not dissolved Afghan political capacity.

**Qualification.** The inheritance was not empty. Humayun had Delhi–Agra prestige, military resources and scope for action, as Dadrah and Gujarat later show. Nor should Kamran's position be presented as an instant terminal fracture: it relieved Humayun of some north-western burdens before becoming acute in the later crisis.

**Conclusion.** The phrase 'unfinished conquest' therefore means a mismatch between territorial claim and institutional depth. It explains vulnerability without denying agency: Humayun still had to choose priorities, and Sher Khan still had to build an alternative coalition.

#### Mains 2. Assess structural and personal factors in Humayun's failure.

**15 marks | 200 words | Original UPSC-style practice**

**Model solution**

**Claim.** Humayun's fall cannot be explained either by a private moral defect or by structures that made choices irrelevant. Structural fragility set the field; sequencing and judgement shaped how he lost it.

**Structural field.** Babur's stretched finances, large beg assignments and shallow local roots restricted the administrative reach of the new Mughal regime. Kamran's Lahore–Punjab position represented a de facto partition inside a Timurid political culture of princely shares. In the east, Sher Khan possessed a Bihar base, Afghan networks and later Bengal resources. These conditions meant that a ruler could not safely assume that a military advance would yield settled authority.

**Personal and strategic choices.** Humayun nevertheless displayed ability: at Mandsor he avoided a frontal assault on Bahadur Shah's defended camp and used a supply squeeze; he took Mandu, Champaner and Ahmedabad. The failure followed when victory was treated as equivalent to administration. Askari's Gujarat charge lacked co-ordination and noble commitment. In Bengal, the six-month Chunar siege and long stay at Gaur gave Sher Khan time to threaten communications. At Chausa, placing the Karmnasa behind his position and permitting surprise were concrete operational errors.

**Qualification.** The old 'opium' caricature is inadequate. Mirza Haider's observation is a source, not a total explanation; the record also shows sustained campaigning. Equally, rehabilitation cannot make Chausa or the Bengal sequence disappear.

**Conclusion.** Humayun's ambition outran the fiscal, logistical and administrative capacity available to enforce it. Personal error made an inherited vulnerability exploitable by a capable opponent.

#### Mains 5. Analyse the Bengal campaign as a test of logistics and imperial ambition.

#### Mains 10. Explain the significance and limits of Humayun's restoration in 1555.

**20 marks | 250 words | Original UPSC-style practice**

**Model solution**

**Claim.** Humayun's recovery of Delhi in 1555 was historically significant because it restored the Mughal dynastic foothold, but it was enabled by Sur fragmentation and did not cancel the earlier evidence of institutional fragility.

**Why restoration was possible.** The Sur order that had displaced Humayun after Kannauj did not remain politically unified. This fragmentation opened a field that Humayun, now backed by the Kabul–Qandahar base recovered after Safavid assistance, could exploit. Delhi recovery therefore depended on a convergence of external recovery, opponent fragmentation and renewed Mughal agency. It was not a simple replay of the 1530 inheritance.

**Why it mattered.** Delhi carried accumulated imperial prestige; its recovery restored a political centre and made the Mughal line viable at the moment before Akbar's accession. The later architectural memory of Humayun's Tomb—UNESCO describes a grand 1560s garden-tomb patronised by Akbar—can be used only to illustrate dynastic memorialisation and a restored imperial language. **Limits and continuity.** The period 1530–40 had revealed weak finances, unrooted nobles, insecure communications, Timurid fraternal competition and the force of an Afghan alternative under Sher Khan. A Delhi recovery did not retrospectively solve those problems. Humayun died in 1556, leaving Akbar an opening rather than a completed institutional achievement. Akbar consolidated a restored foundation; later Mughal capacities did not already exist in 1555.

**Qualification.** Restoration nevertheless refutes a terminal-failure thesis. Humayun showed resilience, diplomatic adaptability and an ability to regain a centre after a profound defeat. The historical verdict must therefore hold collapse and recovery together.

**Conclusion.** 1555 marks dynastic rehabilitation, not the erasure of 1540. Its significance lies precisely in preserving a fragile opening through which a later, more durable Mughal consolidation became possible.

- **Achievement:** avoids blanket incompetence; real operational ability and courage.
- **Failure chain:** incomplete revenue settlement → reluctant nobles → Askari/co-ordination problem → regional recovery → Mughal withdrawal.
- **Verdict:** military success, temporary political gain, failed direct administration.

#### F. Sher Khan and the Bengal turn

- **Sher Khan:** Bihar base; Chunar; **Surajgarh 1534**; **Gaur 1538**; Afghan coalition and state-building capacity.
- **No stereotype:** leadership + networks + regional roots + terrain + resources, not an essential 'tribal' quality.
- **Bengal motive:** imperial ambition as well as the Mahmud Shah appeal; Bengal is the prize.
- **Six-month Chunar siege:** communications risk makes it rational; time cost lets Sher Khan take Gaur.
- **At Gaur:** distant government, horses, reluctant officers, westward Afghan pressure and Hindal's rear crisis.

#### H. Exile, recovery and significance

- **Sindh/Marwar:** regional rulers did not automatically rescue a dispossessed claimant.
- **Shah Tahmasp:** assistance supports recovery of **Qandahar and Kabul, 1545**; religious/political conditions need precise sourcing.
- **1555:** Sur fragmentation + recovered north-west base + Mughal agency → Delhi recovery.
- **1556:** Humayun dies; restoration is a platform for Akbar, not proof that previous fragility vanished.
- **Heritage anchor:** UNESCO's Humayun's Tomb account supports dynastic memory/conservation and garden-tomb discussion only.
