#### 1. Visual foundation
```text
THE SEVEN NOLAN PRINCIPLES OF PUBLIC LIFE (UK Committee on Standards in Public Life, 1995)
cited verbatim by the 2nd ARC (para 2.2.5)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Selflessness   - decide solely in terms of public interest
2. Integrity      - no financial/other obligation that could compromise duty
3. Objectivity    - appointments/contracts/benefits on merit alone
4. Accountability - answerable to the public; submit to due scrutiny
5. Openness       - be as open as possible; restrict info only when public interest demands
6. Honesty        - declare private interests; resolve conflicts to protect public interest
7. Leadership     - promote and support these principles by personal example
```

**Core proposition:** ✅ ARC 2.2.6 — these principles are "of general applicability in every
democracy," and from them flows the need for both a **Code of Ethics** (broad guiding principles)
and a **Code of Conduct** (precise standards of acceptable and unacceptable behaviour). Conduct
standards become sanctionable where they are incorporated into the applicable legal or service-rule
framework; the ARC definition alone does not create a penalty (fully treated in `16`).

#### 2. Essential definitions
| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Ethical dilemma** | A situation in which two or more legitimate duties, values or loyalties genuinely conflict, such that honouring one requires compromising another — distinct from a simple temptation to break a known rule. |
| ✅ **Legal violation** | A breach of a specific, codified rule/law, independent of whether the underlying situation involved any genuine value conflict. |
| ⚠️ **Ethical dilemma vs legal violation** | A dilemma may exist even where *no law is broken* (e.g., choosing between two lawful options with different ethical costs); a legal violation may occur without any real dilemma (e.g., simple theft). GS-IV case studies often contain genuine value conflicts, but some principally test malpractice, corruption or failure to follow a clear duty. |
| ⚠️ **Conflict of interest vs corruption** | A conflict of interest is a *situation*, not an act of wrongdoing: a private interest (family, financial, past employment) that could reasonably be thought to compromise official judgment. The **ethical failure is the non-disclosure or non-recusal**, not the existence of the interest. Corruption requires an actual abuse of office — under the PC Act, an "undue advantage" (see `19`). Every conflict of interest left unmanaged is a corruption risk; not every conflict of interest is corruption. Nolan's "honesty" principle is specifically about declaring such interests. |
| ✅ **Code of Ethics** (ARC 2.2.6) | Broad guiding principles of good behaviour and governance. |
| ✅ **Code of Conduct** (ARC 2.2.6) | A precise, unambiguous list of acceptable and unacceptable behaviour and action. Sanctions depend on the applicable law, service rule or other enforceable framework. |
| ⚠️ **Status and role morality** | Public status brings asymmetric authority, privileged information and control over public resources; role morality therefore imposes stricter restraint, reason-giving and public-purpose duties than private preference alone. |
| ⚠️ **Political neutrality** | A permanent civil servant gives frank, evidence-based advice and then implements lawful elected policy without partisan favour; neutrality does not mean ignorance of policy values or indifference to constitutional duties. |
| ⚠️ **Responsibility and accountability** | Responsibility is the duty to perform and own a decision; accountability adds answerability to appropriate external scrutiny, reasons, remedy and institutional learning. |
| ⚠️ **Bounded discretion** | Discretion is structured judgment within legal purpose, relevant facts, equal treatment, proportionality, recorded reasons and review — not personal freedom to substitute private morality for public authority. |

### SESSION 2 — Recognising and diagnosing ethical dilemmas

#### 3. Dilemma-diagnosis anatomy — a pre-answer diagnostic nested inside Basic 22's eight elements
⚠️ This five-step sequence **diagnoses and names a dilemma before writing a full case-study
answer** — it is a preparatory diagnostic tool, not itself a 20-mark answer format. In a full
Section-B answer it maps onto, and feeds into, Element 1 (facts/naming the dilemma) and Element 5
(ethical evaluation) of the controlling eight-element architecture set out in
`22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md` Sec. 1 (facts, stakeholders, constraints, at
least three realistic options, ethical evaluation, decision, implementation/communication,
residual-risk mitigation) — this file supplies the Nolan-grounded diagnostic step; `22` remains the
controlling answer architecture.

1. **Identify the competing values/duties** — e.g., loyalty to a superior vs duty to the public;
   compassion for an individual vs impartial rule application; confidentiality vs transparency.
2. **Check for a legal floor** — is one of the options actually illegal, removing it from
   consideration regardless of its apparent ethical appeal?
3. **Apply a moral-theory lens** (see `08`) — duty, consequence, virtue — to each remaining option.
4. **Weigh institutional role and long-term systemic effect**, including the higher restraint
   created by public status, the elected/permanent-executive boundary, public/private relationship
   risks and the effect of precedent on similarly placed citizens.
5. **Decide, document and be prepared to be accountable** — Nolan's "accountability" principle
   requires that the resolution be defensible to public scrutiny, not merely privately justified.

### SESSION 4 — Legal floors, public propriety and UPSC close-option traps

#### 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims
- ✅ The Nolan Committee (UK, formally the Committee on Standards in Public Life, set up in October
  1994) issued the seven principles of public life in its First Report of May 1995; the 2nd ARC's
  4th Report (2007) reproduces that 1995 wording verbatim at para 2.2.5 as a model for Indian
  public life. ⚠️ The UK's own official wording has since been revised (notably after the 2013
  *Standards Matter* review) — quote the ARC's 1995 text when citing the ARC, and check gov.uk if
  you need the current UK formulation.
- ✅ ARC 2.2.6: a Code of Ethics ≠ a Code of Conduct — the former states broad guiding principles,
  while the latter states precise acceptable and unacceptable behaviour. Enforcement depends on
  the legal or service framework adopting the conduct standard.
- ⚠️ An ethical dilemma requires a genuine conflict between legitimate values; a mere temptation to
  break a known rule for personal gain is not, by itself, a dilemma.

#### 6. UPSC traps
- ❌ Every ethical problem in a case study is really about whether a law was broken. -> Many GS-IV
  cases contain genuine value conflicts where no law is necessarily broken by every option, while
  others test a clear violation or abuse that should not be disguised as a dilemma.
- ❌ The Nolan principles are India-specific. -> They originate from the UK's 1995 Committee on
  Standards in Public Life and are cited by the ARC as being "of general applicability in every
  democracy," not as an Indian invention.
- ❌ "Accountability" and "responsibility" are simply synonyms in the Nolan framework. -> Nolan's
  "accountability" specifically means submitting to external scrutiny for decisions taken — see `11`
  for the fuller accountability-vs-responsibility distinction.

### SESSION 6 — Conflict of interest: types, disclosure and proportionate response

#### 9. Probable questions

- ⚠️ **Mains (10 marks):** Distinguish an ethical dilemma from a legal violation, with an example

- ⚠️ **Mains (15 marks):** Using the Nolan principles as a checklist, evaluate how a civil servant

#### 10. Conflict of interest — full actual/potential/apparent taxonomy (historical demand)
> ⚠️ 2018 GS-IV Q3(a) (historical demand, routed here): illustrate conflict of interest as
> actual vs potential, with examples. Section 2's table already distinguishes conflict of interest
> from corruption; this section supplies the full, operationally usable three-way taxonomy.

| Type | Precise meaning | Worked Indian/administrative example | Operational response |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Actual conflict of interest** | A private interest and an official duty are *presently* in direct opposition, and a decision is about to be, or has been, taken. | A procurement officer evaluating a tender in which their sibling's firm is a bidder. | **Mandatory recusal** from the specific decision; reassignment of the file to another officer; the interest must be formally declared on record. |
| ⚠️ **Potential conflict of interest** | A private interest *could* come into opposition with official duty in the future, though no specific decision currently requires action. | An officer holds shares in a company operating in a sector they may later be transferred to regulate. | **Proactive disclosure** at the point of posting/appointment; a standing declaration reviewed periodically; no recusal needed yet, but the interest is logged for future screening. |
| ⚠️ **Apparent (perceived) conflict of interest** | No actual private interest exists, but a *reasonable observer*, aware of the relevant facts, could suspect that official judgment may be compromised. | An officer processing a routine, rule-bound approval for a childhood friend's application, where the outcome would have been identical for any applicant. | **Transparency, not automatic recusal** — document that the decision followed identical, published criteria; consider voluntary recusal only if public confidence would otherwise be seriously undermined. |

- ⚠️ **Why the three-way split matters:** treating every *apparent* conflict as if it were *actual*
  produces excessive, paralysing recusal that itself damages service delivery; treating every
  *potential* conflict as irrelevant until it becomes actual removes the chance to manage it
  proactively — GS-IV rewards naming which of the three types is in play before prescribing a
  response.
- ⚠️ **The Nolan link:** all three types trace back to Nolan's "honesty" principle (declare private
  interests; resolve conflicts to protect public interest) — see Section 1 — but the *degree* of
  institutional response (recusal vs disclosure vs transparent documentation) should scale with the
  type, not apply uniformly.
- ⚠️ Cross-link: `04_Aptitude-and-Foundational-Values-for-Civil-Service.md`, Section 9, treats the
  paired historical quotation (Warren Buffett on integrity in hiring) for this same 2018 question.

### SESSION 7 — Coercion, undue influence and unequal workplace power

#### 11. Coercion vs undue influence (historical demand)
> ⚠️ 2023 GS-IV Q2(b) (historical demand, routed here): "differentiate" coercion from undue
> influence in a work environment.

| Aspect | Coercion | Undue influence |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Mechanism** | Compelling action through an explicit or implicit **threat of harm** (job loss, punitive transfer, physical/reputational harm) — the target's will is overborne by fear. | Exploiting a **position of trust, authority or dependency** to persuade someone to act against their genuine interest or judgment — the target's will is bent, not broken, often without their full awareness. |
| ⚠️ **Consent quality** | Consent, if given, is not free — it is extracted under duress. | Apparent consent is given, but it is not fully autonomous — it is shaped by an asymmetric relationship (superior-subordinate, mentor-mentee, examiner-candidate). |
| ⚠️ **Workplace example** | A supervisor threatening an adverse confidential report unless a subordinate signs off on a flawed inspection certificate. | A senior officer subtly pressuring a junior colleague to "recommend" the senior's preferred vendor by repeatedly emphasising the junior's career depends on the senior's goodwill, without ever issuing a direct threat. |
| ⚠️ **Public-office example** | A minister's aide threatening to block a routine transfer/posting unless a file is expedited outside due process. | A retiring official steering a post-retirement job offer from a firm they recently regulated, through informal goodwill built over years — no explicit quid pro quo, but the influence is real. |
| ⚠️ **Detection difficulty** | Easier to detect once reported (a specific threat can usually be identified/evidenced). | Harder to detect — no explicit threat exists; it surfaces only through pattern-of-conduct review (repeated favour-seeking, relationship-based decision skew) or a "reasonable observer" test similar to the apparent-conflict-of-interest test in Section 10. |
| ⚠️ **Institutional response** | Protection/redress channels (internal complaints committee, vigilance escalation), and treating extracted "consent" as void for the underlying decision. | Cooling-off periods (e.g., post-retirement employment restrictions), rotation of postings to break entrenched dependency relationships, and mandatory recording of reasons for discretionary recommendations. |

- ⚠️ **Why the distinction is exam-critical:** case studies routinely present *undue influence* (a
  respected senior "advising" a certain outcome) disguised as ordinary professional courtesy —
  misreading it as legitimate guidance, rather than naming it as undue influence, is the commonest
  scoring error; conversely, over-labelling ordinary firm instruction as "coercion" dilutes the
  precision GS-IV rewards.

### ETHICS DEEP-REVIEW CORE CONTROL

- **Must remember:** Public service is a fiduciary role grounded in constitutional authority and public trust; status, power and discretion create role duties, while actual, potential and apparent conflicts of interest require different preventive responses.
- **Close distinction:** Integrity, impartiality, objectivity, empathy, compassion, tolerance, accountability and political neutrality are distinct and may conflict; bounded discretion requires relevant reasons, consistency, proportionality, documentation and review.
- **Evidence / authority / application limit:** Dilemmas must name stakeholders, facts, assumptions, legal constraints, value conflicts and institutional channels; loyalty to a superior never defeats legality, citizen rights or reasoned dissent, while resignation remains a last rather than reflexive step.

### 1. Architecture

**Analytical claim:** The Nolan principles function as an aspirational *ceiling* while the Code of
Conduct functions as an enforceable *floor* — genuine ethical dilemmas live in the gap between
them, where conduct is technically lawful but the Nolan-standard resolution is contested.

### 2. Concepts and distinctions
| Concept | Precise meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Selflessness vs self-interest bias** (Nolan Principle 1) | Requires decisions "solely in terms of public interest" — the most demanding principle because self-interest can be subtle (reputational, career, ideological) not just financial. |
| ✅ **Objectivity in Nolan's specific sense** | Confined to public appointments, contracts, and awarding of benefits — narrower than a general "be unbiased" injunction; it is procedural (merit-based selection), not a claim about value-neutrality in policy itself. |
| ⚠️ **Nested dilemmas** | Complex administrative situations often contain more than one dilemma simultaneously (e.g., a disaster-relief case combining resource-allocation fairness, personal-versus-duty conflict, and transparency-versus-order-maintenance) — advanced answers should decompose and address each nested dilemma distinctly. |
| ⚠️ **Genuine vs manufactured dilemma** | A "manufactured" dilemma disguises a simple rule violation as a values conflict (e.g., presenting bribery as "compassionate flexibility") — an advanced skill is distinguishing real value conflicts from rule violations dressed up as dilemmas. |
| ⚠️ **Status and role morality** | Public office confers authority, informational advantage and control of common resources; the role therefore justifies heightened restraint, impartiality, reasons and scrutiny rather than special personal privilege. |
| ⚠️ **Political neutrality** | The permanent executive owes lawful implementation to the elected government while retaining duties of frank advice, constitutional fidelity, non-partisanship and recorded professional dissent. |
| ⚠️ **Public/private boundary** | Family, financial, associational and post-employment relationships become ethically relevant when they intersect with official power; proportionate disclosure, screening, recusal or documentation protects public confidence. |
| ⚠️ **Bounded discretion** | Administrative judgment is legitimate only within authority, relevant facts, equal criteria, proportionality, written reasons and review; discretion without those safeguards becomes arbitrariness. |

### 3. Detailed treatment
#### The seven Nolan principles — precise, non-overlapping readings
- ✅ **Selflessness:** decisions "solely" in public interest, not "primarily" — the ARC's citation
  (2.2.5) uses the strong term, useful for identifying even partial self-interest as a violation.
- ✅ **Integrity:** freedom from financial/other obligation that "might influence" official duty —
  note this is a *risk-based* standard (potential influence), not requiring proof of actual bias,
  which is why disclosure/recusal regimes exist even absent demonstrated wrongdoing.
- ✅ **Accountability:** "must submit... to whatever scrutiny is appropriate to their office" — an
  explicitly submission-based, externally-facing standard, distinguishing it from mere internal
  self-assessment (see `11` for accountability vs responsibility).
- ✅ **Openness:** "as open as possible," restricting information "only when the wider public
  interest clearly demands" — this reverses the default; secrecy requires justification, not
  disclosure (directly underpinning RTI's presumption of disclosure, see `15`).
- ✅ **Honesty:** specifically about *declaring private interests* and resolving resulting conflicts
  to protect public interest — narrower than a general truthfulness norm; it targets conflict-of-
  interest management specifically.
- ✅ **Leadership:** promoting the principles "by leadership and example" — makes senior officials
  responsible not just for their own conduct but for modelling standards that shape subordinates'
  and institutional culture.

#### Distinguishing genuine dilemmas from manufactured ones
- ⚠️ A genuine dilemma survives even after removing all self-interest and rule-violation from the
  scenario — e.g., choosing between two lawful, legitimate but competing policy priorities
  (economic development vs environmental protection in a specific project) is a genuine dilemma.
- ⚠️ A manufactured dilemma dissolves once self-interest or rule-violation is named plainly — e.g.,
  "should I accept this gift from a contractor to maintain a good working relationship" is not a
  genuine dilemma; it is a conflict-of-interest violation framed euphemistically as one.
- ⚠️ GS-IV case studies (see `22`) deliberately blend both types; a strong answer explicitly tests
  and states which type it is facing before proceeding to resolution.

#### Nested dilemmas in complex cases
- ⚠️ The 2025 GS-IV DC-Vijay case study nests at least three distinct dilemmas: (1) personal grief
  vs public duty (an emotional-intelligence dilemma, see `05`), (2) resource-allocation fairness in
  disaster relief (a distributive-justice dilemma, see `08`'s Rawls/utilitarian tension), and
  (3) transparency vs maintaining public calm during the crisis (an openness-Nolan-principle
  dilemma) — advanced answers decompose and resolve each nested layer rather than treating the case
  as a single monolithic problem.

### 4. Institutional and reform architecture
- ✅ ARC 2.2.6's Code of Ethics/Code of Conduct distinction operationalises the Nolan principles:
  the Code of Ethics states the aspirational Nolan-type standard; the Code of Conduct translates
  narrower, objectively verifiable elements into precise standards. Those standards are
  sanctionable only where the applicable law or service framework supplies enforcement (see `16`).
- ⚠️ 2025 GS-IV Q5(a) itself presents a premise contrasting an operating Code of Conduct with a
  Code of Ethics "not yet put in place" and asks for a suitable model. Use that as the question's
  framing; do not generalise it into a live status claim for every category of Indian public office
  without separate official verification.

### 5. Indian applications and boundary cases
- ⚠️ A civil servant recusing themselves from a tender evaluation involving a relative's firm
  operationalises Nolan's integrity principle even absent any proven wrongdoing — the risk-based
  standard justifies precautionary recusal.
- ⚠️ Boundary case: an official disclosing sensitive but non-security-critical information under RTI
  despite departmental reluctance operationalises Nolan's openness principle (secrecy requires
  justification) — but must still respect legitimate exemptions (security, ongoing investigation)
  under law (see `15`).

### 6. Limitations and trade-offs
- ⚠️ The Nolan principles, being aspirational, carry no direct sanction; without a linked Code of
  Conduct they risk becoming what the ARC (2.4.3) itself calls "not comprehensive... a list of
  prohibitions" if left unaccompanied by ethics guidance, or purely rhetorical if left unaccompanied
  by enforceable rules.
- ⚠️ Distinguishing genuine from manufactured dilemmas requires judgment that can itself be biased —
  officials under real pressure may sincerely (but wrongly) perceive a rule violation as a
  legitimate values conflict, a risk that institutional checks (peer review, vigilance oversight)
  must guard against.

### 7. Must-Know Facts for Advanced Prelims
- ✅ The Nolan Committee's formal name is the UK Committee on Standards in Public Life (established
  1994, seven principles issued 1995).
- ✅ ARC's own recommendation (2.4.5) was that a Code of Ethics be created *in addition to* the
  existing Code of Conduct for Ministers, monitored by dedicated units in the PM's/CM's offices with
  an annual report to the legislature.
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q5(a) explicitly frames its own problem as a Code of Conduct "already in operation"
  and a Code of Ethics "not yet put in place." Attribute this to the paper's premise rather than
  treating it as proof of status across all public-office categories.
- ✅ ARC 2.4.5(b)-(d) also specify *how* such a Code should be monitored: dedicated units in the
  offices of the Prime Minister and Chief Ministers, empowered to receive public complaints, with
  an annual report on observance — including specific violations and action taken — submitted to
  the appropriate legislature, and all three documents (Code of Ethics, Code of Conduct, annual
  report) placed in the public domain.

### 8. Advanced Prelims traps
- ❌ Nolan's "objectivity" principle requires value-neutral policymaking generally. -> It is
  specifically about merit-based decisions in appointments, contracts and benefits — a narrower,
  procedural standard.
- ❌ A genuine ethical dilemma always involves two "equally good" options. -> Options can be
  legitimately unequal in ethical weight; the dilemma is that both options honour some legitimate,
  competing duty, not that they are equivalent in merit.
- ❌ The 2025 paper proves that no Indian public body has any Code of Ethics. -> The paper supplies
  a problem premise for the answer; implementation status varies by institution and must be
  independently verified before making a general claim.
- ❌ The Nolan wording the ARC reproduces is the current UK text. -> The ARC quotes the Committee on
  Standards in Public Life's **1995** formulation; the UK's official wording has since been
  revised. Cite the ARC's version as the ARC's, not as today's UK standard.

### 11. Mains-ready framework
**Central thesis:** Ethical dilemmas in Indian public service occupy the gap between an aspirational
Nolan-type Code of Ethics and an enforceable Code of Conduct; resolving them requires first testing
whether the dilemma is genuine or manufactured, then decomposing any nested sub-dilemmas, and
finally checking the proposed resolution against all seven Nolan principles for public
defensibility.

1. State the dilemma(s) precisely; test genuine vs manufactured.
2. Identify how public status, role morality, political neutrality or a private relationship shapes
   the duty.
3. Decompose nested dilemmas if more than one competing-duty pair exists.
4. Apply the relevant Nolan principle(s), constitutional boundary and bounded-discretion tests.
5. Apply a moral-theory triangulation (`08`) to each sub-dilemma.
6. Give a recorded, reviewable resolution defensible under external scrutiny.

### ONE-PAGE CONCEPT GRID

| Concept / thinker | Exam-ready formulation |
|---|---|
| Public office is a public trust | Public office confers entrusted authority, information and discretion for public purpose; status therefore increases rather than relaxes the duty of restraint, reason-giving and conduct that can retain citizens' confidence. |
| Role morality is bounded by constitutional public purpose | Role morality gives an official special duties of fidelity, competence and impartial implementation, but it cannot licence discrimination, concealment or personal loyalty where these defeat constitutional values, lawful authority or public interest. |
| Public interest is not majoritarian preference | Public interest concerns constitutionally permissible, evidence-based welfare of the community, including affected minorities and future citizens; it is not identical to electoral popularity, a minister's preference or the loudest group's immediate demand. |
| Status creates an appearance obligation | Because public authority depends on confidence, officials should avoid impropriety and its reasonable appearance; this is not a demand for private perfection but a duty to manage relationships and conduct that could credibly compromise official judgment. |
| Integrity excludes compromising obligations | Nolan integrity requires public office holders not to place themselves under financial or other obligations that might influence official duty; risk of compromised judgment matters before proof of actual bias or corrupt payment. |
| Objectivity is merit-based official procedure | Nolan objectivity requires appointments, contracts and benefits to be decided on merit and stated criteria; it is a procedural guard against favouritism, not a claim that public policy can be wholly value-neutral. |
| Impartiality and political neutrality differ | Impartiality applies relevant criteria equally among persons; political neutrality requires non-partisan advice and implementation under the elected government. Neither requires indifference to constitutional rights, evidence or unlawful directions. |
| Accountability is answerability to scrutiny | Accountability requires an office holder to explain and justify decisions to appropriate external scrutiny and accept consequences or correction; it exceeds private sincerity and differs from merely having responsibility to perform an assigned task. |
| A genuine dilemma conflicts legitimate duties | An ethical dilemma arises when two legitimate duties, values or loyalties cannot both be fully honoured; it differs from a simple temptation to obtain gain by breaking a known rule or disguising favouritism as compassion. |
| Legality is a floor, not the whole ethical inquiry | A legal prohibition removes an option from ordinary ethical choice, but technical legality does not automatically establish morality or propriety; officials must also test public purpose, equality, constitutional values, reasons and foreseeable institutional effects. |
| Stakeholder mapping prevents tunnel vision | Dilemma resolution begins by identifying affected citizens, weaker groups, colleagues, institutions and future interests; mapping them exposes hidden burdens before an official chooses among lawful alternatives or invokes a public-good slogan. |
| A defensible resolution is reasoned and reviewable | After excluding unlawful options, an official should compare realistic alternatives through duties, consequences and virtues, select a proportionate option, record reasons, communicate it fairly and mitigate residual harm under independent or administrative review. |

### CORE REVISION SPINE

- **Actual conflict calls for recusal:** An actual conflict exists when a private interest and an immediate official decision directly collide; the normal safeguard is prompt declaration, removal from the particular decision and reassignment, rather than waiting to prove corrupt motive or outcome.
- **Potential conflict is managed prospectively:** A potential conflict is a private interest that could later intersect with official duty; proactive disclosure and a register enable later screening, while automatic present recusal is unnecessary where no relevant decision is before the officer.
- **Apparent conflict requires confidence protection:** An apparent conflict exists where a reasonable informed observer could suspect compromised judgment despite no proved private benefit; transparency, reasons and sometimes voluntary recusal preserve confidence without mechanically treating appearance as established corruption.
- **Disclosure does not itself resolve conflict:** Declaration is a necessary first safeguard but does not cleanse every conflict; the institution must assess materiality, decide recusal, reassignment or conditions, record the response and ensure the private interest cannot distort public decision-making.
- **Coercion overbears choice through threat:** Coercion compels conduct through an explicit or implicit threat of harm, such as punitive transfer, dismissal or retaliation; apparent consent under such duress is not a reliable ethical defence for the pressured official or decision.
- **Undue influence exploits dependency without threat:** Undue influence exploits trust, authority or dependency to bend another's autonomous judgment without an overt threat; it requires attention to relationship patterns, career vulnerability and informal pressure rather than only searching for a provable quid pro quo.
- **Discretion needs legal purpose and recorded reasons:** Discretion is not personal freedom: it must stay within authority, pursue statutory and constitutional purpose, use relevant facts and equal criteria, remain proportionate and be documented so that citizens, supervisors and courts can review it.
- **Automation can reduce but not erase accountability:** Automating rule-bound services can reduce arbitrary discretion and improve traceability, but accountable administration still requires accessible alternatives, human review of exclusion, reliable data and responsibility for biased design, implementation and redress.
- **Openness has a justified public-interest limit:** Nolan openness favours maximum disclosure, but information may be restricted when a clear wider public-interest and lawful ground supports it; secrecy must be justified, narrowly tailored and not used to conceal embarrassment, favouritism or maladministration.
- **Honesty includes private-interest declaration:** In the Nolan formulation, honesty specifically requires declaration of private interests relating to public duties and resolution of resulting conflicts; ordinary truthfulness remains important, but this principle directly targets transparent conflict management.
- **Leadership shapes institutional ethical climate:** Nolan leadership requires senior office holders to promote public-life values through personal example, fair systems and response to misconduct; ethical culture cannot be created by slogans while leaders reward target achievement obtained through improper means.
- **Metrics illustrate monitoring, not complete ethics:** Disposal, pendency and response-time metrics can make administrative monitoring visible, but they do not by themselves prove correctness, fairness, satisfaction, impartiality or absence of misconduct; qualitative review and remedy remain necessary.
