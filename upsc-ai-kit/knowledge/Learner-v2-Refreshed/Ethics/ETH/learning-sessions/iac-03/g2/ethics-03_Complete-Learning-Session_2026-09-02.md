**Core proposition:** ✅ Attitude has three components — cognitive, affective, behavioural (the ABC
model) — and is distinct from **value** (broad, enduring, cross-situational) and from **belief**
(a single cognitive claim); attitude is the evaluative stance toward a *specific* object.

#### 2. Essential definitions
| Concept | Exam-ready meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Attitude** | A relatively stable evaluative disposition (positive/negative) toward a specific object, person, event or idea, comprising cognitive, affective and behavioural components. |
| ✅ **Cognitive component** | Beliefs/knowledge/information the person holds about the attitude object. |
| ✅ **Affective component** | The emotional charge (like/dislike, comfort/discomfort) attached to the object. |
| ✅ **Behavioural (conative) component** | The predisposition/intention to act in a particular way toward the object. |
| ✅ **Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger, 1957) | Psychological discomfort from holding two inconsistent cognitions/attitudes, or from acting against one's own attitude — resolved by changing the attitude, the behaviour, or rationalising the gap. |
| ⚠️ **Attitude vs value vs belief vs aptitude** | Value = general, enduring, cross-situational standard (e.g., honesty); attitude = evaluative stance toward one specific object (e.g., "I dislike this particular policy"); belief = a single factual/causal claim a person holds true; aptitude = the trainable capacity to perform a role or task well (see `04`). |

### SESSION 2 — How attitudes form and change through experience and persuasion

#### 3. How attitudes form and change (mechanism)
1. Attitudes form through **direct experience, social learning (family/peers/media), and
   reinforcement** — repeated positive/negative outcomes attach evaluative charge to an object.
2. **Persuasion** changes attitude via the classical Hovland-Yale ("Yale attitude change") levers:
   the *communicator's* credibility and expertise, the *message's* content/framing, and the
   *audience's* predispositions — with the *channel/medium* added by later communication research.
3. **Cognitive dissonance** is a powerful internal driver of attitude change: when behaviour and
   attitude conflict (e.g., a civil servant who values transparency is asked to withhold routine
   information), the discomfort pushes either attitude change, behaviour change, or self-justifying
   rationalisation — the last being an ethical risk in administration.
4. Attitudes influence but do **not mechanically determine** behaviour — situational constraints,
   social pressure and institutional incentives can produce behaviour inconsistent with a held
   attitude (the attitude-behaviour gap).
5. ✅ William James's teaching (2025 GS-IV Q3(b), verbatim: "a human being can alter his life by
   altering his attitudes") captures the *malleability* of attitude — it is not fixed temperament
   but a chosen, revisable stance.

#### 5. Must-Know Facts for Prelims

- ✅ Cognitive dissonance theory was proposed by Leon Festinger (1957).

  apart is a frequently tested distinction.

### SESSION 5 — Traps that cost marks in attitude questions

#### 6. UPSC traps
- ❌ Attitude and value mean the same thing. -> Value is a broad, enduring standard; attitude is a
  specific evaluative stance toward a particular object — they are related but distinct levels.
- ❌ A positive attitude guarantees consistent ethical behaviour. -> Situational pressures and
  institutional incentives can produce an attitude-behaviour gap; attitude is necessary but not
  sufficient for ethical conduct.
- ❌ Persuasion is inherently unethical manipulation. -> Persuasion is a neutral influence process;
  its ethical status depends on the truthfulness of the message and the respect for audience
  autonomy, not on the act of persuading itself.

### SESSION 9 — Moral attitude versus political attitude in civil service

#### 11. Moral attitude vs political attitude (official syllabus clause)
> ✅ The official GS-IV syllabus clause reads: "Attitude: content, structure, function; its
> influence and relation with thought and behaviour; **moral and political attitudes**; social
> influence and persuasion." Sections 1-10 above cover the general ABC structure; this section
> closes the moral-vs-political distinction the clause names explicitly.

| Dimension | ⚠️ Moral attitude | ⚠️ Political attitude |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | An evaluative stance toward what is right/wrong, just/unjust, honest/dishonest — grounded in ethical reasoning (duty, consequence, virtue — see `08`) and applied to conduct generally. | An evaluative stance toward a specific party, ideology, policy programme or contest for power — grounded in political preference/affiliation. |
| **Object** | Conduct, character, and the rightness of an act or rule (e.g., "bribery is wrong regardless of who benefits"). | A particular political actor, platform or outcome (e.g., preferring one party's manifesto or leader over another's). |
| **Scope** | General and portable across contexts — a moral judgment about honesty applies whether the actor is from one's own party or the opposition. | Context-bound to the contest itself — a political attitude is, by design, partisan and does not claim universal applicability. |
| **Source** | Ethical reasoning, socialisation into values (family, education, religion/philosophy — see `02`), and professional codes (see `09`, `16`). | Political socialisation, ideological identification, group/party loyalty, and electoral competition. |
| **Civil-servant obligation** | Must be actively retained and exercised — a civil servant is expected to judge right from wrong (corruption, discrimination, procedural unfairness) and act on that judgment regardless of who is affected. | Must be **bracketed/suspended in official conduct** — Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, Rule 5 restricts a government servant's participation in politics and demonstrations, and Rule 7 restricts canvassing in elections, operationalising the constitutional expectation (Article 309-linked service rules) of political neutrality/non-partisanship. |

**Core proposition:** ⚠️ A civil servant is required to *retain* a moral attitude (an active,
reasoned sense of right and wrong that informs every decision) while *bracketing* a personal
political attitude in official conduct — because moral judgment is what impartial, lawful
administration requires, whereas an official who lets *political* preference (for a party, leader
or programme) visibly steer a decision compromises the non-partisanship that lets the same officer
serve any elected government of the day (see `04`'s "non-partisanship" as a foundational value, and
`09`'s Nolan "objectivity"/"selflessness" principles).

##### Why the two cannot be treated alike
1. **Function:** a moral attitude is the very instrument of ethical decision-making in office (e.g.,
   refusing an improper instruction because it is wrong); a political attitude, if allowed to
   operate inside official decisions, substitutes partisan preference for the public interest the
   office exists to serve.
2. **Institutional design:** the political-neutrality/non-partisanship obligation (Conduct Rules;
   the "permanent executive" convention under a rotating political executive) exists precisely so
   that a civil servant's *political* attitude never determines who receives a benefit, a licence or
   a posting — while the *moral* attitude (impartiality, honesty, rule-of-law fidelity) is exactly
   what makes that neutrality trustworthy rather than merely mechanical compliance.
3. **Behavioural translation:** a moral attitude should visibly translate into behaviour (reporting
   corruption, applying rules impartially); a political attitude translating into official
   behaviour (favouring a party worker's file, canvassing on duty) is itself the misconduct the
   Conduct Rules exist to prevent.

##### Limits — what non-partisanship does not require
- ⚠️ **Policy awareness is not partisanship.** A civil servant is expected to understand the
  political executive's policy priorities, brief ministers candidly (including with unwelcome
  advice — the "constructive dissent" duty, see `09`), and implement lawfully enacted policy
  faithfully — none of this is "political attitude" in the sense the neutrality obligation
  restricts; it becomes partisan only when the officer's *personal* preference for a party/leader,
  rather than the law and the record, visibly drives a discretionary decision.
- ⚠️ **Private political belief is not itself misconduct.** The Conduct Rules restrict *public*
  political activity, canvassing and organised political association by a serving official, not
  the mere private holding of a political opinion or the exercise of one's vote — the obligation is
  behavioural non-partisanship in office, not enforced political blankness of mind.
- ⚠️ **Moral objection to a lawful policy is not "political attitude" either.** An officer who
  raises a reasoned ethical/legal objection to an instruction on file (Sec. 8-level file-noting
  practice, see `22`) is exercising moral judgment about propriety/legality, not political
  preference — provided the objection is framed in terms of law, rules and public interest rather
  than partisan alignment.

##### Evidence units
1. *Claim:* Non-partisanship is a codified conduct obligation, not an informal norm. *Evidence:*
   Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, Rule 5 (participation in politics/demonstrations)
   and Rule 7 (election canvassing restrictions). *Significance:* gives a named, citable legal
   anchor for "bracketing political attitude" answers. *Limitation:* the Rules restrict *conduct*,
   not private belief — over-claiming a duty of political "blankness" of mind is itself a trap.
2. *Claim:* Moral judgment must survive political change even as political attitude is bracketed.
   *Evidence:* the "permanent executive" convention — the same civil servant serves successive,
   differently aligned elected governments. *Significance:* shows why moral attitude (impartial,
   rule-bound judgment) is the constant, while political attitude is deliberately made irrelevant to
   official duty. *Limitation:* the line blurs in practice when policy implementation itself is
   contested as partisan — the test remains whether the officer's *personal* party preference, not
   the lawfully enacted policy, is driving the decision.

### ETHICS DEEP-REVIEW CORE CONTROL

- **Must remember:** Attitude has cognitive, affective and behavioural components, while aptitude is capacity, value is an evaluative commitment, opinion is a stated judgment and conduct is observable action; attitude-behaviour gaps arise through incentives, norms and constraints.
- **Close distinction:** Persuasion is reason-giving or influence that preserves agency; coercion removes meaningful choice, manipulation hides or distorts reasons, nudging changes choice architecture, and propaganda systematically biases information.
- **Evidence / authority / application limit:** Administrative persuasion must disclose purpose, use credible evidence, protect vulnerable groups and preserve review or exit; named campaigns require source and outcome discipline rather than assuming that communication caused behaviour change.

**Answer:** A

#### Original Mains Practice 4 — 15 marks

**Question:** Analyse how cognitive dissonance operates as an ethical risk multiplier in public administration. Suggest institutional mechanisms to interrupt the rationalisation pathway. (Answer in 250 words) Answer in about 200 words.

**Model solution**

Cognitive dissonance arises when an official's behaviour contradicts a held attitude. Festinger identifies three resolution paths: change the attitude, change the behaviour, or add a self-justifying cognition. The third path, rationalisation, is the ethical risk multiplier because it lets officials with sound values gradually normalise small compromises.

Consider an officer who values transparency but is directed to withhold a routine file under a stretched RTI exemption. She convinces herself the exemption is broader than it is. The discomfort resolves without changing the unethical behaviour. Over time, 'everyone does it' becomes 'it is harmless' becomes settled practice. Small facilitation payments rationalised once as unavoidable become customary, then deserved. This is how dissonance multiplies ethical risk.

Institutional mechanisms interrupt this pathway. Regular ethics review sessions let officers examine their own justifications under guided reflection. Transparent decision records make it harder to sustain private rationalisation when colleagues see the reasoning. Audit trails document actual exemption scope, preventing stretched interpretations from going unexamined. Whistleblower protection ensures that an officer who chooses the second path is not punished for principled objection. Rotation breaks the settled relationships that sustain rationalisation.

The verdict is that cognitive dissonance is not itself unethical but its rationalisation pathway, unchecked by institutional design, converts individual psychological resolution into systemic ethical erosion.

### 2. Concepts and distinctions
| Concept | Precise meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Attitude-behaviour gap** (LaPiere, 1934) | Stated attitudes and actual behaviour can diverge sharply under real situational pressure — a caution against assuming declared values predict conduct, and against assuming a general attitude predicts a specific act. |
| ✅ **Cognitive dissonance resolution paths** (Festinger) | (a) change attitude to match behaviour, (b) change future behaviour to match attitude, (c) add a justifying cognition (rationalisation) — the third path is the ethically risky one in administration. |
| ⚠️ **Persuasion vs manipulation vs propaganda** | Persuasion respects audience autonomy and uses truthful argument; manipulation exploits cognitive biases/asymmetric information; propaganda uses repetition/emotional framing at scale, often state-sponsored, to bypass critical evaluation. |
| ⚠️ **Attitude strength** | Attitudes held with high certainty, personal relevance and frequent rehearsal resist persuasion and predict behaviour better than weakly held attitudes — relevant to why some officials resist and others succumb to corrupt pressure. |

### 3. Detailed treatment
#### The attitude-behaviour gap in administration
- ⚠️ An official may sincerely hold a pro-transparency attitude (cognitive + affective components
  intact) yet withhold information under superior pressure (behavioural component overridden) —
  this is a textbook attitude-behaviour gap, not hypocrisy per se.
- ⚠️ Institutional design (RTI's legal mandate, whistleblower protection — see `15`, `19`) exists
  precisely because persuasion/attitude alone cannot guarantee behaviour; a **binding external
  constraint** closes the gap that attitude alone leaves open.

#### Cognitive dissonance as an ethical risk multiplier
- ✅ Festinger's third resolution path — adding a self-justifying cognition — explains why officials
  who begin with sound values can gradually rationalise small compromises ("everyone does it," "it's
  a victimless adjustment") into settled corrupt practice.
- ⚠️ Administrative counter-measure: regular ethics review, peer accountability and transparent
  audit trails interrupt the rationalisation pathway before it consolidates into habit.

#### Persuasion at algorithmic scale — the 2025 social-media question
- ✅ 2025 GS-IV Q1(a) tested "key ethical dilemmas" of social media in "the present digital age."
- ⚠️ Advanced reading: social-media platforms operationalise the Hovland-Yale variables at
  unprecedented scale and precision — the *audience* variable is now algorithmically segmented
  (micro-targeting), which can shade legitimate persuasion into manipulation by exploiting
  individual cognitive biases invisible to the audience itself.
- ⚠️ Name the *specific* dilemmas the 2025 question invites: privacy and data harvesting;

  autonomy**, not the technology itself.

### 4. Institutional and policy application
- ⚠️ Public information campaigns (financial literacy, sanitation behaviour change under Swachh
  Bharat, digital-payment adoption) are legitimate state-run persuasion exercises; their ethical
  legitimacy rests on truthful content and freedom from coercion, distinguishing them from
  propaganda.
- ⚠️ Civil-service training (Mission Karmayogi's behavioural-competency modules) targets attitude's
  cognitive and affective components through simulation/case-based learning, while institutional
  rules (codes of conduct, audit) close the residual attitude-behaviour gap.

### 5. Indian applications and boundary cases
- ⚠️ A district administration running a targeted, community-leader-led persuasion campaign to
  change attitudes toward girl-child education operates ethically because it is transparent, fact-
  based and non-coercive.
- ⚠️ Boundary case: a government social-media campaign that uses emotionally manipulative,
  fear-based messaging without disclosing sponsorship shifts from persuasion toward propaganda —
  an ethically contestable practice even if the underlying policy goal is legitimate.

### 6. Limitations and trade-offs
- ⚠️ Persuasion-based reform (attitude change) is slower and less certain than rule-based reform
  (binding institutional constraint), but more durable once internalised — a trade-off administrators
  must weigh when choosing between an awareness campaign and a mandatory regulation.
- ⚠️ Over-reliance on institutional constraint without attitude change risks purely compliance-based
  ("box-ticking") conduct that reverts once monitoring relaxes — echoing the code-of-ethics vs
  code-of-conduct distinction in `16`.

### 7. Must-Know Facts for Advanced Prelims
- ✅ LaPiere's 1934 study ("Attitudes vs. Actions", *Social Forces*) toured the United States with a
  young Chinese couple and was refused service only once in some 250 establishments, yet a large
  majority of the same establishments later said in writing that they would not serve Chinese
  guests — founding the "attitude-behaviour gap" literature. ⚠️ It shows that a *general* stated
  attitude predicts a *specific* act poorly; it does not show that attitudes never shape behaviour.
- ✅ Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957) identifies three resolution paths: change
  attitude, change behaviour, or add a justifying cognition.
- ✅ The Hovland-Yale approach (Hovland, Janis & Kelley, *Communication and Persuasion*, 1953)
  centres on the communicator/source, the message and the audience; the *channel* is a later
  addition from wider communication research, not part of the original triad.

### 8. Advanced Prelims traps
- ❌ A strongly held attitude always predicts behaviour reliably. -> Attitude strength improves but
  does not guarantee prediction; situational and institutional pressures can still override it.
- ❌ Cognitive dissonance is always resolved by changing behaviour to match the ethical attitude. ->
  It is equally, and often more easily, resolved by rationalising the unethical behaviour instead —
  the ethically risky path administrators must guard against.
- ❌ Persuasion and manipulation are the same ethically. -> Persuasion preserves audience autonomy
  and relies on truthful argument; manipulation exploits bias or conceals intent.

### ONE-PAGE CONCEPT GRID

| Concept / thinker | Exam-ready formulation |
|---|---|
| Attitude is object-specific, not a general life principle | An attitude is a relatively stable evaluative disposition directed toward a specific object, person or idea, comprising cognitive, affective and behavioural components; it is narrower than a value, which operates across situations and is not tied to any single object. |
| The cognitive component carries beliefs, not feelings | The cognitive component of attitude refers to the beliefs, knowledge and informational claims a person holds about the attitude object; it supplies the factual basis on which evaluative judgement rests and is distinct from the emotional charge that constitutes the affective component. |
| The affective component carries emotional charge | The affective component of attitude is the emotional response, whether positive or negative, attached to the attitude object; it can persist even when new factual information contradicts the cognitive component, making training that targets only beliefs insufficient for durable attitude change. |
| The behavioural component is a tendency, not a guarantee | The behavioural or conative component of attitude is the predisposition to act in a certain way toward the attitude object; it does not guarantee actual behaviour because situational pressures, institutional incentives and social norms can override the predisposition and produce an attitude-behaviour gap. |
| Attitudes form through experience, learning and reinforcement | Attitudes form through direct experience with an object, social learning from family, peers and media, and reinforcement through repeated positive or negative outcomes; their origin explains why two officials in the same office can hold opposite evaluations of the same beneficiary community. |
| Hovland-Yale persuasion rests on source, message and audience | The Hovland-Yale attitude-change approach identifies the communicator's credibility, the message's content and framing, and the audience's predispositions as the three original persuasion variables; the channel or medium is a later addition from wider communication research and was not part of the original 1953 Yale triad. |
| Cognitive dissonance creates three resolution paths | When behaviour contradicts a held attitude, cognitive dissonance produces psychological discomfort that can be resolved by changing the attitude to match behaviour, changing future behaviour to match the attitude, or adding a self-justifying cognition; the third path is the ethically dangerous one because it enables rationalisation of misconduct. |
| The attitude-behaviour gap shows that good attitudes are necessary but insufficient | LaPiere's 1934 study demonstrated that a general stated attitude predicts a specific act poorly; institutional design through binding external constraints such as RTI mandates, whistleblower protection and audit trails exists precisely because attitude alone cannot guarantee consistent ethical behaviour. |
| Persuasion is ethically distinct from manipulation and propaganda | Persuasion respects audience autonomy and relies on truthful argument; manipulation exploits cognitive biases or asymmetric information while concealing intent; propaganda uses repetition and emotional framing at scale, often state-sponsored, to bypass critical evaluation rather than engage it. |
| Algorithmic targeting changes the audience variable at unprecedented scale | Social-media platforms operationalise persuasion variables at a scale the Hovland-Yale model did not anticipate: the audience variable is now algorithmically segmented through micro-targeting, which can shade legitimate persuasion into manipulation by exploiting individual cognitive biases invisible to the audience itself. |
| Legitimate government campaigns rest on truthfulness and non-coercion | Public information campaigns such as financial literacy drives, sanitation behaviour change under Swachh Bharat, and digital-payment adoption are legitimate state persuasion exercises whose ethical standing depends on truthful content, transparent sponsorship and freedom from coercion rather than on the mere fact that a government is communicating. |
| The same digital channel enables both legitimate persuasion and disinformation | Social media raises a distinctive GS-IV dilemma because the same channel used for legitimate public information campaigns can be used for disinformation and political manipulation; the ethical test is transparency of intent and respect for audience autonomy rather than the technology itself. |

### CORE REVISION SPINE

- **Moral attitude judges right and wrong; political attitude evaluates parties and power:** A moral attitude is an evaluative stance toward what is right or wrong, grounded in ethical reasoning and applicable across contexts; a political attitude is a stance toward a specific party, ideology or contest for power, which is by design partisan and does not claim universal applicability.
- **CCS Conduct Rules require behavioural non-partisanship, not mental blankness:** Central Civil Services Conduct Rules 1964 restrict a government servant's participation in politics and demonstrations under Rule 5 and election canvassing under Rule 7, operationalising political neutrality; the obligation covers official conduct and public activity, not the private holding of a political opinion or the exercise of one's vote.
- **Policy awareness is duty, not partisanship:** A civil servant is expected to understand the political executive's policy priorities, brief ministers candidly including with unwelcome advice, and implement lawfully enacted policy faithfully; this becomes partisan only when the officer's personal preference for a party rather than law and record drives a discretionary decision.
- **Moral objection to a lawful policy is not political attitude:** An officer who raises a reasoned ethical or legal objection to an instruction in a reasoned written note is exercising moral judgement about propriety and legality, not political preference, provided the objection is framed in terms of law, rules and public interest rather than partisan alignment.
- **Attitude strength improves but does not guarantee behavioural prediction:** Attitudes held with high certainty, personal relevance and frequent rehearsal resist persuasion and predict behaviour better than weakly held attitudes; however, even strong attitudes can be overridden by intense situational pressure, making institutional safeguards necessary even for committed officers.
- **William James's teaching affirms attitude malleability, not determinism:** The proposition that a person can alter life by altering attitudes captures the malleability of attitude: it is not fixed temperament but a chosen, revisable stance that can be reshaped through reflection, training and experience; it must not be misread as claiming that attitude change alone mechanically determines outcomes.
- **Persuasion-based reform is slower but more durable than rule-based constraint:** Attitude change through persuasion is slower and less certain than rule-based reform through binding institutional constraints, but more durable once internalised; over-reliance on institutional constraint without attitude change risks purely compliance-based conduct that reverts once monitoring relaxes.
- **Training must target affective, not only cognitive, component for durable change:** Civil-service training that targets only the cognitive component through lectures and manuals is insufficient for durable attitude change; simulation-based and case-based learning that engages the affective component through empathetic exposure is necessary to reshape long-held biases toward marginalised communities.
- **Positive mindset reads rules with their public purpose:** A positive mindset interprets rules and regulations with their public purpose in view, applying them to enable fair access and citizen welfare; a negative mindset treats rules chiefly as obstacles, sources of power or shields against responsibility, producing delay, denial and extraction.
- **Positive attitude under stress preserves judgement and citizen trust:** A positive attitude is an essential characteristic of a civil servant under stress because it preserves analytical judgement, prevents panic from amplifying a crisis, and maintains citizen trust in the administration's competence and fairness during difficult situations.
- **Attitude toward vulnerable groups shapes discretionary service quality:** A field officer's negative attitude toward a beneficiary community, formed through biased social conditioning, can distort service delivery even where rules are formally neutral; training must therefore target the affective component through immersive exposure rather than relying on rule-knowledge alone.
- **Purposive interpretation avoids both literalism and arbitrary benevolence:** A sound administrative reading of rules avoids obstructive literalism that denies citizens entitled services and arbitrary benevolence that bypasses mandatory conditions for favoured applicants; the positive mindset interprets with public purpose while remaining accountable to law, published criteria and equal treatment.
