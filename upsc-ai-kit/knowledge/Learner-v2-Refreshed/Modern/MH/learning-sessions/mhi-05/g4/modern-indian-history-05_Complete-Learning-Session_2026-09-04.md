### SESSION 1 — FOUNDATION — 1. FROM COMMERCIAL-FISCAL BASE TO MILITARY-FISCAL STATE: RING-FENCE, BUFFER, SUBSIDIARY DEPENDENCY, PARAMOUNTCY, ANNEXATION

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** British territorial expansion was a graduated process in which the Company moved from protecting buffers to controlling allies and finally annexing selected territories.

**Technical definition:** The expansion ladder joined ring-fence strategy, subsidiary dependency, the claim of paramountcy and direct annexation to a Bengal-financed fiscal-military state.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE/ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Company expansion after 1765 combined changing policy postures with an expanding fiscal-military capacity rather than following one predetermined blueprint.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

- **ring-fence**
- **buffer**
- **subsidiary dependency**
- **paramountcy**
- **annexation**
- **fiscal-military state**

**How to use them:** Define ring-fence and buffer policy first, show how subsidiary dependency removed effective sovereignty, then connect paramountcy and annexation to the Company's fiscal-military state.

Topic 04 established that Buxar and the 1765 Treaty of Allahabad converted the Company from a trading concern with political leverage into Bengal's direct revenue sovereign. This topic begins where that one ends: it asks how a firm holding one province's Diwani became, within roughly sixty years, the paramount power over virtually the entire subcontinent except the Punjab and Sindh. The honest answer is not a single master plan executed from Calcutta, but a recognisable escalating ladder of policy postures that different Governors-General climbed at different speeds, for different local reasons, using the same underlying fiscal-military capacity built on Bengal's revenues.

FACT: five successive postures recur across this topic and are worth naming precisely, because examiners routinely test whether a candidate can place a specific episode on the correct rung. **Ring-fence/buffer policy** treated a neighbouring state as a security perimeter to be kept stable and friendly, not conquered -- the Company's early stance toward Awadh after 1765 and its early stance toward the Sikh state before 1809 both fit this description. **Subsidiary dependency** (examined fully in Part IV) converted a formally sovereign ally into a state that had surrendered its own army, diplomacy and, often, part of its revenue, while its ruler remained on the throne. **Paramountcy**, a term historians attribute to Lord Hastings's explicit articulation from 1813, asserted that the Company's own security interests entitled it to override, arbitrate, or threaten any Indian state's internal or external conduct, whether or not a subsidiary treaty existed. **Annexation** -- the direct absorption of territory into Company-administered provinces -- was the final rung, reached by outright conquest (Mysore's core territory in 1799, Sindh in 1843, the Punjab in 1849), by succession doctrine (the Lapse cases culminating in 1848-56), or by an administrative pretext (Awadh, 1856).

ANALYSIS: reading these five postures as a ladder, rather than as unrelated episodes, does two things for an answer. First, it explains why the same decade could see the Company simultaneously act as a cautious neighbour (toward the Sikh state before 1839), an intrusive over-lord (toward Hyderabad and Awadh after 1798-1801), and an outright conqueror (toward Mysore in 1799) -- these were not contradictory policies but different rungs applied to different states at different moments of relative strength. Second, it prevents the common error of treating "expansion" as one uniform process; a 15- or 20-mark answer earns marks precisely by naming which rung a given episode belongs to, rather than describing all of Company expansion as generic "conquest."

### SESSION 2 — FOUNDATION — 2. CONTINGENCY, NOT INEVITABILITY: READING TERRITORIAL EXPANSION AS A SEQUENCE OF CHOICES

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** the clearest textbook evidence for genuine contingency is that the Company lost battles and signed treaties it did not dictate on its own terms.

**Technical definition:** The correct formulation, and the one that earns marks in a "was British expansion inevitable" question, is that expansion was structurally favoured but not predetermined: each governor-general's forward policy was itself a contested choice, reversible in principle, whose cumulative effect nonetheless built an increasingly self-reinforcing advantage.

⚠️ ANALYSIS: a persistent temptation in narrating 1767-1856 is to write it backwards -- to treat the 1856 map of British India as the predetermined destination of a single continuous plan set in motion at Plassey. Every governor-generalship examined in this package resists that reading. Company directors in London genuinely wavered between expansion and retrenchment: Warren Hastings's era mixed opportunistic war with real caution; the Court of Directors recalled Wellesley in 1805 specifically because his wars had produced a financial crisis, not because London approved of unlimited conquest; Cornwallis returned in 1805 with explicit non-intervention instructions; and even Dalhousie's annexations were debated, not unanimous, within Company and parliamentary circles of the day. Local governors-general on the spot frequently exceeded, reinterpreted, or reversed instructions from London based on immediate security or revenue calculations, meaning the trajectory of expansion was produced by repeated local decisions, not executed from a single fixed blueprint.

FACT: the clearest textbook evidence for genuine contingency is that the Company lost battles and signed treaties it did not dictate on its own terms. Haidar Ali forced the Madras Council to accept peace on his terms in 1769; the Marathas defeated a British force at Wadgaon in 1779, forcing the humiliating Convention of Wadgaon; the First Anglo-Maratha War ended in 1782 with the negotiated Treaty of Salbai, a genuine settlement rather than a dictated surrender; and Yeshwant Rao Holkar fought Company forces to a standstill in 1804-05, contributing to Wellesley's recall. None of these outcomes fit a narrative of predetermined, uncontested conquest.

ANALYSIS: this does not mean expansion was accidental or that no consistent pattern existed -- the pattern (war, alliance, and annexation feeding a growing fiscal-military base that made the next round of expansion easier) is real and repeats across all three fronts this topic covers. The correct formulation, and the one that earns marks in a "was British expansion inevitable" question, is that expansion was **structurally favoured but not predetermined**: each governor-general's forward policy was itself a contested choice, reversible in principle, whose cumulative effect nonetheless built an increasingly self-reinforcing advantage.

**Directly answers the 2022 Mains GS-I Q2 demand** (examined in full as a solved Mains model in Part IX): why did Company armies, mostly Indian soldiers, defeat larger and often well-equipped Indian armies with such consistency? The first and most load-bearing part of the answer is fiscal: Bengal's Diwani, secured in 1765, gave the Company a large, increasingly reliable direct-tax base entirely independent of trade profit or metropolitan remittance, and this revenue is what financed every subsequent campaign this topic covers -- Mysore, Maratha, Sindh and Sikh alike. Historians studying the Company's army (notably Douglas Peers) describe this fusion of taxation and military build-up as a form of **"military fiscalism"**: the army was not merely funded by the state, it was itself one of the state's primary instruments for extracting and defending revenue, creating a self-reinforcing loop between fiscal capacity and military capacity.

FACT: the Company's sepoy armies expanded steadily after the conquest of Bengal and were composed predominantly of Indian soldiers under a comparatively thin European officer corps. Their strategic importance came from permanence, regular finance, drill, artillery support and organised logistics rather than from an unsupported aggregate headcount. Recruitment drew heavily from north Indian military-labour networks, while later expansion brought additional regional groups into Company service.

ANALYSIS: three structural features distinguished this force from most opposing Indian armies of the period, and each is a legitimate, source-grounded exam point. **Permanence and regularity of pay**: soldiers who are paid reliably, in cash, on a fixed schedule are more disciplined and less prone to desertion or extortion of the local population than irregularly paid troops, and several regional Indian states struggled to match this regularity once their own revenue bases were strained by war. **A standing officer corps and artillery arm**: European-trained artillery and engineering officers gave the Company a durable technical edge in siege warfare (decisive at Seringapatam in 1799 and repeatedly in the Maratha wars), even where individual Indian armies matched or exceeded Company numbers. **Integrated logistics**: a functioning commissariat, credit network and transport system let Company armies sustain campaigns far from their home bases (Wellesley's armies fighting simultaneously in the Deccan and north India in 1803 is the clearest example), whereas several opposing forces depended on more localised, less continuous supply arrangements.

FACT: beyond fiscal depth and standing military organisation, the Company drew on three further, well-evidenced advantages. **Credit and banking access**: Company forces could draw on organised credit markets (including, in Bengal, banking houses such as the Jagat Seths in the Plassey era) to bridge campaign expenses ahead of revenue collection, a facility not every regional treasury could match on comparable terms. **Maritime reinforcement**: naval control of the Bengal, Bombay and Madras coastlines let the Company move troops, artillery and supplies by sea between presidencies faster than most opponents could redeploy overland -- Clive and Watson's relief of Calcutta by sea in January 1757, and the ability to reinforce Madras from Bengal during the Mysore wars, both illustrate this. **Diplomacy that produced serial isolation of opponents**: this is the single most consistently documented mechanism across all three fronts this topic covers. Cornwallis brought the Marathas, the Nizam and the rulers of Travancore and Coorg into alliance against Tipu in the Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790-92); the same Maratha–Nizam combination had fought alongside the Company against Haidar Ali in the First Anglo-Mysore War, then against the Company in the Second; and Wellesley methodically detached Hyderabad, the restored Mysore state, and then the Peshwa himself from any common front, before finally confronting Sindhia, Bhonsle and Holkar in sequence rather than together.

FACT: this diplomacy relied heavily on **Indian allies and recruits**, not on Company arms alone. The Nizam of Hyderabad fought alongside the Company against Mysore in three of the four Anglo-Mysore Wars; substantial numbers of Indian soldiers disbanded from defeated Mysorean and Maratha armies were absorbed, where the Company's own recruitment system could accommodate them, into its expanding sepoy ranks; and Company campaigns regularly depended on Indian intelligence networks, supply contractors and local guides. ANALYSIS: this recruitment and alliance pattern means the "British versus Indians" framing that sometimes appears in popular retellings is imprecise -- a more accurate description, and the one the 2022 PYQ itself invites ("armies... mostly comprising of Indian soldiers"), is that Company campaigns were fought predominantly by Indian personnel under a British-organised fiscal-military and diplomatic system, against other Indian rulers who were rarely able to sustain a comparably durable coalition against it.

FACT: this advantage was neither immediate nor unbroken, and any complete answer must include the Company's own limitations and reverses, both to avoid overclaiming and because these episodes are independently examinable. Haidar Ali twice defeated Company forces decisively enough to dictate peace terms (1769 and again through the early 1780s, when he "inflicted one defeat after another" on Company armies in the Carnatic); the Marathas defeated a Company force at Wadgaon in 1779, forcing a full renunciation of gains; and Yeshwant Rao Holkar's mobile Maratha tactics, combined with the Raja of Bharatpur's resistance, inflicted heavy losses on Lord Lake's forces in 1804-05 and contributed directly to Wellesley's recall. In the Anglo-Sikh Wars, the Company suffered severe losses at Ferozeshah (1845) and again at Chillianwala (1849) before recovering to win the decisive engagements that followed. ANALYSIS: including these reverses is not a concession that weakens the "why Company armies won" argument -- it strengthens it, by showing that Company success rested on a durable, resilient system (fiscal depth, replaceable manpower, alliance-building) that could absorb individual defeats, rather than on uninterrupted battlefield dominance.

### SESSION 5 — CORE — 5. MYSORE'S STATE CAPACITY: HAIDAR ALI'S RISE, ARMY REFORM AND TIPU'S "MILITARY FISCALISM"

#### DEFINITION / WHAT THIS IS CALLED

**Plain-language definition:** The historian Burton Stein has described Tipu's revenue system as a form of "military fiscalism": taxes collected from a wide base directly by state officials specifically to mobilise resources for a large standing army, part of a deliberate project to build centralised military hegemony in place of the older segmentary, intermediary-sharing structure inherited from Vijayanagara. ⚠️ ANALYSIS: this is the same underlying logic -- revenue depth converted into permanent military capacity -- that the Company itself was applying at greater scale from its Bengal base (subtopic 3); Mysore is best understood not as a "traditional" state overwhelmed by a "modern" one, but as a rival state pursuing a structurally similar fiscal-military strategy on a smaller territorial and resource base.

**Technical definition:** ANALYSIS: this combination -- genuine, source-attested reform ambition, alongside a resource base that could not fully sustain every ambition -- is the correct, qualified position for an exam answer; overstating Mysore's modernity (claiming it rivalled contemporary European industrial capacity) is as much an error as dismissing Tipu's reforms as mere myth.

**How to use them:** Frame the answer through Haidar Ali's rise; define army reform, connect Tipu's "military fiscalism" with "military fiscalism" to explain the mechanism, and use Haidar for the decisive comparison or qualification.

FACT: Mysore rose from a Vijayanagara-era vice-royalty into an assertive regional power across the eighteenth century, with its centralised military authority strengthening from the reign of Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar in the late seventeenth century. Haidar Ali, a man of comparatively humble origin who began as a junior Mysore army officer, rose through military talent and, by 1761, ousted the corrupt dalavai (chief minister) Nanjaraj and effectively took charge of the state, leaving the Wodeyar king as a titular figurehead. Haidar modernised his army with French-trained officers, building an efficient, European-disciplined infantry and artillery organised through the risala system -- fixed-strength units with clear chains of command reporting directly to the ruler -- and used this reformed force to subjugate local hereditary chiefs (deshmukhs and palegars) who had previously controlled the countryside's agricultural surplus.

FACT: Haidar, and after him Tipu Sultan, replaced indirect, intermediary-based land-revenue collection with direct assessment and cash collection by salaried state officials, sharply enlarging the state's own resource base while curbing the older Mughal-derived jagir system. The historian Burton Stein has described Tipu's revenue system as a form of **"military fiscalism"**: taxes collected from a wide base directly by state officials specifically to mobilise resources for a large standing army, part of a deliberate project to build centralised military hegemony in place of the older segmentary, intermediary-sharing structure inherited from Vijayanagara. ⚠️ ANALYSIS: this is the same underlying logic -- revenue depth converted into permanent military capacity -- that the Company itself was applying at greater scale from its Bengal base (subtopic 3); Mysore is best understood not as a "traditional" state overwhelmed by a "modern" one, but as a rival state pursuing a structurally similar fiscal-military strategy on a smaller territorial and resource base.

FACT: Tipu's ambitions extended beyond the army. **Elimination reasoning:**

##### Drill 1 - Company armies were overwhelmingly Indian, not "British"

**Claim:** State explicitly, as the 2022 PYQ itself frames it, that these armies were "mostly comprising of Indian soldiers" under a comparatively thin layer of British officers. Never describe Company victories as demographically "British"; explain how Indian recruitment was integrated with regular finance, command, artillery and logistics.

**Exam move:** Any answer that explains Company success purely through "British" military or technological superiority, without naming the Indian sepoy composition, has misread the question's own framing and lost an easy opening mark.

##### Drill 2 - Contingency, not inevitability

**Claim:** Present expansion as a sequence of case-by-case choices shaped by local alliance opportunities and each ruler's internal politics (subtopic 2), not as a predetermined "rise of British power" narrative; cite the Company's own reverses (Wadgaon 1779, the Second Mysore War, Holkar's 1804-05 resistance) as direct evidence against inevitability.

**Exam move:** A question asking "how" or "why" expansion succeeded rewards naming at least one specific Company reverse alongside the successes -- this single habit demonstrates the contingency argument rather than merely asserting it.

##### Drill 3 - Tipu's reforms were real but bounded, not myth and not modern industry

**Claim:** Credit Tipu's revenue centralisation, agrarian and irrigation encouragement, and "military fiscalism" (Burton Stein's term) as genuine and source-attested, while also naming Irfan Habib's caution that Mysore's modernising ambition ran ahead of its actual resources -- never claim Mysore rivalled contemporary European industrial capacity, and never dismiss the reforms as fabricated nationalist myth either.

**Exam move:** A balanced answer on Tipu names one specific reform (direct revenue assessment, replacing intermediaries) and one specific limit (resource constraint per Habib) in the same paragraph.

##### Drill 4 - The Mysore wars' alliance pattern shifted every time; it was never fixed

**Claim:** State precisely: Marathas and the Nizam sided WITH the Company in the First (1767-69) and Third (1790-92) wars, but WITH Haidar Ali AGAINST the Company in the Second (1780-84) war; only in the Fourth (1799) was there no comparable counterweight, because Hyderabad was already a Subsidiary Alliance dependency since 1798.

**Exam move:** Never write "the Marathas and the Nizam always supported the Company against Mysore" -- this is factually wrong for the Second War specifically and is one of the most common alliance-sequencing errors in this topic.

##### Drill 5 - Tipu's French connection was real diplomacy, not proof of an imminent French invasion

**Claim:** Confirm Tipu's genuine outreach to Revolutionary France, Kabul, Arabia and Turkey (subtopic 7), while also naming Edward Ingram's assessment that the actual probability of French military intervention by 1798-99 was low -- Wellesley's invocation of the "French danger" functioned significantly as a pretext for a war he had independent reasons to want.

**Exam move:** State both halves together -- "real diplomatic outreach, exaggerated invasion threat" -- rather than either denying the French contact occurred or accepting Wellesley's threat assessment at face value.

##### Drill 6 - Bassein (1802) was a turning point, not the end of Maratha resistance

**Claim:** Bassein removed the Peshwa -- the confederacy's nominal head -- from the field of opposition, but Sindhia, Bhonsle and Holkar all continued fighting through the Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803-05), and the confederacy was defeated only by 1818 in the Third War; never state that Bassein ended Maratha resistance outright.

**Exam move:** A "significance of Bassein" answer earns full marks only by explaining WHY it mattered (removing the confederacy's legitimating head, enabling serial isolation) rather than by overstating WHAT it immediately ended.

##### Drill 7 - Distinguish a cash subsidy from a territorial cession precisely

**Claim:** A cash subsidy was a direct annual payment; a territorial cession transferred revenue-yielding land in lieu of, or partial substitution for, that payment (as at the Nizam's 1800 treaty and Awadh's 1801 settlement) -- both produced dependency, but only cession permanently reduced the state's own resource base.

**Exam move:** For any named Subsidiary Alliance case, state explicitly whether payment was in cash or in ceded land; this is a frequently tested precision point, and the 2018 Prelims PYQ solved above turns on a closely related distinction.

##### Drill 8 - Name the responsibility paradox in both directions

**Claim:** State both halves explicitly -- the Subsidiary Alliance stripped a ruler of army, treasury and diplomacy while leaving him formally responsible for governance outcomes, and the Company then used resulting administrative strain (fiscal pressure, Resident interference, disbanded soldiers turning to Pindari banditry) as its own justification for further intervention.

**Exam move:** An answer naming only "the ruler lost power" or only "the Company blamed misgovernance," without connecting the two as cause and consequence, has described half the mechanism -- exactly as Topic 04's Dual Government paradox required both halves.

##### Drill 9 - Napier's Sindh quote is genuine; the "Peccavi" pun is not

**Claim:** Cite only Napier's attested diary entry ("We have no right to seize Sind, yet we shall do so...") as evidence of the annexation's dubious legality; do not repeat the popular "Peccavi" ("I have sinned"/"I have Sindh") telegram story, which is a widely circulated but historically unverified anecdote excluded from this package.

**Exam move:** If a question invites a quotation on Sindh's annexation, use the diary entry only, and never present the Peccavi pun as a sourced historical fact.

##### Drill 10 - Ranjit Singh never fought an Anglo-Sikh War

**Claim:** State plainly that Ranjit Singh died in June 1839, a full six years before the First Anglo-Sikh War began in December 1845; the Treaty of Amritsar (1809) was a negotiated boundary settlement between the Company and a militarily strong, sovereign equal, not a Subsidiary Alliance and not a war treaty.

**Exam move:** Any statement implying Ranjit Singh fought, negotiated after defeat, or was annexed by the Company is a basic actor-timeline error; the wars followed only after his death produced succession chaos (subtopic 19).

##### Drill 11 - Match each Anglo-Sikh war to its own battles and settlement, never mix them

##### Mains 5 - 20 marks | 300 words

**Question:** "British territorial expansion in India after 1798 proceeded through a variety of mechanisms, not a single method of conquest." Discuss with reference to Mysore, the Marathas, Sindh and the Sikh Empire.

**Directive alignment:** "Discuss with reference to" four named cases requires a genuinely comparative essay, not four disconnected mini-narratives.

**Introduction:** Between 1799 and 1849, the Company annexed Mysore-adjacent control, Maratha territory, Sindh and the Punjab through four distinct legal-political mechanisms, not a uniform method.

**Answer:** Mysore (1799) fell through outright war and post-conquest settlement -- Tipu's death at Seringapatam followed by Wodeyar restoration under subsidiary control, not lapse or misgovernance annexation. #### 1. Snapshot & core idea

**Deeper — expansion moved from ring-fence to paramountcy.**

- ✅ Early Company policy often sought buffers, but after Bengal the Company increasingly used war, diplomacy, Residents and subsidies to dominate Indian states.

- ✅ Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan resisted through military reform, diplomacy and attempts to balance British power.
- ✅ Maratha weakness lay in confederate rivalries; British diplomacy exploited divisions among the Peshwa, Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad.
- ✅ Sikh power was consolidated under Ranjit Singh, but after his death court factionalism and military tensions enabled British victory in the Anglo-Sikh wars.
