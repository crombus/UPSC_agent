#### 1. Purpose and scope

This topic covers execution mechanics — how to allocate the paper's
available time across choice, planning, drafting and revision for two
essays. It assumes the analytical topics (`01`–`12`) are already
understood; here they are sequenced under a clock.

#### 2. Core terms in plain language

- **Choice-and-decode time:** minutes spent scanning prompts and
  selecting (`01`) plus initial decoding (`02`/`03`).
- **Planning time:** minutes spent building the argument map (`05`) and
  paragraph plan (`07`) before drafting prose.
- **Drafting time:** minutes spent writing continuous prose for the
  chosen structure.
- **Revision buffer:** minutes reserved at the end for the sweep
  described in `14`/`15`.

#### 3. ✅ Exam facts / source basis

- ✅ The Essay paper baseline is **3 hours (180 minutes)** for two essays
  of about 1000–1200 words, one chosen from each Section. Use the current
  official paper/admit card if an examination notice changes this.
- ✅ The 2024 paper prints "(125 × 2 = 250)"; the locally held 2025 copy
  has no marks line. Neither fact creates an official phase-wise timing
  rule.
- ⚠️ The minute plan below is a pedagogical starter, not a UPSC
  prescription. Its value is that it can be timed, diagnosed and adjusted.

#### 4. The central idea and common misreading

⚠️ **Central idea:** allocate time deliberately across all four phases
(choice, planning, drafting, revision) for *both* essays, rather than
spending most of the time on the first essay and rushing the second. ❌
**Common misreading:** starting to write immediately after reading the
prompt, skipping planning entirely, and discovering the structure has no
clear thesis only after several paragraphs are already drafted.

#### 5. Basic decomposition questions

1. How much total time do I have for both essays combined? (✅ Start with
   the verified 180-minute baseline; check the current official
   paper/admit card for any change.)
2. How much time will I spend on choice-and-decode versus planning
   versus drafting versus revision, for each essay?
3. Am I allocating roughly similar planning discipline to both essays,
   or favouring the one I feel more confident about?
4. What is my trigger to stop planning and start drafting (e.g. a
   completed argument map)?
5. What is my trigger to stop drafting and start revising (e.g.
   reaching the upper end of the word range)?

#### 11. ❌ Traps and repair moves

- ❌ **Skipping planning to "save time."** → Repair: even a compressed
  plan (thesis + 3–4 line argument map) is faster overall than
  discovering structural problems mid-draft.
- ❌ **Over-investing in the first essay, leaving little time for the
  second.** → Repair: pre-commit to a time cap per essay and track it.
- ❌ **No protected revision buffer.** → Repair: treat the revision
  sweep (`14`/`15`) as non-negotiable, not optional "if time permits."
- ❌ **Continuing to search for a better thesis indefinitely.** → Repair:
  set a decision deadline (Section 8) and commit.
- ❌ **Calling a partial draft a mock.** → Repair: a paper mock always
  means two complete essays under the 180-minute clock; partial drafting
  is a named micro-drill, not a substitute.

#### 11A. Recovery protocol under the clock

| If this phase overruns | Pre-decided recovery action |
|---|---|
| Choice/decode | Commit to the current-best thesis; do not reopen all options |
| Planning | Keep thesis, three clusters and counter-view; delete surplus branches |
| Drafting | Reduce the number of clusters, not the mechanism/qualification inside those retained |
| Revision | Run relevance plus factual/quotation-risk checks first; language polish comes last |

underlying skill to repair, rather than a reason to permanently compress

define operative terms, state the central thesis, develop three to five linked

#### 1. Advanced proposition and boundary

**Proposition:** ⚠️ time-budgeting should be personalised from diagnosed
bottlenecks (measured in practice, per `basic/13` Section 12), not
copied from a generic split — a candidate whose actual weakness is slow
thesis-formation needs a different allocation than one whose weakness is
over-long drafting. **Boundary:** ❌ this topic does not teach the
underlying skills (`01`–`12`) — it teaches how to sequence and recover
them under a real clock.

#### 2. Concept definition and taxonomy

- **Bottleneck phase:** the phase that most consistently overruns its
  planned allocation across your own timed attempts.
- **Recovery protocol:** a pre-decided, specific action taken when a
  phase overruns mid-attempt (Section 6), rather than an improvised
  panic response.
- **Two-essay budget interdependence:** the fact that overrunning on
  essay one directly reduces available time for essay two — the budget
  must be tracked jointly, not per-essay in isolation.
- **Absolute vs. personalised budget:** ✅ The paper baseline is 180
  minutes. `basic/13` supplies a labelled pedagogical 10 + 85 + 85
  starter plan; ⚠️ modify phase allocations only from logged evidence,
  never by silently taking time from Essay 2.

#### 3. Tension pairs and hidden assumptions

- **Perfectionism vs. completion:** the hidden assumption that spending
  extra time refining the first essay improves the overall paper; in
  practice, both essays must be completed and one must be chosen from each
  displayed section, so overrunning on the first directly compromises the
  second.
- **Fixed template vs. adaptive budget:** the assumption that a single
  time-split should be memorised and applied identically to every prompt;
  in fact issue-based prompts (`03`) may need more scoping time, while
  well-decoded aphorisms (`02`) may need less. The 25/95/180 completion
  checkpoints remain fixed unless the official paper says otherwise.

#### 4. Levels of analysis and temporal-spatial scale

⚠️ Track time at three levels: the whole-paper level (both essays plus
any shared reading/choice time), the single-essay level (choice through
revision), and the paragraph level (drafting pace per planned paragraph,
from `07`'s paragraph-count plan) — a bottleneck can appear at any of
these levels and needs a level-appropriate fix.

#### 5. Stakeholder map and distributional effects

⚠️ Not directly applicable in the usual sense — the only "stakeholder"
in this topic's own scope is the candidate's own performance across two
essays; the relevant distributional question is how minutes are
allocated fairly between the two required essays rather than
concentrated in one.

#### 6. Causality, mechanisms and feedback loops — recovery protocols

```text
PHASE OVERRUNS its planned time
        |
        v
DIAGNOSE: which phase, and by how much?
        |
   choice/decode overrun --------> commit to current-best thesis now (05, Section 8)
   planning overrun --------------> compress to a 3-4 line argument map, skip further options
   drafting overrun ---------------> cut to fewer, better-developed paragraph clusters (07)
   revision overrun (i.e. no time left) --> do only the relevance + evidence-risk sweep (14/15),
                                              skip the language-polish pass
```
⚠️ Deciding these recovery actions *in advance* (not in the moment of
panic) is what separates an advanced execution plan from a merely
aspirational one.

#### 7. Necessary/sufficient conditions and exceptions

⚠️ A pre-decided recovery protocol (Section 6) is necessary for
resilient execution but not sufficient — it must be paired with honest,
ongoing time-tracking (checking elapsed time at natural checkpoints, e.g.
after choice, after planning) to be triggered at all. **Exception:** if a
candidate's diagnosed bottleneck is consistently the *same* phase across
many practice attempts, it is often more efficient to fix the underlying
skill (e.g. faster decoding via `02`) than to keep compensating with a
recovery protocol every time.

#### 8. Paradox, dialectic and counterfactual test

**Paradox:** spending slightly more time on planning than feels
comfortable usually *reduces* total time to a finished essay, because it
prevents a mid-draft structural rewrite, which is far more costly than
an unhurried planning phase. **Counterfactual test:** "If I had stopped
planning two minutes earlier, would my draft have hit a structural
problem I did not foresee?" If yes, planning time was actually
under-allocated, not over-allocated, even though it felt slow at the
time.

#### 9. Ethical conflict and synthesis

⚠️ Not a substantive ethical topic in itself; the closest relevant
"conflict" is between the temptation to keep polishing a stronger essay
and the fairness (to your own overall score) of ensuring the weaker
essay also receives an adequate, complete attempt.

#### 10. Evidence/source-risk and India application

⚠️ Not directly applicable — this topic manages process, not content;
evidence-risk discipline for the content itself is owned by `09`/`12`.
The only relevant "evidence" here is the candidate's own logged timing
data from repeated practice attempts (Section 12 of the basic file,
extended in `16`).

#### 11. Advanced structure, paragraph sequencing and style

⚠️ Under a compressed drafting phase (Section 6), prefer cutting the
*number* of paragraph clusters (e.g. from four dimensions to three)
over cutting the *depth* of each remaining cluster — but still complete
the required essay and preserve a conclusion. The 1000–1200-word range
remains the target; do not mistake an emergency cut for a preferred
short-essay strategy.

#### 12. ❌ Failure modes and revision protocol

- ❌ **No pre-decided recovery protocol**, leading to panic-improvised
  cuts mid-draft. → Protocol: memorise Section 6's table before any
  timed attempt.
- ❌ **Ignoring diagnosed, repeated bottlenecks** and re-applying the
  same generic split every time. → Protocol: adjust your personal time
  budget based on your own logged pattern (Section 7 exception).
- ❌ **Treating the second essay's time budget as "whatever is left."**
  → Protocol: pre-commit to a hard cap for essay one regardless of how
  well it is going.

### Time Management, Planning and Execution: ARGUMENT, EVIDENCE AND COHERENCE FIREWALLS

- Do not replace three-hour execution, two-essay budgeting and revision with a generic GS-style catalogue.
- Do not cross the ownership boundary: quality diagnosis belongs to 15.
- Do not use an example without explaining its argumentative function.
- Do not treat a pedagogical scaffold as an official UPSC marking rule.
- Do not let a counter-view replace the thesis; use it to qualify the thesis.
- Do not use an unverified quotation, statistic, attribution or anecdote.
- Do not multiply dimensions that repeat the same claim in new vocabulary.
- Do not end with aspiration unsupported by the preceding argument.
- Do not sacrifice the second essay's time budget to perfect the first.
- Do not mistake novelty of phrasing for originality of thought.
