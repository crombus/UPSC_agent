## 1. Snapshot & core idea


**Foundation — Weathering, Mass Movement & Groundwater (G.C. Leong).**

✅ **Weathering** is in-situ physical disintegration and chemical alteration of rock at or near the
surface. **Erosion** detaches and removes material; transport and deposition may follow. **Regolith**
is the broader mantle of unconsolidated material above bedrock and may be residual or transported, so
it is not always unsorted material still resting on its parent rock.
- ⚠️ Mechanical weathering includes frost cracking, salt crystallisation, thermal stress and unloading.
  **Exfoliation/sheeting is not one universal process:** metre-scale sheets commonly reflect unloading
  and topography-parallel joints, whereas thermal stress can cause surface flaking or granular breakdown.
- ✅ Water expands by about **9%** on freezing, but effective frost cracking also requires liquid-water
  supply, repeated freezing conditions and connected pores or fractures.
- ⚠️ **Mass movement** is gravity-driven downslope movement. Modern description separates the
  movement—fall, topple, slide, spread or flow—from the material—rock, debris or earth; water content
  and velocity are modifiers, not the sole classification axes.
- ✅ Soil creep is slow deformation/movement that may tilt trees, fences and posts. Slides, flows and
  falls can be rapid, but “landslide” is an umbrella term rather than a synonym for a rapid fall.
- ⚠️ Slope failure occurs when driving stress exceeds shear strength. Pore-water pressure reduces
  effective normal stress; antecedent moisture, material, discontinuities, toe removal, loading and
  drainage must be assessed before naming a trigger as the complete cause.
- ⚠️ An **aquifer** is a saturated geological unit that stores and transmits usable quantities of
  groundwater. An **aquitard** transmits water slowly; avoid treating any natural layer as perfectly
  impermeable at every scale.
- ⚠️ The **water table** is the upper surface of saturation in an unconfined aquifer. A confined
  aquifer is described by a **potentiometric surface**; artesian conditions require confinement and
  hydraulic head, not a compulsory syncline, chalk/limestone aquifer or two clay beds. A flowing
  artesian well occurs only where that head exceeds local ground elevation.

**Standard — Weathering Controls, Chemical Processes & Karst Hydrology (Majid Husain).**

✅ Rate and depth of weathering reflect interacting controls: rock/mineral composition and structure,
climate, relief and drainage, organisms/vegetation, time and land use. Climate strongly influences the
available reactions, but no single factor is universally decisive. Mechanical processes are favoured
where thermal, freezing or salt stresses are effective; chemical alteration is commonly rapid in warm,
moist and well-drained settings.
- ✅ Frost action works where water expands ~9% on freezing — powerful in humid micro-thermal, sub-arctic, polar and high-altitude climates.
- ✅ Crystallisation (salt weathering) and exfoliation domes (concentric shells stripped from a rock mass) are advanced physical forms.
- ✅ Chemical weathering is more pronounced in karst / limestone regions because solution + carbonation are highly effective there.
- ✅ Karst is typified by dominant solution, LACK of surface water, and stream sinks (dolines), cave systems and resurgences (springs); it lacks a well-integrated surface drainage.
- ⚠️ Thick, soluble and well-fractured carbonate rock with sustained recharge favours karst, but karst
  is not confined to pure limestone above a fixed water table; dissolution also occurs in dolomite,
  evaporites and both vadose and saturated zones.
- ⚠️ Springs are natural groundwater-discharge points controlled by water-table, potentiometric,
  fracture or conduit geometry. They are not all simply intersections of one regional water table
  with the land surface.

## 2. Key classification / data

| Process / term | Mechanism | Result / diagnostic feature |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ Mechanical weathering | Frost cracking, salt crystallisation, thermal stress, unloading | Fracturing, flaking/sheeting, granular breakdown, scree |
| ✅ Chemical weathering | Solution, carbonation, hydrolysis, oxidation | Limestone solution, rusty oxidation, clay formation |
| ✅ Biological weathering | Plant roots, burrowing/trampling animals | Rock split along joints and bedding planes |
| ⚠️ Mass movement | Gravity acting against material strength; water can add weight and raise pore pressure | Fall, topple, slide, spread, flow and creep in rock/debris/earth |
| ⚠️ Groundwater | Storage and flow through connected pores, fractures or conduits | Aquifer/aquitard, water table, potentiometric surface, springs |
| ✅ Solution | Dissolved ions are removed in water; especially effective in soluble rocks | Widened joints/grikes; karst |
| ✅ Carbonation | Water + CO2 forms weak carbonic acid that dissolves carbonate minerals | Limestone dissolution and karst development |
| ✅ Hydrolysis | Minerals chemically combine with water | Feldspar → clay minerals |
| ✅ Oxidation | Oxygen via water reacts with minerals such as iron | Reddish/rusty rotted rock |

## 4. Must-Know Facts (Prelims)


**Foundation — Weathering, Mass Movement & Groundwater (G.C. Leong).**
- ✅ Weathering is in situ; erosion entails detachment/removal and commonly transport. Do not define
  erosion as “weathering plus transport”.
- ⚠️ Exfoliation/sheeting may reflect unloading; thermal stress can produce surface flaking. Scree is
  angular debris accumulated below a slope, not a diagnostic product of exfoliation alone.
- ✅ Water expands by about **9%** on freezing → freeze-thaw shattering where cracks repeatedly wet and freeze.
- ✅ Solution is the most potent process in LIMESTONE regions.
- ⚠️ Aquifer = saturated unit that stores and transmits usable groundwater; water table = upper
  saturation surface of an unconfined aquifer.
- ⚠️ Artesian = confined groundwater under sufficient head; “flowing” requires potentiometric head
  above the wellhead. Confining beds need not be perfectly impermeable.

**Standard — Weathering Controls, Chemical Processes & Karst Hydrology (Majid Husain).**
- ✅ Six weathering controls: rock structure, climate, slope, vegetation, time, land use (Majid Husain).
- ⚠️ Chemical alteration is commonly rapid in warm, moist, well-drained settings; mechanical and
  biological processes still operate simultaneously.
- ✅ Four chemical processes: SOLUTION, CARBONATION, HYDROLYSIS, OXIDATION.
- ✅ Carbonation uses weak carbonic acid (water + CO2) on limestone.
- ✅ Karst signatures: dolines (stream sinks), caves, resurgences; poor surface drainage.
- ⚠️ Frost cracking is most effective where fractured rock repeatedly freezes with liquid water
  available; permanent deep cold alone is insufficient.

## 5. UPSC Traps

> 🔑 Trap: Do not confuse similar-looking landforms, processes, scales or Indian locations; UPSC often tests the exact mechanism and setting.


**Foundation — Weathering, Mass Movement & Groundwater (G.C. Leong).**
- ❌ Weathering and erosion are the same. → Weathering is in-situ breakdown; erosion adds transport of debris.
- ❌ Soil creep is a rapid, visible movement. → Creep is slow; other mass movements range from very
  slow to extremely rapid. “Landslide” includes slides, flows, falls and related slope movements.
- ❌ All wells are artesian. → Artesian conditions require a confined aquifer and hydraulic head;
  only some artesian wells flow at the surface.

**Standard — Weathering Controls, Chemical Processes & Karst Hydrology (Majid Husain).**
- ❌ Carbonation “turns limestone into liquid.” → Carbonic-acid water dissolves carbonate minerals and
  carries their ions in solution; the rock does not literally melt.
- ❌ Karst regions have dense surface drainage. → Karst LACKS integrated surface drainage — water sinks underground via dolines to form caves and resurgences.
- ❌ Chemical weathering is strongest in cold dry climates. → It is strongest in hot-humid climates; cold/high-altitude zones favour physical (frost) weathering.

### 8.1 Modern process controls

- ⚠️ **Slope classification:** use movement type plus material. Rate, water content and activity state
  refine the description; they do not replace it. Subsidence is predominantly vertical ground
  deformation and should not be inserted into a downslope mass-movement taxonomy.
- ⚠️ **Effective stress:** shear strength is commonly expressed as
  \(c' + (\sigma_n-u)\tan\phi'\). Force-based versions must retain area consistently; otherwise
  cohesion, normal force and pore pressure have incompatible units.
- ⚠️ **Susceptibility, hazard and risk:** susceptibility describes where terrain is prone to fail;
  hazard adds probability/intensity in time; risk combines hazard with exposure and vulnerability.
  This is a conceptual interaction, not a universal literal multiplication formula.
- ⚠️ **Managed aquifer recharge (MAR):** purposeful recharge may use surface infiltration or wells,
  including recharge to already saturated or confined aquifers where hydraulic capacity exists.
  Design is site-specific and requires aquifer characterisation, source-water quality, pretreatment,
  clogging control, monitoring and an abstraction budget. No universal pit/trench dimensions, fill
  percentages or runoff coefficient are safe controls.
- 📰 **Current hydrogeology control:** CGWB's **NAQUIM 2.0** replaces a single 1971 province map as the
  modern planning framework by mapping aquifer geometry, weathered/fractured hard rock, alluvium,
  basalt and water quality at finer user-oriented scales.

## 9. Soils: formation, profile, world types and India's soil geography

> **Why this section exists:** weathering produces regolith, and regolith becomes soil — yet **no
> file in this folder taught soil formation or any soil type**, although this file is the routed
> owner for *black cotton soil formation from volcanic rock*, `30_Primary-Economic-Activities-
> Agriculture.md` uses "chernozem", "alluvial" and "black soil" as evidence without defining them,
> and `15_Hot-Wet-Equatorial-Climate.md` is routed a demand on rainforest soil nutrient status.
> Soil is the hinge between physical and economic geography, and it was missing.

### 9.1 From rock to soil

```text
PARENT ROCK
  -> WEATHERING (physical breakup + chemical alteration)  [Section 1 of this file]
  -> RESIDUAL REGOLITH / PARENT MATERIAL: unconsolidated mineral material
  -> + ORGANIC MATTER from plants and soil organisms
  -> + WATER MOVEMENT (leaching down, capillary rise up)
  -> HORIZON DIFFERENTIATION over time
  -> SOIL: a vertically organised, biologically active natural body
```

⚠️ Soil formation is controlled by five interacting factors, none of which is sufficient alone:

| Factor | How it acts | Diagnostic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ Climate | Temperature and moisture influence weathering, decomposition and translocation | Strong at broad scales, but interacts with parent material, relief, organisms and time |
| ⚠️ Parent material | Supplies mineral composition and texture | Dominant where soils are young or where the rock is distinctive, e.g. basalt |
| ⚠️ Relief | Governs drainage, erosion and depth of accumulation | Thin skeletal soils on steep slopes; deep soils in valleys and plains |
| ⚠️ Organisms | Add organic matter, mix horizons, cycle nutrients | Grassland root turnover builds deep humus; forest litter builds a surface layer |
| ⚠️ Time | Allows horizons to differentiate | Immature soils on floodplains and volcanic ash; mature, strongly leached soils on old stable surfaces |

> 🔑 **Trap:** soil is not merely crushed rock. Pedogenesis combines mineral parent material,
> organisms, organic matter, structure and water/solute movement. Young alluvium can function as soil
> before a strongly differentiated profile develops, so horizon differentiation is not a compulsory
> threshold separating all regolith from all soil.

### 9.2 The soil profile

| Horizon | Content | Exam significance |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ O | Surface organic litter | Often thick where litter accumulates slowly; thin/absent where production is low or decomposition/removal is rapid |
| ⚠️ A (topsoil) | Mineral matter mixed with organic matter; may show eluviation | Agriculturally important surface mineral horizon |
| ⚠️ E | Strongly leached, pale | Diagnostic of podzolisation under cool, wet, acidic conditions |
| ⚠️ B (subsoil) | Subsurface horizon altered by development; may contain illuvial clay/Fe/Al | Not every B horizon is purely illuvial |
| ⚠️ C | Weathered parent material | Links the soil to its rock |
| ⚠️ R | Unweathered bedrock | The base of the profile |

- ⚠️ **Eluviation vs illuviation:** material is removed from an eluvial horizon and deposited in an
  illuvial horizon. E horizons express eluviation most clearly; not every A or B horizon is defined
  solely by that pair.

### 9.3 Pedogenic processes and modern taxonomy

| Process | Climatic setting | What happens | Resulting soil character |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Podzolisation** | Cool, humid, coniferous forest | Acidic litter mobilises iron and aluminium downward | Pale, leached, acidic, low-fertility soils requiring liming — the taiga belt |
| ⚠️ **Laterisation** | Warm, moist tropical settings with strong leaching | Desilication/base loss and relative Fe-Al enrichment; some materials harden on exposure/drying | Lateritic profiles vary; a strict wet-dry alternation is not universal |
| ⚠️ **Calcification** | Semi-arid, low leaching | Evaporation exceeds percolation; calcium carbonate accumulates | Base-rich soils; calcrete/kankar nodules |
| ⚠️ **Salinisation / sodification** | Arid settings and poorly drained irrigation | Soluble salts accumulate; exchangeable sodium can disperse clays | Saline and sodic conditions require different diagnosis and treatment |
| ⚠️ **Gleying** | Waterlogged, poorly drained | Anaerobic conditions reduce iron | Grey-blue mottled, peaty and marshy soils |
| ⚠️ **Humification / melanisation** | Temperate grassland | Deep root turnover builds abundant humus | **Chernozem** — the deep black earths of the steppe and prairie granaries |

**USDA Soil Taxonomy — current control.** The current system recognises **12 soil orders**:
Alfisols, Andisols, Aridisols, Entisols, Gelisols, Histosols, Inceptisols, Mollisols, Oxisols,
Spodosols, Ultisols and Vertisols. The original **1975 USDA Soil Taxonomy had 10 orders**;
**Andisols** and then **Gelisols** were added subsequently, producing the current 12-order system. The
zonal/intrazonal/azonal scheme is also historical and useful only as a broad
genetic heuristic. “Azonal” does not universally mean “no B horizon”, and it must not control modern
classification.

> 🔑 **Trap** (routed as a Prelims demand): luxuriant rainforest does not prove uniformly fertile
> soil. Many old, highly leached equatorial upland soils hold much of the active nutrient capital in
> biomass and litter cycling; alluvial, volcanic and younger soils are important exceptions. See
> `15_Hot-Wet-Equatorial-Climate.md`.

### 9.4 India's principal soil groups

⚠️ Indian school geography commonly uses the broad groups below, while ICAR-NBSS&LUP maps soils
through modern survey/taxonomic units at several scales. Historical seven-group and eight-group lists
are not rival universal taxonomies; state their source, date and purpose. Learn the broad groups as
**parent material + climate/relief/time -> properties -> management consequence**, not as one-to-one
products of rock alone.

| Soil | Origin and distribution | Key properties | Crop / management consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Alluvial** | River and coastal deposits; northern plains, valleys and deltas | Highly variable; khadar is younger/flood-renewed, bhangar older and may contain kankar; fertility is not universally ranked | Supports intensive agriculture; some command areas face waterlogging/salinity |
| ⚠️ **Black / regur** | Strongly associated with Deccan basalt, but properties vary with parent material and landscape | Smectitic clay commonly swells wet and cracks dry; moisture retention is high | Cotton association reflects moisture/structure, not universal chemical richness |
| ⚠️ **Red and yellow** | Crystalline igneous and metamorphic rocks of the Peninsula under moderate rainfall | Red from iron oxide; yellow where hydrated; generally light-textured and low in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus | Responds strongly to fertiliser and irrigation; supports millets, pulses and oilseeds without them |
| ⚠️ **Laterite/lateritic** | Strong tropical weathering and leaching on suitable surfaces | Fe-Al enrichment; some horizons harden on exposure/drying | Highly variable; management and local profile determine use |
| ⚠️ **Arid / desert** | Aeolian, alluvial and weathering materials in the dry north-west | Sandy to loamy, low organic matter, locally calcareous/saline | Supports pastoral and dryland uses; irrigation can raise salinity risk without drainage |
| ⚠️ **Saline / sodic** | Natural salts and/or irrigation-drainage imbalance | Salinity = soluble salts; sodicity = exchangeable sodium and structural dispersion | Drainage/leaching address salinity; amendments such as gypsum are for suitable sodic soils |
| ⚠️ **Peaty and marshy** | Waterlogged, high-organic settings in humid deltas and backwaters | Very high organic content; acidic; anaerobic | Special-purpose wetland cultivation; drainage alters it fundamentally |
| ⚠️ **Forest and mountain** | Hill slopes under forest cover | Thin, immature, texture varies sharply with altitude and slope | Highly erosion-prone once cleared; suited to horticulture and plantations on terraces |

> 🔑 **Trap:** the 2021 objective demand tests the strong black-soil–volcanic-rock association.
> Teach that association independently without claiming that every black soil has identical chemistry
> or that basalt alone determines the soil.

> 🔑 **Trap:** Francis Buchanan described and named laterite in his **1807** Malabar journey, not
> 1810. Strong leaching is central; seasonal drying can promote hardening, but a compulsory
> wet-and-dry alternation is not the universal definition.

### 9.5 Soil degradation: the applied half

| Degradation type | Mechanism | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ Sheet, rill and gully erosion | Raindrop impact and runoff on unprotected slopes | Ravine lands of the Chambal-Yamuna tract; deforested hill slopes |
| ⚠️ Wind erosion | Deflation of dry, bare, fine material | The arid north-west |
| ⚠️ Salinisation and waterlogging | Irrigation without drainage; canal seepage raising the water table | Canal command areas |
| ⚠️ Nutrient mining and organic-matter decline | Continuous intensive cropping without balanced replenishment | Intensively cropped irrigated belts |
| ⚠️ Acidification | Leaching in high-rainfall areas | Humid eastern and north-eastern uplands |
| ⚠️ Sealing and contamination | Urban expansion, mining spoil, industrial effluent | Peri-urban and mining districts |

- ⚠️ **Conservation logic:** contour bunding and terracing reduce slope length and runoff velocity;
  shelterbelts reduce wind velocity; gully plugging and check dams arrest headward erosion;
  organic-matter restoration and balanced nutrient use rebuild structure; drainage plus gypsum
  reclaims sodic land. The organising principle is **reduce the erosive energy, restore the
  protective cover, and close the nutrient cycle**.

> ⚠️ **Factual caution:** do **not** quote a national percentage of degraded land, a soil-loss rate
> in tonnes per hectare, an ICAR survey figure or a soil-organic-carbon value from memory. The
> classification, process and consequence are the marks-bearing content here; institutional
> desertification and land-degradation targets belong to `Environment-and-Ecology`.

### 10.2 Reusable 15-mark spine — groundwater decline and food security

1. **Thesis:** the Gangetic groundwater decline is not a water problem that later became an
   agricultural problem; it is the *direct hydrological expression* of a cropping pattern,
   an energy-pricing regime and a procurement geography.
2. **The physical system:** an exceptionally productive alluvial aquifer, recharged by monsoon
   rainfall and river seepage, with abstraction concentrated in the dry season.
3. **The imbalance:** withdrawal by dense tube-well irrigation exceeding recharge; falling water
   tables; rising pumping depth and energy cost; declining well yields; in coastal analogues,
   quality failure through intrusion (Section 7 of this file).
4. **Transmission to food security:** the belt is a national foodgrain surplus region, so a regional
   water constraint becomes a **national availability** question; the burden falls first on
   smallholders who cannot deepen wells, so it is a **distributional** question before it is an
   aggregate one.
5. **Balance — the counterpoint that earns marks:** groundwater irrigation is also what made the
   region's productivity and drought resilience possible. The answer must not read as an argument
   against tube wells; the failure is of *governance of the commons*, energy pricing and crop
   choice, not of irrigation itself.
6. **Response logic, matched to mechanism:** demand side — crop diversification away from
   water-intensive cereals in low-recharge tracts, micro-irrigation, energy-pricing reform;
   supply side — managed aquifer recharge, canal-groundwater conjunctive use, wetland and pond
   restoration; institutional — participatory aquifer-level management, since an aquifer is a
   common-pool resource whose boundary does not match any administrative one.
7. **Conclusion:** graded — the aquifer can be stabilised, but only if the policy instruments act on
   the demand drivers rather than on the symptom.

### 10.3 Reusable 10-mark spine — soil as the hinge between physical and economic geography

Parent material and climate -> pedogenic direction (leaching, calcification, humification) ->
soil property (texture, moisture retention, nutrient status) -> crop and settlement pattern ->
degradation risk -> conservation response. Then qualify: soil sets probabilities, not destiny —
irrigation, fertiliser and terracing repeatedly override the "natural" suitability, at a cost.

### 10.4 Evidence units available in this file

> **Claim:** parent rock can dominate soil character even against the climatic trend.
> **Evidence:** the Deccan basalt weathers to a clay-rich black soil that retains moisture and
> self-mulches by cracking, sustaining rain-fed cotton in a semi-arid belt. **Significance:** it
> shows that a single lithological accident can create a national crop region. **Limitation:** the
> broad group is variable; many profiles are nitrogen/organic-matter poor and have a narrow workable
> moisture window, so its
> advantage is specific rather than general.

> **Claim:** luxuriant vegetation is not evidence of fertile soil. **Evidence:** equatorial
> rainforest stands on heavily leached, nutrient-poor soils, with the nutrient capital held in the
> biomass and cycled rapidly. **Significance:** it explains long fallows in shifting cultivation and
> the rapid yield collapse after clearance. **Limitation:** volcanic and alluvial tropical soils are
> genuine exceptions, so the generalisation must be stated as a leaching relationship, not as a
> blanket rule about the tropics.

> **Claim:** irrigation can degrade the very land it makes productive. **Evidence:** canal command
> areas without adequate drainage develop waterlogging and surface salt accumulation through
> capillary rise. **Significance:** it reframes salinity as an engineering-and-governance failure
> rather than only a natural hazard. **Limitation:** diagnosis must distinguish salinity from sodicity;
> drainage, leaching and amendments are site-specific, and implementation/collective action matter.
