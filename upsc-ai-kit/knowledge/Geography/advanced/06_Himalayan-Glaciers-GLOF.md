## 1. Himalayan glacier geography and terminology

⚠️ Himalayan glacier response is heterogeneous across monsoon- and westerly-influenced sectors,
altitude/aspect, hypsometry, debris cover, glacier geometry and thermal/dynamic regime. “Himalayan” is
not one mass-balance or retreat rate.

- Most large glaciers within India's current inventory occur in the Greater and Trans-Himalayan sectors,
  including Ladakh/Karakoram and Zanskar, with important glacierised basins in Himachal Pradesh,
  Uttarakhand, Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh.
- The GSI *Inventory of Himalayan Glaciers*, SP-34 (2009), cited in Lok Sabha UQ 3249 on
  **20 March 2025**, records **9,575 glaciers covering 37,465 km²** within its stated national boundary.
  This is an inventory of record, not a 2025 measurement or a decline from the book-era arc-wide
  “about 15,000” estimate.
- The annexure combines Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh under the inventory's boundary/vintage. State/UT
  reorganisation does not create a new glacier trend.
- Counts depend on boundary, minimum size, mapping method and year; area, volume/mass, snow cover and
  lake inventories are different quantities.

### ELA, climatic snowline, firn line and snout

- **ELA:** glacier-specific annual-balance zero altitude.
- **Firn line:** end-of-season visible firn/bare-ice boundary; a proxy for ELA only under suitable
  surface conditions.
- **Climatic snowline:** regional climatic concept, varying with accumulation and ablation controls.
- **Snout:** geometric glacier terminus, often far below ELA/snowline.

⚠️ There is no single fixed Himalayan snowline or universal north-/south-facing rule. Latitude,
temperature, monsoon/westerly precipitation and its phase, aspect/solar radiation, wind redistribution,
avalanching, slope and topography interact. The two old textbook aspect statements are contradictory
because the balance between accumulation and ablation is sector-specific; do not leave the issue as a
50:50 unresolved factual choice.

## 2. Current glacier-change evidence

📰 **MoES PIB PRID 2150828, 31 July 2025** reports:

- accelerated **heterogeneous** Himalayan glacier mass loss;
- mean snout-change statistics with large spreads: HKH **14.9 ± 15.1 m/a**; Indus
  **12.7 ± 13.2**, Ganga **15.5 ± 14.4**, Brahmaputra **20.2 ± 19.7 m/a**; Karakoram
  **−1.37 ± 22.8 m/a** described as comparatively minor length change;
- cumulative Indian Himalayan glacier mass loss about **−26 m w.e. (1975–2023)**;
- upper Chandra mean annual mass balance **−0.51 ± 0.28 m w.e. a⁻¹**, cumulative
  **−3.54 m w.e. (2015–2022)**—the negative signs are present in the official release;
- Chandra glacier/lake monitoring by NCPOR and Himansh station.

https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2150828

⚠️ Snout rate, area change and mass balance are not interchangeable. Large overlapping uncertainties
mean basin point estimates should not be ranked as cleanly separated “fastest/slowest” populations.
Surging or stable/advancing termini do not disprove regional loss; surge mechanisms are internal/dynamic
and varied, not one universal subglacial-drainage switch.

## 4. Water-resource interpretation

Glaciers store precipitation and modify seasonal/interannual runoff, but basin effects depend on monsoon
and westerly precipitation, seasonal snow, groundwater, glacier cover/hypsometry, reservoirs and demand.

- For many Ganga/Brahmaputra reaches, monsoon rainfall dominates annual flow; snow/ice contribution may
  matter more during selected seasons or drought years.
- Upper Indus/Trans-Himalayan basins can have greater relative dependence on snow/ice because monsoon
  penetration is weaker, but quantify shares only from dated basin studies.
- “Peak water” is a glacier/basin/model-specific transition, not a universal curve or dated Indian
  forecast. Increased melt runoff can precede later decline, but rainfall and storage can mask it.
- Do not claim melt is always negatively correlated with rainfall or that Himalayan rivers will dry up.

## 5. Glacial lakes and GLOF risk

📰 **NRSC Glacial Lake Atlas (2023):** **28,043** lakes/water bodies ≥0.25 ha in Indian Himalayan
river basins, of which **7,570** are within India and **20,473** are transboundary under the atlas
boundary. This does not mean every transboundary lake can flood India or that every mapped lake is
hazardous.

https://www.nrsc.gov.in/nrscnew/assets/pdf/atlas/glaciallake/IHR_GlacialLake_Atlas.pdf

📰 **CWC August 2025 report:** monitoring cohort of **2,843** lakes/water bodies above 10 ha. The
change 477 → 902 → 2,843 is expansion of surveillance cohorts, not lake proliferation.

https://cwc.gov.in/sites/default/files/Monitoring_Report_Aug_2025.pdf

⚠️ Screen risk through lake volume/depth and hydrological connection, dam/outlet material and geometry,
freeboard/seepage, glacier/permafrost and upstream slope stability, displacement-wave potential, valley
routing, downstream exposure and warning/vulnerability. Dam type alone cannot rank risk; bedrock dams
are not absolutely fail-proof and moraine dams are not uniformly unstable.

## 8. UPSC traps and Mains angles

- ❌ Snowline = snout or ELA. → Distinguish climatic, surface, balance and geometric observations.
- ❌ Fastest point-estimate retreat basin = most water-vulnerable. → Uncertainties overlap; dependence is
  basin/season-specific.
- ❌ Every GLOF is a moraine breach caused by warming/earthquake/rain. → Identify lake/dam, immediate
  trigger, failure/release mechanism and cascade.
- ❌ Lake count = dangerous-lake count. → Inventory, monitoring, hazard and downstream risk differ.
- ❌ EWS announced = EWS operational = event predicted. → Keep implementation and capability distinct.

### Mains use

- **2020 GS-I:** explain glacier loss through measured quantity, mass balance/lag, basin-season
  hydrology, glacial-lake/slope/sediment pathways and qualified current evidence.
- **GLOF:** connect glacier/permafrost/lake change to dam/outlet condition, immediate trigger,
  displacement/overtopping/breach, sediment cascade and downstream exposure; then cite current Indian
  inventory, monitoring and mitigation architecture.
