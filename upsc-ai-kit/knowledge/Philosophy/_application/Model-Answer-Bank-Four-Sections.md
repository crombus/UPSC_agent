## 1. PAPER I, SECTION A - WESTERN PHILOSOPHY

### Question

**How does Kant respond to Hume's scepticism with regard to a priori judgments? Discuss.**
(2024, 10 marks)

### Demand map

| Element | Required move |
|---|---|
| Hume's scepticism | Explain the denial of rationally known necessary connections |
| Kant's response | Show how synthetic a priori cognition is possible |
| Discuss | State achievement and cost/limit |

### Model answer

Hume's empiricism divides propositions into relations of ideas and matters of fact. Relations
of ideas are necessary but merely analytic; matters of fact are informative but contingent.
Causal necessity, therefore, cannot be perceived: repeated conjunction produces only a habit
of expectation. This scepticism threatens the necessity claimed by mathematics and natural
science. ✅

Kant accepts Hume's point that necessity cannot be derived from experience, but reverses its
conclusion. Experience itself is possible only because the mind supplies **a priori forms and
concepts**. Space and time are forms of intuition, while categories such as causality organize
the manifold of sensation. Thus the judgment that every event has a cause is not extracted
from repeated observation; it is a **synthetic a priori condition of objective experience**.
The mind does not merely copy an already ordered world: appearances conform to the cognitive
conditions under which they can be experienced. ✅

Kant therefore rescues necessity without returning to dogmatic rationalism. Yet the rescue has
a boundary: categories are valid only for **phenomena**, not things-in-themselves. ⚠️ His reply
defeats scepticism about experienced nature by limiting metaphysical knowledge beyond
experience.

### Why it scores

- Hume is stated before Kant is introduced.
- The argument moves from the impossibility of deriving necessity to conditions of experience.
- The conclusion gives both Kant's achievement and the phenomenal limit.

## 2. PAPER I, SECTION B - INDIAN PHILOSOPHY

### Question

**Is Buddhist Nirvana consistent with Ksanikavada and Nairatmyavada? Critically discuss.**
(2025, 15 marks)

### Demand map

| Term | Required move |
|---|---|
| Kṣaṇikavāda | All conditioned entities are momentary |
| Nairātmyavāda | No permanent substantial self |
| Nirvāṇa | Cessation of craving, suffering and conditioned becoming |
| Critically discuss | Explain continuity without substance and test objections |

### Model answer

The apparent difficulty is sharp: if every conditioned state is momentary and no enduring self
exists, who is bound, who practises, and who attains nirvāṇa? Buddhism answers by rejecting the
assumption that moral and causal continuity requires a permanent substance. ✅

According to **kṣaṇikavāda**, each conditioned event arises and perishes in a causal series.
According to **nairātmyavāda**, the person is not an ātman behind that series but the dependently
arisen five aggregates. Continuity is therefore neither strict identity nor total
disconnection: one moment conditions its successor, as one flame lights another. Karma belongs
to this causal stream, not to an unchanging owner. ✅

Nirvāṇa is consistent with these doctrines because it is not the eternal enjoyment of a
substantial soul. It is the cessation of ignorance, craving and the dependent chain that
produces suffering. The stream conventionally called a person undergoes transformation until
the causes of renewed becoming cease. ✅

The objection is that a merely momentary stream cannot sustain responsibility or liberation:
the agent and the recipient of the fruit are numerically different. The Buddhist reply is that
causal dependence, not numerical identity, grounds responsibility; complete difference would
break causation, while permanent identity would make change and liberation impossible. ⚠️

Thus nirvāṇa is not an exception to momentariness and no-self. It is their soteriological
completion: because the person is dependently constituted rather than eternally fixed, the
causes of suffering can be removed.

## 3. PAPER II, SECTION A - SOCIO-POLITICAL PHILOSOPHY

### Question

**What is meant by justice as fairness? Explain Rawls' theory of justice.**
(2023, 10 marks)

### Demand map

| Element | Required move |
|---|---|
| Justice as fairness | Fair terms chosen under fair conditions |
| Rawls' device | Original position and veil of ignorance |
| Principles | Equal liberties; fair opportunity and difference principle |

### Model answer

Rawls calls his conception **justice as fairness** because principles regulating the basic
structure of society are those free and rational persons would choose under conditions that
prevent bargaining advantage. ✅

The thought experiment is the **original position**. Its parties choose principles behind a
**veil of ignorance**: they do not know their class, caste, gender, talents, religion or
conception of the good. They know general facts about society but cannot tailor rules to their
own future position. This models impartiality rather than historical consent. ✅

Rawls argues that the parties would choose two principles. First, each person must have an
equal scheme of basic liberties compatible with the same scheme for all. Second, social and
economic inequalities are permissible only when offices are open under fair equality of
opportunity and the inequalities work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged - the
**difference principle**. The first principle has priority over distributive gain. ✅

The theory improves on utilitarianism by refusing to sacrifice the basic claims of some for a
larger aggregate welfare. Critics such as Nozick object that patterned redistribution violates
entitlements, while communitarians question the abstract, unencumbered chooser. ⚠️ Yet Rawls'
enduring contribution is to connect liberty and equality through a publicly justifiable
procedure centred on the worst placed.

## 4. PAPER II, SECTION B - PHILOSOPHY OF RELIGION

### Question

**Distinguish between cognitivist and non-cognitivist account of religious language. Does
the cognitivist account lead to any contradiction? Answer with reference to the philosophical
views of R. B. Braithwaite.** (2024, 20 marks)

### Demand map

| Part | Required move |
|---|---|
| Distinguish | Compare truth-aptness, function and verification |
| Contradiction/problem | Explain pressure on factual God-talk |
| Braithwaite | Moral intention, stories and way of life |
| Evaluate | State what his account saves and loses |

### Model answer

A **cognitivist** account treats religious utterances as assertions capable of truth or
falsity. “God is loving” purports to describe reality, even if its verification differs from
that of an ordinary empirical claim. Classical theism, Aquinas' analogy and many realist
theologies are cognitivist in this sense. A **non-cognitivist** account denies that the primary
function of such utterances is factual description. It interprets them through attitude,
commitment, practice, symbol or a form of life. ✅

The cognitivist faces several pressures. Literal predicates can anthropomorphize an infinite
God; wholly equivocal predicates communicate nothing. Verificationists add that no observable
condition conclusively verifies or falsifies “God exists”. Attributes such as omnipotence and
perfect goodness also generate apparent paradoxes when stated without qualification. These
are problems for particular accounts of religious predication, not a demonstration that every
cognitivist claim is formally contradictory. Analogy, eschatological verification and
qualified conceptions of divine attributes are cognitivist replies. ⚠️

R. B. Braithwaite offers a non-cognitivist alternative. A religious assertion primarily
expresses an intention to follow a moral policy - paradigmatically, an agapeistic way of life.
Religious stories sustain that commitment without needing to function as empirically
verifiable histories. Thus “God is love” declares and reinforces a form of conduct rather than
reporting an observable divine property. ✅

Braithwaite explains why religious language can guide life even when theoretical proof is
unavailable. He also avoids making faith compete directly with natural science. However, his
reduction is too severe. Believers ordinarily intend more than a moral resolution: they claim
that God, liberation or ultimate reality is real. Different religions can share moral policies
while contradicting one another about reality. His account therefore preserves practical force
at the cost of ontological reference. ⚠️

The best verdict is layered: non-cognitivism captures the performative and ethical use of
religious language, but cannot exhaust its self-understood truth-claim. Cognitivism need not be
contradictory if it rejects crude literalism and explains how its predicates apply.

### 5.1 Western — Husserl, 2021, 20 marks

**Present a critical exposition of Husserl’s criticism of ‘natural attitude’. How does Husserl
propose to address the problems involved in natural attitude through his phenomenological
method?**

```text
Frame  : natural attitude = unexamined existential posit of a ready-made world.
Expose : objectivism/naturalism and unnoticed constituting acts.
Method : epoché → phenomenological reduction → intentional correlation → eidetic analysis.
Critic : solipsism/idealism and the question of intersubjectivity.
Reply  : suspension is methodological, not denial; constitution explains sense, not creation.
Verdict: gains reflexive rigour, but the transcendental turn bears a heavy justificatory burden.
```

Discuss.**

```text
Frame  : Nyāya infers Īśvara; it does not claim direct perception.
Kāryāt : composite world as effect → intelligent maker.
Āyojanāt: initial conjunction/ordering of atoms requires intelligence.

Śruteḥ  : Vedic testimony requires an omniscient source in the later Nyāya construction.
Critic  : analogy, regress, atomism without God, Buddhist/Mīmāṃsā objections.
Verdict : cumulative explanatory case is stronger than any isolated inference.
```

### 5.5 Western — rationalist substance, 2018, 20 marks

**What is the reason for the difference in the definitions and classifications of substances
made by Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz in spite of the fact that they all belonged to the
rationalist school of thought? Discuss.**

```text
Frame  : common rationalist demand for an intelligible, self-grounding ultimate.
Descartes: strict substance = God; created mind/extension admitted through real distinction.
Spinoza : applies self-conception strictly → one infinite substance, attributes and modes.
Leibniz : extension is composite → true substances are simple, active, windowless monads.
Cause   : each repairs a tension in the predecessor—dependence, interaction, individuality.
Critic  : shared term may mask incompatible criteria of independence and simplicity.
Verdict : one rationalist method produces 3/1/∞ because its metaphysical constraints differ.
```

### 5.6 Indian — Sāṃkhya causation, 2020, 20 marks

**“A mango tree is grown out of a mango seed.” How will Sāṃkhya system explain this process
through their theory of causation by rejecting their rival perspectives?**

```text
Frame  : satkāryavāda—effect pre-exists latently in its material cause.
Argument: non-being cannot be produced; determinate effects require determinate material;
          production needs material capacity; cause/effect share continuity.
Apply   : seed possesses the specific causal potency; tree is manifestation/transformation,
          not a gross miniature hidden spatially inside it.
Rivals  : Nyāya ārambhavāda/new production; Advaita vivarta/apparent transformation;
          Buddhist dependent event-series without enduring Prakṛti.
Critic  : “pre-existence” risks becoming unfalsifiable; explain latent potency precisely.
Verdict : Sāṃkhya best explains material continuity, but not necessarily conscious teleology.
```

### 6.1 PAPER I, SECTION A — WESTERN · Quine, radical translation (20 marks)

#### Question

**"Indeterminacy of translation is not a claim about our ignorance but about the world."
Examine Quine's argument from radical translation and assess whether it strengthens his
critique of empiricism.** *(Original practice question — 20 marks)*

#### Demand map

| Element | Required move | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| "Not ignorance but the world" | Show the thesis is **ontological**, not epistemic | Writing "we cannot be sure of the translation" |
| Argument from radical translation | Reconstruct the *gavagai* steps, including the identity-predicate move | Naming *gavagai* and stopping |
| "Strengthens the critique of empiricism" | Connect it to the **first dogma** — the failure of synonymy/analyticity | Treating Two Dogmas and *Word and Object* as unrelated |
| Examine | Give the strongest objection (Chomsky), a reply, and a graded verdict | Ending with praise |

#### Model answer

Quine's critique of empiricism has a destructive and a constructive half. "Two Dogmas of
Empiricism" argues that analyticity cannot be defined without circling through synonymy,
definition and semantical rules, each of which presupposes the notion being explained. That is a
negative result: it shows only that no one has *given* a criterion. Radical translation supplies
the positive diagnosis — there is nothing for such a criterion to track. ✅

The thought experiment is disciplined. A field linguist must translate a wholly unrelated
language with no bilingual informant and no dictionary. His evidence is exhausted by observable
behaviour: what utterances the native assents to and dissents from under what publicly
observable stimulations. A rabbit runs past; the native says "**Gavagai**." The linguist records
assent under rabbit-stimulation and tentatively writes "Rabbit." ✅

The difficulty is that "undetached rabbit parts", "rabbit stage" and "instantiation of
rabbithood" are confirmed by *exactly the same* stimulations. Quine's decisive move is to block
the obvious escape: the linguist cannot simply ask "Is this gavagai the same as that one?",
because that question already requires him to have translated the **identity predicate** and the
apparatus of individuation — plurals, articles, quantifiers — which is precisely what is in
dispute. Any residual discrepancy can be absorbed by compensating adjustments elsewhere in the
manual: a different reading of "gavagai" offset by different **analytical hypotheses** about the
particles. ✅

Hence two manuals may be incompatible with each other yet each compatible with the totality of
possible behavioural evidence. Quine's conclusion is not that we cannot *tell* which is right.
It is that there is **no fact of the matter** to be right or wrong about — meanings are not
entities that translation discovers. This is why the thesis reads as a claim about the world
rather than about our limits. ✅

The bearing on empiricism is direct. Classical empiricism assumed a two-part story: sensory
evidence on one side, meanings on the other, with each statement possessing its own meaning and
its own confirmation conditions. If synonymy has no behavioural anchor, the second half of that
story collapses, and with it the analytic/synthetic partition and the reductionist dogma. What
survives is holism: our beliefs face the tribunal of experience as a corporate body, and
sentences are revisable anywhere in the web. ⚠️

The strongest objection is **Chomsky's**: indeterminacy is nothing but the ordinary
underdetermination of theory by evidence, which afflicts physics equally. If Quine remains a
realist about physical theory despite underdetermination, he is inconsistent in being an
eliminativist about meaning. Quine's reply is that the two cases differ in kind: in physics the
rival theories are answerable to a further physical fact even if we cannot reach it, whereas in
translation the behavioural evidence *is* all the fact there is, because meaning was never
posited as anything beyond dispositions to verbal behaviour. ⚠️ **Davidson** concedes
indeterminacy but confines it, arguing that charity plus a truth-theory yields far more
determinacy than Quine allows. ⚠️

The verdict should be graded. The argument's force is entirely proportional to its behaviourist
premise: grant that all evidence for meaning is dispositional evidence, and the conclusion is
close to inescapable; deny that premise, and the argument becomes a strong scepticism rather
than an ontological result. What is not negotiable is the achievement. Quine converted a
suspicion about analyticity into a structural claim about what semantic facts could be, and in
doing so made empiricism defend not merely its criterion of meaning but its very conception of
meaning. ⚠️

### 6.2 PAPER I, SECTION B — INDIAN · Yoga, the samādhi distinction (15 marks)

#### Question

**Distinguish *samprajñāta* from *asamprajñāta samādhi*. Is *asamprajñāta samādhi* identical
with *kaivalya*? Discuss.** *(Original practice question — 15 marks)*

#### Demand map

| Element | Required move | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Distinguish | Use **content** and **residue** as the two axes, not "lower/higher" | Calling one "deep concentration" and the other "deeper" |
| Identity question | Answer **no**, and say precisely what still blocks liberation | Treating asamprajñāta as liberation |
| Discuss | Connect to *cittavṛtti-nirodha*, *saṃskāra* and *dharmamegha* | Listing the eight limbs instead |

#### Model answer

Yoga defines itself as *yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ* — the restriction of the modifications of the
mind-field. *Samādhi* is the eighth limb, in which that restriction becomes stable rather than
episodic. The syllabus distinction concerns **how much** has been restricted. ✅

**Samprajñāta samādhi** is *with* cognition. The mind is absorbed in an object and retains
conceptual support; the classical account tracks a graded refinement of supports — gross
deliberation, subtle reflection, joy, and the mere sense of "I am" — each stage dropping a
coarser prop while remaining object-directed. It is therefore still a *vṛtti*, though an
extraordinarily purified one, and its function is instrumental: it matures **vivekakhyāti**,
discriminative discernment between puruṣa and prakṛti. At its summit arises *ṛtambharā prajñā*,
truth-bearing insight, which is the hinge to the next stage. ✅

**Asamprajñāta samādhi** is *without* cognition. Every *vṛtti*, including the discriminative one,
has ceased. But cessation of modifications is not the same as destruction of their **latent
deposits**: *saṃskāras* remain, and they retain the capacity to generate mental activity again
when conditions permit. The distinction is therefore not merely intensity of concentration but a
difference in what is left standing — object plus latency in the first case, latency alone in the
second. ✅

This settles the second question. *Asamprajñāta samādhi* is **not** identical with *kaivalya*.
Kaivalya is the isolation of puruṣa, the final and irreversible discontinuance of prakṛti's
purpose for that puruṣa. Asamprajñāta is a state the yogin enters and from which, so long as
seeds remain, he can fall back. What exhausts the residue is *dharmamegha samādhi*, in which even
the highest discriminative attainment is no longer desired; the seeds are burnt, karma ceases to
accumulate, and kaivalya follows. ✅

The natural objection is that this multiplies stages without explanatory gain: if all *vṛttis*
have ceased, what work is the further "cloud of dharma" doing? The Yoga reply is precise. The
system holds that bondage is caused not by conscious mental events alone but by *saṃskāra* and
the afflictions (*kleśas*) in latent form; a doctrine that liberated the yogin at the mere
cessation of active cognition would leave the mechanism of rebirth untouched and would make
relapse inexplicable. The extra stage is thus demanded by the theory of bondage, not added for
piety. ⚠️

A second objection cuts deeper: a contentless state is by definition unreportable, so its
existence rests on testimony rather than argument. The defensible reply concedes the epistemic
point and relocates the claim — asamprajñāta is posited as the necessary condition of a result
that *is* observable in conduct, namely the permanent absence of affliction-driven action. ⚠️

Verdict: the distinction is not one of degree along a single scale of absorption but of kind
along two axes — presence of object-content, and presence of latent residue. Yoga's real
achievement is to have kept these apart, so that liberation is defined by the exhaustion of
latency rather than by the vividness of any experience. ⚠️

### 6.3 PAPER II, SECTION A — SOCIO-POLITICAL · Punishment (20 marks)

#### Question

**"Punishment should address the offender as a citizen, not merely process him as a danger."
Examine the communicative and restorative accounts of punishment, and assess whether they can
answer the retributivist's demand for public censure.** *(Original practice question — 20 marks)*

#### Demand map

| Element | Required move | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| "As a citizen" | Identify the **justifying question** each theory answers | Listing theories in sequence |
| Communicative account | State Duff's structure: censure addressed *to* the offender, seeking repentance | Calling it "reformative" |
| Restorative account | State repair, dialogue, and Braithwaite's reintegrative shaming | Calling it "letting the offender off" |
| Assess against retributivism | Face the **denunciation** objection head-on | Concluding that "a balance is needed" |

#### Model answer

Theories of punishment answer different questions, and most confusion in this area comes from
running them together. The **general justifying aim** asks why the institution should exist at
all; **distribution** asks who may be punished and how much; **mode** asks what punishment should
consist in. Retributivism answers the first by desert and constrains the second by
proportionality. Deterrence answers the first by consequences but notoriously under-constrains
the second. The communicative and restorative families intervene chiefly at the level of aim and
mode. ✅

The **communicative** account, associated with Antony Duff, treats punishment as a form of moral
address. The offender is not a hazard to be neutralised but a member of a political community
who has wronged fellow members; the sentence therefore *communicates* censure to him, and invites
him to recognise the wrong, repent and repair. Hard treatment is not incidental: a penance that
cost nothing would not focus attention or express the community's seriousness. On this view
punishment is a secular penance imposed by a community that is itself answerable for the
justice of its institutions. ✅

The **restorative** account shifts the centre of gravity from the offender-state dyad to the
triangle of offender, victim and community. Its characteristic practices — conferencing,
mediation, reparation agreements — aim at repairing the relationships that the offence damaged.
John Braithwaite's **reintegrative shaming** supplies its most careful mechanism: shaming that
condemns the *act* while sustaining the offender's bond with the community reduces reoffending,
whereas *disintegrative* stigma expels him and pushes him toward criminal subcultures. ✅

Both accounts answer the question set. The offender is addressed as a bearer of moral standing
whose response matters, rather than as an object of risk management. Both also explain something
retributivism struggles with: why the *manner* of punishment, and not only its quantum, is
morally significant. ⚠️

The retributivist's objection is nonetheless serious. Public denunciation is owed to the victim
and to the community independently of whether the offender repents or the parties reconcile; a
process that turns on the victim's willingness to participate risks making the gravity of a wrong
depend on contingencies — the victim's temperament, the offender's articulacy, the community's
cohesion. Worse, restorative settlements can vary wildly between similar cases, offending
against the equal treatment that public justice requires. ⚠️

The best reply distinguishes the two accounts here. Duff's communicative view is **not**
anti-retributive: censure is deserved, proportionality still caps the sentence, and the offender's
refusal to repent does not invalidate the punishment, since the address was owed whether or not it
is accepted. Restorative practice, in its strongest form, likewise does not abolish condemnation;
it insists that condemnation be delivered in a form that leaves reintegration possible, and it
accepts that grave offences and unwilling participants require the ordinary court. What it
refuses is the identification of censure with vengeance. ⚠️

The residual difficulty should be conceded rather than smoothed over. Communication presupposes
an interlocutor who is minimally capable of moral response, and a community with the standing to
address him; where the state itself has denied a group equal membership, the moral premise of the
address is weak. That is an argument for reforming the political relationship, not for reverting
to bare desert. ⚠️

Verdict: the communicative and restorative accounts can meet the demand for public censure,
provided they retain proportionality as a constraint and treat reconciliation as an aim rather
than a condition of legitimate punishment. Their real contribution is to make the *mode* of
punishment answerable to the standing of the person punished — which is precisely what a theory
built only on desert or only on deterrence leaves undetermined. ⚠️

### 6.4 PAPER II, SECTION B — PHILOSOPHY OF RELIGION · Religious experience (15 marks)

#### Question

**Compare Stace's and Zaehner's classifications of mystical experience. Does either
classification settle the question of the object of religious experience?**
*(Original practice question — 15 marks)*

#### Demand map

#### Model answer

Religious experience raises two separable questions: what is its *nature*, and what is its
*object*. Typologies of mysticism belong to the first. Whether they can decide the second is
exactly what the question tests. ✅

**W. T. Stace** (*Mysticism and Philosophy*, 1960) offers a **two-fold** classification.
*Extrovertive* mysticism perceives a unity in and through the multiplicity of the senses — the
one shining through the many. *Introvertive* mysticism excludes all sensory and conceptual
content, leaving a unitary pure consciousness. His methodologically decisive move is a further
distinction: between the **experience itself** and the **interpretation** the mystic places on
it. Stripping away doctrinal interpretation, he claims, reveals a **common core** across
traditions — which is why a Christian, an Advaitin and a Buddhist can be said to report the same
thing differently. ✅

**R. C. Zaehner** (*Mysticism Sacred and Profane*) rejects that convergence. He distinguishes
**three** types — nature mysticism, monistic mysticism, and theistic mysticism — and denies a
common core. On his account the union with a personal God who remains other than the soul is
categorically different from an experience of undifferentiated oneness; treating them as one
phenomenon differently described is not neutrality but a substantive metaphysical decision. ✅

The comparison therefore turns on two axes. **Number of types:** two versus three. **Common
core:** asserted versus denied. Everything else follows. Stace buys cross-cultural unity at the
price of assigning all difference to interpretation. Zaehner preserves the traditions'
self-understanding at the price of a typology that is theologically loaded — his ranking of
theistic experience above monistic is not derived from the phenomenology. ⚠️

The strongest objection to Stace is that "interpretation" does all the work: if enough is
subtracted as interpretation, any two experiences can be made to coincide, and the common core
becomes an artefact of the method rather than a finding. **Katz**-style constructivism presses
further — the mystic's tradition shapes the experience itself, not merely its report, so there is
no uninterpreted experience to recover. Stace's available reply is that some description must be
possible or comparative study collapses entirely, and that his core is minimal rather than
doctrinally rich. ⚠️

The second question must be answered in the negative. A classification is a description of
*explananda*; it cannot by itself establish that anything answers to them. Even a perfectly
agreed typology leaves open whether the object is a personal God, an impersonal absolute, or a
naturalistically explicable state — and the disagreement between Stace and Zaehner is itself
evidence that the phenomenology under-determines the ontology. This is why the debate migrates to
epistemology: **Alston's** perceptual model, which assesses mystical experience as a doxastic
practice, and the standard tests of coherence, testimony, moral fruits and defeater-response. ⚠️

Verdict: Stace and Zaehner are not two answers to one question but two decisions about how much
difference the description should be allowed to absorb. Neither settles the object of religious
experience, and the honest conclusion is that classification prepares the epistemological
question rather than replacing it. ⚠️
