# MASTER PHILOSOPHY DEFINITIONS GLOSSARY

> **Scope:** exam-safe definitions for the complete UPSC Philosophy Optional syllabus:
> Western Philosophy, Indian Philosophy, Socio-Political Philosophy and Philosophy of
> Religion. Definitions are starting points, not substitutes for arguments, objections and
> evaluation in the owner files.
>
> **Indian-language companion:** for IAST, Devanāgarī, Pali equivalents and the expanded
> school-wise map, use [Sanskrit-and-Pali-Terms.md](Sanskrit-and-Pali-Terms.md).

## 1. Foundational philosophical vocabulary

| Term | Exam-safe definition | Nearest distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Philosophy | Rational and critical inquiry into reality, knowledge, value, reason and meaning | Unlike mere opinion, philosophy requires arguments and conceptual clarity |
| Metaphysics | Inquiry into the most general structure and kinds of reality | Ontology is the part concerned specifically with what exists |
| Ontology | Study of what exists and the basic categories of being | Epistemology asks how such claims can be known |
| Epistemology | Study of knowledge, justification, belief, truth and the means of cognition | Psychology describes how people think; epistemology evaluates whether cognition is justified |
| Logic | Study of valid inference and the principles distinguishing good reasoning from fallacy | Truth concerns premises/conclusions; validity concerns inferential form |
| Ethics | Philosophical inquiry into right action, good character, value and obligation | Metaethics studies the status/meaning of moral claims |
| Axiology | General study of value, including moral and aesthetic value | Ethics is one branch of axiology |
| Aesthetics | Study of beauty, art, taste and aesthetic experience | Art criticism evaluates particular works |
| Political philosophy | Normative inquiry into state, power, authority, justice, liberty, rights and political obligation | Political science also explains empirical institutions and behaviour |
| Philosophy of religion | Rational study of religious concepts, claims, experience, language and justification | Theology reasons from within a particular revelatory tradition |
| Metaphilosophy | Inquiry into philosophy's nature, method, limits and purpose | It is philosophy about philosophical activity |
| Concept | General unit of thought used to classify, identify or reason about things | A word expresses a concept but is not identical with it |
| Proposition | Truth-apt content asserted or denied by a sentence | Different sentences may express the same proposition |
| Argument | Set of premises offered as reasons for a conclusion | An explanation shows why something occurred; an argument shows why a claim should be accepted |
| Deduction | Inference in which true premises guarantee the conclusion | Induction makes the conclusion probable rather than necessary |
| Induction | Reasoning from observed cases or evidence to a broader probable conclusion | It is ampliative: the conclusion goes beyond the premises |
| Abduction | Inference to the best available explanation | It does not deductively prove that the explanation is true |
| Validity | Property of an argument whereby the conclusion cannot be false if the premises are true | Soundness adds actual truth of the premises |
| Soundness | Deductive validity plus true premises | A valid argument can remain unsound |
| Truth | Property of a proposition's being correct or corresponding to how things are, depending on the theory adopted | Justification concerns reasons for belief, not truth alone |
| Correspondence theory | Truth consists in agreement between proposition and fact/reality | Coherence theory locates truth in systematic consistency |
| Coherence theory | Truth or justification consists in fitting coherently within a system of beliefs | Mere consistency may not ensure contact with reality |
| Pragmatic theory | Truth is understood through successful inquiry, practical consequences or warranted assertibility | It need not mean whatever is immediately useful |
| Knowledge | Traditionally, true belief supported by adequate justification, though Gettier problems challenge sufficiency | True belief without justification is lucky correctness |
| Belief | Propositional attitude of accepting something as true | Belief may be false or unjustified |
| Justification | Epistemic support that makes a belief rationally acceptable | Causal origin alone may not justify a belief |
| A priori | Knowable independently of particular sensory experience | It need not be innate or analytically true |
| A posteriori | Knowable through or dependent upon experience | It is also called empirical knowledge |
| Analytic proposition | Proposition true by meaning or conceptual relations | Synthetic propositions add content not contained merely in meanings |
| Synthetic proposition | Proposition whose truth is not fixed by meanings alone | Kant allows synthetic a priori judgments |
| Necessary truth | Truth that could not have been otherwise | Contingent truth is true but could have been false |
| Contingent truth | Truth dependent on how the world happens to be | It is not logically necessary |
| Universal | Repeatable feature, kind or concept applicable to many particulars | A particular is an individual instance |
| Particular | Individual entity or occurrence | It may instantiate universals or possess properties |
| Substance | Relatively independent bearer of properties or fundamental entity | A property depends on or characterises something |
| Property | Attribute, feature or quality predicated of an entity | Relation connects two or more entities |
| Essence | What makes a thing the kind of thing it is | An accidental property can change without destroying identity |
| Existence | Actual being or instantiation | Essence concerns what a thing is |
| Cause | Factor that produces, explains or is systematically responsible for an effect | Correlation alone does not establish causation |
| Determinism | View that every event follows necessarily from prior conditions and laws | Fatalism says the outcome occurs regardless of intervening causes/actions |
| Fatalism | View that certain outcomes are unavoidable whatever agents do | Determinism can still treat deliberation as causally effective |
| Free will | Capacity for agency or choice sufficient for responsibility | Its exact conditions divide compatibilists and incompatibilists |
| Compatibilism | View that free will can coexist with causal determinism | Libertarianism requires indeterministic freedom |
| Libertarianism | View that genuine free action is incompatible with determinism and humans sometimes possess it | Not the same as political libertarianism |
| Hard determinism | Determinism is true and genuine free will does not exist | Hard incompatibilism may deny freedom whether determinism is true or false |
| Monism | Reality is ultimately of one fundamental kind or principle | Dualism posits two irreducible kinds; pluralism more than two |
| Dualism | Reality contains two irreducible principles, such as mind and matter | Interactionism is one possible relation between them |
| Pluralism | Reality, value or truth involves irreducible multiplicity | It does not imply that every view is equally correct |
| Materialism / physicalism | Reality is fundamentally material or physical, and mental phenomena depend on it | Property dualism may accept one substance but irreducible mental properties |
| Idealism | Mind, consciousness or intelligible structure is fundamental to reality | Subjective idealism differs from absolute idealism |
| Realism | Relevant entities exist independently of our thought, language or experience | Anti-realism makes their status dependent on conceptual or linguistic conditions |
| Naturalism | Reality and knowledge are continuous with nature and natural inquiry, without appeal to supernatural explanation | Methodological naturalism need not entail atheism |
| Rationalism | Reason supplies substantial knowledge not derived solely from sense experience | Empiricism gives experience the foundational role |
| Empiricism | Knowledge or concepts arise primarily from experience | It need not deny all a priori truths |
| Scepticism | Doubt that knowledge or adequate justification is possible in some or all domains | Methodological doubt is a temporary procedure, not necessarily final scepticism |
| Dogmatism | Uncritical or insufficiently examined acceptance of claims | In Kant, dogmatic metaphysics means reason operating without critique |
| Foundationalism | Some justified beliefs are basic and support non-basic beliefs | Coherentism treats mutual support within a system as primary |
| Coherentism | Beliefs are justified through their coherence with the total belief system | It rejects a one-directional basic-belief structure |
| Internalism | Justification depends on factors accessible from the subject's perspective | Externalism permits truth-linked factors not reflectively accessible |
| Externalism | Knowledge/justification may depend on reliable external relations or processes | It is not the same as metaphysical realism |

## 2. God, mind and world: basic positions

| Term | Exam-safe definition | Nearest distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Theism | Belief that a divine reality or God exists; classical theism usually adds personality, transcendence, creation and perfect attributes | Deism denies or minimises ongoing divine intervention |
| Classical theism | View of God as necessary, personal, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good and creator/sustainer of the world | Pantheism identifies God with the whole of reality |
| Monotheism | Belief in one God | Henotheism gives supreme devotion to one deity without necessarily denying others |
| Polytheism | Belief in multiple gods | It need not deny an underlying unity or hierarchy |
| Henotheism | Worship of one deity as supreme without categorical denial of other deities | Monotheism denies multiple gods |
| Deism | Belief in a creator known by reason who does not ordinarily intervene through miracles or revelation | Classical theism affirms providential involvement |
| Pantheism | God and the totality of reality are identical | Panentheism says the world is in God but God exceeds it |
| Panentheism | All things exist in God while God is more than the world | It avoids simple God-world identity |
| Atheism | Denial or absence of belief that God exists | Agnosticism suspends judgment about knowability or evidence |
| Agnosticism | View that God's existence is unknown, unknowable or not adequately established | It is compatible with practical religious commitment or non-belief |
| Transcendence | God's being beyond or not reducible to the world | Immanence is divine presence within the world |
| Immanence | God's presence and activity within reality | It need not eliminate transcendence |
| Mind-body dualism | Mind and body are irreducibly different substances or kinds | Property dualism posits irreducible properties rather than two substances |
| Interactionism | Mind and body causally affect one another | Parallelism denies direct interaction |
| Parallelism | Mental and physical sequences correspond without directly causing each other | Leibniz explains correspondence through pre-established harmony |
| Epiphenomenalism | Physical processes cause mental events, but mental events do not cause physical events | Interactionism grants mental causation |
| Personal identity | Conditions under which a person remains the same through time | Numerical identity differs from mere qualitative similarity |

## 3. Plato and Aristotle

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Plato's Form / Idea | Eternal, intelligible, non-sensible reality that explains the character and knowability of sensible particulars |
| Participation | Relation by which sensible particulars possess or imitate features of Forms |
| Realm of Forms | Intelligible order of stable realities accessible to reason rather than sense |
| Sensible world | Changing world of perceptible particulars that imperfectly instantiate Forms |
| Recollection | Plato's account that learning is recovery of knowledge the soul possessed before embodiment |
| Dialectic | Rational ascent through questioning and division toward knowledge of first principles and the Good |
| Form of the Good | Highest Platonic principle that makes knowledge and being intelligible |
| Allegory of the Cave | Image of education as turning the soul from appearances toward intelligible reality |
| Divided Line | Plato's hierarchy of imagination, belief, mathematical thought and understanding |
| Substance (Aristotle) | Primary being: an individual concrete thing; secondarily, its species or essence |
| Form (Aristotle) | Organising actuality or essence making matter a determinate thing |
| Matter | Potential substrate capable of receiving form |
| Hylomorphism | Aristotle's view that ordinary substances are compounds of matter and form |
| Four causes | Material, formal, efficient and final explanatory factors |
| Material cause | That out of which something is made |
| Formal cause | Structure or essence making a thing what it is |
| Efficient cause | Source of change or production |
| Final cause | End, function or purpose for the sake of which something occurs |
| Potentiality | Capacity to become or act in a certain way |
| Actuality | Realisation or fulfilment of a capacity |
| Unmoved Mover | Pure actuality that explains eternal cosmic motion as final cause without itself changing |

## 4. Rationalism: Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Methodic doubt | Descartes' deliberate suspension of any belief open to doubt in order to find certainty |
| Cogito | Self-verifying insight that the act of thinking guarantees the thinker's existence while thinking |
| Clear and distinct idea | Cognition presented with such clarity and separation that, under Descartes' rule, it is true |
| Innate idea | Idea belonging to the mind's rational structure rather than copied from sensation |
| Cartesian substance | Thing able to exist independently except for dependence on God |
| Res cogitans | Thinking substance or mind |
| Res extensa | Extended substance or matter |
| Cartesian dualism | View that mind is unextended thinking substance and body is extended non-thinking substance |
| Pineal interaction | Descartes' proposed physiological site associated with mind-body interaction; it does not itself solve the metaphysical problem |
| Trademark argument | Descartes' claim that the idea of an infinite perfect God requires an adequate infinite cause |
| Ontological argument (Descartes) | God's necessary existence follows from the clear and distinct idea of a supremely perfect being |
| Cartesian circle | Objection that Descartes uses clear and distinct ideas to prove God while relying on God to guarantee those ideas |
| Substance monism | Spinoza's doctrine that only one substance exists |
| Deus sive Natura | “God or Nature”: Spinoza's single infinite substance understood under infinitely many attributes |
| Attribute (Spinoza) | What intellect perceives as constituting the essence of substance; humans know thought and extension |
| Mode (Spinoza) | Finite modification of the one substance |
| Conatus | Each thing's striving to persevere in its being |
| Necessitarianism | Spinoza's view that everything follows necessarily from divine nature |
| Freedom as understanding | Spinozan freedom consists in acting from adequate understanding of necessity rather than from passive emotion |
| Monad | Leibniz's simple, indivisible, non-extended centre of perception and appetite |
| Windowlessness | Monads do not causally exchange contents with one another |
| Pre-established harmony | God coordinates monadic and bodily sequences from creation so they correspond without interaction |
| Perception (Leibniz) | Internal representation of multiplicity within a monad |
| Apperception | Reflective awareness of one's perceptions |
| Principle of Sufficient Reason | Nothing exists or occurs without an adequate reason why it is so rather than otherwise |
| Identity of Indiscernibles | No two distinct entities can possess exactly all the same properties |
| Compossibility | Mutual compatibility of possible substances within one possible world |
| Best possible world | God's choice of the compossible world optimising order, variety and perfection |

## 5. Empiricism: Locke, Berkeley and Hume

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Tabula rasa | Locke's denial of innate ideas: the mind begins without supplied conceptual content |
| Idea (Locke) | Immediate object of thought, derived from sensation or reflection |
| Simple idea | Elementary content passively received by the mind |
| Complex idea | Content actively formed by combining, comparing or abstracting simple ideas |
| Sensation | Experience of external objects as a source of ideas |
| Reflection | Inner awareness of the mind's own operations |
| Primary quality | Quality allegedly inseparable from bodies, such as shape, motion and number |
| Secondary quality | Power in objects to produce sensations such as colour, taste and sound |
| Representative realism | External objects exist independently but are known through ideas representing them |
| Nominal essence | Abstract idea associated with a general name and used for classification |
| Real essence | Underlying constitution making a thing what it is |
| Personal identity (Locke) | Continuity of consciousness, especially memory, rather than identity of substance alone |
| Immaterialism | Berkeley's denial of material substance; reality consists of minds and ideas |
| Esse est percipi | For sensible objects, to be is to be perceived |
| Idea (Berkeley) | Passive object of perception |
| Spirit / mind (Berkeley) | Active perceiver and will, known through notion rather than as an idea |
| Master argument | Berkeley's claim that an unconceived sensible object cannot be consistently conceived |
| Abstract idea criticism | Berkeley's denial that the mind forms fully general images detached from all particular features |
| Divine perception | God's continuous perception secures order and persistence of sensible reality |
| Impression (Hume) | Forceful immediate content of sensation, emotion or reflection |
| Idea (Hume) | Faint copy of an impression in thought |
| Copy principle | Every simple idea derives from a corresponding simple impression |
| Relations of ideas | Necessarily true propositions discoverable by thought |
| Matters of fact | Contingent propositions whose denial is conceivable and whose knowledge depends on experience |
| Constant conjunction | Repeated pairing of event-types that grounds expectation of causation |
| Necessary connection | Felt determination of the mind to expect one event after another, not an observed power in objects |
| Problem of induction | Past regularity cannot non-circularly justify the assumption that nature will remain uniform |
| Bundle theory | Self is not a separately perceived substance but a collection or stream of perceptions |
| Mitigated scepticism | Moderate Humean acceptance of ordinary life and empirical inquiry despite limits on rational justification |

## 6. Kant and Hegel

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Transcendental inquiry | Investigation of a priori conditions that make experience or knowledge possible |
| Transcendental idealism | Objects of experience conform to our forms of cognition, while things as they are in themselves remain unknowable |
| Empirical realism | Within experience, objects are genuinely public and objective, not private mental inventions |
| Synthetic a priori judgment | Informative judgment known independently of particular experience and necessary for experience |
| Form of intuition | A priori framework of sensibility: space for outer sense and time for inner sense |
| Category | Pure concept of understanding organising sensible intuition into objective experience |
| Transcendental unity of apperception | Necessary unity of self-consciousness expressed by the possible “I think” accompanying representations |
| Schematism | Temporal mediation that makes pure categories applicable to sensible appearances |
| Phenomenon | Object as experienced under human forms of intuition and categories |
| Noumenon | Thing considered independently of our mode of sensible cognition; a limiting concept, not known object |
| Idea of reason | Unconditioned concept of soul, world or God that guides inquiry but cannot constitute theoretical knowledge |
| Antinomy | Conflict in which reason appears able to prove opposite claims about the world as a totality |
| Paralogism | Invalid rational-psychology inference from unity of thought to a substantial immortal soul |
| Regulative use | Use of an idea to guide systematic inquiry without claiming a corresponding knowable object |
| Constitutive use | Use of a concept as determining an object of possible knowledge |
| Absolute idealism | Hegel's view that reality is an intelligible self-developing whole culminating in self-knowing Spirit |
| Dialectic (Hegel) | Immanent development in which a determination generates its own contradiction and is transformed |
| Determinate negation | Negation that preserves specific content while overcoming a limited form |
| Aufhebung / sublation | Simultaneous cancellation, preservation and elevation of a determination |
| Geist / Spirit | Social-historical self-developing rational life, not merely an individual mind |
| Alienation | Condition in which Spirit or human agency confronts its own product as foreign |
| Master-slave dialectic | Account of recognition in which unequal domination produces dependence and transformative labour |
| The Absolute | Whole reality understood as self-developing and self-knowing Spirit |

## 7. Moore, Russell and Early Wittgenstein

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Common-sense realism | Moore's defence of ordinary beliefs about bodies, persons and the external world against idealism/scepticism |
| Refutation of idealism | Moore's argument that awareness and its object are distinct, so experience does not imply that reality is mental |
| Naturalistic fallacy | Moore's charge that “good” cannot be definitionally reduced to a natural property |
| Logical atomism | Russell's view that complex reality and language can be analysed into logically independent atomic facts and propositions |
| Atomic fact | Simplest fact consisting of particulars related or possessing qualities |
| Molecular proposition | Proposition formed from simpler propositions by logical connectives |
| Knowledge by acquaintance | Direct awareness of an entity without inferential description |
| Knowledge by description | Knowledge of something as uniquely satisfying a descriptive condition |
| Theory of descriptions | Russell's contextual analysis eliminating apparently referring definite descriptions from logical form |
| Incomplete symbol | Expression that has no meaning in isolation but is defined through the propositions containing it |
| Logical construction | Entity represented through a logical structure of more basic data rather than treated as independently inferred |
| Picture theory | Early Wittgenstein's view that a meaningful proposition depicts a possible state of affairs by sharing logical form |
| Logical form | Structural possibility common to proposition and represented fact |
| Saying | Expressing a possible fact through a meaningful proposition |
| Showing | What is manifested in meaningful language or reality's structure but cannot itself be stated as another factual proposition |
| State of affairs | Possible combination of objects |
| World (Tractatus) | Totality of facts, not merely totality of things |

## 8. Logical Positivism and Later Wittgenstein

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Verification principle | A factual statement is cognitively meaningful only if empirically testable in principle |
| Strong verification | Requirement that experience conclusively establish a proposition |
| Weak verification | Requirement that evidence render a proposition probable or relevantly confirmable |
| Analytic statement | Meaningful statement true by linguistic rules or definitions |
| Synthetic statement | Meaningful factual statement answerable to possible experience |
| Pseudo-proposition | Grammatically declarative expression lacking cognitive meaning under the verification criterion |
| Rejection of metaphysics | Positivist classification of transcendent metaphysical assertions as cognitively meaningless rather than simply false |
| Protocol sentence | Basic observation report intended to anchor empirical verification |
| Unity of science | Project of integrating meaningful knowledge within a common empirically accountable framework |
| Meaning as use | Later Wittgenstein's view that, in many cases, a word's meaning lies in its role within practice |
| Language-game | Rule-governed linguistic activity embedded in a human practice |
| Form of life | Shared patterns of activity and response that provide the background of language-games |
| Family resemblance | Overlapping similarities without one essence common to every member of a concept |
| Rule-following | Participation in a public normative practice, not private interpretation repeated indefinitely |
| Private language | Alleged language whose meanings refer to intrinsically private items knowable only to one speaker |
| Private-language argument | Argument that purely private signs cannot have standards distinguishing correct from merely seeming-correct use |
| Criterion | Publicly usable standard governing application of a concept |
| Symptom | Contingent evidence for a condition, unlike a criterion partly constitutive of correct application |
| Philosophical therapy | Dissolution of conceptual confusion by examining actual language use rather than constructing theory |

## 9. Phenomenology and Existentialism

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Phenomenology | Disciplined description of structures of experience as they present themselves |
| Intentionality | Consciousness is always consciousness of or directed toward something |
| Epoché | Suspension of the natural attitude's unexamined commitment to world-existence |
| Phenomenological reduction | Reflective turn from objects simply assumed to the structures through which they are experienced |
| Eidetic reduction | Movement from individual examples to invariant essence through imaginative variation |
| Essence (Husserl) | Invariant structure without which an experience or object-type would not be what it is |
| Noesis | Subjective act or mode of intending |
| Noema | Object as intended or sense-correlate of the intentional act |
| Psychologism | Reduction of logical validity or meaning to contingent psychological processes |
| Lifeworld | Pre-theoretical world of lived meaning presupposed by scientific abstraction |
| Existentialism | Family of philosophies centring concrete existence, freedom, choice, responsibility, anxiety and authenticity |
| Existence precedes essence | Sartre's claim that humans first exist and then define themselves through choices |
| Subjectivity | First-person lived appropriation and agency, not mere arbitrary opinion |
| Anxiety / angst | Disclosure of freedom, possibility, finitude or lack of fixed worldly support |
| Despair (Kierkegaard) | Failure of the self properly to relate itself to itself and its grounding power |
| Leap / faith commitment | Non-deductive existential commitment where objective certainty cannot settle personal religious existence |
| Bad faith | Sartrean self-deception that treats oneself as a fixed thing or denies one's facticity |
| Facticity | Given conditions of one's situation, history, body and past |
| Transcendence (Sartre) | Consciousness's projection beyond what is given toward possibilities |
| Authenticity | Owning one's freedom, finitude and situation rather than hiding within impersonal roles |
| Dasein | Heidegger's term for the being whose own Being is an issue for it |
| Being-in-the-world | Primordial practical involvement of Dasein in a meaningful world, prior to subject-object separation |
| Thrownness | Dasein's finding itself already situated in conditions it did not choose |
| Care | Unified structure of Dasein as thrown projection amid involvement |
| Das Man / the “they” | Impersonal social normativity through which one ordinarily thinks and acts as “one does” |
| Being-towards-death | Authentic anticipation of one's own non-transferable finite possibility |
| Temporality | Ecstatic unity of future projection, inherited past and present involvement grounding Dasein's existence |

## 10. Quine and Strawson

| Term | Exam-safe definition |
|---|---|
| Analytic-synthetic distinction | Claimed division between truths based on meaning and truths based on fact; Quine argues no non-circular account establishes it |
| Reductionism | Dogma that each meaningful statement can be translated into immediate experience or separately tested |
| Confirmation holism | Experience tests interconnected systems of belief rather than isolated statements |
| Web of belief | Quine's image of knowledge as a revisable network with more central and peripheral commitments |
| Ontological commitment | A theory is committed to entities over which its indispensable quantified variables range |
| Naturalised epistemology | Study of knowledge as part of empirical inquiry into how sensory input produces theory |
| Radical translation | Translation from an entirely unknown language using behavioural evidence alone |
| Indeterminacy of translation | Total behavioural evidence may support incompatible translation manuals equally well |
| Inscrutability of reference | Evidence may fail to determine uniquely what terms refer to |
| Descriptive metaphysics | Strawson's project of describing the actual fundamental structure of our conceptual scheme |
| Revisionary metaphysics | Project of replacing ordinary conceptual structure with a theoretically improved one |
| Basic particular | Entity that can serve as a fundamental public object of identification; material bodies and persons are central cases |
| Person | Primitive individual to whom both physical and psychological predicates are ascribed |
| M-predicate | Predicate applicable to material bodies, such as weight or location |
| P-predicate | Predicate ascribing psychological states or actions to persons |
| Identifying reference | Practice of picking out a particular as the subject of predication within a shared framework |

| Integral Yoga | Aurobindo's transformation of mind, life and body through psychic, spiritual and supramental change | Sri Aurobindo |
| Involution | Self-concealment of consciousness in matter that makes evolution possible | Sri Aurobindo |
| Supermind | Integral truth-consciousness mediating unity and multiplicity | Sri Aurobindo |

## 12. Socio-Political Philosophy

| Term | Exam-safe definition | Nearest distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Equality | Norm that persons deserve equal moral concern and relevantly equal status/opportunity | Equality need not require identical treatment |
| Formal equality | Same legal rule or treatment for similarly situated persons | Substantive equality addresses unequal starting conditions |
| Substantive equality | Measures equality by actual conditions, capabilities and disadvantage | It may justify differential treatment |
| Equality of opportunity | Fair access to positions and advantages | Equality of outcome concerns resulting distribution |
| Justice | Giving persons their due through fair institutions, distributions, procedures and relations | Legality alone may be unjust |
| Distributive justice | Fair allocation of benefits, burdens, opportunities and resources | Corrective justice repairs a wrong |
| Procedural justice | Fairness of decision-making rules and processes | A fair procedure may still produce substantively disputed outcomes |
| Corrective justice | Rectification of wrongful loss or gain between parties | Retributive justice concerns punishment |
| Social justice | Fair institutional relations addressing structural disadvantage and exclusion | Charity does not alter underlying rights or structures |
| Justice as fairness | Rawls's conception selecting equal liberties and fair distribution from an original position | Utilitarianism aggregates welfare |
| Original position | Hypothetical fair choice situation for principles of justice | It is not a historical contract |
| Veil of ignorance | Device excluding knowledge of one's social position and natural advantages | It models impartiality |
| Difference principle | Inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged | It does not demand strict equality |
| Liberty | Protected sphere or capacity of agency free from unjustified domination/interference | Licence means absence of all constraint |
| Negative liberty | Freedom from interference by others | Positive liberty concerns self-direction or effective agency |
| Positive liberty | Freedom as self-mastery, autonomy or capacity to act | It may become paternalistic if imposed |
| Republican liberty | Freedom as non-domination or absence of arbitrary power | Domination may exist without actual interference |
| Sovereignty | Supreme authority to make and enforce final decisions within a political order | Power may exist without legitimate authority |
| Legal sovereignty | Institution recognised by law as final law-making authority | Political sovereignty identifies the actual ultimate political power |
| Popular sovereignty | Ultimate political authority belongs to the people | It requires institutional expression rather than permanent direct rule |
| Internal sovereignty | Supreme authority within a territory | External sovereignty concerns independence among states |
| External sovereignty | International independence and juridical equality of a state | Global interdependence may constrain without abolishing it |
| Command theory of law | Austin's view of law as command of a determinate sovereign backed by sanction | It struggles with constitutional and customary law |
| Pluralist sovereignty | Laski's denial that the state has morally unlimited, indivisible authority over associations | Legal centralisation may coexist with social pluralism |
| Right | Justified claim, liberty, power or immunity held by a person | A benefit without entitlement is not necessarily a right |
| Claim-right | Right correlating with another's duty | Liberty-right correlates with another's lack of claim |
| Liberty-right | Absence of duty not to perform an act | It does not itself impose assistance duties |
| Power-right | Ability to alter legal or normative relations | Immunity protects against another's power |
| Immunity-right | Protection against another's normative power | It is not merely freedom from interference |
| Duty | Normative requirement to act or refrain from acting | A supererogatory act is good but not required |
| Accountability | Obligation to explain and justify conduct and face consequences | Responsibility may exist without an external answerability process |
| Authority | Legitimate right to rule or issue binding directives | Coercive power alone is not authority |
| Legitimacy | Justified acceptability of an institution's right to rule | Effectiveness does not guarantee legitimacy |
| Political obligation | Moral duty, if any, to obey the state or law | Particular unjust laws may generate duties of resistance |
| Monarchy | Government in which supreme office is held by a monarch, usually through hereditary succession | Constitutional monarchy limits the ruler through law |
| Theocracy | Political order in which divine law or religious authority grounds governmental legitimacy | A religious society is not automatically a theocracy |
| Democracy | Rule based on political equality, participation, contestation and public accountability | Majoritarianism treats numerical will as sufficient |
| Deliberative democracy | Legitimacy arises through reason-giving among free and equal citizens | Aggregative democracy centres preference counting |
| Participatory democracy | Citizens directly and continuously engage in collective decisions | Representative democracy delegates decision-making |
| Anarchism | Rejection of coercive hierarchical state authority as unjustified or unnecessary | It does not necessarily mean disorder |
| Marxism | Theory of historical materialism, class struggle and capitalist exploitation oriented toward classless society | Socialism is broader than Marxism |
| Historical materialism | Productive forces and relations shape social structures and historical conflict | It need not imply simple mechanical economic determinism |
| Class | Social position defined by relation to productive resources and labour | Status groups may be organised by honour rather than ownership |
| Alienation (Marx) | Worker's estrangement from product, activity, species-being and other persons under capitalism | Exploitation concerns appropriation of surplus |
| Exploitation | Unjust extraction or appropriation of another's labour/advantage | Inequality alone does not prove exploitation |
| Socialism | Family of views favouring social control of productive resources, equality and cooperation | It includes non-Marxist and democratic forms |
| Humanism | View affirming human dignity, agency, welfare and rational/moral potential | Secular humanism does not require hostility to religion |
| Secularism | Norm governing religion-state relations to secure liberty, equality and non-domination | It is not simply absence of religion |
| Principled distance | Context-sensitive Indian secularism allowing engagement with or distance from religion to protect equal freedom | Strict separation bars most state-religion engagement |
| Multiculturalism | Recognition and accommodation of culturally diverse identities within equal citizenship | Cultural protection does not immunise internal oppression |
| Recognition | Public acknowledgment of identity and equal status | Redistribution addresses material inequality |
| Crime | Legally prohibited wrong subject to public punishment | Immorality is not automatically criminality |
| Punishment | Intentional imposition of deprivation by legitimate authority in response to wrongdoing | Revenge is private and not necessarily proportionate |
| Retributivism | Punishment is justified because wrongdoers deserve proportionate censure | Deterrence justifies punishment by future consequences |
| Deterrence | Punishment is justified by preventing offences through fear of consequences | General deterrence targets others; specific deterrence the offender |
| Incapacitation | Preventing crime by restricting the offender's ability to reoffend | It does not itself reform the offender |
| Rehabilitation | Reforming capacities, dispositions or social conditions linked to offending | It can become paternalistic |
| Restorative justice | Repairing harm through victim, offender and community participation | It does not simply replace accountability with forgiveness |
| Capital punishment | State execution as criminal penalty | Its debate concerns desert, deterrence, error, dignity and arbitrariness |
| Corruption | Abuse of entrusted power for private or sectional gain | Conflict of interest is a risk condition, not always completed corruption |
| Mass violence | Large-scale organised violence against civilian or social groups | Genocide additionally requires specific intent to destroy a protected group |
| Genocide | Specified acts committed with intent to destroy, wholly or partly, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group | Not every atrocity legally constitutes genocide |
| Development | Expansion of human capacities, welfare, freedom and sustainable institutional possibilities | Economic growth measures output increase |
| Economic growth | Increase in aggregate or per-capita production/income | It may occur without equitable development |
| Social progress | Improvement in freedom, capability, justice, health, education and social relations | Technological change alone is not progress |
| Capability | Real freedom or opportunity to achieve valuable beings and doings | Resources are means, not capabilities themselves |
| Functioning | Achieved state or activity, such as being educated or nourished | Capability is the genuine opportunity to achieve it |
| Empowerment | Expansion of agency, resources, voice and capacity to shape decisions | Welfare provision without agency may not empower |
| Sex | Biological classification and embodied characteristics | Gender concerns socially organised roles and identities |
| Gender | Socially constituted roles, norms, identities and power relations associated with sex | It is not reducible to biological difference |
| Patriarchy | Systemic social order privileging male authority and reproducing gender hierarchy | Individual prejudice is one mechanism within a wider structure |
| Gender discrimination | Disadvantage imposed because of sex/gender through direct, indirect or structural processes | Formal neutrality may still have discriminatory effects |
| Intersectionality | Analysis of how multiple structures such as gender, caste and class interact to produce distinct disadvantage | It is more than adding separate inequalities |
| Adaptive preference | Preference adjusted downward under persistent deprivation or restricted options | Expressed choice may therefore not prove genuine freedom |
| Caste | Hereditary, endogamous and hierarchically ordered social grouping historically tied to status and occupation | Varna is an ideal schema; jati is lived social organisation |
| Untouchability | Practice of exclusion and degradation based on notions of hereditary pollution | It is constitutionally abolished but may persist socially |
| Affirmative action | Targeted measures correcting historical exclusion and improving effective equality | It is not an exception to equality when equality is substantive |

## 13. Philosophy of Religion

| Term | Exam-safe definition | Nearest distinction |
|---|---|---|
| God | Ultimate divine reality conceived through attributes such as necessity, knowledge, power, goodness and relation to the world | Traditions disagree whether God is personal |
| Omnipotence | God's possession of maximal power to do all that is logically possible and consistent with divine nature | It does not require making contradictions true |
| Omniscience | God's complete knowledge of all truths | Foreknowledge raises questions about human freedom |
| Omnibenevolence | God's perfect goodness and love | It sharpens the problem of evil |
| Necessary being | Being that cannot fail to exist and does not depend on another | Contingent beings could fail to exist |
| Creation ex nihilo | Creation of the world from no pre-existing material | Emanation portrays reality as flowing from the divine |
| Providence | Divine sustaining, ordering and guidance of creation | Deism minimises special providence |
| Cosmological argument | Inference from contingent, caused, changing or dependent reality to a first or necessary ground | It begins from the world's existence |
| First-cause argument | Version of cosmological reasoning inferring an uncaused cause | A temporal first event is not required by every version |
| Argument from contingency | Inference from contingent beings to a necessary being explaining their existence | It relies on a sufficient-reason principle |
| Teleological argument | Inference from order, complexity, purposiveness or fine-tuning to intelligence/design | Evolution challenges some biological-design forms |
| Ontological argument | A priori argument deriving God's existence from the concept of a maximally perfect or necessary being | It does not begin from empirical world-features |
| Moral argument | Inference from objective moral obligation/value to a divine ground, legislator or highest good | Secular moral realism offers an alternative |
| Religious experience | Experience interpreted as awareness of, encounter with or relation to divine/ultimate reality | Interpretation may be tradition-shaped |
| Mystical experience | Alleged direct, unitive or transformative awareness of ultimate reality | Not all religious experience is mystical |
| Numinous | Otto's non-moral experience of the holy as mystery that is both terrifying and fascinating | It differs from ordinary fear or aesthetic awe |
| Extrovertive mysticism | Unity experienced through the transformed perception of the external world | Introvertive mysticism turns away from sensory multiplicity |
| Introvertive mysticism | Experience of undifferentiated unity through inward withdrawal | Its content and interpretation remain contested |
| Revelation | Disclosure of divine truth through event, person, scripture or experience | Natural theology reasons without special revelation |
| General revelation | Divine disclosure through nature, reason or universal experience | Special revelation is historically or scripturally particular |
| Special revelation | Particular divine disclosure through prophets, texts or saving events | Its authority may depend on faith/tradition |
| Faith | Trustful commitment to religious truth or God, potentially involving but exceeding evidence | Blind credulity is belief without responsible grounds |
| Fideism | Priority or independence of faith over rational proof | Evidentialism requires adequate evidence for belief |
| Evidentialism | Belief is rational only in proportion to sufficient evidence | Reformed epistemology permits properly basic theistic belief |
| Natural theology | Rational inquiry into God using general reason and experience | Revealed theology begins from special revelation |
| Reformed epistemology | View that belief in God may be properly basic without inferential proof | Proper basicality remains defeasible |
| Problem of evil | Challenge of reconciling evil with an omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good God | The logical and evidential forms differ |
| Moral evil | Suffering caused by morally responsible agents | Natural evil arises from disease, disaster or non-moral processes |
| Natural evil | Suffering not directly caused by human wrongdoing | Some theodicies link it to stable natural laws |
| Logical problem of evil | Claim that God and evil are logically inconsistent | A defence needs only show possible consistency |
| Evidential problem of evil | Claim that the kinds/amounts of evil make God's existence improbable | It does not claim strict contradiction |
| Defence | Possible account showing that God and evil may be logically compatible | A theodicy claims the account is actually or plausibly true |
| Theodicy | Attempt to provide morally sufficient reasons why God permits evil | It bears a heavier explanatory burden than defence |
| Free-will defence | Evil is possibly permitted because significant freedom requires risk of misuse | It more directly addresses moral than natural evil |
| Soul-making theodicy | World permits moral/spiritual growth through challenge, freedom and distance from God | Horrendous or crushing evil challenges proportionality |
| Eschatological resolution | Claim that afterlife fulfilment defeats or redeems present suffering | Compensation alone may not justify permitting evil |
| Soul | Non-reducible principle of personal life, identity or consciousness | Materialism rejects a separable immaterial substance |
| Immortality | Continued personal or spiritual existence beyond bodily death | Survival need not mean bodily resurrection |
| Rebirth | Continuity across successive lives through soul, karma or causal process | Buddhism explains continuity without an eternal self |
| Resurrection | Restoration or re-creation of embodied personal life after death | It differs from reincarnation into repeated earthly lives |
| Liberation | Release from ignorance, bondage, sin or rebirth into ultimate fulfilment | Its meaning varies by tradition |
| Religion without God | Religious orientation centred on liberation, sacred value or ultimate concern without a creator deity | It is not necessarily secular atheism |
| Religious naturalism | Religious meaning and value grounded within nature without supernatural deity | It can retain reverence, ritual and ultimate concern |
| Religion | Structured relation to what is regarded as sacred or ultimate through beliefs, practices, experiences and communities | No single essence may cover every tradition |
| Morality | Norms and reasons concerning right action, good character and value | Religion may support morality without being its only possible basis |
| Divine-command theory | Moral obligation depends on God's commands | It faces the Euthyphro dilemma |
| Euthyphro dilemma | Is conduct good because God commands it, or commanded because independently good? | Modified command theories ground goodness in divine nature |
| Moral autonomy | Capacity to recognise and act on moral reasons through rational agency | Heteronomy subjects morality to an external will or desire |
| Religious exclusivism | One tradition uniquely possesses saving or final truth | Inclusivism permits truth/salvation elsewhere through the privileged tradition |
| Religious inclusivism | One tradition is normatively final while others contain truth or mediate salvation | Pluralism denies one tradition's exclusive finality |
| Religious pluralism | Multiple traditions are valid responses to or paths toward ultimate reality | It does not entail that all doctrines are equally true |
| Absolute truth | Truth independent of viewpoint or tradition | Human access to it may remain perspectival |
| The Real (Hick) | Ultimate transcategorial reality differently experienced and conceptualised by traditions | It is not simply one tradition's personal God |
| Cognitivism | Religious statements make truth-apt claims | Non-cognitivism treats them primarily as expressions, commitments or practices |
| Non-cognitivism | Religious utterances do not primarily state facts but express attitudes, commitments or forms of life | It does not necessarily call religion meaningless |
| Analogical language | Divine predicates are used neither univocally nor equivocally but by proportionate similarity | It aims to avoid anthropomorphism and total agnosticism |
| Symbolic language | Religious symbols point beyond themselves and participate in the reality they disclose | A symbol is richer than a conventional sign |
| Univocal predication | Term has the same meaning when applied to God and creatures | It risks reducing divine transcendence |
| Equivocal predication | Term has wholly different meanings in divine and creaturely use | It threatens meaningful God-talk |
| Via negativa | Speaking of God by denying creaturely limitations | It may be combined with analogy and eminence |
| Verification | Conditions under which a claim could be established as true | Eschatological verification postpones fulfilment to an afterlife |
| Falsification | Conditions under which evidence would count against a claim | Flew argues endlessly qualified religious claims risk death by qualification |
| Eschatological verification | Religious claim could be verified, though not necessarily falsified, after death | Hick's celestial-city parable illustrates it |
| Blik | Hare's unfalsifiable fundamental orientation shaping interpretation of experience | It is non-cognitive but practically powerful |
| Religious language-game | Religious utterances gain meaning through rule-governed use within a form of life | Critics question communication across forms of life |

## 14. Essential comparison pairs

| Pair | One-line distinction |
|---|---|
| Theism / deism | Theism ordinarily affirms ongoing providence; deism affirms a creator with minimal intervention |
| Pantheism / panentheism | Pantheism identifies God and reality; panentheism includes the world in a God who exceeds it |
| Rationalism / empiricism | Rationalism grants reason substantial independent content; empiricism roots content/knowledge primarily in experience |
| A priori / innate | A priori concerns justification independent of experience; innate concerns psychological origin |
| Substance / property | Substance bears or exists independently; property characterises or depends |
| Cause / reason | Cause produces/explains an event; reason justifies a belief or action |
| Determinism / fatalism | Determinism works through causal conditions including choices; fatalism makes outcomes unavoidable regardless of choices |
| Negative / positive liberty | Negative liberty removes interference; positive liberty concerns self-direction and effective agency |
| Equality / sameness | Equality requires equal concern/status, not identical treatment in every circumstance |
| Power / authority | Power is capacity to secure outcomes; authority is legitimate right to command |
| Responsibility / accountability | Responsibility assigns duty or authorship; accountability requires explanation, review and consequences |
| Defence / theodicy | Defence shows possible consistency of God and evil; theodicy claims God's actual reasons |
| Exclusivism / inclusivism / pluralism | Exclusivism privileges one true path; inclusivism allows others through it; pluralism recognises multiple valid paths |
| Cognitivism / non-cognitivism | Cognitivism treats religious claims as truth-apt; non-cognitivism treats them as expression, commitment or practice |
| Puruṣa / prakṛti | Puruṣa is conscious witness; prakṛti is unconscious material nature |
| Ātman / anātman | Ātman affirms enduring self; anātman denies permanent independent self-substance |
| Māyā / śūnyatā | Māyā explains Advaita appearance relative to Brahman; śūnyatā denies intrinsic essence in all phenomena |
| Satkāryavāda / ārambhavāda | Satkāryavāda says effect pre-exists in cause; ārambhavāda says effect is newly produced |

## 15. Answer-writing rule

For a 10/15/20-mark answer, do not stop after the definition. Use:

```text
Definition -> philosopher/school -> argument -> nearest distinction
-> objection -> reply/qualification -> directive-specific verdict
```

If a term has multiple traditions, state the school before defining it. For example,
“substance” in Aristotle, Descartes, Spinoza, Locke and Nyāya does not carry one identical
meaning.
