# MCQ 005 — Articles 32 and 226

**Area:** Polity - Fundamental Rights and Constitutional Remedies  
**Difficulty:** Hard  
**Status:** Completed

## Question

With reference to the writ jurisdictions of the Supreme Court and High Courts, consider the
following statements:

1. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 32 is confined to enforcement of Fundamental
   Rights, whereas a High Court may exercise Article 226 jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights as
   well as other legal rights.
2. The right to move the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, whereas
   relief under Article 226 is ordinarily discretionary.
3. A High Court may exercise Article 226 jurisdiction when the cause of action arises wholly or
   partly within its territory, even if the seat of the authority concerned is outside that
   territory.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only  
B. 2 and 3 only  
C. 1 and 3 only  
D. 1, 2 and 3

## Learner response

**D. 1, 2 and 3**

## Evaluation and explanation

**Result:** Correct  
**Correct answer:** D. 1, 2 and 3

### Statement analysis

1. **Correct.** Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to enforce the rights contained in Part III
   of the Constitution. Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue writs for enforcement of
   Fundamental Rights and "for any other purpose", which includes enforceable ordinary legal
   rights.
2. **Correct.** Article 32 is itself guaranteed as a Fundamental Right. A person may ordinarily
   approach the Supreme Court when a Fundamental Right is violated. Article 226 confers a
   discretionary public-law remedy: a High Court may decline relief for reasons such as an
   effective alternative remedy, delay, disputed facts or inequitable conduct, although these are
   rules of prudence rather than absolute bars.
3. **Correct.** Article 226(2) permits jurisdiction when the cause of action arises wholly or
   partly within the High Court's territorial jurisdiction, notwithstanding that the government,
   authority or person's seat is outside it.

### Objectives and functions

| Provision | Court | Core function | Breadth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article 32 | Supreme Court | Guaranteed constitutional remedy for Fundamental Rights | Part III rights |
| Article 226 | High Courts | Judicial review of public action and protection of rights | Fundamental Rights plus other legal rights |

### The five constitutional writs

| Writ | Function |
|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | Requires production of a detained person and tests the legality of detention |
| Mandamus | Commands a public authority to perform a legally required public duty |
| Prohibition | Prevents an inferior court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction |
| Certiorari | Quashes an order of an inferior court, tribunal or authority for jurisdictional or public-law error |
| Quo warranto | Tests the legal authority by which a person occupies a public office |

### Timeline and constitutional development

- **1949:** The Constituent Assembly adopted the constitutional-remedies framework. B. R.
  Ambedkar described Article 32 as the Constitution's "heart and soul".
- **26 January 1950:** Articles 32 and 226 came into force with the Constitution.
- **1963:** The Fifteenth Amendment inserted the cause-of-action basis for High Court territorial
  jurisdiction; it now appears in Article 226(2).
- **1978:** The Forty-fourth Amendment protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension through an
  Article 359 presidential order during an Emergency.

### Wider distinction

The Supreme Court's power under Article 32 is narrower in subject matter but constitutionally
guaranteed. A High Court's Article 226 power is wider in subject matter and often provides the
more factually accessible remedy. Article 32 does not make the Supreme Court a general court of
first instance for every legal grievance.

### UPSC trap

"Article 226 is wider than Article 32" refers principally to the rights enforceable, not to a
claim that High Courts are institutionally superior to the Supreme Court. Statement 3 also turns
on the cause of action, not merely the physical location of the respondent authority.
