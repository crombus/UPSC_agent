Why does Cārvāka challenge inference (anumāna) as an independent source of certain knowledge?

A. Perception directly presents every universal relation needed for inference.  
B. A universal concomitance cannot be established with certainty from finite cases without relying on further inference.  
C. Testimony establishes every inferential conclusion without depending on a speaker.  
D. Inference concerns only remembered objects and never unobserved ones.

**Source:** `upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Carvaka.md` §§1.1–1.2, especially the discussion of vyāpti and the limits of finite observation.

**Explanation:** Cārvāka argues that inference depends on *vyāpti*, an invariable concomitance between a sign and what it indicates (for example, smoke and fire). Finite observations cannot establish an exceptionless universal with certainty; trying to establish it by another inference risks circularity or regress. Cārvāka is not necessarily denying practical habits of inference, but denies that they provide strict certainty about what exceeds perception.

- **A:** Incorrect; perception supplies particular observed cases, not every past, present, future, near and distant instance needed to establish an unrestricted universal.
- **B:** Correct; this is the Cārvāka challenge to establishing *vyāpti* with certainty.
- **C:** Incorrect; Cārvāka also questions testimony (*śabda*) as an independent source, since it depends on a speaker's reliability or inference from words.
- **D:** Incorrect; inference is used to reach beyond what is presently perceived; the objection concerns its certainty, not that it only concerns remembered objects.

**Contrast:** Nyāya accepts inference as an independent *pramāṇa* and defends *vyāpti* through repeated observation, attention to counter-conditions and supportive reasoning.
