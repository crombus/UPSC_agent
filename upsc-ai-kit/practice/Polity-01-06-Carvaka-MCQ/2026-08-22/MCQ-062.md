# MCQ 062

**Topic:** Polity 06 - Citizenship  
**Difficulty:** Hard  
**Coverage point:** Assam cut-off, enemy aliens and limits of single citizenship  
**Status:** Completed

## Question

Consider the following statements:

1. The citizenship cut-off associated with the Assam Accord is 24 March 1971.
2. Enemy aliens are excluded from the protections concerning arrest and detention under
   Article 22.
3. Equality before law under Article 14 is available only to Indian citizens.
4. Single citizenship prevents states from granting any residence-based concessions or
   preferences.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1, 2 and 3 only  
B. 1 and 2 only  
C. 3 and 4 only  
D. 1, 2 and 4 only

## Response and explanation

**Your answer:** B  
**Correct answer:** B  
**Result:** Correct

Statements 1 and 2 are correct.

### Meaning of the terms

- **Assam Accord cut-off:** the historical date used to distinguish relevant migration categories
  for the special Assam citizenship framework.
- **Enemy alien:** a national of a state at war with India, treated differently from an ordinary
  foreigner for specified constitutional protections.
- **Single citizenship:** one constitutional citizenship of India rather than separate Union and
  State citizenship.
- **Residence preference:** a benefit or eligibility condition linked to residence without
  creating a separate state citizenship.

### Statement-by-statement analysis with examples

1. **Correct.** The relevant cut-off is 24 March 1971; persons entering after that date fall within
   the post-cut-off category under the Assam framework.  
   **Example:** The Assam NRC exercise used this historical cut-off when examining legacy
   documentation.
2. **Correct, with precision.** Article 22(3)(a) excludes enemy aliens from the ordinary arrest
   safeguards in Article 22(1)-(2), such as consultation with a lawyer and production before a
   magistrate within twenty-four hours.  
   **Example:** An ordinary foreigner generally remains a “person” for many Fundamental Rights,
   but an enemy alien is a specific constitutional exception under Article 22.
3. **Incorrect.** Article 14 protects **any person**, citizen or foreigner, subject to recognised
   constitutional distinctions.  
   **Example:** A foreign visitor may invoke equality before law, although they cannot claim the
   citizen-only freedoms under Article 19.
4. **Incorrect.** Single citizenship prevents separate state citizenship, not every
   residence-linked distinction.  
   **Examples:** States may charge different educational fees or offer certain resident
   concessions; Parliament may prescribe residence requirements for specified public employment
   under Article 16(3).

> **Hard distinction:** citizenship, domicile and residence are related but legally different
> concepts.

### Learner doubt: How can diplomats possess immunity if foreigners may lack some arrest safeguards?

The categories and legal sources are different:

| Category | Legal position |
|---|---|
| Ordinary foreigner | Generally protected as a “person” under Articles such as 14 and 21 |
| Enemy alien | Specifically excluded from Article 22(1)-(2) ordinary-arrest safeguards |
| Accredited diplomat | Receives status-based immunity under international diplomatic law and Indian implementing law |

**Diplomatic immunity is not derived from Article 22.** It arises principally from the Vienna
Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961 and India's Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention)
Act, 1972. An accredited diplomatic agent enjoys personal inviolability and broad immunity from
the receiving state's criminal jurisdiction.

**Example:** If an accredited diplomat is accused of an offence, India ordinarily cannot arrest
and prosecute that person while immunity remains. India may request the sending state to waive
immunity or declare the diplomat *persona non grata*, requiring departure.

An **enemy alien**, by contrast, is a national of a hostile state and has no diplomatic status
merely because they are a foreigner.

> **Core distinction:** Article 22 governs constitutional safeguards; diplomatic immunity is a
> separate treaty-and-status protection that may prevent arrest from arising in the first place.
