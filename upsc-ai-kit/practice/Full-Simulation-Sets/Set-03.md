### A1. A nation is secure when its citizens trust both borders and institutions.
Thesis: Military capability deters external coercion, but a state that forfeits its citizens' confidence becomes vulnerable from within: intelligence dries up, compliance falls and rumour outruns official communication. Security is thus two-sided, requiring credible defence alongside institutions whose fairness citizens can verify. The proposition joins strength abroad to legitimacy at home.
Structure: Define security as capability combined with cohesion. | Show internal legitimacy as an operational rather than sentimental asset. | Examine policing, rights and minority confidence. | Add the cyber and information domains, where trust is the target. | Balance necessary secrecy against institutional accountability.
Dimensions: Deterrence rests on capability and credibility, which require sustained modernisation, logistics and indigenous industrial depth over decades. | Rights protection is a security asset, because populations expecting fair treatment cooperate with police and report emerging threats. | Social cohesion decides whether a provocation stays an incident or becomes a riot, making equal protection a counter-escalation instrument. | Cyber threats attack confidence directly, since strikes on payment systems, hospitals and information environments aim at public trust.
Examples: Prakash Singh (2006) directed police reforms meant to insulate investigation from political interference and improve public confidence. | The 2020 cyber incident affecting Mumbai's power supply showed civilian infrastructure as a strategic target. | CERT-In's directions of 2022 sought faster national visibility of cybersecurity incidents across critical sectors. | The Mizo Accord of 1986 converted an insurgency into constitutional politics through negotiated political accommodation.
Counterview: Security sometimes requires acting against public opinion. Counter-terror operations, surveillance of hostile networks and negotiations with armed groups cannot always be transparent or popular, and excessive proceduralism costs lives. Trust must therefore be built through oversight by accountable institutions rather than through disclosure of every operational decision.
Conclusion: A secure India needs a defended frontier and a citizen who believes her local police station will treat her fairly. Neglecting either produces a state that is armed but brittle, or trusted but exposed.

### A2. Urbanisation will be India's opportunity only if cities become governable.
Thesis: Cities concentrate productivity because proximity lowers the cost of exchange and learning. That advantage is cancelled when commuting is unreliable, housing unaffordable, drainage broken and no elected authority answerable for the city as a whole. The proposition makes governance capacity, not the pace of urban growth, the decisive variable.
Structure: Explain agglomeration gains and the congestion costs that offset them. | Show the gap between the 74th Amendment's promise and actual devolution. | Analyse municipal finance as the foundation of everything else. | Take housing and mobility as the tests citizens experience daily. | Add climate risk as the emerging stress test of urban governance.
Dimensions: Municipal revenue remains small relative to municipal responsibility, leaving cities dependent on transfers and unable to plan over long horizons. | Fragmented authority defeats accountability, since water, transport, policing and land often answer to different state agencies. | Housing policy decides whether workers live near employment or surrender income and hours to peripheral commuting. | Urban climate risk concentrates where drainage was built over and where housing quality cannot buffer heat or flooding.
Examples: The 74th Amendment of 1992 created urban local bodies, yet functions, funds and functionaries were devolved unevenly. | The Reserve Bank's report on municipal finances found Indian city revenues to be roughly one per cent of GDP. | Odisha's Jaga Mission granted land rights to slum households and upgraded settlements in place rather than relocating them. | Mumbai's deluge of 2005 exposed the consequences of building over the Mithi river's drainage capacity.
Counterview: Governability is not achieved by adding mayors. Empowered city governments lacking revenue, technical staff or political competition can be captured by builders and contractors, and metropolitan problems spill across municipal limits. Devolution needs own revenue, professional cadres, transparent planning and metropolitan coordination, or it simply relocates dysfunction.
Conclusion: India will urbanise regardless of policy; the choice is between productive cities and congested ones. Empowered, financed and accountable city governments separate agglomeration as an asset from agglomeration as a liability.

### A3. Strategic autonomy is sustained by domestic capability.
Thesis: Autonomy is the ability to refuse. A state dependent on one supplier for weapons, chips, fuel or finance may announce independence but cannot practise it under pressure. The proposition identifies capability, meaning industrial, technological and energy depth, as the material foundation that turns a declared posture into a usable one.
Structure: Define autonomy as issue-based choice rather than equidistance. | Trace the evolution from non-alignment to contemporary multi-alignment. | Map real dependencies in defence, technology and energy. | Argue that capability rather than rhetoric converts choice into leverage. | Concede the limits of autarky and the necessity of partnerships.
Dimensions: Defence industrial depth decides whether procurement choices survive sanctions, denial of spares or wartime disruption of supply. | Technological capability in semiconductors, space and telecommunications determines who writes standards and who merely adopts them. | Energy dependence constrains diplomacy, because concentrated import sourcing converts a foreign-policy choice into an economic vulnerability. | Diplomacy multiplies capability through coalitions, yet partners bargain harder with a state unable to produce credible alternatives.
Examples: Continued Russian energy and defence purchases alongside Quad cooperation illustrate issue-based alignment in practice. | ISRO's launch and interplanetary programme, including Chandrayaan-3 in 2023, gives India independent access to space. | The Semiconductor Mission announced in 2021 addresses a supply chain where dependence is strategically consequential. | The International Solar Alliance, launched with France in 2015, shows India building institutions rather than only joining them.
Counterview: Self-reliance can degenerate into costly protection. Import substitution in the licence-raj decades produced expensive low-quality industry and slower growth, and no country manufactures an entire aerospace or semiconductor chain alone. Modern capability is built inside trusted networks, so autonomy that rejects interdependence would shrink India's real options.
Conclusion: Strategic autonomy is a budget line before it is a doctrine. Research funding, manufacturing depth and diversified energy sourcing decide whether India's declared independence of judgement survives its first serious test.

### A4. Public health is built before a patient reaches a hospital.
Thesis: Hospitals treat individuals; public health protects populations. Most avoidable deaths are settled earlier by clean water, immunisation, nutrition, air quality and early detection, long before any admission. The proposition corrects an inversion in which health policy is measured by beds and machines rather than by the conditions keeping people out of them.
Structure: Distinguish medical care from public health as different production functions. | Show that the largest historical mortality gains came from prevention. | Examine primary care and continuity as the missing middle. | Add surveillance as the early-warning layer. | Address the political economy that favours visible hospital construction.
Dimensions: Preventive measures such as immunisation, safe water and vector control avert disease at a small fraction of treatment cost. | Sanitation generates health externalities, since one household's practice changes the infection burden of an entire settlement. | Primary care supplies continuity, screening and referral, thereby reducing avoidable hospitalisation and catastrophic out-of-pocket expenditure for poorer families. | Surveillance converts scattered cases into an actionable signal, provided reporting is timely, trusted and shielded from misuse.
Examples: India was certified polio-free in 2014 after sustained immunisation, surveillance and community mobilisation. | Accredited Social Health Activists extended community-level health contact under the National Rural Health Mission from 2005. | The Swachh Bharat Mission from 2014 treated sanitation as a determinant of diarrhoeal disease and child nutrition. | Health and wellness centres under Ayushman Bharat sought to strengthen comprehensive primary care alongside hospital insurance.
Counterview: Prevention cannot substitute for treatment. Cancer, trauma, cardiac emergencies and complicated childbirth demand equipped hospitals and specialists, and insurance shields families from catastrophic bills that primary care cannot avert. India's deeper problem is not misallocation between the two, but chronic underfunding of the health system overall.
Conclusion: The most efficient health rupee is usually spent before illness, but it must be added to hospital capacity rather than traded against it. Public health succeeds quietly, which is exactly why it stays politically underfunded.

### B5. Equality sometimes requires treating unequal circumstances differently.
Thesis: Identical treatment of unequally situated people reproduces the inequality it claims to ignore. One staircase, one medium of instruction or one competitive threshold offers formally equal access while functionally excluding some. The proposition defends differentiation justified by relevant disadvantage and publicly reasoned, not differentiation driven by preference or patronage.
Structure: Distinguish formal equality, substantive equality and arbitrary discrimination. | State the constitutional basis for reasonable classification. | Apply it to caste disadvantage, disability and regional capacity. | Identify limits of proportionality, review and continuing evidence. | Warn against differentiation that becomes permanent entitlement without measurement.
Dimensions: Reasonable classification requires an intelligible differentia and a rational connection to the objective, which blocks arbitrary preference. | Caste-based measures address structured historical exclusion, but need schooling and anti-discrimination enforcement to translate into outcomes. | Disability rights show accommodation as a precondition of access, since a ramp or screen reader creates usable rather than notional equality. | Fiscal federalism applies the same logic between states, transferring more to units with weaker revenue capacity and greater need.

### B6. Markets discover prices; societies must still decide values.

### B7. A rule gains legitimacy when those bound by it can understand and challenge it.

Conclusion: A citizen should be able to learn what a rule requires, why it applied to her, and where to contest it. Meeting that modest standard would do more for legal legitimacy in India than most substantive amendments.

### B8. Education should prepare citizens to revise answers, not merely remember them.

Conclusion: The educated citizen holds firm knowledge lightly enough to update it. India's reform will succeed only when examinations reward that ability, because assessment, not policy language, decides what teachers actually teach.

### Q13 [15 marks]

### Q1 [10 marks]
Distinguish the money market from the capital market and explain why a developed economy needs both.

Model answer (144 words): Money and capital markets differ chiefly in financing horizon and function. The money market redistributes short-term liquidity through call money, repo, Treasury Bills, Commercial Paper and Certificates of Deposit. It helps banks and firms bridge cash-flow mismatches and transmits monetary conditions. The capital market mobilises medium- and long-term finance through equity, dated Government securities, State Development Loans and corporate bonds. The distinction is not absolute safety versus risk. A short instrument can default or face rollover stress, while a long sovereign bond can be credit-safe yet price-sensitive. Both primary issuance and secondary liquidity matter: issuance transfers funds to the borrower; trading supplies price discovery and exit. A developed economy needs an integrated ladder. Money markets stabilise working liquidity, whereas capital markets fund infrastructure, enterprise and public borrowing. Transparent benchmarks and resilient settlement must connect them without allowing leverage or maturity mismatch to transmit shocks.

### Q10 [10 marks]
Distinguish hedging, speculation and arbitrage in derivatives markets.

Model answer (146 words): Hedging uses a derivative to offset a pre-existing exposure. An exporter expecting dollar receipts may sell dollars forward, accepting basis and timing risk in exchange for more predictable rupee cash flow. Speculation creates or enlarges exposure: a trader buying index options without an offsetting portfolio seeks profit from price movement. Arbitrage combines offsetting positions to exploit inconsistent prices across related claims. The contract name does not reveal purpose. A future can hedge inventory or create a leveraged directional bet. Position size, maturity and underlying match determine whether total risk falls. ### Q20 [15 marks]

### Q1 (Compulsory)
(a) "Sensible things are those only which are immediately perceived by sense." Explain Berkeley's theory of knowledge with reference to the above statement. [10 marks]
Model answer: The sentence fixes a definition, and the whole theory follows from it. For Berkeley the objects of human knowledge are ideas - imprinted on the senses, perceived by attending to the mind's own operations, or formed by memory and imagination. A "sensible thing" is by definition what is immediately perceived by sense. The word immediately is doing the work. Locke held that we perceive ideas which represent external objects, so that the object is reached mediately, by inference. Berkeley's definition removes the inference: if what is immediately perceived is an idea, and a sensible thing is what is immediately perceived, then an apple, a stone or a tree is nothing but a collection of such ideas. Two consequences follow at once. First, the "veil of perception" disappears, and with it the sceptical problem it generated. Second, since ideas cannot exist unperceived, for sensible things to be is to be perceived (Principles 3), and "absolute existence without relation to their being perceived" is unintelligible. Verdict. Berkeley's theory of knowledge is direct realism about ideas: we know the world itself, not a representation of it - though the price is that the world is not material.

Model answer: Thesis. The criterion applied. A sentence carries cognitive meaning only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. This sentence is neither. The subject-term. "The Soul of Superman" supplies no empirical criterion of application: "soul" has none in the required sense, and the bearer is fictional. On Carnap's first route to pseudo-statement status, a sentence containing such a term essentially can acquire no truth-conditions. The value-predicate. On Ayer's account in Language, Truth and Logic (1936) a value term adds no factual content; it evinces approval and may evoke it. The predicate is therefore expressive rather than descriptive, and it cannot supply the truth-conditions the subject-term has failed to provide. Verdict and qualification. (c) Distinguish descriptive from revisionary metaphysics, and explain why Strawson describes his own project as descriptive. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Descriptive metaphysics describes the actual structure of our thought about the world; revisionary metaphysics proposes a better structure - and Strawson's project is descriptive because its conclusions are claims about the conditions of a scheme we already use. The distinction. In Individuals: An Essay in Descriptive Metaphysics (1959) the descriptive metaphysician maps how our concepts hang together; the revisionary metaphysician recommends replacing them. Strawson's own examples are Aristotle and Kant on the descriptive side, and Descartes, Leibniz and Berkeley on the revisionary side. The relation. The two are not simply opposed: revisionary metaphysics is at its most valuable in the service of the descriptive, because dismantling part of the actual scheme reveals what it was doing. (d) Distinguish between the transcendental and the transcendent in Kant, and explain why the distinction is indispensable to the Transcendental Dialectic. Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: The distinction. Transcendental names an enquiry into the a priori conditions under which objects can be given to us. It is legitimate and unavoidable; the deduction of the categories and the unity of apperception are transcendental. Transcendent names the employment of concepts beyond all possible experience. It is illegitimate, because a category has objective sense only through its schema, and a schema is a determination of time. Why the Dialectic requires it. Reason's principle - that for every conditioned the whole series of conditions is given - is a regulative maxim directing enquiry toward systematic unity. Read constitutively, it becomes a claim about an existing unconditioned object, and the enquiry becomes transcendent. Each of the three collapses is then a specific transcendent move. The Paralogisms convert a formal condition of thought into a soul-substance. The Antinomies treat the world-series as a given whole. The Ideal hypostatises a standard of complete determination into an existing most real being. Kant's distinctive claim. Transcendental illusion is natural and unavoidable, unlike a logical mistake that vanishes once exposed. Criticism prevents us from being deceived; it does not make the appearance disappear. Verdict. The anchors. 19 states that "to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life", and says it while asking whether the builders' "Slab!" is a word or a sentence - showing that a vocabulary cannot be specified apart from an activity. 23 closes its list with the clause that "the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life". ### Q2
(a) "I never can catch myself at any time without perception, and never can observe anything but the perception." How does this statement by Hume problematize the philosophical notion of personal identity? How does Kant deal with this problem in his Critique of Pure Reason? [20 marks]
Model answer: The sentence is the copy principle turned on the perceiver. If every legitimate idea traces to an impression, an idea of the self would require an impression constant and invariable through a whole life. Introspection delivers no such thing - only heat and cold, light and shade, love and hatred, pain and pleasure, succeeding one another in perpetual flux, and never the one who is finding them. Hume's conclusion follows: the self is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions" (Treatise I.iv.6). The problem this creates is threefold. First, the substantial self is dissolved, so nothing owns the experiences and no bearer survives change. Second, diachronic identity loses its ground: the unity we feel is a fiction produced by resemblance and causation, which let the imagination pass smoothly along the series and mistake an easy transition for an identical object. Third, and most damagingly, the Appendix confesses that Hume cannot state the principle that unites the perceptions: "all my hopes vanish". Having denied both a substance and any real connexion between distinct existences, he has removed every resource such a principle could use - and the Enquiry silently drops the topic altogether. Kant does not answer by restoring a soul. He accepts that no inner intuition reveals a simple substance, so the rational psychologist's argument is conceded to Hume. His move is transcendental: the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations if they are to belong to one experience at all. The transcendental unity of apperception is therefore a formal condition of synthesising a manifold under rules - not a further item discoverable inside the bundle, and not an intuition of an enduring substance. Verdict. Kant answers the missing unity at the level of possible experience without proving an immortal self or a simple substance. The Humean may reply that a condition of description is not the discovery of a subject; Kant concedes the non-entity point and insists that rule-governed unity is presupposed by judgment. The exchange shows that the fault lay in the atomism of impressions, not in the self.

(b) "Berkeley destroys Locke's resemblance thesis but not his distinction between primary and secondary qualities." Critically examine. [15 marks]
Model answer: The statement is largely correct, and stating why requires separating two theses Locke runs together. Thesis A - the explanatory distinction. Primary qualities are solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest and number: they are inseparable from body, since every fragment retains them, and they are the properties a corpuscular physics must attribute to any body. Secondary qualities are powers, grounded in primary-quality configurations, to produce sensations in us. Thesis B - the resemblance claim. Our ideas of primary qualities resemble the qualities themselves; our ideas of secondary qualities resemble nothing in the object. Berkeley attacks both. His parity argument (Principles 9-15) observes that the relativity considerations Locke accepts against secondary qualities apply equally to primary ones. But relativity does not entail mind-dependence: that a coin looks elliptical from an angle is exactly what a genuinely circular coin should produce. Thesis A therefore survives, though it must be restated as a distinction between measurable-structural and response-dependent properties rather than as a claim about what is "really in" the object. The likeness principle (8) is another matter. If an idea can be like nothing but an idea, and the alleged originals are unperceivable, then the resemblance could never be checked and the claim is empty. Thesis B falls. The available rescue is isomorphism: structural correspondence requires no qualitative likeness, as a map corresponds to terrain without being terrain-coloured - but this concedes Berkeley's point about likeness and defends something weaker. Verdict. Correct with one qualification: what survives is not Locke's distinction as he framed it, but its explanatory core. Locke's error was to make the division depend on a picture rather than on explanatory role - which is why physics retains the distinction while his version of it does not.

(c) Discuss the linguistic theory of necessary propositions and assess whether it succeeds in reconciling empiricism with the necessity of logic and mathematics. (Answer in about 250 words.) [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The linguistic theory reconciles empiricism with necessity by a genuine strategy rather than a concession, but the reconciliation is purchased at a price that later criticism showed to be steep. The problem. Logic and mathematics are necessary, known a priori and certain, whereas empiricism holds that all knowledge derives from experience. Kant answered by making such truths synthetic a priori; Mill by making them empirical generalisations. The positivist answer. Necessary propositions are analytic: true solely in virtue of the meanings and conventions governing their symbols. They are tautologies and give no information about the world. This yields all three properties from one source - necessary because nothing could count against them, a priori because grasping the meanings suffices, and certain because denial is self-contradiction - while the emptiness that secures them is exactly what an empiricist can afford. Why the rivals fail. Kant requires a third class the positivists deny. Mill is refuted by two observations: we do not discover "2 + 2 = 4" by counting pebbles, and no experience could refute it, since an apparent counter-instance is attributed to miscounting rather than to arithmetic. Assessment. The account must still explain why an empty discipline is indispensable to physics, and the available reply - that applicability belongs to the embedding empirical theory - is defensible rather than decisive. Quine argues in "Truth by Convention" (1936) that conventions cannot generate their consequences without presupposing logic, and in "Two Dogmas" (1951) that analyticity cannot be non-circularly defined. Verdict. The reconciliation succeeds on its own terms and fails on Quine's; it is the best empiricist account of necessity available before the category of analyticity was itself put in question.

### Q3
(a) "Logical positivism failed by its own standards and changed philosophy anyway." Critically examine this assessment of the movement. (Answer in about 350 words.) [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Both halves of the assessment are defensible, but only if the failure is described as plural and structural rather than as defeat by a single celebrated objection. The failure, ranked. First, the strong/weak fork. Strong verification, requiring conclusive establishment by a finite observation set, excludes universal laws, the past and other minds - it convicts science. Weak verification, requiring only relevance, is so permissive that an auxiliary hypothesis readmits metaphysics. Ayer's 1946 replacement by direct and indirect verifiability was shown by Church in 1949 to admit what it was designed to exclude. Second, the protocol debate of 1932-35: Schlick's incorrigible affirmations cannot be recorded, Neurath's protocols are revisable, and Carnap's 1932 ruling that their form is a matter of choice ends foundationalism inside the Circle. Third, analyticity and holism: Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction and sentence-by-sentence reductionism together. Only fourth comes self-refutation - the criterion is neither analytic nor verifiable - and it admits a serviceable reply, that the principle is a proposal or a meta-linguistic rule, whose cost is that a proposal may simply be declined. What was not a repair. Popper's falsifiability is a criterion of scientific demarcation, not of meaning, and he held metaphysics to be meaningful. The later Wittgenstein abandons any single criterion of meaning whatever, so he pressures the school without supplying its missing formulation. What changed anyway. Schlick's difference-argument (1932) survives as a heuristic of significance: philosophers who reject the criterion still ask what difference a proposed distinction is supposed to make. The demand for explicit rules rather than obscurity became a professional norm. The agenda of modern philosophy of science and language - confirmation, demarcation, holism, the status of analyticity - is the agenda of positivism's unsolved problems. And the separation of criteria of meaning, of truth and of scientific status is a permanent gain that predates none of the school's critics. Verdict. The assessment is accurate, provided "failed" names four pressures and not one, and provided "changed philosophy" names a surviving method and agenda rather than a surviving doctrine.

(b) "No statement is immune to revision." How far can this claim be sustained in the case of logic? [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The claim can be sustained in principle and only weakly in practice: logic is protected by entrenchment and by the cost of revision rather than by any certificate of analyticity. The claim and its source. It stands in 6 of "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) as the converse of "any statement can be held true come what may". The early essay extends revisability even to a law of logic, mentioning the proposed revision of the law of excluded middle as a means of simplifying quantum mechanics. The strongest candidate. Birkhoff and von Neumann, "The Logic of Quantum Mechanics" (Annals of Mathematics, 1936), proposed a non-distributive lattice of quantum propositions - a formal proposal. The distinct philosophical claim that logic is therefore empirically revisable, and specifically that the distributive law should be given up, is Putnam's, in "Is Logic Empirical?" (1968). The two must never be merged into "Putnam/Birkhoff". Putnam later abandoned the position. The resistance, from Quine himself. In Philosophy of Logic (1970) Quine argues that anyone who "denies" a law of logic has merely changed the subject, and he invokes the maxim of minimum mutilation. The later Quine is therefore a poor witness for the earlier Quine's boldest claim. Assessment. The objection that unrestricted revisability leaves science without a fixed point is met by the formula that no statement is revisable by nature, only by choice - and choice is disciplined by conservatism, simplicity and predictive success. But the concession is substantial: if revising logic normally costs more than any theory can pay, "revisable" does little work. Verdict. Holism is right about confirmation and overstated about revisability. The defensible position is Duhem's thesis with Quine's motivation: testing is holistic, the choice of revision is pragmatic, and logic is revisable in a sense so attenuated that little follows from it.

(c) Assess the view that Kant's phenomena-noumena distinction is an empty boundary that his own system cannot sustain. [15 marks]
Model answer: The charge. Two objections combine. Hegel argues that the unknowable in-itself is an empty abstraction produced by fixing a permanent opposition between appearance and reality. Jacobi presses the affection problem: "without the assumption of things in themselves I cannot enter the system; with it I cannot remain" - for if causality is a category valid only of appearances, Kant cannot say that things in themselves cause our sensations. The reply. Kant's noumenon in the negative sense is a limiting concept (Grenzbegriff): something considered merely as not an object of our sensible intuition. Its emptiness is deliberate, since a boundary that had content would no longer be a boundary. A noumenon in the positive sense would require intellectual intuition, which Kant expressly denies we possess. The Schematism supplies the ground: a category without a schema can be thought but determines no object, which is exactly what "thinkable but not knowable" means. Against Hegel, the distinction is therefore not a knowledge-claim about a hidden object; against Jacobi, Kant's language is more careful than the objection allows - he speaks of what affects sensibility without claiming theoretical cognition of a noumenal cause. What survives the reply. The affection problem is a genuine tension, not a misreading. The two-aspect reading, on which the same objects are considered under two epistemic standpoints, reduces the pressure by making affection an aspect of one cognitive relation rather than a transaction between worlds; but the textual dispute with the two-world reading remains live. Verdict. The boundary is not empty in the pejorative sense: it does indispensable work, licensing the Principles for everything that can appear and forbidding their extension to soul, world and God. It is, however, a boundary Kant can state more securely than he can explain - and that is the honest formulation.

### Q4
(a) "Quine and Strawson criticise empiricism, but they do not criticise it together." Discuss. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The statement is correct: the two attack empiricism at different levels with different tools, and the only documented exchange between them is a disagreement. Quine's level - confirmation. "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) removes two assumptions. The analytic/synthetic boundary fails because definition merely records synonymy, interchangeability salva veritate over-generates extensionally and requires a necessity that presupposes analyticity, and semantical rules give only "analytic-in-L". Reductionism fails because statements "face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body". What replaces both is confirmation holism, and what survives is empiricism without the dogmas - sensory evidence and naturalism retained. Strawson's level - reference. Individuals (1959) argues that identification requires re-identification, that re-identification requires a single unified spatio-temporal framework, and that material bodies are its enduring, publicly observable occupants. Sense-data cannot occupy the role: they are private, momentary and non-spatial. And since P-predicates must be other-ascribed on public criteria yet self-ascribed without observation, the concept of a person is logically primitive, destroying the no-ownership alternative as well as the Cartesian one. Why this is not a joint critique. The methods are opposed. Quine is a naturalist who regiments science into canonical notation and denies any first philosophy; Strawson practises connective analysis and offers arguments that are transcendental in form. Their conclusions differ correspondingly: Quine ends with a revisable web in which no statement is immune; Strawson ends with conditions internal to a conceptual scheme that his method is not designed to revise. And the decisive evidence is textual: Grice and Strawson, "In Defence of a Dogma" (1956), defend the very distinction Quine attacked, arguing that a distinction with agreed application needs no reductive analysis. Quine's rejoinder - that ordinary intelligibility is not theoretical legitimacy - leaves a live dispute about standards of explication, not a shared programme. What may legitimately be said jointly. Empiricism failed twice over: about how theories are confirmed and about how we so much as refer. That is a problem-field, not a thesis. Verdict. Read together they pressure both empiricist confirmation atomism and sense-datum reconstruction without becoming one school; and an answer that treats them as co-authors of a single anti-empiricist doctrine misdescribes the only exchange the record contains.

(b) "No course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule." Critically examine Wittgenstein's treatment of this paradox. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The paradox is a reductio of the picture on which grasping a rule consists in attaching an interpretation to it; its conclusion is not that rules determine nothing but that rule-following is exhibited in trained practice. How the paradox is generated. 185 imagines a pupil taught the "+2" series to 1000 who continues 1004, 1008, 1012 and protests that he went on "in the same way". Repeating the examples does not refute him; it comes natural to him to take the order as we should take "add 2 up to 1000, 4 up to 2000". Nothing already possessed - sign, examples, image, explanation - settles the matter, since each stands in the same need of application. The diagnosis. 201's second part identifies the misunderstanding "from the mere fact that in the course of our argument we give one interpretation after another; as if each one contented us at least for a moment, until we thought of yet another standing behind it." Since interpretation is glossed as substituting one expression of the rule for another, the regress is structural, and the first step was the error. The resolution. 201's third part asserts "a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases"; 202 adds that obeying a rule is a practice. Two misreadings. Treating the paradox as a sceptical result ignores 201's own word "misunderstanding". Treating community behaviour as the criterion reinstates the collapse of "right" into "seems right", since a practice has mistakes where a regularity has exceptions. Assessment. The ruling: philosophically valuable, since neither interpretation nor disposition fixes normativity; exegetically doubtful, since a sceptical solution is the constructive theory 128 and 133 disavow.

(c) Is Aristotle's view of nature of identity in consonance with his metaphysical view of causes as processes? Discuss giving suitable examples. [15 marks]
Model answer: Identity. For Aristotle a substance is what it is through its substantial form or essence (to ti en einai). At Metaphysics VII (Z).17 substance is sought as a cause: the question "why is this matter a house?" is answered by the form. Matter supplies determinate capacities, not a second identity. Causes as processes. Causation for Aristotle is not bare succession. The four causes are four answers to "why?" about one ongoing thing, and in natural substances the formal, efficient and final causes coincide, so a cause is exhibited in a development rather than as an event preceding it. Are they consonant? Yes, and for a structural reason: the same form does both jobs. It states what the thing is and it is the actuality towards which its determinate potential moves. Example 1 - the oak. The acorn's matter has the potential for oak-form; parent and nutrition supply efficient causation; the oak-form is both what it becomes and what the growth is for. Throughout, the individual remains this developing organism. Example 2 - the sculptor's bronze. Casting is accidental change to the bronze, which persists; but the coming-to-be of a living thing is substantial generation, in which a new substantial form constitutes a new substance rather than modifying an old one. Objection and reply. Defining a process by its completed form reads the end back into the beginning and understates contingency and failed development. Aristotle can reply that a potentiality is always a capacity of this material under these conditions, not a guarantee of success - the acorn reliably tends to oak, not to certainty. Verdict: consonant, and more successfully than a static essence theory - but the consonance is purchased with internal teleology, which is the doctrine's real price.

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) "Buddhist epistemology is not a shortened Nyaya list." Examine this claim with reference to the two means of valid knowledge and the exclusion theory of meaning. [10 marks]
Model answer: The claim is correct, and the reason is structural rather than numerical. Dignaga and Dharmakirti admit two means of valid knowledge because they hold that the number of means is fixed by the number of object-types. There are exactly two: the unique particular (svalaksana), which is momentary, causally efficient and ineffable, grasped by non-conceptual perception; and the constructed general character (samanyalaksana), grasped by inference. Comparison, postulation, non-apprehension and testimony are reduced to these, not omitted from a list. The exclusion theory (apoha) confirms the point. Since only unique particulars are ultimately real, a general term cannot denote a real universal; "cow" functions as "not-non-cow", generality being constructed negatively upon shared causal capacity. Both theses follow from momentariness and no-self, which is also why the instrument of knowledge is held to be non-different from its result. A shortened Nyaya list would still contain an enduring knower and real universals; Buddhist epistemology contains neither.

(b) Delineate the main points of difference between the theory of intrinsic validation (svatah pramanyavada) and theory of extrinsic validation (paratah pramanyavada) in classical Indian philosophy. [10 marks]
Model answer: The two theories divide on four cells, not one. Intrinsic validation, held by Mimamsa, treats both the origin (utpatti) and the ascertainment (jnapti) of validity as intrinsic: the conditions that generate a cognition generate its validity, and the cognition presents itself as trustworthy unaided. Invalidity, by contrast, is extrinsic in both respects, arising from a defect (dosa) and becoming known through a defeater (badhaka), contradiction or practical failure. Extrinsic validation, held by Nyaya, treats both the origin and the ascertainment of validity as extrinsic relative to the mere occurrence of the cognition. The points of difference follow. First, burden of proof: Mimamsa places it on the challenger, Nyaya on the cognition. Second, mechanism: Nyaya certifies by successful activity (pravrtti-samarthya) or confirming perception (samvadi-pratyaksa), while Mimamsa denies that later success can generate validity. Third, regress: a validating cognition would itself need validation, producing anavastha. Fourth, the decisive distinction: validity itself versus our ascertainment of it. Placed more widely, Samkhya is presented as intrinsic on both counts, and Buddhist epistemology as the mirror-image, extrinsic validity with intrinsic invalidity - which Mimamsa rejects, since illusion does not announce itself as illusion. The essential qualification is that intrinsic validity is defeasible default warrant, not infallibility.

(c) "In Visistadvaita philosophy, the relationship between God and the world is parallel to that between an individual self and its body." Critically discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: The statement is accurate as far as it goes. In Ramanuja's system selves (cit) and matter (acit) constitute ultimate reality's body (sarira), while ultimate reality is their indwelling soul (saririn). Bodyhood is defined by three conditions: what is entirely supported by an indwelling self, entirely controlled by it, and existing for its purposes. All three hold of the world with respect to God. Two clarifications are essential. First, "body" is functional, not physical: it means whatever is supported, controlled and used by an indwelling self, which is why conscious selves can also be called God's body. Second, the relation is inseparable dependence (aprthaksiddhi): the modes are real and distinct but cannot exist or be conceived apart from their substance - stronger than a conceptual relation, weaker than identity. Critically, the parallel is asymmetric. An ordinary self is affected, limited and pained by its body's defects; God's essential auspicious nature is untouched by the world's imperfections. The analogy therefore holds for support, control and purpose, and fails for vulnerability. Madhva presses this further: a real self cannot be another's instrument at all.

(d) Explain the difference between Samprajnata Samadhi and Asamprajnata Samadhi. [10 marks]
Model answer: Absorption (samadhi) is the state in which the mind-field becomes radically unified and transparent. Patanjali distinguishes two principal forms. Object-supported absorption (samprajnata) retains a cognitive support; a subtle seed of cognition remains, and it is therefore called with-seed (sabija). Yoga-sutra 1.17 characterises it by four accompanying factors - gross deliberation (vitarka), subtle reflection (vicara), refined lucid joy (ananda) and bare I-am-ness (asmita). The separate coalescence analysis of Yoga-sutra 1.42-1.44 further classifies object-absorption as savitarka and nirvitarka for gross objects and savicara and nirvicara for subtle objects; these two grids must not be conflated. Supra-cognitive absorption (asamprajnata) is defined at Yoga-sutra 1.18 as preceded by the practice of the idea of cessation and as having impression-residue (samskara-sesa). No object stands forth as support; the modifications have ceased, yet latent impressions may remain. The difference is therefore twofold: presence or absence of an object-support, and the reduction of mental activity to a bare residue. A further condition follows: when even that residue is restrained, absorption becomes seedless (nirbija, Yoga-sutra 1.51). Supra-cognitive absorption is not sleep, which is itself one of the five modifications.

(e) Distinguish between many-sidedness (anekantavada), standpoint theory (nayavada) and conditional predication (syadvada). Why is the order of derivation philosophically significant? [10 marks]
Model answer: The three doctrines occupy different levels and are derived in a fixed order. Many-sidedness is metaphysical: the real object is ananta-dharmatmakam vastu, constituted by innumerable aspects, so no single unqualified proposition exhausts it. Standpoint theory is epistemic: because finite cognition is partial, every ordinary judgement expresses a naya, one legitimate perspective among many; a standpoint that denies the rest becomes its perversion. Conditional predication is logical-linguistic: assertion must carry syat, stating the respect in which it holds, and the sevenfold predication is its formal schema. The order matters for two reasons. First, it makes the logic non-arbitrary. If many-sidedness came last, the syat operator would look like a debating trick; derived first, it makes qualification obligatory. Second, it protects realism. The aspects are in the object; only access is partial. Reversing the order would make Jainism a theory about language or attitude rather than about the world. Hence a script that names the three terms but not their levels has recited without explaining.

### Q6
(a) "Ignorance of dependent origination is suffering while its knowledge is cessation of suffering." Present an account of Buddhist soteriology in the light of above statement. [20 marks]
Model answer: The quoted statement is not an epistemic slogan but a causal thesis: misreading how things exist is itself the engine of suffering, and correcting the misreading dismantles it. Why ignorance is productive. Ignorance (avidya) is not blankness. It is the active misconstrual of conditioned processes as stable possessions - treating what is dependently arisen as though it had intrinsic nature and belonged to an owner. That misconstrual generates appropriation: what is taken as "mine" is defended, pursued and mourned. This is why ignorance heads the twelve links (dvadasa-nidana), conditioning formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing-and-death. Where the chain can be cut. Feeling (vedana) arises unavoidably from contact; craving (trsna) does not arise unavoidably from feeling. That gap is the operative joint, and the Noble Eightfold Path (aryastangamarga) is built to work on it. Grouped under three trainings, wisdom (right view, right intention) attacks ignorance directly, ethical discipline (right speech, action, livelihood) removes the conduct that continually renews craving, and meditative discipline (right effort, mindfulness, concentration) stabilises the attention in which feeling is prevented from hardening into demand. Why cessation follows. The reverse order (pratiloma) of the chain states that with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease, and so on to the cessation of ageing-and-death. Cessation is therefore not an additional event to be hoped for; it is entailed by removing a condition in a dependently arisen series. This converts the third Noble Truth from an assertion into a consequence, and it is exactly what the quoted sentence claims. What liberation is. Cessation of conditioned suffering (nirvana) is the extinction of attachment, aversion and delusion. Since the self was never found, nothing is annihilated; the process of grasping simply ceases. The practitioner is the conventional person, and continuity of practice is carried by the causal series (santana), not by a soul. Critical assessment. The soteriology is unusually tight: diagnosis, cause, cessation and therapy are one argument, and the metaphysics of impermanence and no-self are entailments of the therapeutic claim rather than additions to it. Its contested point is whether knowledge alone, however transformative, can uproot deeply conditioned craving; the tradition answers by making insight inseparable from conduct and attention, which is why the path has eight factors and not one.

(b) "The doctrine of two truths is what separates emptiness from nihilism." Critically examine, with reference to the four-cornered analysis. [15 marks]
Model answer: The claim is substantially correct, though it needs a supplement. The problem. The four-cornered analysis (catuskoti) sets out four alternatives - it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not - and Nagarjuna denies all four. To a realist this looks like the abolition of every position, hence nihilism, and the further charge that the denial is itself a thesis, so the claim to have no thesis (pratijna) is self-refuting. The first defence is the negation type. Madhyamaka negation is non-implicative (prasajya-pratisedha) rather than implicative (paryudasa-pratisedha), and it is scoped: the four corners are denied of things taken as possessing intrinsic nature (svabhava). Denying them therefore withdraws a malformed question rather than asserting four incompatible propositions, and no fifth positive thesis is installed. Nagarjuna's supplement is that his own words are empty too, so they generate no ontological commitment; empty utterance cures empty reification as one magically created figure may counteract another (nirmitaka). The second defence is the two truths, and this is what the claim identifies. At conventional truth (samvrti-satya), persons, causation, moral consequence, compassion and the path all function; at ultimate truth (paramartha-satya), nothing possesses own-being. Conventional truth is not falsehood but the operative domain of Buddhist practice. Without it, the fourfold denial would indeed leave nothing standing. Critically, the two truths are necessary but not sufficient: the negation nuance must also be stated, or the doctrine still looks self-refuting. And whether a non-implicative negation can be sustained without covertly functioning as a thesis remains disputed, as the internal Prasangika-Svatantrika division shows. The accurate verdict is that the two truths block nihilism, while the scoped negation blocks self-refutation, and emptiness needs both.

(c) "A candidate who is never seen to be studying during the day time secures a high position in a competitive exam." How would the Bhatta Mimamsakas and the Naiyayikas explain the success of this candidate? Discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: The Bhatta Mimamsakas explain the case by postulation (arthapatti). Two facts are accepted: the candidate tops the examination, and the candidate is never seen studying by day. Examination success is incompatible with no preparation at all, so the two facts generate explanatory tension. To let both stand, some unobserved preparation must be posited, and the minimally reconciling postulate is study at night or otherwise outside observed daytime. The night-study is neither perceived here nor remembered; it is postulated because otherwise the accepted data remain inexplicable. This is postulation from perceived facts (drstarthapatti), structurally identical to the canonical case of stout Devadatta who does not eat by day. The Naiyayikas frame the same case as an inference. Wherever there is examination success there is preparation; this candidate has examination success; therefore this candidate had preparation. The reason (hetu) is success, the probandum (sadhya) is preparation, and the transition runs through a concomitance (vyapti). The Bhatta reply is that the reduction misses what actually generates the cognition. The reasoning does not begin from a sign but from a clash of accepted facts; the movement is from explanatory tension to a reconciling postulate rather than from sign to signified; and the case shows no subject-reason-probandum structure. Above all, the concomitance is slipped in too quickly: "wherever there is success there is preparation" is not independently established, and if it is claimed as known, it was abstracted from lived cases by exactly the postulating move under dispute. The reduction is therefore circular. Discussion should end even-handedly. Nyaya's account is simpler and keeps the instrument-list short; the Bhatta account is more faithful to how the cognition actually arises, which is why Mimamsa treats explanatory necessity as epistemically basic.

### Q7
(a) Does the admission of Svayamprakasavada necessarily lead to the admission of Svatahpramanyavada? Discuss after the Naiyayikas, the Mimamsakas and the Advaita Vedantins. [20 marks]
Model answer: Self-luminosity (svayamprakasatva) asks whether a cognition is manifest without another cognition. Intrinsic validity (svatah-pramanya) asks whether a cognition is entitled to prima facie truth without external certification. Manifestation and truth-entitlement are distinct questions, so no entailment can be read off the definitions; it would have to be argued, and no school argues it. The Naiyayikas accept neither thesis. A cognition is known through a distinct later cognition (anuvyavasaya) of the form "I know the pot," and validity is ascertained extrinsically through successful activity (pravrtti-samarthya) or confirming perception (samvadi-pratyaksa). Nyaya is therefore not the awkward case of self-luminosity without intrinsic validity; it simply denies both. The Mimamsakas divide, and this division decides the question. Prabhakara holds that one cognition discloses knower, known and knowing together (triputi-samvit), comparing cognition to a lamp that shows itself while showing the room; he also holds intrinsic validity. Kumarila denies self-luminosity: cognition is like a painter who does not paint himself, and its occurrence is inferred from the object's acquired knownness (jnatata). Yet Kumarila is among the strongest defenders of intrinsic validity, arguing that if every cognition needed another to certify it an infinite regress (anavastha) would follow. Kumarila is therefore the decisive counterexample. A single actual case of intrinsic validity without self-luminosity refutes a claim of necessity. Nor is his combination unstable: cognition is inferred from what it leaves behind in the object, while its truth-entitlement is settled by the anti-regress argument, so the two issues are handled by different machinery. The Advaita Vedantins affirm both, but on different ground. Self-luminosity belongs to consciousness as such and not merely to cognitive episodes, while empirical cognition carries prima facie authority until sublated within a doctrine of levels of reality. Their pairing is therefore a substantive metaphysical commitment rather than a definitional consequence - which is exactly what the question's word "necessarily" is testing. The answer is accordingly negative. The two doctrines travel together in Prabhakara and in Advaita, come apart in Bhatta, and are jointly rejected by Nyaya. Two qualifications complete the discussion. First, independence is not irrelevance: a self-luminous cognition makes intrinsic validity easier to defend. Second, the standing objection that intrinsic validity cannot explain error is answered on both Mimamsa routes - Prabhakara by admitting no false cognition, Kumarila by tracing falsity to extrinsic defect and later sublation. The verdict, then, is that manifestation and truth-entitlement are two properties, not one under two names.

(b) 'The doctrine of Relativism of Jain Philosophy cannot be logically sustained without postulating 'Absolutism'.' Critically examine this view and give reasons in the favour of your answer. [15 marks]
Model answer: The quoted view rests on an equivocation between two senses of "absolute". Jaina non-absolutism (anekantavada) denies one-sided exclusivism by finite knowers; it does not deny that comprehensive knowledge is possible. Once the two are separated, the alleged self-contradiction dissolves. The charge runs: the doctrine claims universal scope; universal scope appears absolute; therefore the doctrine covertly relies on what it attacks. The Jaina reply is threefold. First, the doctrine is itself asserted syat, so it obeys its own rule; reflexive qualification exhibits the discipline it teaches rather than exempting itself from it. Second, the thesis is not that all judgements are equally true but that truth is aspect-conditioned, so judgements remain gradable by whether they respect their own conditions. Third, within the system omniscience (kevala-jnana) is the internal case of complete knowledge, available when knowledge-obscuring karma is destroyed; ordinary discourse remains standpoint-bound because ordinary cognition is ksayopasamika. It should also be said that "relativism" mislabels the position. Jainism is ontologically realist: the aspects are objectively in the thing, and only finite access to them is partial. Relativism relativises truth; non-absolutism relativises exclusive claims. Critically, the reply succeeds against the charge as printed but does not leave the field untouched. The unconditioned status has been relocated rather than abolished: omniscience now carries the completeness that finite discourse is denied. A critic may say this concedes the substance of the objection at a higher level. My position is therefore qualified agreement with the Jaina: the doctrine is logically sustainable and not self-refuting, but it is not standpoint-free, and honesty requires saying that its consistency is purchased by admitting one privileged cognitive state.

(c) Explain Citta and its modifications in the philosophy of Yoga. Why does Yoga philosophy prescribe cessation of modifications of Citta? Give reasons in support of your answer. [15 marks]
Model answer: The mind-field (citta) is the unified inner apparatus of determinative intellect (buddhi), ego-maker (ahamkara) and sensory mind (manas). It is a product of primordial material nature and therefore insentient (jada), yet as a working whole it is lucid-predominant and so transparent enough to take the form of objects and to mirror the conscious witness (purusa). It is the psychical instrument of experience, not the self. A modification (citta-vrtti) is any operative form the mind-field assumes. Patanjali classifies them exhaustively as five: valid cognition (pramana) through perception, inference and authoritative testimony; error (viparyaya), as in taking nacre for silver; verbal construction (vikalpa), as in "the horn of a hare"; sleep (nidra), a positive mode grounded on absence-content and evidenced by the waking report "I slept and knew nothing"; and memory (smrti), the non-loss and re-presentation of experienced content. Each may be afflicted (klista) or unafflicted (aklista). Cessation is prescribed for five connected reasons. First, the mind-field by nature takes the form of whatever it attends to. Second, the witness's consciousness is reflected in that form. Third, in the absence of discriminative knowledge the changing form is appropriated in the first person - "I know", "I suffer", "I desire", "I remember". Fourth, the stream of modifications is therefore not a symptom of bondage but its running mechanism. Fifth, restraining the stream uncovers the already-free nature of the seer, as a pool reflects the sky faithfully once its ripples settle. Two qualifications complete the answer. Restraint (nirodha) is revelatory rather than destructive: it removes concealment, not consciousness, and does not annihilate the mind as a substance. And valid cognition is included among the modifications deliberately, because what binds the seer is not the falsity of a mental form but the fact that it is a form at all.

### Q8
(a) Compare the Buddhist and Jaina responses to the problem of change and permanence, and assess which secures the reality of the world at lower philosophical cost. [20 marks]
Model answer: Both are non-Vedic renouncer traditions, both deny a creator, and both must explain how a thing can change and still be spoken of as the same. Their solutions are opposites. The Jaina solution. The real (sat) possesses origination (utpada), decay (vyaya) and persistence (dhrauvya) together. Permanence belongs to substance (dravya) and change to modes (paryaya), so the gold persists while bracelet gives way to ring. Since the real has innumerable aspects, cognition is standpoint-relative (naya) and assertion carries the operator syat, generating the sevenfold predication. Reality is many-sided (anekantavada), and the soul (jiva) is a real conscious substance obscured by action-matter. The Buddhist solution: deny the persisting substance altogether. Whatever is conditioned is impermanent, and the criterion of the real as causally efficient (sat = arthakriya-samartha) radicalises this into momentariness (ksanikavada), since a permanent entity can produce its effect neither all at once (yaugapadya) nor successively (krama) without ceasing to be permanent. Apparent persistence is a causal series (santana) of sufficiently similar moments; the person is a designation on five aggregates; no soul is admitted. The costs, weighed. Jainism secures the world cheaply in ontology and expensively in logic. Nothing needs to be re-described: things really persist and really change. But predication must be permanently qualified, the schema of assertion becomes sevenfold, and the system must carry six substances, innumerable souls and material action-matter. Buddhism secures the world cheaply in logic and expensively in re-description. Assertion stays ordinary and the ontology is austere - momentary causally efficient events - but almost every ordinary notion must be relocated to conventional truth: the same thing, the same person, the rememberer, the deserver. Assessment. If "reality of the world" means the truth of ordinary claims taken at face value, Jainism secures it at lower cost, because it never demotes them. If it means an ontology free of unexplained composites and unverifiable substances, Buddhism secures it at lower cost, because it posits less. The decisive contrast is methodological: Jainism qualifies the sentence to save the world; Buddhism qualifies the world to save the sentence. The standard error must be avoided: Jainism affirms the soul emphatically, and only Buddhism denies it.

(b) How do Naiyayikas respond to Carvaka's objections against inference (anumana) and establish inference as an independent means of knowledge? Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: The objection to be met. The Carvaka holds that inference (anumana) is not an independent means of valid knowledge because its indispensable universal, invariable concomitance (vyapti), cannot be established: not by perception, which surveys only finite cases; not by inference, which would need a further vyapti and so runs into circularity or regress; and not by testimony, whose own validity is unproved. The hidden limiting condition (upadhi) sharpens this, since what looked like a bare universal may all along have been conditioned - fire yields smoke only with wet fuel. The Nyaya response. The Naiyayika abandons the demand for an omniscient survey and builds vyapti instead from agreement in presence (anvaya), agreement in absence (vyatireka), the systematic elimination of suspected upadhis, and hypothetical reasoning (tarka), which shows that denying the relation makes experience incoherent. Later Nyaya adds samanyalaksana-pratyaksa, so the relation is grasped between class-characters rather than tallied instance by instance. Why inference is then independent. Independence rests on paramarsa, the mark cognised as qualified by the concomitance. That composite cognition is neither a fresh perception of fire nor a speaker's report, so inference is irreducible to perception or testimony. Critical assessment. The reply works by redefinition rather than refutation. Nyaya concedes that finite observation cannot entail an unrestricted universal and denies only the epistemological conclusion, since warrant does not require entailment. Three residues remain: tarka is not itself a means of valid knowledge; samanyalaksana-pratyaksa presupposes Nyaya realism about universals; and elimination removes only suspected conditions. Verdict: the Naiyayika defeats the strong thesis, since a universal denial of inference cannot itself be perceived and other minds are never perceived either. He does not restore certainty; he substitutes disciplined, defeasible warrant - the fallibilist position a refined Carvaka could accept.

(c) Which Samkhya proof for the existence of Prakrti actually shows that there can be only one Prakrti? Justify your answer. [15 marks]
Model answer: The proof is avibhagat vaisvarupyasya - the argument from the undivided source of the diversified universe. Samkhya offers five inferences for primordial material nature. From the finitude and determinateness of things (bhedanam parimanat) it argues that the limited presupposes a subtler ground. From the uniformity running through effects (samanvayat) it argues from coordinated diversity to a common material basis. From operation due to causal power (saktitah pravrttes ca, the standard reading of Samkhyakarika 15) it argues that determinate production requires a potent source. From the distinction between cause and effect (karana-karya-vibhagat) it argues that manifest effects imply a subtler unmanifest cause. Each of these four is compatible with a plurality of material roots. Several reservoirs could each be subtler than their products, each display internal pattern, each possess productive power, and each differ in stage from its effects. None of the four takes unity as its premise, so none can deliver unity as its conclusion. The fifth argument alone does. It begins from the observation that cosmic diversity is not a heap but one interconnected whole, and reasons that a diversified yet undivided cosmos points back to an original source in which later differentiations are not yet separated. Admitting many disconnected primal materials would therefore destroy the very undividedness from which the inference proceeds. Justification, stated as a bridge: the premise of proof five is the world's undivided interconnectedness, so plurality at the root would falsify the premise and not merely complicate the conclusion. The verdict holds within Samkhya's own inferential licence; a Nyaya atomist declines it by tracing interconnectedness to coordination among many causes rather than to a single origin.

## Philosophy-II

Time: 3 Hours | Maximum marks: 250

### Q1 (Compulsory)
(a) Explain the distinction between a claim-right and a liberty, and show why the distinction matters for the relation between the individual and the state. [10 marks]
Model answer: The distinction. A claim-right is a justified claim whose correlative is a duty in a determinate other: my right against assault is your duty not to assault me. A liberty or privilege is a freedom to act whose correlative is merely a no-right in others: nobody may demand that I desist, but nobody is bound to assist me either. Why it matters. First, it disciplines rights-talk. Claims of the form "I have a right to X" are inflated whenever no bearer of a corresponding duty can be named. Second, it fixes what the state owes: liberties require forbearance, while claim-rights may require provision, protection and remedy, which is a far heavier institutional demand. Verdict. The distinction therefore separates what the state must merely not do from what it must actually do - and confusing the two produces either hollow entitlements or an unbounded expectation of provision.

(b) "Corrupt practices reveal an inherent tension between particularistic and universalistic normative standards." Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons and justification for your answer. [10 marks]
Model answer: Position. I agree, and the weight falls on "inherent": the tension is ineliminable because it lies between domains, not between rival moralities. The two standards. Particularistic norms weight family, caste, friendship or patronage; universalistic norms require impartial application of public criteria. Why particular duties are genuine. This step makes the tension inherent. Personal relations generate real duties, not bias to be suppressed; were they mere temptation there would be no tension, only weakness of will. The four-step argument. Personal relations generate genuine duties; office is entrusted for a specified common purpose; discharging private loyalty through office costs unrepresented citizens; corruption occurs when a legitimate loyalty is acted upon in a role governed by universal standards. The characterisation. Corruption is therefore a category error of role executed for advantage - a valid norm imported into a domain that excludes it. The remedy that follows. Not the abolition of personal loyalty but the definition of role boundaries, conflicts of interest and recusal - role-differentiation rather than the suppression of duty. Limit. Boundaries work cleanly for discrete decisions and poorly for pervasive influence, informal networks and agenda-shaping, where recusal has nothing to attach to. Verdict.

(c) "Merit is a moral entitlement." Examine this claim. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The claim conflates two criteria operating in opposite temporal directions, and it is false as stated. The distinction. Desert is backward-looking and moralised: it tracks what a person has done, sacrificed or suffered, and governs honours, recognition, praise and blame. Merit is forward-looking and functional: it tracks who will discharge a role well, and governs offices and appointments. A candidate may have great desert and little merit, or great merit and no desert. Why the conflation matters. Treating merit as a moral entitlement converts a claim about fitness for a task into a claim about what is owed. The strongest objection to merit itself. Measured merit encodes prior access to nutrition, language, coaching and networks, so a selection score is partly a record of others' investment. Rawls presses further: natural talent, family advantage and even the capacity for effort are morally arbitrary, since nobody deserves the starting position from which effort is exerted. The reply. Rawls does not conclude that desert is meaningless. Legitimate expectations generated within just institutions remain valid, and honour and gratitude remain intelligible responses to effort. What collapses is desert's claim to be foundational. Verdict.

(d) Compare socialism and communism as two distinct political ideologies. [10 marks]
Model answer: Declare the vocabulary first. In Marxist usage "socialism" often names a transitional lower phase; in wider political theory it names the whole family. This answer uses the wider sense, and the distinction is drawn against communism as the Marxian higher phase. Four axes. Property: socialism means social control in varied forms - public, cooperative, guild, worker or mixed; communism means common control with classes abolished. State: socialism may retain a democratic and active state; communism expects the political state, as an instrument of class domination, to lose that function. Distribution: socialism may use contribution, need or mixed principles; communism distributes according to need in the higher phase. Method: socialism may be revolutionary or gradual; communism is reached through transition. Why they are distinct in kind. Socialism is a family admitting utopian, Marxian, Fabian, guild, market and Gandhian members, and leaves the state, the distributive principle and the method open. Communism specifies all three. A doctrine that leaves questions open is not a milder version of one that closes them. Control. Neither is identical with social democracy, which retains private property under regulation, operates a welfare-regulatory state and proceeds by constitutional reform. Verdict.

(e) Do you agree that the rights concerning land and property have empowered women? Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: Reading the stem. The verb is "have empowered", so this is a conversion question. Establishing that such rights exist answers a question that was not asked. The record, classified. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, is an enacted amendment giving daughters in a Mitakshara joint family coparcenary status by birth on the statutory terms. Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) is a Supreme Court judgment clarifying that right, including that the father need not have been alive at commencement. Together they settle entitlement and interpretation. Why that is not empowerment. Four conversion failures, none of them a defect in the law: waiver pressure, framed as family loyalty; informal partition, keeping the division outside the record; record practices, maintained by the social order the law was meant to change; and fear of family rupture, which attaches a relational price to claiming. Where the failures sit. All four operate at the access, control, return and exit levels - inside the household. That is why the property question becomes the public/private question. The concession. The recognition gain is real and independent: equal inheritance marks daughters as full members rather than temporary dependants, and that status change does not wait on possession. Verdict.

### Q2
(a) "Human rights and human dignity would no longer be the product of a particular culture, rather a common human aspiration for an ideal world." Discuss. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The statement is defensible in the precise form it uses: human rights express a common human aspiration, not the property of one civilisation - provided universality of aspiration is not confused with uniformity of institutional articulation. The universalist claim. Human-rights theory holds that certain rights belong to all human beings simply as human beings. Its argument is that moral concern can be universalised beyond citizenship, religion and culture, and its ground is dignity, equal worth and protection against cruelty, arbitrariness and degradation. Its presupposition is a minimum moral horizon common to humanity. The relativist challenge. Conceptions of personhood, community, authority and obligation differ across traditions, and the vocabulary of individual claim-rights is historically particular in its formulation. Traditions organised around role, duty and reciprocity may reasonably resist translation into an idiom not their own. Adjudicating the tension. The two positions answer different questions. On the ground of dignity, the universalist holds that every human has equal worth while the relativist holds that dignity is interpreted through culture; the defensible line is that core protections may be universal while institutional expression varies. On rights language, the historical point about Western formulation is conceded; universality of aspiration need not imply uniformity of articulation. On community against individual, rights can be read relationally - as protections of persons within relationships - without losing protective force. The Indian dharma idiom illustrates this: it condemns cruelty and enjoins restraint while beginning from obligation rather than claim, which shows that the aspiration is not idiom-bound. Verdict. The stem is right that dignity is a common aspiration rather than a cultural product, and wrong if read as requiring one institutional form for all. The residual difficulty is genuine: which protections belong to the non-negotiable minimum, and who fixes the boundary, remain disputed - but a universality that can be waived by cultural appeal would protect nobody, and that is the one concession analysis should refuse.

(b) "Duties are of the nature of obligation while Rights are of the nature of entitlement. Therefore there is no necessary connection between the two." Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons and justification for your answer. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. I agree with the statement's premise and reject its inference. Obligation and entitlement are genuinely distinct concepts; but analytic distinguishability does not entail the absence of a necessary connection. Conceding the premise. Duties are obligations to do, avoid, respect or support; rights are justified claims, liberties, powers or immunities. The two are analytically distinguishable, which is why we have two words rather than one. Refusing the inference - the Hohfeldian split. "Right" names four juridical positions, each with its own correlative: a claim-right correlates with a duty; a liberty correlates only with a no-right in others; a power correlates with liability; an immunity correlates with disability. Therefore correlativity is strict for claim-rights - my right against assault simply is your duty not to assault me - and absent for liberties, since a liberty imposes no positive duty of assistance. The connection is conceptual for one class of rights and institutional for the rest. Why the connection is not dispensable. Rights are socially actionable, not isolated metaphysical ornaments. A legal right to remedy implies institutions' duty to hear claims. Two consequences follow: entitlements without correlative obligations remain empty, and obligations without any corresponding claims risk authoritarianism. The concepts are distinguishable but not socially separable. The strongest point on the other side. For liberties the correlativity thesis really is false as stated, so a candidate who claims that every right entails a duty has overreached. The honest position concedes this rather than defending an untenable universal. Verdict. Not agreed as a whole: the premise is sound, the inference invalid. Logical strictness does not hold for every use of "right", but normative necessity does - a right-worthy order requires duties somewhere in the background, and the two are inseparable in practice even where they are separable in analysis.

(c) "Guilt is individual; responsibility admits of collective forms." Critically examine this claim in the context of mass violence. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. The claim is correct and is the most important control in this area, because "collective responsibility" conceals at least five different claims. The five kinds, distinguished. Criminal guilt - this person planned, ordered, incited or committed; defensible, and attaches to individuals only. Organisational liability - a body with decision procedures and a chain of command answers for what it decided; defensible where the body has genuine agency. Political responsibility - members of a polity bear responsibility for what is done in their name and by their institutions; partly defensible, grounding reparation, memory and reform, never guilt. Moral responsibility for omission - bystanders who could have acted at acceptable cost bear some responsibility; defensible but graded by knowledge, capacity and risk. Metaphysical or inherited guilt - guilt by ancestry or membership; rejected, because it is the very logic that generates atrocity. Why differentiation is required. Mass violence fragments agency through dehumanisation, moral exclusion, diffusion of responsibility, bureaucratic routine and suppressed dissent. Arendt's account of wrongdoing performed through cliche, careerism and failure of judgment describes that setting exactly - but concerns the psychology of perpetration, not the degree of guilt, and must be stated so, or it reads as an excuse. Critical examination. Objection: political responsibility is incoherent, since citizens who opposed the policy, who were children, or who had no effective voice cannot owe reparation merely by membership. Reply: the category is grounded in membership of an ongoing institutional order whose acts were done in the members' name and whose liabilities they inherit - a structure familiar from corporate and successor-state obligation - and it yields duties to repair, remember and reform rather than blame. Residue: the reply is weakest for those who had no effective voice, on whom the duty may fall most heavily despite their being least able to prevent the wrong. Verdict.

### Q3
(a) "Punishment is a constrained practice, not a free exercise of state power." Discuss with reference to corruption, mass violence and capital punishment. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Punishment is legitimate only as a constrained practice: its aim may be forward-looking, but its application is bound by legality, culpability, proportionality, dignity, procedure, equality and a preserved route back to membership. The three applications test those constraints differently. The constraints, stated. A defensible punishment presupposes a public law known in advance; a voluntary act or relevant omission; proportionate culpability; fair procedure and reliable proof; a competent and impartial authority; proportionality between wrong, responsibility and sanction; and equal application without status bias. Each blocks a specific abuse, and naming the abuse converts the list into an argument. Corruption - pressure on the concept of a public wrong. Victims are diffuse and no assault occurs, so the harm-based case for criminalisation is weak. The constraint holds because the wrong is the betrayal of entrusted office and the displacement of public rules by private access - wrongs surviving where no measurable loss is shown. Remedy follows diagnosis: attack monopoly, discretion and impunity, with certainty of detection as the operative lever. Mass violence - pressure on attribution. Division of labour, bureaucratic routine and diffused responsibility fragment agency, tempting an answer either to acquit everyone below the leadership or to convict a community. The constraints forbid both: guilt is individual, while responsibility admits collective forms - organisational liability, political responsibility grounding reparation, memory and reform, and graded responsibility for omission - inherited guilt being rejected outright. Arendt's thesis is psychology, not a reduction of guilt. Capital punishment - pressure on every constraint at once. The penalty is irreversible and eliminates the agent whose responsibility, capacity for reform and rights grounded the constraints. Desert may be granted and still cannot be reliably administered through a fallible, unequally resourced process; incapacitation fails necessity once secure imprisonment exists; deterrence is contested, and the burden under uncertainty lies with whoever proposes the irreversible penalty. Bachan Singh (1980) is a judgment, Law Commission Report No. 262 (2015) an advisory report. Objection and reply. Objection: so many constraints leave the state unable to punish effectively. Reply: they do not reduce the capacity to punish the guilty; they reduce the capacity to punish conveniently - which is their whole point. Verdict. Punishment is constrained or it is power: corruption tests whether a public wrong needs a visible victim, mass violence whether responsibility survives fragmentation, and capital punishment whether any constraint holds where error is uncorrectable.

(b) Compare Berlin and Green on the meaning of liberty, and assess whether their positions can be reconciled. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Berlin and Green fear different failures, and their positions are reconcilable at the level of institutional design while remaining opposed at the level of authorisation. The axis of dispute. The question is not how much freedom each wants but what destroys it. Berlin's anxiety is coercion exercised in the name of a higher freedom; Green's is formal liberty accompanied by no power to use it. Berlin at full strength. Negative liberty asks within what area a person is left alone; positive liberty asks who governs her and by what self she is directed. Berlin does not deny the appeal of self-mastery; he identifies the step that makes it dangerous - the split between a "real self" and an "empirical self", after which a ruler may claim to speak for the first and present coercion as liberation. His background commitment is value pluralism: human goods are many and not always harmonisable. Green at full strength. Liberty is the positive power to do or enjoy something worth doing or enjoying, tied to self-realisation. Mere non-interference is hollow where persons are crippled by ignorance, addiction, destitution or social subordination, so coercion is not freedom's only enemy: disabling conditions also matter. Education is the standing example, because it equips agency rather than merely leaving persons alone. The reconciliation. The two are compatible if positive freedom is read as enabling rather than perfectionist: institutions may enlarge a person's effective capacity while leaving the choice of ends with her. On that reading Green supplies what Berlin's negative pole omits, and Berlin supplies the constraint Green's programme needs. Where reconciliation fails. It fails at authorisation. If any agency is empowered to identify what is "worth doing", the real-self structure reappears, and no agreed test separates enablement from prescription in contested cases. Verdict.

(c) Are Marxian Socialism and individual freedom consistent ? Discuss critically. [15 marks]
Model answer: Fix the concept of freedom first. The stem is decided by which conception is in play. For Marx, freedom is non-domination together with self-development, not merely the absence of interference; the question "freedom from whom, and for what activity?" is therefore prior. The case for consistency. Marx criticises equal legal right where formally identical rules operate over radically unequal social conditions: formal liberty coexists with structural dependence when workers lack independent access to the means of production. On this account collective ownership is not a restriction on freedom but its material precondition, since it removes the standing power of some over others' work and life-chances. The case against. Collective ownership specifies who does not own; it does not by itself specify who decides. Where "collective" power becomes bureaucratic command, the worker exchanges a private superior for an official one, and the standing subjection that the concept of freedom was introduced to remove reappears intact. M. N. Roy states the internal criticism precisely: dictatorship cannot reliably serve emancipation, because concentrated power reproduces domination. The conditional verdict. Marxian socialism and individual freedom are consistent only if collective ownership enlarges real self-development and democratic control. If it does not, the emancipatory claim defeats itself. Residual. Marx supplies no institutional mechanism guaranteeing the condition, and the Critique of the Gotha Programme two-phase account is a normative projection rather than a demonstrated law.

### Q4
(a) Discuss whether Amartya Sen's idea of justice is an improvement upon Rawl's theory of justice. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. "Improvement" is criterion-relative. Judged by reach into realised lives, Sen improves on Rawls; judged by institutional determinacy and the protection of basic liberties by rule, Rawls retains the advantage. The criterion must be stated before the verdict. Rawls at full strength. Principles are chosen in the original position behind a veil of ignorance, yielding equal basic liberties with lexical priority, then fair equality of opportunity, then the difference principle. The construction answers the aggregation objection: no one may be sacrificed for a larger total, because no chooser can gamble on occupying someone else's position. Sen's criticism. Sen distinguishes niti - the correctness of institutions, rules and procedures - from nyaya, justice as actually realised in people's lives. A society may have fine rules on paper and produce grave injustice in practice. He therefore rejects transcendental institutionalism: we can identify manifest injustices and rank alternatives without first agreeing on a perfectly just basic structure. The metric. Sen replaces primary goods with capability - what persons are actually able to do and to be - because disability, age, gender, climate, health and social position change how resources convert into functioning. Equal income does not yield equal mobility or voice. First objection and reply. Comparative judgement without a benchmark may under-determine the choice between two rival improvements. Sen answers that public reasoning and manifest injustice supply enough orientation, and that demanding a complete ideal before acting is itself a source of paralysis. Second objection and reply. Capability comparison is measurement-heavy and open-ended. Sen prefers reasoned public assessment to spurious exactness, deliberately declining a rigid final list - a defensible choice for plural societies that nonetheless costs determinacy. What Rawls still does better. Institutions remain the indispensable precondition of any realised justice, and the lexical priority rule protects liberty by rule rather than by ongoing judgement. Verdict. Sen improves on Rawls in diagnosis and reach and does not displace him in institutional design; the honest ruling is that the two are complementary, with priority decided by the criterion invoked.

(b) Discuss gender as a cultural category as opposed to sex as a biological category. [15 marks]
Model answer: How to read "opposed". As contrasted, not partitioned. The two are categories of different things - bodily and reproductive characteristics on one side, socially produced roles, expectations and identities on the other - but they interact, since pregnancy, illness and ageing are at once bodily and socially organised. The contrast, stated. Sex names bodily and reproductive characteristics, with the qualification that biological variation is more complex than a rigid social binary. Gender names socially produced roles and identities. Gender hierarchy names the patterned ranking of masculinised over feminised roles - and not every difference is discriminatory; unjust subordination is the issue. How a cultural category acquires force - six channels. Family allocates food, education, inheritance and mobility. Language supplies the unmarked default. Labour decides which work is paid and which is "natural". Law governs succession, guardianship and marriage. Media fix the repertoire of imaginable lives. Sanction - ridicule, exclusion, coercion - attaches a cost to departure. The sixth channel is what converts a norm from a description into a constraint. Construction is not unreality. Money, citizenship and caste are socially constructed and all three are enforceable. Construction is a claim about origin, not about strength. The depth marker. Butler presses further: the categories through which bodies are read as sexed are themselves produced within a gendered discourse, so gender is sustained by compelled reiteration rather than expressed from an inner essence. The category-stability objection and the strategic-essentialism reply must travel with it, and the exchange is live. Verdict. Gender is cultural in origin and material in effect: made by institutions, and distributing opportunity, rights and resources as reliably as any natural law would have done.

(c) Distinguish civil disobedience from conscientious objection, resistance and revolution, and examine whether a constitutional democracy can consistently accommodate any of them. [15 marks]
Model answer: The four rungs. Conscientious objection aims to avoid personal complicity, may be private, seeks exemption and typically accepts the penalty, and accepts the system's legitimacy. Civil disobedience aims to persuade the majority, is essentially public, breaches a specific law with acceptance of legal consequences, and appeals to the system's own principles. Resistance aims to obstruct a specific grave injustice, may be covert, may refuse the penalty, and denies the legitimacy of the acts obstructed. Revolution aims to replace the constitutional order, is organised and often armed, and denies the system's legitimacy wholesale. The two defining features of civil disobedience. Publicity with appeal to shared principles, and acceptance of the legal penalty - the second being what demonstrates that the actor contests one law rather than repudiating law as such. Non-violence is standardly added; on the Gandhian account it is constitutive, since means and ends are inseparable. Can a constitutional democracy accommodate them? It can accommodate the first two without contradiction, because both accept the order's legitimacy and neither claims a legal right to disobey. No legal order can recognise a right to civil disobedience, since acceptance of the penalty is part of the concept. In India, Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b) protect protest subject to reasonable restrictions; they do not protect law-breaking, and Article 51A is non-justiciable. Verdict. Accommodation is moral and political rather than legal: the order tolerates and learns from penalty-accepting dissent, while resistance and revolution answer to progressively heavier burdens of justification. Ambedkar's objection stands wherever constitutional channels are genuinely open to the group in question.

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) Write an essay on Spinoza's notion of God and His attributes. [10 marks]
Model answer: Spinoza rejects the creator-creation dualism of classical theism. God is the one absolutely infinite substance, expressed by Deus sive Natura - "God, or Nature." Substance exists in itself and is conceived through itself; because an infinite substance excludes any rival of the same attribute, only God possesses full ontological independence. An attribute is what intellect perceives as constituting the essence of substance. God has infinitely many attributes, although humans know Thought and Extension. These are not two substances; each expresses the same divine reality. Minds, bodies and events are dependent modes. No finite mode is the whole God. God is immanent, not a transcendent craftsman. Natura naturans is active self-caused nature; natura naturata is the necessary modal order. God does not deliberate among possible worlds. Freedom means acting from one's own nature without external constraint. This secures unity and necessity but abandons personal providence and libertarian choice. Spinoza's God is metaphysically supreme, though not the personal deity of classical religion.

(b) "Toleration granted by the strong to the weak is permission, not equality." Examine. (Answer in about 150 words.) [10 marks]
Model answer: The statement is correct, and its force comes from the power component in the concept of toleration itself. Toleration requires three things: an objection to what is tolerated, reasons that outweigh without cancelling that objection, and the power to interfere combined with deliberate restraint. The third condition means that toleration is always exercised by someone who could have done otherwise. Forst's ladder makes the consequence explicit. Permission is the powerful allowing a minority to exist on sufferance; coexistence is a pragmatic modus vivendi; respect is reciprocal recognition as equal moral-political persons; esteem positively values the other's beliefs. Only respect and esteem are compatible with equal citizenship, and most historical toleration was permission. Vivekananda reaches the same conclusion from the other direction, demanding acceptance rather than toleration because toleration still condescends. Toleration is thus indispensable - the objection component makes it a moral achievement - and insufficient.

(c) Explain why classical Mimamsa is often regarded as the sharpest counterexample to the claim that religion requires God. [10 marks]
Model answer: Because it removes God from a tradition that keeps everything else religion is usually thought to need: scripture, ritual, duty, merit and a promised fruit. Three functions a deity normally performs are reassigned. Authority: the Veda is authorless (apauruseya), so no speaker's reliability needs establishing, and the theistic arguments from the text's status lose their premise. Duty: Jaimini's Mimamsa-sutra 1.1.2 defines dharma as what is characterised by injunction and is beneficial, so dharma is known from injunction alone, not from any deity's command. Desert: unseen potency (apurva) is postulated to connect a perished momentary act with a much later fruit, answering the Nyaya demand for a superintendent of karma. The deities named in the mantras are elements within the ritual structure - the dative of the offering - rather than agents who hear and grant. Two qualifications are owed. Nyaya objects that an unconscious potency cannot select the right fruit for the right agent; Mimamsa replies that the relation is law-like rather than deliberative, which the objector must then argue against. And later authors of the school drift toward theism, so the atheistic reading is of the classical system.

(d) Does the doctrine of divine simplicity strengthen or weaken the notion of a personal God? Give reasons for your answer. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Non-composition (divine simplicity) strengthens the metaphysics of a personal God and weakens its religious grammar; the verdict must therefore be graded rather than flat. The doctrine. Simplicity denies every composition in God - matter and form, substance and accident, essence and existence - so that wisdom, goodness and power name one identical reality under different creaturely modes of conception. Aseity, the claim that God derives nothing, is its close companion. The strengthening reason. Whatever is composite depends on its parts and on whatever unites them; a first, underived ground cannot be dependent. Simplicity therefore secures precisely the non-derivation that makes God worth calling ultimate rather than merely greatest. The costs. Two objections bite. Modal collapse: if God is identical with the divine act of will and exists necessarily, whatever is willed appears necessary, so creation loses contingency. Plantinga's objection in Does God Have a Nature? presses that if God is identical with a property, then God is a property, and no property is an agent who knows or loves. Verdict. That narrowing is the real cost.

(e) Is there any compatibility between 'Freedom of Will' and 'Omnipotent God'? Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: Omnipotence should mean the power to actualize every logically possible state of affairs consistent with divine nature, not constant exercise of exhaustive control. Human freedom, minimally, requires that some actions issue from the agent rather than from external compulsion. On these definitions, compatibility is possible. A creator may endow persons with secondary causal agency and refrain from determining each choice. Such self-restraint is an exercise of power: permission differs from inability to control. Moral responsibility presupposes this sphere. The difficulty is sharper if freedom requires the categorical ability to do otherwise. An omnipotent God could override every choice; moreover, infallible divine foreknowledge appears to close alternatives. The standard reply distinguishes knowledge from causation and, on a timeless model, denies that God first predicts and then fixes the act. Yet non-causal certainty may still threaten alternative possibilities. Thus omnipotence and agency are conditionally compatible if divine power can sustain non-coerced secondary causes. Libertarian freedom remains difficult when omniscience and providence are added.

### Q6
(a) All evil is either sin or punishment for sin." - St. Augustine. Critically discuss. [20 marks]
Model answer: Augustine's sentence partitions all evil into sin and its punishment. The metaphysics behind it must be stated before it is tested. Being, insofar as it is being, is good; God creates beings, not evil substances; corruption is parasitic, depending on a good nature to corrupt; and moral fault is disordered willing. Evil is therefore privation of a due good (privatio boni), not a rival creative principle. Privation does not make pain unreal: rot is not a rival substance to wood but the real loss of the wood's proper integrity, and blindness is a determinate lack in a creature whose nature calls for sight. On this basis the partition works as follows. Moral evil is sin - the misuse of created freedom. Natural evil is punishment - the consequence of a fallen, disordered order following the Fall. The critical half has four parts. First, innocent suffering: infants and the congenitally afflicted fit a retributive reading poorly. Second, animal and pre-human suffering: no free-will defence and no retributive account applies to a creature that neither chooses nor sins, and a cosmic fall preceding human history is historically and scientifically strained. Third, the metaphysics of privation is strained for intense positive pain, which does not feel like an absence. Fourth, and most sharply, inherited guilt: the claim that later persons are personally guilty for an ancestor's act is far harder to defend than the claim that they inherit a damaged condition and a disordered social-natural order. The second reading reduces the objection but still owes an account of why a good creator permits the condition and its unequal effects. Two comparators sharpen the verdict. Hick's soul-making replaces punishment with development: humans are created immature, at an epistemic distance, in a law-governed world where courage and compassion become possible. Indian karma replaces a single Fall with a beginningless series and thereby answers the distribution question - why this person - that Augustine cannot; its own cost is unverifiability and the risk of victim-blaming. Verdict: treating the printed sentence as the object of analysis rather than as a certified translation , the thesis succeeds for moral evil and preserves divine goodness in creation, but it is incomplete for natural, innocent and animal suffering, where a developmental or distributional account does more work.

(b) Explain the main tenets of the Process Theodicy as an explanation of the problem of evil. [15 marks]
Model answer: Process theodicy answers evil by revising omnipotence, not by justifying permission. Its tenets are six. (1) Creativity, not God, is the ultimate; actual entities are self-creative occasions of experience. (2) God is dipolar: the primordial nature is the ordered envisagement of possibilities, supplying each occasion its initial aim; the consequent nature receives and preserves what the world becomes. (3) Power is intrinsically distributed: to be actual is to possess some self-determination. (4) Divine power is therefore persuasive by metaphysical necessity - for Griffin, God cannot coerce, which is what separates process theodicy from the free-will defence, where God chooses not to intervene. (5) Evil is the deviation of finite occasions from the divine lure; God is not culpable because God could not unilaterally prevent it. (6) God is "the fellow-sufferer who understands" (Whitehead) - solidarity, not detachment. The layers must be named separately. Whitehead, Process and Reality (1929), supplies the dipolar God, the initial aim and the consequent nature. Hartshorne, The Divine Relativity (1948) and Omnipotence and Other Theological Mistakes (1984), argues that classical omnipotence is incoherent: to have all the power is to leave none to anything else. Griffin, God, Power, and Evil (1976) and Evil Revisited (1991), supplies the metaphysical-necessity move. Cobb's A Christian Natural Theology systematises the theology. Criticism: a God who cannot guarantee victory over evil may be unworthy of worship; the metaphysics is contested; and classical theists deny that actuality entails self-determination. The replies are divine solidarity and the objective immortality of achieved value in the consequent nature. Verdict: it purchases God's innocence at the price of God's sovereignty - and the trade must be stated, not concealed.

(c) If God is the Absolute Creator, then the responsibility of the evil cannot belong to the human agent. Critically examine. [15 marks]
Model answer: The stem asserts a conditional: absolute creation entails that human agents cannot be responsible for evil. Its scope must be fixed first - it concerns moral evil, since no one attributes an earthquake to a human agent. Three replies resist the conditional. First, the free-will defence: God creates the capacity for choice, while the agent supplies the act. Significant freedom entails genuine alternatives, and God cannot causally determine a creature to freely choose rightly - the description cancels itself. A world of programmed beneficence lacks the moral authorship of a world containing genuinely responsible agents. Second, Augustine's privation account: God creates natures, all of which are good as created; evil is a privation of a due good arising in a defective will, so what God creates and what goes wrong are not the same item. Third, foreknowledge is not causation: knowing what an agent will freely do does not make the agent do it. A fourth route rejects the antecedent. Process theism denies that God has the unilateral power the stem presupposes: power is intrinsically distributed, so no being determines another's becoming. The critical half must concede what survives. Even granting that God does not cause the act, knowingly choosing to instantiate the whole system, foreseeing its outcomes, remains morally relevant. Foreknowledge does not cause free action, but it does not discharge the decision to create. Verdict: the conditional is false as stated - creaturely authorship is preserved - but it is not empty. It correctly identifies a residual divine responsibility for the system, which the free-will defence answers by pricing rather than by denying.

### Q7
(a) Examine whether the epistemology of deep disagreement strengthens or weakens the case for religious pluralism. (Answer in about 350 words.) [20 marks]
Model answer: It does both, and the balance is what makes it examinable. The apparatus. Within a shared framework, the dispute is between conciliationism or equal weight (Feldman, Christensen), on which discovering peer disagreement should reduce confidence, and steadfastness (Kelly, van Inwagen), on which one may retain a belief because one's own reasoning is part of one's evidence; the independence principle - assess the disagreement without relying on the reasoning under dispute - is what conciliationism needs and steadfastness denies. Deep disagreement, in Fogelin's technical sense (1985), goes further: the parties do not share the framework propositions - Wittgenstein's hinge commitments in On Certainty - against which evidence would count, so the same evidence is not evidence for the same thing. Lynch extends the analysis to disagreement about epistemic principles themselves, where any defence is circular. Why religion is a paradigm case. The parties differ over the sources of evidence, the criteria of adequacy - salvation, liberation, cessation - and the framework in which any datum would count. Where it weakens pluralism. Two consequences cut against it. Exclusivism cannot be convicted of irrationality merely for persisting, which removes the easiest charge against it and gives Plantinga's third reply its technical backing. And pluralism cannot be established merely by pointing at persistence - the persistence that seemed to support Hick's first premise is now explained without any pluralist assumption. Where it strengthens pluralism. Two further consequences cut in its favour. The failure of convergence is predicted rather than scandalous, so it is weak evidence for relativism. And what can be rationally required are the procedural virtues - fallibilism, humility, epistemic charity, and willingness to make one's framework explicit - which is precisely the ethic pluralism recommends. Jain conditional predication is the most developed attempt to make framework-relativity explicit inside the proposition. The verdict. Deep disagreement weakens the case for pluralism as a demonstrated metaphysics and strengthens it as an ethic of engagement. Unresolvability is epistemic, not alethic: one party may still be right, and what fails is our capacity to show which. That is a smaller claim than Hick's, and it is more defensible.

(b) Does the concept of God entail the existence of God? Examine from the perspective of ontological argument. [15 marks]
Model answer: The ontological argument claims a unique entailment: that the very concept of God contains, and therefore yields, God's existence. Anselm defines God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived", where "greater" means more perfect. In Proslogion 2 he argues by reductio: if this being existed only in the understanding, a greater could be conceived - namely the same being existing in reality as well - which contradicts the definition. In Proslogion 3 the claim is strengthened: a being that cannot be thought not to exist is greater than one that can; God therefore exists necessarily, by aseity. Descartes recasts this through essence-containment: existence belongs to a supremely perfect being as three angles belong to a triangle. The entailment fails at a precise point. Gaunilo's perfect-island parody shows the form proves too much; Anselm replies that only God is defined with necessary, non-contingent existence, which an island by definition lacks. That reply protects the second form but shifts the burden onto the coherence of maximal greatness. Kant's objection is decisive: existence is not a real predicate. A hundred real thalers contain no more concept than a hundred possible ones; existence posits an object rather than enriching a concept. Chatterjee and Datta restate this in the Indian idiom of a hundred rupees imagined in one's pocket. Modal reconstructions retain necessary existence but transfer the whole weight to the possibility premise, which a parallel possibility-of-non-existence can offset. Verdict: the concept entails existence hypothetically, not actually. Conceptual analysis yields a conditional, never an instantiation.

(c) Does religious pluralism require abandoning the claim that any religion possesses absolute truth? Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Pluralism begins from durable disagreement among traditions that are internally complex and supported by serious forms of life. Exclusivism preserves determinate truth but struggles to explain morally and spiritually impressive outsiders. Inclusivism recognises them while interpreting their achievements through one tradition's categories. Hick's pluralism distinguishes the ineffable Real from its culturally conditioned appearances, allowing several traditions to mediate salvific transformation. Critics object that Hick creates a higher-order theory that overrides the self-understanding of each religion and makes the Real too indeterminate to guide belief. Pluralism nevertheless need not mean that contradictory doctrines are equally true. It can combine fallibilism, dialogue and comparative judgment: claims remain answerable to coherence, evidence, moral fruit and their capacity to illuminate experience. Absolute certainty should be abandoned, but truth itself need not be. A defensible pluralism treats traditions as corrigible approaches to reality, permits genuine disagreement and rejects both coercive monopoly and an indiscriminate relativism. It also requires reciprocal openness: each tradition must permit its own interpretation to be corrected through encounter with others. Dialogue then becomes an epistemic practice, not merely a strategy of civic tolerance, while constitutional neutrality protects the conditions under which such inquiry can occur. Pluralism is strongest when it combines humility about possession of truth with seriousness about pursuing truth.

### Q8
(a) Present a critical exposition of different arguments offered by St. Thomas Aquinas to prove the existence of God also known as 'Five Ways'. Which one of them do you find philosophically most interesting? Give reasons in support of your answer. [20 marks]
Model answer: Aquinas' Five Ways (ST I, q.2, a.3) are five distinct a posteriori arguments, not one argument restated. Each begins from a general feature of the world and concludes with the cautious formula "and this everyone understands to be God" - itself an admission that further work is needed to identify the terminus with the God of religion. Way 1 argues from motion, understood as the reduction of potentiality to actuality: whatever is moved is moved by another, and an essentially ordered series of movers cannot regress infinitely, so there is an unmoved mover. Way 2 argues from an order of efficient causes to a first efficient cause. Way 3 argues from contingency: if everything were merely possible, at some time nothing would have existed, and nothing would exist now; hence there is a necessary being, and ultimately one necessary of itself. Way 4 argues from degrees of goodness and truth to a maximum which causes all perfection. Way 5 argues from the regular goal-directed activity of things lacking cognition to a directing intelligence, as the arrow is directed by the archer. Critically, the excluded regress is essentially ordered - the stick moves the stone only while the hand moves the stick - so "first" denotes explanatory priority, not chronological firstness. Aquinas himself concedes the difficulty of excluding an endless series of events, and holds that the world's temporal beginning is known by faith, not demonstration; neo-Thomists accordingly recast the excluded series as simultaneous dependence. Way 3 is vulnerable to a quantifier shift at the step from "each thing at some time is not" to "at some time nothing is". Way 4 requires the Platonic machinery of participation, since degrees of heat do not entail a hottest object. Way 5 is exposed to Hume's analogy objection and, in Paley's later form, to Darwin. Kant's overarching charge is that the passage from "a necessary being" to "the most real being" is the ontological argument in disguise. Most interesting: Way 3. It alone poses the deepest question - why is there anything at all rather than nothing? - and it is the only Way whose central premise, the Principle of Sufficient Reason, remains a live philosophical dispute against brute-fact naturalism. Its conclusion is also modally strongest: not merely a first mover but a being whose non-existence is impossible.

(b) Is morality autonomous from religion even when moral life is interpreted as obedience to God? Examine. [15 marks]
Model answer: Divine-command theory gives morality authority, motivation and an objective source, but the Euthyphro dilemma asks whether acts are right because God commands them or commanded because they are right. The first horn risks arbitrariness; the second recognises a standard intelligible apart from command. Modified theories appeal to God's necessarily good nature, yet judgments about goodness are still needed to understand that claim. Kant therefore locates obligation in rational autonomy: fear of punishment or hope of reward cannot constitute a good will. Religion may nevertheless deepen moral life by supplying narratives, communities, exemplars and practices of repentance. Indian traditions also separate the issues: Mimamsa grounds dharma in Vedic injunction without a creator, Buddhism in the causes of suffering, and the Gita joins duty with devotion. Morality is thus normatively autonomous because reasons for action must withstand rational and ethical scrutiny. It need not be culturally isolated from religion, which can interpret, motivate and sustain obligations without being their sole foundation. This distinction also protects believers: a purported command that licenses cruelty must be challenged through conscience, public reason and the deeper ethical resources of the tradition itself. Autonomy is therefore accountable moral judgment, not hostility to faith. Religious motivation remains valuable when it strengthens, rather than replaces, reasons that can be offered to affected persons.

(c) Explain the relation between the God and the Self according to Ramanujacarya. [15 marks]
Model answer: For Ramanuja, God is the supreme personal Brahman, while the individual self is a real, eternal and conscious jiva. Their relation avoids both non-dual identity and an external dualism of independent substances. Brahman is one organic whole qualified by cit, the plurality of selves, and acit, matter, and the world is its real transformation (parinama). The decisive concept is aprthaksiddhi, inseparable existence. The self is a genuine qualifier or mode (prakara) of Brahman. It is numerically distinct and morally responsible, yet it cannot exist or be understood independently of God. Ramanuja further expresses this through sarira-sariri-bhava: the jiva is God's body, while God is its indwelling Self (antaryamin). A body is controlled by, supported by and exists for its self. "Body" therefore indicates metaphysical dependence, not divine physical anatomy. This relation explains both agency and devotion. The jiva knows, acts and bears karma; God is the inner governor and gracious end of action, sustaining the capacity to act and consenting (anumati) to its exercise, so moral authorship remains the jiva's. Bondage obscures the full manifestation of the self's consciousness but does not make individuality illusory. Through right knowledge, disciplined action, bhakti and prapatti, the jiva receives grace, and in liberation enjoys communion and service while retaining its identity. Two contrasts sharpen the account. Against Samkara, the self is not Brahman but belongs to Brahman; against Madhva, whose reflection relation (bimba-pratibimba) keeps five eternal differences, Ramanuja's dependence is internal to one qualified whole. The model preserves unity, religious relation and plurality better than numerical identity, yet if every suffering self is God's body under divine control, responsibility for evil remains a serious pressure. The relation is best formulated as real distinction within complete, purposive dependence.
