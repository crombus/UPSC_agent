### Q1

B. Custom is excluded because only enacted measures are law.

**Answer: A**
Explanation: the option accurately separates Article 13's broad ordinary-law definition from the constitutional-amendment control path.
Trap: The question turns on two different meanings of law: Article 13 ordinary law and Article 368 constitutional amendment.

### B6. Artificial intelligence will reflect the institutions that govern it.

### B7. Water disputes reveal the geography of politics and the politics of geography.

### B8. A constitutional democracy must protect both the mandate and the minority.

### Q1 [10 marks]

Model answer (130 words): A fixed-coupon bond promises contractual cash flows. Its price equals their present value discounted at the required yield. ### Q14 [15 marks]

### Q16 [15 marks]

### Q20 [15 marks]
Explain the major sources of inflation in India and show how headline, core, food and fuel measures aid diagnosis.

Model answer (220 words): Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, but its policy meaning depends on source, breadth and persistence. Demand-pull inflation arises when aggregate spending exceeds available capacity; fiscal stimulus, rapid credit growth or strong purchasing power can close the output gap. Cost-push inflation follows higher wages, taxes, freight or input prices. Supply shocks arise from crop loss, storage and transport failures or administered-price changes. (a) "Percepts without concepts are blind and concepts without percepts are empty." In the light of this statement discuss how Kant reconciles rationalism with empiricism. [10 marks]
Model answer: Kant's own formulation is: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A51/B75). The question paraphrases intuition as percept; its point is the reciprocal dependence of sensible content and conceptual form. The two halves. Thoughts without content are empty corrects rationalism: a concept for which no corresponding intuition is even possible determines no object. Intuitions without concepts are blind corrects empiricism: an unsynthesised manifold is not yet perception of an object, since without a rule there is nothing to be right or wrong about. The reconciliation. Human cognition has two stems: sensibility, receptive, supplying content under the forms of space and time, and understanding, spontaneous, supplying rules through the categories. "The understanding can intuit nothing, and the senses can think nothing." Knowledge is their union. This is not a fifty-fifty compromise. Kant sides with the empiricist about the source of content and with the rationalist about the source of necessity, which is possible only because he has separated the two questions. Verdict. The reconciliation is bought by restricting the categories to possible experience - so it is simultaneously an anti-metaphysical verdict.

(b) "An ostensive definition can be variously interpreted in every case." Examine the significance of this remark for Wittgenstein's account of meaning. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The remark denies ostension the office it had been given - connecting language to the world from outside every practice - and removes the last refuge of the referential model of meaning. The argument. 28 shows that pointing at two nuts while saying "This is called 'two'" leaves the lesson undetermined: the word might be taken for a number, a colour, a shape, a material or a proper name. 29-30 show that the obvious repair - "this number is called two" - borrows an understanding of "number", so that "one has already to know (or be able to do) something in order to be capable of asking a thing's name". The explanations do not terminate in a self-explaining item. The positive condition. 31 supplies it through the chess-king: being shown the piece and told "This is the king" teaches its use only to someone who knows the shape of a game. The definition works "when the place is already prepared", and training (5-6) and correction prepare it. Significance.

(c) "Ideas are timeless and spaceless." Elucidate this statement with reference to Plato. [10 marks]
Model answer: Deriving the property. For Plato a Form (eidos, idea) is a non-material, self-identical universal - Beauty itself, not any beautiful thing. Two consequences follow directly. Being non-material, a Form has no magnitude, shape or position, so it is spaceless; the Form of Justice is not anywhere. Being self-identical, it cannot be generated, destroyed or altered, so it is timeless; it does not become more just or less just. Why it matters. Only what cannot be otherwise can be strictly known. Timeless and spaceless Forms are therefore the proper objects of knowledge (episteme), while spatio-temporal particulars, which both are and are not F, yield only opinion (doxa). This is the load-bearing link between Plato's epistemology and his metaphysics. Two cautions. "Intelligible realm" is a structural expression, not a second location - a spaceless entity cannot be somewhere. Nor are Forms concepts in minds, which would make the standard depend on the beings it corrects. Evaluation. The property is the theory's strength and its wound. What lifts Forms above flux also removes them from the spatio-temporal order they are supposed to explain, which is precisely Aristotle's charge at Metaphysics I.9 that separated Forms cannot cause change or account for generation.

(d) Distinguish Hegel's true infinite from the bad infinite, and explain why the distinction is required by Absolute Idealism. Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: The bad infinite. A spurious infinite (schlechte Unendlichkeit) is one that stands beyond the finite in an endless "and so on". Its defect is structural: because it is defined by excluding the finite, the finite supplies its border, and whatever has a border is limited. An infinite reached by exclusion is therefore finite. The true infinite. The genuine infinite includes finite self-limitation and return within itself. The finite is real as a moment - neither annihilated nor left standing outside as an independent remainder. Why Absolute Idealism requires it. If the Absolute were one object over against finite objects, it would be exactly a bad infinite, and Hegel's whole position would collapse into the two-world picture he rejects in Kant. The distinction is what licenses the claim that the Absolute is not a thing behind the world but the world grasped as a self-developing whole, and it is also what secures the reality of appearance: a whole that annihilated its moments would have restored the exclusion it was meant to overcome. Verdict. The distinction is the logical control behind the system; whether the finite retains genuine irreducibility inside it remains the standing objection.

(e) Can hallucination be regarded as an intentional act by Husserl? Explain. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Yes. On Husserl's account hallucination is a fully intentional act, and the reason exposes what intentionality is. The criterion. Intentionality, inherited from Brentano and transformed, is the thesis that every act of consciousness is consciousness of something. Its structure is the correlation of the noesis, the manner of intending, with the noema, the object precisely as intended. The criterion is directedness, not successful reference. The case. A hallucination has a perceptual-seeming noesis: it is lived in the manner of perceiving, not of imagining. It has a determinate noema - this shape, this colour, at this place, with unseen sides co-intended. Both members of the correlation are present. What is missing. Not structure but intuitive fulfilment. The empty intentions belonging to the object are not honoured, and the failure appears as disappointment when the further course of perception fails to concord. Ideas I 24 grounds cognition in originary givenness only within the limits in which it is given, so an unfulfilled intention is a legitimate object of description. Qualification. Saying yes does not make hallucination veridical. The veridical/non-veridical distinction is retained and re-described as a difference in fulfilment rather than in intentional form.

### Q2
(a) "Kant's Transcendental Deduction is the strongest anti-sceptical argument in modern philosophy and rests on its weakest scaffolding." Critically examine. [20 marks]
Model answer: The strength. The Deduction asks the sceptic to concede almost nothing: that his representations are his. Kant's premise is that "the I think must be able to accompany all my representations" (B131-2); otherwise something would be represented in me that could not be thought, and would be nothing for me. From that premise: combination "can never come to us through the senses", since sensibility is receptive and delivers a manifold but never its unity; every synthesis is an act of spontaneity. A manifold can belong to one consciousness only if combined in a determinate, repeatable way - that is, according to rules. Those rules are the categories, since a category just is the concept of a synthesis in accordance with a logical function of judgement. And an object is "that in the concept of which the manifold of a given intuition is united". Hence the categories are necessarily valid of every object of possible experience. This is anti-scepticism without a premise the sceptic can refuse. It simultaneously answers Hume, who sought the self among impressions and found none because the "I think" is a condition and not a content, and rational psychology, which converted the same formal unity into a substance. The weakness. The metaphysical deduction supplies the inventory: twelve categories read off twelve logical functions of judgement, on the premise that the understanding is a faculty of judging. Fichte, Hegel and later Kantians object that the derivation is artificial and the completeness claim rests entirely on the logic Kant inherited. Kant's defence - that the table of judgements is exhaustive and systematic, and that no more complete rival was offered - is a defence of the best available list, not a proof. Adjudication. The two are separable. The Deduction's strategy is that objectivity is a condition rather than a discovery; its inventory is a historical contingency. Strawson's austere reading detaches the strategy and preserves the result. There is a further dispute - whether the A and B versions argue differently or only differ in exposition - but it concerns route and emphasis rather than the core claim. Verdict. The statement is accurate as it stands. The argument survives the loss of the table; the table would not survive the loss of the argument. Its remaining cost is scope: the conclusion holds of objects as they can be given to us, so the Deduction secures objectivity within possible experience and leaves things in themselves untouched.

(b) "The Second Analogy defeats Hume at the level of entitlement but not at the level of knowledge." Examine this claim. [15 marks]
Model answer: The claim's first half. Hume argued that no impression of necessary connexion is available, so the concept of cause is a product of custom. Kant grants the analysis and denies the conclusion. His master premise is that time itself cannot be perceived (A176/B219), so objective temporal relations must be established by rules found among appearances. The argument runs: apprehension is successive in every case; I nevertheless distinguish surveying a house, where the order is reversible and mine to choose, from watching a ship drift downstream, where it is irreversible. That difference cannot lie in succession itself, nor be read off unperceivable time; it lies in the necessity of the order, which requires a rule - "everything that happens presupposes something upon which it follows according to a rule" (A189). The concept of cause is therefore a condition of experiencing an objective event. The claim's second half. Kant never derives which causes operate. That bread nourishes, that this collision produces that motion, remain empirical discoveries. The Analogy secures the form of nature and leaves the content to science. Verdict. The claim is accurate. Kant wins the entitlement Hume denied and declines the knowledge Hume demanded - which is not an evasion but a consequence of separating form from content. Strawson calls the central transition a "non-sequitur of numbing grossness", since that the order of my perceptions is not up to me does not entail that the event is necessitated by a causal law; Guyer argues that at most some rule is required. Allison replies that Kant is describing the conditions under which a succession can be judged objective, and that only a causal rule can fix an irreversible position in one time. The restriction to appearances then follows: the same argument that defeats Hume forbids inferring a first cause of the world.

(c) "But could we also imagine a language in which a person could write down or give vocal expression to his inner experiences-his feelings, moods and the rest-for his private use?" Critically discuss the answer offered by Wittgenstein to this question. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Wittgenstein's answer is that we could not, and the reason is not that such a language would be unshareable but that its words would have no standard of correct application. Where the remark begins. 243 first sets aside the case that is not at issue: human beings who speak only in monologue, whose language a researcher could learn. The question is then narrowed to words that "refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations". The target is logical privacy - not a code, not soliloquy, not undisclosed feeling. The diary. 258 tests the proposal. The diarist associates "S" with a recurring sensation and gives himself "a kind of ostensive definition" by concentrating his attention. But the ceremony can only mean that it brings it about that he remembers the connection rightly - "and here we have no criterion of correctness ... whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." Since 202 has established that obeying a rule is a practice, and that thinking one obeys is not obeying, no rule has been created. The constructive variant. 270 completes the diagnosis. Suppose writing "S" coincides with a rise in blood pressure registered by a manometer: the sign now has a genuine use - and whether the sensation was identified rightly has become irrelevant. Whatever work the sign does is done by the public criterion. Two objections. Memory: public checking also uses memory. Reply: it uses memory plus something independent - a chart, a record, another speaker - whereas 265 compares the private check to buying several copies of the same newspaper. Crusoe: a solitary man could invent sensation-words. Reply: solitude is not logical privacy; whether an actual community is required remains disputed. Verdict.

### Q3
(a) "We should look not to an ideal language which derives its meaning from facts and has a precise 'logical structure but empirically, to the ways in which languages are actually used." Explain the transition from early views of Wittgenstein to his later views on language and meaning with reference to this statement. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The statement's contrast is accurate in its direction and misleading in one respect: what the later Wittgenstein abandons is not an artificial language in favour of a natural one, but the demand that some single hidden structure explain how any expression means anything. What the early view actually held. The Tractatus - German 1921, bilingual 1922 - gives one general propositional form, sense through the depiction of a possible state of affairs, and a shared logical form that cannot be said but shows itself. Crucially, it does not disparage ordinary language: 4.002 and 5.5563 hold everyday propositions to be, just as they stand, in perfect logical order. The "ideal language" of the quoted statement is therefore best read as the demand for a general logical account of factual sense, not as a proposal to replace English. The pressures. Four, and cumulative. 23's list of countless language-games defeats one general form. 28-31 show that ostension explains a word only where the place is already prepared, so naming cannot ground meaning. Colour exclusion - "this spot is red" against "this spot is green" - strains the independence of elementary propositions and was acknowledged in "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929), though it is a pressure rather than a refutation. 47 shows that "simple" has sense only relative to a game. What "empirically" means. Not that philosophy becomes an empirical science. 109 replaces explanation with description, and 122 sets the goal as a perspicuous representation that lets us see connections. The investigation is of grammar, conducted by comparison (130-131). The later account. 43 states the thesis with its own limitation. 66-67 remove the essence-demand through family resemblance and the spun thread. 19 and 23 place language-games within a form of life, and 241-242 locate agreement in judgments rather than opinions. 201 shows that no interpretation fixes its own application, and 258 turns the result on the inner domain the referential model seemed to fit best. Verdict. The transition is from a theoretical to a therapeutic conception of philosophy: 309's aim is to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle. Its gain is release from the ideal-language model; its costs are the quietism 124 invites and an unresolved question about distinguishing correct use from innovation and error. Both books hold philosophy to be clarification, and both consume their own doctrine - the ladder at 6.54, the plural at 133.

(b) "The Categories and Metaphysics VII (Z) give different answers because they ask different questions." Critically examine this resolution of Aristotle's account of substance. [15 marks]
Model answer: The apparent conflict. In the Categories (2a11 ff.) primary substance is the concrete individual - "this man", "this horse" (tode ti) - and species and genus are secondary substances said of it. In Metaphysics VII (Z) substance is the form or essence (to ti en einai), prior to the compound in being, definition and knowledge; matter is rejected as substance at Z.3; and Z.13 states bluntly that no universal is a substance, although form looks universal. The proposed resolution. The Categories asks which things are substances and answers by a logical-linguistic criterion: the ultimate subject of predication. Metaphysics Z asks what the substance of a thing is and answers by a causal-explanatory criterion, reformulated at Z.17 as "why is this matter a house?". Two different questions may have two different answers, and Aristotle insists that being is said in many ways. Critical examination. The resolution is the safest available and is textually well grounded. But it does not dissolve every difficulty. If form is the species-form, Z.13 generates a genuine aporia which Aristotle does not fully resolve - the reading associated with Owen. If form is individual in each substance - the Frede-Patzig line - the tension disappears but the universality science requires becomes harder to secure. Jaeger's developmental reading, that the Categories is early and Platonic-adjacent, depends on a chronology that is itself disputed. Verdict. The different-questions reading should be led with, because it explains Z.3's rejection of bare matter and Z.17's reformulation as steps in one enquiry rather than reversals. It should then be qualified: the individual remains what exists primarily, but what accounts for its being that individual is its form, and whether that form is individual or specific is still contested.

(c) Assess the view that Hegel's identification of the rational with the actual makes his philosophy a defence of the existing order. [15 marks]
Model answer: The charge. The Preface to the Philosophy of Right declares that "what is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational". Read straightforwardly, this appears to certify whatever exists - and Hegel's own accommodation with the Prussian state has long been cited as evidence that he meant it that way. The technical reply. The sentence turns on actuality (Wirklichkeit), the effective unity of essence and existence - the realised operation of a concept - and not on bare determinate existence (Dasein). An institution can perfectly well exist without being actual, if it fails to realise the concept it claims to embody. Far from blocking criticism, the distinction supplies its standard: a defective institution is measured against the concept it does not realise. wirklich must be translated or glossed, since rendering it "existing" makes the sentence assert the opposite of Hegel's intention. What the reply does not settle. Two residues remain. First, the criterion is only as good as its application, and critics can reasonably ask whether Hegel applied it too gently to the modern constitutional state. Second, the same conservatism charge attaches to his philosophy of history, where a retrospective teleology can rationalise victors and understate contingency, colonialism, class and gender exclusion. The actuality/existence distinction is a defence of the formula; it is not a defence of every use Hegel made of it. Verdict. The view is mistaken as a reading of the doctrine and not baseless as a reading of the man. The formula licenses immanent criticism; Hegel's own political conclusions remain contestable, and the honest answer says both in the same paragraph.

### Q4
(a) Compare Plato's and Aristotle's metaphysics of form, and evaluate whether Aristotle's immanentism is an advance on Plato's transcendence. [20 marks]
Model answer: Frame. Both use the word eidos; the dispute is whether form exists apart from its instances. Everything else - causation, generation, knowledge, substance - follows from that difference. Plato. Forms are eternal, non-material, self-identical universals, independent of sensible particulars and neither in space nor in time. They solve three problems at once: the unity of the many (hen epi pollon), knowledge amid flux, and the objectivity of value. Particulars are what they are by participation (methexis) or imitation (mimesis); the Form is paradigm, the particular imperfect copy. The Sun, Divided Line and Cave (Republic VI 507a - VII 517a) form one continuous argument in which degrees of cognitive clarity track degrees of being, with the Good giving the Forms knowability and being and itself said to lie "beyond being" (epekeina tes ousias) at 509b. For Plato true substance (ousia) is the universal. Aristotle. He inverts this. Primary substance is the concrete individual (tode ti); species and genus are secondary. Form is the immanent what-it-is-to-be (to ti en einai) of a composite (synolon); matter is relative and correlative to form. Change is analysed through subject, form and privation (steresis), explained by the four causes, and understood modally as passage from potentiality (dynamis) to actuality (energeia) - "the actuality of the potential as potential" (Physics III.1, 201a10). The system terminates in the Unmoved Mover, pure actuality moving as final cause (Metaphysics XII ().7). The critique. At Metaphysics I.9 Aristotle charges Plato with duplicating the explananda, leaving participation a metaphor, rendering Forms causally impotent and offering no account of generation; the Third Man regress at Parmenides 132a-b adds a formal difficulty. Evaluation. Immanentism advances on three counts: it removes the regress by removing separation, makes form a genuine cause of this thing, and makes generation intelligible. It does not advance on a fourth: Plato's insight that intelligibility may require something beyond the particular is answered rather than refuted, and Metaphysics VII (Z).13's denial that any universal is a substance sets Aristotle a difficulty as deep as the Third Man. Nor is Plato as thin as Aristotle suggests: the Timaeus supplies a Demiurge ordering becoming after Forms and a Receptacle in which becoming appears, so the accurate charge is that separated Forms themselves explain neither motion nor organisation. Verdict. Aristotle transforms Plato's insight rather than erasing it. The dispute is converted into the problem of universals and every later realism-nominalism debate.

(b) Elucidate the significance of bracketing and reduction in Husserl's phenomenological method. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Bracketing and reduction are significant because of three things they make possible, each of which would otherwise be lost. What is bracketed. Suspension (epoche) puts the general thesis of the natural attitude out of play: the existence-positing is neither affirmed nor denied. The bracket falls on a validity-claim, never on a content - the tree is still described as green, seen from this side, with unseen sides co-intended. What remains, the residue, is the whole field of pure experience, and it is wider than what the natural attitude records. Four operations, not one ladder. The phenomenological reduction leads inquiry back - re-ducere - from what is this object? to how is it given?; the eidetic reduction moves from the factual instance to its essence by free imaginative variation; the transcendental reduction asks for the constituting subjectivity for which sense and validity arise. Eidetic analysis already operates in the Logical Investigations (1900-01), before the transcendental turn of Ideas I (1913), so the operations must not be presented as fixed rungs. The three purposes. (i) Evidence under a changed attitude: the stream is apodictically unavoidable as experienced, though apodicticity is not exhaustive transparency and particular self-descriptions stay corrigible - precisely what Ideas I 24 requires by limiting evidence to what is given within the limits of its givenness. (ii) No substance-inference: consciousness is analysed as intentional life rather than established as a separate mental thing, which reformulates rather than dissolves the mind-body problem. (iii) Eidetic and transcendental questions become askable: only a field cleared of factual commitment can be varied for invariants; without this, phenomenology would be a diary of episodes - that is, empirical psychology. Limitation.

(c) Among the rationalists, whose account of mind-body problem is compatible with the notion of human freedom and free will? Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Compatibility depends on the criterion of freedom, so the criterion must be declared first. Descartes' interactionism separates unextended mind from a mechanically determined body, permitting a libertarian will that can affirm, deny or withhold assent. But Princess Elisabeth's objection of 1643 remains unanswered: no intelligible account explains causal commerce between substances sharing no property, and the pineal gland supplies only a location. Descartes' own reply - that the union is a primitive notion - preserves the phenomenon and abandons the mechanism. Spinoza dissolves interaction by making mind and body the same mode under Thought and Extension (Ethics II, P7). This is metaphysically economical, but it entails necessitarianism: freedom becomes adequate self-determination, and free will as alternative choice is expressly denied. It also abandons ordinary mental causation, since no idea moves a limb, so the economy is bought by revising the very experience the question begins from. Leibniz denies interaction because windowless monads unfold internally, and pre-established harmony coordinates the mental and bodily series. Rational action remains spontaneous, intelligent and contingent, because the actual world is one possible world chosen by God, so truths about action are certain but not absolutely necessary. If "free will" means the ability to do otherwise, Descartes wins - with an unintelligible causal theory. If it means responsible agency compatible with systematic determination, Leibniz offers the strongest account. Spinoza offers the deepest self-mastery but openly rejects free will. Verdict. Leibniz is the best-qualified answer, because his mind-body theory preserves internal agency and moral responsibility without Cartesian interaction - though it remains compatibilist rather than libertarian, and buys correspondence from God in advance.

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) Do these two sentences "Air does not have heat" and "Air is not fire" refer to the same type of absence or abhava, according to the Vaisesikas? Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: No - the two sentences report different kinds of absence (abhava). "Air does not have heat." The locus is air, a substance; the counterpositive is heat, a quality (guna). The claim is that a quality does not, and will not, reside in this locus. In the Vaisesika scheme hot touch is the characteristic touch of fire, so its absence in air is not a contingent shortage but a permanent categorial one. This is absolute non-existence (atyantabhava) of heat with respect to air. "Air is not fire." Here the counterpositive is fire, another substance (dravya). The claim denies identity between two entities, not the residence of a quality in a locus. This is mutual non-existence (anyonyabhava), which is reciprocal difference - as in "a pot is not a cloth". The deciding test. Ask what the counterpositive is. If the missing item is a quality that could have qualified the locus, the absence is absolute. If it is another entity with which the locus might be identified, the absence is mutual. Sameness of grammatical subject does not entail sameness of absence; the classification is fixed by the counterpositive, not by the locus.

(b) "In Jainism the moral quality of an act is fixed by passion rather than by activity." Examine this claim with reference to the parameters of bondage. [10 marks]
Model answer: The claim is accurate if stated with care. Karmic bondage is analysed under four parameters. Activity of body, speech and mind determines the type (prakrti) of karma bound and the quantity (pradesa) of matter bound. Passion determines the duration (sthiti) and the intensity (anubhaga) of its fruition. Since duration and intensity are the parameters that actually decide how heavily an act weighs on the future of the soul, the moral seriousness of an act tracks passion rather than mere movement. The doctrinal expression of this is the contrast between momentary bondage from passion-free activity and durable bondage from passion-coloured activity: a monk's careful walking binds only momentarily, while an angry act binds deeply. The claim must nevertheless be qualified. Activity is not morally irrelevant, since it fixes what is bound at all, and total cessation of activity belongs only to the fourteenth stage. The precise formulation is therefore that activity determines the karmic content while passion determines its karmic weight.

(c) Distinguish between pudgala-nairatmyavada and dharma-nairatmyavada with reference to Buddhist Philosophy. [10 marks]
Model answer: Both are forms of no-self, differing in scope. Personal no-self (pudgala-nairatmya) denies a permanent, independent person (pudgala) behind experience. The person is a designation on the five aggregates, and no owner is found among or behind them. This is the minimum thesis shared across Buddhist traditions, and it is what the chariot analogy establishes. Phenomenon no-self (dharma-nairatmya) extends the same analysis to the constituent factors themselves, denying that any dharma possesses intrinsic nature (svabhava). The difference is therefore between the owner and the inventory on which the owner was denied. Early Buddhism and the realist Abhidharma schools accept the first while treating dharmas as ultimately real; Vaibhasika and Sautrantika are precisely such positions. Mahayana, and Madhyamika above all, presses the second, which is why dependence yields emptiness there but conditioned realism in the earlier schools. The distinction is one of philosophical scope, not of institutional affiliation, and it should not be converted into a contrast between Theravada and Mahayana as wholes.

(d) How does Bhatta's view of nature of word-meaning and sentential-meaning differ from Prabhakara's view? Critically discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: Both sub-schools face the problem of sentence-unity (ekavakyata) and accept the same three conditions for it: mutual expectancy (akanksa), semantic fitness (yogyata) and proximity (sannidhi). They differ over which unit is semantically primary. Kumarila holds connection-of-the-already-denoted (abhihitanvayavada). Each word first denotes its own meaning (padartha) through its denotative power (abhidha); sentence-meaning then arises through a further connecting operation, explained as indirect signification (laksana) or purport-power (tatparya-sakti). The word is primary and sentence-meaning derivative, and the two stages are logical rather than temporal. Prabhakara holds denotation-of-the-connected (anvitabhidhanavada). Words denote their meanings only as already connected with the other meanings in the sentence. His evidence is the learning situation (vyutpatti): a child hears an elder command "bring the cow," sees a younger person comply, and learns the words in their connected, action-guiding sense. Critically, each is charged with what the other explains. Kumarila is charged with positing an unobserved isolated-word stage and an extra operation in every sentence; he replies that the distinction is explanatory, not introspective. Prabhakara is charged with making a word mean differently in every sentence; he replies that recurrent connected uses yield stable competence. The verdict is complementary: Bhatta secures stable lexical meaning, Prabhakara contextual action-guidance.

(e) Explain the two senses of the Middle Path (madhyama pratipad) in Buddhism. Why is the doctrinal sense indispensable to the practical one? [10 marks]
Model answer: The Middle Path operates in two registers. Practically, it rejects sensual indulgence and self-mortification, replacing both with the Noble Eightfold Path (aryastangamarga) grouped under wisdom (right view, right intention), ethical discipline (right speech, action, livelihood) and meditative discipline (right effort, mindfulness, concentration). The rejection is diagnostic: indulgence feeds craving, while mortification exhausts the practitioner without touching it. Doctrinally, it rejects eternalism (sasvatavada) and annihilationism (ucchedavada), replacing both with dependent origination and conditioned continuity. The doctrinal sense is indispensable because the practical path is unintelligible without it. If a permanent self existed, training could not transform it, and the third Noble Truth would fail. If nothing continued, discipline could accumulate no result, and the fourth would fail. Only conditioned continuity - change without a permanent bearer, and continuity without annihilation - makes a path both necessary and effective. Both senses share one logic: Buddhism denies the hidden assumption common to each pair of extremes rather than choosing between them.

### Q6
(a) Elucidate Naiyayikas account of fallacies of the middle term in relation to five characteristics of valid middle term. [20 marks]
Model answer: Nyaya tests the middle term twice - positively, through five marks of a valid reason (hetu), and negatively, through five fallacies of the reason (hetvabhasa). They are one test read in two directions. The five marks. (1) Presence in the subject (paksadharmata): the reason occurs in the subject under consideration. (2) Presence in similar instances (sapaksa-sattva): it occurs where the property is known to hold. (3) Absence from dissimilar instances (vipaksa-asattva): it never occurs where the property is known to be absent. (4) Not contradicted (abadhita): the property is not defeated by a stronger instrument. (5) Not counterbalanced (asatpratipaksa): no equally strong counter-reason exists. The five fallacies, mapped. Irregular (savyabhicara / anaikantika) violates mark 3. The reason strays into dissimilar instances. Subtypes: too common (sadharana), occurring in both similar and dissimilar instances; too restricted (asadharana), confined to the subject so that no contrast class remains; inconclusive by over-width (anupasamharin), so wide that no contrast is available. Unproved (asiddha / sadhyasama) violates mark 1. Subtypes: locus-unproved (asrayasiddha), where the subject is unreal; form-unproved (svarupasiddha), where the reason is absent from the subject; pervasion-unproved (vyapyatvasiddha), where concomitance is unestablished, typically because a conditioning adjunct (upadhi) is present - as wet fuel is in the inference from fire to smoke. Stultified (badhita / kalatita) violates mark 4. The conclusion is defeated by a stronger instrument: fire cannot be inferred to be cold, because perception overrules it. Counterbalanced (satpratipaksa / prakaranasama) violates mark 5. An equally strong opposing reason establishes the contrary property, and the dispute deadlocks with neither side defeated on its own terms. Contradictory (viruddha) is the exception. It does not merely negate one mark; it inverts the concomitance that all five marks presuppose, so that the reason proves the opposite of the intended property - a reason offered for permanence establishing impermanence instead. Evaluation. The correspondence is systematic rather than accidental: because a valid reason is defined by five conditions, exactly five modes of failure are possible, with one of them operating at the level of the relation itself rather than at the level of a condition. The scheme's power nevertheless depends on the prior availability of similar and dissimilar instances, which is exactly what positive-only (kevalanvayi) and negative-only (kevalavyatireki) inferences lack - so the fallacy theory is at its strongest where both classes exist and requires careful handling elsewhere.

(b) Critically evaluate the logical and metaphysical status of Visesa in the context of Vaisesika Philosophy. [15 marks]
Model answer: Metaphysical status. Ultimate particularity (visesa) is the seventh-category individuator residing only in eternal substances: the atoms of earth, water, fire and air, and the eternal instances of ether, time, direction, self and mind. Composite objects need no particularity, since parts, qualities, place and causal history already distinguish them. Particularity is imperceptible and is known inferentially. It gives the Vaisesika school its name, so the system's identity is bound to it. Logical status. The posit answers a specific argument. Two atoms of earth may share every universal and every ordinary quality. If nothing further distinguished them, then by the identity of indiscernibles they would be one, and the school's pluralism would fail at its foundation. Particularity is introduced as the differentiator, and Nyaya frames the introduction inferentially - plurality of exactly similar eternals is otherwise inexplicable - which is consistent, since Nyaya reduces postulation (arthapatti) to inference rather than admitting it as a separate instrument. Two objections. First, the regress of differentiators: if particularity differentiates atoms, what differentiates particularities? The reply is that they are self-differentiating (svato-vyavartaka), distinguishing both their loci and themselves. Second, the charge of an imperceptible ontological IOU: an unperceived entity posited solely to perform one job. Evaluation. The replies are coherent but terminal rather than demonstrative. "Self-differentiating" names the stopping point instead of proving it, and every relational or individuative ontology must stop somewhere. The fair verdict is that particularity is logically indispensable within Vaisesika pluralism and metaphysically stipulative, which is why Jaina, Buddhist and Advaita critics - Sriharsa systematically - treat it, with inherence, as the school's weakest joint.

(c) "Jaina non-violence is a metaphysical consequence, not a sentiment." Critically discuss with reference to the doctrine of the soul and the mechanics of bondage. [15 marks]
Model answer: The claim is well founded. Jaina non-violence (ahimsa) is derived, not asserted. The derivation begins in ontology. Souls are innumerable and graded from one-sense immobile beings - earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied - to five-sense mobile beings. Every one of them is a conscious substance intrinsically endowed with the infinite tetrad and currently obscured. Injury therefore falls on a knower, not on a thing. It continues in the mechanics of bondage. Injury is characteristically produced by passion, and passion fixes the duration and intensity of karmic adhesion. Violence is thus not merely wrong by a rule; it is causally the most efficient way to deepen bondage. Non-violence, conversely, is an operation of stoppage: it closes the channel through which the heaviest karma enters. The doctrine is graded rather than utopian. Ascetics take the great vows; householders take the lesser vows; and carefulness in movement, speech, alms-seeking, handling objects and bodily functions extends the principle into ordinary conduct. Extreme care toward one-sense beings is an implication of the ontology, not an excess of feeling. Critically, two points deserve statement. First, the derivation is only as strong as its premises: a critic who denies that plants or elemental bodies house souls will accept the ethics while rejecting its scope. Second, the account is intention-sensitive rather than consequence-sensitive, which is a philosophical choice with costs of its own. Nevertheless, the central contention holds: Jaina non-violence follows from plural souls and karmic mechanics, and treating it as sentiment removes precisely the argument that makes it distinctive among Indian ethical positions.

### Q7
(a) 'Brahma satyam jaganmithya, Jivo Brahmaiva naparah'. In the light of this statement explain the ontological status of Isvara, Jiva and Saksi as elucidated in Advaita Vedanta. [20 marks]
Model answer: The quoted line compresses Advaita into three claims: ultimate reality is real, the world is dependent appearance, and the individual self is none other than ultimate reality. It is a traditional Advaita summary rather than one of the four standard Upanisadic great sentences, and saying so is itself a mark of textual discipline. Read as a thesis, it fixes the status of all three terms asked. The Lord (isvara). The Lord is ultimate reality conditioned by indescribable illusion (maya-upahita brahman), the omniscient, omnipotent creator and moral governor of the empirical world. His status is therefore fully real at the empirical level (vyavaharika) and sublated at the ultimate level (paramarthika). Theism in Advaita is thus neither false in a crude sense nor final in the last sense - which is exactly the point at which Ramanuja objects that devotion is made provisional. The individual self (jiva). The individual is ultimate reality limited or reflected in ignorance together with the internal organ, appearing as doer, enjoyer and transmigrator. Its status is empirical bondage. Advaita explains the appearance three ways: limitation (avaccheda-vada, pot-space), reflection (pratibimba-vada, the mirrored face) and semblance (abhasa-vada), which stress plurality, vividness and ontological thinness while agreeing that individuality lacks ultimate reality. The second clause of the verse is thus literal: the individual is "none other than" the absolute in essence. The witness (saksi). The witness is pure witnessing consciousness - unaffected, immediate, self-luminous (svayam-prakasa) and non-objectifiable. It illumines all mental states without being altered by them, and is supported by the three-state analysis, where the report "I slept peacefully; I knew nothing" presupposes continuity through deep sleep. Its status is closest to the ultimate standpoint, since it is the individual's own essential consciousness considered apart from adjuncts. The hierarchy. The three are not three substances but one consciousness under three descriptions: the witness closest to the ultimate standpoint, the individual in empirical bondage, and the Lord in cosmic-ordering empirical reality. The first clause of the verse fixes the world as dependently real (mithya) - sublatable, not wholly non-existent - and the whole scheme is intelligible only through the three levels of reality graded by sublation (badha). Critically, the elegance of the hierarchy is purchased by the cost the verse itself concedes: the Lord, and therefore devotion, cannot be final.

(b) Analyse the four schools of Buddhism as an argumentative escalation rather than as a classification. What does each successive position gain and lose? [15 marks]
Model answer: The fourfold matrix is a philosophical doxography, and its explanatory value lies in the fact that each position is generated by an objection the previous one cannot absorb. Vaibhasika holds direct realism about external dharmas and affirms sarvam asti, that dharmas of past, present and future have some mode of existence. It gains a genuine object for every cognition and a real relation between a past act and its future fruit. It loses ontological economy and strains momentariness, since extending existence across the three times sits awkwardly with strict one-moment duration. Sautrantika answers by restricting full existence to the present. Since the object-moment has ceased when its cognition arises, cognition bears only the object's form (akara), from which the external object is inferred. It gains consistency with momentariness. It loses direct access, and now owes an account of why the inferred cause should correspond to the form it produces. Yogacara presses exactly there: if all that is ever given is the form, the external object performs no explanatory work. Representation-only (vijnaptimatra) gains economy, and explains order through store-consciousness (alaya-vijnana) and seeds (bija). It loses the easy explanation of intersubjective agreement, which must now be derived from shared karmic conditioning, and it exposes consciousness itself to the charge of reification. Madhyamika presses that charge: even consciousness lacks intrinsic nature. Emptiness gains complete freedom from reification, including the emptiness of emptiness. It loses any positive ultimate, and must defend a non-implicative negation (prasajya-pratisedha) against the objection that denying all four corners either says nothing or refutes itself. Read as an escalation, the four schools display one method - remove whatever the analysis shows to be unnecessary - applied with increasing severity.

(c) Discuss the debate between the Bhatta and the Prabhakara mimamsakas with reference to the nature of Non-existence (Abhava) and its knowledge. [15 marks]
Model answer: The debate is intra-Mimamsa and runs on two connected questions: what absence (abhava) is, and how it is known. Kumarila Bhatta treats absence as real. It is not a feature of language but a structured content determined by its counterpositive (pratiyogin), so that "absence of pot" has genuine ontological standing and negative cognition has a genuine object. Correspondingly, he admits a sixth instrument, non-cognition (anupalabdhi), defined as non-apprehension of what would be apprehended if present and limited by fitness (yogyata): the object must be perceptible in kind, the locus suitable, the organ functioning, and light, distance and attention proper. His reasons are two refusals. Perception yields positive content - table, colour, shape - whereas "absence of pot" is not a positive datum of that kind, so perceiving the empty table is not yet cognising the pot's absence. Inference needs a positive sign and a known concomitance; if "not-seeing" is offered as the sign, its evidentiary force already presupposes non-apprehension under fit conditions, so the reduction is strained or circular. Prabhakara declines both the ontology and the instrument. What is known, he holds, is simply the locus as unqualified by the absent object: we perceive the table as bare, not as occupied by the pot, and the bare-locus cognition already carries the negative differentiation required. No special faculty for what is not there need be posited. His position is an economy thesis, not a denial that absences are known. Kumarila's counter is that economy purchased by flattening a determinate negative judgement into a positive perception is false economy. For comparison, Nyaya converges with Prabhakara against Kumarila on independence while diverging in its reason, since it holds that absence is itself directly perceived. The graded verdict is that Kumarila is the more faithful phenomenologist and Prabhakara the more economical metaphysician.

### Q8
(a) How do the Naiyayikas prove the existence of God? Do the Yoga philosophers prove God in the same way? If yes, how? And if no, why? Give reasons for your answer. [20 marks]
Model answer: The Nyaya proofs. Later Nyaya affirms God (Isvara) as an eternal, omniscient, bodiless special self who is the efficient cause (nimitta-karana) of the world but never its material cause; atoms remain the material cause. Udayana's Nyayakusumanjali presents converging inferences. From effect (karyat): the world is composite and ordered, hence an effect, hence requires an intelligent maker, as a pot requires a potter. From combination (ayojanat): at the start of a cycle, unconscious atoms and unconscious unseen moral force (adrsta) cannot initiate ordered first motion, so an intelligent impeller is required. From sustenance (dhrtyadeh): the cosmos is maintained, regulated and dissolved, which indicates governing intelligence. From scripture (sruteh): Vedic authority of superhuman scope implies an omniscient source. A moral strand is added: unconscious adrsta cannot allot fruits with precision, so an intelligent administrator is required. Whether Yoga proves God in the same way - no. Classical Yoga's God is a special conscious witness (purusa-visesa) untouched by afflictions, actions, fruits and latent deposits. He is admitted within an already complete soteriology of bondage and release, not inferred as the explanation of cosmic order. His role is practical: devotion to God (Isvara-pranidhana) aids concentration and meditative absorption, and he functions as the beginningless teacher. The Yoga-sutra does not construct Udayana's atomic creator inference, and Samkhya, Yoga's metaphysical partner, dispenses with a creator altogether. The reason for the difference. The two theisms answer different questions. Nyaya needs God because its atomism leaves a gap - inert atoms plus an unconscious moral ledger cannot start themselves - and because its realism about moral order requires an intelligent allocator. Yoga needs God because its practice requires an object of devotion and a guarantor of the possibility of perfect isolation. Argumentative role, not description, is the discriminating factor. Qualified verdict. Both systems accept God, but sameness of name does not prove sameness of demonstration or function. Later Yoga commentators may strengthen theistic cosmology; the safe conclusion is that classical Yoga's God is a soteriological posit and an object of practice, whereas Nyaya's is the conclusion of a cumulative natural theology whose every strand rests on the contested premiss that order and meaning require intelligence.

(b) Examine the Jaina claim that ordinary sense-perception is only conventionally direct. What does this reveal about the relation between Jaina epistemology and Jaina soteriology? [15 marks]
Model answer: Jainism divides valid knowledge into direct (pratyaksa) and indirect (paroksa), but measures directness by absence of mediation rather than by sensory vividness. Ordinary sense-perception depends on sense-organs, the mind, signs and karmic limitation. It is therefore only conventional direct knowledge (samvyavaharika pratyaksa) and strictly belongs with mediated cognition. What is direct in the full sense is knowledge the soul has by itself: limited clairvoyance (avadhi), knowledge of others' mental modes (manahparyaya) and omniscience (kevala). The claim is defensible on its own terms. It preserves ordinary life, since perception remains valid for practical dealings; it distinguishes practical adequacy from metaphysical finality; and it avoids the Nyaya assumption that sense-contact is the paradigm of directness. What it reveals is that Jaina epistemology is not a free-standing theory. The five kinds of knowledge are stations on a single curve of karmic unveiling: mati and sruta are mediated, the remaining three progressively direct, and omniscience is the state in which knowledge-obscuring karma has been destroyed. Knowledge is therefore graded by purity, not by method. Two consequences follow. First, conditional predication becomes obligatory rather than optional, because a ksayopasamika knower cannot make an unconditioned claim. Second, wrong cognition is orientation-relative: kumati, kusruta and vibhanga are cognitions held under wrong vision, so error is a spiritual as well as a logical category. Critically, this integration is the system's strength and its exposure. It gives Jainism a unified account of knowing and liberation, but it makes the epistemology depend on an ontology of soul and karmic matter that an empiricist critic will refuse at the outset.

(c) Explain the nature of God and its role in Kaivalya in yoga philosophy. [15 marks]
Model answer: Yoga defines the Lord (Isvara) as a special conscious witness (purusa-visesa) untouched by affliction (klesa), action (karma), fruition (vipaka) and karmic deposit (asaya). In him lies the unsurpassed seed of omniscience; he is the teacher even of the ancients, being unconditioned by time; and he is denoted by the sacred syllable (pranava, OM), whose repetition with contemplation of its meaning is a canonical practice. He is special, not different in kind: he belongs to the category of conscious witness, and his distinction is that he was never entangled. This admission is what makes Yoga theistic Samkhya (sesvara-samkhya) against Samkhya's Godless tendency. His positive roles are five: object of meditation, aid through dedication (Isvara-pranidhana), remover of the nine obstacles (antaraya), perfect model of untouched status, and timeless teacher-principle anchoring transmission. His role in isolation (kaivalya) must be stated with precision. Isolation is the aloneness of the witness from primordial nature and its evolutes, reached through discriminative discernment (viveka-khyati), matured by higher dispassion (para-vairagya) and consummated in cloud-of-dharma absorption (dharmamegha-samadhi), after which the constituent qualities undergo involution (pratiprasava) and consciousness abides in its own nature. The Lord assists this process by stabilising the mind-field and clearing obstacles, but he does not grant isolation as an external gift. Four negations therefore complete the answer: he is not the material cause; he is not the Nyaya creator and efficient cause of the world-order; he is not a saviour who bestows liberation arbitrarily; and he is not a substitute for discernment. The exact formula is that the Lord is instrumental, not constitutive, in liberation - not redundant, but non-final.

## Philosophy-II

### Q1 (Compulsory)
(a) "A classification of governments that counts rulers without asking whom they serve explains nothing." Comment. [10 marks]
Model answer: Claim. The statement is correct, and Aristotle's grid is the demonstration of why. The argument. Aristotle classifies constitutions by two criteria: how many rule, and whether they govern for the common advantage or their private advantage. Crossing them yields monarchy against tyranny, aristocracy against oligarchy, and polity against democracy in its classical deviant sense. Why counting alone fails. Within any row the paired forms are structurally identical. Monarchy and tyranny are both government by one person; nothing about the number distinguishes the legitimate from the corrupt case. It follows that no purely institutional description - how offices are filled, how many vote, how laws are passed - can by itself deliver a normative verdict. A contemporary restatement. The modern dispute about whether an electoral regime is "really" democratic is Aristotle's purpose axis conducted without his vocabulary: elections settle the numerical question and leave the question of whose advantage untouched. Qualification. The purpose axis is not self-applying, since "common advantage" requires an independent theory of the good and every ruler claims to serve it. The axis is nevertheless checkable indirectly, through whether those affected can contest decisions and whether error can be corrected. Verdict.

(b) "A composite index of development is evidence, not a theory of the good society." Comment. [10 marks]
Model answer: Position. The statement is correct, and its correctness follows from what an index is. What an index is. Every measure occupies an informational space and therefore fixes in advance what will count as evidence and what will be invisible. Output measures see market transactions; basic-needs measures see a deprivation threshold; welfare measures see reported conditions; human-development composites see longevity, knowledge and material command; capability measures see real opportunity; ecological measures see stocks, sinks and irreversibility. Why no index can be the theory. A composite must assign weights between dimensions, and the weights cannot be derived from within any of the dimensions. They are the normative question itself, which is why public reasoning about them is part of development rather than merely a technique for measuring it. Three concealments that decide arguments. Output measures cannot see unpaid work, so a society can appear to grow by monetising labour already performed. Welfare measures cannot see adaptive preference, so deprivation is certified as contentment. Composites cannot see within-group inequality. The qualification. This is not scepticism about measurement. Knowing a measure's blind spot is what makes it usable, and plural measurement with explicit spaces, under a stated deprivation floor, is the correct practice. Verdict.

(c) Distinguish legal from political sovereignty, and explain why the distinction matters for evaluating Austin. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The two answer different questions - who is competent to make law, and what power shapes or compels the law-maker - and the distinction matters because most objections to Austin are answers to the second offered against a thesis about the first. Legal sovereignty. It names the authority recognised by law as competent to make law: the juristic source of validity, and Austin's preferred angle. His sovereign is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and habitually obeying no one. Political sovereignty. It names the actual power behind legal institutions - the force that can compel or shape the legal sovereign. Parties, public opinion, organised interests, military power, religion and economic classes belong here, and this is the standpoint from which Laski attacks. Why they diverge. A constitution may vest law-making power in one body while something else determines what that body can actually do; the divergence is a general feature of political life rather than a constitutional defect. Why it matters for Austin. An unlabelled objection commits a category error. Showing that a legislature is politically constrained does not refute a claim about legal competence; showing that power-conferring rules cannot be redescribed as commands does. Verdict.

(d) Critically examine the challenges faced by a multicultural society with reference to India. [10 marks]
Model answer: Frame. The challenge facing a multicultural society is not that it is diverse. It is that equal citizenship and community-specific protection pull in different directions, and each of the standing challenges is a form of that tension. Four challenges. One - the internal-restriction problem. Recognition granted to a community may be used by authorised representatives against women, dissenters, lower-status members or converts; the criterion is Kymlicka's - external protections are defensible, internal restrictions are not. Two - essentialism. Recognition requires an interlocutor, and identifying one freezes an authorised version of an internally plural culture. Three - fragmentation. Differentiated rights are said to weaken common citizenship; fair recognition can instead deepen allegiance by removing assimilation as the price of membership. Four - majority invisibility. Only minorities appear "cultural" while majority norms pass as neutral, so scrutiny is applied asymmetrically. Indian illustration, labelled as illustration. Linguistic federalism illustrates accommodation; Articles 29-30 protect specified cultural and educational interests and are best read as external protections; personal-law pluralism illustrates accommodation while generating disputes over gender equality. These show what a remedy looks like; they do not settle the internal-restriction question, which is a philosophical dispute. Verdict.

(e) Discuss the main factors responsible for caste discrimination. [10 marks]
Model answer: Define precisely. Caste discrimination is graded, birth-based social closure sustained by a closed marriage circle - not "social stratification", and not a division of labour. The factors, as a chain. Endogamy closes the group and carries membership across generations. Hereditary status assigns social worth by birth, before any act or aptitude. Occupational closure allocates work by birth-group and stigmatises the labour itself. Purity and pollution rules moralise distance, so exclusion is experienced as duty, and religious sanction presents hierarchy as sacred. Social and economic power - land, employment and dependence - enforces the norm. Political mobilisation is ambivalent: it can democratise subordinated groups and can harden competitive identities. Why the chain form matters. Each link makes the next possible, so the system absorbs the reform of any single component: prohibiting the ritual practice leaves the marriage circle, and opening the occupation leaves the sanction. Inter-caste marriage reaches the first link; reservation the third. The consequence. The result is graded inequality - an ascending scale of reverence and a descending scale of contempt - which obstructs solidarity, because each level holds a stake in distinction from below. Verdict.

### Q2
(a) "Monarchy, theocracy and democracy differ less in who rules than in how error is corrected." Discuss. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The statement is broadly correct and needs one qualification: the three forms differ in title as well as in error-correction, but it is the second axis, not the first, that decides the comparative verdict. Why the title axis is insufficient by itself. Monarchy claims heredity and tradition, sometimes divine sanction; theocracy claims revelation and sacred law read by authorised interpreters; democracy claims consent and political equality. Each title is internally coherent, and each has been sincerely believed, so the titles alone cannot adjudicate between them. The eight comparison axes. A comparison must run all three forms down the same axes - source of authority, type of legitimacy claimed, mode of succession, accountability, relation to law, treatment of dissent, equality of citizenship, and capacity to correct error. The last is the one candidates omit and the one that decides. Monarchy on the error axis. Error is corrected by prudent counsel and customary restraint. There is no regular, non-violent procedure for acting on the finding that a ruler is incompetent. The merit objection is therefore not that monarchs are individually worse but that no mechanism exists to respond when one is. Theocracy on the error axis. Correction is structurally hardest here, because the norm is held to be unamendable and its interpreter is insulated: objection to the interpreter can be recast as objection to the source, so dissent is reclassified rather than answered. Democracy on the error axis. Correction is institutionalised through opposition, elections, courts and public criticism, and this is the substance of the epistemic defence - not that citizens judge well, but that misjudgement can be exposed, contested and reversed. The objection. Democracies also fail to correct: form can be retained while substance erodes, so elections, courts, press and opposition may persist while fair contest, independent adjudication and realistic alternation are hollowed out. Reply: this concedes that democracy's advantage is conditional, not automatic; where the conditions survive, correction remains available, and where they do not, the regime has ceased to be constitutional in substance whatever its form. Weber's cross-check. Traditional and charismatic claims solve succession or inspiration but not answerability, while legal-rational authority makes correction a matter of rule rather than of the ruler's grace. Verdict. Classification by number tells us who decides; classification by legitimacy tells us why they are obeyed; the error-correction axis tells us which arrangement can survive being wrong.

(b) "Democracy is best defined by its method, not by its outcome." Critically examine with reference to Schumpeter. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The statement is defensible as a minimum condition and indefensible as a complete definition, and the distinction between those two claims is the whole argument. Schumpeter's position. Rejecting the classical doctrine that democracy realises the will of the people, Schumpeter defines it as the institutional arrangement in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote; the people's function is to produce a government, not to govern. The reasoning. The common good is not uniquely determinable; no determinate will of the people exists, since aggregate preferences are unstable and manufactured; and the ordinary citizen's grasp of remote issues is weak. Therefore democracy cannot be defined by the content of outcomes and can be defined by a method. What the definition secures. It is empirically usable, distinguishes democracy from plebiscitary acclamation, and isolates the minimum without which nothing else survives - an open, uncertain, repeatable contest. The objections. The participatory and deliberative reply is that the account is deflationary: citizenship becomes periodic choice among elites, the developmental value of participation disappears, and no criterion distinguishes a deliberative election from a well-managed one. A sharper objection follows: if preferences are manufactured, elite competition is a contest to manufacture them better rather than a mechanism of accountability. Replies and residue. Schumpeter's defender answers that his is a floor, not a ceiling: participation without competition can be mobilised acclamation. But answering the manufacturing objection requires plural media, independent adjudication and rights of opposition - conditions he does not himself supply, which concedes that a purely procedural definition is incomplete. Verdict. The method is necessary and not sufficient. Democracy is best identified by its method and best evaluated by whether the conditions of that method are genuinely present.

(c) Critically examine the claim that modernisation describes a neutral and inevitable process of social transformation. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. The claim is false as stated, though what modernisation describes is largely accurate; the falsity lies in the words "neutral" and "inevitable". What is accurate. Industrialisation, urbanisation, mass education, occupational differentiation, bureaucratic capacity, scientific rationality and expanding markets are real processes, and denying them is not available to a serious answer. What converts description into theory. Five assumptions: a linear sequence of broadly comparable stages; convergence of institutional differences as capacity grows; a traditional/modern contrast in which inherited authority yields to rational, mobile institutions; diffusion of capital, knowledge and technology as a transmission mechanism; and expert direction, the warrant for planners to identify obstacles and accelerate the transition. The four hostile objections, each located. Dependency denies sequence and convergence: underdevelopment can be produced through unequal incorporation rather than inherited as an earlier stage. Post-development denies expert direction: it questions the authority by which external experts define whole societies as deficient. Cultural and Gandhian critique denies the traditional/modern contrast, refusing to concede that one conception of the good life is simply superior. Marxist criticism denies convergence and diffusion by asking who owns production and appropriates the gains. And the Ambedkarite question tests the whole package: does modernisation dismantle graded hierarchy, or merely reorganise it into new occupations and offices? The objection to the critique. If no comparable sequence exists, cross-societal comparison becomes impossible and the idea of development collapses. Reply: what is denied is a single universal route, not comparability. Capacities such as being nourished, schooled, safe from premature death and able to participate are comparable without presupposing one path. Verdict. Modernisation names real structural transformations but is not a neutral or inevitable law; its institutions count as progress only where they enlarge equal capability, democratic agency and ecological security - which is plural development under universal constraints.

### Q3
(a) "Sustainable development is a policy formula, not a philosophy." Critically discuss with reference to moral standing, duties to future persons and the distribution of ecological burdens. [20 marks]
Model answer: Position. The statement is correct about the formula and wrong as a conclusion: the formula rests on philosophical claims that can be stated, and stating them is what gives it content. Moral standing - the first prior question. Three orientations differ over what counts morally, not over how much nature to protect. Anthropocentrism counts human beings and protects nature instrumentally, permitting destruction where no human interest is engaged. Biocentrism counts every individual living thing but cannot adjudicate between organisms or protect a system as such. Ecocentrism counts wholes but risks subordinating individuals to systemic goods. The discriminating question - would it be wrong to destroy a wilderness no human will ever see, use or know of? - shows they are distinct positions. For a development answer the defensible position is a strong, non-instrumental anthropocentrism supplemented by duties of stewardship, stated and defended rather than gestured at. Depth of questioning. Naess (1973) distinguishes shallow ecology, which fights pollution and depletion in the interest of the health and affluence of the developed world, from deep ecology, which questions the framework generating those symptoms and affirms intrinsic value in non-human life. The difference is one of depth of questioning, not degree of concern. Duties to future persons. The Brundtland Report (1987) builds duties to the unborn into the concept itself (paraphrased). Three problems must be met: the non-identity problem, since different policies produce different people and the person born into a degraded world owes her existence to the degrading policy; reciprocity, since future people cannot vote or sanction; and discounting, since a pure time preference counts a person for less merely because she exists later, which is arbitrary in the way that discounting distance is arbitrary. The duty is best specified as preserving options and avoiding irreversibility. Distribution of burdens. Guha and Martinez-Alier (1997) show that environmental conflict in the global South is typically a struggle over livelihood, so "environment versus development" is a framing available only to those whose subsistence is not at stake. The degrowth argument must therefore be applied asymmetrically - contraction and convergence - and its weakest link, that a non-growing economy can sustain employment and services, conceded. Verdict. Sustainability acquires content only when thresholds, distribution, participation and non-substitutable limits are specified; and the ecological case strengthens the argument for participation, since durable transformation requires the consent and knowledge of those whose livelihoods it reorganises.

(b) Assess the significance of the pluralist lineage - Figgis, Cole and MacIver - for Laski's rejection of monistic sovereignty. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The lineage strengthens Laski's rejection empirically and supplies the concession his own account most needs, but it must locate the school rather than replace the printed thinker. Laski's position. The state is one association among many. Individuals belong to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; associations are not permissions granted by sovereignty. Figgis. Churches and other real associations expose the danger of an absolute state allied with economic concentration. His significance is as the group-personality and anti-absorption precursor: he shows that associations have purposes of their own that the state did not create. Cole. A forerunner of pluralism associated with functional or guild representation and socially organised group life. Significance: he converts plural loyalty into a proposal about representation. The boundary matters - analysis should not become a theory of guild socialism. MacIver. Law and constitution, not a mysterious sovereign will, ground legitimate public force; the state coordinates associations without creating all their purposes. Significance: this is both the strongest sociological supplement to Laski and the honest statement of what pluralism still owes to monism. Assessment. Figgis supplies the moral urgency, Cole the institutional imagination and MacIver the residual coordination role. The last is decisive: because someone must settle conflicts between associations, MacIver's coordination claim measures exactly how much of the monist case survives. Control and verdict. Laski is the printed thinker and the marks follow him; the three names locate the school. The lineage therefore makes the rejection of monism harder to dismiss as one writer's preference, while making explicit that pluralism limits sovereignty rather than abolishing the function it names.

(c) Can humanism be a substitute for religion? Explain and evaluate in the context of the present Indian society. [15 marks]
Model answer: Frame first. "Substitute" is a functional relation: X substitutes for Y when it discharges Y's function. The question is therefore not whether humanism is truer or better than religion, but which of religion's several functions it can carry. Four functions, four verdicts. Moral orientation - humanism supplies this substantially, since suffering, reciprocity, dignity and responsibility can ground conduct; the residual is disagreement over an ultimate foundation. Public ethic - supplied fully, since common humanity and reason can justify equal civic standing; the residual is that abstract reason must learn from cultural and historical experience. Community and solidarity - supplied partly, since associations, education and service create fellowship, but inherited ritual and belonging may not be fully replaceable. Meaning, transcendence and existential consolation - supplied only partly and for some persons; secular humanism does not reproduce every religious horizon. The Indian argument. In a deeply religious and plural society, a humanism that demanded irreligion would violate the equal conscience that secularism exists to protect. Gandhi and Vivekananda show that human concern and faith can reinforce each other; M. N. Roy shows that an ethical humanism can also be naturalistic. India therefore supplies both halves of the answer at once. Verdict. Humanism can substitute for religion's ethical and civic role, but not necessarily for its full communal, meaning-conferring or transcendent role. It is best defended as a universal floor compatible with, and critical of, plural religious consciences - not as their compulsory replacement. One concession completes the evaluation: for a believer who holds the four functions to be inseparable because a single belief grounds them all, the analysis does not apply, which is why the verdict says not necessarily rather than not.

### Q4
(a) Critically examine the claim that the four printed thinkers on sovereignty are answering four different questions rather than giving four answers to one. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The claim is largely true and analytically productive, but it must be qualified: the four questions overlap at one point - every position must say what makes closure possible - and it is that overlap which makes comparison legitimate. The four questions. Bodin and Austin ask who is sovereign. Kelsen and Hart deny that this is the right question and ask what makes a norm valid. Laski denies that any single answer is possible and asks how allegiance is actually distributed. Kautilya asks what the holder of authority is for. Bodin and Austin - the "who" question. Bodin answers: the commonwealth's supreme law-giving authority, absolute and perpetual, limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm. Austin answers: a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk of society, whose authority is illimitable within the legal order. Kelsen and Hart - the validity question. Because "is" does not yield "ought", validity cannot rest on the fact of obedience; Kelsen derives it from a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm (1945), and Hart from a rule of recognition existing as convergent official practice (1961). Laski - the allegiance question. The state is one association among many and must compete for allegiance by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life; monistic sovereignty misdescribes society and licenses domination. Kautilya - the purpose question. The ruler heads a seven-limbed state, governs through calibrated coercion, and is measured by security and welfare; authority is justified by what it delivers. Why the reframing is productive. Read as four answers to one question, the positions look like a disagreement of degree about how much authority is too much. Read as four questions, each exposes a blind spot in the others: Austin cannot see facilities, Kelsen and Hart cannot see danger, Laski cannot see emergency closure, and Kautilya cannot see consent. The qualification. The questions are not wholly independent. Every position must explain how a political community reaches closure, and each does: Bodin through an undivided title, Austin through a determinate superior, Kelsen through a presupposed apex, Hart through an accepted criterion, Laski through a final procedure, Kautilya through a duty-bound head. Comparison is therefore legitimate on that axis and illegitimate on axes the thinkers do not share. Verdict. The claim should be accepted with that amendment: four questions, one shared problem.

(b) Critically analyse the social and political significance of Ambedkar's notion of annihilation of caste. [15 marks]
Model answer: What annihilation means. Not the removal of caste feeling by exhortation, and not the abolition of cultural difference. Caste is sustained by belief in the sacred authority of rules that rank and separate; isolated reforms leave that foundation intact; inter-dining does not open the marriage circle; inter-caste marriage attacks endogamy and creates social kinship; and annihilation requires rejection or radical reconstruction of scriptural authority, social rights and material relations. Social significance. It relocates the target from practices to the marriage circle and to the valuation of persons. Caste is ranked separation, not plurality: it fragments society into unequal, mutually closed groups, destroys fraternity, and denies free choice of occupation and association. Annihilation therefore aims at association and worth, not at etiquette. Political significance. Because caste narrows loyalty and obstructs public spirit, it disables the disposition to common action on which public life depends. A political democracy resting on graded social worth is unstable - one person one vote and one vote one value against unequal worth. Annihilation is thus a condition of social democracy, not a supplement to it. Objection. The argument proves too much: if the sanction is sacred, nothing short of dismantling a religious tradition can help. Reply: the fifth move names authority, rights and material relations together; rights and material relations are alterable politically, and the claim is about insufficiency, not impossibility. Residual limit. How the sanction is dislodged where conversion is not chosen remains underspecified, and state competence over belief is limited. Verdict. Its significance is that it reclassifies caste from a set of objectionable practices into a legitimated order of worth - which is why reform of the practice had repeatedly left the allocation intact.

(c) Can Theocracy be accepted as a valid form of Government? Give reasons and justification in support of your answer. [15 marks]
Model answer: Name the test first. Validity here means acceptability as a general form of government, and the applicable tests are consent, equal citizenship, public reason and revisability. Without naming them, any verdict is assertion. The case in favour, stated charitably. Theocracy claims moral unity and collective purpose; it restrains purely self-interested politics by subjecting rulers to a law they cannot rewrite; and it offers a stable ethical order derived from sacred obligation. The second point is genuinely serious: purely procedural politics supplies no moral limit on what a majority may will. The case against. Citizens of different faiths or none receive no equal standing; liberty of conscience is compromised; dissent becomes impiety rather than legitimate disagreement; clerical mediation produces an interpretive monopoly; and adaptation to a discourse of rights becomes difficult. The decisive structural point. The defect is generated by theocracy's own strongest claim. A law that no human may amend must still be applied, so the interpreter acquires political power insulated from criticism, since objection to him can be recast as objection to the source. Objection and reply. Objection: applying consent and equal-citizenship tests to a form that rejects them begs the question. Reply: the internal/general distinction avoids the circle - theocracy may be coherent and valid within a community of shared belief, and it is the plurality of the society, not the critic's premises, that defeats it as a general form. Verdict. Theocracy may claim internal validity for believers, but as a general form of government in a plural modern society it fails the tests of equal citizenship, public reason and accountability. Judged by moral limitation of the ruler, however, it identifies a real deficiency in purely procedural politics - one that constitutional rights can meet without confessional sovereignty.

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) "Liberation is a family-resemblance term, not the name of a single state." Examine this claim with reference to at least four Indian schools. [10 marks]
Model answer: The claim. One English word covers destinations that differ in what ends, what remains and whether consciousness is present. Four schools. Advaita: ignorance ends, Brahman alone remains, and awareness is self-luminous - liberation as identity. Visistadvaita: karmic bondage ends, the eternal individual soul remains in communion with God - liberation as communion, never absorption. Nyaya-Vaisesika: pain and the qualities sustaining experience end, the self-substance remains without consciousness - liberation as cessation. Samkhya-Yoga: non-discrimination ends, purusa stands alone, conscious but contentless - liberation as isolation. Add Jainism, where karmic matter ends and a perfected omniscient individual remains, and Buddhism, where craving ends and no self is asserted at all. Assessment. These are not degrees of one state; identity and communion are mutually exclusive, and cessation excludes the very awareness that identity requires. Conclusion. The shared vocabulary conceals incompatible destinations, and the first task in any liberation answer is to say which one is meant.

(b) "Analogy protects transcendence only by sacrificing intelligibility." Examine with reference to Aquinas and Scotus. (Answer within 150 words.) [10 marks]
Model answer: The charge identifies analogy's risk but overstates its failure. Aquinas rejects anthropomorphic univocity and empty equivocity. Pure perfection exists in God essentially and eminently: the res significata applies truly, while creaturely modus significandi remains inadequate. Causal participation supplies ordered continuity. Scotus objects that inference requires a semantically univocal concept of being; otherwise analogy disguises equivocation. His certain-and-doubtful argument allows one to be certain that God is a being while doubtful whether finite or infinite. Intrinsic modes restore ontological difference. The Thomist reply is that causal dependence, not a common genus, grounds the middle term. Yet analogy must explain how its proportion is fixed. It preserves qualified intelligibility, not transparency. A defensible conclusion combines analogical predication, enough conceptual continuity for inference, and an apophatic limit on adequacy.

(c) Does the fact that religion motivates moral conduct show anything about the grounding of moral obligation? Examine. [10 marks]
Model answer: It shows nothing about grounding, and the reason is structural rather than empirical. Religion's motivational contribution is real and identifiable: narrative exemplars, ritual formation, community sanction, hope and repentance, and a worldview in which sustained moral effort is intelligible. Behavioural evidence of religious influence on conduct is therefore unsurprising. But grounding, knowledge, motivation and sanction are four separable relations. Grounding asks what constitutes the validity of an obligation; motivation asks what supplies the operative reason to comply. A cause of compliance is not a constituent of obligation, and success in one column proves nothing in another. The point is sharpened by a canonical case: a vow of non-violence may be strengthened by religious discipline while its moral defensibility still requires reasons accessible beyond the community. The strengthening is genuine; the justification is elsewhere. Two further controls follow. Influence is contingent - the same mechanisms can sanction exclusion. And correlation may itself require controls for community, selection and orientation. Religion may therefore be motivationally indispensable to a particular agent and grounding-irrelevant at the same time.

(d) Explain how Buddhism can sustain a religious form of life without belief in a creator God. [10 marks]
Model answer: Religion need not be defined by belief in a creator. Buddhism and Jainism organise doctrine, discipline, community and liberation without a sovereign God. Their objects of reverence are awakened teachers, perfected beings, truth and a path of transformation rather than a creator who commands obedience. Worship can therefore express gratitude, emulation and moral self-formation. Transcendence also need not mean a supernatural person: nirvana transcends craving and conditioned suffering, while Jain liberation releases the soul from karmic bondage. The objection is that ultimate law, the Buddha ideal or liberated beings may perform functions commonly assigned to God. Yet functional similarity is not identity. Neither dependent origination nor karma is an omniscient will, and liberation is not divine grace. Non-theistic traditions thus show that religion can centre on diagnosis, practice and transformation. They do not covertly restore God unless every object of ultimacy is defined as divine, a definition so broad that it erases the distinction under examination. The institutional test is equally important: monastic rules, confession, pilgrimage and ethical vows can sustain a religious form of life without prayer to a creator. The concept of religion should therefore be family-resemblance based, not tied to one Abrahamic model of belief.

(e) Discuss the paradox of omnipotence of God in the context of the existence of evil. [10 marks]
Model answer: Mackie (Mind, 1955) frames the paradox as a dilemma: can an omnipotent being create things it cannot afterwards control? If it cannot, its second-order power - power to determine what powers exist - is limited. If it can and does, its first-order power over those things is limited. Unrestricted omnipotence in both senses is therefore incoherent. Three restrictions dissolve it. Omnipotence may extend only to the logically possible, which almost all theists concede. Or it may be second-order sovereignty freely self-bound in the act of creating free agents. Or it may be abandoned, as in process theism, where power is intrinsically distributed and divine power is persuasive by metaphysical necessity. The connection to evil is exact. The free-will defence requires one of these restrictions: God creates significantly free agents and cannot causally guarantee that they choose well. For Plantinga this restraint is chosen; for Griffin it is metaphysically necessary. Verdict: the paradox does not refute theism; it forces theism to specify which sense of omnipotence it is defending, and to pay for the choice.

### Q6
(a) Can reason assess a claimed revelation without becoming its final authority? Critically examine the relation between faith, reason and revelation. [20 marks]
Model answer: Faith, reason and revelation answer different questions. Revelation claims a disclosure unavailable to unaided inquiry; faith is the trusting commitment by which a person receives it; reason tests meaning, consistency and implications. A strict fideism associated with Tertullian or Kierkegaard protects the existential risk of faith, but it cannot distinguish revelation from fanaticism when rival claims conflict. At the opposite extreme, rationalism accepts only what can be independently proved and thereby reduces revelation to dispensable philosophy. Aquinas offers a mediating account: natural reason can establish some truths about God, while mysteries such as Trinity exceed but do not contradict reason. Indian traditions supply parallel models. Nyaya defends testimony through the competence and trustworthiness of the speaker; Mimamsa treats Vedic testimony as intrinsically authoritative; Buddhism asks claims to withstand inquiry and experiential testing. Reason therefore need not be the source of revelation to remain its critical tribunal. It can expose contradiction, coercion, failed prediction and morally destructive interpretation, while acknowledging that commitment also involves trust and practice. The balanced position is critical faith: revelation may enlarge the field of reasons, but no appeal to revelation should be insulated from coherence, evidence, ethical scrutiny and dialogue with rival claims. This position distinguishes assessment from authorship. A court can test whether a religious restriction violates equal dignity without deciding the truth of the creed; similarly, philosophy can examine a revelation's conceptual coherence without claiming to generate its sacred content. Historical transmission also matters: testimony reaches believers through language, institutions and interpretation, all of which are fallible. Critical reason is therefore unavoidable even in identifying what has allegedly been revealed. Faith remains distinctive because commitment outruns demonstration, but it should be proportioned to the quality of testimony and remain corrigible when interpretation produces contradiction or grave injustice.

(b) "Karma explains the distribution of suffering, but the explanation carries a moral cost." Discuss with reference to the theistic and non-theistic schools. [15 marks]
Model answer: The explanatory claim. Karma answers a question theism finds hard: not why suffering exists, but why this person suffers. Act and its unseen potency (adrsta/apurva) mature as stored, fructifying and future karma within a beginningless series, so present condition is consequence rather than allocation. The two administrations. In Buddhism, Jainism and Mimamsa the law is self-regulating: no judge is required, and the moral order is a basic feature of how things work. In Nyaya and the theistic Vedantas God is the superintendent (karmadhyaksa) who ratifies and dispenses desert rather than authoring it. The fork matters: the first purchases economy at the price of a law without a legislator; the second retains a personal God at the price of subordinating him to a law he did not make. The moral cost. Because present suffering is read as the maturation of past action, the doctrine can be turned into an inference from suffering to fault. That inference is (i) epistemically unwarranted, since no one has access to the ledger, and (ii) practically corrosive, since it converts a metaphysical explanation into a social verdict. The replies. The traditions themselves block the inference: explanatory karma does not license social judgement, and the duty of compassion and relief is untouched by any account of desert. Beginninglessness also removes the "original fault" framing that most easily supports blame. Assessment. The explanatory gain is real and distinctive; the cost is not intrinsic to the doctrine but is easily produced by careless use. Conclusion. Karma should be presented as an account of why the world is orderly, never as an instrument for reading a sufferer's past.

(c) How far does Jain many-sidedness (anekantavada) resolve the problem of conflicting religious truth-claims? (Answer in about 250 words.) [15 marks]
Model answer: It resolves a substantial part of the problem and leaves a residue that must be conceded. The apparatus is three-tiered. Many-sidedness (anekantavada) holds reality to be many-sided; standpoint theory (nayavada) makes explicit the standpoint from which a judgement is made; conditional predication (syadvada) prefixes assertions with syat, "in a certain respect", and the sevenfold scheme (saptabhangi) enumerates the modes. Framework-relativity is thereby marked inside the proposition rather than left implicit. This is not relativism. Relativism denies that there is a fact of the matter; conditional predication states the respect under which a predicate holds, so the assertions remain determinate and checkable. Predication is regulated, not abandoned. Its power appears at two points. In the absolute-truth debate it denies the premise that a finite formulation can exhaust the absolute, so belief in one absolute Truth no longer entails exclusivism. In the epistemology of disagreement it addresses Fogelin's problem directly: deep disagreement arises because parties do not share the framework against which evidence would count, and the Jain device makes that framework explicit - a genuine Indian contribution to a live Western problem, and worth far more than the parable of the elephant. Its limit is exact. The apparatus works wherever a difference of aspect, level or soteriological orientation can be specified. Where two claims contradict on the same question in the same respect - a personal creator exists, or does not - no index of standpoint helps. There, pluralism must distinguish literal conflict, symbolic language and different soteriological aims, and acknowledge whatever contradiction survives.

### Q7
(a) Compare the God-world-human relation in Spinoza, Samkara and Ramanuja, and assess which model best preserves both metaphysical ultimacy and religious life. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Judged by metaphysical economy Spinoza wins, by non-anthropomorphic ultimacy Samkara wins, and by religious adequacy Ramanuja wins; so the assessment must name its criterion before it names a winner. The common axes. Three questions discipline the comparison: what is the world's ontological status, what is the human self, and what religious relation remains available? Spinoza. There is one infinite substance, God or Nature (Deus sive Natura), expressed through infinitely many attributes, of which we know Thought and Extension; finite minds and bodies are modes. God is the immanent, not the transitive, cause, and acts from the necessity of the divine nature, so freedom means self-determination rather than choice. Religious life becomes the intellectual love of God - understanding the necessary whole - while petition and grace disappear. Samkara. Brahman alone is real; the world is apparent transformation (vivarta), empirically available but sublatable; the self is not a part or mode but Brahman itself. The personal Lord is Brahman at the transactional level, so worship is valid but not final. Ultimacy is protected at the cost of making the worshipper's standpoint provisional. Ramanuja. Brahman is a qualified unity whose body is the real plurality of selves (cit) and matter (acit), related by inseparability (aprthaksiddhi); the world is real transformation (parinama), and God is both material and efficient cause. Selves remain eternally real, so devotion and surrender are ultimate rather than provisional. The objection is that defect in the body should infect the indwelling self; the reply distinguishes the controller's perfection from the condition of what is controlled. Assessment. If the criterion is parsimony, one substance with modes is unbeatable - but it abolishes address. If the criterion is freedom from anthropomorphism, non-dualism is strongest - but it sublates the devotee. If the criterion is the intelligibility of worship, qualified non-dualism is strongest - but it must keep answering the defect objection. Verdict. No model secures both goods without remainder; Ramanuja pays the smallest religious price, and Samkara the smallest anthropomorphic one.

(b) "Karma answers the question that secular ethics leaves open, but at a cost of its own." Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: The claim is largely right about the answer and right to insist on the cost. The open question is the undetected, costly, unreciprocated act - Glaucon's Ring of Gyges, Hobbes's Foole, Hume's sensible knave, whom Hume concedes he cannot fully answer. Every secular reply stops at the same place. Kant explains why the law binds, not why this agent will obey it. Gauthier's constrained maximisation needs dispositions to be detectable. Eudaimonism needs a thick nature the sceptic may decline. Reciprocity has an explicit exception clause for exactly the undetected case. Karma closes that gap in a way no theistic sanction does as cleanly. It is exhaustive - there is no unobserved act - and impersonal - there is no judge to petition or deceive. The Ring of Gyges presupposes that invisibility to persons matters; where the moral order is coextensive with the causal order, it does not. The Gita strengthens the position further: niskama-karma (2.47, 3.19) removes the fruit of action from deliberation, so the prudential question is not merely answered but dissolved. The costs are two. The first is unverifiability, which is the standing objection to this grounding as arbitrariness is to divine command and formalism to Kant. The second, if karma is presented as cosmic bookkeeping, is that it becomes prudence on a larger scale - Gyges with a bigger ring. The tradition's own answer to the second cost is niskama-karma, which is why the citation matters rather than decorates. A control must accompany any use of the doctrine: karma motivates the agent's own accountability and licenses no observer's inference of guilt from another's suffering. Karma therefore answers the motivational question with a reach no secular theory matches, and pays for it in verifiability rather than in moral worth - which is a better bargain than divine sanction makes.

(c) Discuss the relationship between man and God according to any one of the religions in India. [15 marks]
Model answer: In Ramanuja's Visistadvaita, the relation between man and God is neither numerical identity nor external dualism. Reality is one Brahman qualified by cit, conscious selves, and acit, non-conscious matter. The individual jiva is therefore a real and eternal mode or qualifier of Brahman, and the world is God's real transformation (parinama) rather than an appearance. Ramanuja explains this through the sarira-sariri relation. As a body is controlled by, supported by and exists for its indwelling self, so every jiva is the "body" of God, who is the inner controller (antaryamin). The relation is aprthaksiddhi: the self is distinct from God but cannot exist or be conceived independently of God. Hence the jiva is not Brahman, although it is inseparably dependent upon Brahman. This ontology grounds religious life. The person remains a genuine knower, agent and bearer of karma; devotion is therefore not directed by an illusory self toward a provisional deity. Bondage contracts the self's manifested consciousness, while liberation expands it through knowledge, bhakti, self-surrender (prapatti) and divine grace. Liberation is loving communion and service, not the dissolution of individuality. The body analogy preserves unity and difference, but raises the objection that embodied evil may implicate God. Ramanuja distinguishes defects of the dependent body from the perfection of its controller, though divine responsibility remains a live pressure, and he adds that God consents to (anumati) rather than authors each finite act. The relationship is best described as distinct, dependent and inseparable communion.

### Q8
(a) Discuss the main points of distinction between a priori and a posteriori arguments for the existence of God. Which one according to you should be preferred over the other? Give reasons and justifications for your answer. [20 marks]
Model answer: The distinction is not merely one of subject matter but of evidential source, method, modal outcome and characteristic vulnerability. An a priori argument reasons from the concept of God alone, without empirical premises. The ontological argument is the sole classical instance: Anselm derives existence, and then necessary existence, from the concept of that than which nothing greater can be conceived; Descartes derives it from the essence of a supremely perfect being. Its method is conceptual analysis, and its promised reward is enormous - if it works, it yields a necessary God, immune to empirical revision. An a posteriori argument reasons from some general feature of the world. Aquinas' Ways begin from motion, causation, contingency, degrees and purposive order; Paley begins from means-end adjustment; Kant's moral argument begins from the structure of moral obligation; and Nyaya's Isvara-anumana begins from the world as a composite effect requiring an omniscient intelligent maker. Their method is inference from effect to cause, or inference to the best explanation, and their conclusions are correspondingly probable rather than necessary. Their vulnerabilities differ accordingly. The a priori route faces Kant's charge that existence is not a real predicate, and Gaunilo's demonstration that the form proves too much; modal repairs shift the burden to an unestablished possibility premise. The a posteriori route faces Hume: the world-artefact analogy is weak, and from a finite effect one may infer only a finite cause. It is also exposed to scientific advance - Darwin against Paley, cosmological alternatives against the Kalam - whereas the a priori route is immune to empirical developments precisely because it is empirically empty. Preference. I prefer the a posteriori route, on three grounds. First, dialectical: it begins from premises the opponent already grants - that things change, that order exists, that we are obligated - whereas the a priori route asks the opponent to grant a contested concept. Second, epistemic: it does not require the illegitimate treatment of existence as a determining predicate. Third, traditional convergence: two independent civilisations, Thomist Europe and Nyaya India, arrived at structurally similar a posteriori arguments, which suggests the route tracks something real in the explanatory demand. But the preference must be graded. The price is that a posteriori arguments deliver at most a probable, possibly finite cause, and never the God of developed religion. If the criterion were modal strength rather than evidential contact, the ranking would reverse.

(b) Examine whether a quality-transcending absolute can perform the religious functions performed by a personal God. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. A quality-transcending absolute can perform the contemplative religious functions completely and the dialogical ones only by relocating them to a lower level; whether that relocation is a solution or a concession is the point at issue. The functions in question. A personal God supports petition, providence, grace, command, forgiveness and covenantal address, because knowing, willing and loving are predicated of it - analogically, not univocally. The Advaita reply. Samkara does not abolish these functions; he assigns them to the personal Lord (Isvara), Brahman understood through the cosmic power of appearance at the transactional level (vyavaharika), while quality-transcending Brahman is ultimate (paramarthika). Negation (neti neti, Brhadaranyaka Upanisad 2.3.6) removes limiting predicates, not reality; Brahman remains being, consciousness and bliss as its essential nature. What the absolute does better. It secures non-anthropomorphic ultimacy and unity, and it disarms the objection that finite psychology has been projected upward. Contemplative realisation - the recognition of an identity never actually lost - needs no responsive interlocutor at all. Objection and reply. Ramanuja objects that an entity with no determinations whatever is neither knowable, nameable nor worshippable, so Brahman must be qualified (savisesa). The Advaitin replies that what is denied is limitation, not determinacy as such, and that devotion is genuinely valid at its own level. The residual difficulty is that the worshipper's standpoint is finally sublated, so devotion is preserved as provisional rather than ultimate. Verdict. On a contemplative criterion the absolute performs every function; on a relational criterion it performs them only at a level it also cancels. The honest conclusion states the criterion first and then the cost.

(c) What role do the concepts of evil and profane play to provide a firm foundation to religion? [15 marks]
Model answer: The question is functional, not theological: it asks what work negative categories do in constituting religion. Durkheim, in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), holds that all known religious beliefs presuppose a classification of all things into two opposed classes, profane and sacred, and defines religion as a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, uniting adherents into a single moral community, a church. The definition is deliberately God-free. A correction must be made at once: the profane is the ordinary, not the wicked; evil, impurity and the demonic are three further and distinct categories. Otto's The Idea of the Holy (1917) supplies the object-side - the numinous, mysterium tremendum et fascinans - while Eliade's The Sacred and the Profane (1957) shows the sacred irrupting as hierophany, generating sacred space around an axis mundi and recoverable sacred time. The founding sequence follows. A religion first names a predicament - sin, impurity, suffering (duhkha), ignorance (avidya), karmic bondage. The predicament is marked by a boundary - pure and impure, sacred and profane - which generates ritual, prohibition and moral code. The boundary generates institutions: priesthood, initiation, purification, penance. The predicament generates a soteriology, the ordered path out. Indian material is the clearest case: the Buddhist First Noble Truth makes suffering the explicit foundation of an entire religion; Jain asrava and bandha name the predicament and samvara and nirjara the exit. India's founding defect is more often ignorance or bondage than moral evil, which yields diagnosis-and-insight rather than sin-and-forgiveness. Verdict: the binary is an ideal type, not a law, and wonder and gratitude are independent religious roots; but evil and the profane found religion structurally - they supply the predicament and the boundary without which soteriology would be idle. They found religion, not theism.
