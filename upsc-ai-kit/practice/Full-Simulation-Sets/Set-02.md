### Q1
Which statement best describes institutional features absent from India's Article 368 process?

A. There is no referendum, no special constituent convention and no joint sitting to resolve House disagreement.
B. A referendum is mandatory, but a constituent convention is optional.
C. A joint sitting is available, though State ratification is not.
D. A special constituent body must approve every amendment before Parliament votes.

**Answer: A**
Explanation: India entrusts amendment to Parliament and, selectively, State legislatures without direct popular ratification or a separate amending assembly.
Trap: Do not confuse Parliament acting in a constituent capacity with the creation of a separate constituent body.

### A1. Silence may preserve peace for a day and injustice for a generation.
Thesis: Silence buys immediate calm at compounding cost. What is not named cannot be remedied, and an unremedied wrong hardens into custom that later generations struggle even to recognise as wrong. The proposition contrasts the short-run value of quiet with the long-run price of a normalised injury.
Structure: Distinguish prudent restraint from complicit silence. | Show how unspoken wrongs acquire the authority of tradition. | Trace speech that changed law, from social reform to workplace rights. | Concede the genuine value of timing and confidentiality in negotiation and communal tension. | Offer a test for the point at which silence becomes complicity.
Dimensions: Moral courage is costly and unequally distributed, since whoever is best placed to see a wrong is often least able to survive reporting it. | Reform movements converted private suffering into public argument, which is why testimony almost always precedes remedial legislation. | Diplomatic quiet can protect negotiations and lives, yet permanent silence about atrocity signals that violation carries no reputational price. | Law responds to articulated harm, so an injury lacking a public name possesses neither a remedy nor even a statistic.
Examples: The Vishaka guidelines of 1997 followed Bhanwari Devi's refusal to stay silent about sexual violence at work. | Satyendra Dubey's murder in 2003 after exposing highway corruption led towards whistle-blower protection legislation in 2014. | Rammohan Roy's public campaign against sati showed reform requiring open argument rather than private moral discomfort. | The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 made reporting a legal duty instead of a personal choice.
Counterview: Speech is not automatically just. Premature disclosure has collapsed hostage negotiations, and public accusation without evidence has destroyed innocent reputations and inflamed communal violence. Rumour amplified as courage kills. The defensible claim is narrower: silence is culpable when it shields the powerful, and prudent only when it shields the vulnerable.
Conclusion: Judge silence by its beneficiary. Quiet that protects a perpetrator or an institution is complicity dressed as civility; quiet that protects a survivor or an ongoing remedy may be the braver choice.

### A2. The value of knowledge lies partly in knowing its limits.
Thesis: Knowledge grows dangerous when it forgets its own boundary conditions. A model trained on one context, a statistic collected for one purpose, or an expert judgement offered beyond its field carries the authority of evidence without its warrant. Knowing the limit is therefore part of the knowledge, not a concession made against it.
Structure: Explain that every finding carries a domain of validity. | Show forecasting failures as boundary failures rather than mere arithmetic errors. | Examine expert advice in government and the problem of unstated assumptions. | Discuss decision-making under uncertainty through precaution and revisability. | Warn against the opposite error of dismissing expertise altogether.
Dimensions: Scientific claims remain conditional on method, sample and context, so transferring them across settings silently changes what is being asserted. | Governance must decide before evidence matures, which makes stated assumptions and a review date part of honest technical advice. | Expertise deserves weight without deference, because specialists share the incentives, blind spots and institutional interests of their own field. | Uncertainty is manageable through precaution, staged commitment and reversible choices rather than through a performance of confidence.
Examples: Early COVID-19 projections in 2020 varied enormously, and policy improved once models were published with assumptions and ranges. | Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum (1996) read the precautionary principle into Indian environmental jurisprudence. | Environmental impact assessments lose credibility when project proponents commission the studies establishing acceptable risk. | Groundwater estimates based on average recharge concealed local exhaustion across Punjab and parts of western India.
Counterview: Excessive humility is exploited. Regulated industries routinely amplify residual uncertainty to postpone action, and officials invoke complexity to escape accountable choice, while citizens need usable guidance rather than hedged prose. Acknowledging limits must therefore accompany a clear recommendation, a named decision-maker and a date for reconsideration.
Conclusion: Mature knowledge states what it knows, what it assumes and what would falsify it. In public life that discipline converts expertise from an authority claim into an accountable input citizens can weigh for themselves.

### A3. Identity is a home when open, and a prison when sealed.
Thesis: Belonging supplies language, memory and the confidence to act, which is why identity functions as a home. It becomes confinement once membership is policed, exit is punished and the group's boundary matters more than its members' freedom. The proposition measures identity by whether a person may leave it, question it or combine it.
Structure: Establish the enabling functions of belonging. | Identify what seals an identity: policed exit, internal hierarchy, external threat. | Test India's federal and constitutional accommodation of plural identity. | Examine migration, where identity travels but entitlements often do not. | Conclude on rights of exit and overlapping membership.
Dimensions: Culture supplies usable competence and confidence, since language, craft and ritual are resources a person actually deploys in daily life. | Pluralism sustains common citizenship without demanding uniformity, provided group authority never overrides an individual's constitutional rights. | Nationalism becomes civic or exclusionary depending on whether membership rests on allegiance to shared institutions or on ascribed descent. | Migration tests openness, because domicile rules, language and documentation decide whether a newcomer is treated as citizen or guest.
Examples: The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 accommodated linguistic identity within the Union instead of treating it as incipient secession. | The Sixth Schedule and Article 371 provisions protect distinct tribal and regional arrangements inside one constitutional framework. | One Nation One Ration Card portability lets a migrant claim food entitlements outside her home state. | The Eighth Schedule's recognition of twenty-two languages supports plural public life without displacing shared civic identity.
Counterview: Openness is often demanded only of the weak. Small linguistic and tribal communities that dissolve their boundaries frequently lose land, language and bargaining power to a dominant culture that never opens itself in return. Some closure is protective, and constitutional safeguards exist precisely to secure it for vulnerable groups.
Conclusion: A defensible identity is one a member could revise without exile and a stranger could enter without humiliation. India's constitutional design aims at exactly that combination of protection without imprisonment.

### A4. Care is an economic infrastructure hidden in private life.
Thesis: Every worker who reaches an office was fed, nursed and raised by labour national accounts never priced. Care qualifies as infrastructure because output collapses without it, yet it is classified as affection rather than production and its cost is charged silently to women. The proposition reclassifies a private duty as a public input.
Structure: Show that care meets the functional definition of infrastructure. | Explain how measurement failure produces policy failure. | Connect the care burden to women's employment and health outcomes. | Consider public provision, workplace design and social security responses. | Address the risk of professionalising care badly.
Dimensions: Time-use evidence shows unpaid domestic and care work absorbing a large share of women's day, directly constraining paid employment and rest. | Female labour force participation responds to childcare, eldercare and safe transport at least as strongly as it responds to wages or training. | Health systems quietly depend on family caregivers for nursing, medication adherence and rehabilitation after a patient is discharged. | Social policy can socialise part of the burden through creches, parental leave, old-age support and decent wages for care workers.
Examples: India's Time Use Survey of 2019 recorded women spending far more time than men on unpaid domestic work. | MGNREGA's worksite creche requirement recognises childcare as a condition of women's participation in public works. | Accredited Social Health Activists perform community health duties for honoraria rather than regular wages and benefits. | The Maternity Benefit Amendment Act, 2017 extended paid leave while concentrating the cost on formal-sector employers.
Counterview: Treating care as infrastructure invites two errors. Marketised care can become low-paid, insecure work performed largely by poorer women for wealthier households. And measuring care does not redistribute it: without men's participation and enforceable employer obligation, valuation may simply add statistics to an unchanged burden.
Conclusion: The practical test is whether caring costs a woman her job. Creches, portable entitlements, decent pay for care workers and shared domestic responsibility would turn a hidden subsidy into a recognised, financed public input.

### B5. India's demographic future depends more on capability than on numbers.
Thesis: A young population is an opportunity only where the young can read, stay healthy, find productive work and choose their own lives. Numbers create potential; capability converts it into output and freedom. The proposition locates India's demographic outcome in classrooms, clinics and workplaces rather than in population arithmetic.

Dimensions: Foundational literacy and numeracy determine whether additional years of schooling yield competence or merely accumulate certificates. | Nutrition and anaemia shape cognition and stamina, making health spending an economic policy rather than only a welfare transfer. | Employment quality matters more than headcount, since informal low-productivity work cannot absorb rising educational aspiration. | Women's low participation withholds nearly half the potential workforce, turning safety, care support and social norms into macroeconomic variables.
Examples: ASER surveys have repeatedly found many rural children in higher grades unable to read a Standard II text. | NIPUN Bharat, launched in 2021, targets foundational literacy and numeracy as a measurable national mission. | Kerala's early investment in schooling and public health preceded both its demographic transition and its human development record. | National Family Health Survey rounds show persistent anaemia among adolescent girls and women of reproductive age.
Counterview: Capability without demand becomes frustration. Better-trained graduates entering a slow-growing labour market produce educated unemployment and out-migration, as several Indian states already show. Skilling cannot substitute for manufacturing, urban services and small-firm growth, so supply-side capability and demand-side investment must advance together.
Conclusion: India's advantage is temporary and unevenly spread across states. Whether it becomes a dividend depends on foundational learning, women's employment and job creation arriving before the working-age share begins to shrink.

### B6. Climate resilience is a test of federalism as much as of technology.
Thesis: Panels, forecasts and flood models are necessary but inert without a government close enough to act on them. A warning must become an evacuation and heat data must become a changed work schedule, and those conversions happen in districts and wards. The proposition relocates climate capacity from laboratories to the federal chain of command.
Structure: Show adaptation as inherently local while mitigation remains national and global. | Trace the chain from national mission to municipal implementation. | Examine finance and staffing as the binding constraint on local action. | Add equity, since exposure and recovery differ sharply within one city. | Conclude on coordination achieved without recentralisation.
Dimensions: Adaptation is place-specific, since drainage, heat stress and crop damage differ across districts even inside a single state. | Fiscal devolution decides capacity, because assigned functions without predictable funds and trained staff produce plans instead of protection. | Scientific inputs need translation, as forecasts help only when converted into local protocols for hospitals, schools and workplaces. | Equity determines outcomes, since informal settlements and outdoor workers face the highest exposure and the slowest recovery.
Examples: State Action Plans on Climate Change created state frameworks whose implementation still depends on district and municipal capacity. | Ahmedabad's municipal heat plan assigned duties to hospitals, water suppliers and labour departments during declared alerts. | The Fifteenth Finance Commission provided distinct mitigation and response windows within disaster risk management funding. | Odisha's cyclone shelters and trained community volunteers turned central forecasts into completed evacuations.
Counterview: Localism has limits. Basins, coastlines and airsheds cross municipal and state boundaries, and small local bodies can neither finance protective infrastructure nor resist powerful land interests. Some resilience needs central standards, interstate coordination and financing that overrides local preference, so devolved execution requires binding national norms.
Conclusion: Resilience is federal engineering. It needs a mayor with money, a district officer with usable data, and a national framework that makes both obligations mandatory rather than merely exemplary.

### B7. Digital public infrastructure must expand agency, not merely transactions.
Thesis: Counting payments, authentications and enrolments measures throughput rather than empowerment. Digital public infrastructure enlarges agency only when a person can obtain a service without an intermediary, understand what was done with her data, correct an error and carry her credentials elsewhere. The proposition demands a citizen-side test of success.
Structure: Define agency as choice, portability, comprehension and remedy. | Acknowledge the genuine scale achievement of India's digital rails. | Identify exclusion at the last mile. | Examine privacy and consent as conditions of agency rather than obstacles. | Address market concentration and the role of open standards.
Dimensions: Inclusion fails at the margin, where connectivity, worn fingerprints, missing documents and language exclude those most dependent on the service. | Privacy protects agency, since a person unable to know or contest data use cannot meaningfully consent to anything. | Open standards stop public rails from becoming private gatekeeping by keeping switching costs low and entry genuinely feasible. | Service delivery requires traceability, because a failed transaction without receipt, timeline and appeal is indistinguishable from denial.
Examples: UPI's interoperable design lets small merchants accept payments without being captured by a single payment application. | Puttaswamy (2017) requires legality, necessity and proportionality before the state intrudes upon informational privacy. | The Open Network for Digital Commerce attempts to unbundle discovery from platform control in online retail. | CoWIN issued vaccination certificates at national scale while relying on assisted registration for those without smartphones.
Counterview: Agency language can excuse inaction. Transaction efficiency itself delivers welfare: direct benefit transfers cut delay and diversion, and instant payments lowered the cost of credit and trade for small firms. Insisting on ideal empowerment before scaling would have denied millions the concrete gains already achieved.
Conclusion: Judge digital infrastructure by the citizen with the weakest connection and the thinnest paperwork. If she obtains the service, corrects her record and complains successfully, the system has expanded freedom rather than volume.

### B8. Agricultural prosperity requires moving from output security to income and ecological security.
Thesis: The postcolonial food emergency justified maximising tonnage, and that mission succeeded. The binding problems now are farm income, price volatility, aquifer depletion and soil health, none of which improve automatically with higher yields. The proposition asks for a changed objective for agricultural policy, not a repudiation of its earlier achievement.
Structure: Credit the output-security era for ending dependence on imported grain. | Show why yield growth no longer resolves stagnant farm incomes. | Read subsidies, procurement and cropping incentives as ecological signals. | Examine risk management and diversification as income strategies. | Sequence reform so that transition does not impoverish the cultivator.
Dimensions: Marketing reform matters because realised income depends on price, storage and market access rather than on physical production alone. | Water pricing and free power push cultivation towards thirsty crops in regions whose aquifers cannot sustain that choice. | Risk instruments such as insurance, credit and price support decide whether a bad season becomes indebtedness or a manageable setback. | Diversification into millets, horticulture, livestock and agroforestry spreads income sources while reducing ecological pressure at the same time.
Examples: Green Revolution procurement in Punjab and Haryana secured national grain supply while entrenching paddy in a semi-arid belt. | Punjab's Preservation of Subsoil Water Act, 2009 delayed paddy transplanting to cut evaporation-driven groundwater extraction. | Operation Flood built farmer-owned dairy cooperatives that raised rural incomes through processing and marketing. | The International Year of Millets in 2023 promoted crops requiring far less irrigation than rice in dryland regions.
Counterview: Ecological reform can become a middle-class demand financed by farmers. Withdrawing power subsidies or assured procurement without a substitute income transfers risk to cultivators carrying thin margins and heavy debt. Sequencing is therefore decisive: assured markets, remunerative prices for alternative crops and credible income support must precede withdrawal.
Conclusion: Food security remains non-negotiable, yet it is now compatible with lower water use and higher farm incomes. Reform must change what agricultural policy rewards without gambling with the farmer's income or the nation's plate.

### Q1 [10 marks]
Explain glacier mass balance and the apparent paradox of a retreating yet forward-flowing glacier.

Model answer (111 words): Mass balance controls geometry, while the terminus retreats when local loss exceeds ice delivery even though ice motion remains downslope. A glacier is compacted and recrystallised land ice thick enough to deform or slide under gravity; a persistent snowfield need not be a glacier because it may not flow. Accumulation adds snow and refrozen water, ablation removes mass, and net balance is their difference over a stated glacier and period. The equilibrium-line altitude balances annual accumulation and ablation; the climatic snowline is a long-term regional limit, while the snout is the glacier terminus. Ice can continue moving down-valley while the snout retreats when ablation at the terminus exceeds forward ice delivery.

### Q2 [10 marks]
Explain how wind and episodic water jointly create arid landforms.

Model answer (127 words): Aeolian erosion and deposition interact with weathering, flash runoff, fan building, playa flooding and evaporation. Large thermal ranges, salt crystallisation and episodic wetting weaken exposed rock, while sparse cover leaves debris available to wind and flash runoff. Wind moves coarse particles mainly by surface creep, sand by saltation and fine dust by suspension; deflation removes loose material and abrasion sandblasts exposed surfaces. Deflation hollows, desert pavement, ventifacts, yardangs, zeugens, rock pedestals and inselbergs reflect different combinations of wind, structure and weathering. A barchan has horns pointing downwind under limited sand and one prevailing wind; transverse, longitudinal, parabolic and star dunes indicate different sand, vegetation and wind regimes. Wadis, alluvial fans, bajadas, pediments, playas and salt flats show that episodic runoff and evaporation are integral to desert geomorphology.

### Q3 [10 marks]
Distinguish mineral-resource geography from energy geography with suitable Indian examples.

Model answer (111 words): Mineral geography asks where ores and industrial minerals occur, while energy geography asks where usable power can be produced, so India's shield-based ore belts and its solar and wind corridors do not overlap. Economic geography classifies minerals as ferrous metallic, non-ferrous metallic, non-metallic industrial and mineral fuels, so coal and petroleum are mineral resources and not merely fuels. Energy resources are classified twice over: conventional commercial against non-conventional newer sources, and exhaustible stock against renewable flow. Solar capacity concentrates in the dry high-insolation belts of Rajasthan and Gujarat while wind concentrates along Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Karnataka and Maharashtra coasts and passes, so the renewable map does not mirror the coal map.

### Q4 [10 marks]
Explain how caste is assuming new identities and associational forms in contemporary India.

Model answer (112 words): Caste is changing register rather than disappearing: ritual allocation and public purity enforcement have weakened while association, network capital and political mobilisation have expanded, and endogamy has changed least. Endogamy, hierarchy, hereditary membership and traditional occupational linkage are the four classically cited defining features of caste, and they weaken at very different rates. Urbanisation, schooling and non-farm employment have broken most hereditary occupational allocation, making the caste-occupation link the weakest joint of the system, although stigmatised occupations and their inheritance have not disappeared. Jati-level associations and caste panchayats arbitrate disputes, enforce endogamy norms and organise political mobilisation, which is how caste re-enters public life as organisation and network rather than as ritual.

### Q5 [10 marks]
Contrast the isolationist, assimilationist and integrationist positions in Indian tribal policy.

Model answer (111 words): Elwin diagnosed exploitative contact and prescribed protection, Ghurye diagnosed cultural lag and prescribed absorption, and Nehru rejected both extremes in favour of development on tribal terms, while what actually occurred was uneven acculturation. Verrier Elwin argued from fieldwork among central Indian tribal groups that unregulated contact with markets, moneylenders and settlers exploited tribal communities and eroded their culture, so protective limitation of outside contact was necessary. G.S. Dadabhai Naoroji founded the East India Association in London in 1866 to place Indian questions before British public opinion. The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha emerged in 1870 as an important western-India forum claiming to represent public interests. ### Q7 [10 marks]
Why was Champaran a methodological turning point in India's national movement?

### Q8 [10 marks]

### Q9 [10 marks]
Why should temple vocabulary be read as a grammar of movement and hierarchy rather than as a decorative glossary?

Model answer (121 words): Temple vocabulary describes ritual approach. The devotee does not enter a temple into a flat space but moves from the outer platform and porch into progressively restricted zones. Terms such as jagati, ardhamandapa, mandapa, antarala and garbhagriha therefore map sacred hierarchy. At Khajuraho, UNESCO's plan sequence itself proves that spatial order is doctrinally meaningful. In South India too, the distinction between vimana, gopura, prakara and mandapa explains how temple growth changed devotional experience over time. This matters in UPSC because analysis should explain how architecture works, not merely what it is called. Once vocabulary is linked to access, procession, congregation and sanctity, the temple becomes legible as an institution of worship and power. ### Q10 [10 marks]
Why did the fiscal crisis become a social revolution in France?

Model answer (105 words): Fiscal breakdown opened the political arena, but legal privilege, subsistence stress, Enlightenment legitimacy and multi-class mobilisation determined the Revolution's depth. The financially troubled monarchy summoned the Estates-General in May 1789, opening the old regime to a constitutional challenge. ### Q11 [15 marks]
Account for the world distribution of hot and mid-latitude deserts.

Model answer (161 words): Subtropical subsidence, offshore flow, cold currents, continentality and rain shadow combine differently across named deserts. Descending air in subtropical high-pressure belts warms adiabatically, lowers relative humidity and suppresses cloud development, helping locate many hot deserts around the subtropical margins. On western subtropical margins and some interiors, prevailing airflow may be offshore or have travelled over dry land, limiting oceanic moisture supply; it is one cause among several. Cold currents such as the Humboldt Current or Peru Current and the Benguela Current stabilise lower air and reduce convection, so coastal deserts may be cool or foggy yet receive little rain; cold water does not automatically produce rainfall. Mid-latitude interiors lie far from maritime moisture sources and may be enclosed by large land masses, creating severe annual thermal ranges and low precipitation. Mountains remove moisture on windward slopes and leave leeward basins dry; Patagonia east of the Andes is a classic example, while rain shadow often reinforces rather than solely creates a desert.

### Q12 [15 marks]
Analyse the dual role of the summer maritime mechanism in making eastern-margin regions both agriculturally rich and hazard-prone.

Model answer (151 words): The same summer inflow of warm moist maritime air that gives a long wet growing season supporting rice, tea and dense populations also brings typhoons, floods and surges onto the same low-lying coastal plains. The mechanism that makes a region agriculturally rich is the same one that makes it hazardous: the summer inflow of warm moist maritime air gives a long wet growing season supporting dense rural populations while also bringing tropical cyclones onto the same low-lying coastal plains. Typhoons are intense tropical cyclones that originate in the Pacific Ocean and move westwards to the coasts bordering the South China Sea, most frequent in late summer July to September, and can be very disastrous. Warm wet summers support luxuriant forests, with regional examples including eucalyptus forests in NSW, sugar-cane thriving in Natal, the maize belt of the Gulf-type region, and rice and tea as characteristic crops of the monsoonal China-type lands.

### Q13 [15 marks]
Examine the significance of straits, canals and isthmuses in international trade.

Model answer (160 words): Narrow passages compress world movement into small spaces, so they shorten routes, concentrate traffic and convert commercial geography into strategic geography by making disruption at a single point a global cost event. Transport is the circulatory system of an economy because it moves raw materials, labour, finished goods, information and strategic power across space, and trade geography grows out of connectivity, cost, time and chokepoints. Containerisation standardised the cargo unit, collapsed handling time and cost, made intermodal transfer routine, permitted fragmentation of production across countries, and concentrated traffic at fewer deep-draught, high-crane-productivity, well-connected hubs with feeder services. Canals and straits matter because they shorten or control routes, so a disruption at one narrow passage lengthens voyages and raises freight and insurance costs far beyond the affected region. ### Q14 [15 marks]
Cultural diversity in India marks disadvantage but does not produce it. Critically examine.

Regional adaptation, not imperial imitation, best explains provincial Indo-Islamic architecture. Discuss.

### Q1 [10 marks]
Differentiate the role of B.N. Rau from that of the Drafting Committee in Indian constitution-making.

### Q4 [10 marks]
Why is the Indian Constitution better described as adapted rather than copied?

Model answer (142 words): The Indian Constitution borrowed tested devices but did not reproduce any one foreign system. Britain supplied cabinet responsibility, rule of law, writs and single citizenship; India rejected hereditary monarchy and unlimited parliamentary sovereignty. The United States influenced Fundamental Rights and judicial review; India rejected presidential government, rigid separation and dual citizenship. Canada influenced a strong-Centre federation, while Ireland supplied Directive Principles and Australia the Concurrent List. ### Q7 [10 marks]

### Q1 [10 marks]
Why do the three methods of measuring GDP converge conceptually yet diverge statistically?

Model answer (148 words): The production, income and expenditure methods observe the same circular flow. Production creates value added; that value becomes compensation, operating surplus and mixed income; final users absorb the resulting output through consumption, capital formation and net exports. Conceptually, therefore, output equals income equals final expenditure. Measured totals differ because the routes use distinct evidence. Production estimates combine enterprise surveys, crop and industrial indicators and administrative records. Income data face mixed household-enterprise accounts and under-reporting. Expenditure estimates depend on consumption, inventory, trade and government data with different reporting dates. Price adjustment, informal-sector extrapolation and later returns add further variation. MoSPI's 27 February 2026 methodology uses Supply and Use Table integration to improve production- expenditure reconciliation. The residual remains a statistical discrepancy, not additional production. Better surveys, coherent classifications and transparent revision tables can reduce it, but independent estimation means a zero discrepancy is not a realistic proof of quality.

### Q2 [10 marks]
How did colonial land-revenue systems shape the agenda of post-Independence land reform?

Model answer (122 words): Colonial revenue settlements created distinct obstacles. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 placed zamindars between the State and cultivators, strengthening rent-receiving interests. Ryotwari dealt directly with recorded cultivators, but direct assessment did not guarantee affordable demand or secure title. Mahalwari made a village estate or co-sharer body responsible, tying revenue administration to local hierarchy. Post-Independence reform therefore required several instruments. Intermediary abolition removed rent-receiving layers; tenancy laws regulated rent, eviction and ownership; ceilings addressed concentration; consolidation tackled scattered parcels; and records identified actual rights. The causal lesson is that a revenue label did not determine one outcome. Regional variants, political power and record quality shaped each legacy. Reform had to alter both legal relationships and the administrative information through which they were enforced.

### Q3 [10 marks]

### Q5 [10 marks]
Explain why fiscal, revenue and primary deficits must be read together.

Model answer (146 words): Fiscal deficit equals total expenditure minus non-debt receipts and broadly measures the year's borrowing requirement. Revenue deficit equals revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts and reveals current-account dissaving. Primary deficit subtracts interest payments from fiscal deficit and isolates the current non-interest gap. Together they answer different questions. A falling fiscal deficit may still conceal weak revenue balance; a high fiscal deficit may finance productive capital expenditure; and a low primary deficit may reflect a large inherited interest burden rather than low debt. Analysis must add financing mix, debt maturity, off-budget liabilities and expenditure outcomes. It must also preserve fiscal year and vintage. Union Budget 2026-27 figures are Budget Estimates, not realised outcomes. Therefore no single deficit is a complete measure of fiscal quality or sustainability.

### Q10 [10 marks]
Distinguish inflation, disinflation, deflation, reflation and stagflation.

Model answer (135 words): Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; its rate is the percentage change in a stated index. Disinflation means this positive rate falls, so prices may still rise. Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level and can increase real debt burdens and weaken output. Reflation is deliberate policy support to restore demand and prices from depressed conditions. Stagflation combines inflation with stagnant output and weak employment, often after an adverse supply shock. India's pandemic experience shows why the distinctions matter: logistics disruption raised essential prices despite slack. Demand-led inflation supports restraint; supply-led stagflation requires bottleneck repair plus credible expectation management. A single commodity spike is not automatically general inflation, while lower inflation does not restore the old price level. Precise vocabulary is therefore the first step in policy assignment.

### Q11 [15 marks]

## Philosophy-I

### Q1 (Compulsory)
(a) "Spinoza's God does no explanatory work outside the system." Examine this claim by comparing the role of God in Descartes and Spinoza. [10 marks]
Model answer: For Descartes, God is a bridge. The cogito yields one certainty; divine veracity converts a rule read off that single case into a general licence to trust clear and distinct perception, and then licenses belief in an external world. Remove God and the system contracts to isolated intuitions. For Spinoza, God is not an added cause but the system itself. God, or Nature, is the one substance; Thought and Extension are its attributes; minds and bodies are its modes. Ethics I, P18 makes God the immanent, not the transitive, cause, so nothing is produced outside God and nothing needs to be certified from outside. The claim is therefore largely correct. Descartes' God performs an external epistemic function and can fail - exactly what Arnauld's Circle alleges. Spinoza's God performs no such function, because no gap between knower and known requires bridging: the order of ideas simply is the order of things (Ethics II, P7). One qualification is needed. Spinoza's God does other work: it grounds the necessity and intelligibility of everything that follows. Verdict. Descartes' God is a guarantor and can be attacked; Spinoza's God is a structure and can only be rejected wholesale.

(b) "The golden mountain is very high." Discuss this statement in the context of Russell's theory of descriptions. [10 marks]
Model answer: The problem. The sentence appears to be about something - a golden mountain - and nothing answers to that phrase. If the phrase were a name, the sentence would either be meaningless or would commit us to a non-existent object of the kind Meinong admitted; the golden mountain is his own stock example. The analysis. "The golden mountain" is an incomplete symbol: it has no meaning in isolation and disappears on analysis. The sentence asserts (Ex)[Gx & (Ay)(Gy -> y = x) & Hx] - there is at least one golden mountain, at most one, and it is very high. The verdict. The existence conjunct is false, so the whole conjunction is false. The sentence is therefore neither meaningless nor about a subsisting entity, and asserting it commits us to nothing. Scope. "The golden mountain is not very high" is false on the primary occurrence, where the description takes wide scope, and true on the secondary occurrence, where the negation does - so the apparent conflict with excluded middle is an ambiguity of scope rather than a defect in logic. Point.

(c) Explain Heidegger's notion of das Man and show why 'falling' is not a moral failing. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Das Man names the impersonal "anyone" in whose name things are done, and falling is the ontological structure of absorption in that anonymity - which is why neither carries blame. The "they". Dasein exists with others - being-with (Mitsein, 26) is constitutive, not added on - and tends to lose itself in what "one" (man) does, says and thinks. Das Man is not "other people" but the anonymous norm-setter: one reads what one reads, one retires when one retires, and nobody in particular is answerable. Its three marks. Idle talk (Gerede) - passing along received opinions without genuine understanding; curiosity (Neugier) - flitting restlessly from novelty to novelty; ambiguity (Zweideutigkeit) - everything seems accessible and nothing is genuinely appropriated. Each is a deficient mode of an existentiale rather than a vice. Why falling is not a failing. Falling (Verfallen) is Dasein's tendency to be absorbed in the world of its concern and in das Man. It is expressly not a theological "fall from grace" but an ontological structure: Dasein is inauthentic proximally and for the most part (zunachst und zumeist), and this is a structural feature of being-in-the-world, not a deficiency to be repented. Consequence.

(d) "The relation between cause and effect is one of constant conjunction". Examine Hume's criticism of causation in the light of the above statement. [10 marks]
Model answer: The quoted sentence is accurate but incomplete, and examining it is the answer. Hume's analysis begins with what observation supplies: temporal priority, spatial contiguity and, across repeated instances, constant conjunction. What observation never supplies is a power or tie binding cause to effect. Applying the copy principle, Hume asks which impression the idea of necessary connexion is copied from. No external impression answers, and repetition adds nothing to the objects themselves. The internal route also fails: I have no impression of how the will moves a limb. What repetition does alter is the mind. After constant conjunction it is determined to pass from the idea of the cause to that of the effect, and this felt determination is an impression of reflection from which the idea of necessity is copied. Causal necessity is therefore projected onto objects, not read off them, and the mechanism is custom or habit. Verdict. Constant conjunction alone is mere regularity; Hume's account requires priority, contiguity and the felt determination. He does not deny causation - he relocates its necessity from the world to the observer.

(e) "The verification principle is a criterion of meaning and not of truth." Examine this claim. (Answer in about 150 words.) [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The claim is correct, and the whole doctrine depends on it. The distinction. The principle asks not whether the world is as a sentence says, but whether there is any way the world could be that would make a difference to it. A sentence passes if it is analytic or if some possible experience bears on its truth. The decisive illustration. "The earth is flat" is demonstrably false and fully meaningful, because we know exactly what observations settle it. "The Absolute is beyond time" receives no truth-value at all, because no observation is even relevant to it. The first is refuted; the second is disqualified. Consequence. Because the verdict falls on the sentence rather than the object, the positivist may not add that the Absolute does not exist - a denial would need the very truth-conditions he says are missing. Verdict. Meaning, not truth: the criterion is a test of candidacy, and mistaking it for a test of truth destroys both halves of the doctrine.

### Q2
(a) "The history of rationalist substance is a movement from dualism through monism to pluralism." Analyse the logic and the cost of this movement. [20 marks]
Model answer: The sequence three, one, infinitely many captures a real dialectic, but its logic lies in changing criteria rather than in a preference for numbers. All three thinkers seek self-explanatory reality accessible to reason; they differ over what makes something ontologically basic. Descartes uses principal attributes and real distinction. God is infinite substance; mind and body are created substances whose essences are thought and extension. The gain is a clear basis both for a mathematical physics and for personal consciousness. The cost is a definitional equivocation, conceded in Principles I.51, and an interaction problem that his own criterion generates. Spinoza applies independence consistently. If substance is conceived through itself, dependent created substances cannot qualify; and substances sharing an attribute cannot be distinguished. God, or Nature, alone remains, while minds and bodies become modes under Thought and Extension. The gain is unity and the dissolution of interaction, since the order of ideas is the order of things. The cost is necessitarianism and the threat of acosmism. Leibniz accepts that extended composites are not ultimate but refuses to sacrifice individuals. Simplicity, perception and appetition define infinitely many monads; the identity of indiscernibles guarantees their uniqueness. The gain is active plurality and internal agency. The cost is that bodies become well-founded phenomena and that correspondence requires pre-established harmony and a divine selector. Movement | Problem solved | New problem generated | ---|---|---| Descartes -> Spinoza | dependence and interaction between created substances | finite individuality and contingency weaken | Spinoza -> Leibniz | restores genuine individual centres of activity | harmony and complete concepts strain explanation | The movement is therefore neither linear progress nor arbitrary disagreement. Each successor enforces one rationalist demand more strictly and pays elsewhere, because coherence, individuality and experiential plausibility cannot all be maximised at once. Verdict. Rationalist substance evolves through internal criticism, and the lesson is conceptual rather than historical: the definition of the basic unit already contains a theory of causation, of individuality and of freedom, so choosing a criterion of substance is choosing a metaphysics. That is also why the movement terminates only when Locke and Hume attack the innate resources which made the vocabulary of substance available in the first place.

(b) "All determination is negation." Comment with reference to Spinoza. [15 marks]
Model answer: The formula is associated with Spinoza's Letter 50 to Jarig Jelles of 2 June 1674 - "determination is negation" - and was universalised into the Latin slogan by Hegel, which is how it entered the textbook tradition. It is not a sentence of the Ethics, and saying so is itself part of a correct comment. Its meaning is this. To determine a finite thing is to bound it as this rather than that; every limit excludes alternatives. Finitude therefore contains negation. In Spinoza's metaphysics three consequences follow. For God: substance is absolutely infinite and determined by nothing external, so God involves no negation whatever and is pure positive self-expression through infinite attributes. For finite modes: each is limited by other modes of the same nature - a body occupies this region and excludes others, a particular idea expresses only a bounded causal perspective - so their determination marks dependence and limitation. For knowledge and freedom: inadequate knowledge treats a finite thing in isolation, whereas adequate knowledge understands it through the affirmative whole of Nature, and the free person sees limitation and necessity under the aspect of eternity (sub specie aeternitatis) rather than imagining an independent will. Hegel's criticism is that Spinoza's infinite may swallow all finite determination, turning the world into negation - the acosmism charge. Spinoza can answer that what is negated is independent self-subsistence, not every form of reality: modes positively express divine power in determinate ways. Verdict. The dictum illuminates why finite beings are dependent, but it becomes reductive if limitation is mistaken for unreality. Spinoza needs both the negation of independence and the affirmation of modal expression.

(c) "Blue is one object of sensation and green is another, and consciousness, which both sensations have in common, is different from either." Present an account of Moore's refutation of idealism with reference to this statement. [15 marks]
Model answer: Location and target. The sentence is from "The Refutation of Idealism" (Mind, 1903). Its target is the principle to be is to be perceived (esse est percipi), which requires that the object's being be nothing over and above its being perceived - and therefore that the sensation and its object be one thing. The argument the sentence states. Every sensation contains an act of awareness and an object of awareness. The quoted sentence identifies which is which: blue and green are the objects, and consciousness is what "both sensations have in common". If awareness were identical with its object, then the sensations of blue and of green would share nothing, since their objects differ, and would differ in nothing further, since there is nothing else in them. Both consequences are false, so awareness and object are distinct. The diagnosis. Consciousness is transparent or diaphanous: when we introspect the sensation of blue what we find is the blue, and the act is looked through rather than at. This explains why the conflation is so tempting; it is not itself a premiss of the refutation, and the argument above does not require isolating the act introspectively. The result, exactly bounded. Esse est percipi is not established, because the identity it requires has been denied on structural grounds. It does not follow that any object exists unperceived: distinguishing object from act shows only that the object is not constituted by the act. No theory of perception is offered - Moore leaves the relation between sense-data and material objects unsettled. And Absolute Idealism is untouched, since it argues from internal relations and the incompleteness of finite determinations, not from the perceptual equation. Verdict.

### Q3
(a) "The Tractatus is a book that succeeds by failing." Critically examine this claim with reference to the doctrine of saying and showing. [20 marks]
Model answer: The claim, stated precisely. The Tractatus offers an account of the conditions under which a sentence has sense, derives from it a boundary around the sayable, and then discovers that its own sentences lie outside that boundary. The claim under examination is that this is the book's method rather than an accident: it succeeds as an elucidation precisely by failing as doctrine. The derivation that produces the failure. A proposition depicts only by sharing logical form with a possible situation. To depict that sharing, a further picture would have to stand outside logical form; but every picture already has logical form, and each attempted statement of form would need its own form stated. Hence "what can be shown, cannot be said" (4.1212). The result extends to the existence of objects, to the logical truths that show the scaffolding of the world (6.124), and to value, since "the sense of the world must lie outside the world" (6.41) and "there can be no propositions of ethics" (6.42). Why this convicts the book. Every sentence of the Tractatus is about logical form, objects, the general form of the proposition and the limits of language. None depicts a contingent state of affairs, so by its own criterion none says anything. Wittgenstein does not conceal this: 6.54 states that anyone who understands him recognises his propositions as nonsensical, having used them as steps to climb beyond them, and must then throw away the ladder. The two ways of reading the outcome. On the standard ineffability reading the failure is productive: something genuinely manifests itself - logical form, value, the mystical - which no proposition could carry. On the resolute or therapeutic reading of Diamond and Conant, no ineffable truths remain; the sentences are plain nonsense, and the therapy is the reader's coming to see that he was never given a doctrine. Assessment. The claim is largely correct, with one refinement. As a diagnosis the book succeeds: it unifies metaphysics, logic and value under one structural error - the attempt to state a condition of stating - and 6.53 converts that diagnosis into a method. As doctrine it fails, and the failure is self-inflicted rather than imposed by critics. What the slogan obscures is that "succeeding by failing" is intelligible on only one of the two readings: the standard reading can say what survives, whereas the resolute reading must say that nothing does.

(b) "Consciousness is what it is not and is not what it is." In the light of this statement bring out the chief features of Sartre's conception of consciousness. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The formula is a definition, not a paradox: it says that consciousness is constituted by a distance from itself that it can never close. The contrast. Being-in-itself (en-soi) is full, solid, self-identical and opaque - "it is what it is" - and contingent, "de trop". Being-for-itself (pour-soi) is nihilating and self-surpassing, and never coincides with itself. "Is what it is not." Consciousness is always ahead of itself, projecting toward possibilities it does not yet possess; it is defined by a future that does not yet exist. "Is not what it is." Consciousness cannot coincide with its past or its facticity. Sartre's waiter is a waiter in his facticity and is not one in his transcendence: he can question the role, resent it, leave it. How this is possible. Nihilation (neantisation): consciousness stands back from what is and negates it - questioning, imagining alternatives, projecting beyond it. The for-itself is "the being by which nothingness (neant) comes into the world." What it generates. Freedom, since nothing in me is fixed enough to determine me; bad faith, the attempt to be self-identical anyway by collapsing into facticity or transcendence alone; ecstatic temporality, since the for-itself exists as past-facticity and future-project; being-for-others, since the Look confers an outside I cannot occupy; and the impossible desire to be a pour-soi-en-soi, which is God - hence "man is a useless passion". Objection. Is non-self-identity coherent, or a contradiction in phenomenological dress? The reply is that the for-itself has no completed nature with which to be identical, since it exists as a relation to its own possibilities rather than as a thing with properties; whether that succeeds is contested.

(c) Examine Hume's refutation of Causal relation and Kant's response to it. [15 marks]
Model answer: Hume. Observation of causal sequences delivers only temporal priority, spatial contiguity and constant conjunction; no impression of a power or tie is given, and repetition adds nothing to the objects. The internal route through volition fails equally. What repetition alters is the mind, which after constant conjunction is determined to pass from cause to effect - an impression of reflection from which the idea of necessity is copied. Hence two definitions of cause: regular succession of similars, and an object whose appearance conveys the thought to the other. The consequence is that inductive inference rests on custom, not reason. Kant. He does not dispute the negative finding; he agrees that necessary connexion is not copied from a sensory impression. His answer is transcendental. In the Second Analogy the category of cause is an a priori rule required to distinguish an objective succession of events from the merely subjective succession of my perceptions. "Every event has a cause" is therefore synthetic a priori for possible experience: the understanding supplies necessary temporal ordering to phenomena, and objective empirical causation is rescued without any claim about causality among things in themselves. Examination. The Humean challenge is that Kant has built the category into the definition of objective experience rather than proving it. Kant's reply is that without rule-governed synthesis, experience of an event would not be possible, so the presupposition is a condition of the fact to be explained. Verdict. Kant is correctly aimed: Hume's conclusion follows from the atomism of impressions, and it is that atomism, not the inference, which Kant attacks. Whether a condition of possible experience amounts to knowledge of nature remains contested - which is why analysis should close by grading, not by declaring a winner.

### Q4
(a) "Anxiety is not a stronger kind of fear." Examine the role of anxiety in Kierkegaard, Heidegger and Sartre, and assess whether a mood can disclose a philosophical truth. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Fear and anxiety differ in kind, not degree, and the shared argument in all three thinkers turns on that difference - though the conclusion each draws is different. The general argument. (i) Moods are not private colourings added to a neutral world; they are ways in which one's situation is disclosed. (ii) Fear discloses an entity as threatening and keeps us within the world of concern. (iii) Anxiety has no entity as its object and therefore cannot disclose an entity. (iv) Yet it plainly discloses something, since it has definite content and is not mere confusion. (v) Therefore what it discloses is a structure. (vi) Therefore anxiety is philosophically privileged: the one mood in which existence as a whole becomes an issue. Kierkegaard. The Concept of Anxiety (1844), by Vigilius Haufniensis: "the object of anxiety is a nothing"; what appears is possibility, "the possibility of being able". Anxiety is "the dizziness of freedom", present already in innocence, and the presupposition of the leap. Heidegger. Being and Time 40: in anxiety "the world has the character of complete insignificance" - the totality of involvements collapses and Dasein is thrown back on its bare that-it-is. That in the face of which and that for which one is anxious are the same: being-in-the-world. Anxiety discloses Dasein as solus ipse and frees it for authentic choosing. In "What is Metaphysics?" (1929) he adds that it reveals the Nothing (das Nichts) - "nothing noths", never "noughts". Sartre. Being and Nothingness (1943), Part I. On the cliff path I feel fear of the drop, then realise that nothing prevents me from throwing myself over - vertigo before my own freedom. The gambler finds yesterday's firm resolution present but powerless: it must be remade now, and nothing guarantees he will remake it. Anguish is distinct from nausea, which discloses the contingency of the in-itself. Assessment. The naturalist replies that anxiety is a clinical or neurochemical state with no cognitive credentials. The counter is that the existentialist claim is not that anxious people know more, but that the structure of the mood - having no object - is itself the datum requiring explanation, and no reduction of it to an unnoticed object has succeeded. Verdict: a mood can disclose a structure without becoming infallible cognition; naturalistic and clinical explanations remain compatible competitors rather than refuted rivals, and the three analyses must not be merged into one mood theory.

(b) Examine the protocol-sentence debate and assess its significance for the verification theory of meaning. (Answer in about 250 words.) [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The protocol-sentence debate is the most damaging objection to logical positivism precisely because it was raised inside the Vienna Circle: the school that made verification the criterion of meaning could not agree on what verification terminates in. The question. What is the form and status of protocol sentences (Protokollsatze) - incorrigible foundations, or ordinary revisable sentences? Schlick. In "Uber das Fundament der Erkenntnis" (1934) the base consists of affirmations (Konstatierungen) such as "here now blue", absolutely certain because understanding the sentence and verifying it are one and the same act. Schlick's own catch is fatal: an affirmation cannot be written down and preserved, since recording it yields a hypothesis about a past moment. Its certainty is momentary and unusable. Neurath. In "Protokollsatze" (1932) protocols are physicalist, observer- and time-indexed, and fallible like any other sentence; "statements are compared with statements, not with 'experiences'". His image is of sailors rebuilding their ship on the open sea. Carnap. In "Uber Protokollsatze" (1932) the form of a protocol is not a fact to be discovered but a matter of choice of language - tolerance applied to the Circle's own foundations, which effectively ends foundationalism inside the Circle. Significance. The criterion presupposes that observation-statements are epistemically privileged. Schlick's base is certain but unrecordable, Neurath's is recordable but revisable, and Carnap's is conventional; a chosen base cannot ground a criterion advertised as a discovery about meaning. Verdict. Schlick's objection that coherence alone admits a consistent fairy-tale is not fully met by Neurath's appeal to encyclopaedic scientific practice, which makes the selection sociological; and the debate moved positivism toward intersubjective, system-level testing without thereby making Quine's later holism a positivist conclusion.

(c) "We can affirm the truth of any sentence in our total system, in the face of whatever experience, just so long as we are prepared to make adjustments elsewhere." Discuss this statement in the light of Quine's 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism'. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The sentence is a claim about where blame for a failed prediction may be assigned, not a licence to believe anything: the clause "just so long as we are prepared to make adjustments elsewhere" names the price, and the price is the doctrine. Where it sits. It belongs to 6 of "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), after both dogmas have been removed. Statements "face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body", so the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science. The mechanism. A hypothesis entails an observation only with auxiliaries: the testable claim is (H & A) -> O. When experience yields not-O, modus tollens delivers only not-(H & A) - H is false or some element of A is false - and logic never names the guilty conjunct. Three responses are therefore available: revise a peripheral belief, revise a deep interior statement, or plead hallucination and revise the observation itself. The third is what makes the quoted sentence literally true. The historical illustration. Anomalies in Uranus's orbit were met by revising the auxiliary assumption about the number of planets, and Neptune was found (1846). The identical strategy applied to Mercury produced the hypothesised planet "Vulcan", which does not exist; there the correct revision was the deep one - general relativity (1915). Same logical situation, opposite correct responses, no advance rule. What blocks relativism. Revision is chosen on grounds of simplicity, conservatism and fecundity, and in Philosophy of Logic (1970) Quine adds the maxim of minimum mutilation and warns that "change of logic" is change of subject. No statement is revisable by nature; only by choice, and choice has costs. Verdict. The statement is right about confirmation and easily misread as relativism; truncating it at "come what may" is what produces the misreading.

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) "All human knowledge is empirical and therefore relative." Critically examine Jaina theory of sevenfold judgement (saptabhanginaya) in the light of above statement. [10 marks]
Model answer: Jainism agrees with half of the statement and rejects the other half. It agrees that human knowledge is limited: ordinary cognition is ksayopasamika, partial because knowledge-obscuring karma is only partly removed, and ordinary perception is only conventionally direct. It refuses the inference to relativism. The sevenfold judgement - the seven forms generated by affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under the operator syat - records the respects in which a predication holds, using the indices of substance, place, time and mode. Each form is a definite assertion, not a probability estimate; syat does not mean "perhaps". Critically, the difference from empiricist relativism is ontological. For the empiricist, relativity attaches to knowledge because there is no access beyond experience. For Jainism, relativity attaches to finite judgement while the aspects remain objectively in the object, and omniscience remains internally possible. Hence the statement describes a Jaina premise, not a Jaina conclusion: partiality of knowing does not entail relativity of truth.

(b) Discuss the nature and different stages of Samadhi as per Patanjala yoga and examine the role of Isvara in it. [10 marks]
Model answer: Absorption (samadhi) in Patanjala Yoga is the highest refinement of attention, not passivity, and it is the threshold of liberating discernment. Object-supported absorption (samprajnata) retains a support and is with-seed (sabija); Yoga-sutra 1.17 characterises it by deliberation, reflection, joy and bare I-am-ness, while Yoga-sutra 1.42-1.44 separately classifies coalescence (samapatti) as savitarka and nirvitarka for gross objects and savicara and nirvicara for subtle ones. The clarity of subtle coalescence yields truth-bearing insight (rtambhara prajna). Supra-cognitive absorption (asamprajnata) has impression-residue; when even that is restrained, absorption is seedless (nirbija); cloud-of-dharma absorption (dharmamegha-samadhi) is the culmination. The Lord (Isvara) is a special conscious witness (purusa-visesa) untouched by affliction, action, fruition and deposit, denoted by the sacred syllable (pranava). Dedication to him (Isvara-pranidhana) is a positive observance, a component of preparatory discipline and an independent means to absorption; it stabilises the mind-field and removes the nine obstacles (antaraya). Examined strictly, however, his role is instrumental and not constitutive. He is neither material cause nor the Nyaya creator, and he does not bestow isolation; liberation still follows the practitioner's own discriminative discernment. Yoga's theism is therefore real but bounded - practice-enabling rather than creation-first.

(c) Do you think Carvaka's philosophy is positivistic in nature? Give reasons and justifications for your answer. [10 marks]
Model answer: Yes in spirit, no in method. The case for. The label is not imported by the examiner: Chatterjee and Datta write that the Carvaka theory "may also be called positivism, because it believes only in positive facts or observable phenomena", and compare its view of inference to that of "the pragmatists and logical positivists". Carvaka restricts the knowable to what perception presents; rejects God, soul, action and moral consequence (karma) and heaven as unwarranted; refuses scriptural authority; grounds value in this-worldly pleasure and pain; and even adopts a quasi-verificationist register, since questions about rebirth and heaven are said to become "meaningless". The case against. Positivism is built on inference - science is systematised inference - which Carvaka denies means of valid knowledge (pramana)-status. Positivism admits unobservables that earn their explanatory keep; Carvaka refuses them. Positivism offers a constructive programme with formal and semantic tools; Carvaka offers a polemical minimalism. Comte's law of three stages and sociology have no Indian counterpart. Verdict: an illuminating analogy, not a historical identity. Radical empiricism or naturalistic materialism is the more accurate description. The paradox is that Carvaka reaches a materialist conclusion by an anti-inferential route.

(d) Critically discuss the metaphysical status of a Jiva and a Purusa according to Samkhya philosophy. [10 marks]
Model answer: For Samkhya the conscious witness (purusa) is the metaphysical reality: pure, self-luminous, inactive and changeless consciousness, wholly beyond the three qualities. The empirical self (jiva) is not a further principle in the enumeration of twenty-five; it is the witness as empirically conditioned through identification with intellect, I-maker, mind, body and the karmic stream. The mechanism is reflection. The determinative intellect, being predominantly luminous and therefore transparent, receives the reflection of the witness; the resulting cognition generates false appropriation (abhimana) - "I am the doer, knower, enjoyer". The insentient organ thus appears conscious while the inactive witness appears to be an agent. The empirical self is precisely that two-way appearance stabilised into an apparent person. Two consequences follow. Bondage is misidentification rather than a real modification of the witness, and liberation is the correction of attribution rather than the destruction of an entity. Critically, the account is elegant but exposed: if the witness is never really conditioned, it becomes unclear in what sense there was ever a problem for it, and Samkhya's reply - that the problem was always one of attribution - clarifies the question rather than demonstrating an answer.

(e) Explain the ground on which Carvaka rejects inference as a valid source of knowledge. [10 marks]
Model answer: The ground is the impossibility of establishing invariable concomitance (vyapti). Inference moves from a perceived mark to an unperceived sadhya, and is licensed only by an invariable concomitance - "wherever smoke, there fire". For inference to be a means of valid knowledge (pramana), that universal must be beyond doubt. It cannot be had by perception: we cannot perceive all cases of smoke and fire even now, "to speak nothing of those which existed in the past or will exist in the future"; and the retreat to class-characters fails, since "smokeness" itself is not perceptually known while all instances are not perceived. Nor by inference: that would be a petitio principii, since the further inference needs its own invariable concomitance (vyapti). Nor by testimony: testimony's own validity is inferred; and if inference depended on it, "no one could infer anything by himself." Hidden limiting condition (upadhi) completes the case: fire yields smoke only where fuel is wet, and hidden conditions "may always remain hidden and escape notice", so unconditionality is never certified. Hence truth is "only an accident, and a separable one" in inference - not the guaranteed character a means of valid knowledge (pramana) requires.

### Q6
(a) "Jainism preserves the reality of the world at the cost of the simplicity of logic; Advaita preserves the simplicity of logic at the cost of the reality of the world." Critically examine this comparison. [20 marks]
Model answer: The comparison is illuminating but requires correction on both sides. On the Jaina side it is broadly accurate. Jainism refuses to treat either permanence or change as appearance. The real is defined as originating, decaying and persisting together, so both are secured - but the price is that predication must be indexed. Assertion carries the operator syat; opposed predications are separated by substance, place, time and mode; and the exhaustive schema of qualified assertion contains seven forms rather than a simple affirmation and denial. Ontological economy is sacrificed too: six substances, innumerable souls and material action-matter are admitted because each performs explanatory work. The world is saved, and logic becomes intricate. On the Advaita side the statement needs care. Advaita does not abandon the world; it grades it. The world is empirically real and only ultimately mithya, and sublation, not denial, is the operative notion. What Advaita gains is a single ultimate reality with a straightforward account of contradiction: opposed characterisations belong to different levels, and the lower is finally cancelled. So the accurate formulation is that Advaita preserves logical simplicity by admitting levels of reality, whereas Jainism preserves ontological plurality by admitting respects of predication. That reformulation locates the real difference. Both schools face the problem of apparently opposed predicates. Advaita separates them vertically, by rank, with the lower cancelled at realisation. Jainism separates them horizontally, by aspect, with nothing cancelled at all. Vertical separation buys a simple logic and requires a doctrine of appearance; horizontal separation buys an unqualified realism and requires a doctrine of conditional assertion. Critically, each pays where the other saves. Samkara charges that Jaina qualification erodes assertoric force and that a doctrine which qualifies even itself cannot claim universal scope. The Jaina replies that reflexive qualification is obedience rather than exemption, and counter-charges that treating the world as ultimately mithya purchases simplicity by demoting the very reality it set out to explain. Neither charge is answerable from a neutral standpoint, because each presupposes what the other denies. The defensible verdict is that the quoted comparison identifies a genuine trade-off but mislabels the Advaita side. Jainism trades logical simplicity for unqualified realism; Advaita trades ontological plurality, not the world itself, for a single unsublated real.

(b) Compare and contrast the views of Sankara and Ramanuja regarding the status of the world. [15 marks]
Model answer: Both thinkers agree on three things: the world depends entirely on ultimate reality; there is no second independent principle; and scripture is the final authority for what the world is. They diverge on what dependence permits. For Sankara the world is an apparent transformation (vivarta). Ultimate reality does not really change; the world is a sublatable appearance on a real substrate, as the snake is on the rope. It is therefore dependently real (mithya) - denied absolute reality but retaining public, law-governed empirical validity - and emphatically not wholly non-existent (tuccha). The three levels of reality make this intelligible: the absolute alone is ultimate, the world and the Lord are empirical, dream and shell-silver are apparent. For Ramanuja the world is real. It is the non-conscious (acit) dimension of ultimate reality's body, related by inseparable dependence (aprthaksiddhi). Creation is a real manifestation in which the subtle cause-state of the non-conscious mode becomes the gross effect-state, while God's essential auspicious nature is unaltered. Because effects are real, the world is a meaningful field of duty, devotion and divine manifestation. The contrast can be tabulated: reality of the world - dependent appearance against real manifestation; change in the absolute - denied against accepted in a qualified sense through modes; soteriology - the world sublated in knowledge against the world meaningful in devotion; the God-world relation - empirical against an intrinsic body-soul relation. The evaluative line is that each protects what the other qualifies: Sankara protects absoluteness by reducing world-status, Ramanuja protects world-status by qualifying absoluteness. Vallabha's unchanged real manifestation shows the fork is not exhaustive, but the two positions remain the decisive alternatives.

(c) "So long as there are changes and modifications in citta, the self is reflected therein, and, in the absence of discriminative knowledge, identifies itself with them." Present an appraisal of Yoga Soteriology in the light of the above statement. [15 marks]
Model answer: The quoted statement compresses Yoga's entire diagnosis. The mind-field really changes, since Yoga inherits real transformation (parinamavada) and recognises transformations of restraint, absorption and one-pointedness in a persisting substrate. Because the mind-field is lucid-predominant and transparent, the witness's light is reflected in it (pratibimba); and because the relation is mere proximity (sannidhi-matra), the whole transformation occurs on the mind's side while the witness remains unchanged (aparinamin). Where discriminative discernment (viveka-khyati) is absent, the borrowed light is read as the mind's own and the witness's witnesshood is read as agency - the conflation named egoity (asmita). Appraised positively, the soteriology is strong. It supplies a rigorous phenomenology of mental process - modification, memory, sleep, impression, affliction, absorption. It integrates psychology, ethics and metaphysics, since restraints, moral causation and mental restraint form one system. It offers an operational path rather than speculation: practice and dispassion, preparatory discipline, the eight limbs and graded absorption. And the isolation chapter's realist arguments against consciousness-only idealism show genuine metaphysical commitment. The weaknesses are equally locatable. Mere proximity names the relation between an inactive witness and an active nature more clearly than it explains it, and Advaita and Nyaya press exactly there. The Lord's explanatory role is limited: he aids practice but is neither creator nor material cause nor the giver of isolation. And the value of contentless pure consciousness can be questioned by schools that define fulfilment through knowledge, relation, bliss or activity. Two matters remain genuinely contested: the commentarial status of joy and I-am-ness within the absorption taxonomy, and whether isolation is austere aloneness or blissful self-abidance. The balanced verdict is that Yoga's account of how bondage operates is more secure than its account of how the two principles are related at all.

### Q7
(a) Explain with reference to Yoga Philosophy, the nature of klesas. How does the removal of these lead to kaivalya? [20 marks]
Model answer: The afflictions (klesa) are not surface thoughts but the causal colourings of mental life that generate binding action and sustain the cycle of rebirth. Yoga lists five: ignorance (avidya), egoity (asmita), attachment (raga), aversion (dvesa) and clinging to life (abhinivesa). Ignorance is the root and the field in which the rest grow; it is defined as taking the non-eternal for the eternal, the impure for the pure, the painful for the pleasant and the non-self for the self. Egoity is the identification of the seer with the instrument of seeing. Attachment and aversion follow remembered pleasure and remembered pain. Clinging to life is instinctive and, significantly, present even in the learned - which shows that afflictions are carried by impressions rather than by opinions. Afflictions operate at four intensities: dormant (prasupta), attenuated (tanu), interrupted (vicchinna) and fully active (udara). Dormancy is not destruction, and interruption is merely the temporary dominance of an opposing tendency, so the absence of felt affliction is weak evidence of progress. Their removal leads to isolation through a stated causal chain. Afflictions motivate action; action deposits a karmic store (karmasaya) rooted in the afflictions; the store ripens as a threefold fruition (vipaka) of birth (jati), life-span (ayus) and experience (bhoga); and that experience generates fresh reaction, closing the loop in renewed birth. Because the first link is psychological, the engine can be jammed at its source. Yoga's therapy is graded accordingly. Preparatory discipline (kriya-yoga) - austerity (tapas), self-study (svadhyaya) and dedication to the Lord (Isvara-pranidhana) - is prescribed expressly to attenuate the afflictions and prepare absorption. The eight-limbed discipline purifies conduct, embodiment and attention. Meditation burns the subtle forms of affliction that ethical restraint alone cannot reach. As the mind-field is purified, absorption stabilises and discriminative discernment (viveka-khyati) matures. With higher dispassion (para-vairagya) the practitioner ceases to crave even powers and exalted states, and cloud-of-dharma absorption (dharmamegha-samadhi) rains down the fullness of discernment. Afflictions and binding action cease; the constituent qualities undergo involution (pratiprasava) for that witness; and consciousness abides in its own nature (svarupa-pratistha). This is isolation (kaivalya) - not union, not annihilation, but the ending of a misidentification whose machinery has been dismantled at the root.

(b) Explain the differences of conception of Reality between Carvaka and Jainism. [15 marks]
Model answer: Epistemic root. Carvaka admits one means of valid knowledge (pramana), perception (pratyaksa). Jainism admits a fivefold knowledge - mati, sruta, avadhi, manahparyaya, kevala - and hence inference. Every later divergence follows from this. Definition of the real. For Jainism, sat is utpada-vyaya-dhrauvya - origination, decay and persistence together; the gold that becomes bracelet and then ring persists as dravya while its paryayas change. Carvaka has no such analysis: elements are eternal, combinations transient, and that is all. Constituents. Four bhutas for Carvaka; six dravyas for Jainism - individual self (jiva), pudgala, dharma, adharma, akasa, kala. Akasa is rejected by the Carvaka as inferred; for Jainism it is a distinct substance providing accommodation. Soul and consciousness. Carvaka: no individual self (jiva); the self is the living body, and consciousness an emergent by-product. Jainism: infinitely many individual self (jivas), with consciousness their inseparable essence - expressly denying, as the sources put it, the Carvaka view that it is "a mere accidental property, arising only under some conditions". Standpoint. Jainism's anekantavada, with nayavada and syadvada, holds reality many-faced and every judgement conditioned. Carvaka admits a single standpoint and no level-distinction at all. Action and moral consequence (karma) and liberation. Jainism: action and moral consequence (karma) as subtle matter adhering to the individual self (jiva), bhavabandha and dravyabandha, with liberation (moksa) through the three jewels and kevala-jnana. Carvaka: no action and moral consequence (karma), and maranam eva apavargah. Verdict. Their agreement is instructive: both are Veda-rejecting school (nastika), both realists about the perceptible world, both reject a creator. The divergence is over standpoints and substances - which is why Jainism can hold change and persistence together while Carvaka can only assert them side by side.

(c) How do the Advaita Vedantins react to the Prakrtiparinamavada of the Samkhya philosophy? How do the Samkhyas defend their own position in this regard? Discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Samkhya's doctrine of the real transformation of primordial nature holds that the effect pre-exists in its material cause and is genuinely manifested through the modification of the three qualities: in equilibrium each quality transforms homogeneously, and on disturbance the qualities transform heterogeneously to yield the manifest principles. Advaita reacts on three fronts. First, an unconscious first principle cannot be the cause described in the Upanisads, where the source is spoken of as conscious and knowing. Second, an ultimate that really transforms becomes mutable, and a mutable principle is dependent rather than ultimate; the world's dependence is therefore better explained by superimposition. Third, Advaita substitutes apparent transformation (vivarta) for real transformation, so that Brahman is the cause without undergoing change, as a rope is the "cause" of the snake seen in it. Samkhya defends itself equally on three fronts. First, the selectivity of production is real: curd comes from milk and not from water, and only a real material continuity explains that restriction, whereas an appearance-theory leaves selectivity unexplained. Second, mutability does not disqualify nature from being ultimate, because nature is by definition the principle whose being is modification; the objection would tell only against a changeless ultimate. Third, treating the world as appearance purchases the immutability of the cause at the cost of the reality of bondage and release, which Samkhya's realism refuses to surrender. Both schools deny production from sheer non-being, so the dispute is not about the pre-existent effect but about the reality of the transformation. The exchange therefore terminates in a clash of first principles: Samkhya preserves the reality of the world and pays with a mutable ultimate, while Advaita preserves an immutable ultimate and pays with the ultimate unreality of the world.

### Q8
(a) Analyse the Jaina fourteen stages of spiritual development. How do the two ladders explain spiritual relapse, and what does this show about the relation between insight and conduct in Jainism? [20 marks]
Model answer: The fourteen stages (gunasthana) map the soul's movement from false vision to final liberation, and they are graded by karmic condition rather than by external observance. Stages one to three describe wrong, falling and mixed orientation. Stage four, right vision without restraint, is the decisive breakthrough: right vision (samyag-darsana) becomes stable enough to orient the path even though conduct has not yet followed. Stage five introduces partial restraint through lay vows. Stages six and seven mark full restraint with and then without negligence. Stages eight to ten describe progressive purification of the passions, from unprecedented purification through advanced purification to the condition in which only subtle greed remains. Stages eleven and twelve mark the crucial difference between suppression and destruction of delusion. Stage thirteen is the embodied omniscient being, and stage fourteen the momentary activity-free condition immediately preceding the perfected state. The two ladders explain relapse without treating it as mere failure. On the ladder of suppression (upasama-sreni), delusion is covered rather than removed; such a soul may reach the eleventh stage, but because suppressed delusion retains its potency the fall is necessary, not accidental. On the ladder of annihilation (ksapaka-sreni), delusion is destroyed, so the twelfth stage is stable and the ascent continues to omniscience. Three philosophical consequences follow. First, appearance and condition come apart: two souls may look identical at the tenth stage and have opposite futures, which is why Jainism refuses to identify spiritual attainment with observable conduct alone. Second, insight and conduct are interdependent rather than alternative. Right vision at stage four is genuine yet insufficient; conduct without right vision has no orientation; and the ladder records the joint operation of the three jewels. Third, omniscience is decoupled from disembodiment: destroying the destructive karmas yields omniscience at stage thirteen, while the non-destructive karmas sustain body, status, lifespan and felt experience until the end. Critically, an Advaita-style objection holds that a fourteen-stage ladder mechanises liberation, substituting a karmic audit for insight. The Jaina reply is that insight which does not destroy karma has not yet done its work, and that the ladder simply records the difference between a covered and a destroyed defilement. The scheme is therefore best evaluated as a psychology of purification with an unusually honest account of relapse, and its taxonomic weight is the price of that honesty.

(b) 'Both Ascetic and materialist are partial in their negation of each other'. Explain Sri Aurobindo's integral philosophy in the light of the above statement. [15 marks]
Model answer: The printed statement criticises the ascetic and the materialist for what they deny, not for what they affirm. The ascetic affirms Spirit and denies Matter; the materialist affirms Matter and denies Spirit. Each position is credible on its own evidence and incredible on the other's, and both are partial because each absolutises one pole of reality and rejects the other. Aurobindo's integral philosophy keeps both affirmations and drops both denials: Spirit is the truth of Matter, and Matter is the field of Spirit's manifestation. Each half is licensed by a doctrine rather than asserted. Involution licenses the first: the Divine conceals itself through graded planes until, in Matter and the Inconscient, consciousness is wholly hidden but not abolished, so matter is Spirit in self-oblivion rather than an anti-spiritual substance. Evolution licenses the second: what was involved re-emerges, Matter yielding Life and Life yielding Mind, so earthly existence is the very field in which the Divine becomes manifest. The metaphysical condition of both is a dynamic Absolute. Existence-Consciousness-Bliss is not static being but consciousness-force, which is why manifestation neither requires an illusion nor diminishes the Divine; and the Supermind supplies the mediating truth-principle that lets the One become Many without fragmentation. The consequences are practical. Against the ascetic, liberation is a beginning rather than a terminus, and Integral Yoga seeks the transformation of mind, life and body rather than release from them. Against the materialist, consciousness is not a late by-product but the involved reality that evolution is recovering. The aim is therefore neither world-flight nor flat naturalism but divine life on earth, realised individually in the gnostic being. The synthesis is only as secure as the involution premise on which it rests, but it does succeed in diagnosing both partialities from a single principle.

(c) Explain the concepts of 'Anyathasiddha' and 'Ananyathasiddha' in the context of Asatkaryavada. [15 marks]
Model answer: Non-pre-existence of the effect (asatkaryavada) holds that the effect does not lie latent in its cause but is a genuinely new production. This creates an immediate difficulty. Since the effect is not located in the cause, causal necessity must be located instead in the antecedent relation - and a bare antecedent relation over-generates, because innumerable things precede any effect. Nyaya's definition therefore builds in a filter. A cause is an unconditional and invariable antecedent: prior (purvavrtti), invariably prior (niyata-purvavrtti) and not otherwise established (ananyathasiddha). The complete cause is the causal complex (karana-samagri), including the absence of a blocking condition (pratibandhakabhava). Ananyathasiddha marks indispensable causal relevance: the item's connection to the effect is not accounted for by anything else. Anyathasiddha marks an antecedent that is otherwise established and is therefore excluded, however reliably it precedes. Five classical exclusions, each a different kind of irrelevance: the colour of the potter's staff is a quality of an instrument, not a producer; the potter's father is a remote cause of the potter, not of the pot; the sound of the staff is a co-effect of the same operation; all-pervading space is an ever-present background, not a distinctive antecedent; the potter's donkey is an accidental companion. The pair is thus not a refinement but a load-bearing device: without it, an antecedent-based definition of cause could not survive, and the doctrine of new production would collapse into the claim that everything preceding a pot causes it. Samkhya presses the residual question of restriction - why only these causes yield this effect. Nyaya answers by invariability rather than by latency, which is coherent but leaves the restriction unexplained at a deeper level.

## Philosophy-II

### Q1 (Compulsory)
(a) Discuss Kautilya's concept of sovereignty in the light of 'Danda-neety'. [10 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. For Kautilya, sovereignty is measured by the ruler's capacity to calibrate force, not by his freedom to use it; dandaniti is therefore a doctrine of proportion rather than of severity. Where coercion sits. Coercive force (danda) is one of the seven constituents of the state (saptanga), alongside the ruler (svami), ministers (amatya), territory with its people (janapada), fortified defence (durga), treasury (kosa) and ally (mitra). Placing it among the constituents makes the capacity to punish a permanent structural feature of rule rather than an emergency power. The calibration argument. Without danda, society falls into disorder; with excessive danda, the ruler breeds resentment and instability. The art of sovereignty therefore lies in ordered, proportionate enforcement - danda is the force and niti the policy that governs its use. What this shows about sovereignty. The ruler is head of an organism rather than an unlimited master: authority is real, but its legitimacy and durability are judged by order and by security and welfare (yogaksema). Fear without welfare is self-defeating; welfare without order is impossible. Verdict. Kautilyan sovereignty is strong precisely because it is duty-bound.

(b) "Toleration is not equality." Comment. [10 marks]
Model answer: Claim. The statement is correct, and it identifies the single most useful control in this clause. The distinction. Toleration is permission to dissent: one party restrains coercion against what it disapproves of. Equality is a claim about standing: no party holds the position from which permission could be granted. Why the difference is structural, not one of degree. Toleration presupposes an asymmetry - a permitter and a permitted. That asymmetry is a civic rank, and softening its exercise does not remove it. The decisive evidence is institutional: a state with an established religion can be highly tolerant of others while its dissenters remain unequal citizens. Toleration therefore survives precisely the arrangement that equality abolishes. The same structure at the level of culture. The diversity ladder repeats the point: tolerance restrains coercion but preserves a hierarchy of permission, and a mature order moves through accommodation and recognition to equal citizenship and reciprocal recognition. What toleration nonetheless supplies. It is a necessary condition. Without restraint on coercion, plural communities cannot share a polity at all, and recognition has nothing to build on. Qualification. Verdict.

(c) "Untouchability is a symptom of caste, not a synonym for it." Examine the significance of this distinction. [10 marks]
Model answer: Position. The distinction is not terminological housekeeping; it decides what a remedy must reach. The four terms. The textual fourfold order is a normative classification and is not identical with the lived order; the lived birth-group is a locally organised, generally endogamous group, of which there are thousands with wide regional variation; the caste system is the institutional order joining birth, marriage closure, ranked status, allocated occupation and restricted association; and untouchability names practices imposing extreme exclusion and stigma on designated groups. Why untouchability is a symptom. The ranking principle - that birth fixes worth - operates throughout the order. Untouchability is its most extreme expression, so a rule abolishing the practice can succeed while birth continues to allocate marriage, occupation and standing everywhere else. The doctrinal consequence. This is why Ambedkar holds that untouchability cannot be eliminated without annihilating caste, and why reform limited to the practice is rejected as insufficient. The evaluative consequence. It also fixes the terms of the Gandhi-Ambedkar dispute: a position can condemn untouchability without opposing an idealised duty-order that still allocates by birth. Verdict. Merging the terms makes both positions unstatable and converts a structural argument into a moral one.

(d) Comment on Plato's critique of Democracy. [10 marks]
Model answer: The exact target. Plato attacks rule driven by unrestrained appetite and unexamined opinion, not participation as such. Stating the target precisely is what prevents the answer from becoming a summary of prejudices. The argument, in three moves. First, rule is a craft requiring knowledge: the ship-of-state analogy denies that navigation may be entrusted to the unskilled merely because they are numerous. Second, democracy mistakes liberty for qualification, so an equal right to rule is treated as an equal capacity to rule, levelling knowledge and ignorance. Third, unregulated liberty makes restraint appear oppressive, and out of that licence emerges the demagogue who flatters the many, attacks elites and seizes absolute power - so democracy turns into tyranny. The correction that must be made. This is not a defence of hereditary monarchy. Plato's rulers are selected by training and knowledge, which is why the modern reply targets unaccountable expertise rather than inheritance. The reply. Expertise informs policy while equal citizens retain authority over common purposes; constitutional liberty is ordered by rights and accountability; plural media, opposition and review can expose manipulation; and legitimate plurality is preferable to enforced unity. Verdict.

(e) Distinguish between economic growth, human development and social progress. Why does the distinction matter? [10 marks]
Model answer: The three concepts. Economic growth is increase in aggregate or per-capita output; its question is whether more is being produced. Human development, in the framework associated with Mahbub ul Haq and the UNDP, is expansion of longevity, knowledge and a decent material standard; its question is whether basic human options are widening. Social progress is justified improvement in social relations and institutions, assessed through freedom, equality, justice, dignity, participation, sustainability and solidarity; its question is whether society has become defensibly better. Three informational spaces. The three are measured in different spaces, which is why none entails the next. Output cannot see distribution, unpaid work or externalities; a national human-development composite cannot see within-group inequality or political freedom; and social progress cannot be settled by any aggregate at all, since the weights between its criteria are the normative question. Why it matters - three consequences. It supplies the discriminating test: income rising alongside intensified unfreedom, humiliation or ecological destruction is growth without progress. It makes the modal questions answerable, since necessity and sufficiency can only be assessed between distinct conditions. It locates the missing term in most development failures - not more output but standing and agency. Verdict.

### Q2
(a) "The concept of supreme legal authority survives every criticism examined; the image of a determinate personal sovereign does not." Critically examine this verdict with reference to Bodin, Austin, Laski and Kautilya. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The verdict is sound but requires two amendments: the surviving concept is final procedure rather than final will, and Kautilya shows that a duty-bound personal head can survive where an illimitable one cannot. What the image asserts. Bodin's sovereign is the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth; Austin's is a determinate human superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk and obeying no one, whose authority is absolute, indivisible, illimitable and inalienable. The internal criticisms. Austin cannot explain succession, persistence or power-conferring rules. Kelsen (General Theory of Law and State, English edition 1945) replaces the commander with a hierarchy of norms terminating in a presupposed basic norm; Hart (The Concept of Law, 1961) replaces him with a union of primary and secondary rules resting on a rule of recognition accepted from the internal point of view. Both remain positivists: the image dissolves, the concept of ultimate validity does not. The external criticisms. Federalism distributes competence; international law binds without a world sovereign; custom precedes command; democracy makes authority derivative and answerable. Laski adds that absolutism is philosophically false, politically dangerous and sociologically unrealistic. Each attacks the personal image; none shows that a legal order can dispense with an ultimate criterion of validity. What Bodin retains. Bodin survives better than Austin because his sovereign is already limited by divine law, natural law and the fundamental laws of the realm, and because perpetuity locates authority in an office. His weakness is enforcement, not conception. What Kautilya adds. The seven-limbed state makes sovereignty systemic; calibrated coercion makes force proportionate; security and welfare make legitimacy conditional on outcomes. A head bound by dharma, counsel, treasury and welfare is not the illimitable superior the criticisms destroy, which is why Kautilya is the least damaged of the four. The amendments. First, what survives is best described as a final procedure for closure rather than a final will, since Hart's rule of recognition and a constitutional amending procedure both perform closure without personal supremacy. Second, the three dissolutions must not be merged: a norm-hierarchy, an official practice and a plurality of associations are rivals, not allies. Verdict. The quoted verdict holds.

(b) 'There is no permanent friend or permanent enemy.' Discuss this statement in the light of Kautilya's view on Sovereignty. [15 marks]
Model answer: Provenance first. The printed line is the examiner's framing. What can be shown is that the verified doctrines make alliances conditional and policy responsive. Thesis. On Kautilya's model, friendship and enmity are positional rather than personal, so the conditionality the line asserts follows from the structure of interstate relations and needs no quotation to establish it. The circle of states. The mandala framework locates friends and enemies relationally within a competitive and unstable states-system, in which position rather than sentiment fixes alignment. The six-fold policy. Sadgunya supplies the gears: sandhi (peace or treaty), vigraha (hostility or war), yana (marching or expedition), asana (remaining poised), samsraya (seeking shelter or alliance) and dvaidhibhava (dual policy). Policy therefore changes with circumstance, relative power and interest - a thesis of strategic flexibility, not a verbatim maxim. Why permanence is unavailable. If alignment is defined by relative position, then a state that gains or loses power changes position, and a change of position changes which measure is prudent. Conditionality falls out of the model rather than being asserted about it. The internal counterweight. External flexibility is not amorality. Sovereignty is also bound internally by the seven limbs (saptanga), by calibrated coercion (dandaniti) and by the duty to security and welfare (yogaksema). Kautilya is realistic but not normless. Objection and reply. Objection: such realism is cynical. Reply: strategic flexibility operates alongside the ruler's internal duty toward order and welfare, and misrule weakens sovereignty itself. Verdict. The statement is a fair interpretation of Kautilya's external sovereignty and a poor summary of his theory as a whole: it captures the mandala logic exactly and omits the welfare-bound, organismic account of internal authority that gives that logic its purpose.

(c) "Principled distance is superior to strict separation." Critically evaluate. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. The claim is correct on one specific test and costly on another, and the evaluation must name both. The two models. Strict separation places institutional distance between state and religion; it guards against establishment and may ignore social inequality among religions. Principled distance makes distance context-sensitive: the state may abstain to protect autonomy and intervene to secure equal citizenship and reform oppressive practice, on reasons drawn from liberty, equality and anti-domination. The test on which principled distance wins. The argument for secularism identifies two failure modes. Religious qualification for citizenship creates unequal citizens - the establishment error. Total immunity of communities from public norms leaves internal domination unchallenged - the abandonment error. Strict separation is calibrated against the first alone, so it cannot address intra-religious domination without ceasing to be strict. The cost. The named risk of principled distance is that discretion becomes inconsistent or partisan. Unless the model specifies who judges and on what principle, "context-sensitive" is indistinguishable from selective. Objection and reply. Objection: principled distance is unprincipled practice under a respectable name, since any pattern of engagement can be redescribed after the fact. Reply: the model excludes specific things in advance - engagement that establishes or endorses a faith, abstention that leaves members dominated where the state could relieve it, differential treatment unjustified by the three values, and asymmetric application to majority and minority claims. Residual: the exclusions are only as good as the institution applying them, and the model supplies no guaranteed adjudicator. Verdict. Judged by the prevention of intra-religious as well as inter-religious domination, principled distance is superior - at the price of a discretion that must itself be principled, symmetric and reviewable. Superiority is conditional on the safeguard, not established independently of it.

### Q3
(a) Assess the claim that misrecognition is an independent dimension of injustice rather than a consequence of material inequality. [20 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. The claim is defensible in its analytical form and indefensible in its separatist form: recognition and redistribution are analytically distinct and practically entangled. The two grammars. Maldistribution - exploitation, deprivation, economic marginalisation - is located in the economic structure; its collectivity is a class defined by position in production; used alone it produces economism. Misrecognition - status subordination, stigma, cultural devaluation - is located in institutionalised patterns of cultural value; its collectivity is a status group defined by esteem; used alone it produces culturalism. The argument for independence. Fraser's decisive premise is a pair of cases: a well-paid group can be despised, and a respected group can be poor. Neither wrong is reducible to the other, so a one-dimensional theory will misdiagnose one of them. Her unifying standard is parity of participation - arrangements are just when all adult members can interact as peers - requiring jointly an objective condition of material independence and voice and an intersubjective condition of institutionalised equal respect. The rival position. Honneth holds instead that recognition is the fundamental moral grammar of social conflict, distributive struggles being themselves struggles over what a society esteems. Three spheres - love, rights and solidarity - generate self-confidence, self-respect and self-esteem, injured respectively by abuse, denial of rights and denigration of a way of life. Adjudication. Each has a located weakness. Fraser offers a thinner account of why contempt wounds, since an institutional criterion cannot easily explain an injury felt as damage to the self. Honneth risks psychologising political economy, since automation, capital movement and price changes devastate groups without anyone withholding esteem; his reply that productive contribution is itself a culturally instituted evaluation stretches "recognition" until it explains less. The practical stake. The remedies can pull apart: redistribution often requires dissolving the group as a category, recognition often requires affirming its distinctness, so a programme doing both can appear to affirm an identity it is trying to render irrelevant. Fraser's affirmative/transformative distinction manages this - what is owed is transformation of the value patterns blocking equal standing, including standing inside the group. Indian illustration, bounded. Article 17's abolition of untouchability illustrates a remedy aimed at a status injury; reservations under Articles 15 and 16 combine both. These are dated legal facts and establish nothing about which theory is correct. Verdict.

(b) "Fraternity is the least attended and most necessary of Ambedkar's three principles." Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. The claim is correct about necessity and requires care about what fraternity is. The triad as a structure. Liberty without equality permits domination by the already powerful; equality without liberty suppresses individuality; both without fraternity lack the social disposition to recognise others as equals at all. The three are not parallel goods but mutual conditions. What fraternity names. Not sentimentality or goodwill, but the social basis of democracy - a sense of common associated life among social equals, supported by rights and institutions. It is therefore not the alternative to rights but what rights require in order to function. Why it is most necessary here. Caste is ranked separation: it narrows loyalty to the birth-group and obstructs public spirit, so the disposition to common action among persons who do not know one another is exactly what the order withdraws. A political democracy resting on graded social worth is unstable - one person one vote and one vote one value against unequal worth - and the instability is a deficit of fraternity, not of procedure. The objection. The concept is too vague to do this work: if it cannot be legislated or measured, any failure can be attributed to its absence and any success to its presence. Reply: it is testable where caste operates - whether association across the boundary is free, and whether common action extends beyond the birth-group. The residual limit. Cultivation remains unspecified: no quota produces fraternity, and the owner does not say how it is produced. Verdict. It is most necessary precisely because it is the element no safeguard delivers - which is why safeguards without it remain formal.

(c) Are monarchy and theocracy necessarily related? Discuss with reference to the theory of Divine Right. [15 marks]
Model answer: Thesis. Monarchy and theocracy are conceptually distinct forms that the doctrine of Divine Right has historically bridged; they are therefore related contingently, not necessarily. The doctrine. The Divine Right of Kings holds that the monarch rules by God's will and is answerable primarily to God, not to the people. Historically it strengthened absolutism by sacralising political authority: if the king answers to God alone, no earthly forum may call him to account. The two forms, distinguished. In monarchy the ruler is a king, and divine sanction may legitimise his office. In theocracy sovereignty is explicitly vested in God and interpreted through clergy, scripture or sacred law. The compressed formulation is that divine right makes monarchy theologically legitimated, whereas theocracy makes theology politically constitutive. Why the difference is not verbal. The two produce different chains of answerability. Under divine right the chain runs God -> king -> subjects, and the king is the decisive figure. Under theocracy it runs God -> sacred law -> authorised interpreters -> subjects, and the interpreter is decisive, since sacred law does not apply itself. Consequently the characteristic crisis differs: divine-right monarchy breaks at succession and at the king's personal conduct; theocracy breaks at rival interpretation. Objection and reply. Objection: in practice both claim God's authority, both treat dissent as impiety and both escape ordinary accountability, so the distinction is scholastic. Reply: the convergence is admitted, but the conceptual difference predicts where each regime fails, and a form of government is analysed by its characteristic crisis. The distinction does weaken where the ruler is also chief interpreter, and that should be conceded. Verdict. Not necessarily related: monarchies have rested on heredity, conquest or tradition with no theological claim, and theocratic arrangements have existed without a king. Divine Right is the bridge that explains the historical intersection without establishing a conceptual identity.

### Q4
(a) "Neither the village nor centralised modernity is inherently emancipatory for caste." Examine this claim with reference to Gandhi and Ambedkar. [20 marks]
Model answer: Position. The claim is correct, and its force lies in replacing a contrast of eras with a test. The two positions. Gandhi treats decentralised community as a possible school of responsibility, meaningful labour and self-rule: it is where conduct is enacted daily, and his constructive programme of education, sanitation and common service presupposes exactly that setting. Ambedkar warns that the actually existing village can concentrate caste authority, dependency and exclusion - precisely because face-to-face dependence makes exit costly and observation constant. Why this is not tradition against modernity. The disagreement is not about eras. Centralised modern institutions can reproduce caste through recruitment, networks and the informal transmission of standing; local community can reproduce it through dependence and surveillance. Neither scale carries emancipation as a property. The test that replaces the contrast. Ask whether scale, technology and community enlarge mobility, equal status, constitutional rights and exit from inherited occupation. That test is applied to both, and it is what makes the claim examinable rather than rhetorical. Running the test. Gandhi's setting scores on shared practice, since common service attacks the ritual rule that certain labour pollutes those who perform it; it scores poorly on exit, because dependence is unchanged and there is nowhere to go. Ambedkar's institutional route scores on rights and exit, since a benefit that can be withdrawn is not a right and representation supplies voice; it scores poorly on everyday relations, which is why he insists that constitutional morality and fraternity must accompany the machinery. Objection and reply. The test is impossible to apply, since mobility and status are not measurable. Reply: it is applied comparatively rather than absolutely - whether an arrangement enlarges or contracts each of the four - and the owner asserts no measurement of any Indian case. Verdict. Neither scale is emancipatory without anti-caste power. The village is not condemned as such and modernity is not credited as such; what decides is whether either arrangement opens the marriage circle, dissolves dependence and secures rights that do not depend on goodwill.

(b) Does technological development lead to progress in the ethical standards of the society? Explain. [15 marks]
Model answer: Position. No - not on its own. Technology changes capacities and social relations; it does not supply its own ends. The optimist mechanism, stated fairly. Communication can widen knowledge and sympathy; medicine reduces suffering; productivity can reduce drudgery; transparency tools can aid accountability. Each is a real mechanism, not a straw position. The defeater, and it is structural. Every one of those capacities is double-edged in the same respect. The communication capacity that widens sympathy enables surveillance and manipulation. The medicine that reduces suffering can be distributed unequally, so the capacity exists while the relief does not. The automation that removes drudgery displaces and deskills. The information systems that support accountability reproduce the bias in their inputs. What the symmetry shows. These are one point made four times: the direction of the effect is fixed by who controls the capacity, who can reach it, and what it is aimed at - and none of the three is supplied by the technology. Verdict. Technical progress becomes social progress only when governed by ethical purposes, fair access, accountability and human control. The concession that must be made. The relation is not simply null. Technology reshapes which moral problems arise, creating new occasions for choice, new possible harms and new meaningful duties. What it does not do is supply the criterion by which any of them is settled. Link to progress. This is the non-inevitability argument applied: capacity enlarges for care and destruction alike, institutions distribute its control and risks, and moral learning can be reversed - so progress requires agency, institutions and judgment rather than following from capability.

(c) Critically evaluate Laski's view on sovereignty as a satisfactory position in political theory. [15 marks]
Model answer: Naming the test. A position is satisfactory in political theory if it describes political life accurately, protects the values it claims to protect, and supplies an account of the function it displaces. Laski passes the first two tests decisively and the third only partly. The position. Laski rejects the monistic doctrine that the state possesses absolute, indivisible and unlimited sovereignty. Individuals belong simultaneously to family, church, union, guild, cooperative, profession and locality, and these bodies command real loyalty; the state is therefore one association among many, uniquely important but not the sole source of obligation. Allegiance must be earned by protecting rights, securing welfare and enabling meaningful life, not claimed by legal title. Why absolutism is rejected - three grounds of three kinds. Philosophically, no human institution is entitled to unlimited moral obedience; politically, absolute sovereignty legitimises tyranny and suppresses liberty; sociologically, real societies distribute power among many groups. Their independence is the argument's strength: answering one leaves the others standing. First objection and reply. Without a final sovereign, conflict between associations becomes insoluble. Laski replies that legal finality does not eliminate conflict but often suppresses legitimate diversity, and that modern orders need a final procedure, not an unanswerable will. Second objection and reply. Shared sovereignty is conceptually incoherent. His real claim is that the monistic concept itself misdescribes overlapping authority. Residual problem. Neither reply supplies a decision procedure for emergencies, defence or redistribution; MacIver's residual coordinating role concedes exactly this. Verdict. Satisfactory as a critique of absolutism and as a defence of liberty through social plurality; not satisfactory as a complete replacement for the concept of final authority. Note also the phase caution: "Laski abolishes sovereignty" is as inaccurate as "Laski accepts Austin".

### Q5 (Compulsory)
(a) State and evaluate the proofs for the existence of God as propounded in Jainism. [10 marks]
Model answer: Jainism denies God as creator: the world was never created, since all substances are eternal and change only through modes (utpada-vyaya-dhrauvya). But "Jainism denies God, and yet does not deny godhead. Every liberated soul is a god." Its proofs are accordingly proofs of attainable divinity. First, from the intrinsic nature of the jiva: every soul possesses infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and power (ananta-catustaya), obscured by material karma rather than absent - for we observe degrees of knowledge, not its absence. Second, from gradation to a limiting case: mati, sruta, avadhi and manahparyaya mark progressive removal of karmic obstruction, whose complete removal is kevala-jnana, omniscience. Third, from the testimony of the omniscient Tirthankaras (apta-vacana), mortals who won liberation by personal effort - "we are all potential Jinas". Evaluation: these establish a plural, achieved, non-creative godhood and rest wholly on Jaina premises - intrinsic omniscience and material karma. They neither prove nor attempt a cosmic God. Sharma's dilemma bites: if karma is inseparable from the soul, liberation is impossible; if separable, bondage is.

(b) "The Euthyphro dilemma refutes divine command theory but leaves theistic ethics standing." Discuss. [10 marks]
Model answer: The claim is correct, and stating why requires care about what the dilemma targets. The Euthyphro (10a) asks whether the pious is loved because it is pious or pious because it is loved. On the first horn, command constitutes goodness - so cruelty would have been good had it been commanded, and "God is good" collapses into "God commands what God commands". On the second, the command tracks a goodness it does not create, and the constitutive thesis is abandoned. That is a refutation of a theory of grounding, not of theism. A theist may occupy the second horn deliberately: natural law does exactly this, grounding an objective order of human goods in divine reason while allowing practical reason to know much of it without revelation. Modified divine command theory takes the other route, grounding commands in an essentially loving nature, which blocks arbitrariness at the cost of conceding that "loving" is a moral concept we already grasp. The dilemma therefore removes one theistic ethics and leaves at least two others intact.

(c) Distinguish between rebirth and reincarnation, and explain why the distinction matters for a comparative account of Indian soteriology. [10 marks]
Model answer: The distinction. Reincarnation - literally re-enfleshing - presupposes a substantial soul that re-enters a new body. Rebirth is the wider and more neutral term for renewed becoming within a beginningless series, and it commits the user to a continuity-bearer without specifying that the bearer is a substance. Why it matters. (i) It fixes school-attribution: reincarnation is apt for Vedanta, Nyaya and Jainism, and incorrect for Buddhism, whose re-becoming (punarbhava) has no transmigrating self. (ii) It prevents a false consensus: writing "all Indian schools accept reincarnation" silently imports a substance-metaphysics into a school that denies it. (iii) It shapes the modal verdict on necessity questions, since the counter-example only exists once the wider term is available. (iv) Sanskrit registers the range - punarjanma, punaravrtti, punarbhava, samsara - so the distinction is lexical as well as doctrinal. Conclusion. The distinction is not terminological fastidiousness: it is what keeps a comparative account from begging the central metaphysical question.

(d) Can a religious utterance be both performative and truth-apt? Discuss with reference to prayer, confession and doctrinal assertion. [10 marks]
Model answer: Religious language performs several acts, but performance does not exhaust cognition. A confession avows commitment, a prayer petitions or praises, and a liturgical formula helps constitute a worshipping practice. Austin's speech-act distinction therefore explains why an utterance may do something rather than merely report a fact. Yet many religious sentences also make truth-claims: 'God is creator' differs from a promise because believers offer reasons, draw implications and reject contradictory assertions. Hare's blik account captures orientation but risks insulating belief from evidence; Wittgensteinian language-games capture use but need not make practices immune to criticism. Aquinas' analogy preserves reference without univocal description, while apophatic theology disciplines overstatement. Thus performative and cognitive functions can coexist. Their relative weight depends on genre: prayer is primarily performative, creed is more assertoric, and myth may disclose meaning without functioning as literal history.

(e) Is Hinduism poly-theistic? Give reasons for your answer. [10 marks]
Model answer: Hinduism is polytheistic at the level of ritual plurality: many deities have distinct names, forms, myths and devotional communities, and the Rgvedic pantheon is genuinely function-divided. But the criterion must be announced first - polytheism means plurality of ultimates, not of names, forms or access-points. Three texts block the simple label. Rgveda 1.164.46 says the one existent is spoken of variously by the wise, naming Indra, Mitra, Varuna and Agni as designations of a single referent. Rgveda 10.129 (Nasadiya) resolves origins into "the One" and then doubts whether even the highest overseer knows. Brhadaranyaka 3.9 reduces "three and three thousand" gods to one. Vedic religion is therefore better called henotheistic or kathenotheistic - F. Max Muller's terms for the pattern in which the invoked deity is addressed as supreme without the others being denied. Devotional and philosophical structures reinforce this. The chosen deity (istadevata), descent-forms (avatara) and the Vaisnava, Saiva and Sakta theisms are each monotheistic in form. Advaita makes deities empirically valid but non-dual Brahman ultimate; Ramanuja affirms one personal Brahman qualified by real selves and world; Nyaya posits one omniscient Isvara as cosmic governor.

### Q6
(a) Compare St. Thomas Aquinas' five ways of knowing God with the arguments of the Nyaya School of Indian Philosophy for the existence of God. [20 marks]
Model answer: Aquinas and the Naiyayikas both practise a posteriori natural theology: each begins from a general feature of the world and argues to an ultimate intelligent reality. Their convergence is real, and so is their divergence. Aquinas' Five Ways (Summa Theologiae I, q.2, a.3) argue from change to a prime mover; from efficient causation to a first cause; from contingency to a necessary being; from degrees of perfection to a maximum; and from purposive regularity in non-cognitive nature to a divine intelligence. The regress he excludes is essentially ordered - the stick moves the stone only while the hand moves the stick - so "first" means explanatory priority, not the earliest event in time. Nyaya's Isvara-anumana proceeds through several routes. Karyat: composite objects are effects, since they are made of parts (savayava) and possess intermediate magnitude (avantara-mahattva); material causes cannot achieve the required order and co-ordination without an intelligent maker, who must be omniscient, since only such a being can directly know atoms. Ayojanat: inert atoms need purposive combination. Dhrtyadeh: the world's support and dissolution require governance. Padat / sruteh: Vedic authority presupposes an omniscient author. Finally, the argument from adrsta: the unconscious stock of merit and demerit cannot allot determinate joys and sorrows, so God is karmaphaladata. The convergence: both infer intelligence from order; both add attributes stepwise rather than by definition; both are cumulative rather than single-inference systems, Udayana multiplying routes exactly as Aquinas multiplies Ways. The divergence is decisive. Aquinas' God is the cause of being, creating ex nihilo; Nyaya's Isvara is the efficient cause only, ordering eternal, uncreated atoms while souls, space, time and adrsta remain co-present with Him. Aquinas' proofs are praeambula fidei drawn from reason alone; two Nyaya arguments are explicitly scriptural. Aquinas' God is unconditioned; Nyaya's is morally constrained by adrsta. Consequently the critiques differ in weight. Hume's analogy objection strikes Nyaya hardest, since its inference is expressly artefact-modelled on pot and potter; while the redundancy objection - that karma and atoms could operate without a supervisor - is uniquely Indian and, as C. D. Sharma presses it, uniquely damaging. Verdict: the same inferential architecture, but not the same God: Aquinas' terminus is metaphysically ultimate, Nyaya's is cosmically administrative.

(b) Critically evaluate the Buddhistic arguments against the existence of God as the creator of the world. [15 marks]
Model answer: Buddhism rejects a creator God (jagat-karta) without rejecting spiritual ultimacy; its critique is a systematic refusal of explanatory surplus rather than bare atheism. Four canonical lines recur. First, if God is eternal and unchanging, genuine creation is hard to explain, since willing or producing appears to involve change; Dharmakirti-type reasoning sharpens this - a permanent, immutable cause fully present at all times should produce its effect always and at once, never in temporal succession. Second, if God creates from desire or purpose, this implies lack, preference or incompleteness, which is inconsistent with perfection. Third, suffering and evil sit uneasily with an omnipotent benevolent creator. Fourth, dependent origination already explains the arising of phenomena through conditions, so a creator is redundant. To these may be added the methodological point that questions of a first beginning fall among the avyakrta, undeclared because soteriologically idle - the wounded man who insists on knowing the archer before the arrow is removed. Evaluation must be even-handed. The change objection assumes a temporal model of divine willing; classical theism answers with a single eternal act producing a temporal effect. The desire objection assumes creation must remedy a lack; theists appeal to gratuitous diffusive goodness. Most importantly, Nyaya replies that dependent origination explains sequence but not desert-appropriate allocation: an unconscious nexus cannot proportion determinate fruit to determinate merit. Explanatory economy, moreover, is a methodological preference, not a disproof. Verdict: Buddhism establishes that a coherent, ethically serious cosmology is possible without a creator. That is a substantial philosophical achievement - but it shifts the burden of proof rather than closing the question.

(c) Can a non-theistic religion retain worship, transcendence and liberation without covertly reintroducing God? Discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Religion need not be defined by belief in a creator. Buddhism and Jainism organise doctrine, discipline, community and liberation without a sovereign God. Their objects of reverence are awakened teachers, perfected beings, truth and a path of transformation rather than a creator who commands obedience. Worship can therefore express gratitude, emulation and moral self-formation. Transcendence also need not mean a supernatural person: nirvana transcends craving and conditioned suffering, while Jain liberation releases the soul from karmic bondage. The objection is that ultimate law, the Buddha ideal or liberated beings may perform functions commonly assigned to God. Yet functional similarity is not identity. Neither dependent origination nor karma is an omniscient will, and liberation is not divine grace. Non-theistic traditions thus show that religion can centre on diagnosis, practice and transformation. They do not covertly restore God unless every object of ultimacy is defined as divine, a definition so broad that it erases the distinction under examination. The institutional test is equally important: monastic rules, confession, pilgrimage and ethical vows can sustain a religious form of life without prayer to a creator. The concept of religion should therefore be family-resemblance based, not tied to one Abrahamic model of belief. This preserves genuine doctrinal difference instead of translating every path into disguised theism.

### Q7
(a) "Religion supplies morality with motivation and scope; morality supplies religion with a standard. Neither supplies the other with a foundation." Critically evaluate. [20 marks]
Model answer: The thesis is defensible in all three of its parts, and evaluating it requires separating grounding from everything else. Neither supplies a foundation - the first half. Morality is not conceptually grounded in religion. The Euthyphro forces the choice between arbitrariness, on which "God is good" is a tautology, and independence, on which goodness is prior to command. Modified divine command theory blocks the first horn by grounding commands in an essentially loving nature, and Alston denies the regress by treating God as the supreme standard; but describing that nature as loving uses a moral concept we must already possess, so non-arbitrariness is bought with conceptual independence. Four independent non-theistic groundings confirm the result empirically: Kantian self-legislation; Mimamsa's duty from injunction alone, which retains scriptural authority while removing any personal commander; Santideva's derivation of impartial altruism from the ownerlessness of suffering; and the Jain vow-structure around non-injury. Neither supplies a foundation - the second half. Religion is not grounded in morality either. A religion is more than an ethic: it makes claims about ultimate reality, transformation and liberation, and reducing it to morality misdescribes it as thoroughly as reducing morality to command misdescribes morality. Religion supplies motivation and scope. The mechanisms are identifiable - exemplars, ritual formation, community sanction, hope and repentance, a sustaining worldview - and the scope claim is strongest in the hard case. Where secular theories stop at the undetected, costly act, karma is exhaustive and impersonal, so invisibility to persons is irrelevant, and niskama-karma (2.47, 3.19) removes the fruit from deliberation entirely. Morality supplies a standard. Conscience criticises religious practice, and does so most powerfully from within: prophetic denunciation of ritual without justice, and reform against caste and sati. A claimed command remains answerable to identification, interpretation, consistency, proportionality, human dignity and the standing of the authority invoked. The qualifications that decide the grade. The motivational advantage is priced: if it operates through sanction it is heteronomous and forfeits moral worth, so only the divine-love and niskama-karma versions win it cleanly. The influence is contingent: the same mechanisms can sanction exclusion. And the asymmetry the thesis implies needs its own explanation - morality is constitutive of what qualifies as a religion, while religion is only supportive of morality. Verdict, and the defeater. The thesis is correct: the relation is one of motivation, scope and mutual criticism, not of foundation in either direction.

(b) Critically examine the idea of Brahman in Advaita philosophy of Samkara. Does Samkara's conception of Brahman leave room for theism? Discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: Samkara's Advaita identifies nirguna Brahman as the sole ultimate (paramarthika) reality: non-dual, self-revealing consciousness beyond limiting, relational predicates. Sat-cit-ananda does not add three properties to Brahman; these are its essential nature-defining marks (svarupa-laksana), as against the relational definition of Brahman as world-cause (tatastha-laksana, "that from which arises the origin of this"). Neti neti (Brhadaranyaka 2.3.6) removes objectifying descriptions rather than reducing Brahman to nothing, and Taittiriya 2.9 marks the failure of speech, not the absence of reality. The experienced plurality of self, world and deity is explained through maya and superimposition (adhyasa). The world is dependent and sublatable, not sheer non-being; it is the world-appearance, not Brahman, that is properly called indeterminable (anirvacaniya). Brahman does not really transform: the world is apparent transformation (vivarta), as a snake appears on a rope. Viewed in relation to maya, Brahman is saguna Isvara, omniscient creator, moral governor and object of worship at the transactional (vyavaharika) level. This clearly leaves room for theism. Devotion, meditation, scripture and divine governance possess genuine empirical validity and purify the mind for liberating knowledge. Yet the room is provisional, because knowledge finally sublates the distinction among worshipper, worshipped and world; ultimate Brahman is neither a person alongside persons nor a creator in an unsublatable relation. Ramanuja objects that a quality-less absolute is unintelligible and demotes religion, since what is wholly undetermined is neither knowable, nameable nor worshippable. Advaita replies that finite predicates limit ultimacy and that negation removes superimposition, not reality. The reply preserves absoluteness but leaves positive intelligibility and the final status of devotion under pressure. Therefore Samkara is neither a straightforward theist nor an atheist: Advaita validates empirical theism as path while transcending it in non-dual realisation as goal.

(c) Assess the claim that moral criticism of religious practice is necessarily an external, secular imposition. [15 marks]
Model answer: The claim is false, and the evidence that refutes it comes from inside the traditions. Moral criticism of religion is standardly internal. The Hebrew prophets denounce festivals and sacrifices offered without justice; the Buddha criticises Vedic sacrifice as ineffective; Kabir and Nanak attack ritualism and caste-observance; the Gita (2.42-43) disparages those enchanted by promises of ritual reward. Reform movements against caste and sati argued in religious vocabulary, appealing to non-harm, equality, conscience and spiritual unity. The structural point is more important than the examples. Interpretation of a claimed command already deploys moral concepts: identifying which reading is faithful, which authority is legitimate, and which application is proportionate cannot be done without them. The six tests to which any claimed command remains answerable - identification, interpretation, consistency, proportionality, human dignity and the moral status of the authority invoked - are therefore not imported after the fact. They are already operating inside the interpretive act. This answers the standing objection that morality judging revelation is arrogance, the finite judging the infinite. What is being judged is a human claim about a command, not the divine as such. Two qualifications are owed. First, religious moralities conflict with one another over duties, authority, exceptions and interpretation, so internal criticism does not converge on a single moral code. Second, the reply that an immoral religious practice is internal corruption rather than a counter-example works only if morality is genuinely treated as constitutive of worthy religion - which is a substantive commitment, not a rhetorical escape. Moral criticism of religion is therefore neither necessarily secular nor necessarily hostile. It is most powerful precisely where it is internal, because there it cannot be met by the charge of imposing alien standards.

### Q8
(a) Critically compare the Semitic doctrine of resurrection with the Indian doctrine of rebirth, and assess whether either succeeds in securing the identity of the person who survives. [20 marks]
Model answer: Frame. These are not variants of one idea. They differ in number of lives, mechanism, judge, telos, identity-bearer, treatment of the body and direction of time. Cullmann (1955) argues that Greek immortality of the soul and Hebrew resurrection of the dead are opposed rather than complementary - the first releases the soul from the body, the second treats the person as a psychosomatic unity that only a divine act can restore. The grid. Resurrection: one earthly life; a divine act at the end of history; God as judge; restored embodied life in communion; the body essential, a "spiritual body" still a body; linear time. Rebirth: a beginningless series; karma maturing through the wandering-on; the karmic law itself, with God ratifying in Nyaya and Ramanuja; exit from the series as telos; the body discarded like clothing (Gita 2.22) while the subtle body carries dispositions; cyclical time with a linear exit. Identity under resurrection. Two problems: the gap between death and raising, and the re-assembly objection where matter is shared between bodies. Three replies: Aquinas (the rational soul as form secures continuity); van Inwagen (God preserves the body or a core, leaving a simulacrum); Hick (a unique psychophysical replica with memory and character is the person). The duplication objection is decisive against the third as stated: if one replica, why not two? Identity under rebirth. The memory objection: nothing of prior lives is recalled. The reply distinguishes episodic memory from metaphysical continuity and appeals to latent dispositions - coherent, but evidentially empty. Buddhism avoids the problem by abandoning strict identity for causal descent, at the cost of the ownership worry. Assessment. Neither secures identity, but for opposite reasons: resurrection must show that a re-constituted person is numerically the same; rebirth must show that a being with no memory of me is me. Conclusion. They answer different questions - how an essentially embodied person can survive, and how moral desert can be discharged when one life is too short - and both leave the identity question open.

(b) The problem of evil is a direct offshoot of how God is conceptualised in a system." Critically discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: The thesis is correct, and its demonstration is straightforward. State the classical set: God is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good and creator, and evil exists; the contradiction requires the bridging principles that a good being eliminates evil as far as it can and that omnipotence has no relevant limit. The argument bites only where all four attributes are held together. Now vary the concept. Weaken omnipotence and the process God, whose power is persuasive by metaphysical necessity, is simply not the kind of being who could have prevented the evil unilaterally - the charge lapses. Weaken personality and Advaita's nirguna Brahman is untouched: evil is real at the empirical level (vyavaharika) and sublated under liberating knowledge at the ultimate level (paramarthika). Remove the creator and Buddhism, Jainism and Mimamsa have no defendant at all: suffering is explained by conditions and by deeds, and the moral law is self-operating. Only classical theism carries the full burden. The critical half is the price list. The process route surrenders sovereignty and any guarantee of final victory over evil. Advaita risks minimising present suffering. Non-creator systems give up providence, divine rescue and a personal guarantor of justice. Concept-dependence is therefore not a solution: it moves the cost from the argument into the theology. The theodicies - free-will, soul-making, privation - exist precisely because some theists refuse to pay that price and try to keep every attribute. Verdict: the problem is a stress-test of a particular God-concept, not of religion as such - which is why the same suffering that refutes one theology leaves another entirely untouched.

(c) What is the logical problem of evil? Discuss. [15 marks]
Model answer: The logical problem alleges that a set of theistic beliefs cannot all be true. J. L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (Mind, 1955), sets out the propositions: God is omnipotent; God is perfectly good; and evil exists. The full classical profile adds omniscience, which blocks the reply that evil escaped divine notice. Crucially, these propositions are not formally contradictory by themselves. Nothing in them, plus logic alone, entails both a proposition and its negation. The contradiction is manufactured by two bridging principles: first, that a perfectly good being eliminates evil as far as it can; second, that an omnipotent being has no relevant limit on preventing any evil whose prevention is logically possible. Mackie sorts the replies into adequate solutions, which honestly abandon one proposition, and fallacious solutions, which appear to keep all three while covertly limiting power or redefining goodness. The theist has three exits, each attacking a bridge. Insert "unless there is a morally sufficient reason" into the goodness bridge, and the whole space of defences and theodicies opens. Deny the power bridge and one gets Plantinga's free-will defence: significant freedom entails genuine alternatives, God cannot causally determine a creature to freely choose only rightly, so a world containing moral good may be unavailable without moral evil. Plantinga needs only possibility, which is why transworld depravity is asserted as possible and never as actual. Denying the reality of evil - privation, or the empirical level in Advaita - is the third and most costly exit. Verdict: the logical problem is widely regarded as answered; the live debate has migrated to Rowe's evidential form, where consistency no longer suffices.
