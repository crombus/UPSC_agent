## 5. Political and military completion

- Humayun inherited weak finance, uneven control, eastern Afghans and a small
  unrooted ruling coalition.
- Brothers were analysed through patrimonial claims, commands and chronology.
- Afghan chiefs competed as well as coalesced; Sher Khan built, rather than
  inherited automatically, a Bihar-Bengal coalition.
- Rajput houses pursued distinct interests; the Karnavati-rakhi story remained
  later tradition.
- Gujarat was military victory without administrative settlement.
- Bengal exposed route, river, monsoon, supply and communications weakness.
- Chausa (1539) and Kannauj/Bilgram (1540) were distinguished operationally.
- Defeat combined inherited fragility, overreach, Afghan resources, fraternal/
  noble coordination, logistics, decisions and contingency.

## 6. Exile and restoration

- Hamida Banu and household networks were restored as political actors.
- Akbar's 1542 Amarkot birth was placed in local-refuge context.
- Shah Tahmasp's aid involved ceremony, sectarian presentation and Qandahar
  negotiation, not total conversion/Persianization.
- Qandahar/Kabul recovery from 1545 involved fraternal conflict and local power.
- Sur fragmentation after Islam Shah was bounded to political transition.
- Lahore, Machhiwara, Sirhind, Bairam Khan and Delhi formed the 1555 reconquest.
- Restoration was not full consolidation.

## 7. Culture, women and assessment

Dinpanah/Purana Qila phases and astrological/ceremonial interests were treated
as courtly cosmology/political performance, not proof of eccentric incapacity.
Gulbadan and Hamida Banu illuminate gendered household politics with source
limits. Humayun's Tomb post-dates him and remains a memory/architecture bridge.

Humayun was assessed as an ambitious ruler who made serious errors within a
structurally shallow empire, preserved dynastic networks in exile and restored
sovereignty without completing consolidation.
