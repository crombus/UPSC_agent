## Q1. Do you think that Constitution of India does not accept principle of strict separation of powers rather it is based on the principle of ‘checks and balance’? Explain.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Salient Features and Constitutional Parts](../../topics/polity/salient-features-and-constitutional-parts.md) → Separation of powers and checks and balances · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain with assessment  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional design → Separation of powers  
**Canonical owner:** [Salient Features](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/03_Salient-Features.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `separation-of-powers-checks-and-balances`

**Demand decoding:** Take a position, distinguish functional separation from watertight separation, and demonstrate reciprocal constitutional controls among the three organs.

**Compact framework:** Qualified thesis → functional allocation → checks → judicial doctrine → conclusion.

### Model answer

The Constitution rejects a watertight separation but distributes primary functions and creates checks to prevent concentration of power.

- **Functional allocation:** Articles **53/154** vest executive power; Articles **79/168** establish legislatures; Articles **124/214** establish superior courts. Articles **121/211** protect judicial conduct from ordinary legislative discussion, while Article **50** directs separation of judiciary from executive.
- **Checks:** Parliament controls the executive through collective responsibility under Articles **75(3)/164(2)**, questions and finance. The executive participates in legislation through ordinances under Articles **123/213** and assent. Courts review legislation and executive action under Articles **13, 32 and 226**; judges may be removed by Parliament.

In *Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955), the Supreme Court held that Indian separation is not rigid. *Kesavananda Bharati* and *Indira Nehru Gandhi* made separation/judicial review elements of the basic structure.

**Conclusion:** India follows separated functions with institutional overlap and mutual accountability—checks and balances, not isolated compartments.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly accepts the proposition, supplies article-wise institutional evidence and states the controlling judicial position.

## Q2. “The Central Administrative Tribunal which was established for redressal of grievances and complaints by or against central government employees, nowadays is exercising its powers as an independent judicial authority.” Explain.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Administrative Tribunals](../../topics/polity/administrative-tribunals.md) → CAT’s status and judicial independence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Tribunals → Central Administrative Tribunal  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Statutory and quasi-judicial bodies  
**Canonical owners:** [Administrative Tribunals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Administrative-Tribunals.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cat-independent-judicial-authority`

**Demand decoding:** Explain CAT’s constitutional/statutory foundation, judicial functions and present autonomy while qualifying the claim through constitutional judicial review.

**Compact framework:** Origin → judicial character → independence → constitutional limits.

### Model answer

The Central Administrative Tribunal was created under Article **323A** and the Administrative Tribunals Act, **1985** to decide Union-service disputes. It summons evidence, applies natural justice, grants binding relief and punishes contempt under Section **17**.

Its authority became more judicially independent after *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997). The Supreme Court invalidated exclusion of High Court review, held tribunals to be courts of first instance in their assigned fields, and preserved scrutiny under Articles **226/227** as part of the basic structure. CAT is therefore not a departmental appellate body; its benches adjudicate disputes between government and employees.

However, executive influence over appointments, service conditions and infrastructure qualifies its independence. *Madras Bar Association* cases require secure tenure and judicially weighted selection.

**Conclusion:** CAT performs independent adjudication within its domain, but remains supplementary—not substitutive—to constitutional courts and requires stronger institutional insulation.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both the transformation into an adjudicatory authority and the precise limits of that independence.

## Q3. What are the methods used by the farmers’ organisations to influence the policy-makers in India and how effective are these methods?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Pressure Groups](../../topics/polity/pressure-groups.md) → Farmers’ organisations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Identify methods; Assess effectiveness  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Pressure groups → Farmers’ organisations  
**Canonical owner:** [Pressure Groups](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/44_Pressure-Groups.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `farmers-organisations-policy-influence`

**Demand decoding:** Classify the influence channels and give a differentiated verdict on their effectiveness, representativeness and policy consequences.

**Compact framework:** Insider methods → outsider methods → electoral/legal methods → effectiveness and limits.

### Model answer

Farmers’ organisations operate as interest groups through multiple channels:

- **Institutional lobbying:** memoranda, consultations with ministries, appearances before committees and engagement with bodies such as the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices.
- **Collective mobilisation:** rallies, marches, *dharnas*, road/rail blockades and market boycotts; the Bharatiya Kisan Union’s mobilisations illustrate bargaining through numbers.
- **Electoral leverage:** endorsements, candidate pressure and issue voting in agriculturally important States.
- **Coalition and communication:** alliances with unions and civil society, social-media campaigns and expert studies.

These methods have secured loan waivers, price-support debate, electricity concessions and modification of contentious measures. The Swaminathan Commission’s **C2+50%** recommendation became a policy anchor.

Yet effectiveness is uneven: movements are fragmented by crop, class, caste and region; large landholders often dominate; disruptive tactics may impose public costs; and implementation frequently trails announcements.

**Conclusion:** Farmers’ organisations are effective veto and agenda-setting actors, but less consistently successful in securing coherent, inclusive agrarian reform.

**Why this earns marks:** It classifies methods before giving a balanced, evidence-based assessment rather than merely listing protests.

## Q4. From the resolution of contentious issues regarding distribution of legislative powers by the courts, ‘Principle of Federal Supremacy’ and ‘Harmonious Construction’ have emerged. Explain.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre–State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → Legislative conflicts and interpretation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain doctrines  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Centre–State relations → Legislative distribution  
**Canonical owner:** [Centre–State and Inter-State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/13_Centre-State-and-Inter-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `federal-supremacy-harmonious-construction`

**Demand decoding:** Define both doctrines, locate their constitutional basis, explain their sequence of application and illustrate judicial use.

**Compact framework:** Distribution → reconciliation first → Union priority if irreconcilable → qualification.

### Model answer

Articles **245–246** and the Seventh Schedule distribute legislative fields, but entries overlap because they describe broad subjects.

**Harmonious construction** requires courts first to read competing entries together so each legislature retains meaningful operation. Under “pith and substance”, incidental encroachment does not invalidate a law.

**Federal supremacy** applies when a genuine, irreconcilable conflict remains. Article **246(1)** gives the Union List predominance over State and Concurrent Lists; Article **254** makes a parliamentary law prevail over a repugnant State law on a Concurrent subject, subject to Article **254(2)** and possible later parliamentary override. Residuary power also lies with Parliament under Article **248**.

Thus supremacy is not the first interpretive move. *State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries* requires a broad, coordinated reading of entries.

**Conclusion:** Harmonious construction preserves federal domains; federal supremacy is the constitutional tie-breaker, not a licence for routine central encroachment.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the doctrines as a sequenced judicial method and anchors each in constitutional text and cases.

## Q5. What can France learn from the Indian Constitution’s approach to secularism?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → Indian and French secularism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Derive comparative lessons  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitution → Secularism  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Fundamental rights  
**Canonical owners:** [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md) · [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/07_Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-france-secularism-lessons`

**Demand decoding:** Contrast *laïcité* with India’s principled-distance model and identify transferable lessons without claiming that either model can be transplanted unchanged.

**Compact framework:** Models → Indian strengths → lessons for France → qualification.

### Model answer

French *laïcité*, rooted in the **1905** separation law, protects the public sphere from religious control; India instead combines equal citizenship with “principled distance”.

France may draw four lessons:

- **Positive freedom:** Articles **25–28** protect conscience and religious practice, subject to public order, morality, health and other rights.
- **Substantive equality:** Articles **14–16** permit intervention against exclusion. In *Shirur Mutt* and later cases, autonomy is protected but secular activity remains regulable.
- **Accommodation:** Articles **29–30** secure minority cultural and educational rights, enabling integration without enforced cultural invisibility.
- **Proportionality:** Restrictions should answer demonstrable harm rather than treat every visible religious symbol as incompatible with citizenship.

India’s model also warns against selective appeasement and majoritarian capture. French common citizenship remains valuable.

**Conclusion:** France can supplement institutional separation with accommodation, minority protection and proportionate regulation; the lesson is equal freedom through principled engagement, not Indian-style transplantation.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives precise constitutional lessons while preserving the historical and institutional distinctiveness of both models.

## Q6. Despite consistent experience of high growth, India still goes with the lowest indicators of human development. Examine the issues that make balanced and inclusive development elusive.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Social Justice, Inclusion and Welfare-State Framework](../../topics/social-justice/social-justice-concept-inclusion-and-welfare-state-framework.md) → Inclusive human development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Inclusive development → Human development deficits  
**Canonical owner:** [Social Justice, Inclusion and Welfare-State Framework](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/01_Social-Justice-Concept-Inclusion-and-Welfare-State-Framework.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `growth-human-development-inclusive-development`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the growth–human-development disconnect through structural, fiscal, institutional and social barriers and end with an integrated remedy.

**Compact framework:** Disconnect → distribution/capabilities/jobs/governance → convergence response.

### Model answer

Economic growth enlarges resources, but human development depends on their distribution and conversion into health, education and dignified livelihoods.

Balanced inclusion remains elusive because:

- growth has been capital- and skill-intensive, while informality, low female labour participation and disguised agricultural unemployment persist;
- unequal land, credit, digital access and schooling reproduce caste, gender, tribal and regional disadvantage;
- low-quality public health, nutrition, water and education weaken capabilities despite schemes;
- fragmented programmes, exclusion errors and weak local capacity reduce last-mile delivery;
- fiscal and administrative centralisation limits context-specific State and local responses;
- environmental degradation and displacement impose disproportionate costs on vulnerable groups.

Articles **38, 39, 41, 46 and 47** envisage a capability-oriented welfare state. The Finance Commission, NITI Aayog’s Aspirational Districts approach and social audits can support convergence, but need adequate funds and accountability.

**Conclusion:** India must shift from growth-first redistribution to employment-rich, regionally balanced growth backed by universal basic services and empowered local institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It examines causal barriers rather than merely asserting inequality and links remedies to constitutional social policy.

## Q7. There is a growing divergence in the relationship between poverty and hunger in India. The shrinking of social expenditure by the government is forcing the poor to spend more on non-food essential items squeezing their food-budget. Elucidate.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Poverty–hunger divergence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Elucidate causal relationship  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty and hunger → Household expenditure squeeze  
**Canonical owner:** [Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `poverty-hunger-social-expenditure-divergence`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why income-poverty reduction need not eliminate hunger, trace the non-food expenditure mechanism and identify policy implications.

**Compact framework:** Measurement divergence → expenditure squeeze → consequences → corrective framework.

### Model answer

Poverty lines measure command over a minimum consumption basket, whereas hunger reflects food quantity, dietary quality, absorption and intra-household distribution. Hence monetary poverty may fall while malnutrition persists.

When affordable public provision is inadequate, poor households must purchase medicines, schooling, transport, rent, fuel, water and sanitation. Their rising share compresses food expenditure, causing substitution from pulses, milk, eggs and vegetables towards cheaper calories. Informality, food inflation, disease and unsafe water deepen the deficit.

Right-to-food jurisprudence under Article **21**, the National Food Security Act, **2013**, ICDS and Mid-Day Meals recognise that food security requires entitlements, not aggregate availability. Cereal support alone cannot cure malnutrition.

**Conclusion:** The remedy is not only higher income: protect real food budgets through universal-quality health and education, nutrition-sensitive PDS, maternity support, safe water and accountable local delivery, as required by Articles **39(b), 47**.

**Why this earns marks:** It elucidates the exact household-budget transmission mechanism and distinguishes income poverty from multidimensional nutrition deprivation.

## Q11. On what grounds a people’s representative can be disqualified under the Representation of Peoples Act, 1951? Also mention the remedies available to such person against his disqualification.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Legislative disqualification under RPA · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Enumerate grounds; Explain remedies  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Disqualification of representatives  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpa-disqualification-grounds-remedies`

**Demand decoding:** Organise statutory grounds by section, distinguish disqualification from corrupt-practice proceedings, and identify the competent remedial forums and limits.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional setting → RPA grounds by category → remedies → due-process qualification.

### Model answer

Articles **102(1)(e)** and **191(1)(e)** authorise Parliament to prescribe disqualifications. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 provides:

1. **Conviction for offences (Section 8):** specified electoral, corruption, terrorism, social and economic offences; other offences attracting imprisonment of at least two years. In *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013), immediate disqualification of sitting members was affirmed by invalidating Section 8(4).
2. **Corrupt practices (Section 8A):** disqualification, up to six years, follows the President’s decision on the Election Commission’s opinion after a person is found guilty in an election proceeding.
3. **Dismissal for corruption or disloyalty (Section 9).**
4. **Government contracts (Section 9A).**
5. **Office under a government company (Section 10).**
6. **Failure to lodge election-expense accounts (Section 10A).**

**Remedies**

- Challenge conviction and sentence through appeal/revision; an appellate court’s **stay of conviction**, not merely bail or suspension of sentence, can avert the Section 8 consequence (*Navjot Singh Sidhu*).
- Challenge the election-court finding or election result through statutory appeal to the Supreme Court under Section **116A**, where applicable.
- Seek judicial review under Articles **226/32** against jurisdictional error, denial of natural justice or unconstitutional action.
- Under Section **11**, the Election Commission may remove or reduce certain disqualifications except those under Section 8A; Section 8A itself follows the President–EC statutory process.

**Conclusion:** The Act protects electoral integrity, but disqualification must follow the correct statutory ground, competent authority and fair hearing; judicial relief is ground-specific rather than an automatic stay.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives section-wise grounds and accurately distinguishes appellate, judicial-review and Election Commission remedies.

## Q12. “Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot be enlarged into absolute power.” In the light of this statement explain whether Parliament under Article 368 of the Constitution can destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution by expanding its amending power?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../topics/polity/amendment-and-basic-structure.md) → Limits on constituent power · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain constitutional limit  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional amendment → Basic-structure doctrine  
**Canonical owner:** [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/10_Amendment-and-Basic-Structure.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `article-368-limited-amending-power`

**Demand decoding:** Trace the doctrine, explain why constituent power cannot self-convert into unlimited power, and apply it to attempts to exclude judicial review.

**Compact framework:** Text and history → doctrine → attempted expansion → judicial answer → qualification.

### Model answer

Article **368** grants a wide constituent power: Parliament may amend any provision through the prescribed special procedure. But “amend” does not mean destroy the Constitution’s identity.

In *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*, amendments prevailed over fundamental-rights objections. *Golaknath* then denied power to amend Fundamental Rights. The controlling settlement came in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973): Parliament may amend every provision, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot damage the **basic structure**.

The **42nd Amendment** attempted to enlarge power by adding Article 368(4), barring judicial review of amendments, and Article 368(5), declaring no limitation on constituent power. In *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980), the Supreme Court invalidated both. Limited amending power and judicial review are themselves basic features; an authority created by the Constitution cannot, through that authority, become sovereign over the Constitution.

Thus amendments may recalibrate institutions but cannot abrogate constitutional supremacy, republican democracy, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review, separation of powers or the Fundamental Rights–Directive Principles balance. *I.R. Coelho* further subjected post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions to basic-structure review.

The doctrine does not freeze the Constitution: courts test the amendment’s **effect**, not whether it changes an important provision.

**Conclusion:** Parliament cannot expand Article 368 into an absolute, self-validating power; constitutional change remains permissible, constitutional destruction does not.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly answers the hypothetical through the exact amendment clauses and the decisive line of cases.

## Q13. “The reservation of seats for women in the institutions of local self-government has had a limited impact on the patriarchal character of the Indian Political Process.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Women and Gender Justice](../../topics/social-justice/women-and-gender-justice.md) → Women’s political representation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Gender justice → Political empowerment  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Panchayati Raj  
**Canonical owners:** [Women and Gender Justice](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/05_Women-and-Gender-Justice.md) · [Panchayati Raj](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/23_Panchayati-Raj.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `women-local-government-patriarchy`

**Demand decoding:** Assess both transformative gains and persistent patriarchal constraints, explain why descriptive representation is insufficient, and propose institutional deepening.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional change → demonstrated gains → limits → enabling reforms → verdict.

### Model answer

Articles **243D** and **243T**, inserted by the 73rd and 74th Amendments, reserve at least one-third of seats and chairperson offices for women; many States raised this to 50%. This transformed **descriptive representation** by bringing millions of women into public office.

The impact is substantial:

- women leaders have often prioritised drinking water, sanitation, health, education and violence-related concerns;
- repeated electoral exposure builds confidence, political skills and new role models;
- reservation has opened institutions to Dalit, Adivasi and backward-class women through intersecting quotas.

Yet its effect on the wider patriarchal political process is limited:

- “sarpanch pati” or proxy control, family-based candidate selection and bureaucratic disregard weaken autonomy;
- unpaid care, poor mobility, violence, low literacy, digital gaps and lack of campaign finance constrain participation;
- rotation can prevent constituency-building and parties rarely promote local women to State legislatures or Parliament;
- elite capture and inadequate devolution of **funds, functions and functionaries** reduce all representatives’ agency.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission favoured capacity and accountability reforms in local governance. Solutions include training and legal support, independent control of accounts, action against proxy functioning, safe meeting spaces, women’s collectives, stable devolution and internal party democracy.

**Conclusion:** Reservation has cracked, but not dismantled, patriarchy. It must be converted from numerical presence into substantive voice, resources, autonomy and upward political mobility.

**Why this earns marks:** It avoids the false choice between success and failure and assesses representation through outcomes, agency and institutional conditions.

## Q14. “The Attorney-General is the chief legal adviser and lawyer of the Government of India.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Attorney-General](../../topics/polity/attorney-general.md) → Constitutional role and limits · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2019 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional offices → Attorney-General for India  
**Canonical owner:** [Attorney-General and Advocate-General](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/33_Attorney-General-and-Advocate-General.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `attorney-general-role-independence`

**Demand decoding:** Explain appointment, duties, privileges and restrictions, then evaluate the tension between representing government and upholding constitutional legality.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional basis → advisory/litigation/parliamentary roles → privileges → limits → normative assessment.

### Model answer

Article **76** creates the Attorney-General for India (AGI). Appointed by the President, the appointee must be qualified to be a Supreme Court judge and holds office during the President’s pleasure.

**Chief legal adviser:** The AGI advises the Union Government on legal matters referred by the President and performs duties assigned under the Constitution or law. Under Article **143** references or major constitutional disputes, the office assists in articulating the Union’s legal position.

**Chief lawyer:** The AGI represents the Union before the Supreme Court and other courts when required and has a right of audience in all Indian courts. Article **88** permits participation in proceedings of both Houses and their committees, without a vote, enabling legal advice during legislation.

The office enjoys parliamentary privileges for such participation under Article **105(4)**. Unlike a full-time civil servant, the AGI may undertake private practice subject to restrictions: no advice against the Government of India, no defence of accused persons without permission, and no directorship without approval.

This hybrid design gives expertise and professional independence but creates risks of conflicts and dependence because tenure, remuneration and allocation of work are executive-controlled. The AGI’s client is the Government, yet advice must remain faithful to the Constitution, candour to courts and professional ethics—not partisan convenience. The office differs from the Union Law Minister, Solicitor-General and independent constitutional courts.

**Conclusion:** The description is accurate, but incomplete: the AGI is government’s principal adviser and advocate within a higher duty to constitutional legality and the administration of justice.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers the complete constitutional design and critically explains the office’s dual professional responsibility.

**Demand decoding:** Identify why individual legislative agency has declined, connect each cause to debate/output quality, recognise countervailing institutions and propose reforms.

**Compact framework:** Role → causes → consequences → qualifications → reforms.

### Model answer

Parliamentarians are expected to legislate, scrutinise the executive, represent constituencies and control public finance. Their individual law-making space has narrowed.

**Causes**

- The Tenth Schedule’s anti-defection whip, though aimed at stability, is often extended beyond confidence and money matters, suppressing independent judgement.
- The executive dominates the agenda, drafting and timing of Bills; private members’ business receives little effective time.
- Disruptions, shortened sittings and hurried passage reduce deliberation.
- Fewer Bills being referred to Department-related Standing Committees weakens specialised scrutiny.
- Party centralisation, limited research support and constituency/service burdens reduce legislative capacity.
- Ordinances and delegated legislation shift norm-making away from the floor.

The Dinesh Goswami Committee and Law Commission favoured limiting disqualification to votes affecting government survival. Parliament should ensure minimum sittings, mandatory pre-legislative consultation, routine committee scrutiny, recorded reasoned voting, stronger research staff and protected private members’ time.

**Conclusion:** Reviving the informed individual legislator—within responsible party government—is essential for deliberative, accountable and technically sound law-making.

**Why this earns marks:** It establishes a clear cause–effect chain and proposes reforms that preserve both party government and legislative autonomy.

### Model answer

Neo-liberal reform reduced direct central allocation and expanded markets, private participation and competitive federalism. - Subsidiarity assigns a function to the lowest competent level, reducing information and transaction costs.

National government should set rights and equalisation standards while local bodies choose context-sensitive means.

### Model answer

India seeks recognition as an autonomous pole with legitimate regional and global interests; the United States has often viewed partners through alliance roles serving its wider strategy. Friction arises when Washington expects alignment while offering less than equal strategic accommodation.

**Examples**

- During the Cold War, US alliances with Pakistan and the 1971 deployment of the *USS Enterprise* conflicted with India’s security concerns; India rejected bloc politics.
- After 2001, continuing US military support to Pakistan and its Afghanistan policy generated doubts about recognition of India’s regional stakes.
- The civil nuclear agreement acknowledged India’s exceptional nuclear status, yet denial of a permanent UNSC seat and incomplete access to global nuclear governance limited status satisfaction.
- US sanctions logic—earlier after the 1998 tests and later the CAATSA threat over India’s S-400 purchase—clashes with strategic autonomy and legacy Russian dependence.
- Trade disputes over market access, intellectual property, visas and withdrawal of GSP benefits show that strategic rhetoric does not erase economic divergence.
- US expectations on Iran can conflict with India’s energy, connectivity and independent foreign-policy choices.

Nevertheless, convergence on terrorism, China’s assertiveness, the Indo-Pacific, the Quad, defence trade and LEMOA has deepened. Differences also arise from domestic politics and unequal economic capacity, not merely status.

The relationship works best as a partnership, not an alliance: consultation without veto, technology sharing, reformed multilateralism and respect for issue-based alignment.

**Conclusion:** Durable ties require the US to treat India as a co-shaper rather than a subordinate balancer, while India translates autonomy into predictable burden-sharing.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the core status–autonomy mismatch through historical and contemporary examples while acknowledging substantial strategic convergence.
