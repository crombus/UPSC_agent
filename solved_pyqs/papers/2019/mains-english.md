### Q1(a). “Indian universities are Indian in their geographical locations only”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Indian higher education · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Argumentative essay → institutional purpose and intellectual autonomy · `qualifying-english/essay-indian-universities.md`

**Model essay**

A university situated in India is not necessarily Indian in intellectual character. The provocative claim that Indian universities are Indian only in their geographical location points to inherited curricula, dependence on foreign theories, weak engagement with local society and an educational culture that often rewards imitation. The charge contains truth, but it should lead to renewal rather than cultural isolation.

Modern Indian universities grew substantially under colonial rule. Their structures, disciplines and examination systems were shaped by European models. After independence, many institutions retained syllabi that treated Western experience as universal while presenting Indian knowledge as a specialised option. Students could learn political theory without seriously examining Indian debates on statecraft, or study economics without understanding informal work, village institutions and regional inequality.

Language deepens this distance. English gives access to global scholarship and connects a multilingual country, but exclusive dependence on it can separate universities from communities. A capable student may know the vocabulary of a foreign textbook yet be unable to explain the same idea in a local language or apply it to a nearby problem. Indian-language scholarship, translation and technical terminology therefore deserve sustained support.

The criticism also concerns research priorities. Universities sometimes reward topics fashionable abroad because international publication brings prestige. Yet India offers urgent questions in public health, agriculture, water, urbanisation, social discrimination, law, ecology and technology. Research becomes genuinely Indian when it asks rigorous questions arising from Indian realities, not when it merely praises the past.

Indianisation must not mean rejecting knowledge because it originated elsewhere. Algebra, constitutionalism, modern medicine and scientific method belong to humanity. Universities flourish through comparison, criticism and exchange. Replacing one orthodoxy with another, suppressing inconvenient evidence or demanding ideological loyalty would destroy the freedom essential to learning. An Indian university must be intellectually autonomous enough to study both indigenous traditions and foreign ideas critically.

The country’s own intellectual inheritance is diverse. Buddhist logic, classical grammar, mathematics, medicine, literature, Islamic scholarship, Bhakti and Sufi traditions, anti-caste thought, tribal ecological knowledge and the modern social reform movements all deserve serious study. They should be approached through evidence, translation and debate rather than ceremonial celebration. Students must encounter both achievement and exclusion, continuity and conflict.

Institutional culture matters equally. A university cannot claim an Indian character while remaining inaccessible to poor, rural, marginalised or disabled students. Scholarships, hostels, libraries and mentoring widen the social base of knowledge. Campuses should permit disagreement without violence and train students to listen across linguistic, regional and religious differences.

Evaluation must also change. An examination system dominated by recall encourages coaching and discourages independent inquiry. Students should learn to frame questions, test sources, conduct fieldwork and communicate findings clearly. Assessment through research, laboratories, projects and reasoned argument would make education less mechanical while preserving transparent academic standards.

Finally, universities should serve society without becoming departments of government or industry. Extension work, legal clinics, public-health projects, local archives and technology partnerships can connect scholarship with citizens. At the same time, basic research and unfashionable inquiry must be protected, because their value may emerge only later.

Indian universities need not choose between the local and the global. Their task is to interpret universal knowledge through Indian questions and carry Indian experience into global debate. They become truly Indian not through location, slogans or exclusion, but through intellectual freedom, social inclusion, multilingual scholarship and responsible engagement with the civilisation and people around them.

**Why this earns marks:** It tests the proposition, identifies colonial and institutional continuities, and offers a balanced model of critical, inclusive Indianisation.

### Q1(b). “Journalism has been rendered into sensationalism”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Media ethics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Argumentative essay → public information and media incentives · `qualifying-english/essay-journalism-sensationalism.md`

**Model essay**

Journalism exists to discover, verify and communicate matters of public importance. Sensationalism seeks attention by exaggerating conflict, emotion or novelty. The complaint that journalism has been rendered into sensationalism reflects a real crisis: in a crowded media market, dramatic presentation can displace patient reporting. Yet the decline is neither universal nor irreversible.

Competition for audiences creates the first pressure. Newspapers, television channels and digital platforms depend on circulation, ratings, subscriptions or advertising. A complex investigation may take months, whereas an alarming headline can attract immediate attention. Crime, celebrity dispute and political confrontation are therefore repeatedly displayed even when issues such as health, education, labour or local government affect more lives.

The twenty-four-hour news cycle intensifies this tendency. Channels must fill time before facts are known. Speculation is presented as breaking news; panels replace reporting; accusation precedes verification. Visual drama may turn disaster into spectacle and private grief into consumable content. Once an error spreads across platforms, a later correction rarely travels as far.

Digital media have accelerated both access and distortion. Anyone can record events, expose abuse and challenge powerful institutions. At the same time, algorithms reward material that provokes fear, anger or surprise. Misleading thumbnails, clipped videos and false urgency flourish because users share before checking. Established news organisations may imitate these methods to remain visible.

Sensationalism damages democracy. Citizens cannot make reasoned choices when every disagreement is portrayed as a national crisis. Trial by media can prejudice reputations and due process. Communal or regional tensions may worsen when unverified rumours are repeated. Constant alarm also creates fatigue: audiences eventually distrust even accurate warnings.

However, journalism cannot be reduced to its worst examples. Reporters continue to investigate corruption, explain court decisions, cover remote conflicts and document the lives of citizens ignored by power. During disasters, accurate reporting can direct relief and correct official failure. Independent local journalism often performs these duties with limited resources and considerable risk.

Reform must protect freedom rather than invite censorship. Government control can silence legitimate scrutiny under the pretext of preventing sensationalism. The stronger remedies are editorial independence, transparent ownership, separation of news from advertising, disclosure of conflicts, careful correction policies and professional protection for reporters who resist commercial pressure.

Media literacy is equally necessary. Audiences should learn to distinguish a report from opinion, identify anonymous or unsupported claims and consult more than one source. Platforms should reduce financial rewards for demonstrably deceptive content while making their moderation rules transparent. Public-service and subscription-supported models can also reduce dependence on immediate clicks.

The daily news agenda also needs proportion. A responsible newsroom follows a policy after its announcement, reports whether public money produced results and gives affected citizens space to speak. Such continuity is less dramatic than a studio quarrel, but it converts isolated events into public understanding and holds authority accountable over time.

Speed and vivid storytelling are not themselves wrong. A compelling report can bring neglected suffering to public attention. The ethical boundary is crossed when drama distorts proportion, evidence or dignity. Journalism regains trust when verification comes before velocity and public relevance before spectacle. Sensationalism may currently dominate many screens, but journalism survives wherever facts are checked, power is questioned and citizens are treated as participants in democracy rather than targets for excitement.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects sensationalism to commercial, technological and professional incentives while defending press freedom and practical reform.

### Q1(c). “Information is not knowledge”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Information literacy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → facts, understanding and judgment · `qualifying-english/essay-information-not-knowledge.md`

**Model essay**

The digital age has placed more information within reach than any earlier generation possessed. A search can produce thousands of facts in seconds. Yet access does not guarantee understanding. Information consists of data, statements or signals; knowledge arises when they are verified, organised, interpreted and connected with experience. The distinction is essential for education, citizenship and wise action.

A list of symptoms is information; knowing how they relate, which are significant and when professional help is required approaches knowledge. A table of rainfall figures provides information; understanding patterns of drought, soil and cultivation turns it into useful knowledge. Context gives facts meaning.

Verification is equally important. Information may be incomplete, outdated or false. Repetition does not make a claim reliable. Knowledge requires asking who produced the material, for what purpose, by what method and with what evidence. It remains open to revision when better evidence appears. Mere confidence, especially when amplified by social media, is not knowledge.

Education often confuses memory with mastery. A student may reproduce definitions yet fail to apply them to a new problem. Genuine learning connects ideas, compares explanations and recognises exceptions. Teachers should therefore cultivate questioning, experiment, discussion and writing rather than reward only accumulation.

Technology illustrates the paradox. Search engines and artificial intelligence can retrieve or generate material rapidly, but users must judge relevance and accuracy. An abundance of unfiltered material can create confusion rather than clarity. The person who knows what to ignore, what to test and what remains uncertain possesses a skill more valuable than simple recall.

Knowledge also differs from wisdom. A person may know how to manipulate markets, weapons or personal data without judging whether such action is just. Wisdom joins knowledge with ethical purpose, foresight and humility. Thus the movement from information to good action requires not one but several transformations: data into understanding, understanding into judgment, and judgment into responsibility.

The distinction has democratic consequences. Citizens encounter statistics, slogans, videos and opinion polls daily. Without knowledge of institutions and historical context, isolated facts can be arranged to support prejudice. Public debate improves when people compare sources, distinguish evidence from assertion and accept that complex issues rarely have instant solutions.

Experience contributes another dimension. Manuals can inform a farmer about crops, but knowledge also develops through seasons, local soils and observed risk. A doctor combines scientific literature with clinical judgment; a craftsperson unites instruction with practice. Experience alone may preserve error, while theory alone may ignore reality. Knowledge grows through their conversation.

Institutions should therefore organise information rather than merely distribute it. Libraries, universities, newsrooms and public agencies must preserve reliable records, explain methods and make evidence intelligible. Individuals must cultivate attention in an economy designed to fragment it.

Humility is a final test of knowledge. The better a person understands a difficult subject, the more clearly that person can identify uncertainty, competing explanations and the limits of available evidence. Information often produces quick certainty; knowledge teaches where certainty is justified and where judgment must remain provisional.

Information is indispensable raw material, but it becomes knowledge only through disciplined thought. The informed person possesses facts; the knowledgeable person understands relations, causes, limits and consequences. In an age of abundance, the central educational task is not simply to obtain more information, but to learn how to transform it into truthful understanding and humane judgment.

**Why this earns marks:** It clearly distinguishes information, knowledge and wisdom and applies the distinction to education, technology, democracy and experience.

### Q1(d). “Even a layman can transform a society”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Civic agency · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → ordinary citizenship and collective change · `qualifying-english/essay-layman-transform-society.md`

**Model essay**

Societies are often described as products of rulers, experts and famous reformers. Yet institutions ultimately rest on the habits and cooperation of ordinary people. A “layman” may lack specialised training or official authority, but can recognise injustice, organise neighbours, model a better practice and compel institutions to respond. Social transformation frequently begins with such ordinary agency.

An individual’s first power is example. A resident who segregates waste, challenges discrimination, teaches a child or refuses a bribe may appear insignificant. Repeated consistently, however, such conduct changes expectations. Others discover that an alternative is possible. Custom, which often sustains injustice, can also spread reform.

Ordinary people possess local knowledge that distant experts may lack. A villager knows which well dries first, a commuter knows where harassment occurs, and a patient knows how a public clinic actually functions. When citizens document these experiences and present them collectively, policy becomes more responsive. Expertise remains valuable, but it improves when joined with lived knowledge.

Organisation multiplies individual influence. Self-help groups, resident associations, cooperatives, unions and volunteer networks convert scattered concern into durable action. A person need not be a celebrated leader to call a meeting, maintain records or persuade others. Small acts of coordination can improve sanitation, protect common resources and secure public services.

Democratic institutions create further opportunities. Voting is only the beginning. Citizens can attend local meetings, seek information, submit grievances, monitor expenditure, assist vulnerable neighbours and participate in public consultation. Courts, legislatures and administrations are more likely to act when a persistent public demand is supported by evidence.

Technology has widened reach. A phone can record abuse, connect volunteers or raise resources during an emergency. Yet digital visibility is not the same as transformation. Outrage that lasts a day may change little. Reliable facts, patient organisation and follow-up are necessary if attention is to become institutional reform.

The proposition must not romanticise individual action. Structural inequality, violence and lack of resources can silence even courageous citizens. Governments cannot transfer their duties to volunteers, and complicated problems require trained professionals. An ordinary person should not replace a doctor, engineer or judge. The layperson’s role is to initiate, participate, scrutinise and help direct expertise toward public need.

History shows that major movements depend on unnamed participants. Reformers may articulate a cause, but change occurs when ordinary people adopt new practices, endure costs and sustain organisations. The worker who joins a peaceful protest, the parent who insists that a daughter remain in school, and the witness who reports wrongdoing all alter the social environment in which authority operates.

Ordinary agency is strongest when it is constitutional and inclusive. A reform that humiliates opponents or excludes a minority may replace one injustice with another. Listening, non-violence, accurate evidence and willingness to negotiate allow citizens to build legitimacy rather than merely display anger. Means shape the society that a movement finally creates.

Transformation need not mean instant revolution. It may consist of a neighbourhood becoming safer, a discriminatory custom losing acceptance or a neglected right becoming enforceable. Such changes accumulate. The ordinary citizen’s limitations are real, but so is the power of moral example joined with collective action. A layman can transform society because society is not an object controlled only from above; it is a continuing pattern made and remade by the choices of its members.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains mechanisms of ordinary agency, acknowledges structural limits and distinguishes civic participation from amateur substitution for expertise.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Official wording:** “Make a précis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give a title to it. The précis should be written in your own language.”  
**Marks:** 75  
**Source length:** 882 words · **Target:** about 294 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Untitled one-third précis · `qualifying-english/precis-writing.md`

**Compression logic:** Retain the passage’s governing sequence—Gandhi’s civilisational nationalism, critique of modernity, constructive alternative, satyagraha, selective use of modern means, political inheritance and coalition-building—while removing quotations, repetition and subsidiary detail.

**Model précis — 284 words, deliberately untitled**

In *Hind Swaraj*, Gandhi grounded Indian nationhood in an ancient civilisation whose assimilative and moral power had long united diverse peoples. He contrasted it with modern Western civilisation, especially industrial capitalism, which separated economics from morality, generated conflict and supported imperial aggression. Indians, by adopting capitalism and its legal and political institutions, had helped create their own subjection. Gandhi therefore urged restraint in consumption and a return to a village-based, self-sufficient economy.

He also distrusted parliamentary democracy because party interests and discipline prevented representatives from expressing either the people’s general will or their own moral autonomy. Independence could not merely reproduce British government without British rulers. True swaraj meant individual self-control and required an Indian alternative to Western liberal institutions.

Gandhi pursued this demanding ideal through satyagraha, or truth-force. It went beyond ordinary civil disobedience: protesters were to use superior moral strength to transform the oppressor. Non-violence was its unconditional principle.

Nevertheless, Gandhi did not reject every instrument of modernity. *Hind Swaraj* itself used the modern form of a dialogue between reader and editor. He effectively employed newspapers and railways to communicate ideas and organise campaigns. His deeper purpose was to challenge the Raj’s claim that Western civilisation was morally superior.

Nor did Gandhi introduce mass politics without preparation. Tilak and Annie Besant’s Home Rule Leagues had created favourable ground, though they could not revive the Congress fully. Gandhi succeeded by occupying a centrist position: he combined the moderates’ goal of swaraj with methods resembling extremist resistance, while non-violence reassured moderates and property owners. By supporting the anti-British aspect of the Khilafat cause and minimising its pan-Islamic tendency, he also allied with younger Muslim leaders and temporarily united Hindus and Muslims against British rule.

**Why this earns marks:** It is untitled as directed, stays near one-third of 882 words, preserves the argument’s order and uses independent wording.
