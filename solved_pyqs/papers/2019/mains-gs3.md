## Q1. Taxes subsumed in GST and revenue implications
**Primary route:** Economy → [Taxation, GST, Finance Commission and Fiscal Federalism](../../topics/economy/taxation-gst-finance-commission-and-fiscal-federalism.md) → GST design and revenue · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 1  
**Official question:** Enumerate the indirect taxes which have been subsumed in the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in India. Also, comment on the revenue implications of the GST introduced in India since July 2017.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Taxation → GST and Revenue  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/taxation-gst-finance-commission-and-fiscal-federalism`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Enumerate + Comment  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/10_Taxation-GST-Finance-Commission-and-Fiscal-Federalism.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/10_Taxation-GST-Finance-Commission-and-Fiscal-Federalism.md)

### Demand decoding
List the principal Union and State levies absorbed into GST, then assess—not merely describe—its revenue effects during its first two years.

### Compact framework
`multiple origin-based levies → dual destination-based GST + input credit → wider base, but transition losses and compensation pressure`

### Model answer
GST subsumed Union **central excise duty**, additional excise duties, service tax, additional customs duties (CVD and SAD), and related cesses/surcharges. State levies absorbed included VAT/sales tax, Central Sales Tax, purchase tax, luxury tax, entry tax, entertainment tax except that imposed by local bodies, taxes on advertisements, lotteries, betting and gambling, and related cesses/surcharges.

Revenue effects since July 2017 were mixed. A common destination-based tax and input-tax credit reduced cascading, formalised supply chains through invoice matching and widened the base. Consuming States gained against producing States. However, rate changes, filing complexity, refund delays, fake invoices and the initial transition depressed buoyancy. Collections often fell below budget expectations, while States were protected by the Compensation Act’s 14% annual growth guarantee, increasing pressure on the compensation cess.

Thus GST created a stronger long-run tax architecture, but its early revenue promise depended on simpler rates, compliance and settlement.

### Examiner rationale
The answer enumerates both tax groups and gives a balanced, time-specific revenue verdict supported by the credit, destination and compensation mechanisms.

## Q2. GDP growth, inflation and the economy's condition
**Primary route:** Economy → [Growth, Development, HDI, IHDI and MPI](../../topics/economy/growth-development-hdi-ihdi-and-mpi.md) → Quality of macroeconomic performance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 2  
**Official question:** Do you agree with the view that steady GDP growth and low inflation have left the Indian economy in good shape? Give reasons in support of your arguments.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Macroeconomy → Growth Quality and Inflation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/growth-development-hdi-ihdi-and-mpi`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Evaluate a proposition  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/03_Inflation-Price-Indices-and-Business-Cycles.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/03_Inflation-Price-Indices-and-Business-Cycles.md)

### Demand decoding
Take a qualified position and test headline growth and inflation against employment, investment, demand, finance and distribution as they stood in 2019.

### Compact framework
`stable prices + past growth = macro cushion; slowing output + jobs/investment stress = not wholly “good shape”`

### Model answer
The proposition is only partly correct. Moderate inflation after flexible inflation targeting improved purchasing-power stability and credibility, while India remained among faster-growing large economies.

Yet headline indicators concealed weakening fundamentals by 2019:

- real GDP growth fell to **6.8% in 2018–19**, with the January–March quarter at 5.8%;
- the PLFS reported **6.1% unemployment in 2017–18**, alongside low female participation;
- private investment and exports remained subdued, while stressed bank balance sheets and the IL&FS-triggered NBFC liquidity shock constrained credit;
- weak rural wages, farm distress and slowing consumption indicated demand stress; and
- low food inflation benefited consumers but could compress cultivators’ returns.

GDP also does not measure job quality, inequality, health, education or ecological depletion. Low inflation caused by weak demand is not supply-side strength.

India therefore had useful macroeconomic stability, but not an economy uniformly in good shape; employment-rich investment and financial repair were essential.

### Examiner rationale
The response rejects a headline-only inference using contemporaneous growth, labour, finance and rural-demand evidence, while acknowledging the genuine benefit of price stability.

## Q3. Integrated Farming System and sustainable production
**Primary route:** Economy → [Irrigation, Inputs, Credit, Insurance and Sustainable Agriculture](../../topics/economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture.md) → Integrated farming · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 3  
**Official question:** How far is Integrated Farming System (IFS) helpful in sustaining agricultural production?  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Integrated Farming Systems  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Assess extent  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md) · Secondary: [`Economy/basic/30_Economics-of-Animal-Rearing-Livestock-Dairy-Poultry-and-Fisheries.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/30_Economics-of-Animal-Rearing-Livestock-Dairy-Poultry-and-Fisheries.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain the crop–livestock–allied-enterprise mechanism and give a graded verdict on productivity, income, resilience and constraints.

### Compact framework
`diverse enterprises + waste recycling + year-round outputs → lower risk, higher resource efficiency and durable production`

### Model answer
Integrated Farming System combines crops with complementary enterprises such as dairy, poultry, fisheries, horticulture, agroforestry, beekeeping or biogas on one farm.

It sustains production because:

- crop residues feed animals, while dung, pond silt and compost restore soil nutrients;
- diversified outputs spread climatic, pest and price risks beyond one crop;
- livestock and horticulture generate regular income and year-round family employment;
- mixed canopies, rotations and biological inputs improve soil, water and biodiversity;
- farm ponds and fish culture increase water productivity; and
- household nutrition becomes more diverse.

ICAR models show that integration can raise returns per unit of land and recycle wastes, especially for smallholders. However, benefits are not automatic: enterprises require skills, veterinary services, capital, reliable markets, cold chains and suitable combinations. Water-intensive or poorly managed livestock units may merely shift environmental pressure.

IFS is therefore highly useful when designed agro-climatically and supported by institutions, not as a uniform package.

### Examiner rationale
The answer explains the circular resource mechanism, covers resilience and livelihoods, and qualifies the extent of benefit with implementation conditions.

## Q4. National Watershed Project in water-stressed areas
**Primary route:** Economy → [Irrigation, Inputs, Credit, Insurance and Sustainable Agriculture](../../topics/economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture.md) → Watershed development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 4  
**Official question:** Elaborate the impact of National Watershed Project in increasing agricultural production from water-stressed areas.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Watershed Management  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Elaborate impact and mechanism  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md)

### Demand decoding
Identify the programme context and trace how ridge-to-valley treatment changes water, soil, crops and livelihoods, while noting outcome conditions.

### Compact framework
`ridge-to-valley treatment → slower runoff + recharge + soil conservation → assured moisture, diversification and higher cropping intensity`

### Model answer
The World Bank-assisted **Neeranchal National Watershed Project** strengthened PMKSY’s Watershed Development Component through scientific planning, capacity-building, monitoring and livelihood support in rain-fed areas.

Its production impact operates through:

- ridge-to-valley works—contour trenches, bunds and check dams—that reduce runoff and soil erosion;
- farm ponds and recharge structures that improve protective irrigation and groundwater availability;
- vegetative cover, pasture and afforestation that rebuild biomass and soil moisture;
- water budgeting and better crop practices that raise water-use efficiency;
- diversification towards pulses, oilseeds, horticulture and livestock; and
- community institutions and livelihood activities that maintain assets and reduce vulnerability.

These measures can increase cropped area, cropping intensity, yields and drought resilience while lowering siltation downstream. Their impact, however, depends on treatment of the whole hydrological unit, equitable benefit-sharing, maintenance, reliable extension and restraint on post-recharge groundwater extraction.

Thus watershed projects raise production most durably when physical works are joined to participatory aquifer and land-use governance.

### Examiner rationale
The response connects each watershed intervention to a production outcome and avoids equating construction expenditure with durable water security.

## Q5. Visvesvaraya and Swaminathan
**Primary route:** Economy → [Land Reforms, Green Revolution and Cropping Systems](../../topics/economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems.md) → Agricultural transformation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 5  
**Official question:** How was India benefitted from the contributions of Sir M. Visvesvaraya and Dr. M. S. Swaminathan in the fields of water engineering and agricultural science respectively?  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture and Infrastructure → Scientific Contributions  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Explain contributions and benefits  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md)

### Demand decoding
Treat each figure separately, name concrete innovations or institutions, and connect them to India's development rather than writing biographies.

### Compact framework
`Visvesvaraya: engineered water control and planning; Swaminathan: science-policy package for food security`

### Model answer
**Sir M. Visvesvaraya** advanced water engineering through patented automatic sluice gates, first used at Khadakvasla and later adapted elsewhere. As Mysore’s Dewan, he promoted the Krishna Raja Sagara project, irrigation, flood protection, sanitation and planned industrialisation. India gained improved storage regulation, irrigated agriculture, urban water management and an engineering-led development ethos.

**Dr M. S. Swaminathan** combined genetics, breeding and public policy. Working with Indian scientists and Norman Borlaug, he helped adapt high-yielding dwarf wheat to Indian conditions and championed the seed–water–fertiliser–credit–procurement package underlying the Green Revolution. This raised food-grain output, reduced import dependence and strengthened national food security. He later advocated an **“evergreen revolution”**—productivity growth without ecological harm—and farmer-centred conservation of biodiversity.

Their legacies are complementary: Visvesvaraya expanded controlled water and infrastructure capacity; Swaminathan converted scientific crop improvement and institutions into food security. Both also show that technology succeeds when embedded in public systems.

### Examiner rationale
The answer uses named contributions and explicitly explains their national benefits while avoiding hagiography and acknowledging institutional context.

### Compact framework
`Gaganyaan capability → small indigenous orbital platform → sustained microgravity science and human-spaceflight autonomy`

### Model answer
In June 2019, ISRO announced plans for a small indigenous space station after the **Gaganyaan** programme. ## Q7. Coastal sand mining
**Primary route:** Environment and Ecology → [Coastal and Marine Ecology, CRZ and Blue Economy](../../topics/environment/coastal-and-marine-ecology-crz-blue-economy.md) → Sand mining impacts · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 7  
**Official question:** Coastal sand mining, whether legal or illegal, poses one of the biggest threats to our environment. Analyze the impact of sand mining along the Indian coasts, citing specific examples.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Environment and Ecology → Coasts → Sand Mining  
**Proposed topic slug:** `environment/coastal-and-marine-ecology-crz-blue-economy`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Analyse with examples  
**Canonical owner:** [`Environment-and-Ecology/basic/24_Coastal-and-Marine-Ecology-CRZ-Blue-Economy.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Environment-and-Ecology/basic/24_Coastal-and-Marine-Ecology-CRZ-Blue-Economy.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain coastal sediment-budget mechanisms, cover ecological and livelihood consequences, and embed Indian examples.

### Compact framework
`sand removal > natural replenishment → sediment deficit → erosion, habitat loss, salinity and livelihood damage`

### Model answer
Beaches and dunes are dynamic sediment reserves that absorb wave energy. Mining removes material faster than rivers and littoral drift can replace it, creating a negative sediment budget.

Consequences include:

- beach erosion, shoreline retreat and greater storm-surge exposure;
- destabilised river mouths and altered currents, transferring erosion downdrift;
- saline intrusion into coastal aquifers and soils;
- destruction of turtle nesting grounds, benthic organisms, mangroves and fisheries;
- damage to houses, roads, tourism and fisher access; and
- heavy-mineral mining also risks radioactive or chemical contamination from mishandled tailings.

At **Alappad, Kerala**, mineral-sand extraction became associated with land loss and protests. Illegal beach-mineral mining on **Tamil Nadu’s southern coast** raised concerns over dune removal and regulatory capture. Extraction from coastal rivers in Goa and Karnataka can starve beaches of replenishment.

Even licensed mining is harmful if cumulative sediment budgets are ignored. CRZ enforcement, replenishment studies, satellite monitoring and alternative construction material are therefore essential.

### Examiner rationale
The answer explains why mining causes harm, spans ecological and human impacts, and uses geographically specific Indian evidence.

## Q8. Disaster vulnerability and its types
**Primary route:** Disaster Management → [Disaster Risk, Resilience and Sendai Framework](../../topics/disaster-management/disaster-risk-resilience-and-sendai-framework.md) → Vulnerability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 8  
**Official question:** Vulnerability is an essential element for defining disaster impacts and its threat to people. How and in what ways can vulnerability to disasters be characterized? Discuss different types of vulnerability with reference to disasters.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Disaster Management → Risk Concepts → Vulnerability  
**Proposed topic slug:** `disaster-management/disaster-risk-resilience-and-sendai-framework`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Characterise + Classify  
**Canonical owner:** [`Disaster-Management/basic/01_Concepts-Risk-Resilience-and-Sendai.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Disaster-Management/basic/01_Concepts-Risk-Resilience-and-Sendai.md)

### Demand decoding
Define vulnerability as susceptibility and limited coping capacity, then distinguish its interacting dimensions with disaster examples.

### Compact framework
`hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity = disaster risk; vulnerability is multidimensional and dynamic`

### Model answer
Vulnerability is the set of physical, social, economic, environmental and institutional conditions that make exposed people or assets susceptible to harm and limit their capacity to anticipate, cope and recover. It is dynamic, place-specific and unequal; the same cyclone therefore produces different losses.

Its principal types are:

- **Physical:** unsafe buildings, dense settlements or infrastructure in floodplains and unstable slopes.
- **Social:** age, disability, gender exclusion, poor health, weak networks or low risk literacy; for example, inaccessible warnings.
- **Economic:** insecure livelihoods, absent savings or insurance, and dependence on climate-sensitive farming.
- **Environmental:** degraded mangroves, wetlands, forests or dunes that otherwise buffer hazards.
- **Institutional/political:** weak building-code enforcement, unplanned urbanisation, poor last-mile warning and exclusion from decisions.

These dimensions interact: a landless household in a cyclone-prone informal settlement is exposed physically, economically and socially. Vulnerability assessment must therefore combine hazard maps with disaggregated livelihood, demographic, ecosystem and governance data, followed by targeted capacity-building.

### Examiner rationale
The response distinguishes vulnerability from hazard and exposure, provides a complete typology and demonstrates interaction rather than offering isolated labels.

**Official question:** The banning of “Jamaat-e-Islami” in Jammu and Kashmir brought into focus the role of over-ground workers (OGWs) in assisting terrorist organizations. Examine the role played by OGWs in assisting terrorist organizations in insurgency affected areas. Discuss measures to neutralize influence of OGWs.  
**Marks / limit:** 10 / 150 words  
**Classification:** Internal Security → Terrorism → Over-ground Worker Networks  
**Proposed topic slug:** `internal-security/jammu-kashmir-and-cross-border-terrorism`  
**Difficulty / demand:** High · Examine + Prescribe  
**Canonical owner:** [`Internal-Security/basic/05_Jammu-Kashmir-and-Cross-Border-Terrorism.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Internal-Security/basic/05_Jammu-Kashmir-and-Cross-Border-Terrorism.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain the enabling functions of covert civilian networks and propose intelligence, legal, financial and legitimacy-based measures with safeguards.

### Compact framework
`OGWs connect clandestine cadres to society → logistics, intelligence, finance and recruitment; isolate networks without alienating civilians`

### Model answer
OGWs are covert facilitators sustaining militants without necessarily carrying weapons. They may provide shelter, transport, food, SIM cards and medical aid; conduct reconnaissance; move arms and money; identify targets; spread propaganda; recruit or radicalise youth; organise stone-pelting or intimidation; and warn cadres about security operations. - intelligence-led mapping of recruiters, financiers, couriers and safe houses, with inter-agency databases;

- witness protection and security for local representatives against coercion;
- prosecution based on admissible evidence and periodic review, not indiscriminate detention;
- surrender, counselling, education and livelihood pathways for peripheral or coerced participants; and
- credible grievance redress, accountable policing and community communication to reduce recruitment.

The aim should be to separate committed facilitators from frightened or misled civilians. Over-broad labelling can generate the alienation on which insurgent networks depend.

The answer identifies concrete OGW functions and balances hard disruption with evidentiary safeguards, rehabilitation and legitimacy.

## Q11. Inclusive growth, inclusiveness and sustainability
**Primary route:** Economy → [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) → Sustainable inclusion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 11  
**Official question:** It is argued that the strategy of inclusive growth is intended to meet the objectives of inclusiveness and sustainability together. Comment on this statement.  
**Marks / limit:** 15 / 250 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Inclusive Growth → Inclusion and Sustainability  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth`  
**Difficulty / demand:** High · Analytical comment  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/23_Poverty-Inequality-Social-Sector-and-Inclusive-Growth.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/23_Poverty-Inequality-Social-Sector-and-Inclusive-Growth.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain why inclusion and sustainability can reinforce each other, identify tensions and conditions, and deliver a qualified judgement.

### Compact framework
`broad capabilities and participation + resource-efficient production + fair transition → durable, shared prosperity`

### Model answer
Inclusive growth is not merely faster GDP with ex-post welfare. It expands productive opportunities, capabilities and voice across classes, genders, regions and generations. Properly designed, it can unite **inclusiveness** with **sustainability**.

**Why the objectives reinforce each other**

- Public investment in health, education, nutrition and skills raises productivity while reducing inherited inequality.
- Financial inclusion, infrastructure and secure rights enable small farmers, workers and enterprises to participate in markets.
- Renewable energy, public transport, efficient irrigation and climate-resilient agriculture reduce resource intensity while widening access.
- MGNREGA water-conservation assets can combine wage security with ecological restoration.
- Clean cooking and sanitation improve health, reduce unpaid labour and lower local environmental damage.
- Participation of gram sabhas, women and forest-dependent communities improves legitimacy and stewardship of commons.

However, neither objective follows automatically. Growth based on mining displacement, groundwater depletion, pollution or precarious work may raise output while excluding people and transferring costs to future generations. Conversely, conservation that removes livelihood access without compensation is socially unsustainable. Green technologies can also displace workers or remain unaffordable.

Policy must therefore apply polluter-pays and precautionary principles, price resources while protecting basic needs, invest in universal services, recognise community rights, and provide reskilling and social protection for a **just transition**. Outcomes should be judged through jobs, distribution, health and natural-capital indicators, not GDP alone.

Inclusive growth can meet both goals, but only when distribution and ecological limits shape the growth process itself rather than being later corrections.

### Examiner rationale
The answer explains complementarities through named Indian mechanisms, exposes real trade-offs and concludes conditionally rather than accepting the proposition as automatic.

Post-1991 liberalisation reduced direct state production but expanded government responsibilities for infrastructure, human development, regulation and safety nets. Public expenditure management therefore requires reconciling scarce fiscal space with complex outcomes.

**Major challenges**

- Interest, salaries, pensions, subsidies and defence create downward rigidity, compressing capital expenditure.
- Populist or poorly targeted subsidies can crowd out health, education and maintenance.
- FRBM deficit targets may induce pro-cyclical cuts in productive capital spending or creative off-budget borrowing.
- Optimistic revenue assumptions, supplementary demands and year-end spending weaken budget credibility.
- Centrally sponsored schemes cross Union–State–local chains; delayed releases, low local capacity and fragmented schemes impede last-mile results.
- Input-based line-item control rewards spending, not outcomes; poor project appraisal creates delays and cost overruns.
- Public enterprises, guarantees and extra-budgetary resources can obscure contingent liabilities.

Reforms include the FRBM framework, Medium-Term Expenditure Framework, merger of Plan and non-Plan classification, advancement of the Budget, outcome budgets, Public Financial Management System, Treasury Single Account-type controls, DBT and Government e-Marketplace. Yet digital tracking cannot replace realistic costing, parliamentary scrutiny and independent evaluation.

Sound management means not merely spending less, but converting each rupee into equitable and durable public value.

The answer clarifies the post-liberalisation paradox, links institutional problems to budget outcomes and evaluates concrete reforms with appropriate limits.

## Q15. Protecting traditional medicinal knowledge from patents
**Primary route:** Science and Technology → [Intellectual Property Rights and Patents](../../topics/science-and-technology/intellectual-property-rights-and-patents.md) → Traditional knowledge protection · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 15  
**Official question:** How is the Government of India protecting traditional knowledge of medicine from patenting by pharmaceutical companies?  
**Marks / limit:** 15 / 250 words  
**Classification:** Science and Technology → IPR → Traditional Knowledge  
**Proposed topic slug:** `science-and-technology/intellectual-property-rights-and-patents`  
**Difficulty / demand:** High · Explain legal and institutional mechanisms  
**Canonical owner:** [`Science-and-Technology/basic/17_Intellectual-Property-Rights-and-Patents.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Science-and-Technology/basic/17_Intellectual-Property-Rights-and-Patents.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain how India prevents wrongful patents through documented prior art, patent-law exclusions, opposition and biodiversity/access rules; distinguish defensive from positive protection.

### Compact framework
`codify prior art + examiner access + patent exclusions/opposition + biodiversity consent and benefit sharing`

### Model answer
India’s strategy responds to “biopiracy” episodes such as foreign patents concerning turmeric wound healing and neem. It combines prior-art documentation with patent and biodiversity law.

**Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL):**

- CSIR and the Ministry of AYUSH converted codified knowledge from Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Yoga and related texts into searchable patent-compatible classifications.
- Translation into languages used by major patent offices enables examiners to find prior art that was previously inaccessible.

- Section **3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970** excludes inventions that are traditional knowledge or mere aggregation/duplication of known properties.

Patent examination, pre- and post-grant opposition and international advocacy at WIPO complement these protections.

The answer names the principal institutions and statutory mechanisms, explains how each defeats wrongful novelty claims, and distinguishes patent prevention from positive community rights.

## Q17. Ecosystem carrying capacity and regional planning
**Primary route:** Environment and Ecology → [Ecosystem Structure and Function](../../topics/environment/ecosystem-structure-and-function.md) → Carrying capacity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2019 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 17  
**Official question:** Define the concept of carrying capacity of an ecosystem as relevant to an environment. Explain how understanding this concept is vital while planning for sustainable development of a region.  
**Marks / limit:** 15 / 250 words  
**Classification:** Environment and Ecology → Ecosystems → Carrying Capacity  
**Proposed topic slug:** `environment/ecosystem-structure-and-function`  
**Difficulty / demand:** High · Define + Apply to planning  
**Canonical owner:** [`Environment-and-Ecology/basic/01_Ecosystem-Structure-and-Function.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Environment-and-Ecology/basic/01_Ecosystem-Structure-and-Function.md)

### Demand decoding
Define carrying capacity in ecological and planning terms, identify its components, and show how assessment changes regional choices while acknowledging uncertainty.

### Compact framework
`resource-support capacity + waste-assimilative capacity ≥ cumulative demand; otherwise thresholds and irreversible degradation`

### Model answer
An ecosystem’s **carrying capacity** is the level and pattern of population or economic activity that it can support over time without unacceptable decline in resource stocks, ecological functions or resilience. For planning, it includes both **supportive capacity**—water, land, biomass and habitat available—and **assimilative capacity**—the ability to absorb pollution and waste without crossing thresholds.

Its assessment is vital because regional development creates cumulative pressures that project-wise clearance may miss:

- water budgets can determine permissible crops, industries, hotels or urban density;
- slope stability and erosion limits guide roads, mining and construction in the Himalayas;
- air-shed capacity can cap polluting units in industrial clusters;
- tourism capacity can align visitors, transport, waste and sewage with fragile islands or hill towns;
- ecological-flow and sediment needs inform dams and river-basin plans;
- habitat connectivity and biodiversity thresholds guide zoning of infrastructure.

Planning can then designate conservation and no-go areas, locate activities in lower-impact zones, set extraction and emission ceilings, phase projects, require common treatment facilities and trigger restoration. Public participation also reveals customary use and distributional impacts hidden by aggregate estimates.

Carrying capacity is not a single permanent number. Technology, consumption, seasons, climate change and ecosystem recovery alter it, while some thresholds remain uncertain. Assessments must therefore be cumulative, basin or landscape-based, periodically revised and governed by precaution.

Sustainable development respects ecological limits while allocating scarce environmental space fairly, rather than treating carrying-capacity studies as a post-clearance formality.

### Examiner rationale
The answer supplies a precise two-part definition, converts it into sector-specific planning decisions and qualifies the concept’s apparent numerical certainty.

**Classification:** Disaster Management → Landslides → Hazard Zonation Mapping  
**Proposed topic slug:** `disaster-management/landslides-avalanches-and-glof-risk`  
**Difficulty / demand:** Moderate · Explain mechanism and use  
**Canonical owner:** [`Disaster-Management/basic/10_Landslides-Avalanches-and-GLOF-Risk.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Disaster-Management/basic/10_Landslides-Avalanches-and-GLOF-Risk.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain how maps are produced and, more importantly, how susceptibility classes inform prevention, preparedness and response.

### Compact framework
`landslide inventory + terrain/trigger layers → susceptibility zones → regulated land use, safer engineering, monitoring and evacuation`

### Model answer
Landslide hazard-zonation mapping divides terrain into classes of relative susceptibility or hazard by combining past landslide inventories with slope, geology, soil, drainage, land cover, rainfall, seismicity and human disturbance. Remote sensing, GIS, field surveys and instruments allow these layers to be updated.

It supports mitigation in several ways:

1. **Land-use regulation:** high-risk slopes can be kept free of settlements, quarrying, dumping and critical facilities; safer zones can receive development.
2. **Infrastructure design:** road and hydropower alignments can avoid unstable formations; where avoidance is impossible, maps guide drainage, retaining structures, rock bolts, slope grading and bio-engineering.
3. **Preparedness:** overlaying hazard with population and asset exposure identifies vulnerable villages, schools, hospitals and lifelines for evacuation routes, shelters and drills.
4. **Monitoring and warning:** mapped hotspots can receive rain gauges, ground-motion sensors and rainfall thresholds, making surveillance affordable and targeted.
5. **Prioritisation:** scarce funds can be directed to slope stabilisation and drainage before monsoon; response teams can pre-position equipment.
6. **Recovery:** reconstruction can apply build-back-better siting rather than recreate exposure.

The Geological Survey of India’s susceptibility mapping and State-level studies are therefore planning tools, not merely atlases.

Limitations matter: coarse maps cannot determine the safety of an individual plot; road cutting, fires, earthquakes and extreme rainfall change conditions. Zonation must be legally integrated into master plans, ground-truthed at project scale, publicly accessible and periodically revised.

Thus mapping converts landslide knowledge into anticipatory regulation and preparedness—the essence of mitigation.
