### Q1(a). “Precepts without concepts are blind and concepts without precepts are empty.” In the light of this statement discuss how Kant reconciles rationalism with empiricism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → intuition and concepts · synthesis of rationalism and empiricism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Kant—synthetic a priori judgements; space and time; categories · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kant · sensible intuition and understanding · conditions of experience · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-intuitions-concepts-reconciliation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the printed statement through Kant’s two stems of knowledge, show what he accepts and rejects in rationalism and empiricism, and state the critical limit.

**Argument map:** sensory matter from experience → a priori forms and categories organise it → cognition requires their synthesis → synthetic a priori knowledge → validity limited to appearances.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant reconciles empiricism and rationalism by holding that knowledge begins with experience but is not wholly derived from it.

Empiricism is right that cognition requires sensible intuition: objects are given through sensations ordered by the a priori forms of space and time. Rationalism is right that the mind contributes universal structures: the understanding thinks what is given through categories such as substance and causality. Intuitions without concepts are “blind” because an unorganised manifold is not objective knowledge; concepts without intuitions are “empty” because pure thought alone supplies no object.

In the transcendental synthesis, imagination and understanding unify the sensible manifold under the unity of apperception. This explains synthetic a priori judgements: mathematics rests on forms of intuition, while principles such as causality are conditions under which events can be experienced objectively.

**Objection and reply:** The synthesis may seem to impose subjective forms on reality. Kant replies that these forms are objectively valid for every possible experience, though not for things-in-themselves.

**Evaluation:** His critical idealism joins empirical content to a priori form without endorsing either sensory passivity or dogmatic reason.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both halves of the dictum, the reconciliation, synthetic a priori knowledge and its phenomenal limit.

### Q1(b). “History is a process of dialectical change.” In the light of this statement discuss Hegel’s approach in understanding history.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → dialectic · history and freedom · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Hegel—dialectical method; absolute idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hegel · philosophy of history · contradiction, sublation and freedom · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hegel-history-dialectical-change`

**Demand decoding:** Explain dialectical historical development, identify its end as growing consciousness of freedom, and critically qualify teleology and Eurocentrism.

**Argument map:** finite social form contains contradiction → conflict and determinate negation → sublation (*Aufhebung*) → higher institution and self-consciousness → progressive realisation of freedom.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Hegel, history is intelligible development in which Spirit comes to know and institutionalise freedom through contradictions within successive forms of life.

A historical order embodies a partial conception of freedom but generates tensions it cannot resolve. Dialectical negation does not merely destroy it: sublation (*Aufhebung*) cancels, preserves and raises its rational content. Thus Oriental despotism knows only one as free; the Greek and Roman worlds know some as free; the modern constitutional state recognises freedom in principle as universal. Individuals may pursue private aims, yet the “cunning of reason” makes their actions vehicles of a larger rational development.

Change is therefore neither linear accumulation nor an external thesis–antithesis formula. Contradiction is internal to institutions, and its resolution produces a more adequate unity.

**Objection:** This teleology can retrospectively justify violence, privilege Europe and understate contingency. **Reply:** Hegel’s claim is not that every event is good, but that philosophy can reconstruct a rational development in the consciousness of freedom.

**Evaluation:** Dialectic illuminates structural conflict and historical learning, but its alleged necessity should be treated as interpretive rather than predictive.

**Why this earns marks:** It links dialectic, sublation, Spirit and freedom while adding the strongest historical criticism.

### Q1(c). “That thing is said to be free which exists solely from the necessity of its own nature, and is determined to action by itself alone.” Discuss Spinoza’s views on freedom and determinism in the light of the above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Spinoza · necessity and freedom · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Spinoza; substance; determinism and freedom · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Spinoza · self-determination · adequate ideas and necessity · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `spinoza-freedom-through-necessity`

**Demand decoding:** Interpret the quoted definition, distinguish divine from human freedom, and evaluate Spinoza’s compatibilist replacement of free will.

**Argument map:** God/Nature acts from its essence → every mode follows necessarily → ignorant humans feel contingently free → adequate understanding increases activity → freedom as rational self-determination.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Spinoza denies freedom as uncaused choice but retains freedom as action flowing from one’s own understood nature rather than from external determination.

God or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*) alone is absolutely free: as self-caused substance, it exists and acts from the necessity of its essence. Yet divine freedom is not choice among alternatives; the whole order follows necessarily. Finite persons are modes within this causal order. Their belief in free will arises because they know their desires but not the causes determining them.

Human freedom is nevertheless possible by degree. Under passive affects we are acted upon by inadequate ideas and external causes. Through adequate ideas, reason and the intellectual love of God, we understand necessity and act from our conatus—our power of persevering—more coherently. Freedom is thus activity and self-determination within necessity.

**Objection:** If every thought is necessitated, responsibility appears illusory. Spinoza replies that responsibility concerns the difference between passive compulsion and rational agency, not causal exemption.

**Evaluation:** This is a powerful compatibilism, though critics may find it too revisionary to preserve genuine alternative possibilities.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the quotation, divine necessity, human bondage and Spinoza’s positive conception of freedom.

### Q1(d). How does Kierkegaard argue against Hegel’s idea of universal spirit in favour of the individual as the essence of spirit? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Kierkegaard · existing individual against system · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Kierkegaard; choice, responsibility and authentic existence · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kierkegaard and Hegel · individuality, subjectivity and universal Spirit · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kierkegaard-individual-against-universal-spirit`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Kierkegaard’s existential objection to Hegelian mediation, explain choice and faith, and assess whether he refutes or merely redirects Hegel.

**Argument map:** system abstracts from existence → lived choice cannot be retrospectively mediated → individual confronts anxiety, guilt and faith → spirit is inward self-relation → objection of irrational individualism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kierkegaard argues that spirit is realised by the concrete existing individual through choice and inward commitment, not exhausted by the universal self-development of a speculative system.

Hegel interprets finite persons as moments in the dialectical self-knowledge of universal Spirit. Kierkegaard objects that a system may classify existence but cannot exist, choose or suffer for the individual. Ethical responsibility demands the first-person “either/or”; it cannot be dissolved retrospectively into a rational synthesis. In religious existence, the single individual stands “before God.” Abraham’s ordeal in *Fear and Trembling* illustrates faith as a passionate relation that cannot be translated without remainder into universal ethical mediation.

The self is therefore a relation that relates itself to itself and to the power that established it. Anxiety, despair and commitment disclose spirit more concretely than impersonal historical reason.

**Objection:** Privileging inwardness may license irrationalism and isolate persons from social institutions. **Reply:** Kierkegaard does not reject reason or ethics generally; he denies their capacity to substitute for personal appropriation and responsibility.

**Evaluation:** He decisively exposes the system’s existential blind spot, though he does not by itself disprove Hegel’s social account of freedom.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly contrasts universal mediation with the existing individual and supplies criticism and qualification.

### Q1(e). What are the main arguments offered by Kant to prove that apriori synthetic judgements are possible? Discuss with examples.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → synthetic a priori judgements · transcendental proof · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Kant—possibility of synthetic a priori judgements; space and time; categories · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kant · mathematics and principles of experience · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-synthetic-a-priori-possibility`

**Demand decoding:** Define synthetic and a priori, give Kant’s mathematical and transcendental arguments, and restrict their validity to possible experience.

**Argument map:** some judgements extend knowledge with necessity → neither analytic reason nor induction explains them → a priori intuition grounds mathematics → categories ground objective experience → phenomenal validity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant proves the possibility of synthetic a priori judgements by showing that the mind’s a priori forms and concepts are constitutive conditions of any possible experience.

A judgement is synthetic when its predicate adds information not contained in the subject, and a priori when it is necessary and universally valid independently of particular experience. “7 + 5 = 12” is synthetic because twelve is not obtained by merely analysing the concepts seven and five; construction in pure temporal intuition yields it. Geometry similarly rests on pure spatial intuition.

In natural science, “every event has a cause” is not analytic and cannot acquire necessity from repeated observation. Kant’s transcendental argument holds that an objective temporal sequence is experienceable as an event only if the category of causality orders it by a rule. Categories are valid because objects of experience must conform to the conditions under which they can be experienced.

**Objection and reply:** Non-Euclidean geometry challenges Kant’s geometry. This weakens his specific mathematical illustration, but not the broader strategy of identifying constitutive conditions.

**Evaluation:** Synthetic a priori knowledge is secured for appearances, not for supersensible things-in-themselves.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the category, presents both principal arguments, gives examples and states the critical limit.

### Q2(a). Critically analyse Hume’s argument that causality is a matter of habit/custom involving psychological principle of association.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume · causation, custom and association · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Hume; theory of knowledge; scepticism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hume · necessary connection · constant conjunction and habit · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hume-causality-habit-association`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Hume’s negative and positive accounts of causation, explain association and induction, then assess whether psychological expectation exhausts causal necessity.

**Argument map:** copy principle → no impression of necessary connection → experience gives contiguity, succession and conjunction → repetition produces customary inference → necessity becomes felt determination → sceptical and realist objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hume argues that experience never reveals a necessary power binding cause to effect; causal belief arises when repeated conjunction generates a customary transition of imagination.

All meaningful ideas must derive from impressions. Inspecting a single event—one billiard ball striking another—reveals motion, contact and succession, but no impression of a necessary tie. Reason cannot infer the effect a priori because contrary outcomes are conceivable without contradiction. Nor can past regularity rationally prove future regularity: such an argument presupposes the uniformity of nature and is circular.

What experience supplies is:

1. **spatiotemporal contiguity** of cause and effect;
2. **temporal priority** of the cause; and
3. **constant conjunction** of similar kinds of events.

After repetition, the principle of association makes the mind pass from the impression of the cause to a lively idea of the effect. This felt determination is the source of the idea of necessary connection. Hume accordingly offers two definitions: a cause is an object regularly followed by another, and an object whose appearance carries thought to the other.

**Strengths:** The account exposes why bare observation cannot disclose metaphysical power, explains expectation naturalistically, and identifies the logical problem of induction.

**Objections and replies:**  
- A regularity can be accidental; night regularly follows day without causing it. Hume can add stable counterfactual patterns, but these go beyond his simple conjunction account.  
- Scientific causation appears to concern mechanisms and powers, not merely expectation. A realist may infer dispositions as the best explanation; Hume replies that such necessity is never directly experienced.  
- Kant argues that causality is an a priori condition of objective event-experience, not learned association. Hume may answer that this asserts rather than empirically derives necessity.

**Evaluation:** Hume successfully undermines rationalist certainty about causal power and explains causal belief, but reducing necessity to psychological compulsion underdescribes causal explanation, intervention and laws. His enduring achievement is to separate observed regularity from the stronger modal claim we habitually make.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs impression theory, induction, association and both definitions before giving several targeted criticisms.

### Q2(b). Present an exposition of Aristotle’s distinction between actuality and potentiality. Does it provide a solution to the problem of being and becoming as presented in ancient Greek philosophy? Discuss with suitable examples.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → actuality and potentiality · being and becoming · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—form and matter; actuality and potentiality; causation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Aristotle · *energeia*, *entelecheia* and *dunamis* · change without non-being · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aristotle-actuality-potentiality-becoming`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the distinction and its hylomorphic setting, apply it to the Parmenidean–Heraclitean problem, and judge how far it succeeds.

**Argument map:** Parmenides denies coming-to-be; Heraclitus stresses flux → potential being differs from sheer non-being → change actualises a determinate capacity → substance persists → explanatory gain and limits.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Aristotle explains becoming as the actualisation of a real potential in a persisting subject, thereby avoiding both Parmenides’ denial of change and an ontology of unstructured flux.

Potentiality (*dunamis*) is a capacity to be or act otherwise under appropriate conditions; actuality (*energeia* or fulfilled *entelecheia*) is the realised exercise or completion of that capacity. A seed is potentially a tree, bronze potentially a statue, and a person who knows grammar potentially engaged in its exercise. Potentiality is determinate: a seed is not potentially every object. In natural change, matter bears contrary forms while substantial form specifies the end; actuality is prior in definition and explanation because a capacity is identified by the actuality toward which it tends.

Parmenides argues that what-is cannot arise from what-is-not. Aristotle answers that change does not arise from absolute non-being. The tree comes from a seed that is actually a seed but potentially a tree. The subject is one in actuality under one description and potential under another. Against Heraclitean flux, a persisting substance and structured powers make change intelligible.

**Objections:** The doctrine may redescribe rather than fully explain transition; teleological capacities can appear circular because the potential is identified by its outcome. Modern physics also resists treating all change as goal-directed.

**Reply and evaluation:** Aristotle need not claim conscious purpose: natural potentialities are dispositional structures. The distinction provides a coherent conceptual solution to the contradiction between being and becoming, though a complete causal explanation additionally requires material, formal, efficient and final causes.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms, uses several examples, addresses the ancient problem directly and gives a qualified verdict.

### Q2(c). Discuss Descartes’ theory of innate ideas and the grounds on which Locke refutes it.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Descartes · innate ideas · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Descartes; certain knowledge; Empiricism—Locke; theory of knowledge · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Descartes and Locke · innateness, universality and reflection · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `descartes-innate-ideas-locke-critique`

**Demand decoding:** State Descartes’ categories and reasons for innateness, reconstruct Locke’s anti-innatist arguments, and distinguish innate ideas from innate capacities.

**Argument map:** ideas adventitious/factitious/innate → infinity, self and clear principles exceed sensation → Locke attacks universal assent and implicit knowledge → sensation and reflection explain content → residual rational capacity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Descartes treats certain ideas and principles as native to reason, while Locke argues that neither universal assent nor explanatory necessity establishes innate propositional content.

Descartes distinguishes adventitious ideas apparently received through sense, factitious ideas constructed by us, and innate ideas belonging to the mind’s nature. The ideas of thought, substance, God and basic logical or mathematical truths are not copied from sensation. Most importantly, the idea of an infinite perfect being allegedly contains more objective reality than a finite mind can originate; God must therefore be its cause. Innateness does not require continuous awareness: such ideas are dispositions elicited when reason reflects.

Locke’s *Essay* attacks the claim of universal consent. Children and persons lacking developed reasoning do not assent to alleged speculative principles such as identity or practical maxims. To say that a proposition is “in the mind” though never understood makes unconscious knowledge unintelligible. If whatever reason later discovers is innate, then innumerable learned truths become innate and the distinction loses force. Locke instead derives simple ideas from sensation and reflection; the mind combines, compares and abstracts them into complex ideas.

**Qualification:** Locke’s critique is strongest against occurrent innate propositions. Descartes can retreat to innate capacities or constraints, which Locke himself effectively accepts as natural faculties.

**Evaluation:** Locke defeats crude universal-inscription theories, but he does not show that experience alone explains necessity, conceptual structure or the idea of infinity. The dispute survives as one between innate content and innate cognitive form.

**Why this earns marks:** It fairly presents Cartesian motivation, Locke’s distinct objections and the crucial dispositional reply.

### Q3(a). Does the rejection of metaphysics as proposed by Logical Positivists relate to problem of meaning or problem of knowledge or nature of things or all of them together? Discuss with suitable examples.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → verification and rejection of metaphysics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Logical Positivism—verification theory of meaning; rejection of metaphysics; necessary propositions · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Vienna Circle and A. J. Ayer · cognitive meaning, knowledge and ontology · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `logical-positivism-metaphysics-meaning-knowledge-reality`

**Demand decoding:** Decide the primary target of anti-metaphysics, explain consequences for knowledge and ontological claims, illustrate, and test verification against major objections.

**Argument map:** meaningful propositions are analytic or empirically verifiable → metaphysical statements satisfy neither → primary diagnosis is semantic → therefore no knowledge-claim and no warranted ontology → criterion’s self-reference and scientific-law problems.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Logical positivism rejects metaphysics primarily as a problem of cognitive meaning; this semantic verdict blocks metaphysical knowledge and suspends claims about ultimate reality, but it does not directly prove what kinds of things exist.

The Vienna Circle combines empiricism with the logical analysis of language. A cognitively meaningful proposition is either:

1. **analytic**, true by linguistic or logical rules—e.g. “all bachelors are unmarried”; or
2. **empirically verifiable**, at least in principle—e.g. “copper conducts electricity.”

Necessary propositions are tautological and say nothing factual about the world. Statements such as “the Absolute enters into but transcends all being” or “reality is a supersensible substance” are neither analytic nor connected to possible observations. They are not false descriptions of a hidden realm; they fail to express testable propositions. Carnap’s critique of “Nothing nothings” diagnoses violations of logical syntax, while Ayer treats ethical and theological utterances as non-cognitive unless empirically testable.

Thus the rejection has three connected levels:

| Level | Positivist claim |
|---|---|
| Meaning | Metaphysical strings lack cognitive significance. |
| Knowledge | What lacks truth-conditions cannot be known or refuted as fact. |
| Nature of things | No metaphysical inventory follows; only empirically warranted discourse is licensed. |

The order matters. Positivists do not discover empirically that there is no transcendent reality; they deny that traditional formulations state a factual issue.

**Objections:** The verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable and therefore appears self-defeating. Strict verification excludes universal laws and historical propositions; weak verification becomes too permissive. Quine challenges the analytic–synthetic distinction, while later Wittgenstein shows that meaningful use exceeds scientific assertion. Metaphysical frameworks may also guide science without being directly testable.

**Reply and evaluation:** A positivist can present verification as a methodological proposal and replace conclusive verification with confirmability. This preserves its demand for evidential accountability but weakens the claim that metaphysics is literally meaningless. Anti-metaphysics is therefore chiefly semantic, epistemically consequential, and only indirectly ontological.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the “meaning/knowledge/nature” alternatives in their logical order and evaluates the criterion with exact objections.

### Q3(b). Elucidate the significance of bracketing and reduction in Husserl’s phenomenological method.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Phenomenology](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/phenomenology.md) → bracketing and reduction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Phenomenology (Husserl)—method; theory of essences; avoidance of psychologism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Husserl · epoché, phenomenological reduction and constitution · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology (Husserl)](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `husserl-bracketing-reduction-significance`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish epoché from reduction, explain the shift from natural attitude to transcendental subjectivity, and prevent the sceptical or idealist misreading.

**Argument map:** natural attitude posits world → epoché suspends existential commitment → reduction redirects attention to intentional appearing → eidetic variation discloses essences → transcendental constitution and anti-psychologism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Bracketing and reduction suspend unexamined commitments about the world so that phenomenology can describe how objects are given to consciousness and identify their essential structures.

In the natural attitude we simply take the world and its objects as existing independently. The phenomenological epoché or “bracketing” does not deny this existence; it withholds the thesis of existence from methodological use. Attention then shifts from the object merely posited to the object-as-experienced and to the intentional act through which it appears.

The phenomenological reduction conducts inquiry back (*re-ducere*) to the correlation between consciousness and its intended object—the noetic act and noematic sense. Eidetic reduction uses imaginative variation to distinguish contingent features from invariant essence: varying imagined triangles discloses what belongs to triangularity. The transcendental reduction further reveals subjectivity as the field in which objective sense, identity through changing profiles and intersubjective validity are constituted.

Its significance is threefold: it makes presuppositionless description possible; avoids psychologism by investigating ideal meanings rather than causal mental events; and explains objectivity through intentional constitution.

**Objection:** Complete suspension may be impossible, and transcendental reduction may collapse the world into consciousness. Husserl replies that constitution means disclosure of sense, not arbitrary creation, and later appeals to embodiment and intersubjectivity.

**Evaluation:** Reduction is a disciplined change of standpoint, not scepticism; its power lies in revealing conditions of givenness, though its transcendental claims remain contested.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates the methodological stages, states their anti-psychologistic role and addresses the central idealism objection.

### Q3(c). “Consciousness is what it is not and is not what it is.” In the light of this statement bring out the chief features of Sartre’s conception of consciousness.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Sartre · consciousness, nothingness and freedom · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Sartre; existence and essence; choice, responsibility and authentic existence · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sartre · being-for-itself · facticity, transcendence and bad faith · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `sartre-consciousness-what-it-is-not`

**Demand decoding:** Interpret both clauses of Sartre’s paradox, derive intentionality, nothingness, temporality and freedom, and assess the account.

**Argument map:** consciousness is non-substantial intentionality → distances itself from given facticity → projects unrealised possibilities → temporal self-transcendence → freedom, anguish and bad faith.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Sartre’s formula expresses consciousness as being-for-itself (*pour-soi*): it never coincides with a fixed essence but nihilates what is given and projects itself toward possibilities.

Being-in-itself (*en-soi*) “is what it is”: complete, opaque and self-identical. Consciousness, by contrast, is intentional—always consciousness of something—and has no substantial ego hidden inside it. It “is not what it is” because it can take distance from its present facticity: I am a waiter, but I am not reducible to that social role. It “is what it is not” because it exists toward possibilities not yet actual: future projects organise the meaning of the present.

Nothingness enters the world through negation, questioning and absence. Consciousness is temporal: it surpasses a factical past toward a projected future. This non-coincidence grounds radical freedom and responsibility, accompanied by anguish. Bad faith tries to evade it by treating oneself either as a fixed thing or as pure freedom without facticity.

**Objection:** Sartre exaggerates freedom and underestimates embodiment, social oppression and unconscious motivation. His later work acknowledges situation and material constraints, but insists that constraints acquire lived meaning through projects.

**Evaluation:** The formula powerfully captures reflexivity and possibility, yet freedom is better understood as situated rather than absolute.

**Why this earns marks:** It unpacks both clauses and systematically derives Sartre’s ontology, temporality, ethics and principal criticism.

### Q4(a). Why does Strawson consider person to be a primitive concept? What implication does it have for the mind-body dualism? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → persons as basic particulars · anti-dualism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Quine and Strawson—theory of basic particulars and persons · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: P. F. Strawson · person predicate · ownership of experiences · Cartesian dualism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `strawson-person-primitive-concept-dualism`

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Strawson calls “person” primitive because our conceptual scheme identifies one and the same basic particular as the subject of bodily and psychological predicates; personhood cannot be constructed from an independently identified mind plus body.

- **M-predicates**: weighs sixty kilograms, is walking, is in the room; and
- **P-predicates**: believes, remembers, suffers, intends.

Both are ascribed literally to the same individual. Psychological predicates are not primarily reports about a private Cartesian substance, nor are they merely translated descriptions of bodily behaviour. Moreover, the concept of a mental state is learned and used in a practice that permits both self-ascription without observation and other-ascription on behavioural criteria. The possibility of identifying “my” experiences presupposes the concept of a subject who can also be publicly identified.

This undermines Cartesian mind–body dualism. Descartes starts with a private mind and then confronts the problems of attaching it to a body and knowing other minds. **Reply and evaluation:** Strawson offers a transcendental point about the conditions of intelligible identification, not a scientific theory of composition. It blocks the Cartesian demand to reconstruct persons from private consciousness, though it does not conclusively prove metaphysical monism. Its positive achievement is a non-reductive, embodied concept of agency.

### Q4(b). Why according to Russell is the proposition — “The present king of France is bald” problematic? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → Russell · theory of descriptions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Moore, Russell and Early Wittgenstein—logical constructions; incomplete symbols; logical atomism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Russell · non-denoting definite description · existence, uniqueness and scope · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `russell-present-king-france-problem`

**Demand decoding:** State the puzzles produced by treating the description as a name, give Russell’s quantified analysis, and assess Strawson’s presupposition objection.

**Argument map:** grammatical subject seems to denote → no present king → meaningfulness and excluded-middle puzzles → description as incomplete symbol → existence + uniqueness + predication → sentence false, negation scope clarified.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The sentence is problematic because its grammatical subject appears to name a non-existent object; Russell solves the puzzle by analysing the description away rather than admitting a strange entity.

If “the present king of France” were a genuine name, meaningful discourse would seem to require its bearer. Meinong therefore allowed non-existent objects, while saying both “the king is bald” and “the king is not bald” appears to violate excluded middle.

Russell analyses the sentence as:

\[
\exists x[Kx \land \forall y(Ky \rightarrow y=x)\land Bx]
\]

That is: at least one present king of France exists, at most one exists, and that object is bald. The existence clause is false, so the entire proposition is false without referring to a subsisting king. The negation is scope-sensitive: “It is not the case that there exists exactly one king who is bald” is true; “There exists exactly one king who is not bald” is false.

The definite description is therefore an incomplete symbol whose meaning appears only in the whole proposition. Logical form differs from surface grammar.

**Objection:** Strawson argues that ordinary utterance presupposes, rather than asserts, the king’s existence; when the presupposition fails, the utterance is neither true nor false. Russell’s account may thus misdescribe conversational use.

**Evaluation:** Strawson better captures pragmatics, but Russell supplies a precise logical analysis and avoids ontological inflation. Context may determine whether falsity or presupposition failure is the better verdict.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact puzzles, formal analysis, negation scope and strongest criticism.

### Q4(c). What were the main reasons that led Wittgenstein to shift from picture-theory of meaning to use-theory of meaning? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Later Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/later-wittgenstein.md) → picture theory to meaning-as-use · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Early Wittgenstein—picture theory, saying and showing; Later Wittgenstein—meaning and use, language-games · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Wittgenstein · critique of logical essence · ordinary linguistic practice · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Secondary route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `wittgenstein-picture-to-use-shift`

**Demand decoding:** State the early theory, identify its internal and practical inadequacies, explain the later alternative, and avoid presenting the shift as complete discontinuity.

**Argument map:** proposition pictures fact through shared logical form → theory privileges description and simples → language has commands, questions, avowals and flexible rules → meaning arises in use within language-games and forms of life.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Wittgenstein shifted because the picture theory imposed one logical essence on language and could not adequately explain the diversity, normativity and context-dependence of actual linguistic practices.

In the *Tractatus*, an elementary proposition pictures a possible atomic fact because its elements share logical form with objects. This powerfully explains truth-functional representation, but creates difficulties:

1. **Over-privileging description:** Commands, questions, jokes, promises, mathematical expressions and sensation avowals do not simply picture facts.
2. **Unclear simples:** The theory requires logically simple objects and names, yet offers no workable method for identifying them.
3. **Context:** The same word performs different functions in different circumstances; reference alone does not determine meaning.
4. **Rule-following:** Understanding involves knowing how to continue a practice, not attaching an occult object to each sign.
5. **Philosophical method:** Ideal logical analysis often creates rather than dissolves confusion by ignoring ordinary grammar.

The *Philosophical Investigations* therefore says that, for a large class of cases, meaning is use. Language consists of multiple language-games embedded in forms of life. Their relations are often family resemblances, not one common essence. Rules operate through publicly checkable practices.

**Criticism and qualification:** Pure use-theory may seem to make meaning conventional and unable to criticise practices. Yet “use” is normative, not mere frequency, and the world still constrains successful language-games. There is also continuity: both periods treat philosophical problems as arising from misunderstanding language and reject private metaphysical explanations.

**Evaluation:** The shift replaces a rigid representational model with pragmatic plurality, though representation remains one important language-game rather than disappearing.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies several reasons, the later positive account, criticism and a continuity thesis.

Thus the eye perceives a cloth by conjunction, its colour through inherence in the cloth, and colourhood through inherence in the colour.

Extraordinary perception (*alaukika pratyakṣa*) has three forms:

1. **Universal-character perception (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa*):** perceiving one smoky object together with smokiness enables awareness of smoke generally, including unobserved instances.
2. **Knowledge-mediated perception (*jñānalakṣaṇa*):** seeing sandalwood as fragrant through memory-associated cognition.
3. **Yogic perception (*yogaja*):** extraordinary apprehension of remote or subtle objects by an accomplished yogin.

Nyāya holds universals (*sāmānya*) to be real, eternal, repeatable entities inhering in many individuals. Recognition—“this is a cow,” “that too is a cow”—and successful common predication appear to involve direct awareness of cowhood, not inference after each individual perception.

**Objections:** Buddhists explain classification through exclusion (*apoha*) and conceptual construction; nominalists invoke resemblance. Universal-character perception also risks collapsing perception into inference and granting awareness of indefinitely many unseen instances.

**Evaluation:** Ordinary perception of an instantiated universal is plausible as aspect-perception, but the stronger claim that one perceptual act reaches all instances is insufficiently justified. Nyāya is strongest in explaining objective classification, weaker in its expansive extraordinary mechanism.

**Why this earns marks:** It classifies both forms, names the technical relations and gives a balanced verdict on universal perception.

### Q7(a). Elucidate Naiyayikas account of fallacies of the middle term in relation to five characteristics of valid middle term.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → inference · valid reason and fallacies · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—theory of *pramāṇa* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nyāya · five marks of a valid inferential reason · fallacious reason (*hetvābhāsa*) · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-five-marks-hetvabhasa`

**Demand decoding:** State the five characteristics of a valid middle term, correlate their failure with the major fallacies, and explain with standard examples.

**Argument map:** inference requires reason in subject + positive concomitance + negative concomitance + undefeated status + no equal counterreason → violation generates five principal fallacies.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya tests an inferential reason (*hetu*) through five characteristics; the principal fallacies of the middle term (*hetvābhāsa*) arise when one or more of these conditions fail.

In “the hill has fire because it has smoke,” a valid reason must possess:

1. **Presence in the subject (*pakṣasattva*):** smoke must be present on the hill.
2. **Presence in similar instances (*sapakṣasattva*):** smoke occurs in positive cases where fire exists, such as a kitchen.
3. **Absence from dissimilar instances (*vipakṣāsattva*):** smoke is absent where fire is absent, such as a lake.
4. **Uncontradictedness (*abādhitatva*):** no stronger cognition defeats the conclusion.
5. **Absence of an equal counterreason (*asatpratipakṣatva*):** no sound reason establishes the opposite conclusion.

Their failure explains the five standard fallacies:

| Fallacy | Defect and example |
|---|---|
| **Irregular or inconclusive (*savyabhicāra/anaikāntika*)** | Positive or negative concomitance fails. “The hill has fire because it is knowable”; knowability occurs with and without fire. It may be over-common, under-common or non-exclusive. |
| **Contradictory (*viruddha*)** | The reason proves the opposite. “Sound is eternal because it is produced”; producedness pervades non-eternality. |
| **Unestablished (*asiddha*)** | Subject, reason or pervasion is unproved. “The sky-lotus is fragrant because it is a lotus” has an unreal subject; “sound is visible” lacks the reason in the subject. |
| **Counterbalanced (*satpratipakṣa*)** | An equally forceful reason proves the contrary, so absence of counterreason fails. |
| **Defeated (*bādhita*)** | Stronger perception or testimony contradicts the conclusion: “fire is cold because it is a substance.” |

The mapping is not always one-to-one. *Savyabhicāra* primarily violates positive/negative concomitance; *asiddha* violates subject-presence or proof of pervasion; *bādhita* and *satpratipakṣa* directly violate the fourth and fifth conditions.

**Critical note:** The framework is rigorous because it combines formal relevance, empirical concomitance and defeat conditions. Yet establishing universal concomitance (*vyāpti*) without circularity remains difficult; Nyāya relies on repeated agreement, absence of counterinstances and hypothetical reasoning (*tarka*).

**Evaluation:** The five marks make Nyāya inference a sophisticated defeasible logic rather than a merely formal syllogism, while the problem of securing *vyāpti* remains its epistemic core.

**Why this earns marks:** It states all five marks, correlates all five fallacies with examples and adds the deeper *vyāpti* issue.

### Q7(b). Liberation is defined by Advaita Vedantins as ‘attainment of that which is already attained’. How does Sankara illustrate this statement? Discuss with your own comments.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → liberation as recognition · already-attained Brahman · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Vedānta—self; Brahman; ignorance; superimposition; liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Śaṅkara · *prāptasya prāpti* · knowledge and removal of ignorance · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `advaita-liberation-already-attained`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why liberation is not a produced event, present Śaṅkara’s illustrative analogies, and critically assess knowledge-only liberation.

**Argument map:** self is always Brahman → ignorance creates apparent bondage → knowledge removes error, not bondage as a real entity → lost-necklace and tenth-man analogies → liberation is recognition.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Śaṅkara, liberation (*mokṣa*) is “attainment of the already attained” (*prāptasya prāpti*) because the individual self is eternally Brahman; only ignorance of this fact, not a real separation, must be removed.

The classic illustrations are:

- **The necklace:** A woman searches anxiously for a necklace already around her neck. A friend points it out. No new necklace is produced or brought near; knowledge ends the false sense of loss.
- **The tenth man:** Ten travellers cross a river. Each counts only the other nine and grieves that the tenth is lost. When told “you are the tenth,” the missing person is not recovered from elsewhere; ignorance is corrected.
- **The prince or lion motif:** One who wrongly identifies with a lower condition regains confidence through recognition of an always-existing identity.

Likewise, superimposition (*adhyāsa*) makes pure consciousness identify with body, agency and suffering. Scriptural hearing, reflection and contemplation generate self-knowledge; action cannot produce an eternal result. The liberated-while-living person (*jīvanmukta*) knows “I am Brahman” while past momentum sustains embodiment.

**Critical comments:** The theory preserves liberation’s unconditionality and explains why finite action cannot create infinity. However, if bondage is unreal, the urgency of discipline and the persistence of suffering become difficult. Rāmānuja also argues that liberation is real communion with God, not mere correction of error.

**Evaluation:** Śaṅkara’s analogy compellingly presents liberation as transformative recognition, provided one accepts non-dual identity and levels of reality.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the named principle, multiple illustrations, the knowledge-mechanism and an independent critical assessment.

### Q7(c). Explain Chitta and its modifications in the philosophy of Yoga. Why does Yoga philosophy prescribe cessation of modifications of Chitta? Give reasons in support of your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Yoga](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/yoga.md) → mind-field and its modifications · cessation and isolation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Yoga—mind-field; mental modifications; afflictions; concentration; isolation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Pātañjala Yoga · *citta*, *cittavṛtti-nirodha* and liberation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Yoga](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Yoga.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `yoga-citta-vritti-cessation`

**Demand decoding:** Define *citta* and classify its five modifications, then causally connect cessation with ending affliction, karmic impressions and misidentification.

**Argument map:** material mind reflects consciousness → modifications present objects and appropriate seerhood → afflictions colour modifications → action-impression cycle sustains bondage → practice and dispassion still the mind → seer abides in itself.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In Yoga, the mind-field (*citta*) is the subtle material instrument comprising intellect, ego-function and mind; its changing modes obscure the distinction between pure consciousness and nature, so their cessation is required for isolation-liberation (*kaivalya*).

The *Yoga Sūtra* defines Yoga as cessation of the modifications of the mind-field (*citta-vṛtti-nirodha*). Five modifications may be afflicted or unafflicted:

1. valid cognition (*pramāṇa*);
2. error (*viparyaya*);
3. conceptual construction (*vikalpa*);
4. sleep (*nidrā*); and
5. memory (*smṛti*).

Though *citta* is unconscious and belongs to primordial nature (*prakṛti*), its clarity allows it to reflect consciousness (*puruṣa*). Because the seer identifies with its modes—“I know,” “I suffer,” “I act”—the afflictions (*kleśas*), especially ignorance and egoism, produce action, karmic deposits and renewed experience. Each mode leaves an impression (*saṃskāra*), and impressions generate further modes.

Cessation breaks this cycle. Practice (*abhyāsa*) stabilises attention; dispassion (*vairāgya*) weakens attachment; the eight limbs purify conduct, body, breath, senses and attention; concentration culminates in meditative absorption (*samādhi*) and discriminative insight.

**Qualification:** Cessation is not destruction of mind or unconscious blankness. Unafflicted cognition and discriminative knowledge are used to remove afflicted patterns; finally even their residual impressions cease.

**Evaluation:** Yoga provides a psychologically acute account of habit and attention. Its claim that pure consciousness is wholly separate from mind depends, however, on Sāṃkhya dualism.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the ontology, all five modifications, the bondage mechanism and the precise rationale for cessation.

### Q8(a). “Ignorance of dependent origination is suffering while its knowledge is cessation of suffering.” Present an account of Buddhist soteriology in the light of above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → dependent origination · suffering and cessation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Buddhism—dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*); momentariness; no-self · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Buddhism · Four Noble Truths · twelve links, Middle Path and liberation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `buddhist-soteriology-dependent-origination`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how ignorance drives the conditioned cycle, relate forward and reverse orders to the Four Noble Truths, and show how ethical, meditative and wisdom practices realise cessation.

**Argument map:** ignorance conditions formations → consciousness through ageing-and-death → suffering is conditioned, not fated → cessation of ignorance removes craving and appropriation → Noble Eightfold Path embodies practical Middle Path → insight into impermanence and no-self culminates in nirvāṇa.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Buddhist soteriology rests on dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*): suffering arises through conditions headed by ignorance, and liberation becomes possible because removing those conditions ends the process.

The Four Noble Truths diagnose suffering (*duḥkha*), its origin, its cessation and the path. Dependent origination gives the causal grammar of the diagnosis. In its twelve-link form:

> ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six sense bases → contact → feeling → craving → clinging → becoming → birth → ageing-and-death.

Ignorance (*avidyā*) is not lack of information alone. It misconstrues impermanent, conditioned aggregates as permanent, satisfying and self. Feeling then conditions craving, craving hardens into appropriation, and karmically charged becoming perpetuates the cycle. No unchanging soul transmigrates; a causally continuous stream arises, like one flame conditioning another. This is the doctrinal middle between eternalism and annihilationism.

The reverse order states: with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease; ultimately birth, ageing and death cease. Knowledge here is transformative wisdom (*prajñā*), directly seeing impermanence, suffering, no-self (*anātman*) and emptiness of independent origination. Because every factor is conditioned, suffering is neither divinely imposed nor metaphysically necessary.

The Noble Eightfold Path (*āryāṣṭāṅgamārga*) operationalises this insight:

- **wisdom:** right view and intention;
- **ethical discipline:** right speech, action and livelihood;
- **meditative discipline:** right effort, mindfulness and concentration.

It is the practical Middle Path (*madhyamā pratipad*; Pali: *majjhimā paṭipadā*) between sensual indulgence and self-mortification. Ethical restraint weakens coarse craving, concentration steadies the mind, and wisdom removes ignorance. Nirvāṇa is the extinction of greed, hatred and delusion, not annihilation of a substantial self that Buddhism already denies.

**Objections and replies:** The chain may appear circular or temporally ambiguous. Buddhist traditions interpret it across lives, within a single cognitive episode, or structurally; its soteriological force does not depend on one chronology. If all is conditioned, liberation may seem conditioned too; the path conditions the ending of defilements, while nirvāṇa is described as unconditioned cessation.

**Evaluation:** Dependent origination unites metaphysics, psychology and practice: insight changes conduct because it reveals where the cycle can be interrupted. Its strength is a process account of suffering without fatalism or a permanent self.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates the twelve links, Four Noble Truths, both Middle Paths, no-self, the Eightfold Path and a reasoned account of cessation.

### Q8(b). Write a note on Nyaya notion of Pragabhava (prior non-existence). How does this notion help Naiyayikas in defending their position on causation against the Samkhya view of causation? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → prior absence · causation controversy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—theory of causation; theory of categories; Sāṃkhya—causation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nyāya *asatkāryavāda* versus Sāṃkhya *satkāryavāda* · prior absence (*prāgabhāva*) · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Secondary route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-pragabhava-causation-samkhya`

**Demand decoding:** Define the temporal and relational features of prior absence, show how it supports effect-nonexistence before production, and test it against Sāṃkhya’s manifestation theory.

**Argument map:** effect absent in material cause before production → production terminates prior absence → new whole arises from parts → supports *asatkāryavāda* → Sāṃkhya says effect exists potentially/unmanifest → ambiguity of “same effect.”

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya uses prior non-existence (*prāgabhāva*) to argue that an effect does not exist before production and is genuinely originated, thereby defending effect-nonexistence (*asatkāryavāda*) against Sāṃkhya’s pre-existent-effect theory (*satkāryavāda*).

Prior absence is the absence of an effect in its material cause before production. The jar is absent in the clay until the potter’s operation produces it. It is beginningless—there is no first moment of the jar’s prior absence—but it ends when the jar arises. It has a determinate locus, clay, and counterpositive, the jar; it is not sheer nothingness.

Nyāya argues that production is meaningful only if it brings into existence a new whole. Threads exist before weaving, but the cloth as an organised substance does not. If the finished effect already existed, causal activity would be redundant and one could ask why any particular means is required. Production terminates the effect’s prior absence.

Sāṃkhya replies that the effect exists in its cause in an unmanifest form. Nothing can arise from sheer non-being; only an appropriate cause yields a particular effect; causal power and conservation imply transformation (*pariṇāma*), not new creation. Oil is obtained from sesame, not sand.

Nyāya answers that “non-existent effect” does not mean production from nothing: existent causes, qualities and operations produce a previously absent relational whole. Causal specificity is explained by powers without identifying effect and cause.

**Critical evaluation:** The dispute partly turns on identity criteria. Sāṃkhya stresses material continuity and potential structure; Nyāya stresses the novel whole and its properties. Prior absence supports genuine novelty, but does not by itself prove that no determinate potential corresponding to the effect pre-exists. A balanced account may combine material continuity with emergent organisation.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines *prāgabhāva* precisely, reconstructs both causal theories and gives an independent verdict.

### Q8(c). Do words refer to universals or particulars or both? Present an exposition of Nyaya and Mimamsa position with regard to above question along with suitable examples.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → word meaning · universal and particular · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā—theory of knowledge; Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—theory of categories and *pramāṇa* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā · denotation, universals and qualified individuals · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-mimamsa-word-universal-particular`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why neither bare universal nor isolated particular suffices, distinguish Nyāya and major Mīmāṃsā emphases, and illustrate sentence use.

**Argument map:** repeated word applies across individuals → universal supplies stable meaning → communication concerns concrete qualified objects → Nyāya denotes individual qualified by universal/form → Mīmāṃsā privileges universal but reaches particular through contextual connection → comparison.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā both reject the view that common words denote utterly bare particulars, but differ in emphasis: Nyāya makes the primary referent a qualified individual, whereas Mīmāṃsā gives greater semantic primacy to the universal and reaches the particular through sentence context.

For Nyāya, a word such as “cow” conventionally signifies an individual cow qualified by cowhood and a characteristic form. Bare cowhood cannot be brought, milked or tied; a wholly unique particular could not explain the word’s repeatable application. The referent is therefore the particular-as-qualified (*jāti-viśiṣṭa-vyakti*). In “Bring the cow,” cowhood narrows the class, while demonstrative and context identify the actionable individual.

Mīmāṃsā stresses that a stable word–meaning relation must concern an enduring universal (*jāti*), since particulars are innumerable, transient and not all present when the convention is learned. “Cow” primarily expresses cowhood. Yet Vedic injunction and ordinary action require concrete objects. Bhāṭṭa authors explain reference to the individual as connected with the expressed universal and assembled through sentence meaning; Prābhākara’s connected-signification theory makes words convey already connected qualified meanings within the sentence.

Both criticise extreme particularism: if each word referred only to one unique item, linguistic learning and novel application would be impossible. They also avoid a bare-universal theory by recognising contextual reference to individuals.

**Objections:** Buddhist exclusion theory (*apoha*) explains “cow” as exclusion of non-cows without a real universal. It avoids multiplying eternal entities but may presuppose the similarity it seeks to explain.

**Evaluation:** Nyāya gives the most balanced account of reference to qualified particulars; Mīmāṃsā better explains the stability of linguistic convention and sentence-governed action. Words semantically depend on universals but ordinarily refer to particulars under universal descriptions.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “universals, particulars or both,” distinguishes the schools and uses concrete linguistic examples.
