### Q1(a). “OTT and the future of Multiplexes”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “OTT and the future of Multiplexes” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → media technology, consumer behaviour and adaptation · `qualifying-english/essay-ott-future-multiplexes.md`

**Model essay**

For more than a century, cinema-going has combined art, technology and a shared social experience. The rise of over-the-top, or OTT, streaming platforms has altered this arrangement. Films and series can now reach viewers directly through internet-connected devices, often at a time, place and pace chosen by the viewer. This convenience has led some to predict the decline of multiplexes. Yet the future is more likely to involve adaptation and coexistence than the complete victory of one medium over the other.

OTT platforms possess clear advantages. A monthly subscription may provide access to a large library at a cost lower than several theatre visits. Viewers avoid travel, queues and fixed show timings. They can pause a programme, select subtitles and watch content from many regions and countries. Streaming has also expanded opportunities for stories that may not receive a wide theatrical release. Smaller productions, documentaries and regional-language works can find specialised audiences without competing immediately for expensive screens.

The pandemic accelerated these habits by closing theatres and making home entertainment necessary. Producers discovered that a film could be released directly to viewers, while audiences became comfortable watching premieres at home. Data-driven platforms also recommend content according to individual preferences. Multiplexes, by contrast, must fill a limited number of screens and seats and therefore tend to favour films expected to attract large crowds.

However, convenience is not the whole meaning of cinema. A theatre offers a large screen, powerful sound, darkness, concentration and a collective emotional response. Spectacle, action, animation and visually ambitious films often gain impact in this setting. A family outing or a meeting with friends cannot be reduced to the content being watched. Multiplexes sell an occasion as well as a film. For major releases, audiences still value the excitement of an opening weekend and the feeling of participating in a public event.

OTT also has limitations. The abundance of choice can produce distraction rather than satisfaction. Home viewing competes with telephone calls, household work and social-media notifications. Subscription costs accumulate when desirable programmes are divided among several platforms. Unequal internet access and inadequate devices also prevent streaming from being universally convenient. Moreover, films financed for streaming may struggle to recover very high production costs without theatrical revenue.

The real challenge for multiplexes is therefore not mere survival but reinvention. They must improve affordability, cleanliness, accessibility and service instead of depending on costly food and premium pricing. Flexible ticket rates, local-language programming, festivals, restored classics, live events and independent films can widen their appeal. Better screens and sound must provide an experience clearly superior to an ordinary television. Smaller towns also offer room for expansion if prices suit local incomes.

Producers are already experimenting with “windows”: a film appears first in theatres and later on a streaming service. The theatrical run creates publicity and revenue; OTT extends the film’s life and reaches viewers who could not attend. Different films may require different strategies. A grand historical drama may need the theatre, while an intimate story may find its natural audience online.

The future of multiplexes will not be secured by resisting OTT. It will depend on understanding what streaming cannot fully reproduce: immersion, occasion and collective experience. OTT has permanently changed distribution and audience expectations, but it has not abolished the desire to gather before a large screen. The two formats can remain complementary if each develops its distinctive strengths.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares the media fairly, explains their economics and experiences, and reaches a qualified coexistence thesis rather than making a simplistic prediction.

### Q1(b). “Global warming in an unequal world”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Global warming in an unequal world” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → climate justice, vulnerability and shared responsibility · `qualifying-english/essay-global-warming-unequal-world.md`

**Model essay**

Global warming is planetary in scale but unequal in both origin and effect. Greenhouse gases mix in one atmosphere, yet the people who contributed least to their accumulation often face the gravest risks. Differences of wealth, geography, technology and political power determine who can escape a flood, insure a crop, cool a home or rebuild after disaster. Climate change therefore cannot be understood only as an environmental problem; it is also a question of justice.

Industrial development has created prosperity while releasing large quantities of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases. The historical contribution of affluent countries is far greater than that of many poorer societies. Within every country, too, emissions are unequal. A wealthy household with large homes, private vehicles and frequent air travel generally consumes more energy than a low-income family dependent on public transport and modest electricity use. Treating all persons as equally responsible conceals these differences.

The effects are similarly uneven. Small farmers depend directly on rainfall and are vulnerable to drought, unseasonal rain and heat. Coastal communities face erosion, stronger storms and saline water. Outdoor workers suffer dangerous heat without air-conditioned protection. Women, children, elderly persons and people with disabilities may find evacuation and recovery especially difficult. A rich city can build drainage systems and emergency services; an informal settlement may lose homes and livelihoods in a single night.

Climate change can deepen existing inequality. Crop failure raises food prices, but the burden consumes a larger share of a poor family’s income. Water scarcity increases unpaid labour, often performed by women and girls. Disease and heat reduce the earnings of workers who cannot work from home. Repeated disasters force families to sell assets or incur debt, making each later shock harder to survive. Thus, climatic harm is multiplied by social disadvantage.

Justice does not mean that developing countries should repeat a carbon-intensive path without restraint. Their citizens also need clean air, secure water and resilient agriculture. Renewable energy, efficient transport and sustainable cities can support development while limiting future emissions. Yet transition requires finance and technology. Poor countries cannot be asked to choose between basic development and climate action while affluent societies preserve wasteful consumption.

A fair response rests on differentiated responsibility. Those with greater historical emissions, present capacity and financial resources should reduce emissions faster and support adaptation elsewhere. Climate finance must be accessible and additional rather than merely relabelled aid. Assistance is needed for early-warning systems, resilient infrastructure, crop protection and unavoidable loss and damage. Technology partnerships should expand capability rather than create permanent dependence.

Equality within countries is equally important. Carbon pricing or the withdrawal of fossil-fuel support can hurt low-income households unless accompanied by public transport, affordable clean energy and social protection. Workers and regions dependent on carbon-intensive industries need retraining and alternative employment. A “just transition” must distribute both the costs and opportunities of change.

Global warming reveals the illusion that privilege can provide permanent isolation. Wealth may delay suffering, but no gated community can indefinitely escape unstable food systems, mass displacement or ecological breakdown. Effective climate action requires shared effort, but shared effort is not the same as identical sacrifice. The world must protect the vulnerable, restrain excessive consumption and enable dignified development. Only a response grounded in fairness can command the trust and cooperation needed to address a truly global danger.

**Why this earns marks:** It links historical and present inequality to unequal vulnerability, then develops differentiated responsibility, adaptation and a just transition.

### Q1(c). “Social media and the marketing of the self”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Social media and the marketing of the self” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → identity, attention and digital self-presentation · `qualifying-english/essay-social-media-marketing-self.md`

**Model essay**

Social media was initially celebrated as a means of connection and self-expression. It still performs those functions, but it has also turned personal identity into something displayed, measured and marketed. Profiles, photographs, opinions and daily routines are arranged before an audience. Approval arrives through likes, shares, followers and comments. In this environment, the self can begin to resemble a product competing for attention.

Self-presentation is not new. People have always chosen their clothes, manners and words according to social situations. What is new is the scale, permanence and measurability of digital presentation. A user may address friends, relatives, employers and strangers at once. The platform records the response, while algorithms reward content that keeps others watching. The temptation is therefore to present not the whole person but the version most likely to attract engagement.

This marketing can have practical benefits. Artists, teachers, small entrepreneurs and professionals can display their work without access to traditional media. A craftsperson can reach customers directly; a job-seeker can demonstrate expertise; a social worker can mobilise support for a cause. Marginalised persons may find communities in which experiences ignored offline are recognised. Used thoughtfully, a public profile can communicate genuine ability and widen opportunity.

The difficulty begins when visibility becomes a constant requirement. Ordinary moments are evaluated for their potential as content. Holidays, meals, exercise and even grief may be staged for an imagined audience. Filters and careful selection create a polished life from which failure, boredom and uncertainty disappear. Viewers compare their unedited existence with another person’s edited display and may experience inadequacy, envy or anxiety.

Commercial influence intensifies the process. Brands pay creators whose personalities appear trustworthy and accessible. The boundary between recommendation and advertisement becomes unclear because the product is woven into a personal story. The influencer markets goods, but also markets the self as an attractive lifestyle. Followers may mistake commercial persuasion for friendship, while creators feel pressure to maintain an image on which their income depends.

The marketed self also affects public conversation. Strong certainty, outrage and novelty often travel further than hesitation or complexity. People may adopt fashionable opinions to signal membership in a group. A disagreement then threatens not merely an idea but a public identity. This encourages performance over reflection and can reduce citizenship to branding.

Privacy is another cost. Information shared for attention may later be used by advertisers, employers, political campaigns or malicious strangers. Children featured by parents cannot meaningfully consent to a permanent digital record. Even adults may discover that an old joke or impulsive statement continues to define them long after they have changed.

The solution is not withdrawal from social media. Digital platforms are now important spaces of work, culture and association. What is needed is greater honesty and control. Paid promotions should be disclosed; platforms should provide meaningful privacy choices; schools should teach media and advertising literacy. Users must distinguish purposeful communication from compulsive display and preserve parts of life that need no audience.

A person necessarily presents different aspects of the self in different contexts. The danger arises when market logic becomes the measure of personal worth. Followers are not friendship, visibility is not value, and a successful profile is not a complete life. Social media can serve the self, but it should not require the self to become a product.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes ordinary self-presentation from platform-driven branding and balances opportunity with psychological, commercial, civic and privacy costs.

### Q1(d). “Happiness is a state of mind”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Happiness is a state of mind” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → inner disposition, circumstance and meaningful living · `qualifying-english/essay-happiness-state-of-mind.md`

**Model essay**

To call happiness a state of mind is to suggest that it depends not only on what happens to us but also on how we interpret and respond to experience. Two persons in similar circumstances may feel very differently: one notices deprivation, while the other values what is present. This truth gives human beings some freedom over their well-being. Yet it should not be distorted into the claim that suffering is imaginary or that material conditions do not matter.

The mind shapes happiness through attention. A person absorbed only in what is missing may remain dissatisfied despite achievement. Desire continually moves its goal: the promotion, possession or praise once expected to bring lasting joy soon becomes ordinary. Gratitude interrupts this movement by making familiar goods visible—health, friendship, useful work, beauty and security. Contentment is therefore not passivity but the ability to appreciate without constantly comparing.

Interpretation also matters in adversity. Failure can be treated as a final judgement or as information for another effort. Difficulty may reveal patience, courage and companionship that comfort would leave undeveloped. Practices such as reflection, meditation, prayer and disciplined routine can prevent every passing emotion from ruling the mind. Happiness in this sense is not endless excitement; it is a steadier capacity for balance and meaning.

Relationships are central to this state. Affection, trust and service draw attention beyond the isolated ego. Shared work or care for another person often brings deeper satisfaction than private consumption. Happiness pursued directly can become elusive, whereas it often appears as a by-product of purposeful activity, creative absorption or contribution to a community.

Nevertheless, the statement has limits. Hunger, illness, violence, discrimination and insecurity are not merely incorrect attitudes. A person denied safety or dignity cannot reasonably be instructed to think positively while injustice remains unchanged. Mental resilience may help someone endure hardship, but it does not excuse society from removing avoidable suffering. Adequate income, healthcare, education, freedom and supportive relationships create conditions in which inner well-being becomes more attainable.

Mental health also deserves care. Depression and anxiety cannot always be overcome by willpower or cheerful advice. They may require professional treatment, medication, social support and time. Describing happiness solely as a choice can burden distressed people with guilt for pain they did not choose.

At the same time, material abundance alone cannot guarantee happiness. Wealth can provide comfort and opportunity, but not automatically trust, purpose or peace. Competition may convert every gain into a new standard and every neighbour into a rival. Societies therefore need to measure progress not only through production but also through health, leisure, environmental quality, belonging and dignity.

The wisest reading of the statement combines inner agency with outer responsibility. We can train attention, moderate desire, cultivate relationships and choose meaningful action. We must also build institutions that reduce fear, deprivation and exclusion. Happiness is indeed a state of mind, but the mind lives in a body, a society and a material world.

Lasting happiness is neither a possession nor a continuous mood. It is an orientation that allows joy without clinging, sorrow without despair and ambition without permanent dissatisfaction. Circumstances influence it; character and perspective give those circumstances meaning.

**Why this earns marks:** It develops the inward thesis while qualifying it through material injustice and mental health, producing a balanced reflective argument.

<a id="2023-mains-english-q02a"></a>
### Q2(a). “What is the correlation between rights and duties?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → “What is the correlation between rights and duties?” — 15 marks · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill / slug:** Comprehension → relationship and explanation · `qualifying-english/comprehension-correlation-rights-duties.md`

Rights and duties are two sides of the same coin. Every right claimed by one person creates a corresponding duty in others to respect it, while that person must respect the equal rights of others. Citizens’ claims against the state likewise carry a responsibility to contribute useful work to society and strengthen the conditions that make those rights real. Duties therefore do not diminish rights; reciprocal performance gives rights fuller reality. Separating the two produces selfishness, anarchy and eventual social disintegration.

### Q2(d). “Elaborate the principle on which society works.” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → “Elaborate the principle on which society works.” — 15 marks · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill / slug:** Comprehension → explanation of governing principle · `qualifying-english/comprehension-social-reciprocity.md`

Society works on reciprocity: “he who takes gives and he who gives takes.” Enjoyment of one person’s right depends on others respecting it, and that person must in turn respect their equal claims. An individual right is therefore part of the common good rather than an isolated privilege. Citizens also receive enabling conditions from the state and reciprocate through useful service. If people demand freedom for themselves while leaving obligations to others, social cooperation gives way to the law of might.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official wording:** “Make a précis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give a title to it. The précis should be written in your own words:”  
**Printed passage length:** 885 words  
**Marks:** 75  
**Language skill / slug:** Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · `qualifying-english/precis-natural-and-sinful-anger.md`

**Compression plan:** Target about 295 words. Preserve the sequence: natural anger and its survival function → anger’s limited usefulness → sinful anger arising from pride, memory and symbolism → the author’s restrained expression of anger → anger at impersonal, unchangeable situations. Remove extended examples and repetition while retaining the final contrast between deliberate and habitual unpunctuality.

> **Study-only title:** *Natural Anger and the Anger of Pride*  
> **Examination instruction:** Do **not** write this or any other title in the answer booklet.

**Model précis (about 295 words; untitled examination response)**

Except for pride, every sin perverts an innocent and necessary natural impulse. Natural anger is an involuntary response to a genuine threat when escape is impossible. Its physical effects suppress fear and enable a creature to defend itself or its young; neglected young creatures also express legitimate anger. Because it answers a real danger, it ends when the danger passes. Even sinful anger can sometimes overcome laziness and provoke people into doing their best.

Anger becomes sinful when it is futile because a situation must be accepted, or unnecessary because the situation could be handled better without it. Human memory allows an offence to persist after the event, while symbolism gives events meanings beyond themselves. Pride then makes a person treat the self as the centre of the world. Sinful anger consequently answers not a threat to existence but a frustration of the belief that one’s wishes matter more than everything else. People welcome the independent existence of others only while it does not obstruct them, and prefer willing submission to forced obedience.

The author’s upbringing discouraged physical displays of anger, so his evidence comes mainly from introspection and literature. He seldom becomes angry when people deliberately oppose his wishes. Impersonal events provoke him more: an unfavourable hand at cards, unhelpful traffic lights or a crowded restaurant. Such situations displease him, cannot be changed and leave no individual to blame.

This absence of personal responsibility is decisive. Deliberate lateness causes less anger because the author may have helped create the offender’s motive and can hope to improve their relationship. Habitual unpunctuality is more infuriating because it is unrelated to him and seems alterable only if the late person becomes someone different.

**Why this earns marks:** It is approximately one-third of the printed length, follows the source’s movement from natural to sinful and personal anger, uses independent wording and keeps the examination response untitled.
