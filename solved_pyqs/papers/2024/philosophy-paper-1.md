### Q1(a). Differentiate between Plato’s and Aristotle’s conceptions of form.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Form · separation versus immanence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—Ideas; substance; form and matter · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Plato and Aristotle · Form · transcendence, immanence and individuation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `plato-aristotle-form-contrast`

**Demand decoding:** Contrast the ontological status, explanatory role and epistemic accessibility of Form, while noting Aristotle's continuity with Plato.

**Argument map:** Plato's separate universal → participation problem → Aristotle's immanent form in substance → hylomorphic explanation → individuation and pure-form qualifications.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Plato treats Forms as separate, eternal intelligible realities; Aristotle relocates form within concrete sensible substance as its essence and actuality.

For Plato, the Form of Beauty is one, immutable and independent of the many beautiful particulars that participate in or imitate it. Forms are objects of knowledge (*epistēmē*), whereas changing particulars yield opinion (*doxa*). This secures universality but makes participation obscure, as the *Parmenides*' Third Man problem shows.

For Aristotle, form is normally immanent: this individual is a compound (*synolon*) of matter and form. Form makes matter a determinate substance, explains its essence and functions as actuality relative to matter's potentiality. Hence humanity is not a separately existing entity alongside humans.

**Qualification:** Aristotle retains a Platonic priority of intelligible structure, and pure actuality is form without matter; moreover, whether substantial form is universal or individual remains disputed.

**Evaluation:** Aristotle improves explanatory contact with change and substance, but matter's role in individuation and the status of form remain difficult.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates location, function and knowledge of Form, then adds the central objection and qualification.

### Q1(b). How does Kant respond to Hume’s scepticism with regard to a priori judgments? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → synthetic a priori judgement · transcendental response to Hume · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Kant—possibility of synthetic a priori judgments; categories · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kant and Hume · necessity, causation and transcendental conditions · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Secondary route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-response-hume-a-priori`

**Demand decoding:** Explain Hume's challenge, Kant's synthetic a priori alternative and its transcendental justification; do not claim knowledge of things-in-themselves.

**Argument map:** Hume: necessity not impressed → habit → Kant: experience presupposes forms/categories → causal rule as condition of objective succession → phenomenal validity only.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant accepts Hume's point that necessity cannot be derived from repeated impressions, but argues that objective experience itself is possible only through a priori forms and categories.

Hume reduces causal expectation to custom: observation gives succession, never necessary connection. Kant's “Copernican revolution” asks not how concepts copy objects, but how objects of possible experience must conform to our cognitive conditions. Space and time are a priori forms of intuition; categories such as causality are pure concepts of understanding. In the Second Analogy, an objective event—as distinct from a reversible subjective sequence of perceptions—requires the rule that the later state follows the earlier necessarily.

Thus “every event has a cause” is synthetic because the predicate adds to the concept of event, yet a priori because it is a condition of possible experience.

**Objection and reply:** Hume may charge that Kant redescribes psychological necessity. Kant replies with a transcendental claim about objectivity, not association. However, its validity is restricted to phenomena, not things-in-themselves.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the precise Humean problem, Kant's transcendental argument and its critical limit.

### Q1(c). What arguments are offered by Moore to prove that there are certain truisms, knowledge of which is a matter of common sense? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → Moore · common-sense truisms · comparative certainty · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Moore—defence of common sense · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: G. E. Moore · truisms · knowledge, certainty and scepticism · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `moore-common-sense-truisms`

**Demand decoding:** Identify Moore's truisms and reconstruct his comparative-certainty argument, then assess whether assertion constitutes proof.

**Argument map:** ordinary propositions certainly known → philosophical premises less certain → modus ponens against scepticism/idealism → proof-of-hands → begging-question objection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Moore defends common-sense truisms less by deriving them from deeper premises than by arguing that they are more certain than philosophical arguments denying them.

In “A Defence of Common Sense,” Moore lists propositions such as: a human body now exists; it was born earlier; the Earth existed long before it; and other persons have bodies and experiences. He claims to know these and therefore know that an external world and other minds exist. His “Proof of an External World” adds: “Here is one hand; here is another”; hands are external objects; therefore external objects exist. The premises are known, distinct from the conclusion and sufficient if knowledge need not include a proof of the premises.

Moore reverses scepticism: if sceptical reasoning implies that he does not know he has hands, its premises are less credible than the truism they attack.

**Objection:** The proof may beg the question because the sceptic disputes perceptual knowledge itself. Moore establishes comparative rational confidence, not a neutral refutation.

**Evaluation:** His achievement is methodological—philosophy must not lightly overthrow ordinary certainty—rather than a complete theory of knowledge.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies both named essays, the argument and its strongest limitation.

### Q1(d). Why does later Wittgenstein think that there cannot be a language that only one person can speak — a language that is essentially private? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Later Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/later-wittgenstein.md) → private-language argument · rule-following · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Later Wittgenstein—meaning and use; language-games; critique of private language · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Wittgenstein · essentially private language · criteria and correctness · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `wittgenstein-private-language`

**Demand decoding:** Target an essentially private language, not private experience; explain why memory cannot supply a correctness criterion.

**Argument map:** meaning requires rule-governed use → rule requires correct/incorrect distinction → private ostensive definition lacks independent criterion → “seems right” collapses into “is right.”

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Later Wittgenstein does not deny private sensations; he denies that signs referring to objects knowable only to one speaker could acquire genuinely rule-governed meaning.

In *Philosophical Investigations* §§243–271, a diarist associates “S” with a recurring private sensation. An inner ostensive definition cannot fix its meaning: on later occasions the diarist consults memory, but no independent criterion distinguishes correct recollection from merely seeming correct. Where “whatever seems right is right,” talk of correctness loses its function.

Meaning is use within a language-game sustained by repeatable practices and public criteria. Sensation language is learned through natural expressions and shared circumstances—crying, injury, treatment—not by naming an occult inner object. The beetle-in-the-box analogy shows that private objects cannot determine the public use of “pain.”

**Objection and reply:** A solitary Robinson Crusoe could use a language, but only because its rules and practices are in principle checkable and derive from a social form of life; it is not essentially private.

**Evaluation:** The argument blocks private semantic foundations, though debate remains over whether normativity requires an actual community.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes privacy of experience from privacy of meaning and reconstructs the criterion argument.

### Q1(e). How does Kierkegaard define truth in terms of subjectivity? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Kierkegaard · truth as subjectivity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Kierkegaard; authentic existence · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kierkegaard · subjective truth · appropriation, passion and faith · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kierkegaard-truth-subjectivity`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the existential “how” of truth without converting Kierkegaard into a relativist who denies objective facts.

**Argument map:** objective uncertainty → inward appropriation → passionate commitment → religious faith → subjectivism objection → domain qualification.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Kierkegaard, “truth is subjectivity” means that in existential and religious matters truth must be inwardly appropriated and lived, not merely possessed as an objective proposition.

In the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, objective inquiry asks *what* is true; subjective truth concerns *how* the existing individual relates to it. A person may know Christianity's doctrines yet live without commitment, while another relates with “infinite passion” under objective uncertainty. Because existence requires choice before speculative certainty is available, faith involves risk and personal transformation.

The formula therefore does not make arithmetic or history subjective. Nor does sincerity make any belief true: the relevant relation must be sustained through responsible existence, and Kierkegaard's religious writings retain a determinate Christian object.

**Objection:** Passion may sanctify error or fanaticism. **Reply:** Kierkegaard's target is detached Hegelian system-building, not evidence in ordinary domains; subjective appropriation supplements rather than universally replaces objective truth.

**Evaluation:** The account insightfully distinguishes knowing a doctrine from existing in it, but needs objective and ethical constraints to avoid fideism.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains appropriation, uncertainty and passion while resisting the standard relativist misreading.

### Q2(a). Is rejection of Locke’s notion of primary qualities instrumental in Berkeley’s leaning towards idealism? In this context, also discuss how subjective idealism is different from the absolute idealism proposed by Hegel.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Berkeley · primary qualities · subjective idealism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Berkeley; substance and qualities; Hegel—absolute idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Berkeley, Locke and Hegel · primary/secondary quality distinction · two idealisms · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `berkeley-primary-qualities-hegel-idealism`

**Demand decoding:** Establish the argumentative role of rejecting primary qualities, reconstruct Berkeley's idealism, and compare it systematically with Hegel rather than merely naming both “idealists.”

**Argument map:** Locke's resemblance distinction → inseparability/relativity/master argument → material substance removed → ideas and spirits → subjective idealism versus dialectical Absolute → strengths and limits.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Berkeley's rejection of Locke's primary-quality doctrine is instrumental because it removes the last alleged representational bridge to mind-independent matter; yet Berkeley's immaterialism is radically different from Hegel's absolute idealism.

**From Locke to Berkeley**

Locke holds that primary qualities—extension, figure, motion, solidity and number—belong to bodies and resemble our ideas, whereas secondary qualities are powers producing colour, taste or sound. Berkeley attacks this asymmetry:

1. **Inseparability:** extension and shape are never perceived without colour or other sensible qualities.
2. **Relativity:** perceived size, motion and shape vary with perspective and conditions, as do colour and taste.
3. **Likeness principle:** an idea can resemble only another idea, not an allegedly unperceived material quality.
4. **Master argument:** attempting to conceive an unconceived sensible object only produces an idea presently conceived.

Once primary qualities lose mind-independent status, “material substance” becomes an unknowable support stripped of all content. Berkeley therefore concludes *esse est percipi* for sensible things: objects are ordered collections of ideas perceived by finite spirits and continuously sustained by God's perception.

**Berkeley and Hegel**

| Axis | Berkeley's subjective idealism | Hegel's absolute idealism |
|---|---|---|
| Basic reality | Active spirits and passive ideas | Self-developing Absolute Spirit/Idea |
| Method | Empiricist critique of abstraction and matter | Dialectical development through determinate negation |
| Objectivity | Stability guaranteed by God's ideas and laws | Subject and object are moments within one rational whole |
| Nature/history | Given order of ideas | Necessary stages of the Absolute's self-externalisation and return |
| Finite subject | Receives ideas | Is a mediated moment, not the ultimate measure of reality |

**Objections and replies:** Berkeley risks phenomenalism and dependence on a theological guarantor; he replies that ordinary objects remain real because ideas are independent of our will. Hegel avoids reducing reality to my ideas, but his dialectical necessity may absorb contingency and individuality.

**Evaluation:** Rejecting primary qualities is a decisive negative route to Berkeleyan immaterialism, though positive theses about spirits and God require further argument. Berkeley pluralises perceivers under God; Hegel comprehends finite mind and world within an internally articulated Absolute.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both clauses, gives Berkeley's actual arguments, and compares the idealisms across ontology, method and objectivity.

### Q2(b). How does Spinoza establish that God alone is absolutely real with his statement — “Whatever is, is in God”? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Spinoza · substance monism · God or Nature · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Spinoza; substance; God; determinism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Spinoza · *Deus sive Natura* · substance, attributes and modes · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `spinoza-whatever-is-in-god`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the geometrical proof from substance to monism, clarify “in” as immanence rather than spatial containment, and assess modal reality.

**Argument map:** substance self-conceived → no shared attribute substances → necessarily existing infinite substance → God → modes in God → immanent causation → acosmism/freedom objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Spinoza establishes God's exclusive absolute reality by defining substance as self-caused and self-conceived, proving that an absolutely infinite substance necessarily exists, and reducing all finite things to its modes.

In *Ethics* I, substance is “in itself and conceived through itself”; a mode is “in another and conceived through another.” Two substances sharing an attribute could not be distinguished, while substances with different attributes could not cause or explain one another. Since substance's essence involves existence, an absolutely infinite substance possessing infinite attributes necessarily exists. This is God, or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*). No second substance can exist, because it would either share an attribute with God or limit the absolutely infinite.

Hence Proposition 15: “Whatever is, is in God, and nothing can be or be conceived without God.” “In” denotes ontological dependence and immanent causation, not location. Thought and Extension are attributes known to us; minds and bodies are finite modes expressing one necessary order.

**Objections:**  
- Jacobi charges fatalism: necessity eliminates a personal creator and libertarian freedom. Spinoza replies that divine freedom means acting from one's own nature, and human freedom is adequate understanding of necessity.  
- Hegel's “acosmism” objection says finite individuality disappears in substance. Spinoza can answer that modes possess determinate reality as expressions of divine power, though not independent existence.  
- The transition from conceptual self-causation to existence may appear ontological rather than demonstrative.

**Evaluation:** The system powerfully unifies reality and rejects external creation, but God's absolute reality is secured only by demanding definitions and leaves the positive autonomy of finite persons contested.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs the proof, explains immanence and evaluates its consequences for modes and freedom.

### Q2(c). Critically examine Kant’s objections against the ontological argument for the existence of God.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → critique of ontological proof · existence not predicate · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Kant—critique of proofs for the existence of God · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kant, Anselm and Descartes · existence, concept and modality · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Secondary route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-ontological-argument-critique`

**Demand decoding:** State the proof fairly, develop Kant's predicate and modality objections, and evaluate later replies.

**Argument map:** most perfect/necessary being → existence inferred from concept → existence not real predicate → analytic denial does not posit subject → possibility cannot yield actuality → modal reply and limits.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant argues that no analysis of the concept of God can establish God's actuality, because existence does not add a determination to a concept and logical necessity cannot be converted into existential necessity.

Anselm reasons that the greatest conceivable being must exist in reality; Descartes treats existence as inseparable from a supremely perfect being, like three angles from a triangle.

Kant's objections in the *Critique of Pure Reason* are:

1. **Existence is not a real predicate.** One hundred actual thalers contain no conceptual property absent from one hundred possible thalers; actuality posits the object, not an extra perfection.
2. **Analytic necessity is conditional.** If a triangle exists, it has three angles; one may deny triangle and angles together without contradiction. Likewise, denying both God and divine predicates is not self-contradictory.
3. **Concept and instantiation differ.** Even a complete concept does not show that anything corresponds to it.
4. **“Necessary being” lacks determinate content.** We can understand necessary propositions, but cannot infer an absolutely necessary object beyond possible experience.

**Critical assessment:** Kant decisively defeats a simple predicate version of Descartes' argument. However, modern modal arguments, such as Plantinga's, treat necessary existence as a mode rather than an ordinary property: if a maximally great being is possible, it exists necessarily. Yet the disputed premise is precisely metaphysical possibility; the argument cannot establish it by definition alone. Hegel also objects that Kant separates thought and being too sharply, but this presupposes Hegel's speculative logic.

**Evaluation:** Kant shows that definition cannot manufacture actuality. Ontological reasoning may clarify what divine necessity would mean, but without an independently justified possibility premise it does not prove existence.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes Kant's several objections and tests them against the strongest modal reply.

### Q3(a). Explain Russell’s notion of incomplete symbols. Also explain how this notion leads to the doctrine of logical atomism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → Russell · incomplete symbols · logical constructions and atomism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Russell—logical atomism; logical constructions; incomplete symbols · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Bertrand Russell · contextual definition · descriptions, classes and facts · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `russell-incomplete-symbols-logical-atomism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain contextual elimination with examples, then show the methodological—not deductively automatic—connection to logical atomism.

**Argument map:** misleading grammatical unit → contextual analysis → descriptions/classes eliminated → ontological economy → atomic propositions/facts → molecular truth-functions → limits.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Russell, an incomplete symbol has no isolated meaning or denotation but contributes to the meaning of a complete proposition through contextual analysis. Eliminating such pseudo-entities supports logical atomism by revealing the simpler facts and constituents required by true propositions.

**Incomplete symbols**

Russell's paradigm is a definite description. “The present king of France is bald” does not contain a name denoting a peculiar nonexistent object. It is analysed as:

1. at least one present king of France exists;
2. at most one exists; and
3. whoever uniquely satisfies that description is bald.

Formally, \(\exists x[Kx \land \forall y(Ky \rightarrow y=x)\land Bx]\). Because the existence condition fails, the proposition is false rather than meaningless. The description disappears in analysis and therefore is an “incomplete symbol.” Classes may similarly be treated as logical fictions, and material objects as logical constructions from sense-data: “wherever possible, substitute constructions out of known entities for inferences to unknown entities.”

**Connection with logical atomism**

Logical analysis separates grammatical surface from logical form. Complex or molecular propositions are truth-functions of simpler propositions; fully analysed atomic propositions contain logically proper names and predicates and correspond, when true, to atomic facts. Incomplete-symbol analysis removes apparent constituents that do no independent referential work, reducing ontological commitment and exposing the genuine simples. Logical atomism thus combines:

- a compositional logical language;
- atomic facts as independent structures;
- complexes analysed through relations among simples; and
- metaphysical economy through logical construction.

**Objections and replies:** Strawson argues that ordinary descriptions presuppose rather than assert existence; Donnellan distinguishes attributive and referential uses. Russell can restrict his analysis to propositionally explicit attributive uses. More deeply, the move from successful analysis to absolutely simple sense-data or facts is not compelled: later philosophy questions whether final analysis exists and whether facts are mutually independent.

**Evaluation:** Incomplete symbols provide a powerful technique for dissolving pseudo-entities and clarifying inference. They motivate logical atomism's search for ultimate structure, but do not by themselves prove its metaphysics.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives formal analysis, multiple applications, the exact bridge to atomism and the main linguistic and metaphysical objections.

### Q3(b). Is the sentence “All objects are either red or not red” meaningful in the same way as “This page is white” is, according to the logical positivists? Discuss with arguments.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → analytic and empirical meaning · verification · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Logical Positivism—verification theory; linguistic theory of necessary propositions · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: logical positivists · tautology versus empirical proposition · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `positivism-tautology-empirical-meaning`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish cognitive meaningfulness from sameness of meaning-type, and explain how analytic and empirical sentences are validated.

**Argument map:** positivist bifurcation → excluded-middle tautology → no factual content → observation statement → experiential verification → objections to verification and analyticity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Logical positivists regard both sentences as cognitively meaningful, but not meaningful in the same way: the first is analytic or tautological, the second synthetic and empirically testable.

“All objects are either red or not red” has the form \(P \lor \neg P\). Its truth follows from logical syntax and the meanings of the terms; no possible observation falsifies it. It is necessary, true *a priori*, and “says nothing” about which colours objects actually possess. In Wittgenstein's and the positivists' idiom, tautologies lack factual content without being metaphysical nonsense.

“This page is white” is contingent. Its meaning lies in possible verification by observing the page under suitable conditions. Contrary experience could falsify it. It therefore conveys information about the world.

The positivist division is:

| Sentence | Status | Validation | Factual information |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red or not-red | Analytic/tautological | linguistic-logical rules | none |
| This page is white | Synthetic/empirical | observation | yes |

**Objections:** “Red” can be vague and objects may fall outside a simple classificatory scheme, though the formal sentence remains an excluded-middle instance once the predicate is fixed. Quine later attacks a principled analytic–synthetic boundary; verification itself faces problems with universal laws and theoretical entities.

**Evaluation:** Within logical positivism the sentences are equally legitimate but semantically and epistemically different. That sharp dualism is useful, yet not immune to Quinean holism.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the exact “same way” demand and links each sentence to the positivists' theory of necessity and verification.

### Q3(c). Among the rationalists, whose account of mind-body problem is compatible with the notion of human freedom and free will? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → mind–body theories · freedom · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz; mind-body dualism; determinism and freedom · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: rationalists · interactionism, parallelism, pre-established harmony · free will · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `rationalists-mind-body-freedom`

**Demand decoding:** Compare all three rationalists and defend a graded answer; distinguish libertarian from compatibilist freedom.

**Argument map:** Descartes interaction and undetermined will → Spinoza necessity/redefined freedom → Leibniz harmony and reasons without compulsion → compatibility judgement → objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Descartes most directly preserves libertarian free will; Leibniz offers the strongest rationalist compatibilism; Spinoza rejects free will in the ordinary sense and redefines freedom as understood necessity.

**Descartes:** Mind (*res cogitans*) and body (*res extensa*) are distinct substances that interact. The will is formally unlimited, and error occurs when it outruns finite understanding. This permits genuine alternative choice, but Princess Elisabeth's objection remains: how can an unextended mind causally move a body? Locating interaction at the pineal gland does not solve the categorical gap.

**Spinoza:** Mind and body are one modal event under Thought and Extension; “the order and connection of ideas” matches that of things. No cross-attribute interaction occurs, and every mode follows necessarily from God or Nature. Humans believe themselves free because they know actions but not causes. Freedom means action from adequate understanding of one's nature, not an uncaused power to do otherwise. This is coherent compatibilism but not free will as ordinarily conceived.

**Leibniz:** Monads do not causally interact; God coordinates their states through pre-established harmony. Human acts are certain within the best possible world yet, Leibniz argues, not absolutely or logically necessary. Freedom requires intelligence, spontaneity and contingency: motives incline without necessitating. This preserves rational agency better than Spinoza while avoiding Cartesian interaction.

**Critical assessment:** Leibniz's position is most compatible with a reason-responsive account of freedom. Yet divine foreknowledge, complete individual concepts and the principle of sufficient reason make alternatives appear merely possible in other worlds, not open to this agent. Descartes better preserves libertarian choice but at a metaphysical cost; Spinoza offers ethical self-determination by changing the concept.

**Evaluation:** The answer depends on the criterion: Descartes for libertarian free will, Leibniz for compatibilist freedom, Spinoza for autonomy as adequate necessity.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares every rationalist and gives a criterion-sensitive verdict rather than a one-name assertion.

### Q4(a). What do the existentialist thinkers mean by the slogan “existence precedes essence”? How is human existence related to human freedom according to them? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → existence before essence · freedom · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks (10+10) · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—existence and essence; choice, responsibility and authentic existence · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Kierkegaard, Heidegger and Sartre · existence, facticity and freedom · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `existence-precedes-essence-freedom`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the slogan's Sartrean meaning, distinguish the existentialist family, then connect existence to situated freedom, responsibility and authenticity.

**Argument map:** no predetermined human blueprint → self-definition through choice → consciousness transcends facticity → responsibility/anguish → bad faith/authenticity → Heideggerian and Kierkegaardian qualifications.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** “Existence precedes essence” means that the human being first exists as a situated agent and only thereafter becomes a determinate self through choices; therefore freedom is constitutive of human existence, though never freedom from all facticity.

**Meaning of the slogan**

Sartre contrasts a paper-knife, designed according to a prior concept, with humanity in an atheistic world. If there is no creator who possesses a human blueprint, no fixed universal nature predetermines what each person must become. Consciousness or being-for-itself (*pour-soi*) is not a completed thing: through nothingness it distances itself from what it already is and projects possibilities.

The slogan has analogues rather than identical formulations across existentialism:

- **Kierkegaard:** the existing individual cannot be exhausted by an abstract Hegelian system; the self becomes itself through passionate ethical and religious choice.
- **Heidegger:** “the essence of Dasein lies in its existence”; Dasein is being-in-the-world, thrown into inherited possibilities and projecting itself toward death.
- **Sartre:** no given essence excuses choice; even refusal to choose is a choice.

**Existence and freedom**

Freedom is the for-itself's capacity to transcend facticity—body, past, social position and circumstance—by assigning it meaning and pursuing possibilities. Sartre therefore says humans are “condemned to be free.” Choice produces anguish because, lacking a ready-made moral law, the agent takes responsibility for a picture of humanity. **Bad faith** denies this structure by pretending to be merely a thing or a role, as in Sartre's waiter; authenticity owns both freedom and situation.

Freedom is not omnipotence. A prisoner cannot simply remove walls, but can interpret, resist, submit or seek escape within a field structured by obstacles. Heidegger similarly understands authentic freedom through resolute appropriation of thrown possibilities, not arbitrary willing.

**Objections and replies:** The slogan may neglect biological, social and unconscious determinants. Beauvoir's distinction between freedom and concrete power, and Sartre's later attention to situation, qualify radical voluntarism. Kierkegaard and Heidegger also deny that freedom is groundless consumer choice: it is anxiety-laden commitment before God or finite being-toward-death.

**Evaluation:** Existentialism rightly makes persons responsible self-interpreting projects, but freedom is convincing only as situated transcendence, not creation of self from nothing.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately fulfils the 10+10 demands, compares thinkers and qualifies freedom through facticity.

### Q4(b). Why does Husserl think that essences exhibit a kind of continuity between consciousness and being? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → essence · intentional correlation · eidetic intuition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Phenomenology (Husserl)—method; theory of essences; avoidance of psychologism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Husserl · noesis–noema correlation · ideal essence and being · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology—Husserl](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `husserl-essence-consciousness-being`

**Demand decoding:** Explain continuity through intentional correlation, not by reducing being to mental content; connect epoché, eidetic variation and anti-psychologism.

**Argument map:** intentionality → object as intended → epoché suspends existential thesis → eidetic variation discloses invariant → correlational a priori joins act and object → idealism/solipsism objection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Husserl finds continuity between consciousness and being because every kind of objectivity has a corresponding mode of givenness, and phenomenology can disclose the invariant essence governing both the intentional act and its intended object.

Consciousness is intentional—always consciousness *of* something. The epoché brackets the natural attitude's unexamined existential commitment without denying the world. Phenomenological reduction then studies the correlation between **noesis**, the act of intending, and **noema**, the object precisely as intended. Through imaginative or eidetic variation, accidental features are varied until an invariant structure appears: for example, a spatial thing must be perspectivally given through profiles while intended as one object.

Essence (*eidos*) is neither a private image nor an empirical generalisation. It is an ideal possibility grasped in eidetic intuition. Husserl's **correlational a priori** means that kinds of being and kinds of consciousness belong together: perceived object/perceiving, remembered object/remembering, number/categorial intuition. Thus consciousness does not create arbitrary being; being is intelligible through lawful modes of manifestation.

This continuity also avoids psychologism. Logical and essential truths are not facts about changing mental episodes, although they become evident in acts of consciousness.

**Objection:** Transcendental idealism may seem to imprison being within consciousness and create solipsism. Husserl replies through horizon, intersubjective constitution and the distinction between the object and any single appearance; yet the status of genuinely consciousness-independent being remains contested.

**Evaluation:** The doctrine illuminates how objectivity is accessible without naïve realism, but continuity becomes reduction only if constitution is mistaken for causal creation.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects intentionality, reduction, eidetic method, anti-psychologism and the idealist objection.

### Q4(c). Explain the nature of the two dogmas that Quine refers to in his paper ‘Two Dogmas of Empiricism’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → Quine · analyticity and reductionism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Quine—critique of empiricism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: W. V. O. Quine · analytic–synthetic distinction; reductionism; confirmation holism · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `quine-two-dogmas`

**Demand decoding:** Identify and explain both dogmas, show Quine's arguments against each and state the holistic consequence without saying every belief is equally revisable.

**Argument map:** analytic/synthetic distinction → failed definitions via synonymy/necessity → reductionism → no sentence has isolated experiential equivalent → web of belief → pragmatic revisability.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Quine's two dogmas are the alleged sharp distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, and reductionism—the belief that every meaningful statement has a unique translation into immediate experience. Rejecting both yields confirmation holism.

**First dogma: analyticity.** Empiricists call a statement analytic when true by meaning alone, such as “No bachelor is married.” Quine argues that attempts to explain analyticity are circular:

- “Bachelor” and “unmarried man” require unexplained synonymy.
- Synonymy defined by interchangeability fails in opaque contexts and depends on necessity.
- Semantic rules explain analyticity only within stipulated artificial languages and do not non-circularly ground ordinary-language synonymy.

He does not deny obvious sameness of meaning; he denies a philosophically sharp boundary adequate to divide all truths.

**Second dogma: reductionism.** Radical verificationism supposes that each statement has its own experiential confirmation conditions. Quine argues that experience tests not isolated sentences but networks of theory plus auxiliary assumptions. Failed prediction leaves choices about where revision should occur.

Together the dogmas sustain one another: isolated empirical content would distinguish factual contribution from linguistic truth; analyticity would protect some sentences from revision. Once abandoned, knowledge becomes a **web of belief** facing the “tribunal of experience” as a corporate body. Even logic is in principle revisable, but central beliefs are retained because changing them is costly; revision is constrained by simplicity, conservatism and predictive success.

**Objection:** Grice and Strawson argue that inability to define analyticity does not show the distinction unreal. Quine's challenge nevertheless exposes the inadequacy of classical empiricist explanations.

**Evaluation:** The essay replaces atomistic verification with pragmatic holism, without entailing that evidence permits arbitrary theory choice.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both dogmas, their interdependence and the qualified holistic result.

**Argument map:** six mental qualities → qualities require substance → body/senses/manas cannot unify them → enduring self inferred → Buddhist objection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya infers an enduring self (*ātman*) as the common substratum of six qualities listed in *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.10.

1. **Desire (*icchā*):** wanting an object presupposes a subject connecting present pursuit with prior experience.  
2. **Aversion (*dveṣa*):** avoidance belongs to one who recalls pain.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives exactly the canonical six, their inference and the principal rival explanation.

### Q5(c). Do these two sentences “Air does not have heat” and “Air is not fire” refer to the same type of absence or abhāva, according to the Vaiśeṣikas? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → absence (*abhāva*) · relational versus absolute difference · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—theory of categories · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Vaiśeṣika · reciprocal absence and absolute absence · qualifier/qualified structure · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `vaisesika-air-heat-fire-absence`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the counter-positive and locus in each judgement and distinguish absence of a property from numerical/type difference.

**Argument map:** heat absent in air → absolute absence in locus → air not fire → reciprocal absence/identity-negation → not same.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The sentences express different absences because their counter-positives (*pratiyogin*) and relational structures differ.

“Air does not have heat” predicates the absence of heat in air. Heat can exist elsewhere—in fire—but is absent from this locus. This is **absolute absence (*atyantābhāva*)** of the property heat in air: the counter-positive is heat and the locus (*anuyogin*) is air.

“Air is not fire” does not merely say that a quality is missing. It denies identity between two positive entities: air is mutually exclusive from fire, and fire from air. This is **reciprocal absence (*anyonyābhāva*)**, expressed as “A is not B.” Its basis is difference (*bheda*), not the non-occurrence of one quality in a locus.

The distinction matters because absence is relationally individuated by counter-positive, locus and delimiting relation. Even if fire is characteristically hot, “not having heat” does not logically equal “not being fire”: other hot entities exist, and a thing may lack a property without being defined through reciprocal difference from its possessor.

**Evaluation:** Vaiśeṣika's taxonomy captures two logical forms—property-negation and identity-negation—though critics may analyse both linguistically without reifying absence as a category.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies both exact *abhāvas* and explains why the propositions are not equivalent.

**Argument map:** Bhāṭṭa: independent denotation then connection → Prābhākara: words denote only connected meanings → compositionality versus contextual immediacy.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā derives sentence meaning after words independently denote their meanings; Prābhākara holds that words directly signify meanings already syntactically connected.

For Kumārila's **theory of connection after denotation (*abhihitānvayavāda*)**, in “Bring the cow,” “bring” and “cow” first denote action and object separately. Prābhākara captures context and action-oriented language, but may struggle to explain how recurrent word-identity is recognised across different connections.

**Evaluation:** The dispute anticipates compositional versus contextual semantics; a defensible synthesis retains lexical contribution while making sentential relation constitutive of actual assertion.

**Why this earns marks:** It contrasts the two causal accounts with terminology, example and reciprocal objections.

### Q6(a). Differentiate between the Cārvākas’ refutation of self as a transcendental category and the Buddhist rejection of ātmā.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Cārvāka](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/carvaka.md) → embodied self versus Buddhist no-self · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Cārvāka—rejection of transcendent entities; Buddhism—no-self (*nairātmyavāda*) · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Cārvāka and Buddhism · material embodiment, aggregates and continuity · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Cārvāka](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Carvaka.md) · **Secondary route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `carvaka-buddhist-self-rejection`

**Demand decoding:** Compare grounds, positive ontology, continuity, ethics and liberation; do not equate denial of a transcendent self with denial of persons.

**Argument map:** common rejection of eternal substance → Cārvāka body-consciousness emergence → Buddhist five aggregates/dependent origination → different continuity and soteriology → objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Both schools reject an eternal, independent self beyond experience, but Cārvāka identifies the person with the living material body, whereas Buddhism deconstructs both bodily and mental constituents into a conditioned, impermanent stream without owner.

**Cārvāka**

Cārvāka's epistemic starting point is perception (*pratyakṣa*). A transcendent self is imperceptible, and inference to it depends on an uncertain universal relation. Consciousness is a property emerging from the organised elements—like intoxicating power from fermented materials—and ordinary “I” refers to the embodied person. With bodily dissolution consciousness ends; karma, rebirth and liberation as post-mortem release are rejected.

**Buddhism**

Buddhism analyses the person into five aggregates (*pañca-skandha*): form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. None is permanent, fully controllable or fit to be called “this is mine, this I am, this is my self.” Dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) explains a causal series without an unchanging substratum. Rebirth and karmic responsibility continue through causal conditioning—neither strict identity nor total difference—illustrated by flame-to-flame continuity. No-self (*anātman/nairātmya*) is soteriological: ending appropriation weakens craving and suffering.

| Axis | Cārvāka | Buddhism |
|---|---|---|
| Positive account | body is the person; consciousness emerges | aggregate-process without owner |
| Method | perception-centred materialism | experiential analysis and dependent origination |
| Continuity after death | denied | causal rebirth without transmigrating self |
| Ethics/liberation | this-worldly prudence; no nirvāṇa | karma, Noble Path and nirvāṇa |
| Ontology | elements materially real | schools vary: dharma realism to emptiness |

**Objections and replies:** Nyāya argues that memory and recognition require one enduring knower. Cārvāka can appeal to bodily continuity; Buddhism invokes causal continuity and, in Yogācāra, store-consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*). Critics reply that causal resemblance may not explain ownership or desert.

**Evaluation:** Cārvāka rejects a self *beyond the body*; Buddhism rejects substantial selfhood *within the entire psycho-physical person*. Their conclusions overlap negatively but diverge in ontology, rebirth and emancipatory purpose.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates grounds and consequences across five dimensions and avoids the common “both simply deny self” error.

### Q6(b). How do the two schools of Buddhism arrive at two opposed conclusions, namely “everything is void” and “everything is real” from the same doctrine of Pratītyasamutpāda? Answer with arguments.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → dependent origination · Madhyamaka and Sarvāstivāda · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Buddhism—dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Madhyamaka and Sarvāstivāda · emptiness versus dharma realism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `buddhism-dependent-origination-void-real`

**Demand decoding:** Identify defensible school referents, derive both conclusions from conditionality, and qualify the slogans through two truths and momentary/tri-temporal dharmas.

**Argument map:** conditioned arising → no independent essence → Madhyamaka emptiness; conditioned efficacy → Sarvāstivāda dharma reality → opposed ontologies, shared anti-self doctrine.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The same formula—“when this exists, that arises”—can emphasise either the absence of independent self-nature or the causal efficacy of conditioned factors. Madhyamaka draws the first conclusion; realist Buddhism, especially Vaibhāṣika, draws the second.

**Madhyamaka: “everything is void.”** Nāgārjuna argues in *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.18 that whatever is dependently originated is emptiness (*śūnyatā*). If a thing possessed intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*), it would exist from itself and could neither arise through conditions nor change. Therefore persons and dharmas are empty of independent essence. This is not nihilism: conventionally, causes, language and suffering function; ultimately, nothing is self-grounding. Emptiness itself is also empty.

**Vaibhāṣika/Sarvāstivāda: “everything is real.”** Vaibhāṣikas analyse apparent wholes into causally efficacious dharmas. Dependent origination requires determinate factors with own-character (*svalakṣaṇa*) standing in lawful causal relations. The Sarvāstivāda thesis “all exists” (*sarvam asti*) affirms dharmas of past, present and future in differentiated modes, while present dharmas possess distinctive causal efficacy. Sautrāntika also treats conditioned events and an external causal order as real, though it rejects direct perception of external objects. Composite persons are conceptual, but causally operative dharmas are not sheer fictions.

**Critical relation:** Both reject an eternal self and arbitrary creation. Madhyamaka argues that realist dharmas recreate substance at a microscopic level and make causation impossible; Sarvāstivāda replies that without real causal factors emptiness threatens to destroy explanation. Madhyamaka answers through the two truths: conventional dependent efficacy does not require intrinsic being.

**Evaluation:** The opposition arises from two readings of dependence—ontological non-self-sufficiency and explanatory causal efficacy. Madhyamaka is more consistent against substance; realism offers a more determinate causal ontology.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives, rather than merely states, both conclusions and prevents “void” from becoming nihilism.

**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `jaina-bhavabandha-dravyabandha`

**Demand decoding:** Define both, explain their causal sequence and reciprocal reinforcement, and connect them to stoppage and shedding of karma.

**Thesis:** Jaina bondage has an internal dispositional aspect, *bhāvabandha*, and an objective material aspect, *dravyabandha*; liberation requires removing both.

**Psychic bondage (*bhāvabandha*)** is the soul's impure state produced by delusion, passions (*kaṣāyas*: anger, pride, deceit and greed) and activities of mind, speech and body (*yoga*). The soul identifies with attachment and aversion, creating the condition for karmic influx (*āsrava*).

**Material bondage (*dravyabandha*)** is the actual association of subtle karmic matter (*karma-pudgala*) with the soul. Incoming particles are bound and classified by:

- kind (*prakṛti*);
- duration (*sthiti*);
- intensity (*anubhāga/rasa*); and
- quantity (*pradeśa*).

The relation is reciprocal: impure dispositions attract and determine karmic matter; bound karma obscures knowledge, perception and energy and conditions further passions. Yet the two are not identical—one is a modification of the conscious soul, the other a real material adhesion in Jaina pluralistic realism.

**Soteriology:** Stoppage (*saṃvara*) prevents new influx through restraint and vigilance; shedding (*nirjarā*) exhausts existing karma through austerity and right conduct, supported by right faith and knowledge.

**Objection and reply:** Material karma may seem to spatialise moral causation. Jainism replies that the soul is embodied and coextensive with its body, making interaction possible within its ontology, though the mechanism remains metaphysically demanding.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes the two bondages, links them causally and derives the corresponding path of release.

### Q7(a). Present an account of evolution of Prakṛti as propounded in Sāṃkhyakārikā. In this context, also explain the difference between buddhi, mahat and ahaṃkāra.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sāṃkhya](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/samkhya.md) → evolution of *prakṛti* · *mahat/buddhi/ahaṃkāra* · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks (10+10) · Syllabus unit: Sāṃkhya—*prakṛti*; *puruṣa*; causation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Īśvarakṛṣṇa · twenty-five principles · intellectual and ego functions · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `samkhya-prakrti-evolution-buddhi-mahat-ahamkara`

**Demand decoding:** Give the ordered twenty-five-principle evolution and devote equal attention to the precise identity/difference of *mahat*, *buddhi* and *ahaṃkāra*.

**Argument map:** guṇa equilibrium disturbed near *puruṣa* → *mahat/buddhi* → *ahaṃkāra* → eleven faculties plus subtle elements → gross elements → functional distinctions → liberation reverses misidentification.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The *Sāṃkhyakārikā* presents evolution as real manifestation (*pariṇāma*) of primordial material nature (*prakṛti*) under the changing dominance of the three *guṇas*, for the experience and liberation of conscious *puruṣa*.

**Evolutionary sequence**

Unmanifest *prakṛti* is the equilibrium of luminosity (*sattva*), activity (*rajas*) and inertia (*tamas*). It is inferred from effects under *satkāryavāda*: the effect pre-exists in its material cause. In the presence of *puruṣa*, equilibrium is disturbed:

```text
prakṛti
  ↓
mahat = buddhi
  ↓
ahaṃkāra
  ├─ sāttvika, activated by rajas → manas + 5 cognitive senses + 5 action faculties
  └─ tāmasa, activated by rajas   → 5 subtle elements (tanmātras)
                                      ↓
                                  5 gross elements (mahābhūtas)
```

Together with *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, these form twenty-five principles. Evolution is not creation from nothing and not consciously planned by *puruṣa*, which remains inactive witness. Classical analogies compare *prakṛti* to milk serving the calf or a dancer performing for a spectator.

**Mahat, buddhi and ahaṃkāra**

- **Mahat** (“great”) and **buddhi** (“intellect/determination”) are not two successive principles. They name the same first evolute from cosmic and functional perspectives. *Mahat* stresses its universal/cosmic priority; *buddhi* stresses ascertainment, judgement, virtue, knowledge, detachment and their opposites.
- **Ahaṃkāra** is the next evolute: ego-making or appropriation—“I” and “mine.” It differentiates the integrated determination of *buddhi* into subject-object organisation and generates the sensory-motor and elemental branches.
- *Buddhi* can reflect consciousness because of *sattva* and enables discriminative knowledge; *ahaṃkāra* misappropriates the activities of *prakṛti* as belonging to *puruṣa*.

**Objections and replies:** An unconscious *prakṛti* appears unable to pursue purposive evolution; Sāṃkhya invokes inherent *guṇa* teleology in the mere presence of *puruṣa*. The unexplained contact of inactive consciousness and unconscious nature remains a serious problem. Advaita also asks why one *prakṛti* and many *puruṣas* are required.

**Evaluation:** The system offers a highly ordered psycho-cosmology and preserves causal continuity, but its dualism makes the initiation and coordination of evolution obscure.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the 10+10 allocation, gives the full sequence and explicitly corrects the trap that *mahat* and *buddhi* are separate stages.

### Q7(b). “So long as there are changes and modifications in citta, the self is reflected therein, and, in the absence of discriminative knowledge, identifies itself with them.” Present an appraisal of Yoga Soteriology in the light of the above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Yoga](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/yoga.md) → *citta-vṛtti* · misidentification · discriminative knowledge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Yoga—*citta*; *cittavṛtti*; afflictions; *samādhi*; *kaivalya* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Patañjali · reflection, restraint and liberation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Yoga](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Yoga.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `yoga-citta-reflection-soteriology`

**Demand decoding:** Use the quotation as the causal key to bondage and liberation; appraise, rather than merely list, eight limbs.

**Argument map:** pure *puruṣa* reflected in mutable *citta* → ignorance and appropriation → affliction/action/rebirth → restraint and purification → discriminative discernment → *kaivalya* → dualist objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Yoga soteriology treats bondage as cognitive misidentification, not a real modification of *puruṣa*: consciousness appears to think and suffer because its reflection is appropriated through the modifications of *citta*.

Yoga defines itself as restraint of mental modifications (*yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*, *Yogasūtra* 1.2). *Citta*—functionally comprising intellect, ego and mind—is material but transparent when *sattva* predominates. Pure *puruṣa* illuminates it; through ignorance (*avidyā*) their conjunction (*saṃyoga*) makes the seer identify with valid cognition, error, imagination, sleep and memory. This yields egoity, attachment, aversion and fear of death—the five afflictions (*kleśas*)—and thus karmic bondage.

Liberation reverses the process:

1. ethical and bodily disciplines purify conduct;
2. withdrawal, concentration and meditation steady *citta*;
3. *samādhi* makes it transparent and eventually seedless;
4. uninterrupted discriminative discernment (*vivekakhyāti*) distinguishes *puruṣa* from every *guṇa*-product;
5. when *prakṛti* has fulfilled its purpose, the *guṇas* recede and *puruṣa* abides in isolation (*kaivalya*).

**Appraisal:** Yoga insightfully connects suffering with identification and offers a disciplined psychology rather than mere theory. Yet if *puruṣa* is inactive and untouched, explaining how it is “bound,” reflected or liberated is difficult. A Buddhist may explain mental change without an eternal witness; Advaita may regard plural isolated selves as incomplete. Yoga replies that bondage is only misattribution in *citta*, while *puruṣa* never changes.

**Evaluation:** Its practical account of deconditioning is powerful, but its success as metaphysics depends on the contested distinction between material mind and contentless consciousness.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives the whole soteriological path from the quoted reflection model and weighs its core dualist problem.

### Q7(c). “Our Yoga is a double movement of ascent and descent.” Discuss the above statement in the context of Sri Aurobindo’s conception of Integral Yoga.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sri Aurobindo](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/sri-aurobindo.md) → Integral Yoga · ascent and descent · transformation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Aurobindo—evolution; involution; Integral Yoga · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sri Aurobindo · psychic/spiritual ascent and supramental descent · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Aurobindo](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Aurobindo.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aurobindo-integral-yoga-ascent-descent`

**Demand decoding:** Explain both movements, their metaphysical basis and why liberation alone is insufficient; distinguish Supermind from ordinary higher experience.

**Argument map:** involution makes evolution possible → aspiration/surrender → ascent beyond mind → descent of higher consciousness → psychic/spiritual/supramental transformation → collective and bodily aim → critique.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Integral Yoga is “double” because human consciousness must ascend from its mental-vital-physical limits toward the Divine, while higher consciousness must descend to transform those lower instruments; escape without transformation is incomplete.

Aurobindo's metaphysics links **involution** and **evolution**. Consciousness is concealed in matter and progressively manifests as life and mind; therefore a further supramental emergence is possible. In the ascending movement, aspiration, rejection of lower impulses and surrender awaken the psychic being and open consciousness beyond ego and ordinary mind to spiritual silence, universality and higher planes.

Ascent alone may yield liberation above life. Integral Yoga therefore requires descent: peace, force, light and ultimately supramental truth-consciousness enter mind, life and body. Aurobindo and the Mother describe three transformations:

1. **psychic transformation**—inner soul governs the nature;
2. **spiritual transformation**—peace, wideness and higher consciousness;
3. **supramental transformation**—division and ignorance are replaced by integral knowledge, including transformation of embodied life.

The movements are reciprocal: ascent creates receptivity; descent supplies transformative power. The goal is divine life, not Patañjali's isolation of *puruṣa* from *prakṛti* or a merely world-negating release.

**Objection:** Supramental descent and bodily transformation lack public verification and may turn evolution into speculative teleology. Aurobindo replies that Yoga is experiential and evolutionary possibility exceeds present achievement, but the evidential challenge remains.

**Evaluation:** The doctrine distinctively integrates transcendence with terrestrial transformation; its philosophical strength is anti-escapism, its weakness the unverifiable scale of its final claim.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains mechanism, stages, metaphysical basis and the principal evidential objection.

### Q8(a). How do I know that I know? Answer this question with reference to the Naiyāyikas, the Bhaṭṭa Mīmāṃsakas and the Prābhākaras.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → cognition of cognition · self-luminosity and higher-order awareness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā—theory of knowledge; Nyāya—*pramāṇa* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nyāya, Bhāṭṭa and Prābhākara · *anuvyavasāya*, knownness and threefold awareness · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `indian-cognition-of-cognition`

**Demand decoding:** Compare three answers to awareness of cognition and keep the issue distinct from the separate debate over intrinsic/extrinsic validity.

**Argument map:** Nyāya first cognition then apperception → Bhāṭṭa cognition inferred from produced knownness → Prābhākara tripartite self-manifestation → regress/objectification tests → qualified comparison.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya knows cognition through a later higher-order cognition; Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā infers it from the object's acquired knownness; Prābhākara holds that cognition reveals object, subject and itself in one episode.

**Nyāya: subsequent apperception (*anuvyavasāya*)**

The first cognition (*vyavasāya*) is object-directed: “This is a pot.” A following mental perception, “I know the pot,” takes the first cognition as its object. Thus cognition is not self-luminous; it is a quality of the self, contacted by the internal sense (*manas*). Nyāya avoids infinite regress because knowing every higher-order cognition is not required for ordinary knowledge. Validity itself is extrinsically known through successful activity or defect-detection.

**Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā: inference from knownness (*jñātata*)**

Kumārila also denies that cognition directly manifests itself. Cognition produces in the object a relational feature—“being-known” (*jñātata* or *prākaṭya*). Seeing the pot as known, the subject infers the preceding cognition: this object possesses knownness; such knownness is produced by cognition; therefore cognition occurred. Bhāṭṭa can still defend intrinsic validity (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*): a cognition is presumed valid until defeating conditions appear, although awareness of the cognitive episode is inferential.

**Prābhākara: threefold awareness (*tripuṭī-saṃvit*)**

Every cognition simultaneously manifests object, knowing subject and itself: “I know the pot.” Cognition is self-luminous (*svaprakāśa*) and does not need a second cognition or an inferred product. This blocks regress and explains immediate first-person awareness. Error concerns mispresentation of relations, not cognition's failure to reveal itself.

| School | How cognition is known | Main strength | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nyāya | later mental perception | realist higher-order model | temporal gap/regress worry |
| Bhāṭṭa | inference from knownness | preserves non-self-luminous cognition | “knownness” seems obscure |
| Prābhākara | cognition self-manifests in a triad | immediacy and regress avoidance | self-objectification puzzle |

**Evaluation:** Prābhākara best captures immediate “mineness” of awareness; Nyāya offers the clearest reflective analysis; Bhāṭṭa mediates realism and intrinsic presumption but at the cost of a controversial property in the object.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives all three mechanisms, separates luminosity from validity and evaluates their explanatory trade-offs.

### Q8(b). “A candidate who is never seen to be studying during the day time secures a high position in a competitive exam.” How would the Bhaṭṭa Mīmāṃsakas and the Naiyāyikas explain the success of this candidate? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → postulation (*arthāpatti*) versus inference · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā—theory of knowledge; Nyāya—theory of *pramāṇa* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Bhāṭṭa and Nyāya · *arthāpatti* reducibility · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `arthapatti-candidate-night-study`

**Demand decoding:** Apply each school's epistemology to the same facts; explain why Bhāṭṭa claims irreducibility and how Nyāya constructs an inference.

**Argument map:** observed success + non-observation of day study → explanatory conflict → night study postulated → Bhāṭṭa independent *pramāṇa* → Nyāya inference from causal regularity → underdetermination objection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā treats the candidate's unseen night study as knowledge by independent postulation (*arthāpatti*); Nyāya reconstructs the same conclusion as inference (*anumāna*).

For the **Bhāṭṭa**, two accepted facts conflict unless a new fact is supposed:

1. the candidate achieved a high rank, ordinarily requiring preparation;
2. the candidate was not seen studying by day.

To make them jointly intelligible, one postulates: “The candidate studies at night.” This resembles the classical Devadatta example—he is stout but does not eat by day, therefore he eats at night. The cognition is not perception, testimony or memory. Nor, Bhāṭṭa argues, is it inference, because the subject need not first know and formulate a universal concomitance between rank and night study; explanatory necessity directly generates the supposition.

The **Naiyāyika** denies an extra *pramāṇa*. The reasoning can be rendered: whoever obtains such a rank must have an adequate cause such as study; this candidate obtained it; no day study occurred; therefore the relevant study occurred at another time, plausibly at night. Nyāya thus absorbs postulation into inference, supported by causal regularity and elimination of alternatives.

**Critical issue:** The conclusion “night study” is not strictly necessary: prior preparation, tutoring, exceptional aptitude or observation gaps are alternatives. Bhāṭṭa must specify that the postulate is the only explanation compatible with all accepted facts; Nyāya must establish a non-deviant *vyāpti*.

**Evaluation:** Bhāṭṭa captures explanatory inference to the best/necessary explanation; Nyāya offers logical economy. In this loose example, both yield probability unless alternatives are explicitly excluded.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies both theories and critically tests whether the stated facts justify certainty.

### Q8(c). On what grounds do the Prābhākaras and the Naiyāyikas reject memory as a source of knowledge? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → memory and novelty · *pramāṇa* · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya—theory of *pramāṇa* · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Prābhākara and Nyāya · memory (*smṛti*) · non-novel cognition · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `prabhakara-nyaya-memory-not-pramana`

**Demand decoding:** State each school's criteria for valid knowledge and common ground; distinguish causal usefulness from independent presentative knowledge.

**Argument map:** memory arises from trace of prior cognition → presents no new object → depends on previous *pramāṇa* → Prābhākara novelty/non-memory criterion → Nyāya non-deviant fresh cognition criterion → counterexamples and reply.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Both Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya reject memory (*smṛti*) as an independent source of valid knowledge because it reproduces previously experienced content through latent impressions and does not freshly disclose an object.

For **Prābhākara**, valid cognition is intrinsically manifest and, crucially, apprehends something not already apprehended (*anadhigata*). Memory arises from a residual trace (*saṃskāra*) left by an earlier experience. “I saw this temple” revives prior content rather than presenting the object anew; therefore it is neither a fresh *pramā* nor generated by an independent *pramāṇa*. This does not make memory false: it may faithfully preserve prior valid cognition.

For **Nyāya**, a valid cognition (*pramā*) is a true, non-deviant, fresh apprehension produced by an appropriate instrument. Memory is caused principally by a trace, not by present contact between a *pramāṇa* and its object. It is dependent on earlier cognition and is not presentative. Nyāya therefore distinguishes:

- **experience (*anubhava*)**, which may be valid or erroneous and can be produced by a *pramāṇa*; and
- **memory**, which may be correct or incorrect but does not independently establish its object.

**Ground shared:** A causal aid to knowledge is not thereby a source of new knowledge. Memory can assist recognition, inference, testimony and action without becoming a separate *pramāṇa*.

**Objection:** Remembering a forgotten fact appears informative now. The reply is that psychological novelty to the present moment differs from epistemic novelty: its warrant remains inherited from the original cognition. False memory also shows that mere seeming recollection cannot certify itself.

**Difference:** Prābhākara embeds rejection within self-luminous cognition and the novelty criterion; Nyāya embeds it within causal realism and externally ascertainable validity.

**Evaluation:** Their exclusion preserves a disciplined account of epistemic sources, though contemporary theories may treat reliable memory as preserving justification even if it does not generate it.

**Why this earns marks:** It states common and school-specific grounds, preserves memory's practical role and answers the apparent-new-information objection.
