### Q1(a). “Paper books are more attractive than their electronic formats.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Paper books are more attractive than their electronic formats.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Argumentative essay → Reading media, attention and accessibility  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-paper-books-and-electronic-formats.md`

**Model essay**

The arrival of electronic books has transformed reading. A reader can carry a library on one device, search a text within seconds and obtain a new title without visiting a shop. Yet paper books remain more attractive to many people. Their appeal is not merely nostalgic. It arises from the distinctive way in which a physical book engages attention, memory, ownership and the senses.

A paper book is a tangible object. Its weight, cover, typeface and texture give it an identity that a file displayed on a uniform screen often lacks. Readers remember where a passage appeared—near the beginning, on a left-hand page or beside a marked paragraph. Such spatial cues can assist recall. Turning pages also gives visible form to progress: the unread pages diminish while the completed portion grows.

Paper may encourage deeper attention. A printed page normally offers only the text before the reader, whereas a connected device also offers messages, links and notifications. These distractions do not make serious electronic reading impossible, but they require greater self-discipline. For long works, many readers find that paper reduces eye fatigue and supports slower reflection. Underlining, annotating margins and returning to an earlier page can feel direct and natural.

Books also carry emotional and social value. A volume received as a gift preserves the memory of the giver. An old book may contain a parent’s notes or bear the signs of repeated reading. Shelves reveal interests and invite conversation. Libraries and bookshops create public spaces in which accidental discovery remains possible. An algorithm recommends what resembles earlier choices; wandering among shelves may introduce a reader to what was never sought.

However, attractiveness must not be confused with universal superiority. Electronic formats have major advantages. They are portable, quickly distributed and often cheaper to store. Adjustable type, screen readers and dictionaries improve access for many readers with disabilities or limited proficiency. Students can search large documents, researchers can retrieve references rapidly, and people in remote places can obtain texts unavailable locally. Digital publication can also spare printing and transport, although devices, electricity and electronic waste carry environmental costs of their own.

Paper has limitations too. Printed books occupy space, deteriorate, cost money to transport and require physical resources. Large-print or specialised editions may be unavailable. For a traveller, carrying several volumes is inconvenient. Thus, the preference for paper partly depends on purpose. A searchable manual, frequently updated report or temporary reference may work better electronically; a novel, poetry collection or demanding philosophical work may benefit from the calm continuity of print.

The two formats need not be enemies. Many readers move between them: they discover or sample a book online, read a printed copy closely and store reference material digitally. Schools and libraries should preserve this flexibility rather than assuming that technological novelty automatically renders an older medium obsolete.

Paper books are more attractive because they turn reading into an embodied and memorable encounter. Their attraction lies in focused attention, physical presence and personal association. Electronic formats extend access and convenience, but they do not cancel those qualities. The wisest judgement is therefore not that every paper book is better than every electronic one, but that print continues to satisfy important human needs which efficiency alone cannot measure.

**Why this earns marks:** It supports the proposition without romanticising print, compares both media across attention, memory, access and sustainability, and reaches a qualified judgement.

### Q1(b). “Ethics in sports.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Ethics in sports.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Analytical essay → Fairness, integrity and responsibility in sport  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-ethics-in-sports.md`

**Model essay**

Sport is admired because ability, discipline and courage are tested under agreed rules. The excitement of competition depends upon uncertainty, but its legitimacy depends upon trust. If spectators believe that results are bought, chemically manufactured or deliberately manipulated, victory loses meaning. Ethics is therefore not an ornament added to sport; it is the foundation that makes sport worth playing and watching.

The first ethical principle is fair play. Competitors must obey both the written rules and their spirit. A player who deceives an official may gain a temporary advantage, yet damages the shared contest from which achievement derives its value. Respect for opponents is equally important. Fierce rivalry is compatible with courtesy because an opponent is not an enemy but a necessary partner in testing excellence.

Doping presents the clearest challenge. Performance-enhancing substances can endanger health, create an unfair advantage and pressure clean athletes to imitate the abuse merely to remain competitive. Effective testing and proportionate sanctions are necessary, but so are education, medical responsibility and protection against contaminated supplements. Justice also requires reliable procedures: an allegation should not destroy a career before evidence is examined.

Corruption extends beyond athletes. Match-fixing, illegal betting, bribery in selection and opaque allocation of hosting rights convert sport into private commerce at the public’s expense. Administrators control funds and opportunities; coaches influence young careers; medical teams hold confidential information. Each role carries duties of transparency, competence and care. Institutions must prevent conflicts of interest rather than relying only on personal virtue.

Ethics also concerns inclusion. Discrimination based on caste, race, sex, disability, region or economic status denies talent a fair chance. Equal opportunity does not always mean identical treatment: safe facilities, appropriate classification and reasonable accommodation may be needed to make competition genuinely fair. Questions concerning categories can be complex, but they should be addressed through evidence, dignity and consistent rules rather than humiliation.

The treatment of young athletes demands special attention. Ambitious training can build character, yet excessive pressure, abuse and early specialisation may damage bodies and minds. Coaches and parents must not treat children as instruments of prestige. Consent, education, rest and protection from harassment are more important than medals. Similarly, an injured athlete should not be forced to continue merely for commercial or national expectations.

Commercialisation creates both opportunities and risks. Sponsorship finances facilities and rewards excellence, but advertising and broadcasting may encourage packed schedules, sensational conflict or the promotion of harmful products. Star athletes also influence millions. They retain freedom of expression, but should recognise that deliberate misinformation, hatred or repeated misconduct can undermine the values their sport publicly represents.

Technology raises new questions. Video review can correct errors, data analysis can improve performance and advanced equipment can broaden possibility. Yet access may be unequal, surveillance may invade privacy and innovation may confer advantages unrelated to human skill. Rules should evolve transparently so that technology assists fair competition rather than secretly determining it.

Ethical sport does not require the absence of ambition. It asks that ambition be disciplined by fairness, safety and respect. Laws and monitoring matter, but the deepest protection is a culture in which winning dishonourably is understood as failure. A medal records a result; character determines its worth. When players, officials, institutions and spectators defend integrity, sport becomes more than entertainment: it becomes a public lesson in excellence pursued without sacrificing humanity.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats ethics as an institutional as well as personal duty and covers fair play, doping, corruption, inclusion, safeguarding, commerce and technology.

### Q1(c). “War as a policy instrument for nations today.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “War as a policy instrument for nations today.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Analytical essay → Statecraft, force and peaceful alternatives  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-war-as-policy-instrument.md`

**Model essay**

War has long been used by states to secure territory, defend sovereignty, deter aggression or compel political change. It is therefore often described as an instrument of policy: organised violence is employed to achieve an objective which diplomacy alone has not secured. In the contemporary world, however, war remains available while becoming increasingly destructive, uncertain and difficult to control.

The clearest justification for force is self-defence. A state attacked by another cannot be expected to protect its people through persuasion alone. Collective security arrangements may also require states to resist aggression lest conquest become rewarding. Credible defensive capability can deter attack and create space for negotiation. In this limited sense, military power continues to support policy even when no battle occurs.

Yet using war to achieve broader political aims is deeply hazardous. Modern conflict harms civilians, destroys infrastructure and displaces populations on a vast scale. Precision weapons do not eliminate error, faulty intelligence or deliberate abuse. Nuclear weapons add an extreme danger: escalation between major powers could produce consequences far beyond the original dispute. Cyber operations and autonomous systems further blur the boundary between peace and war.

War also has a record of defeating its own purpose. A battlefield victory may remove a government without creating legitimate institutions. Intervention can release sectarian or regional tensions, strengthen extremist groups and leave the victor responsible for reconstruction. Nationalism generated by invasion may harden resistance. Because political communities are not machines, coercion cannot reliably manufacture consent.

Economic interdependence raises the cost further. Conflict disrupts food, fuel, shipping, investment and supply chains across countries that are not belligerents. Sanctions and blockades may pressure rulers but also burden ordinary people. Military expenditure diverts resources from health, education and climate resilience. Even the side that wins territory may lose prosperity, reputation and a generation of human potential.

International law consequently seeks to restrict force. The prohibition on aggressive war and the preference for peaceful settlement reflect lessons drawn from catastrophic experience. Law is imperfectly enforced and powerful states sometimes apply it selectively, but abandoning the norm would make every grievance a possible pretext for conquest. Rules, mediation and multilateral institutions remain essential even when they cannot guarantee peace.

Nations possess other instruments: diplomacy, trade, arbitration, development partnerships, cultural influence, targeted sanctions and confidence-building measures. These methods may be slow and frustrating, yet they preserve room for compromise. A durable settlement usually requires the adversary to live with its terms; humiliation may only postpone another conflict.

Pacifism nevertheless faces a hard objection. Negotiation can fail against an aggressor who interprets restraint as weakness. A policy that renounces all force may expose vulnerable populations to domination. Ethical statecraft must therefore distinguish necessary defence from wars of choice. Force, if unavoidable, should have lawful authority, a clear and proportionate aim, protection for non-combatants and a plausible path back to peace.

Today war remains an instrument of policy, but it is a blunt one whose effects routinely escape its user. Military preparedness may deter aggression and defence may be necessary; treating war as an ordinary extension of politics is far more dangerous. Wise policy uses power to prevent war, exhausts peaceful remedies and recognises that the final objective is not an enemy’s destruction but a just and stable peace.

**Why this earns marks:** It acknowledges deterrence and self-defence while testing war against law, civilian costs, escalation, interdependence and political effectiveness.

### Q1(d). “There is no place like home.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “There is no place like home.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Reflective essay → Belonging, memory and the meaning of home  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-no-place-like-home.md`

**Model essay**

“There is no place like home” is often spoken after a journey, when familiar rooms offer relief from uncertainty. Yet home is more than a building to which one returns. It is a network of memories, relationships, habits and security that makes a place one’s own. Its uniqueness lies not in physical perfection but in the meaning accumulated there.

Home is usually our first world. There we learn language, trust, manners and the rhythms of daily life. Familiar sounds and smells can restore memories more powerfully than deliberate recollection. A modest room may therefore hold greater emotional value than a luxurious hotel. The traveller enjoys novelty, but at home does not need to interpret every gesture or prove a right to belong.

The comfort of home is relational. Meals, celebrations, quarrels and reconciliations create a shared history. Family members may understand one another’s silences and weaknesses in ways strangers cannot. Home can offer refuge from public competition, allowing a person to be vulnerable without losing dignity. Even those living alone create belonging through chosen routines, neighbours, pets, books and objects.

Migration reveals this attachment sharply. People leaving a village, city or country for education, work or safety often carry food, songs, language and festivals with them. These practices reconstruct home in unfamiliar surroundings. Digital communication can reduce distance, but cannot fully replace physical presence. The migrant may eventually develop more than one home, proving that belonging can expand without erasing its earlier roots.

Nevertheless, the proverb should not idealise every household. For some people, home is marked by violence, discrimination, control or neglect. A woman denied freedom, a child facing abuse or a person rejected for identity may find greater safety elsewhere. In such cases, leaving home can be an act of courage. A true home must be judged by care and dignity, not merely by kinship or property.

Homelessness makes the phrase morally urgent. Without secure shelter, privacy and an address, access to work, education, sanitation and public services becomes difficult. Home is therefore both an emotional idea and a material necessity. Societies that celebrate family life while tolerating unsafe housing fail to recognise this connection.

The meaning of home also extends beyond the household. A neighbourhood becomes home through familiar streets and mutual help; a homeland provides language, citizenship and collective memory. Yet love of one’s own place should not become hostility to outsiders. Mature belonging gives confidence to welcome others. Just as affection for one family need not diminish respect for another, patriotism need not require contempt for another country.

Modern life frequently loosens attachment to place. Jobs demand mobility, cities change rapidly and online life creates communities independent of geography. Still, the need for a dependable centre remains. People seek spaces where they are known, where responsibility is mutual and where memory connects past and future.

There is no place like home because no other place contains precisely the same combination of intimacy, recognition and remembered life. But home is not automatically created by walls, blood or birthplace. It must be sustained through safety, respect and care. We inherit part of it, build part of it and sometimes must find it anew. Its deepest promise is simple: here one may belong without surrendering one’s dignity.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines personal reflection with migration, homelessness and unsafe households, thereby qualifying rather than merely repeating the proverb.

### Q2(a). “Enumerate the agenda of the British rule in India.” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → “Enumerate the agenda of the British rule in India.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

British rule sought economic and political domination in British interests. Modern civilisation was a cooperative European product, but the British played a considerable part in creating and disseminating it. They industrialised early, reshaped their institutions and ways of thought under liberal culture, and carried that culture worldwide. They consequently claimed to represent or embody civilisation more fully than other nations and to be its historically accredited vehicle. The passage, however, presents this not as a neutral service but as an ideological claim used to universalise British experience and legitimise empire.

<a id="2024-mains-english-q02c"></a>
### Q2(c). “Explain the statement ‘… their civilization, in their view attained the highest possible level of human existence …’” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → “Explain the statement ‘… their civilization, in their view attained the highest possible level of human existence …’” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Language skill:** Comprehension → Contextual explanation of an ideological claim  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-civilisational-hierarchy.md`

The British believed that their civilisation had developed the highest human capacities and was therefore desirable for everyone. They imagined all societies moving towards one universal destination—the British model—along the same route. Societies nearer that destination were considered “advanced” and morally entitled to guide those judged backward, stationary or misdirected. To sustain this hierarchy, the British converted the distinctive facts of their own life into universal standards of value. British society then appeared necessarily superior, while other societies appeared deficient.

### Q2(d). “What made the British think that they gave India a precious gift of civilization?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → “What made the British think that they gave India a precious gift of civilization?” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Language skill:** Comprehension → Cause and ideological reasoning  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-gift-of-civilisation.md`

The British regarded their civilisation as universally desirable and themselves as its foremost vehicle. They acknowledged India’s ancient civic, literary, philosophical, artistic and scientific achievements, but placed those achievements safely in the past. Contemporary India was portrayed as socially oppressive, religiously irrational, intellectually mystical and personally undisciplined. By defining India as only “semi-civilised” and unable to progress unaided, they could describe British guidance, discipline and training as a unique gift valuable enough to compensate for colonial exploitation.

### Q2(e). “According to the passage, the British changed their views about India over a period of time. Why?” — 15 marks

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official wording:** “Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give or suggest a title to it. The precis should be written in your own words:”  
**Printed passage length:** Approximately 697 words  
**Marks:** 75  
**Language skill:** Précis writing → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/precis-development-and-pollution.md`

**Compression plan:** Target approximately 232 words. Preserve the sequence: development-driven exploitation → ecological imbalance and danger to life → industrial sources and accumulated pollution → global transfer of pollution → definitions of pollution and pollutant → visible consequences. Remove measurements, long product lists, repeated warnings and illustrative expansion.

> **Study-only title:** *Development, Pollution and Ecological Danger*  
> **Examination instruction:** Do **not** write this or any other title in the answer booklet.

**Model précis (about 232 words; untitled examination response)**

The global race for development, measured by agricultural and industrial growth, has encouraged reckless exploitation of natural resources and endangered health. Nature must be conserved for future generations, but human activity has disturbed the biosphere’s ecological balance. The resulting conditions threaten humanity and other organisms, while endangered species continue to increase.

Industrialisation has produced useful goods, but chemicals, radioactive materials, poisonous emissions, effluents and mining waste have heavily polluted the environment. Atomic energy adds radioactivity, while factories and mines release poisonous gases, liquid effluents and solid waste. India has accumulated gaseous, liquid and solid wastes because pollution control developed late. Affluent Western countries also contaminate their surroundings through intensive consumption and production and may transfer part of their pollution burden to developing countries.

Pollution is an undesirable physical, chemical or biological change in air, water or soil that harms life, creates health hazards and damages industry, culture, nature and the general environment. A pollutant is any solid, liquid, gas, chemical, biological product or physical factor released in a concentration capable of causing injury or unpleasant effects. Such pollutants are largely residues of what people produce, use and discard. Factory wastes contaminate water; vehicles, industries and power plants foul the air. Polluted rivers, dying fish and trees, choking cities, toxic food chains and recurrent disease have consequently become matters of worldwide concern.

**Why this earns marks:** It is approximately one-third of the printed length, follows the source’s argumentative order, uses independent wording and keeps the examination response untitled.
