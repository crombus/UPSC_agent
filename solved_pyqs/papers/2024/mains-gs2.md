## Q1. Examine the need for electoral reforms as suggested by various committees with particular reference to the “One Nation–One Election” principle.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Electoral reforms and simultaneous elections · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Election Commission → Electoral reforms  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `electoral-reforms-one-nation-one-election`

**Demand decoding:** Establish the wider reform deficit, assess simultaneous elections within committee recommendations, and give a qualified institutional verdict.

**Compact framework:** Reform needs → committee proposals → ONOE gains → constitutional/federal risks → safeguards.

### Model answer

Electoral reform must improve integrity, representativeness and governability. The **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)** sought stronger election machinery and curbs on malpractices; the **Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998)** supported state funding in kind; the **Law Commission’s 170th Report (1999)** supported simultaneous Lok Sabha–Assembly elections while recommending regulation of parties; and the **Law Commission draft report (2018)** found simultaneous elections desirable but constitutionally difficult under the existing framework.

The **High-Level Committee chaired by Ram Nath Kovind (2024)** recommended synchronising Lok Sabha and Assembly polls, followed by local elections within 100 days. This could reduce recurring Model Code disruptions, campaign expenditure and diversion of personnel.

However, premature dissolutions, constructive no-confidence arrangements, truncated terms and nationwide logistical demands require amendments affecting Articles 83, 85, 172, 174 and 356. National campaigns may also overshadow state issues and weaken federal accountability.

**Conclusion:** ONOE can be one element of reform, but only through broad federal consensus, strengthened Election Commission capacity and safeguards preserving legislative confidence and voter choice.

**Why this earns marks:** It places ONOE within the committee-led reform continuum and weighs efficiency against parliamentary federalism.

## Q2. Explain and distinguish between Lok Adalats and Arbitration Tribunals. Whether they entertain civil as well as criminal cases?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Administrative Tribunals](../../topics/polity/administrative-tribunals.md) → Alternative dispute resolution · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Explain; Distinguish  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Administrative Tribunals and ADR → Lok Adalat versus arbitration  
**Canonical owner:** [Administrative Tribunals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Administrative-Tribunals.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `lok-adalat-arbitration-tribunal-distinction`

**Demand decoding:** Compare source, jurisdiction, consent, decision and challenge; directly resolve civil/criminal competence.

**Compact framework:** Legal basis → process → award → jurisdiction → verdict.

### Model answer

Both are alternative dispute-resolution mechanisms, but their authority and methods differ.

| Dimension | Lok Adalat | Arbitral tribunal |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 | Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 |
| Basis | Settlement/compromise facilitated by a statutory forum | Parties’ arbitration agreement |
| Decision | Ordinary Lok Adalat cannot adjudicate merits if settlement fails; Permanent Lok Adalat may decide specified public-utility disputes | Tribunal adjudicates evidence and law |
| Award | Deemed a civil-court decree; final, binding and no appeal | Binding award, challengeable only on limited Section 34 grounds |

Lok Adalats entertain pending or pre-litigation **civil disputes** and **compoundable criminal offences**; they cannot settle non-compoundable crimes. Arbitration concerns disputes capable of private adjudication—principally civil and commercial rights. Criminal liability, matrimonial status, insolvency and other rights in rem are generally non-arbitrable.

**Conclusion:** Lok Adalat is a justice-access and consensual-settlement institution with limited criminal reach; arbitration is contractual civil adjudication and has no criminal jurisdiction.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the jurisdictional trap directly while distinguishing settlement from adjudication.

## Q3. “The growth of cabinet system has practically resulted in the marginalisation of the parliamentary supremacy.” Elucidate.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Executive–legislature relations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Elucidate  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliamentary System → Cabinet dominance  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliamentary System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Parliamentary-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cabinet-dominance-parliamentary-supremacy`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the mechanisms of cabinet dominance while qualifying that Indian Parliament remains constitutionally significant but not legally sovereign.

**Compact framework:** Fusion → party majority → agenda control → weakened scrutiny → retained checks.

### Model answer

In a parliamentary system, the executive emerges from and remains collectively responsible to the legislature under **Article 75(3)**. Yet this fusion can convert parliamentary government into cabinet government.

A stable majority lets the Council of Ministers control the legislative agenda, timetable, money bills and delegated legislation. Party whips and the anti-defection law under the **Tenth Schedule** restrict independent voting. Frequent ordinances, limited sittings, guillotining of demands for grants and reduced committee referral further compress scrutiny. The Prime Minister’s control over portfolios and advice on dissolution strengthens executive leadership.

Nevertheless, “supremacy” requires qualification. Unlike Britain, Parliament in India is limited by the written Constitution, federal division, Fundamental Rights and judicial review—*Kesavananda Bharati* (1973). Questions, no-confidence motions, CAG reports, financial control and department-related standing committees still impose accountability; coalition or minority governments can revive legislative bargaining.

**Conclusion:** Cabinet growth has marginalised Parliament operationally, not extinguished it constitutionally. Stronger committees, longer sittings and freer deliberation are needed.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies concrete dominance mechanisms and avoids wrongly treating Indian Parliament as legally sovereign.

## Q4. “The duty of the Comptroller and Auditor General is not merely to ensure the legality of expenditure but also its propriety.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comptroller and Auditor General](../../topics/polity/comptroller-and-auditor-general.md) → Comptroller and Auditor General · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Polity → CAG → Legality and propriety audit  
**Canonical owner:** [CAG](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/CAG.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cag-legality-propriety-audit`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish compliance from propriety, show CAG’s constitutional value and state the policy-choice limitation.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → legality → propriety → performance → democratic limit.

### Model answer

Articles **148–151** establish the CAG as the independent audit authority, while the CAG’s Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service Act, 1971 defines its mandate.

**Legality audit** asks whether expenditure was authorised by appropriation, incurred by a competent authority and compliant with law and financial rules. **Propriety audit** goes further: even lawful spending may be questioned when it violates prudence, economy, fairness or avoidance of personal benefit. Thus, an authorised procurement can still be improper if specifications unduly favour one bidder or public money buys an evidently wasteful asset.

Performance audit additionally examines **economy, efficiency and effectiveness**, allowing Parliament and Public Accounts Committees to assess whether funds achieved intended outcomes. This converts legislative financial control from formal bookkeeping into substantive accountability.

However, the CAG audits after expenditure, cannot enforce recovery, and should not replace the elected executive’s legitimate policy choice; it assesses implementation and financial consequences against declared objectives.

**Conclusion:** Propriety is essential because constitutional accountability concerns not only whether money could legally be spent, but whether it was spent responsibly.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates three audit tests and qualifies the CAG’s non-policy-making role.

## Q5. Analyse the role of local bodies in providing good governance at local level and bring out the pros and cons of merging the rural local bodies with the urban local bodies.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../topics/governance/local-governance-and-service-delivery.md) → Service delivery and rural–urban transition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Analyse; Bring out pros and cons  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Local Governance → Rural–urban institutional merger  
**Canonical owner:** [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/12_Local-Governance-and-Service-Delivery.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `local-bodies-good-governance-rural-urban-merger`

**Demand decoding:** Link decentralisation to good-governance outcomes, then balance integration gains against representation and constitutional-design costs.

**Compact framework:** Local role → merger rationale → gains → risks → functional alternative.

### Model answer

The **73rd and 74th Amendments** place government near citizens. Panchayats and municipalities plan local development, deliver water, sanitation, roads and welfare, mobilise communities and enable Gram Sabha/ward-level accountability. Their proximity improves information, responsiveness and social audit, though weak devolution of the “3Fs”—functions, funds and functionaries—limits outcomes.

Merging rural and urban bodies around expanding cities may:
- integrate land use, transport, water, waste and peri-urban infrastructure;
- remove fragmented jurisdictions and widen the revenue base;
- coordinate regional economic planning.

Yet merger can:
- dilute village representation and Gram Sabha participation;
- impose urban taxes and regulations before livelihoods become urban;
- centralise power in a large municipality and ignore agricultural commons;
- conflict with distinct constitutional schemes under Parts IX and IX-A.

**Conclusion:** Boundary reform is justified where settlements have become functionally urban, but blanket merger is unsuitable. Metropolitan/district planning committees, inter-local agreements and phased reclassification can secure integration without sacrificing subsidiarity.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses governance mechanisms and offers a constitutionally sensitive alternative to binary merger.

## Q6. Public charitable trusts have the potential to make India’s development more inclusive as they relate to certain vital public issues. Comment.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → Public charitable trusts and inclusive development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Governance → NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society → Public charitable trusts  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `public-charitable-trusts-inclusive-development`

**Demand decoding:** Explain inclusion channels, add concrete examples and qualify capacity with accountability concerns.

**Compact framework:** Comparative advantage → inclusion functions → examples → risks → partnership safeguards.

### Model answer

Public charitable trusts apply permanently dedicated assets to public purposes such as education, health, disability support, nutrition and relief. They can make development inclusive by reaching groups whom standardised state programmes miss.

Their local knowledge enables context-specific delivery; philanthropic capital can finance experimentation and long-horizon institutions; and relative organisational flexibility permits rapid response. The **Tata Trusts’** health and livelihood work and charitable hospitals illustrate how private endowments can complement public provision. Trusts can also amplify marginal voices, mobilise volunteers and create models later scaled by government.

However, potential is not performance. Donor priorities may displace community needs; urban concentration, opaque trustee control, weak outcome measurement and religious/caste exclusion can reproduce inequality. Tax exemption does not by itself establish public value.

**Conclusion:** Trusts are complements, not substitutes, for rights-based state provision. Transparent accounts, independent boards, beneficiary participation, conflict-of-interest rules and outcome-based government partnerships can convert charitable discretion into inclusive capability.

**Why this earns marks:** It links institutional advantages to inclusion while clearly addressing legitimacy and accountability.

## Q7. Poverty and malnutrition create a vicious cycle, adversely affecting human capital formation. What steps can be taken to break the cycle?
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Human-capital formation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security → Poverty–malnutrition cycle  
**Canonical owner:** [Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `poverty-malnutrition-human-capital-cycle`

**Demand decoding:** Show the two-way causal cycle and prescribe lifecycle, income and public-service interventions.

**Compact framework:** Cycle → first 1,000 days → food diversity → health/WASH → income and women’s agency.

### Model answer

Low income reduces access to diverse food, sanitation and healthcare; malnutrition then impairs cognition, immunity, schooling and productivity, reproducing poverty across generations. Breaking this cycle requires a lifecycle approach.

- Protect the **first 1,000 days** through maternal nutrition, antenatal care, breastfeeding support and strengthened Anganwadis under POSHAN 2.0.
- Convert calorie security under the **National Food Security Act, 2013** into nutrition security through pulses, millets, eggs and locally appropriate foods; improve PM POSHAN quality.
- Integrate growth monitoring with timely referral, immunisation, anaemia control and primary healthcare.
- Ensure safe water, sanitation and hygiene, because repeated infection prevents nutrient absorption.
- Raise household capability through MGNREGA, social security, women’s self-help groups and reliable maternity benefits.
- Use disaggregated district data and social audits to identify exclusion.

**Conclusion:** Food supplementation alone cannot end malnutrition; convergence of income, women’s agency, health, care practices and WASH converts nutrition spending into durable human capital.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the causal mechanism and maps each intervention to a break-point in the cycle.

## Q8. The Doctrine of Democratic Governance makes it necessary that the public perception of the integrity and commitment of civil servants becomes absolutely positive. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Civil Services](../../topics/governance/civil-services.md) → Civil-service integrity and trust · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Citizen-Centric Administration → Integrity, commitment and public trust  
**Canonical owner:** [Citizen-Centric Administration](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/07_Citizen-Centric-Administration.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `democratic-governance-civil-service-public-trust`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why both actual integrity and warranted public confidence matter, without reducing administration to image management.

**Compact framework:** Democratic legitimacy → trust mechanisms → deficits → institutional correctives → qualification.

### Model answer

Democratic governance rests on popular sovereignty, rule of law and accountable administration. Civil servants exercise delegated public power; therefore, legality alone is insufficient if citizens reasonably perceive decisions as corrupt, partisan or indifferent.

Positive trust lowers compliance costs, encourages tax payment and welfare uptake, and gives difficult decisions legitimacy. Conversely, opaque discretion, delay and conflict of interest weaken equal citizenship even where no conviction occurs. The **Second ARC’s *Ethics in Governance* report (2007)** therefore linked integrity with codes, disclosure and institutional accountability.

Public confidence must be earned through transparent recruitment and postings, reasoned orders, time-bound services, social audits, effective grievance redress, protection of honest officers and enforcement by vigilance bodies and the Lokpal. Mission Karmayogi can strengthen competence and citizen orientation, but training cannot replace accountability.

**Conclusion:** “Absolutely positive” should mean well-founded confidence, not manufactured reputation. Democratic legitimacy requires civil servants to be impartial and committed—and institutions that allow citizens to verify those qualities.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects integrity to democratic outcomes and qualifies perception with verifiable accountability.

**Demand decoding:** Explain economic de-risking and strategic balancing through examples, while preserving India’s autonomy and capacity constraints.

### Model answer

Pandemic disruption, technology controls and geopolitical rivalry have encouraged Western **de-risking**, rather than complete decoupling, from China. India offers a large market, skilled labour, democratic institutions and an Indian Ocean location.

Economically, “China+1” is visible in expanding electronics assembly, Apple suppliers’ India operations and Western partnerships in semiconductors and clean energy. **Conclusion:** The West increasingly treats India as a diversification and balancing partner, but India converts this opening into national capability rather than bloc alignment.

## Q11. What are the aims and objects of the recently passed and enforced Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024? Whether University/State Education Board examinations, too, are covered under the Act?
**Primary route:** Governance → [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../topics/governance/government-policy-design-and-implementation.md) → Examination integrity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Explain; Determine scope  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Government Policy Design and Implementation → Public examinations  
**Canonical owner:** [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/02_Government-Policy-Design-and-Implementation.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `public-examinations-unfair-means-act-2024`

**Demand decoding:** State the Act’s purpose and principal offences/enforcement design, then answer the coverage question precisely rather than assuming nationwide territorial extent equals universal exam coverage.

**Compact framework:** Object → prohibited conduct → liability/investigation → scheduled authorities → scope verdict.

### Model answer

The **Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024**, brought into force on **21 June 2024**, protects the credibility of public examinations conducted by specified central authorities.

**Aims and design**
- It deters organised cheating, paper leakage and collusion that undermine merit and public confidence.
- “Unfair means” include unauthorised access or leakage of question papers/answer keys, assistance to candidates, tampering with computer systems, manipulation of seating or merit documents, fake examinations and creation of fraudulent websites.
- It targets persons, organised groups and service providers involved in malpractice rather than penalising a candidate merely for an individual offence under this central Act.
- Offences are cognizable, non-bailable and non-compoundable. Ordinary offences attract imprisonment and fine; organised crime and culpable service providers face enhanced punishment and recovery of examination costs.
- Investigation is ordinarily by an officer not below Deputy Superintendent of Police, with power to entrust a central agency.

**Coverage:** The Act is authority-based. Its Schedule names UPSC, SSC, Railway Recruitment Boards, IBPS, NTA and central-government departments/attached offices recruiting staff; the Central Government may notify additional authorities.

Therefore, **university and State Education Board examinations are not automatically covered** unless the concerned authority is brought within the central Act’s notified framework. States may enact their own anti-cheating laws.

**Conclusion:** The Act creates a uniform central deterrent against organised exam fraud, but it is not a blanket code for every examination held in India.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes territorial operation from scheduled-authority coverage and answers both statutory object and scope.

## Q12. Right to privacy is intrinsic to life and personal liberty and is inherently protected under Article 21 of the Constitution. Explain. In this reference discuss the law relating to DNA testing of a child in the womb to establish its paternity.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Privacy, bodily autonomy and DNA testing · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Explain; Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Article 21 privacy and paternity testing  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `privacy-article-21-prenatal-dna-paternity`

**Demand decoding:** Establish privacy doctrine, identify competing rights in prenatal paternity testing, and state the exceptional-necessity standard with statutory presumptions and bodily autonomy.

**Compact framework:** Article 21 → Puttaswamy test → legitimacy presumption → DNA jurisprudence → prenatal safeguards.

### Model answer

In ***K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017)**, a nine-judge Bench held privacy to be intrinsic to liberty, dignity and autonomy under Article 21 and other freedoms in Part III. Privacy protects the body, family, reproductive choice, personal information and decisional autonomy. It is not absolute: invasion requires legality, a legitimate State aim, necessity and proportionality, with procedural safeguards.

Paternity testing creates a conflict among the search for biological truth, the alleged father’s interest, the mother’s bodily and reproductive privacy, and the unborn child’s dignity and future social identity.

Under **Section 116 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** (in force from 1 July 2024, corresponding to Section 112 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872), birth during a valid marriage creates conclusive legitimacy unless non-access is shown. The Supreme Court has therefore rejected routine “roving” DNA orders:

- ***Goutam Kundu* (1993)**: blood testing cannot be ordered as a matter of course;
- ***Bhabani Prasad Jena* (2010)**: the court must apply an “eminent need” test and balance interests;
- ***Aparna Ajinkya Firodia* (2023)**: the child’s legitimacy, privacy and best interests require exceptional restraint.

A prenatal sample is still more intrusive because it implicates the pregnant woman’s bodily autonomy and medical risk. The **PCPNDT Act, 1994** permits prenatal diagnostic procedures only for specified medical purposes and prohibits sex selection; it does not create a routine paternity-testing entitlement.

**Conclusion:** A court may consider DNA evidence only when decisively necessary, lawful and proportionate, after less intrusive evidence fails; prenatal compulsion should be exceptionally rare.

**Why this earns marks:** It links constitutional privacy to the specific evidentiary, child-welfare and bodily-autonomy limits on prenatal DNA testing.

## Q13. What changes has the Union Government recently introduced in the domain of Centre–State relations? Suggest measures to be adopted to build trust between the Centre and the States and for strengthening federalism.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre-State & Inter-State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → Centre–State relations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Identify; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Centre–State Relations → Cooperative and fiscal federalism  
**Canonical owner:** [Centre–State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Centre-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `recent-centre-state-changes-trust-federalism`

**Demand decoding:** Identify recent structural and policy changes—positive and contentious—then offer institutional, fiscal and gubernatorial reforms tied to trust deficits.

**Compact framework:** Recent changes → trust effects → consultation → fiscal compact → neutral institutions.

### Model answer

Recent Union initiatives have altered federal relations in several ways:

1. **Fiscal architecture:** The Fourteenth Finance Commission raised states’ tax-devolution share to 42% (later adjusted to 41% after J&K reorganisation), while rising cesses and surcharges remain outside the divisible pool. GST created joint decision-making through the **GST Council**, but compensation ended in June 2022, sharpening revenue concerns.
2. **Planning:** NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission in 2015, promising cooperative and competitive federalism, though it lacks the former body’s grant-allocation role.
3. **Territorial/administrative change:** The **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** converted a State into two Union Territories. The **GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023** strengthened Union influence over Delhi services.
4. **Policy centralisation:** centrally sponsored schemes, conditional borrowing and centrally designed sectoral targets can improve national coordination but constrain state priorities.

**Trust-building measures**
- Make the **Inter-State Council under Article 263** regular and agenda-driven; strengthen Zonal Councils.
- Consult states before legislation affecting State/Concurrent List fields and revive robust parliamentary committee scrutiny.
- Increase untied transfers, rationalise cesses and adopt predictable compensation/borrowing rules.
- Follow **Sarkaria and Punchhi Commission** norms for impartial Governors, assent to Bills and Article 356.
- Respect GST Council deliberation; *Mohit Minerals* (2022) confirms its recommendations are persuasive, not binding.
- Create time-bound mechanisms for inter-state disputes and transparent scheme evaluation.

**Conclusion:** Federal trust grows when consultation precedes command, fiscal rules are predictable and constitutional offices remain neutral.

## Q14. Explain the reasons for the growth of public interest litigation in India. As a result of it, has the Indian Supreme Court emerged as the world’s most powerful judiciary?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → PIL and judicial power · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Explain; Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Supreme Court → Public interest litigation and judicial activism  
**Canonical owner:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pil-growth-supreme-court-power`

**Demand decoding:** Explain PIL’s institutional growth, assess the “most powerful” claim through jurisdiction and remedies, and qualify it through limits and comparative caution.

**Compact framework:** Origins → access innovations → expanded remedies → power claim → limits.

### Model answer

Public interest litigation grew after the post-Emergency judiciary relaxed **locus standi** under Articles **32 and 226**. Poverty, illiteracy and weak legal aid had excluded affected groups; judges therefore accepted representative petitions and even letters. ***S.P. Gupta* (1981)** broadened standing, while ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984)** used PIL for bonded labour. Expansion of Article 21, investigative commissions, epistolary jurisdiction and “continuing mandamus” made courts accessible for environmental, prison, livelihood and governance claims.

PIL has contributed to an unusually powerful Supreme Court:
- constitutional amendments are reviewable under the **basic-structure doctrine**;
- judicial review covers legislation and executive action;
- Articles 32, 136 and 142 enable direct rights enforcement, broad appellate reach and “complete justice”;
- decisions such as ***Vishaka* (1997)** framed interim norms amid legislative vacuum;
- court-monitored cases sustain administrative compliance.

Yet “world’s most powerful” is rhetorical, not a measurable legal rank. The Court lacks purse and enforcement machinery, depends on executive compliance, faces pendency, and is limited by separation of powers and self-imposed PIL rules. Misuse through publicity litigation and policy micromanagement can impair democratic accountability.

**Conclusion:** PIL transformed the Supreme Court into a globally exceptional rights and governance court, but its legitimacy depends on principled standing, evidence, institutional competence and remedial restraint.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both the social-access origins and doctrinal tools behind power, then supplies a reasoned comparative qualification.

## Q15. Discuss India as a secular state and compare with the secular principles of the US Constitution.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → Secularism in India and the USA · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Discuss; Compare  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitutional Schemes → Secularism  
**Canonical owner:** [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-us-secularism-comparison`

**Demand decoding:** Explain India’s constitutional model, compare it dimension-by-dimension with US non-establishment/free exercise, and avoid the simplistic “India engagement/US total separation” binary.

**Compact framework:** Indian text → US text → comparative axes → case law → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

India has no established religion and the **42nd Amendment (1976)** expressly added “secular” to the Preamble. Its framework combines:

- equality and non-discrimination—Articles 14–16;
- freedom of conscience and religion—Articles 25–28;
- cultural and educational rights of minorities—Articles 29–30;
- reform powers, including Article 25(2), permitting regulation of secular activities and social reform.

***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** affirmed secularism as a basic feature. Indian secularism is often described as **principled distance**: the State may engage with all religions to protect freedom, reform exclusion or secure equality, but cannot identify itself with one faith.

The US **First Amendment** prohibits establishment of religion and protects free exercise. Its historical emphasis is institutional non-establishment—a “wall of separation” associated with ***Everson v. Board of Education* (1947)**—alongside accommodation of genuine religious exercise. Modern US doctrine does not demand hostility to religion; ***Kennedy v. Bremerton* (2022)** rejected the old *Lemon* test in favour of historical practices and understandings.

| Axis | India | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional context | Religious diversity and social reform | Protection from establishment and religious persecution |
| State role | Equal engagement plus reform | Strong non-establishment plus free exercise |
| Community rights | Express minority-cultural rights | Primarily individual-rights framework |

**Conclusion:** Both reject a theocratic State, but India’s transformative, group-sensitive model permits calibrated intervention more readily than the American non-establishment tradition.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares text, purpose and doctrine while avoiding obsolete or absolute characterisations.

## Q16. The Citizens’ Charter has been a landmark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is yet to reach its full potential. Identify the factors hindering the realisation of its promise and suggest measures to overcome them.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Transparency, Accountability, RTI, Grievance Redress and Social Audit](../../topics/governance/transparency-accountability-grievance-redress-and-social-audit.md) → Citizens’ Charters · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Identify; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Transparency, Accountability and Grievance Redress → Citizens’ Charters  
**Canonical owner:** [Transparency, Accountability, Grievance Redress and Social Audit](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/08_Transparency-Accountability-Grievance-Redress-and-Social-Audit.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `citizens-charter-potential-reforms`

**Demand decoding:** Assess implementation—not merely define the Charter—and pair each obstacle with an institutional corrective.

**Compact framework:** Promise → design gaps → capacity/accountability gaps → matched reforms → enforceable service culture.

### Model answer

Introduced in Union ministries in **1997**, Citizens’ Charters seek to disclose service standards, timelines, responsible officials and grievance channels. They shift administration from departmental convenience to citizen entitlement.

**Why potential remains unrealised**
- charters are often drafted internally without citizen consultation or service-process mapping;
- vague promises lack measurable timelines, compensation or enforceability;
- poor publicity, inaccessible language and digital exclusion reduce awareness;
- responsibility is diffused and grievance officers lack authority;
- staff shortages and unreformed procedures make standards unrealistic;
- monitoring measures publication, not actual delivery or user satisfaction;
- multiple portals separate the promise from appeal and remedy.

**Measures**
1. Co-design charters with users, frontline staff and vulnerable groups; publish them in local languages and accessible formats.
2. Use the **Sevottam** model: service standards, grievance redress and capability improvement.
3. Link every standard to a named officer, digital/physical tracking and escalation.
4. Give statutory backing through state right-to-public-services laws, with compensation or penalties for unjustified delay and due-process protection for staff.
5. Re-engineer procedures before fixing timelines; provide adequate personnel and assisted access.
6. Commission independent service audits, public dashboards and periodic revision based on grievance data.

The **Second ARC’s *Citizen Centric Administration* report (2009)** stressed making charters precise, enforceable and organisation-specific.

**Conclusion:** A Charter fulfils its promise only when a publicised service standard becomes a monitored right with an accessible remedy.

**Why this earns marks:** It pairs specific failure mechanisms with operational and legal corrections.

## Q17. In a crucial domain like the public healthcare system, the Indian State should play a vital role to contain the adverse impact of marketisation of the system. Suggest some measures through which the State can enhance the reach of public healthcare at the grassroots level.

**Proposed topic slug:** `public-healthcare-marketisation-grassroots-reach`

**Demand decoding:** Diagnose market failures and propose a state-led package spanning public provision, regulation, financing, workforce and decentralised primary care.

**Compact framework:** Market harms → public primary care → workforce/medicines → regulation/purchasing → local accountability.

### Model answer

Healthcare markets suffer information asymmetry, supplier-induced demand and unequal ability to pay. Unregulated commercialisation can produce over-treatment in profitable urban services while leaving preventive and rural care underprovided. Article **47** therefore places improvement of public health among the State’s primary duties.

**Measures for grassroots reach**

1. **Strengthen comprehensive primary care:** upgrade Sub-Centres and Primary Health Centres as Ayushman Arogya Mandirs with diagnostics, medicines, mental-health and non-communicable-disease services—not merely referral points.
2. **Build the workforce:** fill vacancies, create rural public-health cadres, support ASHAs and community health officers with fair remuneration, housing, training and referral transport.

**Conclusion:** The State must be provider, financier, regulator and steward; insurance alone cannot substitute for accessible public primary healthcare.

## Q18. E-governance is not just about the routine application of digital technology in the service-delivery process. It is as much about multifarious interactions for ensuring transparency and accountability. In this context, evaluate the role of the “Interactive Service Model” of e-governance.
**Primary route:** Governance → [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../topics/governance/e-governance-models-and-user-centricity.md) → Interactive Service Model · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Governance → E-Governance Models and User-Centricity → Interactive services  
**Canonical owner:** [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/05_E-Governance-Models-and-User-Centricity.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `interactive-service-model-e-governance`

**Demand decoding:** Define the model, explain how two-way transactions create transparency/accountability, evaluate examples and limits, and prescribe inclusive design.

**Compact framework:** Definition → interaction chain → governance gains → examples → exclusion/rights limits.

### Model answer

The **Interactive Service Model** moves beyond one-way online information. Citizens can apply, authenticate, pay, track, receive a reasoned decision, complain and appeal through a two-way government platform. The digital interaction itself becomes an accountability architecture.

**Contribution**
- time-stamped applications and status tracking make delay visible;
- standardised workflows reduce discretionary interface and petty rent-seeking;
- digital records create audit trails and identify the responsible office;
- dashboards and feedback reveal recurring service failures;
- online grievance escalation converts information into remedy;
- interoperability reduces repeated submission of certificates.

For example, a land-mutation system that permits online filing, tracks each official action and allows appeal is interactive; a website merely displaying mutation rules is only a broadcasting model. DigiLocker-supported document exchange and integrated service portals can reduce verification burden, provided final delivery is genuinely end-to-end.

Authentication failure, poor connectivity, English-heavy interfaces, inaccessible design, opaque algorithms and absence of human appeal can shift costs onto citizens. Therefore, services require process re-engineering, data minimisation, vernacular and disability-accessible interfaces, assisted/offline channels, reasoned rejection and independent grievance review. **DARPG’s NeSDA** usefully evaluates accessibility, ease of use, privacy, end-service delivery and status tracking.

**Conclusion:** The model democratises administration only when every digital transaction produces visibility, answerability and an effective remedy.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates the model’s causal mechanisms and conditions, rather than treating technology itself as reform.

## Q19. “Terrorism has become a significant threat to global peace and security.” Evaluate the effectiveness of the United Nations Security Council’s Counter-Terrorism Committee (CTC) and its associated bodies in addressing and mitigating this threat at the international level.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [UN and International Institutions: Global Governance](../../topics/international-relations/un-and-international-institutions-global-governance.md) → Counter-terrorism architecture · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** 2024 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q19 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → UN and International Institutions → UNSC Counter-Terrorism Committee  
**Canonical owner:** [UN and International Institutions: Global Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/12_UN-and-International-Institutions-Global-Governance.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `unsc-counter-terrorism-committee-effectiveness`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the CTC ecosystem accurately, assess achievements against enforcement and political limits, and distinguish CTC/CTED from sanctions bodies.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → associated bodies → achievements → deficits → reform verdict.

### Model answer

The UNSC created the **Counter-Terrorism Committee (CTC)** through **Resolution 1373 (2001)** after 9/11. It monitors states’ duties to criminalise terrorist financing, freeze assets, deny safe haven, strengthen borders and cooperate internationally.

Its expert arm, the **Counter-Terrorism Committee Executive Directorate (CTED)**, conducts country assessments, identifies capacity gaps and facilitates technical assistance. The separate **1267/1989/2253 ISIL (Da’esh) and Al-Qaida Sanctions Committee**, supported by its Monitoring Team, administers listings and sanctions; the **UN Office of Counter-Terrorism** provides coordination and capacity support.

**Effectiveness**
- Resolution 1373 created near-universal legal and administrative benchmarks.
- CTED assessments encourage legislation, financial-intelligence units, border controls and international assistance.
- The **Delhi Declaration (29 October 2022)** focused attention on terrorist use of new payment technologies, drones and ICT.
- The sanctions regime constrains travel, finance and arms access for listed actors.

**Limitations**
- CTC recommendations rely on state implementation; it has no independent enforcement arm.
- uneven capacity and politicised definitions permit selective compliance;
- P5 rivalry and opaque listing/delisting can weaken legitimacy;
- sanctions may face due-process and humanitarian concerns;
- adaptive lone actors, encrypted networks and state sponsorship outpace formal reporting.

Reform requires a widely accepted terrorism definition, evidence-based and reviewable listings, stronger CTED follow-up, sustained capacity finance, human-rights safeguards and cooperation with FATF, INTERPOL and regional bodies.

**Conclusion:** The CTC is effective as a norm-setting, monitoring and assistance hub, but cannot by itself overcome geopolitical obstruction or deficient domestic enforcement.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately separates the institutions and delivers a balanced effectiveness judgment with dated evidence.

### Model answer

Maldives’ dispersed atolls lie astride major Indian Ocean sea lanes connecting the Persian Gulf and Red Sea with the Malacca Strait. Its location near India’s west coast and the **Eight Degree Channel** gives it significance for shipping, energy flows and surveillance of the central Indian Ocean.

**For India**
- uninterrupted sea lanes are vital for trade and imported energy;
- Maldivian cooperation improves maritime-domain awareness against piracy, trafficking, illegal fishing and terrorism;

Commercial and infrastructure projects must be assessed for debt, environmental and strategic consequences without treating every external engagement as hostile.

**Conclusion:** Maldives is not merely a buffer but a sovereign maritime partner. Mutual trust determines whether its geography secures trade and regional public goods or becomes an arena of destabilising competition.

**Why this earns marks:** It links precise geography to trade, energy and security while balancing competition with sovereignty and domestic politics.
