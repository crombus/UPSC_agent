## Q1(a). Briefly discuss Plato’s concept of justice.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → Plato · justice as harmony and functional specialisation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q1(a) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Brief discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Justice  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Plato · *Republic* · soul–state analogy · each doing one’s own work  
**Proposed slug:** `plato-justice-harmony-functional-specialisation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Define Platonic justice, reconstruct the soul–city analogy, distinguish justice from mere distribution, and briefly assess its hierarchical implications.

**Argument map:** tripartite soul/city → proper function → rational rule → harmony → competence defence → hierarchy objection → qualified assessment.

### Model answer

For Plato, justice (*dikaiosynē*) is **right order** in soul and city. In the *Republic*, rulers, auxiliaries and producers correspond to reason, spirit and appetite. Justice exists when each performs its proper function without usurping another’s.

Justice is therefore not primarily equal distribution or obedience to enacted law. It is an architectonic virtue that enables wisdom, courage and moderation to coexist in a harmonious whole. A just person is similarly one whose reason governs spirit and appetite.

**Objection:** functional specialisation may naturalise hierarchy and subordinate individual choice to philosopher-rulers. **Reply:** Plato claims functions follow aptitude rather than birth and that rulers serve the good of the whole.

**Assessment:** Plato insightfully connects character with institutions, but his hierarchy lacks consent and safeguards against ruler error. His justice is compelling as harmony, less so as equal citizenship.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the doctrine, mechanism, defence, objection and bounded verdict within the compulsory-part scale.

## Q1(b). Present a brief account of origin and development of Social Contract Theory.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Individual and State](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/individual-and-state.md) → Social contract theory · origin and development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q1(b) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Brief historical-conceptual account  
**Syllabus unit:** Individual and State — Rights, Duties and Accountability  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau · consent and political legitimacy  
**Proposed slug:** `social-contract-origin-development-hobbes-locke-rousseau`  
**Canonical owner:** [Individual and State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Individual-and-State.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain the shift from natural/divine title to agreement, then show how Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau differently develop contractarian legitimacy.

**Argument map:** conventional precursors → modern individualism → Hobbes/security → Locke/rights → Rousseau/self-rule → historical objection → normative reply.

### Model answer

Social-contract thinking has ancient conventionalist precursors, but becomes systematic in early modern Europe when authority must be justified to individuals rather than by divine right or inherited status.

**Hobbes** begins with insecure equality in the state of nature. Individuals authorise an undivided sovereign to secure peace, retaining principally the right of self-preservation. **Locke** portrays the natural condition as governed by natural law but inconvenienced by partial enforcement. Contract creates a limited government held in trust to protect life, liberty and property; breach permits resistance. **Rousseau** argues that unequal society corrupts natural freedom. The legitimate compact forms a people governed by the general will, converting natural liberty into civil and moral freedom.

**Objection:** no historical contract occurred, and early models excluded many persons. **Reply:** the theory’s lasting force is normative: authority must be justifiable on terms free and equal persons could accept.

**Assessment:** it develops from security, through rights, to collective self-legislation, making consent a central test of legitimacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats “origin” and “development” separately and distinguishes all three canonical outcomes.

## Q1(d). Present an exposition of the concept of alienation as propounded by Marx.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → Marxism · alienation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q1(d) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Exposition  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Marx · *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* · fourfold alienation  
**Proposed slug:** `marx-fourfold-alienation-capitalist-labour`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain alienation’s structural basis and four dimensions; do not reduce it to low wages or a subjective feeling.

**Argument map:** private control/commodified labour → product → activity → species-being → others → essence objection → agency-based reply.

### Model answer

In Marx’s *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, alienation is objective estrangement produced by capitalist control of labour. The worker is alienated:

1. **from the product**, which belongs to and confronts the worker as another’s power;
2. **from labouring activity**, experienced as externally compelled rather than self-directed;
3. **from species-being**, because conscious, creative and social activity is reduced to a means of physical survival; and
4. **from other persons**, as owners dominate workers and workers compete with one another.

Alienation is thus broader than poverty: even a well-paid worker remains alienated when others control the purpose, process and product of central activity. Commodity relations make human powers appear as powers of things.

**Objection:** “species-being” assumes a fixed universal human essence. **Reply:** the claim can be reconstructed minimally around meaningful agency, social recognition and control over productive life.

**Assessment:** alienation diagnoses domination within formally free employment; its remedy requires democratic control, not mere state ownership.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately presents all four forms, their structural cause and one philosophically serious qualification.

## Q1(e). Compare socialism and communism as two distinct political ideologies.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → Socialism and communism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q1(e) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Comparison  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Marxism and Socialism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** social ownership · transition · classlessness · state and distribution  
**Proposed slug:** `socialism-communism-comparison`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare aims, ownership, distribution, state and methods while acknowledging the broad family meaning of socialism and the narrower Marxian meaning of communism.

**Argument map:** shared anti-capitalist concern → socialism’s plurality → communism’s higher phase → ownership/state/distribution/method → bureaucratic objection → democratic qualification.

### Model answer

Socialism and communism share the critique that private control of productive assets permits exploitation and domination, but they are not synonyms.

**Socialism** is a broad family favouring social purposes and accountable production. It includes revolutionary, democratic, Fabian, guild and market socialism. Social ownership may coexist with markets, differentiated rewards and an active state.

**Communism**, in the Marxian higher-phase sense, is a classless order based on common control of production, where scarcity and class antagonism have been overcome, distribution follows need, and the political state loses its coercive class function. “Socialism” is often used in Marxist vocabulary for the transitional lower phase, but wider socialism need not aim at complete communism.

**Objection:** both can collapse into bureaucratic state ownership. **Reply:** nationalisation is not socialisation unless workers and citizens exercise accountable control.

**Assessment:** socialism is plural in method and form; communism is a more determinate classless horizon. They differ over transition, state, distribution and ownership.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares precise axes and avoids treating either ideology as merely equal income.

## Q2(a). Delineate the central tenets of Humanism. How does advent of enlightenment in Europe pave the way for Humanism? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → Humanism and Enlightenment · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q2(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Delineation plus historical-philosophical explanation  
**Syllabus unit:** Humanism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** dignity · rational agency · free inquiry · Enlightenment public reason · universal personhood  
**Proposed slug:** `humanism-central-tenets-enlightenment`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Give humanism’s normative and epistemic tenets, then explain distinct Enlightenment mechanisms. Avoid equating humanism with atheism or claiming Europe invented all human-centred thought.

**Argument map:** human dignity/agency → reason and criticism → flourishing/responsibility → Renaissance precursor → Enlightenment attack on inherited authority → science/toleration/rights/education → abstract-universalism objection → plural critical humanism.

### Model answer

Humanism is a family of outlooks that makes human dignity, rational and creative agency, moral responsibility and flourishing central standards of inquiry and institutions. Its main tenets are:

- every person has worth not reducible to caste, church, state or inherited rank;
- authority must be publicly justifiable through reason and experience;
- free inquiry, education and self-correction are conditions of development;
- humans are responsible for alleviating suffering and constructing just institutions;
- solidarity and common humanity qualify egoistic individualism.

Humanism is not necessarily atheistic: Renaissance and religious humanisms coexist with secular forms. Nor does human-centred value entail relativism.

The Renaissance recovery of classical learning first renewed confidence in cultivated agency. The European Enlightenment then deepened this tendency. Scientific inquiry weakened epistemic monopoly; criticism displaced inherited authority; toleration and freedom of conscience challenged compulsory religious uniformity; natural-right and autonomy theories generalised equal moral standing; and public education became a means of emancipation. Thus the human person increasingly appeared as a responsible judge and citizen rather than merely a subject of tradition.

**Objections:** Enlightenment humanism can mistake a European, male or propertied subject for universal humanity, encourage anthropocentric mastery, and neglect socially embedded identity. **Replies:** feminist, anti-caste, postcolonial and ecological criticism need not abandon universal dignity; they reconstruct whose experience defines the “human” and place agency within relations of dependency.

**Assessment:** the Enlightenment decisively institutionalised critical, universalist humanism but did not create all its resources. A defensible contemporary humanism is rational, plural, relational and ecologically bounded.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both clauses, states mechanisms, avoids Eurocentric overclaim and ends with a defended form of humanism.

## Q2(b). Critically evaluate the concepts of liberty and equality as political ideals.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → Liberty and equality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q2(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Critical evaluation  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Equality and Liberty  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** negative/positive liberty · formal/substantive/relational equality · compatibility and tension  
**Proposed slug:** `liberty-equality-political-ideals-critical-evaluation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Define internal variants, show tensions and mutual dependence, evaluate objections, and avoid treating liberty as licence or equality as sameness.

**Argument map:** negative/positive liberty → equal status/opportunity/capability → unrestricted property tension → unequal power undermines freedom → levelling objection → differentiated remedy → reconciliation through justice.

### Model answer

**Liberty** is not mere absence of all restraint. Negative liberty protects a sphere against coercive interference; positive liberty concerns effective agency and self-direction. Both matter: non-interference without education or security can be formally empty, while state-imposed “true freedom” can become paternalistic.

**Equality** likewise has several forms: equal moral and civic status, equality before law, fair opportunity, substantive capability and relational equality without domination. It does not require identical treatment or outcomes; relevant differences may justify differentiated support.

The ideals can conflict. Strong property liberty may generate inequalities that let wealth dominate work and politics; aggressive equalisation may restrict choice, association and incentive. Yet they are also mutually dependent. Extreme inequality converts formal liberty into privilege, while liberty supplies the agency and dissent through which equality remains non-authoritarian. Rawls therefore gives equal basic liberties priority but permits inequalities only under fair opportunity and benefit to the least advantaged.

**Objection:** substantive equality licenses endless intervention. **Reply:** remedies must be proportionate, publicly justified and directed at domination or unequal capability, not uniformity.

**Assessment:** liberty without equality becomes the freedom of the powerful; equality without liberty becomes imposed conformity. Justice must specify equal basic standing, secure effective freedoms and justify remaining inequalities.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates rival meanings, tension, interdependence and institutional reconciliation rather than offering slogans.

## Q2(c). Present an exposition of Gandhi’s views on secularism as one of the foundational principles of democracy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → Gandhi on secularism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q2(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand: Exposition with democratic connection  
**Syllabus unit:** Secularism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Gandhi · equal regard · freedom of conscience · religion and politics · non-domination  
**Proposed slug:** `gandhi-secularism-democracy`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain Gandhi’s religious but non-theocratic secularism and show why conscience, equality and non-violence support democracy.

**Argument map:** religion as ethical truth → equal respect/fallibility → no state religion → conscience/minority security → politics morally informed, not clerically ruled → majoritarian objection → principled constitutional reply.

### Model answer

Gandhi’s secularism is not hostility to religion or a demand that moral faith become wholly private. Religion, for him, signifies commitment to truth (*satya*), non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) and self-purification, not clerical control of the state. Since finite persons grasp truth imperfectly, sincere faith should generate humility and equal regard for other religions, commonly summarised as *sarva-dharma-samabhāva*.

Democracy requires this stance for three reasons. First, freedom of conscience protects the citizen’s moral agency. Second, no majority may convert its faith into unequal civic status for minorities or non-believers. Third, non-violence requires persuasion and dialogue rather than coercive religious uniformity. Gandhi can therefore insist that politics needs ethical religion while rejecting a state religion and theocracy.

**Objection:** “equal respect” may overlook oppressive practices within communities or permit majoritarian religious idiom to dominate public life. **Reply:** Gandhi’s own criterion subjects every practice to truth, non-violence and the dignity of the weakest; constitutional rights must secure this critical boundary.

**Assessment:** Gandhi grounds democratic secularism in fallibilism, conscience and non-domination rather than strict institutional separation alone. Its strength is moral dialogue; its limit is dependence on ethical self-restraint unless reinforced by impartial law and rights.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes religious ethics from theocracy and directly connects Gandhi’s view to democratic legitimacy.

## Q3(a). Do you agree with the view that Aristotle was more successful than Plato in steering a middle course between ‘Statism’ and ‘individualism’? Discuss with arguments.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Individual and State](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/individual-and-state.md) → Plato and Aristotle on person and polis · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q3(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Comparative evaluation with arguments  
**Syllabus unit:** Individual and State  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Plato’s political unity · Aristotle’s plural polis · common good and intermediate spheres  
**Proposed slug:** `aristotle-plato-statism-individualism-middle-course`  
**Canonical owner:** [Individual and State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Individual-and-State.md)

**Demand decoding:** Define the poles, compare political whole, household, property, citizenship and individual good, then give a qualified agreement rather than calling Aristotle a modern liberal.

**Argument map:** both reject atomism → Plato’s functional unity → guardian communism/epistemic rule → Aristotle’s natural polis and plurality → household/property/mixed constitution → exclusion objection → comparative but limited success.

### Model answer

I agree comparatively, though Aristotle is not a modern individualist. **Statism** absorbs the person and intermediate associations into the political whole; atomistic individualism treats society as merely instrumental to antecedently complete individuals. Both Plato and Aristotle reject the latter because human flourishing requires an ethical community.

In Plato’s *Republic*, justice is functional harmony under philosopher-rule. The guardian class loses private property and family; culture is regulated; producers lack political participation. The city’s unity and objective good dominate individual choice. Plato’s defence is perfectionist: rational rulers enable each person to fulfil the function suited to their nature. Yet there is little institutional correction if rulers misidentify either the good or a person’s aptitude.

Aristotle also says the polis is natural and exists for the good life, but criticises Plato’s excessive unity: a polis is essentially a plurality, not one household. He preserves household, private property and differentiated associations, while recommending common use and civic virtue. Citizenship is active participation in ruling and being ruled, and the mixed *polity* balances social interests. Individual flourishing is neither pre-political nor simply sacrificed; it is realised through participation in a differentiated common life.

**Objections:** Aristotle excludes women, slaves and labourers from full citizenship and retains a perfectionist state. His private property may reproduce inequality. **Reply:** these defects preclude modern endorsement but do not erase the conceptual advance: political unity need not abolish social plurality, and shared ends need not eliminate intermediate domains.

**Assessment:** Aristotle more successfully relates individual flourishing and common good because he protects plurality, property and participatory citizenship against Platonic absorption. His is a middle course only within ancient perfectionism, not a rights-based liberal settlement.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the comparison, provides textual-doctrinal axes, states the strongest objections and gives a precisely qualified verdict.

## Q3(b). On what grounds would you accept or reject the idea of capital punishment as an effective deterrent? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Crime and Punishment](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/crime-and-punishment.md) → Capital punishment and deterrence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q3(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Grounds for acceptance/rejection  
**Syllabus unit:** Crime and Punishment — Capital Punishment  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** deterrence · marginal preventive effect · necessity · irreversibility · rights  
**Proposed slug:** `capital-punishment-effective-deterrent`  
**Canonical owner:** [Crime and Punishment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Crime-and-Punishment.md)

**Demand decoding:** Treat deterrence as a comparative empirical claim, then test it against independent moral constraints. Do not assume severity proves effectiveness.

**Argument map:** consequentialist rationale → marginal effect over imprisonment → certainty/swiftness/rationality → empirical uncertainty → irreversibility/unequal administration → retributive objection → rights-bound rejection.

### Model answer

Capital punishment may be defended consequentially if the threat of execution prevents more grave killings than any less harmful feasible penalty. Acceptance therefore requires evidence of a **marginal deterrent effect over secure long-term imprisonment**, not merely the intuition that a harsher penalty must deter more.

That inference is weak. Deterrence depends on perceived certainty and swiftness of detection, not severity alone; many violent crimes are impulsive, affective or committed under low expectations of capture. Where comparative evidence is uncertain, an irreversible punishment bears the heavier justificatory burden.

Independent objections remain even if some deterrence were shown: wrongful execution is irreparable; unequal legal representation and discretionary sentencing threaten consistency; and execution kills a secured person when non-lethal incapacitation is available. *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) restricts the Indian penalty to the “rarest of rare,” while Law Commission Report No. 262 (2015) recommended abolition for ordinary crimes—illustrations of, not answers to, the moral dispute.

**Objection:** justice to victims and public denunciation require death for exceptionally culpable murder. **Reply:** that is retribution, not deterrence, and must be defended separately; severe, reviewable imprisonment can express censure without irreversible error.

**Assessment:** I reject capital punishment as an established effective deterrent because the comparative benefit is unproven and less final alternatives exist. Even demonstrated utility would remain limited by dignity, proportionality and fallibility.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates empirical effectiveness from moral permissibility and gives a clear, qualified position.

## Q3(c). Is economic development a necessary condition, sufficient condition, both or neither, in order to achieve social progress? Give reasons and justifications for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Development and Social Progress](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/development-and-social-progress.md) → necessity and sufficiency of economic development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q3(c) · 15 marks (10+5) · Difficulty: High · Demand: Logical classification with justification  
**Syllabus unit:** Development and Social Progress  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** economic development · capabilities · multidimensional social progress · necessary/sufficient conditions  
**Proposed slug:** `economic-development-necessary-sufficient-social-progress`  
**Canonical owner:** [Development and Social Progress](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Development-Social-Progress.md)

**Demand decoding:** State one of the four logical options explicitly, define both terms, test necessity and sufficiency separately, and distinguish economic development from a material capability floor.

**Argument map:** define progress multidimensionally → not sufficient → not strictly necessary → material-threshold objection → distinguish resources from growth → neither, but important enabling condition.

### Model answer

Under a multidimensional conception of social progress, economic development is **neither strictly necessary nor sufficient**, though adequate material capabilities are normally an important enabling condition.

It is **not sufficient** because increased output, industrialisation or income can coexist with caste and gender domination, authoritarian rule, ecological destruction and unequal access to health or education. Social progress additionally concerns dignity, freedom, justice, participation, recognition and sustainability. Resources must be fairly converted into capabilities.

It is **not strictly necessary** for every instance of progress. A society may expand equal legal status, democratic accountability, non-discrimination or freedom of expression without first increasing aggregate production. These are genuine advances in social relations.

**Objection:** enduring freedom and equality cannot survive severe deprivation; therefore economic development is necessary. **Reply:** this establishes the need for a **material floor**—nutrition, health, education and institutional capacity—not the stronger claim that growth or industrial transformation must precede every social improvement.

Conversely, adequate resources do not automatically become valuable functionings; distribution, public action and social norms mediate conversion.

**Assessment:** the correct logical answer is “neither.” In practice, inclusive and sustainable economic development strongly supports progress, but only when subordinated to human capabilities, equal status and ecological limits.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the formal options, independently tests necessity and sufficiency, and preserves the material-threshold qualification.

## Q4(a). Discuss gender equality as a necessary condition to achieve empowerment of women. Also examine the role of women empowerment in curbing the menace of female foeticide.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Gender Discrimination](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/gender-discrimination.md) → Equality, empowerment and female foeticide · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q4(a) · 20 marks (10+10) · Difficulty: High · Demand: Discussion plus causal examination  
**Syllabus unit:** Gender Discrimination — Female Foeticide; Empowerment  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** formal/substantive equality · capabilities and agency · son preference · constrained reproductive choice  
**Proposed slug:** `gender-equality-empowerment-female-foeticide`  
**Canonical owner:** [Gender Discrimination](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Gender-Discrimination.md)

**Demand decoding:** Treat both halves proportionately. Explain why equality is necessary for empowerment, then show mechanisms by which empowerment can reduce sex selection without blaming women or treating technology as the sole cause.

**Argument map:** equal status/rights/resources → capability and agency → empowerment beyond formal equality → patriarchal son preference → education/property/income/voice/bodily autonomy → technology/enforcement complement → autonomy objection → structural response.

### Model answer

Gender equality is necessary for women’s empowerment because empowerment means durable capability to shape one’s life, resources and governing norms, not merely nominal participation. **Formal equality** secures equal legal status; **substantive equality** addresses unequal starting points, unpaid care, violence and inherited exclusion; **relational equality** removes domination and stigma. Without equal education, property, mobility, bodily autonomy and public voice, “choice” may reflect adaptive preference or dependency rather than agency.

Equality is not by itself sufficient: rights require enforceable remedies, collective organisation and control over material resources. Nevertheless, empowerment without equal status is contradictory, because power granted as benevolence can be withdrawn.

Female foeticide is sex-selective termination rooted in the devaluation of daughters. Patrilineal inheritance, son-centred ritual and old-age expectations, dowry pressures, unequal earning capacity and family control of reproduction generate the preference; diagnostic technology enables but does not create it.

Women’s empowerment can curb it by:

- increasing education, income, land and inheritance control, reducing perceived economic dependency;
- strengthening decision-making and freedom from reproductive coercion;
- increasing public representation and collective resistance to discriminatory norms;
- making daughters visible as equal inheritors and agents rather than burdens.

**Objection:** empowered women may still internalise son preference, while prohibition can become surveillance of pregnant women. **Reply:** empowerment must be transformative and relational, involving men, family institutions and property norms. Regulation should target discriminatory diagnostic practices, coercive households and providers while protecting health and autonomy. The PCPNDT Act 1994 addresses enabling practices but cannot replace status reform.

## Q4(b). What insights does the Arthasastra offer with regard to the concept of sovereignty? Does it have any relevance in the modern times? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → Kauṭilya and the *Arthaśāstra* · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty — Kautilya  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** seven-limbed state (*saptāṅga*) · calibrated coercion (*daṇḍanīti*) · circle of states (*maṇḍala*) · security and welfare (*yogakṣema*)  
**Proposed slug:** `arthashastra-sovereignty-modern-relevance`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** Present sovereignty as institutional capacity, coercive order, welfare and external strategy; identify what can and cannot be transferred to constitutional democracy.

### Model answer

The *Arthaśāstra* offers a functional rather than merely juristic account of sovereignty. The ruler (*svāmī*) is central, but effective rule depends on the seven limbs (*saptāṅga*): ruler, ministers, territory-population, fortified centre, treasury, force and ally. Sovereignty is thus organised capacity, not naked command.

Power must secure subjects’ welfare and security (*yogakṣema*); misrule weakens the state itself. **Objections:** the text presupposes monarchy, tolerates extensive espionage and can subordinate morality to raison d’état. Its unequal interstate order differs from legal sovereign equality, while modern constitutionalism subjects government to rights, elections and international commitments.

**Reply and assessment:** modern relevance requires selective appropriation, not transplantation. Retain institutional interdependence, strategic realism and welfare orientation; reject monarchical legitimacy and unconstrained coercion. Kauṭilya illuminates how sovereignty is sustained, but constitutional rights determine how it may legitimately be exercised.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives four distinct insights, modern applications, limits and a reasoned transfer principle.

### Model answer

## Q5(e). Explain the analogical nature of religious language.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Language](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-language.md) → Analogical language · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q5(e) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand: Explanation  
**Syllabus unit:** Nature of Religious Language — Analogical and Symbolic  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Aquinas · analogy between univocity and equivocity · perfection and mode of signification  
**Proposed slug:** `analogical-nature-religious-language-aquinas`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Language](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Language.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain why analogy is needed, what is genuinely affirmed of God, later attribution/proportionality forms, and the intelligibility objection.

**Argument map:** univocity anthropomorphises → equivocity empties meaning → analogous predication → perfection truly signified, creaturely mode denied → attribution/proportionality → vagueness objection → causal/participatory reply.

### Model answer

Aquinas places religious predication between **univocity** and **equivocity**. If “good” means exactly the same of God and creatures, God is anthropomorphised; if it means wholly differently, “God is good” communicates nothing.

Analogical language preserves ordered similarity within greater difference. Perfections such as goodness and wisdom are genuinely present in God essentially and eminently. The **perfection signified** (*res significata*) applies truly, while the creature-derived **mode of signification** (*modus significandi*) is inadequate to infinite divine being.

Later Thomists distinguish analogy of attribution—secondary uses ordered to a primary case, as medicine is “healthy” by causing health—and proportionality: divine goodness belongs to God in an infinite mode as creaturely goodness belongs finitely to creatures. **Objection:** unless the similarity is independently specified, analogy risks disguised equivocation. **Reply:** causal participation and graded perfections supply a rational connection, though never exhaustive comprehension.

**Assessment:** analogy permits truth-apt God-talk without literal anthropomorphism, but its semantic precision remains contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the mediating logic, technical distinction, examples and main objection.

**Metadata:** Official Q6(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Necessity test with reasons  
**Syllabus unit:** Soul — Immortality; Rebirth and Liberation  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** logical necessity · substantial soul · karmic rebirth · Buddhist and one-life countermodels  
**Proposed slug:** `immortality-rebirth-necessary-robust-liberation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Soul, Immortality, Rebirth and Liberation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Soul-Immortality-Rebirth.md)

**Demand decoding:** Test two claims separately: whether every robust liberation needs an immortal substantial soul and whether it needs serial rebirth. Use genuine countermodels and distinguish cross-tradition possibility from identity of meanings.

**Argument map:** Indian substantialist yes-case → Buddhism refutes immortal-soul necessity but retains rebirth → one-life salvation refutes rebirth necessity → identity/beneficiary objection → conventional or divinely restored person replies → neither universally necessary, both tradition-relative.

### Model answer

The two alleged necessities must be separated: must liberation presuppose **an immortal substantial soul**, and must it presuppose **a cycle of rebirth**? Both are integral to several Indian systems, but neither is logically necessary for every robust conception of release.

Nyāya, Vedānta and Jainism connect bondage across lives to an enduring self, though they disagree about its nature and liberated state. Rebirth extends moral causation and makes liberation release from *saṃsāra*. These theories show coherence, not universal necessity.

Buddhism provides the decisive counterexample to soul-immortality. It denies an unchanging self (*anātman*) yet explains continuity through dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) and karmic causal succession. *Nirvāṇa* is liberation from suffering and craving without an eternal substance. This refutes the necessity of an immortal soul, although Buddhism still accepts rebirth.

To test rebirth separately, one-life traditions of salvation or resurrection conceive robust deliverance from sin, death or estrangement without serial karmic births. Resurrection is not rebirth: it is divine restoration of the person, not repeated earthly embodiment. Advaitic embodied liberation (*jīvanmukti*) shows that release need not await another birth, but cannot alone refute rebirth’s systemic role because Advaita accepts it.

**Objections:** without an enduring subject, “who” is liberated? Buddhist conventional personhood and causal continuity reject the demand for numerical substance. Without prior births, liberation may lack a sufficiently deep bondage. A one-life account replies that alienation or sin can be profound without beginningless transmigration.

**Assessment:** neither immortal soul nor rebirth is universally necessary, and neither is sufficient: endless survival may perpetuate bondage. Each can be internally indispensable to a tradition’s conception, so comparison must not pretend that *mokṣa*, *nirvāṇa* and salvation are identical.

**Why this earns marks:** It independently tests both necessities, uses accurate countermodels and states the semantic qualification.

**Syllabus unit:** Notions of God — Attributes; Relation to Man and the World  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Spinoza · one substance · *Deus sive Natura* · attributes and modes · necessity  
**Proposed slug:** `spinoza-god-substance-attributes-modes`  
**Canonical owner:** [Notions of God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Notions-of-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Show that God is not appended to the metaphysics but is the one infinite substance expressed through attributes and modes; then assess pantheism, determinism and religious adequacy.

### Model answer

Spinoza’s God is embedded in, indeed identical with, his metaphysics of substance. Substance is “in itself and conceived through itself”; because two substances cannot share an attribute and an absolutely infinite substance cannot be limited by another, only one substance exists: **God or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*)**.

God possesses infinitely many attributes, each expressing eternal and infinite essence. Human beings know chiefly thought and extension. Spinoza replies that freedom is action from one’s own nature, paradigmatically divine self-determination, and human blessedness is the intellectual love of God through adequate understanding.

**Assessment:** Spinoza offers a rigorous pantheistic God as infinite intelligibility and immanence, but at the cost of classical personality, providence and libertarian freedom. Its religious force depends on whether understanding necessary unity can replace reciprocal theism.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives God from substance and attributes, explains modes and evaluates the exact theological costs.

### Model answer

Buddhism is a paradigmatic **non-theistic religion**: it does not make an omnipotent creator necessary to explain the world, morality or liberation. Questions remain about karmic continuity without a self and the empirical warrant of rebirth.

**Assessment:** Buddhism decisively shows that creator-belief is not necessary for religion. It replaces divine creation and grace with conditioned causation, disciplined transformation and insight, while retaining every major religious dimension except a supreme creator.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies doctrinal depth, both senses of the Middle Path, the complete religious structure, objections and a defended classification.

**Demand decoding:** Identify the target—divine disclosure, not sincerity—then assess multiple defeasible criteria and explicitly state one’s own hierarchy of justification.

**Argument map:** testimony → coherence → experience/signs → moral fruit/tradition → rival revelations → circularity objection → cumulative but non-conclusive warrant → personal critical verdict.

### Model answer

To justify revelation is to justify the claim that some event or proposition was **divinely disclosed**, not merely that a recipient sincerely believed it.

Several defeasible grounds are possible. **Testimony** gains force from proximity, competence, independence and reliable transmission, though sincerity does not prevent error. **Coherence** can defeat contradiction, but consistency alone does not prove divine origin. **Religious experience** may prima facie warrant the experiencer; an unusual sign supports an occurrence without uniquely identifying a divine cause. **Moral fruits**—truthfulness, compassion and transformation—support an interpretation, though false beliefs can also benefit. Rival revelations must be compared: incompatible claims cannot all be literally true in the same respect.

**Objection:** every standard is tradition-dependent and circular. **Reply:** publicly contestable evidence, declared assumptions and cumulative comparison reduce, but cannot eliminate, circularity.

**My assessment:** independent testimony and coherence should operate as strong negative filters; experience and moral fruit provide positive but defeasible warrant. Self-authentication—“the text is divine because the text says so”—is viciously circular. Revelation may be rationally trusted, but certainty is unjustified where rival claims and natural explanations remain unresolved.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates the epistemic target, evaluates each test, addresses plural revelation and fulfils “your own comments.”

## Q7(c). Present an exposition of ontological proof for the existence of God along with its criticism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Proofs for God](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/proofs-for-god.md) → Ontological proof · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q7(c) · 15 marks (10+5) · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand: Exposition plus criticism  
**Syllabus unit:** Proofs for the Existence of God and their Critique  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Anselm · a priori proof · Gaunilo · Kant  
**Proposed slug:** `ontological-proof-anselm-criticism`  
**Canonical owner:** [Proofs for God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Proofs-for-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Give the proof’s inferential structure before criticism; distinguish conceptual coherence, existence as predicate and modal reformulation.

**Argument map:** greatest conceivable being → understanding → real existence greater → denial self-defeating → Gaunilo parody → Kant predicate objection → modal reply → possibility burden.

### Model answer

Anselm’s ontological proof is *a priori*: it proceeds from the concept of God rather than observed features of the world. God is “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.” Even the fool who denies God understands this concept, so it exists in the understanding. If the greatest conceivable being existed only mentally, a greater being—existing both mentally and in reality—could be conceived. That contradicts the definition; therefore God exists in reality.

**Criticisms:** Gaunilo’s perfect-island parody argues that defining the greatest island cannot produce it, though Anselm replies that only a necessary being has the relevant concept. Kant’s deeper objection is that existence is not a real predicate adding a perfection to a concept: one hundred real coins contain no more conceptual properties than one hundred possible coins. Definitions specify what something would be, not whether it is instantiated.

Modal versions argue that if a maximally great being is possible, it exists necessarily. Yet the possibility premise bears the burden; a critic may deny that the divine attributes are jointly coherent.

**Assessment:** the proof reveals that God, if possible in the stipulated maximal sense, cannot exist contingently. It does not non-circularly establish that such a being is possible or instantiated.

**Why this earns marks:** It proportions exposition and criticism to 10+5 while noting the strongest contemporary repair and residual burden.

## Q8(a). Distinguish between cognitivist and non-cognitivist account of religious language. Does the cognitivist account lead to any contradiction? Answer with reference to the philosophical views of R. B. Braithwaite.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Language](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-language.md) → Cognitivism, non-cognitivism and Braithwaite · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q8(a) · 20 marks (10+10) · Difficulty: High · Demand: Distinction plus contradiction assessment  
**Syllabus unit:** Nature of Religious Language — Cognitivist and Non-cognitive  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** truth-apt God-talk · moral-conative meaning · agapeistic policy · evil/verification pressures  
**Proposed slug:** `cognitivist-noncognitivist-religious-language-braithwaite`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Language](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Language.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare semantic function and truth-aptness, accurately state Braithwaite, identify rather than merely assert alleged contradictions, and evaluate what his account saves and loses.

**Argument map:** cognitivism/truth claim → verification and evil pressures → non-cognitivist practical meaning → Braithwaite intention plus stories → contradiction not automatic → reduction objection → mixed assessment.

### Model answer

A **cognitivist** account treats religious utterances such as “God exists” or “God is loving” as assertions about reality that are true or false. Aquinas, Swinburne and realist theology differ over evidence and predication but retain truth-aptness. A **non-cognitivist** account locates primary meaning elsewhere—in expression, commitment, orientation or a form of life—rather than in describing supernatural facts.

R. B. Braithwaite offers a moral-conative version. Religious assertions express a person’s **intention to follow an agapeistic moral policy**, especially a life of self-giving love. Religious stories sustain and shape this intention without requiring that every narrative be historically or metaphysically true. Thus religious language may be meaningful and distinctive even if it fails empirical verification.

Does cognitivism produce contradiction? One alleged case joins “God is perfectly loving and powerful” with apparently pointless evil. But this is not formally contradictory without added premises about what love and power would prevent; it becomes the problem of evil. A second charge says transcendent factual claims are meaningless because unverifiable. That depends on Ayer’s contested verification criterion and is not an internal contradiction in cognitivism. Different religions may also assert mutually incompatible propositions, but contradiction between accounts does not make the very practice of cognitive God-talk contradictory.

**Objections to Braithwaite:** believers ordinarily intend more than a moral policy; worship, prayer and hope presuppose a real object. Two persons may adopt the same loving policy while disagreeing fundamentally about God, so the theory underdescribes religion. Stories may motivate immoral as well as agapeistic policies.

**Reply:** Braithwaite explains practical meaning and avoids pseudo-scientific treatment of faith; he need not prove that believers never make factual claims, only offer a philosophical analysis of their religious use.

**Assessment:** cognitivism faces evidential and semantic pressures but no automatic contradiction. Braithwaite successfully rescues moral-practical significance at the cost of much realist content. A mixed account may recognise assertion, symbol and commitment together.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both 10-mark halves, accurately reconstructs Braithwaite and distinguishes contradiction from verification and evidential difficulty.

## Q8(b). “In order to be conceived as the ultimate cause of the world, God must necessarily have some form of physical manifestation.” Do you agree with this view? Give reasons and justifications for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Notions of God](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/notions-of-god.md) → God as ultimate cause and physical manifestation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q8(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Agreement test with reasons  
**Syllabus unit:** Notions of God — Relation to Man and the World  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** ultimacy · incorporeality · classical theism · Spinoza · Rāmānuja’s body-of-God relation  
**Proposed slug:** `ultimate-cause-god-physical-manifestation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Notions of God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Notions-of-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Clarify “ultimate cause” and “physical manifestation,” then test classical theist, pantheist and qualified-nondual models. Avoid equating personhood with embodiment.

**Argument map:** ultimate cause requires ontological independence → physical form is composite/conditioned → classical incorporeal cause → manifestation not necessity → Spinoza/Rāmānuja complications → causal-intelligibility objection → qualified disagreement.

### Model answer

I disagree that physical manifestation is **necessary** for God to be the world’s ultimate cause. An ultimate cause is invoked as the non-dependent ground of dependent reality. A physical body, however, is ordinarily extended, composite, located and conditioned by parts or laws; making embodiment essential may therefore compromise ultimacy.

Classical theism conceives God as incorporeal creator and sustainer. Causal efficacy does not require bodily contact: divine causation is not one physical event among others. Advaita likewise treats Brahman as non-objectifiable and non-physical, while the empirical world depends upon it. A personal God need not be anthropomorphic; knowledge and will can be predicated analogically.

Alternative systems do not establish necessity. Spinoza identifies God/Nature with infinite substance expressed under extension and thought, not one finite body. Rāmānuja calls souls and matter Brahman’s body (*śarīra–śarīrī*), signifying dependence and divine control, not anthropomorphic confinement. Manifestation may mediate worship without constituting ultimate causality.

**Objection:** a wholly non-physical cause is unintelligible and disconnected from the physical world. **Reply:** embodiment would relocate rather than solve the interaction problem; the required task is an account of ontological dependence, not visual localisation.

**Assessment:** physical manifestation is possible within particular religions but neither conceptually nor metaphysically necessary for ultimate causation. Indeed, essential corporeality often conflicts with the independence ultimacy requires.

**Why this earns marks:** It explicitly answers the modal claim, compares three systems and distinguishes manifestation, embodiment and causation.

## Q8(c). Discuss the main features of religious experience according to Advaita Vedānta.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Experience](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-experience.md) → Advaita Vedānta · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Metadata:** Official Q8(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Doctrinal discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Religious Experience — Nature and Object (Indian)  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** immediate realisation (*aparokṣānubhūti*) · self–Brahman identity · non-duality · self-luminosity · ineffability  
**Proposed slug:** `advaita-vedanta-religious-experience-features`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Experience](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Experience.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain nature, object, epistemic status, preparation and liberating consequence; distinguish stable knowledge from a transient trance and Brahman’s ineffability from *māyā*’s *anirvacanīyatā*.

**Argument map:** ignorance and subject–object duality → scripture/reflection/contemplation → immediate non-dual recognition → self-luminous and non-relational → beyond objectifying speech → liberation while living → subjectivity/plurality objections → transformative but tradition-mediated assessment.

### Model answer

Advaita Vedānta understands the highest religious experience as immediate, non-dual realisation (*aparokṣānubhūti*) that the self (*ātman*) is Brahman. It is not an encounter between an independent subject and divine object; the presumed duality is sublated in knowledge expressed by “I am Brahman” (*aham brahmāsmi*).

Its principal features are:

- **immediacy:** liberating knowledge is directly assimilated, not mere inferential belief;
- **non-duality:** knower, knowing and known are not ultimately separate;
- **self-luminosity:** consciousness does not require another cognition to reveal it;
- **ineffability:** objectifying language fails before Brahman—“whence words return” (*yato vāco nivartante*)—so negation (*neti neti*) and indirect indication are used;
- **bliss and freedom:** knowledge destroys ignorance (*avidyā*) and bondage, permitting liberation while living (*jīvanmukti*);
- **disciplined preparation:** scriptural hearing, reflection and contemplation (*śravaṇa–manana–nididhyāsana*) remove doubt and habitual error.

A transient absorption (*nirvikalpa samādhi*) should not be equated with stable liberating knowledge. Brahman should not be called *anirvacanīya*: that term describes *māyā* or world-appearance as neither absolutely real nor unreal.

**Objections:** private experience may be psychologically produced, and rival traditions report personal or no-self ultimates. Advaita replies that this is self-recognition rather than sensory episode and tests it through scripture, reasoning and transformation. Yet tradition-mediated interpretation remains a genuine epistemic pressure.

**Assessment:** Advaitic religious experience is best understood as stable non-dual knowledge with liberating consequence, not episodic emotion. Its philosophical power lies in overcoming subject–object duality; its public verifiability remains limited.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the central features, method, technical safeguards, objections and a precise evaluation.
