## Q1. Globalisation, formal employment and informalisation
**Primary route:** Economy → [Employment, Labour Codes, Skills and Demographic Dividend](../../topics/economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend.md) → Informalisation of work · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 1  
**Official question:** How globalization has led to the reduction of employment in the formal sector of the Indian economy? Is increased informalization detrimental to the development of the country?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Employment → Informalisation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Explain causation + Assess developmental effects  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain the channels through which global competition can reduce secure formal jobs, then judge informalisation rather than treating all flexibility or self-employment as inherently harmful.

### Compact framework
`global competition + subcontracting + technology → formal job compression; informality → flexibility/entry but low productivity, protection and demand`

### Model answer
Globalisation did not simply reduce employment; it altered its composition. Import competition and global value-chain pressures induced firms to cut costs through automation, outsourcing and contractual labour. Trade and financial volatility discouraged permanent hiring, while lead firms shifted labour-intensive stages to small suppliers outside effective labour-law and social-security coverage. Organised-sector output could therefore rise without comparable regular employment—India’s “jobless growth”.

Informal work can provide entry-level livelihoods, entrepreneurship and flexibility, especially where formal jobs are scarce. However, pervasive informalisation is developmentally damaging because it produces:

- low and uncertain wages, unsafe work and weak bargaining power;
- exclusion from pensions, insurance and paid leave;
- low training, technology adoption and labour productivity;
- a narrow tax and contribution base;
- weak household demand and inter-generational insecurity; and
- unfair competition for compliant firms.

The answer is not to suppress small enterprises, but to make formalisation beneficial: simpler registration and compliance, portable social security, affordable credit and technology, skill development, labour-intensive export clusters, and enforcement of core wage and safety standards across subcontracting chains.

Thus globalisation creates opportunities, but without domestic institutions its competitive pressures can socialise risk onto workers and weaken inclusive development.

### Examiner rationale
The answer distinguishes output globalisation from employment formalisation, explains firm-level mechanisms and gives a qualified verdict with worker- and enterprise-centred remedies.

## Q2. Gender budgeting and women’s empowerment
**Primary route:** Economy → [Union Budget, Fiscal Policy and Deficit Indicators](../../topics/economy/union-budget-fiscal-policy-and-deficit-indicators.md) → Gender-responsive budgeting · **Secondary route:** [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 2  
**Official question:** Women empowerment in India needs gender budgeting. What are the requirements and status of gender budgeting in the Indian context?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Public Finance → Gender Budgeting  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/union-budget-fiscal-policy-and-deficit-indicators`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Explain requirements + Assess status  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/09_Union-Budget-Fiscal-Policy-and-Deficit-Indicators.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/09_Union-Budget-Fiscal-Policy-and-Deficit-Indicators.md)

### Demand decoding
Define gender budgeting as analysis and redesign of ordinary public expenditure, identify institutional requirements, and assess India’s progress and limitations by 2016.

### Compact framework
`gender-disaggregated diagnosis → allocation → implementation → outcome audit; established statement/cells but weak coverage and outcome linkage`

### Model answer
Gender budgeting is not a separate budget for women. It examines how taxation and public expenditure affect women and men differently, and redesigns programmes to close capability, safety and economic-participation gaps.

Its requirements are:

- sex-disaggregated data and gender-sensitive beneficiary baselines;
- needs assessment across health, education, assets, unpaid care, mobility and safety;
- measurable outputs and outcomes, not merely expenditure labels;
- Gender Budget Cells in ministries and capacity in States and local bodies;
- convergence among sectoral departments;
- participation of women’s groups; and
- expenditure tracking, evaluation and legislative audit.

India institutionalised a **Gender Budget Statement in 2005–06**, separating schemes wholly directed to women from those with a substantial women’s component. Gender Budget Cells and Finance Ministry guidance widened ministerial participation, while programmes in maternal health, education, livelihoods and protection acquired visibility.

Yet classification often became an accounting exercise: departments reported notional shares without proving benefits; unpaid care and tax incidence received little attention; local capacity and sex-disaggregated data remained weak; and allocations did not necessarily translate into outcomes.

Gender budgeting can empower women only when embedded through the entire budget cycle—diagnosis, design, allocation, implementation and gender audit—and combined with property rights, services, safety and decent employment.

### Examiner rationale
The response defines the instrument correctly, supplies the 2005–06 institutional anchor and balances achievements with implementation and outcome deficits.

## Q3. Jan-Dhan Yojana and financial inclusion
**Primary route:** Economy → [Banking Structure, NBFCs and Financial Regulation](../../topics/economy/banking-structure-nbfcs-and-financial-regulation.md) → Financial inclusion · **Secondary route:** [Services, Digital Economy, Fintech and Platform Markets](../../topics/economy/services-digital-economy-fintech-and-platform-markets.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 3  
**Official question:** Pradhan Mantri Jan-Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) is necessary for bringing unbanked to the institutional finance fold. Do you agree with this for financial inclusion of the poorer section of the Indian society? Give arguments to justify your opinion.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Banking → Financial Inclusion  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/banking-structure-nbfcs-and-financial-regulation`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Take a position + Justify with qualifications  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/05_Banking-Structure-NBFCs-and-Financial-Regulation.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/05_Banking-Structure-NBFCs-and-Financial-Regulation.md)

### Demand decoding
Assess whether account opening is necessary and whether it is sufficient for meaningful inclusion of poor households.

### Compact framework
`basic account + RuPay + BC + DBT → access/formality; usage + credit + literacy + consumer protection → meaningful inclusion`

### Model answer
I agree that PMJDY is necessary, but it is not sufficient. Launched in 2014, it provided a basic bank account, RuPay card, accident-insurance cover, overdraft eligibility and access through bank branches and Business Correspondents. Linked with Aadhaar and mobile connectivity, such accounts can receive wages, pensions and subsidies directly, reduce leakage and travel costs, promote saving, and create a transaction history useful for formal credit.

For poor households, this weakens dependence on moneylenders and creates an entry point to insurance, remittances and pensions.

However, financial inclusion means **regular, affordable and suitable use**, not an account count. Dormant or zero-balance accounts, distant or cash-starved correspondents, poor digital connectivity, low literacy, hidden charges and biometric failures can exclude users. An overdraft is not a substitute for adequate productive credit; aggressive sales or fraud can create new vulnerability.

PMJDY should therefore be reinforced through reliable Business Correspondents, interoperable payments, grievance redress, financial literacy, small savings and micro-insurance, responsible credit, and SHG-bank linkage. Gender and regional usage data should guide policy.

Thus PMJDY provides essential financial infrastructure; sustained usage, trust and a complete range of appropriate services convert access into inclusion.

### Examiner rationale
The answer adopts a defensible “necessary, not sufficient” thesis, traces inclusion mechanisms and distinguishes access statistics from usage and welfare outcomes.

## Q4. Smart Cities and Smart Villages
**Primary route:** Economy → [Infrastructure, PPPs, Logistics and Public Investment](../../topics/economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment.md) → Urban and rural infrastructure · **Secondary route:** [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 4  
**Official question:** What are ‘Smart Cities’? Examine their relevance for urban development in India. Will it increase rural-urban differences? Give arguments for ‘Smart Villages’ in the light of PURA and RURBAN Mission.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Infrastructure → Spatial Development  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Define + Examine + Assess spatial inequality + Argue an alternative  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md)

### Demand decoding
Avoid equating “smart” with sensors alone; examine urban utility and distributional risks, then connect PURA and the Shyama Prasad Mukherji Rurban Mission to rural clusters.

### Compact framework
`technology + institutions + core services → liveable city; enclave investment risks divergence; rural connectivity + amenities + livelihoods → smart village`

### Model answer
Smart Cities use integrated planning, reliable core services, accountable institutions and appropriate technology to improve liveability, sustainability and economic opportunity. India’s 2015 Mission combined area-based development with pan-city solutions such as intelligent transport, water metering, waste management and e-governance.

They are relevant because rapid urbanisation has produced congestion, pollution, housing shortages, weak municipal finance and unreliable services. Demonstration projects can improve planning and service standards. However, small retrofitted enclaves, technology-first procurement, weak urban local bodies and displacement of informal settlements may produce “islands of excellence”.

If investment and jobs concentrate further in selected cities, rural–urban differences and distress migration may increase. The corrective is a complementary **Smart Villages** strategy:

- **PURA** envisaged physical, electronic and knowledge connectivity leading to economic connectivity;
- the **Shyama Prasad Mukherji Rurban Mission, 2016** sought cluster-based development of villages preserving rural character while providing urban-like amenities;
- broadband, roads, skills, health, schools, sanitation, storage, agro-processing and local enterprises can create non-farm employment near homes.

Smartness should therefore describe outcomes—universal services, resilience and citizen participation—not gadget density. Balanced development requires empowered municipalities alongside viable rural clusters and stronger intermediate towns, so connectivity narrows rather than digitises spatial inequality.

### Examiner rationale
The answer addresses all four demands, uses the correct mission chronology and evaluates both urban benefits and enclave-based inequality.

## Q5. FDI need, intent–realisation gap and remedies
**Primary route:** Economy → [Industrial Policy, 1991 Reforms, PSUs and Disinvestment](../../topics/economy/industrial-policy-1991-reforms-psus-and-disinvestment.md) → Foreign direct investment · **Secondary route:** [Balance of Payments, Exchange Rates and Forex Reserves](../../topics/economy/balance-of-payments-exchange-rates-and-forex-reserves.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 5  
**Official question:** Justify the need for FDI for the development of the Indian economy. Why there is gap between MOUs signed and actual FDIs? Suggest remedial steps to be taken for increasing actual FDIs in India.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Investment → FDI Realisation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/industrial-policy-1991-reforms-psus-and-disinvestment`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Justify + Diagnose + Prescribe  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/16_Industrial-Policy-1991-Reforms-PSUs-and-Disinvestment.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/16_Industrial-Policy-1991-Reforms-PSUs-and-Disinvestment.md)

### Demand decoding
Distinguish non-binding investment intentions from realised equity flows and explain why enabling conditions determine conversion.

### Compact framework
`FDI = patient capital + technology + markets; MoU intention − land/clearance/infrastructure/demand certainty = realisation gap`

### Model answer
FDI supplies relatively stable, non-debt capital for India’s large infrastructure and industrial requirements. It can bring technology, management practices, global marketing networks, export access and competitive pressure, while creating jobs and supplementing domestic savings. Its developmental value is greatest when it generates local linkages and capabilities rather than merely acquiring existing assets.

An MoU, however, records intention, not a completed investment. Conversion may fail because of:

- delayed land acquisition, environmental and sectoral clearances;
- inadequate power, logistics and industrial infrastructure;
- retrospective or uncertain taxation and changing regulation;
- slow contracts and commercial-dispute resolution;
- federal coordination failures and multiple approvals;
- financing difficulties, weak demand or altered global conditions; and
- inflated announcements made before detailed feasibility.

India should provide time-bound clearances without diluting scrutiny; offer plug-and-play industrial land and reliable infrastructure; stabilise tax and sectoral policy; speed commercial courts and arbitration; rationalise approval routes; and strengthen Centre–State coordination. Public disclosure should distinguish proposals, approvals and realised inflows.

Local supplier development, skills and research partnerships should maximise spillovers, while competition and environmental safeguards prevent enclaves or regulatory races.

Thus higher actual FDI depends less on signing ceremonies than on credible institutions, viable projects and predictable implementation.

### Examiner rationale
The answer separates FDI’s potential benefits from automatic development, correctly explains the MoU–realisation gap and proposes institutionally targeted remedies.

## Q6. “Careless and useless manpower” and inclusive growth
**Primary route:** Economy → [Employment, Labour Codes, Skills and Demographic Dividend](../../topics/economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend.md) → Employability and demographic dividend · **Secondary route:** [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 6  
**Official question:** Comment on the challenges for inclusive growth which include careless and useless manpower in the Indian context. Suggest measures to be taken for facing these challenges.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Inclusive Growth → Human Capital and Employability  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Interpret unusual phrase + Analyse + Prescribe  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md)

### Demand decoding
Treat the phrase critically as a human-capital and labour-utilisation failure, not as a moral condemnation of unemployed people.

### Compact framework
`poor capability + skill mismatch + jobless growth → underused labour; health/education/skills + job creation + inclusion → dividend`

### Model answer
The expression “careless and useless manpower” is misleading: people are not useless; institutions may leave their capabilities undeveloped or underutilised. India’s inclusive-growth challenge is the coexistence of a young population with malnutrition, poor learning, skill mismatch, open unemployment, disguised farm employment and low female labour-force participation.

Employers report unemployability while educated youth lack suitable jobs. Informal work offers low productivity and little social security; social and regional barriers exclude capable workers. Jobless growth can convert the demographic dividend into frustration and inequality.

Measures should include:

- foundational health, nutrition and school learning;
- vocational education, apprenticeships and employer-designed standards under the National Skill Development Mission;
- labour-intensive manufacturing, construction, tourism and care services;
- support for MSMEs through credit, clusters, technology and market access;
- modern agriculture, agro-processing and rural non-farm work;
- safe transport, childcare and equal opportunity for women;
- career information, migration support and portable social protection; and
- outcome-based evaluation of training through placement, retention and wage gains.

MGNREGA can provide a floor during transition, but cannot replace productive structural transformation.

Inclusive growth requires both employable capabilities and enough decent jobs. Responsibility lies jointly with education systems, firms and the State—not solely with the worker.

### Examiner rationale
The answer resolves the problematic wording, links human-capital deficits to labour demand and offers a balanced capability-plus-job-creation strategy.

## Q7. Water-use efficiency and micro-irrigation
**Primary route:** Economy → [Irrigation, Inputs, Credit, Insurance and Sustainable Agriculture](../../topics/economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture.md) → Micro-irrigation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 7  
**Official question:** What is water-use efficiency? Describe the role of micro-irrigation in increasing the water-use efficiency.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Irrigation Efficiency  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Define + Explain mechanism  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md)

### Demand decoding
Define efficiency as useful crop output or beneficial consumption per unit of water, then explain the physical and agronomic mechanisms of drip and sprinkler systems with limits.

### Compact framework
`precise quantity + right place/time → less conveyance, runoff and evaporation → more crop per drop`

### Model answer
Water-use efficiency is the useful crop output, biomass or economic value obtained per unit of water withdrawn or consumed. At field level it concerns the proportion of supplied water beneficially used through crop evapotranspiration rather than lost through conveyance, evaporation, runoff or deep percolation.

Micro-irrigation raises efficiency by applying controlled quantities near the crop:

- **drip irrigation** delivers water to the root zone through emitters, reducing evaporation, runoff and weed growth;
- **sprinklers** distribute water more uniformly than poorly levelled flood irrigation and suit light soils and undulating land;
- frequent, measured application avoids over-irrigation and waterlogging;
- fertigation places soluble nutrients with irrigation water, improving nutrient uptake and reducing leaching;
- lower pumping and labour needs can reduce cultivation costs; and
- moisture control can improve yield and quality, especially in horticulture, sugarcane and vegetables.

The benefit is not automatic. High initial cost, clogged emitters, poor filtration, fragmented holdings, unreliable maintenance and unsuitable water quality can impair performance. Cheap electricity may also induce farmers to expand irrigated area, offsetting basin-level savings.

**Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana’s “Per Drop More Crop”** support should be combined with extension, crop choice, metering and maintenance. Efficiency must be measured at field and basin scales.

### Examiner rationale
The response defines the numerator and denominator, explains distinct micro-irrigation mechanisms and qualifies field-level savings with rebound and maintenance risks.

## Q8. Allelopathy in irrigated cropping systems
**Primary route:** Economy → [Land Reforms, Green Revolution and Cropping Systems](../../topics/economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems.md) → Biological crop interactions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 8  
**Official question:** What is allelopathy? Discuss its role in major cropping systems of irrigated agriculture.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Cropping Systems  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Define scientific concept + Apply to cropping systems  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain chemical interaction among plants and microorganisms, then show both harmful residue effects and beneficial weed-management uses in irrigated rotations.

### Compact framework
`allelochemicals from roots/residues → inhibit or stimulate neighbours; manage rotations/residues to avoid toxicity and suppress weeds`

### Model answer
Allelopathy is the direct or indirect effect—usually inhibitory, sometimes stimulatory—of one plant or microorganism on another through biochemicals released by roots, volatilisation, leaching or decomposing residues. These **allelochemicals** affect germination, root growth, nutrient uptake and soil microbes; the process differs from competition for light, water or nutrients.

In irrigated agriculture its role is important because intensive rotations retain large residues and maintain moist conditions favourable to chemical release:

- in rice–wheat systems, poorly decomposed rice straw can temporarily inhibit wheat germination, whereas selected rice residues can suppress weeds;
- sorghum and sunflower residues or extracts possess strong weed-suppressing potential;
- Brassica residues release isothiocyanates with biofumigation effects;
- continuous monocropping may cause autotoxicity and contribute to declining crop performance;
- crop choice and sequence can therefore either accumulate harmful compounds or interrupt weed and pest cycles.

Managed allelopathy can reduce herbicide dependence through cover crops, mulches, residue extracts and allelopathic cultivars. Yet effects vary with dose, soil, temperature, irrigation and decomposition; a residue suppressing weeds may also injure the next crop.

Hence allelopathy should complement, not replace, integrated weed management. Suitable rotations, tested cultivars, residue timing and field validation are essential before exploiting it in major irrigated systems.

### Examiner rationale
The answer distinguishes allelopathy from resource competition, applies it to named crops and balances weed-control potential against autotoxic and crop-injury risks.

## Q9. Land reforms and agricultural development
**Primary route:** Economy → [Land Reforms, Green Revolution and Cropping Systems](../../topics/economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems.md) → Agrarian reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 9  
**Official question:** Discuss the role of land reforms in agricultural development. Identify the factors that were responsible for the success of land reforms in India.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Land Reforms  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Discuss contribution + Identify conditions of success  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md)

### Demand decoding
Connect abolition, tenancy reform, ceilings and consolidation to incentives and equity, then explain why success varied sharply among States.

### Compact framework
`secure/equitable operational holdings → investment + productivity + inclusion; political commitment + records + mobilisation + implementation → success`

### Model answer
Land reforms sought to alter agrarian power and improve the efficiency of cultivation through abolition of intermediaries, tenancy regulation, ceilings and redistribution, consolidation of fragmented holdings and better land records.

Their developmental role was to:

- give cultivators secure rights and incentives to invest in irrigation, soil and technology;
- reduce exploitative rent and absentee landlordism;
- widen access to land, credit and public programmes;
- consolidate plots for efficient operations; and
- weaken feudal relations, raising rural wages, dignity and demand.

Zamindari abolition removed many intermediaries, but overall results were uneven. Ceiling laws suffered exemptions, benami transfers and litigation, while concealed tenancy denied security.

Relative success—such as tenancy reform in **West Bengal’s Operation Barga**, redistribution and social reform in **Kerala**, and consolidation in parts of north-western India—depended on:

- sustained political commitment and clear legislation;
- organised peasant mobilisation and beneficiary awareness;
- accurate, updated land records;
- capable local administration and time-bound adjudication;
- fewer loopholes and effective prevention of evasion; and
- complementary credit, irrigation, extension and markets.

Thus legislation alone did not produce reform; alteration of local power and implementation capacity did. Contemporary priorities include recording tenants and women’s rights, digitising records with safeguards, and enabling viable leasing without dispossession.

### Examiner rationale
The answer covers the main reform instruments, links them to productivity and justice, and explains interstate variation through political economy and administrative capacity.

## Q10. Crop insurance and PMFBY
**Primary route:** Economy → [Irrigation, Inputs, Credit, Insurance and Sustainable Agriculture](../../topics/economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture.md) → Agricultural risk insurance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2016 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 10  
**Official question:** Given the vulnerability of Indian agriculture to vagaries of nature, discuss the need for crop insurance and bring out the salient features of the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY).  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Crop Insurance  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Establish need + State exact scheme features  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md)

### Demand decoding
Link covariate weather risk to farm income and credit, then reproduce the main 2016 PMFBY design without importing later revisions.

### Compact framework
`weather/yield shock → debt and disinvestment; pooled indemnity + low farmer premium + broad risk cover → resilience`

### Model answer
Indian farming faces correlated risks—drought, flood, cyclone, hail, pests and unseasonal rain—against which savings are inadequate. Small holdings, rain dependence and high input costs mean one crop failure can cause debt, distress sale and reduced investment. Insurance can stabilise consumption, protect credit and encourage productive technology.

Launched for **Kharif 2016**, PMFBY:

- capped farmer premiums at **2% for Kharif food and oilseed crops, 1.5% for Rabi food and oilseed crops, and 5% for annual commercial/horticultural crops**;
- required governments to bear the balance of the actuarial premium, with no upper cap on subsidy;
- insured yield loss from prevented sowing and standing crops against notified non-preventable risks;
- covered specified post-harvest losses for crops kept in cut-and-spread condition and localised risks such as inundation, landslide and hailstorm;
- used an area approach and crop-cutting experiments for yield claims; and
- envisaged technology, including smartphones and remote sensing, for faster assessment.

Success depended on timely subsidy, accurate crop data, prompt claims, tenant coverage and awareness. Basis risk remains when individual loss differs from the notified area.

PMFBY was therefore a major redesign, but insurance must complement irrigation, diversification and disaster-resilient farming.

### Examiner rationale
The answer supplies the exact original premium rates and coverage architecture, while distinguishing risk transfer from wider agricultural risk reduction.
