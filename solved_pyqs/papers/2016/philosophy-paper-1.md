### Q1(a). What is the logical necessity for Locke to introduce the concept of secondary qualities? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Locke’s primary–secondary quality distinction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain with reasons · Syllabus unit: Locke—primary and secondary qualities · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Slug:** `locke-logical-need-secondary-qualities`

**Demand decoding:** Show why Locke’s corpuscular realism requires a class of object-dependent powers whose ideas do not resemble them.

**Argument map:** science describes bodies through size/shape/motion → experience also contains colour/taste/sound → perceptual variation prevents treating these as intrinsic resemblances → secondary qualities are powers grounded in primary texture → distinction preserves both realism and causal explanation.

#### Model answer

Locke distinguishes ideas in the mind from qualities, the powers of bodies to produce those ideas. Corpuscular science recognises solidity, extension, figure, motion/rest and number as inseparable primary qualities. Yet experience also contains colours, tastes, sounds and temperatures. If these were intrinsic features resembling our ideas, the same water could not feel hot to one hand and cold to another, nor the same object look differently coloured under changed illumination.

Secondary qualities are therefore logically required as **powers** of a body’s primary microstructure to cause sensations in suitably placed perceivers. Redness in an apple is not a red item inside it; the apple has a texture capable of producing the idea of red.

This distinction reconciles common experience with mechanical explanation: primary qualities constitute the object’s measurable structure, while secondary qualities explain its variable appearances. Berkeley objects that primary qualities too vary with perspective and that an idea cannot resemble an unperceived quality. Even so, Locke’s causal-power account explains why sensible qualities are relational without making them unreal.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives the distinction from corpuscularism, perceptual relativity and the idea–quality distinction.

### Q1(b). Examine Kant’s criticism on Descartes’ view of Self.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → critique of rational psychology · **Secondary route:** [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Examine · Syllabus unit: Descartes and Kant—self and transcendental apperception · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Slug:** `kant-critique-cartesian-self`

**Demand decoding:** Contrast Descartes’ thinking substance with Kant’s merely formal “I think,” identifying the paralogism.

**Argument map:** cogito establishes occurrence of thought → Descartes infers simple enduring substance → Kant: unity of apperception is condition of experience, not intuition of a soul → grammatical/logical subject is mistaken for noumenal substance → empirical self is knowable, soul-in-itself is not.

#### Model answer

Descartes moves from *cogito, ergo sum* to the claim that the self is a thinking, unextended substance (*res cogitans*) known more certainly than body. Kant accepts that “I think” must be able to accompany all representations: transcendental apperception supplies the formal unity required for one experience.

He denies, however, that this yields knowledge of a soul-substance. In the Paralogisms, rational psychology illegitimately converts the **logical subject** of judgement into a **real, simple, numerically identical and immortal substance**. The “I” is not an intuition presenting an object; it is the empty form through which representations are combined. The category of substance can determine only objects given in intuition, whereas no intuition of the noumenal self is available.

Thus the cogito warrants “thinking occurs” and an empirical self in inner sense, but not Cartesian substantial dualism or immortality. Kant preserves necessary self-conscious unity while restricting metaphysical claims.

**Evaluation:** Kant exposes the inferential gap, though some argue that even transcendental unity implies a subject more robust than a merely formal function.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact paralogism rather than merely contrasting two theories.

### Q1(c). Why does Wittgenstein reject the possibility of private language?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Later Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/later-wittgenstein.md) → private-language argument · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain why · Syllabus unit: Later Wittgenstein—meaning and private language · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Slug:** `wittgenstein-private-language-rejection`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why a language whose signs refer to intrinsically private sensations cannot have standards of correct use.

**Argument map:** language requires rule-governed distinction between right and seeming-right → private diarist associates “S” with sensation → memory supplies no independent criterion → whatever seems correct becomes correct → rule-following collapses → sensation-talk is learned through public practices, not inner ostension.

#### Model answer

Wittgenstein rejects a language whose words refer to sensations knowable only to one speaker. Imagine a diarist who privately names a recurring sensation “S.” On later occasions, how can the diarist know that “S” is being used correctly? Consulting memory merely produces another impression. If whatever seems right counts as right, there is no distinction between correctness and error—and therefore no rule.

Meaning is not fixed by a mental baptism. Ostensive definition itself works only within established practices of identifying, re-identifying and correcting use. Sensation-words such as “pain” are learned through publicly intelligible circumstances, expressions and responses; this does not mean that pain is publicly observable or that first-person authority disappears.

The target is thus not privacy of experience but a necessarily private **criterion of meaning**. A private code translatable through public rules is possible; an essentially private language is not.

**Why this earns marks:** It centres the normativity and criterion argument, avoiding the crude claim that sensations are public objects.

### Q1(d). Explain verification theory. Does it lead to elimination of metaphysics?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → verification and metaphysics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain and assess · Syllabus unit: Logical positivism—verification theory of meaning · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Slug:** `verification-theory-elimination-metaphysics`

**Demand decoding:** State strong/weak verification, explain the anti-metaphysical conclusion and assess self-defeat and over-exclusion.

**Argument map:** meaningful cognitive statement = analytic or empirically testable → metaphysical claims lack conceivable observations → classified as pseudo-propositions → strong verification excludes laws/history → weak verification broadens testability → principle itself is neither analytic nor verifiable → metaphysics criticised, not conclusively eliminated.

#### Model answer

Logical positivists held that a cognitively meaningful statement is either analytic—true by linguistic rules—or empirically verifiable. Strong verification demanded conclusive verification; Ayer’s weak version required that possible experience count for or against a proposition.

Claims such as “the Absolute is perfect” appear neither analytic nor connected with observable conditions. Carnap therefore treated much traditional metaphysics as emotive or grammatically misleading pseudo-discourse, not false theory.

The proposed elimination is incomplete. Strong verification also excludes universal laws, historical claims and statements about other minds. Weak verification saves them but becomes difficult to formulate without admitting some metaphysics. Further, the verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, so stated as a universal criterion it is self-undermining. Metaphysical frameworks may also clarify presuppositions of science rather than compete with observations.

**Evaluation:** Verification powerfully demands evidential accountability and eliminates some empty utterances, but it cannot by itself eliminate all metaphysics without eliminating its own philosophical status.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the theory, its intended consequence and the decisive internal objections.

### Q1(e). Discuss Quine’s attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → first dogma of empiricism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Quine—analyticity and holism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Slug:** `quine-attack-analytic-synthetic`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the circularity among analyticity, synonymy, definition and necessity, then state Quine’s holistic alternative.

**Argument map:** analytic truth proposed as meaning-based → logical truths unproblematic but synonym-substitution needs synonymy → dictionary definition records prior synonymy → interchangeability fails in opaque contexts → semantic rules presuppose analyticity → no non-circular boundary → beliefs face experience as a web.

#### Model answer

Quine calls the analytic–synthetic cleavage empiricism’s first dogma. Logical truths such as “No unmarried man is married” are clear, but reducing “No bachelor is married” to one requires synonymy between “bachelor” and “unmarried man.” Synonymy cannot be grounded non-circularly: definitions record accepted synonymies; interchangeability *salva veritate* fails in intensional contexts; necessity defined through “necessarily true” already presupposes analyticity; and “semantic rules” merely label certain statements analytic without explaining the notion.

Quine therefore rejects a sharp class of truths immune to experience solely by meaning. Statements belong to a web of belief tested collectively. Logic and mathematics occupy the resistant centre because revising them is costly, not because an absolute epistemic boundary separates them from factual claims.

Carnap may reply that analyticity is internal to stipulated linguistic frameworks. Quine answers that the practical distinction between changing meaning and changing belief remains too indeterminate.

**Evaluation:** Quine damages reductive definitions of analyticity, though many philosophers retain a contextual, conventional distinction rather than his complete gradualism.

**Why this earns marks:** It states both the circularity argument and the holistic replacement.

### Q2(a). Does Aristotle treat matter as a ‘Substance’? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → matter, form and substance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Give a reasoned verdict · Syllabus unit: Aristotle—matter and form; substance · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Slug:** `aristotle-matter-as-substance`

**Demand decoding:** Address Aristotle’s changing uses of “substance,” distinguish proximate matter from prime matter, and give a qualified conclusion.

**Argument map:** substance is underlying subject and separable “this” → matter underlies predication/change → suggests substance → but indeterminate matter lacks form, actuality and independent thisness → composite is primary sensible substance → form is substance in essence/actuality → matter is substance only derivatively or relatively.

#### Model answer

Aristotle’s answer depends on the criterion and level of analysis. In the *Categories*, primary substance is an individual “this”—Socrates or this horse—which is neither predicated of nor present in a subject. In the *Metaphysics*, candidates are matter, form and their composite.

Matter appears to qualify because substance is what underlies predicates and persists through change. Bronze remains while acquiring the form of a statue; matter is the subject capable of contrary determinations. Aristotle sometimes accordingly calls matter substance “in a sense,” especially **proximate matter**—the organised materials of a particular thing.

Yet matter cannot be substance in the fullest sense:

1. **Indeterminacy:** Matter considered apart from form is not a definite “this.”
2. **Non-separability:** Prime matter never exists independently; it is a limiting principle of pure potentiality.
3. **Essence:** To ask what a thing is elicits its form—being a house or organism—not merely its materials.
4. **Actuality:** Substance is prior in actuality and intelligibility; form actualises matter’s capacities.
5. **Unity:** A heap of components becomes one substance only through organising form.

The concrete sensible substance is therefore the **hylomorphic composite**. Form is primary as essence and actuality; matter is a constituent and relatively underlying subject. Matter can be called substance relative to a higher form—bricks are matter for a house—but not an independently complete substance.

An apparent difficulty is that form without matter may seem universal rather than individual. Aristotle’s mature account answers by treating the form as immanent in the particular composite, not a separate Platonic universal.

**Evaluation:** Aristotle neither reduces substance to matter nor denies matter ontological reality. Matter is potential and substrative substance; the composite is primary in ordinary existence, and form is primary in definition and explanation.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconciles apparently conflicting texts through the distinctions among matter, form and composite.

### Q2(b). Critically examine Hume’s views on the relation of cause and effect.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume on causation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Critically examine · Syllabus unit: Hume—causation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Slug:** `hume-cause-effect-relation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the negative and constructive accounts of causation, the induction problem and major criticisms.

**Argument map:** effect not analytically contained in cause → no impression of necessary power → experience gives contiguity, priority and constant conjunction → repetition creates customary expectation → necessity is felt determination of mind → objective relation becomes regularity plus projection → critique and qualified defence.

#### Model answer

Hume asks from what impression the idea of necessary causal connection derives. No effect can be deduced *a priori* from its cause: Adam could not infer burning from the bare appearance of fire, and any imagined effect is logically conceivable. Experience shows only that events are contiguous, that the cause precedes the effect, and that similar pairs are constantly conjoined. It never displays a binding power passing between them.

After repeated conjunction, custom produces an expectation of the second event upon perceiving the first. Necessity is therefore not an observed feature of objects but the mind’s felt determination to move from one idea to another. Hume accordingly offers two definitions: cause as regular succession among similar objects, and cause as an object whose appearance conveys thought to its usual attendant.

This generates the problem of induction. The inference that future regularities will resemble past ones cannot be demonstratively proved, because change is conceivable, or probabilistically proved without presupposing the very uniformity in question.

**Criticism:** Mere regularity cannot distinguish cause from accidental correlation or explain asymmetric, singular and counterfactual causation. Reid and later realists argue that agency gives an idea of power; scientific practice seeks mechanisms, not bare sequences. Hume may reply that mechanisms themselves are known through further regularities and that his aim is to delimit empirical warrant, not prohibit causal science.

**Evaluation:** Hume decisively exposes the non-demonstrative basis of causal inference and the role of habit. His reduction of necessity to expectation is less convincing as a complete metaphysics of causation, but remains a powerful epistemology of causal belief.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs the argument, induction problem, objections and a reasoned verdict.

### Q2(c). Discuss Sartre’s notion of ‘Nothingness’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Sartre on nothingness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Sartre · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Slug:** `sartre-notion-nothingness`

**Demand decoding:** Explain nothingness as introduced by consciousness, connect it to negation, freedom and bad faith, and assess its ontology.

**Argument map:** being-in-itself is full self-identity → consciousness is for-itself, intentional distance from what it is → negation reveals absences (Pierre is not in café) → nothingness nihilates given being → enables projects and freedom → bad faith tries to become fixed thing → tension: freedom depends on facticity.

#### Model answer

For Sartre, being-in-itself (*être-en-soi*) is massive, self-identical positivity: it simply is what it is. Consciousness or being-for-itself (*être-pour-soi*) is not another self-contained thing. Because it is intentional—always consciousness of something—it stands at a distance from its objects and from its own given state. This fissure is “nothingness.”

Negation is not merely linguistic. Entering a café expecting Pierre, I experience his absence as organising the whole field: “Pierre is not here.” Consciousness introduces such non-being by separating the actual from possibilities, past from present and facticity from project. The for-itself is “what it is not and is not what it is”: it can negate a given identity and project another. Nothingness is therefore the ontological basis of freedom.

Bad faith attempts to flee this freedom. The waiter who treats himself as nothing but a waiter reduces the for-itself to an in-itself; the person who denies all facticity makes the opposite error. Authenticity accepts both situated facts and transcending possibilities.

**Objection:** Sartre may turn a logical notion—negation—into a mysterious entity. **Reply:** Nothingness is not a substance; it names consciousness’s nihilating structure. Yet his early emphasis on radical freedom can understate bodily, social and historical constraint.

**Evaluation:** Nothingness illuminates how consciousness questions, refuses and projects. It is strongest as a phenomenology of freedom, weaker if read as an independently existing ontological principle.

**Why this earns marks:** It links negation, intentionality, freedom, facticity and bad faith.

### Q3(a). Does Plato’s ‘Theory of Form’ explain the ‘change’ and ‘sensibility’ of matter? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Forms, receptacle and change · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Assess with reasons · Syllabus unit: Plato—Ideas; Aristotle’s critique · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Slug:** `plato-forms-change-sensibility-matter`

**Demand decoding:** Test whether immutable Forms can explain mutable sensible particulars, using participation, the *Timaeus* receptacle and classical objections.

**Argument map:** Forms explain stable intelligible character → sensibles become F by participation → change described as coming-to-participate/ceasing → receptacle receives changing copies → Forms supply pattern, not efficient mechanism → separation and Third Man weaken explanation → qualified verdict.

#### Model answer

Plato introduces Forms to explain how knowledge and predication remain possible despite sensible flux. Beauty itself is eternal and immutable; beautiful particulars change yet are called beautiful because they participate in or imitate the Form. Forms therefore explain the **intelligible character** of sensible things: what makes many changing particulars instances of one kind.

Change may be described as a particular’s coming to participate in one Form and ceasing to participate in its contrary—for example, becoming equal, hot or beautiful. In the *Timaeus*, Plato adds the **receptacle** (*chōra*), a formless “nurse of becoming” that receives shifting images of Forms. Forms are paradigms, the sensible cosmos is the copy, and the Demiurge orders the receptacle mathematically.

This only partly explains change and sensibility:

1. Immutable Forms cannot themselves initiate motion; participation states a relation but not an efficient causal mechanism.
2. If Forms are separate, it is obscure how they are present in spatial particulars.
3. The *Parmenides* shows part–whole and Third Man difficulties in participation.
4. The receptacle explains where sensible becoming occurs but remains notoriously indeterminate.
5. Forms explain common structure better than individual material variation, interaction and generation.

Plato can reply that explanation is primarily formal and teleological, not mechanical: change is intelligible only against invariant measures, and the *Timaeus* supplements Form with receptacle and cosmic intelligence. Nevertheless, Aristotle argues that immanent form, matter, privation and efficient cause explain change more adequately than separated paradigms.

**Evaluation:** The theory explains the knowable order and qualitative determination of sensible matter, but not the full process by which matter changes. It is a necessary formal account within Platonism, not a sufficient causal physics.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes formal intelligibility from efficient explanation and uses both the middle dialogues and *Timaeus*.

### Q3(b). What, according to Kant, are ‘pure concepts’? Examine their role in the process of knowing.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → categories and knowledge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Define and examine · Syllabus unit: Kant—categories and synthetic a priori knowledge · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Slug:** `kant-pure-concepts-knowing`

**Demand decoding:** Identify categories, derive their function in synthesis and objective judgement, and delimit their valid use.

**Argument map:** intuitions provide manifold → pure concepts originate in understanding, not experience → twelve categories follow logical forms of judgement → transcendental deduction: unity of apperception requires rule-governed synthesis → schemata temporalise categories → knowledge arises from intuitions plus concepts → categories apply only to appearances.

#### Model answer

Kant’s pure concepts of understanding are the **categories**: a priori rules not abstracted from experience but required to think any object of experience. From the logical forms of judgement he derives twelve categories under quantity, quality, relation and modality, including unity, plurality, substance, causality, community, possibility and necessity.

Sensibility supplies a manifold in the pure forms of space and time, but an uncombined manifold would not amount to cognition. Imagination synthesises apprehension and reproduction; the understanding brings representations under rules. The Transcendental Deduction argues that because “I think” must be able to accompany all representations, the manifold must be united in one self-consciousness according to categorial rules. Thus categories make objectively valid judgement possible: causality, for instance, converts a merely subjective sequence of perceptions into experience of an event governed by a rule.

Because heterogeneous concepts and intuitions require mediation, transcendental schemata provide temporal rules: the schema of substance is permanence; that of causality, succession according to a rule.

“Thoughts without content are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind.” Categories are therefore necessary but not independently sufficient. Their legitimate use is restricted to appearances given in possible experience. Applying substance, causality or necessity to God, soul or world as things in themselves produces transcendental illusion.

**Evaluation:** Kant explains objective knowledge as active synthesis rather than passive copying, though the derivation of exactly twelve categories and the obscurity of schematism remain disputed.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers origin, deduction, schematism, function and restriction.

### Q3(c). Explain Russell’s view that “the physical object is a logical construction from sense-data”. Why does he call his metaphysical view ‘neutral monism’?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → logical constructions and neutral monism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Russell—logical atomism and neutral monism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Slug:** `russell-physical-object-neutral-monism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain construction rather than inference, perspectives and sensibilia, then relate physical and mental orders to neutral events.

**Argument map:** directly given sense-data vary by observer → inferred permanent substance is epistemically costly → construct object as system/classes of actual and possible appearances → physical space orders perspectives causally → same neutral particulars enter mental biographies and physical causal series → neither mind nor matter is fundamental.

#### Model answer

Russell applies the maxim “wherever possible, substitute logical constructions for inferred entities.” We directly encounter sense-data—coloured shapes, sounds and textures—not a hidden material substance. Yet appearances vary systematically with observer, position and medium. A physical object can therefore be analysed as a structured class or series of actual and possible appearances: the table is the system of perspectives that would occur under appropriate conditions. Unperceived possible appearances are treated as *sensibilia*, entities of the same general type as sense-data, arranged within causal and spatio-temporal orders.

This is not subjective idealism. Physics describes stable relations among perspectives and events; the construction avoids positing an unknowable bearer behind them while preserving public objects.

Russell later calls his view **neutral monism** because the basic constituents are intrinsically neither mental nor physical. The same neutral events can belong to a mental order when related by memory, attention and a person’s biography, and to a physical order when related by causal laws and spatial perspectives. “Mind” and “matter” are different organisations of common neutral stuff, not two substances.

**Objection:** Possible sense-data may covertly presuppose the physical world and other perceivers. Moreover, qualitative experience seems resistant to purely structural analysis. **Evaluation:** The construction clarifies epistemic economy and dissolves substance dualism, though its neutral basis and account of unperceived possibilities remain under-described.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes construction from inference and directly connects it to neutral monism.

### Q4(a). What, according to Husserl, is the task of a philosopher? Do you think his methods are relevant to philosophy? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Phenomenology](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/phenomenology.md) → philosophy as rigorous science · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Discuss and assess · Syllabus unit: Husserl—phenomenological method · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology—Husserl](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Slug:** `husserl-task-method-relevance`

**Demand decoding:** Define Husserl’s philosophical project, explain epoché, reductions, intentional analysis and eidetic method, then assess continuing relevance.

**Argument map:** sciences presuppose natural attitude → philosopher brackets naïve existence claims → describe intentional correlation → eidetic reduction identifies essences → transcendental reduction uncovers constitution by subjectivity → later life-world diagnoses crisis → relevance and limitations.

#### Model answer

Husserl assigns philosophy the task of becoming a **rigorous, presuppositionless science** of the structures through which objects and worlds are given meaning. Natural sciences explain entities while accepting the “natural attitude”—the unexamined positing of an independently existing world. Philosophy must disclose the grounds of such objectivity rather than merely add another empirical theory.

His principal methods are:

1. **Epoché:** suspend or bracket the natural attitude’s existential thesis. This neither denies the world nor doubts it; it redirects inquiry to how the world appears.
2. **Phenomenological reduction:** investigate the intentional correlation between act (*noesis*) and intended object-as-meant (*noema*). Every consciousness is consciousness of something.
3. **Eidetic reduction:** use imaginative variation to distinguish accidental features from invariant essences.
4. **Transcendental reduction:** uncover transcendental subjectivity and its syntheses as conditions for constituted objectivity.
5. **Genetic and life-world analysis:** examine temporality, embodiment, intersubjectivity and the pre-scientific life-world (*Lebenswelt*) that sciences presuppose.

These methods remain relevant because they expose hidden assumptions, provide disciplined first-person description, distinguish modes of givenness, and illuminate perception, embodiment, time, sociality and meaning. They shaped Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, hermeneutics and contemporary philosophy of mind.

Objections persist. Complete presuppositionlessness may be impossible; the transcendental turn can seem idealistic; private reflection appears unable to secure other minds; and eidetic intuition lacks universally agreed tests. Husserl replies through intersubjective constitution and insists that reduction is publicly repeatable analysis, not introspection.

**Evaluation:** Husserl’s claim to an absolute foundation is doubtful, but his methods remain indispensable as reflective correctives to scientism and conceptual abstraction. Their strength lies less in certainty than in making structures of experience explicit.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the complete methodological sequence and offers a balanced relevance judgement.

### Q4(b). What is Hegel’s view on God? Do you think that his interpretation of God was contributive to the colonial and imperial expansionist designs? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → Absolute Spirit, history and empire · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain and assess · Syllabus unit: Hegel—Absolute, dialectic and history · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Slug:** `hegel-god-colonial-imperial-designs`

**Demand decoding:** Present Hegel’s immanent, self-developing Absolute and evaluate whether his philosophy of history legitimated empire without reducing influence to intention.

**Argument map:** God/Absolute is not external creator → Spirit actualises and knows itself through nature, finite mind, religion/philosophy → history is progress in consciousness of freedom → Eurocentric hierarchy casts some peoples as outside world-history → “world-historical” states and civil society’s colonies can legitimise domination → distinguish philosophical contribution from direct causation.

#### Model answer

Hegel rejects a remote God standing outside creation. God is the Absolute Spirit whose nature is self-manifestation: the Absolute differentiates itself in nature and finite spirit and returns to self-knowledge through art, religion and, conceptually, philosophy. Christianity expresses this truth representationally through incarnation and Trinity; philosophy comprehends it as the unity of infinite and finite. God is therefore not dependent on a prior world, but living Spirit essentially reveals itself rather than remaining an inert beyond.

This theology joins Hegel’s philosophy of history. World history is the progress of consciousness of freedom, embodied successively in “world-historical” peoples and states. His schema privileges Europe, depicts Africa as outside historical development, and treats some Asian formations as knowing only that one person is free. He also presents colonisation as one outlet for civil society’s poverty and surplus population.

Such claims could contribute ideological resources to imperialism: conquest may be redescribed as the vehicle of a higher stage of Spirit, while colonised peoples are denied equal historical agency. Postcolonial critics therefore detect a theological-historical legitimation of hierarchy.

Yet Hegel’s God does not straightforwardly command colonial conquest, and imperial expansion had economic, military and political causes independent of his philosophy. His dialectic also supplies resources for recognising domination and struggles for freedom.

**Evaluation:** Hegel did not singly cause imperialism, but his Eurocentric mapping of divine-historical reason could rationalise it. The contribution is justificatory and conceptual, not a sufficient causal explanation.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both theology and political implication with a non-reductive verdict.

### Q4(c). Discuss the various stances on God taken by Rationalists and Empiricists.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → rationalist and empiricist natural theology · **Secondary route:** [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Comparative discussion · Syllabus unit: Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz; Locke, Berkeley, Hume · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Slug:** `rationalists-empiricists-god`

**Demand decoding:** State each major thinker’s distinct position and compare methods, not merely label one side theistic and the other sceptical.

**Argument map:** rationalists infer God through innate ideas/reason → Descartes perfect non-deceiver; Spinoza immanent substance; Leibniz necessary sufficient reason → empiricists begin from experience → Locke modest demonstrative theism; Berkeley divine perceiver; Hume critiques causal/design inferences → methodological contrast with internal diversity.

#### Model answer

Rationalists generally make God central to a reason-governed metaphysics, though their doctrines differ sharply.

- **Descartes:** The idea of an infinite perfect being cannot originate from a finite self; God is its adequate cause. God’s necessary existence is also read from the clear idea of perfection. Divine non-deception guarantees clear and distinct ideas and supports the external world.
- **Spinoza:** God is not a personal transcendent creator but the one infinite substance—“God or Nature” (*Deus sive Natura*). Finite things are modes of divine attributes; freedom means acting from necessity, not choosing contingently.
- **Leibniz:** Contingent beings require a sufficient reason in a necessary being. God chooses the best possible world and grounds eternal truths, while created monads remain distinct.

Empiricists restrict claims to experiential warrant:

- **Locke:** Though rejecting an innate idea of God, he argues demonstratively from our own existence to an eternal, powerful knowing cause.
- **Berkeley:** God is indispensable as the infinite spirit who causes orderly ideas and continuously perceives the world; this secures objectivity without matter.
- **Hume:** He questions whether finite effects justify an infinite perfect cause, attacks analogical design arguments, exposes the problem of evil and suspends judgement beyond experience.

The contrast is methodological, not simply belief versus unbelief: rationalists seek necessity through reason, empiricists demand experiential derivation. Berkeley is more theologically committed than Spinoza’s impersonal monism, while Hume carries empiricist caution into scepticism.

**Evaluation:** Both traditions use God to close explanatory gaps; Hume shows that the conclusion often exceeds the premises.

**Why this earns marks:** It preserves thinker-specific positions while drawing the controlling comparison.

### Q5(b). How does ‘Kṣaṇikavāda’ strengthen the arguments for ‘Nairātmyavāda’? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → momentariness and non-self · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Slug:** `momentariness-supports-non-self`

**Demand decoding:** Show how universal momentariness excludes an enduring owner behind the aggregates while preserving causal continuity.

**Argument map:** real is causally efficient → whatever acts changes and perishes each moment → person is stream of five aggregates → no invariant substance found within/beyond stream → permanent self would be causally inert or mutable → dependent continuity explains memory/karma without identity.

#### Model answer

Momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*) holds that conditioned realities arise, exercise causal efficacy and perish in a moment. What appears continuous is a rapidly ordered causal series. Applied to the person, body, feeling, perception, dispositions and consciousness—the five aggregates (*pañcaskandha*)—are all momentary.

This strengthens non-self (*nairātmyavāda/anātman*) in two ways. First, analysis finds no permanent owner distinct from the aggregates; “person” is a conventional designation for their stream. Second, an unchanging self could neither produce nor receive changing cognitions: if wholly permanent it would lack causal efficacy, while if it changed it would concede momentariness.

Memory and karmic responsibility do not require numerical identity. An earlier moment conditions a later one through dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), as one flame lights another. The stream is neither absolutely identical nor wholly disconnected.

**Objection:** Recognition seems to require one recogniser. **Reply:** Buddhism explains it through causal traces and resemblance within the continuum.

**Why this earns marks:** It joins the metaphysical causal argument to the aggregate analysis and continuity reply.

### Q5(d). Examine Jaina stance of ‘Naya’. How does it differ from ‘Syādvāda’?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → standpoint theory and conditioned predication · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Examine and differentiate · Syllabus unit: Jainism—anekāntavāda, nayavāda and syādvāda · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Slug:** `jaina-naya-versus-syadvada`

**Demand decoding:** Define partial standpoints and distinguish them from the logical-linguistic sevenfold predication.

**Argument map:** many-sided real exceeds one judgement → naya selects one legitimate aspect/standpoint → error arises when partial view claims completeness → syādvāda qualifies predications by “in some respect” → seven modes combine affirmation, negation and inexpressibility → epistemic method versus predicative logic.

#### Model answer

Jaina many-sided realism (*anekāntavāda*) holds that a real object possesses indefinitely many aspects and modes. A standpoint (*naya*) is a partial but legitimate approach that selects one aspect for a practical or theoretical purpose. Substantial and modal, common and specific, conventional and etymological standpoints can each be valid within limits. Fallacy begins when one standpoint is absolutised.

Conditioned predication (*syādvāda*) is the linguistic-logical discipline for expressing such partial truth. The qualifier *syāt* means “in a certain respect, context, place, time or mode.” Sevenfold predication (*saptabhaṅgī*) states that an object is, is not, is and is not, is inexpressible, and the three corresponding combinations—without asserting contradictions in the same respect.

Thus *naya* concerns the **perspective of cognition or inquiry**; *syādvāda* concerns the **qualified formulation of judgements** generated from perspectives. The former supplies standpoints, the latter prevents their dogmatic expression.

**Evaluation:** Their complementarity supports disciplined pluralism, not subjective relativism, because each claim remains constrained by the real object.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the common basis and exact functional difference.

### Q5(e). Evaluate Śaṅkara’s position on Īśvara.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Advaita on Īśvara · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Slug:** `shankara-ishvara`

#### Model answer

For Śaṅkara, ultimate reality is attributeless Brahman (*nirguṇa Brahman*), non-dual consciousness. Īśvara is Brahman viewed through the limiting adjunct of cosmic appearance (*māyā*): the omniscient, omnipotent ruler, creator, sustainer and dissolver of the empirical world. **Why this earns marks:** It evaluates Īśvara through māyā and the two truths without calling the empirical God unreal simpliciter.

### Q6(a). Does the ‘Doctrine of Dependent Origination’ reconcile the two extreme views on the law of causation, namely Satkāryavāda and Asatkāryavāda? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → dependent origination and causation · **Secondary route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/samkhya.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Assess with reasons · Syllabus unit: Buddhism—dependent origination; causation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Slug:** `dependent-origination-between-causal-extremes`

**Demand decoding:** Define all three positions, show how conditional arising avoids identity and sheer novelty, and qualify the word “reconcile.”

**Argument map:** satkāryavāda: effect pre-exists in cause → risks identity/permanence → asatkāryavāda: effect previously nonexistent → risks production from non-being/discontinuity → dependent origination: this-conditioned-that, neither same nor wholly other → causal series without substance → middle path is critical alternative, not synthesis of rival ontologies.

#### Model answer

Pre-existent-effect theory (*satkāryavāda*), defended by Sāṃkhya, holds that the effect already exists latently in its material cause: production is manifestation or real transformation. Non-pre-existent-effect theory (*asatkāryavāda*), associated with Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, holds that the effect as a new whole does not exist before production, though its causes do.

Dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) states: “When this is, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises.” Every conditioned event occurs through a plurality of conditions and ceases when those conditions cease. It rejects:

1. **Strict identity:** If effect already existed as the same entity, production would be redundant and permanence would undermine real arising.
2. **Absolute difference or creation from nothing:** If cause and effect were wholly unrelated, anything could arise from anything and karma would lose continuity.
3. **Substantial causation:** Neither cause nor effect has independent self-nature; each is a moment within a conditioned series.

The sprout is neither numerically identical with the seed nor causally disconnected from it. Similarly, personal continuity is neither an eternal self nor annihilation at every moment. This is the doctrinal Middle Path between eternalism and annihilationism, complementing the practical Middle Path embodied in the Noble Eightfold Path (*āryāṣṭāṅgamārga*).

Whether this is a “reconciliation” needs qualification. Early Buddhism offers a middle causal account securing continuity without identity. Madhyamaka goes further: causal terms are conventionally valid but empty (*śūnya*) of intrinsic nature; Nāgārjuna criticises production from self, other, both or no cause. This does not combine *satkāryavāda* and *asatkāryavāda* into a higher theory; it rejects the substantial assumptions shared by both.

**Evaluation:** Dependent origination functionally mediates their opposing intuitions—continuity and novelty—but philosophically supersedes rather than synthesises them. Its strength is relational causality; its challenge is explaining efficacy if all relata lack intrinsic nature, answered by the distinction between conventional operation and ultimate emptiness.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes mediation from synthesis and includes the early Buddhist and Madhyamaka levels.

### Q6(b). Can the ‘Tattvārtha’ theory of Jainism be acceptable for scientific explanations? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → categories of reality and scientific explanation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain and assess · Syllabus unit: Jainism—categories, realism and many-sidedness · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Slug:** `jaina-tattvartha-scientific-explanation`

**Demand decoding:** Clarify “tattvārtha theory,” identify scientifically suggestive features, and distinguish heuristic compatibility from empirical scientific acceptability.

**Argument map:** Jaina categories map soul/non-soul and bondage/liberation → realism includes matter, space, motion/rest media and time → substances persist through changing modes → many-sided and conditional claims encourage perspectival fallibilism → but soul, karmic matter and cosmic media lack testability → partial methodological relevance, not modern science.

#### Model answer

The Jaina account of reality (*tattvārtha*, “nature of the reals”) classifies the path through soul (*jīva*), non-soul (*ajīva*), karmic influx, bondage, stoppage, shedding and liberation. Its ontology treats substances as possessing origination, cessation and permanence: an entity endures through changing modes. Non-soul includes matter (*pudgala*), space, time, and media of motion and rest.

Several features are congenial to scientific explanation. Jaina realism affirms an observer-independent, structured plurality; *pudgala* is conceived atomistically and subject to combination and transformation; causal explanation preserves continuity amid change. Many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) and standpoint theory (*nayavāda*) warn that descriptions select aspects, while conditioned predication (*syādvāda*) encourages explicit qualification. These resemble methodological fallibilism and model-relative description.

Yet acceptability to modern science requires operational definition, testability, measurement and predictive success. Eternal souls, subtle karmic matter, cosmic media of motion/rest and the inhabited universe are metaphysical-religious postulates not established by those methods. Jaina atoms also should not be equated with modern physical atoms merely because both are minute.

**Evaluation:** Jaina *tattvārtha* can enrich the philosophy of science through plural realism, perspectival caution and process-with-continuity. It cannot be accepted wholesale as a scientific theory. Its scientifically defensible role is heuristic and methodological, not evidential confirmation of its complete cosmology and soteriology.

**Why this earns marks:** It avoids both anachronistic celebration and wholesale dismissal.

### Q6(c). Is the doctrine of ‘Self’ acceptable in the modern age of science and reason? Examine the view in the light of Indian philosophy.

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Examine · Syllabus unit: Indian theories of self · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Slug:** `self-science-reason-indian-philosophy`

**Demand decoding:** Compare major Indian positions and test distinct meanings of self against scientific evidence and rational requirements.

**Argument map:** “self” may mean organism/person/witness/substance → Cārvāka naturalises consciousness → Buddhism denies permanent owner but preserves causal stream → Nyāya/Jaina posit enduring individual → Sāṃkhya/Advaita defend witness-consciousness → neuroscience challenges separable ego, not automatically first-person subjectivity → qualified acceptability.

#### Model answer

Science and reason do not confront one single doctrine of self. Indian philosophy offers competing hypotheses.

Cārvāka treats consciousness as an emergent property of the living body; this is closest to physicalist science, though it may not explain subjectivity fully. Buddhism analyses the person into five changing aggregates and rejects a permanent self (*anātman/nairātmyavāda*). Its process account fits evidence that memory, personality and agency depend on changing bodily and cognitive conditions.

Nyāya infers an enduring self from recognition, memory, desire and ownership of successive cognitions: “I who saw now remember.” Jainism similarly posits plural enduring souls whose modes change. Sāṃkhya distinguishes unchanging witness-consciousness (*puruṣa*) from psychophysical nature, while Advaita identifies self (*ātman*) with non-dual witnessing consciousness rather than the empirical ego.

Neuroscience weakens the idea of a simple, independently acting mental substance by correlating cognition with brain processes and showing that self-representation is constructed. But third-person correlations do not by themselves eliminate first-person awareness, continuity of responsibility or the transcendental question of what makes experience a unified field. Conversely, ineffability is not positive evidence for an immortal metaphysical subject.

**Evaluation:** A separable, immutable ego-substance is not scientifically established. A modest doctrine of self—as embodied personal continuity or the formal/phenomenological subject of awareness—remains rationally defensible. Indian debate is valuable precisely because Cārvāka and Buddhism prevent uncritical reification, while Nyāya and Vedānta expose explanatory questions that reductionism must answer.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a genuinely plural Indian assessment and a graded contemporary verdict.

### Q7(a). Discuss Aurobindo’s views on ‘Evolution’ and ‘Involution’. How do they differ from traditional Yoga philosophy?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sri Aurobindo](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/sri-aurobindo.md) → involution, evolution and Integral Yoga · **Secondary route:** [Yoga](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/yoga.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Discuss and compare · Syllabus unit: Sri Aurobindo—evolution, involution and Integral Yoga · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Sri Aurobindo](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Aurobindo.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Yoga](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Yoga.md) · **Slug:** `aurobindonian-evolution-involution-versus-yoga`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the metaphysical descent and ascent, explain Supermind and transformation, then compare aim, world-status and method with Pātañjala Yoga.

**Argument map:** Sachchidānanda self-conceals through involution → Supermind mediates unity and multiplicity → consciousness involved in matter evolves as life, mind and beyond → psychic/spiritual/supramental transformation divinises life → traditional Yoga isolates Puruṣa from Prakṛti → Aurobindo integrates and transforms nature rather than withdraws.

#### Model answer

Sri Aurobindo regards cosmic evolution as intelligible only because consciousness has first been **involved** in matter. The ultimate reality is existence-consciousness-bliss (*saccidānanda*). Through the creative power of Supermind, unity differentiates without losing itself; successive descent through overmental and mental levels culminates in apparent inconscience and matter. Involution is thus self-concealment, not creation from absolute nothing.

Evolution is the reverse manifestation of what is latent: matter develops life, life develops mind, and mind can exceed itself through higher mind, illumined mind, intuition and overmind toward supramental consciousness. Human beings are transitional. Integral Yoga seeks:

1. **Psychic transformation:** the inner soul organises the nature.
2. **Spiritual transformation:** consciousness universalises and ascends beyond ego.
3. **Supramental transformation:** higher consciousness descends to transform mind, life and even body.

Traditional Pātañjala Yoga explains bondage as the seer’s identification with mental modifications and seeks their cessation (*citta-vṛtti-nirodha*) through eight-limbed discipline, culminating in isolation (*kaivalya*) of consciousness (*puruṣa*) from nature (*prakṛti*). Aurobindo differs in major respects:

- the world is a real manifestation of the Divine, not merely a field to be finally disengaged from;
- liberation is not only ascent or isolation but descent and transformation;
- nature is divinised rather than left to return to equilibrium;
- the goal is collective and terrestrial evolution as well as individual release;
- knowledge, devotion and works are integrated with psychological surrender.

There are continuities: disciplined concentration, purification and liberation from ego remain essential. Nor is classical Yoga simply world-hatred; its practical psychology is incorporated.

**Evaluation:** Aurobindo transforms Yoga from a predominantly soteriological separation into an evolutionary spiritual cosmology. Its synthetic power is considerable, though supramental evolution is metaphysical and experiential rather than empirically demonstrable.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both movements, their mediator and a precise multi-axis comparison.

### Q7(b). What would be Cārvāka’s view on Vyāpti? Can this view be acceptable to the Naiyāyikas? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Cārvāka](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/carvaka.md) → critique of inference and pervasion · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain and assess · Syllabus unit: Cārvāka and Nyāya—perception, inference and vyāpti · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Cārvāka](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Carvaka.md) · **Secondary classification:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Slug:** `carvaka-vyapti-nyaya-response`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Cārvāka’s scepticism about universal concomitance and Nyāya’s defence of inference.

**Argument map:** inference needs exceptionless pervasion → finite perception cannot survey all times/places → unobserved conditions may defeat relation → inference used to prove pervasion is circular → Cārvāka allows practical probability → Nyāya uses repeated agreement/difference, elimination of conditions and tarka → rejects scepticism as self-defeating.

#### Model answer

Pervasion (*vyāpti*) is the exceptionless relation underlying inference: wherever smoke occurs, fire occurs. Cārvāka accepts perception as the only certain means of knowledge and challenges our warrant for such universality. Finite observation covers only some cases, never every place and time. An unnoticed condition (*upādhi*) may explain the observed conjunction, as wet fuel conditions smoke. To establish pervasion by inference would presuppose another pervasion and invite regress or circularity; testimony also depends on inference about a speaker. Hence inference may guide practical life but yields probability, not certain knowledge—especially concerning unobservable souls, karma or afterlife.

Naiyāyikas cannot accept this conclusion because inference (*anumāna*) is an independent means of knowledge. They establish pervasion through repeated observation of agreement in presence (*anvaya*) and absence (*vyatireka*), elimination of defeating conditions, generalisation and counterfactual reasoning (*tarka*). The inference “smoke implies fire” concerns smoke as causally produced by fire, not a few accidental pairings.

Nyāya also charges Cārvāka with performative inconsistency: arguing that inference is unreliable requires inferring beyond the present statement and recognising others’ views. Cārvāka can answer that dialectical and everyday inferences are fallible habits rather than knowledge.

**Evaluation:** Nyāya is right that rational inquiry cannot function without defeasible induction and inference. Cārvāka nevertheless exposes the problem of induction and the need to identify hidden conditions. Nyāya accepts the challenge, not the restrictive epistemology.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the strongest argument and reply on both sides.

### Q7(c). What is the logical necessity for the Mīmāṃsakas to treat ‘Arthāpatti’ as an independent Pramāṇa? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → postulation as a means of knowledge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā—means of knowledge · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Slug:** `mimamsa-arthapatti-independent-pramana`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the inconsistency-resolving structure of postulation and why Mīmāṃsā denies reduction to perception, inference or testimony.

**Argument map:** accepted facts conflict unless unperceived fact posited → “Devadatta is stout and does not eat by day” → must eat at night → cognition is not perception/testimony → not ordinary inference because no independently known vyāpti → necessity arises from inexplicability otherwise → Nyāya reduces it to inference; Mīmāṃsā defends distinctive trigger.

#### Model answer

Postulation (*arthāpatti*) is cognition of an unperceived fact whose acceptance is necessary to make established facts jointly intelligible. If Devadatta is known to be stout and known not to eat by day, one postulates that he eats at night. Likewise, hearing “Devadatta is not at home” when his continued existence is known yields the postulation that he is elsewhere.

Mīmāṃsā treats it as independent because:

1. The postulated fact is not perceived.
2. It is not supplied by testimony; testimony gives only the apparently incompatible facts.
3. It is not memory or comparison.
4. It is not ordinary inference, since the knower need not possess an independently observed universal pervasion such as “all stout non-day-eaters eat at night.”
5. Its distinctive ground is **otherwise-inexplicability** (*anyathānupapatti*): without the postulate, accepted knowledge would remain contradictory or unintelligible.

Nyāya reduces the case to inference: whoever remains stout while not eating by day must eat at another time; Devadatta satisfies the condition; therefore he eats at night. Mīmāṃsā replies that formulating this universal merely restates the postulatory necessity and that cognition arises immediately from reconciling the facts, not from conscious syllogistic mediation.

**Evaluation:** *Arthāpatti* identifies a genuine explanatory mode akin to inference to the best or necessary explanation. Whether it is an independent source rather than a special inferential form depends on how broadly inference is defined. Mīmāṃsā’s logical need is strongest where no antecedently established *vyāpti* is available.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the irreducibility claim and fairly presents the Nyāya reduction.

### Q8(a). How do the Naiyāyikas justify the introduction of ‘Abhāva’ as an independent category? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → absence as a category · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—categories and absence · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Slug:** `nyaya-abhava-independent-category`

**Demand decoding:** Establish the reality, cognition and classifications of absence, explain its relational structure, and answer objections.

**Argument map:** negative cognition/action has objective content → absence cannot reduce to locus or positive counter-correlate → defined as absence of counter-positive in locus → temporal and mutual kinds capture production/destruction/difference → apprehended through non-apprehension under suitable conditions → rival schools reduce/deny; Nyāya defends ontological economy through explanatory need.

#### Model answer

Later Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika adds absence (*abhāva*) to the six positive categories because negative cognitions such as “There is no pot on the floor” are veridical, guide action and require an objective correlate. The cognition is not merely of the bare floor: one may perceive the same floor while seeking different absent objects. Nor is it simply memory of a pot, because its content concerns the locus here and now.

Absence is relationally specified as the absence of a counter-positive (*pratiyogin*) in a locus (*anuyogin*). Nyāya distinguishes:

1. **Prior absence (*prāgabhāva*):** a pot’s absence before production; beginningless but ending with production.
2. **Posterior absence (*pradhvaṃsābhāva*):** its absence after destruction; begins with destruction and is endless.
3. **Absolute absence (*atyantābhāva*):** absence across all relevant times, as colour in air.
4. **Mutual absence (*anyonyābhāva*):** difference—pot is not cloth.

These types explain production, destruction, negation and numerical/qualitative difference without reducing non-being to an entity identical with being.

How absence is known is disputed. Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā accepts non-apprehension (*anupalabdhi*) as an independent means. Nyāya usually treats qualified perception as sufficient: when a competent observer attends to a perceptible locus under suitable conditions and the pot would have been perceived if present, the empty locus is apprehended as pot-qualified-by-absence. The supporting non-apprehension excludes mere ignorance.

Buddhist nominalists may treat negation as conceptual exclusion; Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā analyses it through perception of the locus plus memory. Nyāya replies that these accounts cannot explain the directness, truth conditions and causal role of negative knowledge.

**Evaluation:** *Abhāva* is not a mysterious positive “nothing”; it is a structured negative fact dependent on locus and counter-positive. Its introduction is justified if every true cognition requires an objective correlate, though parsimonious logics can represent negation without a separate ontological category.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the necessity, fourfold taxonomy, cognition debate and balanced evaluation.

### Q8(b). What are Kleśas? How can these be eliminated? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Yoga](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/yoga.md) → afflictions and their elimination · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Yoga—kleśas, discipline and liberation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Yoga](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Yoga.md) · **Slug:** `yoga-kleshas-elimination`

**Demand decoding:** Define and relate the five afflictions, distinguish gross and subtle removal, and connect practice to discriminative knowledge.

**Argument map:** ignorance misidentifies seer and seen → produces egoity → attachment/aversion → clinging to life → afflictions seed karma and rebirth → attenuate through practice, dispassion and action-Yoga → meditation removes active forms → discriminative knowledge burns subtle seeds → kaivalya.

#### Model answer

Afflictions (*kleśas*) are the causes that disturb the mind, generate karmic deposits and sustain rebirth. Patañjali lists five:

1. **Ignorance (*avidyā*):** taking the impermanent, impure, painful and non-self as permanent, pure, pleasant and self.
2. **Egoity (*asmitā*):** identifying the seer with the instruments of seeing.
3. **Attachment (*rāga*):** craving based on remembered pleasure.
4. **Aversion (*dveṣa*):** repulsion based on remembered pain.
5. **Clinging to life (*abhiniveśa*):** deep fear of dissolution, present even in the learned.

Ignorance is the field from which the others arise in dormant, attenuated, interrupted or active forms. Action-Yoga (*kriyā-yoga*)—discipline (*tapas*), self-study (*svādhyāya*) and dedication to the Lord (*Īśvara-praṇidhāna*)—attenuates them. The eight limbs purify conduct, body, breath, senses and attention; sustained practice (*abhyāsa*) and dispassion (*vairāgya*) quiet mental modifications.

Meditation removes gross operations, while subtle afflictions are resolved by tracing them back into their causal source. Finally, uninterrupted discriminative discernment (*vivekakhyāti*) distinguishes consciousness (*puruṣa*) from nature (*prakṛti*), burns karmic seeds and culminates in seedless concentration and isolation (*kaivalya*).

**Evaluation:** Suppression is insufficient; Yoga demands ethical, psychological and cognitive transformation.

**Why this earns marks:** It states all five, their causal order, stages and complete method of removal.

### Q8(c). Give a critical exposition of the concept of ‘Brahman’ as discussed by Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Mādhava.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Brahman in Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Critical comparative exposition · Syllabus unit: Vedānta—Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Slug:** `brahman-shankara-ramanuja-madhva`

**Demand decoding:** Present each doctrine independently, compare world/self/God relations and evaluate explanatory gains and costs. The source prints “Mādhava”; the standard doctrinal comparison is with Madhva.

**Argument map:** Śaṅkara: non-dual attributeless Brahman, world empirically real but sublatable → Rāmānuja: qualified unity, conscious/non-conscious beings as Brahman’s body → Madhva: supreme personal Viṣṇu and irreducible fivefold difference → compare unity, plurality, devotion and problem of dependence/change.

#### Model answer

Śaṅkara’s Advaita identifies Brahman as non-dual existence-consciousness-infinity, ultimately without limiting attributes (*nirguṇa*). The individual self (*ātman*) is Brahman; plurality arises through beginningless ignorance (*avidyā*) and superimposition. The world is transactionally real but ultimately sublatable—not sheer non-being. Īśvara is Brahman associated with *māyā* at the empirical level. Liberation is immediate knowledge of identity.

Rāmānuja’s qualified non-dualism (*Viśiṣṭādvaita*) rejects an attributeless Absolute. Brahman is personal Nārāyaṇa endowed with infinite auspicious qualities. Souls (*cit*) and matter (*acit*) are real and form Brahman’s body, while Brahman is their inner ruler and soul. Unity is organic and qualified: parts depend on the whole without losing reality. Liberation preserves individuality in loving service.

Madhva’s dualism (*Dvaita*) identifies Brahman with supreme Viṣṇu, wholly independent, while souls and matter are eternally dependent realities. Fivefold difference (*pañcabheda*)—God–soul, God–matter, soul–soul, soul–matter and matter–matter—is real. Liberation is by knowledge, devotion and grace, not identity.

Critically, Śaṅkara best secures absolute unity and changelessness but faces the problem of explaining ignorance without compromising non-duality. Rāmānuja preserves world, personality and devotion, yet the body–soul analogy raises how Brahman remains perfect while embodying suffering beings. Madhva protects divine transcendence and religious otherness, but eternal difference and hierarchy of souls make metaphysical unity and justice harder to explain.

**Evaluation:** The three systems progressively move from identity, through qualified dependence, to irreducible difference. Each interprets the Upaniṣadic unity–plurality tension coherently, but purchases its central insight at a corresponding explanatory cost.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares ontology, world, soul, liberation and internal difficulties with a reasoned synthesis.
