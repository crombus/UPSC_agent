## Q1(a). Ethics and social and human well-being

**Official question:** Explain how ethics contributes to social and human well-being. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q1(a) · Directive: Explain · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories.md) → ethics and human well-being · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Ethics and human interface](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/01_Ethics-and-Human-Interface.md) · [Moral theories](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/08_Moral-Theories-Deontology-Consequentialism-Virtue-Ethics.md)

### Demand decoding

Explain the mechanisms by which ethical conduct improves both individual flourishing and collective life; avoid reducing ethics either to private goodness or to legal compliance.

### Model answer

Ethics is reasoned guidance about right conduct, good character and just institutions. It advances **human well-being** by cultivating integrity, self-respect, empathy and meaningful relationships. Virtue ethics calls this human flourishing: capacities become valuable when ordered by courage, temperance and justice.

Ethics advances **social well-being** by:

- creating trust, which lowers the cost of cooperation and exchange;
- restraining violence, deception, discrimination and exploitation;
- making power accountable and public services impartial;
- protecting dignity and rights, especially of weak minorities;
- encouraging fair sharing of benefits, burdens and environmental resources.

For example, ethical disaster administration publishes criteria, reaches excluded persons and prevents diversion. Citizens then cooperate because authority appears legitimate.

Utilitarian concern for welfare, Kantian respect for persons and Gandhi's emphasis on ethical means illuminate complementary dimensions. Yet good intentions alone are insufficient: ethical values need fair laws, capable institutions and scrutiny. Conversely, law without internalised ethics cannot supervise every act.

**Examiner takeaway:** Ethics connects character, trustworthy relationships and just institutions to durable individual and collective flourishing.

## Q1(b). Impartiality and non-partisanship as foundational values

**Official question:** Why should impartiality and non-partisanship be considered as foundational values in public services, especially in the present day socio-political context? Illustrate your answer with examples. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q1(b) · Directive: Explain and illustrate · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Work Culture and Public Funds](../../topics/ethics/work-culture-and-public-funds.md) → foundational civil-service values · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Aptitude and foundational values](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/04_Aptitude-and-Foundational-Values-for-Civil-Service.md)

### Model answer

**Impartiality** means deciding on lawful, relevant and objective criteria without prejudice or favour. **Non-partisanship** means professional service to the elected government without becoming an agent of any party.

They are foundational because civil servants exercise continuous state power while governments change. They:

- secure equality before administration and continuity of institutions;
- protect merit, evidence and public interest from patronage;
- permit frank, neutral advice;
- sustain citizen trust in a polarised society;
- ensure that welfare, policing and regulation do not become electoral rewards or punishments.

Thus a district officer must allocate drought relief by vulnerability, not party affiliation; police must permit comparable political meetings under identical safety standards; and an official should loyally implement lawful policy after recording objective advice.

Non-partisanship is not indifference to constitutional values, and impartiality is not identical treatment where vulnerability requires accommodation. Officers remain responsive to the elected executive, but loyal to the Constitution and public interest rather than a faction.

**Examiner takeaway:** These values reconcile democratic responsiveness with equal, continuous and trustworthy administration.

## Q2(a). Governance, good governance and ethical governance

**Official question:** What do you understand by the terms ‘governance’, ‘good governance’ and ‘ethical governance’? (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q2(a) · Directive: Define and distinguish · Difficulty: Easy-Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Service Delivery and Regulatory Ethics](../../topics/ethics/service-delivery-and-regulatory-ethics.md) → ethical governance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Accountability and ethical governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/11_Accountability-and-Ethical-Governance.md)

### Model answer

| Term | Meaning | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Governance** | Processes and institutions through which public decisions are made and implemented by the state, market and civil society | How is collective power exercised? |
| **Good governance** | Governance that is effective, participatory, transparent, accountable, responsive, equitable and rule-bound | Does it deliver fairly and competently? |
| **Ethical governance** | Exercise of public power through integrity, constitutional morality, respect for dignity, reasoned discretion and priority to public interest | Are both ends and means morally defensible? |

An online pension portal may represent governance; if it is timely, auditable and accessible, it reflects good governance; if it protects privacy, assists digitally excluded citizens and prevents arbitrary rejection, it becomes ethical governance.

The concepts overlap but are not identical. Efficient delivery can still be unethical if it discriminates or invades privacy. Ethical intent can also fail citizens if administration is incompetent. Therefore good governance supplies institutional quality, while ethical governance adds the moral legitimacy of purposes, procedures and consequences.

**Examiner takeaway:** Ethical governance is good governance disciplined by constitutional values and defensible means.

## Q2(b). Mahatma Gandhi's seven sins

**Official question:** Discuss Mahatma Gandhi’s concept of seven sins. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q2(b) · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Indian Moral Thinkers](../../topics/ethics/indian-moral-thinkers.md) → Gandhi's seven social sins · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Indian moral thinkers](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/06_Indian-Moral-Thinkers-and-Philosophers.md)

### Model answer

Gandhi popularised seven “social sins” that separate desirable ends from the moral disciplines that make them legitimate:

1. **Politics without principle** — power without constitutional or moral restraint.
2. **Wealth without work** — unearned extraction and speculation detached from contribution.
3. **Pleasure without conscience** — gratification that externalises harm.
4. **Knowledge without character** — expertise used without responsibility.
5. **Commerce without morality** — profit through exploitation, deception or ecological damage.
6. **Science without humanity** — innovation indifferent to dignity and consequences.
7. **Worship without sacrifice** — ritual without service, self-restraint or concern for others.

Their common insight is that capability and success become destructive when divorced from duty. Data expertise used for manipulative surveillance exemplifies knowledge without character; unsafe cost-cutting is commerce without morality.

The framework remains diagnostic, though institutional safeguards must complement personal virtue. Ethical politics needs disclosure and checks; humane science needs review and accountability. Gandhi therefore joins inner conscience with social responsibility and insists that ethical means are integral to worthy ends.

**Examiner takeaway:** The seven sins expose the moral vacuum created when power, knowledge and prosperity lose responsibility.

## Q3(a). Rawlsian social justice in the Indian context

**Official question:** Analyse John Rawls’s concept of social justice in the Indian context. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q3(a) · Directive: Analyse in context · Difficulty: Moderate-High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories.md) → Rawls and distributive justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Western thinkers](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/07_Western-Moral-Philosophers-and-Thinkers.md) · [Moral theories](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/08_Moral-Theories-Deontology-Consequentialism-Virtue-Ethics.md)

### Model answer

Rawls asks persons behind a **veil of ignorance**, unaware of their caste, class, gender or talents, to choose fair principles. They would select: equal basic liberties; fair equality of opportunity; and inequalities permitted only when they benefit the least advantaged—the **difference principle**.

In India, this supports:

- equal constitutional liberties and protection against discrimination;
- reservations and enabling support to correct structural exclusion;
- universal quality education, health and nutrition to make opportunity substantive;
- progressive taxation and social security;
- priority to deprived regions and vulnerable groups in public investment.

The approach condemns a growth model whose burdens fall on displaced communities while gains accrue elsewhere. It also requires that affirmative action be supported by school quality, anti-discrimination enforcement and periodic evidence-based review.

Rawls, however, focuses mainly on distribution through institutions. India's caste hierarchy, patriarchy, disability and informal labour also demand recognition, capabilities and social transformation. Amartya Sen's capability approach usefully asks what people can actually be and do.

**Examiner takeaway:** Rawls supplies a fairness test for Indian institutions, but formal distribution must address entrenched social power and real capabilities.

## Q3(b). Public Services Code recommended by the Second ARC

**Official question:** Discuss the Public Services Code as recommended by the 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q3(b) · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Corruption and Administrative Ethics](../../topics/ethics/corruption-and-administrative-ethics.md) → public-service code · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Codes of ethics and conduct](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/16_Codes-of-Ethics-and-Codes-of-Conduct.md) · [Probity](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/14_Probity-Concept-and-Philosophical-Basis-of-Governance.md)

### Model answer

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission's fourth report, *Ethics in Governance*, recommended a statutory **Public Services Code** setting a common ethical framework for public servants, with service-specific codes elaborating it.

Its core commitments include:

- allegiance to the Constitution, law and democratic values;
- integrity, honesty, political neutrality, impartiality and merit;
- duty to maintain high standards and improve professional competence;
- accountability, transparency and openness, consistent with legitimate confidentiality;
- economy and effectiveness in using public resources;
- courteous, responsive and fair treatment of citizens;
- avoidance and disclosure of conflicts of interest;
- rejection of misuse of office and improper benefits.

The Code should guide discretion and provide a basis for training, appraisal and accountability, while detailed conduct rules prescribe enforceable behaviour concerning gifts, assets or political activity.

A code alone may become ceremonial. It requires ethical leadership, advice mechanisms, conflict disclosure, protection for honest reporting, fair investigation and proportionate sanctions. It should enable principled judgment, not replace it with another mechanical checklist.

**Examiner takeaway:** The ARC sought a values-based statutory compass connected to operational conduct and credible institutions.

## Q4(a). Kautilya on corruption and national development

**Official question:** “Corruption causes misuse of government treasury, administrative inefficiency and obstruction in the path of national development.” Discuss Kautilya’s views. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q4(a) · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Indian Moral Thinkers](../../topics/ethics/indian-moral-thinkers.md) → Kautilya on corruption · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Indian moral thinkers](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/06_Indian-Moral-Thinkers-and-Philosophers.md) · [Corruption challenges](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/18_Utilization-of-Public-Funds-and-Challenges-of-Corruption.md)

### Model answer

In the *Arthashastra*, Kautilya treats public revenue as the material basis of state capacity and welfare. Misappropriation by officials therefore weakens both the treasury and the ruler's legitimacy.

He recognises the difficulty of detection through memorable analogies: it is hard to know when an official misappropriates funds, just as it is hard to detect when a fish drinks water. He catalogues numerous modes of embezzlement and recommends:

- precise accounts and separation of collection, custody and expenditure;
- inspections, audits and cross-verification;
- intelligence and rotation to detect collusion;
- rewards for honest performance and informants;
- recovery and proportionate punishment for proven loss.

His realism anticipates principal-agent problems: monopoly over information and weak oversight create opportunities for rent extraction. Corruption then diverts development funds, lowers service quality and burdens citizens.

Yet covert surveillance and severe penalties require modern qualification by due process, privacy, independent audit and protection against malicious accusation. Kautilya's enduring lesson is preventive institutional design, not fear alone.

**Examiner takeaway:** Treasury protection requires incentives, records, verification and certain—but lawful—accountability.

## Q4(b). Social influence, persuasion and Swachh Bharat

**Official question:** How could social influence and persuasion contribute to the success of Swachh Bharat Abhiyan? (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q4(b) · Directive: Explain application · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Emotional Intelligence and Ethical Dilemmas](../../topics/ethics/emotional-intelligence-and-ethical-dilemmas.md) → social influence and persuasion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Attitude and persuasion](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/03_Attitude-Content-Structure-and-Persuasion.md)

### Model answer

Sanitation is not only an infrastructure problem; habits, norms and perceived expectations shape toilet use, waste segregation and littering. **Social influence** changes conduct through credible groups and shared norms; **persuasion** changes beliefs through reason, emotion and trusted communication.

Swachh Bharat can use:

- respected local leaders, teachers, sanitation workers and women's groups as messengers;
- public commitments and community monitoring to create descriptive and injunctive norms;
- demonstrations linking sanitation with dignity, health, safety and household savings;
- school-led habit formation and peer influence;
- locally framed messages in familiar languages;
- feedback dashboards and recognition of genuinely clean settlements;
- repeated doorstep counselling rather than one-time publicity.

For example, village mapping of open-defecation risks followed by women-led discussion can convert a private habit into collective responsibility.

Persuasion must remain truthful and respectful. Shaming, coercive photography or exclusion may produce compliance at the cost of dignity, especially where water, toilets or waste collection are absent. Behavioural efforts must therefore accompany usable infrastructure, maintenance and worker safety.

**Examiner takeaway:** Durable cleanliness arises when enabling services and respectful norm change reinforce each other.

## Q5. Law and ethics as tools for controlling human conduct

**Official question:** Law and ethics are considered to be the two tools for controlling human conduct so as to make it conducive to civilized social existence.  
(a) Discuss how they achieve this objective.  
(b) Giving examples, show how the two differ in their approaches. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q5 · Directive: Discuss and distinguish with examples · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories.md) → law, conscience and social order · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Laws, rules and conscience](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/10_Sources-of-Ethical-Guidance-Laws-Rules-Conscience.md)

### Model answer

Law and ethics both coordinate conduct, protect persons and make cooperation possible. **Law** publicly defines minimum duties, rights and sanctions; institutions enforce it predictably. **Ethics** develops conscience, virtues and reasoned standards that guide behaviour even where coercion or rules are absent.

| Basis | Law | Ethics |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Constitution, legislation and precedent | Conscience, reason, values and social reflection |
| Compliance | Externally enforceable minimum | Primarily internal and aspirational |
| Scope | Defined acts and omissions | Motives, character and grey areas |
| Change | Formal procedure | Moral dialogue and social learning |

Paying minimum wages is legally compulsory; paying a living wage during distress may exceed the legal floor. A procurement officer may satisfy technical tender rules yet act unethically by designing specifications for a favoured firm. Conversely, personal moral disapproval cannot override lawful equal treatment.

Law can be unjust and ethics can be subjective. Civilised order therefore needs constitutional law open to moral criticism, plus ethical citizens who respect rights and do not treat legality as the moral ceiling.

**Examiner takeaway:** Law supplies an enforceable floor; ethics supplies motive, judgment and a higher standard.

## Q6. Undesirable attitudes and cultivation of public-service values

**Official question:** Our attitudes towards life, work, other people and society are generally shaped unconsciously by the family and the social surroundings in which we grow up. Some of these unconsciously acquired attitudes and values are often undesirable in the citizens of a modern democratic and egalitarian society.  
(a) Discuss such undesirable values prevalent in today’s educated Indians.  
(b) How can such undesirable attitudes be changed and socio-ethical values considered necessary in public services be cultivated in the aspiring and serving civil servants? (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q6 · Directive: Discuss and prescribe · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Emotional Intelligence and Ethical Dilemmas](../../topics/ethics/emotional-intelligence-and-ethical-dilemmas.md) → attitude formation and change · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Attitude and persuasion](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/03_Attitude-Content-Structure-and-Persuasion.md) · [Foundational values](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/04_Aptitude-and-Foundational-Values-for-Civil-Service.md)

### Model answer

Education does not automatically remove inherited prejudice. Undesirable attitudes include caste and gender hierarchy, communal stereotyping, contempt for manual labour, ableism, excessive status consciousness, tolerance of petty corruption, victim-blaming, environmental indifference and preference for personal connections over fair procedure.

Attitudes contain cognitive, emotional and behavioural components; change must address all three:

- constitutional ethics, social psychology and dilemma-based learning before and during service;
- meaningful field immersion with marginalised communities and sanitation/front-line workers;
- diverse teams, role reversal and structured reflection to challenge stereotypes;
- ethical mentors and leaders who model impartiality;
- citizen feedback, reasoned orders and transparent criteria;
- simulations, counselling and emotional-intelligence practice;
- incentives for service and integrity, with fair sanctions for discrimination and corruption.

Mere lectures may produce socially desirable answers, not changed behaviour. Recruitment should test judgment without assuming that personality tests can guarantee virtue. Organisational culture must reward truthful advice and respectful service; otherwise hidden norms defeat training.

**Examiner takeaway:** Ethical attitudes grow through exposure, reflection, role models and institutions that align values with incentives.

## Q7. Harm caused by anger and its management

**Official question:** Anger is a harmful negative emotion. It is injurious to both personal life and work life.  
(a) Discuss how it leads to negative emotions and undesirable behaviours.  
(b) How can it be managed and controlled? (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q7 · Directive: Explain consequences and management · Difficulty: Easy-Moderate  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Emotional Intelligence and Ethical Dilemmas](../../topics/ethics/emotional-intelligence-and-ethical-dilemmas.md) → emotional self-regulation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owner:** [Emotional intelligence](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/05_Emotional-Intelligence-in-Administration.md)

### Model answer

Anger is an aroused response to perceived injury, frustration or injustice. Unregulated, it narrows attention, exaggerates hostile intent and produces resentment, revenge, anxiety and guilt. Behaviour may become verbal abuse, aggression, impulsive orders, biased appraisal or damaged relationships. At work, fear suppresses honest feedback and creates a toxic culture.

Management requires:

`recognise trigger → pause and regulate arousal → verify interpretation → express concern assertively → solve the underlying problem → review`.

Useful practices include slow breathing, temporary withdrawal where safe, reframing assumptions, active listening, separating the person from the act, non-violent communication, exercise, mindfulness and seeking counselling for recurrent loss of control. Administrators should delay non-urgent punitive decisions, hear the other side and record reasons.

Anger is not always morally worthless: it may signal injustice and energise reform. Aristotle's mean is relevant—right degree, object and manner. The aim is therefore not emotional suppression but transforming anger into proportionate, lawful and constructive action.

**Examiner takeaway:** Emotional intelligence converts an informative emotion from impulsive harm into reasoned response.

## Q8. Weber and independent bureaucratic morality

**Official question:** “Max Weber said that it is not wise to apply to public administration the sort of moral and ethical norms we apply to matters of personal conscience. It is important to realise that the State bureaucracy might possess its own independent bureaucratic morality.” Critically analyse this statement. (150 words, 10 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section A · Official Q8 · Directive: Critically analyse · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Work Culture and Public Funds](../../topics/ethics/work-culture-and-public-funds.md) → bureaucratic ethics and responsibility · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Public-service values and dilemmas](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/09_Public-Service-Values-Status-and-Ethical-Dilemmas.md) · [Laws, rules and conscience](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/10_Sources-of-Ethical-Guidance-Laws-Rules-Conscience.md)

### Model answer

Public administration cannot rest on each officer's private morality. Weberian bureaucracy requires impersonal rules, hierarchy, expertise, political neutrality and continuity. Its role morality demands legality, consistency, confidentiality and faithful execution of lawful democratic decisions. An officer cannot deny a legal benefit because of personal religious or moral dislike.

This institutional morality protects citizens from whim and makes power predictable. It also recognises an ethic of responsibility: officials must consider foreseeable consequences, not merely preserve personal purity.

However, bureaucratic morality cannot be wholly independent of general ethics. Rule obedience produced grave wrongs in authoritarian systems; secrecy, dehumanisation and “just following orders” can erase moral agency. Constitutional values, human dignity and reasoned conscience set outer limits.

A civil servant should therefore provide frank advice, obey lawful orders, refuse manifest illegality, record dissent and use protected review channels. Personal preference must not govern office, but neither can office extinguish responsibility.

**Examiner takeaway:** Legitimate bureaucratic ethics is role-specific yet bounded by constitutional morality and individual accountability.

## Q9. Toxic discharge, livelihood pressure and whistle-blowing

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** A fresh engineering graduate gets a job in a prestigious chemical industry. She likes the work. The salary is also good. However, after a few months she accidentally discovers that a highly toxic waste is being secretly discharged into a river nearby. This is causing health problems to the villagers downstream who depend on the river for their water needs. She is perturbed and mentions her concern to her colleagues who have been with the company for longer periods. They advise her to keep quite as anyone who mentions the topic is summarily dismissed. She cannot risk losing her job as she is the sole bread-winner for her family and has to support her ailing parents and siblings. At first, she thinks that if her seniors are keeping quiet, why should she stick out her neck. But her conscience pricks her to do something to save the river and the people who depend upon it. At heart she feels that the advice of silence given by her friends is not correct though she cannot give reasons for it. She thinks you are a wise person and seeks your advice.  
(a) What arguments can you advance to show her that keeping quiet is not morally right?  
(b) What course of action would you advise her to adopt and why? (250 words, 20 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q9 · Directive: Argue, advise and justify · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Environmental and Administrative Ethics](../../topics/ethics/environmental-and-administrative-ethics.md) → whistle-blowing and environmental harm · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Environment ethics](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/13_Emerging-Ethics-Technology-AI-and-Environment.md) · [Protecting honest officials](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/21_Protecting-Honest-Officials-and-Vigilance-Administration.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders and ethical issues

The engineer and dependent family; downstream villagers; workers and management; shareholders; regulators; future generations and the river ecosystem. Issues are professional duty, preventable harm, corporate loyalty, livelihood, complicity through silence, evidence integrity and retaliation.

### Why silence is morally wrong

- The discharge violates villagers' dignity, health and equal right to safe water; victims cannot consent to hidden risk.
- Professional knowledge creates a special duty to prevent foreseeable harm.
- Kantian ethics rejects treating villagers merely as a cost-saving means; utilitarian reasoning finds grave aggregate harm; virtue ethics requires courage and integrity.
- Others' silence does not remove personal agency. Continued silence normalises wrongdoing and exposes the company itself to larger legal and reputational loss.

### Options and consequences

1. **Remain silent:** protects income temporarily, but prolongs harm and creates moral and possible legal complicity.
2. **Make an impulsive public allegation:** may stop harm, but risks weak evidence, retaliation and procedural failure.
3. **Use an evidence-based, graduated escalation:** best balances effectiveness, fairness and self-protection.

### Advice and safeguards

She should lawfully verify and preserve evidence; record dates and observations; seek confidential technical or legal advice; report through the environmental/safety and ethics hierarchy in writing; demand containment and independent testing; then approach the competent pollution-control authority if internal action fails or danger is urgent. Public disclosure should be a last, responsible route through protected channels, without stealing or fabricating material.

She should avoid lone confrontation, maintain a retaliation record, seek whistle-blower/employee support and make prudent family and employment contingency plans. The company must stop discharge, provide safe water and medical assessment, remediate the river and submit to independent audit.

**Examiner takeaway:** Courage should be effective rather than reckless: prevent harm through verified, documented and protected escalation.

## Q10. Just compensation and rehabilitation for displaced communities

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** Land needed for mining, dams and other large-scale projects is acquired mostly from Adivasis, hill dwellers and rural communities. The displaced persons are paid monetary compensation as per the legal provisions. However, the payment is often tardy. In any case, it cannot sustain the displaced families for long. These people do not possess marketable skills to engage in some other occupation. They end up as low paid migrant labourers. Moreover, their traditional ways of community living are destroyed. Thus, the benefits of development go to industries, industrialists and urban communities whereas the costs are passed on to these poor helpless people. This unjust distribution of costs and benefits is unethical.

Suppose you have been entrusted with the task of drafting a better compensation-cum-rehabilitation policy for such displaced persons, how would you approach the problem and what would be the main elements of your suggested policy? (250 words, 20 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q10 · Directive: Frame an approach and policy · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories.md) → distributive and restorative justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Moral theories](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/08_Moral-Theories-Deontology-Consequentialism-Virtue-Ethics.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders and ethical framework

Displaced families, women, children and forest-dependent groups; host communities; project workers; state; investors; consumers and future generations. The governing principles are prior informed participation, minimum displacement, Rawlsian priority to the least advantaged, restoration rather than one-time charity, cultural dignity, inter-generational justice and transparent accountability.

### Approach

`establish necessity → examine alternatives → social and environmental impact assessment → map all rights-holders and common resources → consult in accessible forms → negotiate a livelihood-restoration plan → pay and resettle before displacement → independently monitor long-term outcomes`.

### Main policy elements

- Count owners, tenants, forest users, artisans, landless workers and women with independent entitlements; prevent a title-only bias.
- Prefer avoidance, land minimisation and land-for-land where feasible.
- Value land, houses, commons, forest access, lost income, transition costs and cultural/community disruption—not merely market title.
- Pay before possession through transparent indexed awards, annuities or benefit-sharing, with financial counselling.
- Provide serviced resettlement near livelihoods: housing, water, schools, health care, transport and places of cultural significance.
- Guarantee skill training tied to actual jobs, project employment where qualified, credit and livelihood grants.
- Resettle communities together where desired and support host settlements.
- Create time-bound grievance, legal-aid and appeal mechanisms; publish compliance.

### Implementation safeguards

Use an independent rehabilitation authority, social audits, joint accounts without displacing women's control, milestone-linked project clearance and longitudinal audits of income, nutrition and schooling. Unresolved restoration duties should survive transfer of project ownership.

**Examiner takeaway:** Justice requires prior, participatory restoration of livelihoods, community and capabilities—not delayed cash alone.

## Q11. Destitute woman without eligibility documents

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** Suppose you are an officer in-charge of implementing a social service scheme to provide support to old and destitute women. An old and illiterate woman comes to you to avail the benefits of the scheme. However, she has no documents to show that she fulfils the eligibility criteria. But after meeting her and listening to her you feel that she certainly needs support. Your enquiries also show that she is really destitute and living in a pitiable condition. You are in a dilemma as to what to do. Putting her under the scheme without necessary documents would clearly be violation of rules. But denying her the support would be cruel and inhuman.  
(a) Can you think of a rational way to resolve this dilemma?  
(b) Give your reasons for it. (250 words, 20 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q11 · Directive: Resolve and justify · Difficulty: Moderate-High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Service Delivery and Regulatory Ethics](../../topics/ethics/service-delivery-and-regulatory-ethics.md) → humane, rule-bound discretion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Public-service dilemmas](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/09_Public-Service-Values-Status-and-Ethical-Dilemmas.md) · [Service delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/17_Citizens-Charters-Work-Culture-and-Service-Delivery.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders and ethical conflict

The woman; other eligible applicants; officer and department; local verification authorities; taxpayers and similarly undocumented persons. The conflict is compassion and substantive justice versus legality, consistency, fraud prevention and limits of authority.

### Options and consequences

1. **Reject mechanically:** protects procedural form but defeats the scheme's purpose and punishes exclusion.
2. **Enrol without authority:** gives immediate relief but creates illegality, unequal discretion and audit risk.
3. **Facilitate lawful verification with interim protection:** reconciles rules with humane service.

### Rational course

I would record her application and findings; check whether rules permit alternative evidence, self-declaration, local-body certification, field verification or relaxation by a competent authority. I would assign an official or service centre to help obtain age, residence, identity and destitution documents, rather than returning an illiterate applicant.

Pending verification, I would connect her to lawful emergency food, shelter, health care or a discretionary relief fund and seek an expedited reasoned order from the authorised officer. If the rules contain no route for genuine undocumented applicants, I would propose a general, auditable exception procedure rather than inventing a personal favour.

### Reasons and safeguards

The scheme exists to aid persons like her; administrative fairness requires meaningful access, not identical paperwork burdens. At the same time, verification, a written speaking order, dual approval, time limits, audit and appeal protect other claimants and public funds.

**Examiner takeaway:** Compassion should activate lawful facilitation and reform, not arbitrary rule-breaking.

## Q12. Domestic violence by a respected superior

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** You are a young, aspiring and sincere employee in a Government office working as an assistant to the director of your department. Since you have joined recently, you need to learn and progress. Luckily your superior is very kind and ready to train you for your job. He is a very intelligent and well-informed person having knowledge of various departments. In short, you respect your boss and are looking forward to learn a lot from him.

Since you have a good tuning with the boss, he started depending on you. One day due to ill health he invited you at his place for finishing some urgent work. You reached his house and before you could ring the bell you heard shouting noises. You waited for a while. After entering the house the boss greeted you and explained the work. But you were constantly disturbed by the crying of a woman. At last, you inquired with the boss but his answer did not satisfy you.

Next day, you were compelled to inquire further in the office and found out that his behaviour is very bad at home with his wife. He also beats up his wife. His wife is not well educated and is a simple woman in comparison to her husband. You see that though your boss is a nice person in the office, he is engaged in domestic violence at home.

In such a situation, you are left with the following options. Analyse each option with its consequences.  
(a) Just ignore thinking about it because it is their personal matter.  
(b) Report the case to the appropriate authority.  
(c) Your own innovative approach towards the situation. (250 words, 20 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q12 · Directive: Analyse options and devise a response · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Conflict Administration](../../topics/ethics/conflict-administration.md) → domestic violence, hierarchy and duty of care · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Public-service dilemmas](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/09_Public-Service-Values-Status-and-Ethical-Dilemmas.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders and ethical issues

The wife and any children; superior; employee; colleagues; police/protection services and department. Issues include safety and dignity, privacy versus preventable violence, power imbalance, evidentiary limits, loyalty, retaliation and victim autonomy.

### Option analysis

1. **Ignore it as private:** avoids intrusion and career risk, but violence is not a merely private disagreement. Silence may enable escalating harm and abandons a vulnerable person.
2. **Report immediately on office hearsay:** may trigger protection and accountability, but an uninformed or poorly timed complaint can expose the wife to retaliation, deny her agency and rest on incomplete facts. Immediate police/emergency contact is nevertheless justified where danger is imminent.
3. **Safety-centred, informed intervention:** discreetly establish a safe channel to the wife through a trusted trained service; provide information on protection, medical, legal and shelter options; document only directly known facts; and escalate according to risk and law.

### Preferred course and safeguards

I would not confront the superior alone or attempt amateur marital mediation. If immediate assault is witnessed or credible imminent danger exists, I would call emergency/police assistance. Otherwise, I would confidentially consult the authorised women's support/protection mechanism, facilitate independent contact with the wife and follow her informed choices within legal reporting duties.

I would report workplace intimidation or retaliation separately through departmental channels. Confidentiality, need-to-know records, professional risk assessment and a no-retaliation plan are essential.

**Examiner takeaway:** Respect for privacy cannot shield violence; intervention must centre safety, evidence and the survivor's agency.

## Q13. Industrial development, CSR and residents' concerns

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** ABC Ltd. is a large transnational company having diversified business activities with a huge shareholder base. The company is continuously expanding and generating employment. The company, in its expansion and diversification programme, decides to establish a new plant at Vikaspuri, an area which is underdeveloped. The new plant is designed to use energy efficient technology that will help the company to save production cost by 20%. The company’s decision goes well with the Government policy of attracting investment to develop such underdeveloped regions. The Government has also announced tax holiday for five years for the companies that invest in underdeveloped areas. However, the new plant may bring chaos for the inhabitants of Vikaspuri region, which is otherwise tranquil. The new plant may result in increased cost of living, aliens migrating to the region, disturbing the social and economic order. The company sensing the possible protest tried to educate the people of Vikaspuri region and public in general that how its Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) policy would help overcome the likely difficulties of the residents of Vikaspuri region. In spite of this the protests begin and some of the residents decided to approach the judiciary as their plea before the Government did not yield any result.  
(a) Identify the issues involved in the case.  
(b) What can be suggested to satisfy the company’s goal and to address the residents’ concerns? (300 words, 25 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q13 · Directive: Identify issues and reconcile interests · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Governance and International Ethics](../../topics/ethics/governance-and-international-ethics.md) → corporate responsibility and stakeholder justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Corporate governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/12_Corporate-Governance-and-International-Ethics.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders

Residents, vulnerable tenants and local businesses; incoming workers; company employees, management and shareholders; local government; consumers; judiciary; environment and future generations.

### Issues involved

- employment and regional development versus disruption, inflation and pressure on housing/services;
- shareholder benefit and 20% cost saving versus stakeholder rights and fair distribution;
- migration framed as “aliens,” risking prejudice against fellow citizens;
- adequacy of environmental and social impact assessment;
- meaningful participation versus one-way corporate “education”;
- government's conflict between investment promotion and regulatory duty;
- whether CSR is being used to purchase acceptance for unmanaged core-business impacts;
- access to remedy after executive non-response.

### Options and consequences

1. **Proceed unchanged:** fastest for investment, but risks litigation, distrust, delay and uncompensated harm.
2. **Abandon the plant:** avoids disruption but loses jobs and cleaner technology without testing mitigation.
3. **Redesign through participatory, binding safeguards:** slower initially but creates a durable social licence.

### Recommended settlement

Commission an independent environmental and social impact assessment with a public baseline on water, housing, prices, traffic, livelihoods and culture. Hold accessible consultations with elected bodies, women, tenants, traders and vulnerable groups; disclose designs and grievance routes.

The company should adopt local skill training and fair recruitment without excluding migrants; worker housing and transport; phased construction; pollution and resource limits; support for expanded water, health, school and waste capacity; local-procurement opportunities; and targeted assistance for demonstrably affected livelihoods. A community-development agreement should contain measurable duties, timelines and funding, but statutory compliance must remain separate from CSR.

Create a joint monitoring body with resident, company and local-government representation; publish indicators; use independent audits and a time-bound grievance/mediation mechanism; preserve judicial review. Tax concessions may be linked to verified compliance.

**Examiner takeaway:** Sustainable investment internalises social costs through participation, enforceable mitigation and shared gains—not publicity alone.

## Q14. Regulating honest NGOs without coercion or corruption

**Official case and demand (verified transcription):** Saraswati was a successful IT professional in USA. Moved by the patriotic sense of doing something for the country she returned to India. Together with some other like-minded friends, she formed an NGO to build a school for a poor rural community.

The objective of the school was to provide the best quality modern education at a nominal cost. She soon discovered that she has to seek permission from a number of Government agencies. The rules and procedures were quite confusing and cumbersome. What frustrated her most was the delays, callous attitude of officials and constant demand for bribes. Her experience and the experience of many others like her has deterred people from taking up social service projects.

A measure of Government control over voluntary social work is necessary. But it should not be exercised in a coercive or corrupt manner. What measures can you suggest to ensure that due control is exercised but well meaning, honest NGO efforts are not thwarted? (300 words, 25 marks)

**Metadata:** 2016 · Mains · GS-IV · Section B · Official Q14 · Directive: Suggest balanced institutional measures · Difficulty: High  
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Service Delivery and Regulatory Ethics](../../topics/ethics/service-delivery-and-regulatory-ethics.md) → proportionate NGO regulation and service delivery · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Canonical owners:** [Service delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/17_Citizens-Charters-Work-Culture-and-Service-Delivery.md) · [Corruption challenges](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/18_Utilization-of-Public-Funds-and-Challenges-of-Corruption.md) · [Case-study method](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ethics/basic/22_Case-Study-Method-and-Answer-Architecture.md)

### Stakeholders and ethical issues

Saraswati and NGO workers; rural children and parents; donors; teachers; regulators; taxpayers and competing institutions. The state must protect child safety, educational standards, finances and land use, while avoiding rent-seeking, arbitrary discretion and barriers that exclude genuine civic initiative.

### Options and consequences

1. **Heavy ex-ante licensing:** may detect risk before operation, but multiplies delay, discretion and bribery.
2. **Near-total deregulation:** encourages initiative, but may expose children and donors to unsafe or fraudulent organisations.
3. **Single-window, risk-based and transparent regulation:** preserves essential safeguards while reducing coercive contact.

### Suggested measures

- Publish one plain-language checklist identifying every approval, legal basis, document, fee and timeline.
- Create a unified online-and-assisted single window with one application, status tracking and interoperable departmental verification.
- Distinguish registration from sectoral permission; remove duplicate affidavits and accept self-certification for low-risk matters, backed by random audit and serious penalties for fraud.
- Impose statutory time limits, deemed escalation and written reasons—not automatic approval where child safety or building safety requires scrutiny.
- Adopt risk-based inspection with checklists, geo-tagged reports, conflict disclosure and rotation of inspectors.
- Provide pre-application help desks, model documents and a facilitation officer for rural/non-technical applicants.
- Enable cashless fees, e-procurement, public dashboards and anonymous bribery complaints with protected investigation.
- Establish an independent, time-bound appeal and grievance mechanism.
- Require proportionate disclosure of governance, audited finances, safeguarding, outcomes and related-party transactions.

### Implementation safeguards

Measure departments on lawful disposal quality, not approval numbers alone. Audit delays and rejection patterns; prosecute solicitation of bribes with due process; and consult NGOs when simplifying rules. Regulation must remain neutral—neither hostility nor favour toward the voluntary sector.

**Examiner takeaway:** Smart regulation replaces repeated discretion with clear standards, risk-based verification, traceability and appeal.
