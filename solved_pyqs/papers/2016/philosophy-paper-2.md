## Q1(a). “Sovereignty is the supreme power over citizens and subjects, unrestrained by law.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → Bodin · absolute sovereignty · legal limits · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(a) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Directive: Discuss  
**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty — Bodin, Austin, Laski and Kautilya  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Bodin · supreme and perpetual power · positive law · higher-law restraints  
**Proposed slug:** `sovereignty-supreme-power-unrestrained-law`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** Identify the Bodinian claim, distinguish freedom from ordinary positive law from total lawlessness, and assess modern constitutional sovereignty.

**Argument map:** final lawmaking power → immunity from own ordinary enactments → divine/natural law and covenants → constitutionalism/pluralism objection → qualified validity.

### Model answer

The statement captures Bodin's definition of sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth. A sovereign must possess a final, undivided authority to make and repeal positive law; otherwise another will is legally supreme. Austin similarly locates sovereignty in a determinate human superior habitually obeyed and not habitually obeying another.

Yet “unrestrained by law” cannot mean arbitrary power. Bodin binds the sovereign by divine and natural law, property and valid covenants. Modern constitutionalism further separates constituent sovereignty from governmental competence: Parliament, executive and courts exercise powers under a higher Constitution.

**Objection:** legal restraints merely relocate, rather than abolish, an ultimately unlimited sovereign.  
**Reply:** finality is necessary for deciding disputes, but final authority may be institutionally dispersed and publicly accountable; it need not be one person's uncontrolled will.

**Assessment:** sovereignty denotes supreme legal finality, not moral or political omnipotence. The definition illuminates absolute monarchy but requires rights, constitutional limits and plural associations to fit a democratic State.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the doctrine precisely and distinguishes legal supremacy from despotism.

## Q1(b). A well-ordered society, according to Rawls, is effectively regulated by a public conception of justice. Do you agree? Give reasons.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → Rawls · justice as fairness · well-ordered society · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(b) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Take a position and give reasons  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Justice  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** public conception · basic structure · stability for the right reasons · reasonable pluralism  
**Proposed slug:** `rawls-well-ordered-society-public-justice`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain Rawls's technical idea and assess whether shared public principles can regulate a plural society.

**Argument map:** shared principles → known institutional compliance → reciprocal expectations → pluralism/inequality objections → public reason plus contestability.

### Model answer

For Rawls, a society is well ordered when everyone accepts, and knows that others accept, the same public principles of justice, its basic structure is publicly known generally to satisfy them, and citizens possess an effective sense of justice. Publicity enables cooperation among free and equal persons; stability then rests on reasons, not fear.

I agree conditionally. A public conception disciplines arbitrary power and supplies common standards for constitutional essentials. Rawls's equal basic liberties, fair opportunity and difference principle also connect legitimacy with the position of the least advantaged.

**Objection:** deep religious, caste and ideological pluralism makes one shared conception unrealistic; formally public principles may conceal structural injustice.  
**Reply:** public agreement need not cover comprehensive doctrines. An overlapping consensus can support political principles while democratic protest exposes defective application.

**Assessment:** a public conception is necessary but insufficient. A well-ordered society also requires effective institutions, material capability and continuing revision by excluded voices. India’s constitutional morality illustrates both the integrating promise and the unfinished social task.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the technical concept, gives reasons and qualifies agreement.

## Q1(c). “Socialism itself is the fulfilment of democracy.” Analyse.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → socialism · democracy · economic power · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(c) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Analyse  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Socialism and Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** political equality · social ownership · substantive democracy · authoritarian risk  
**Proposed slug:** `socialism-fulfilment-democracy`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Test whether democracy's political promise requires socialist control of economic power without equating all socialism with democracy.

**Argument map:** equal vote → unequal property distorts voice → social ownership/welfare → authoritarian objection → democratic socialism as conditional fulfilment.

### Model answer

The claim means that political democracy remains incomplete where concentrated property converts formal equality into unequal power. Marxists argue that workers who vote equally but depend on owners for livelihood lack substantive self-government. Social ownership, economic security and workplace participation can therefore extend democracy from the ballot to production.

Democratic socialists add universal education, health and social protection: citizens can exercise liberty only when basic capabilities are secure.

**Objection:** socialism may centralise economic and political power in a party-state, extinguishing opposition, choice and civil liberty.  
**Reply:** that criticises authoritarian socialism, not the democratic case for dispersing economic domination through cooperatives, welfare guarantees, unions and accountable public ownership.

**Assessment:** socialism is not automatically democracy's fulfilment. It fulfils democracy only when equality is combined with plural parties, constitutional rights, decentralisation and public accountability. Democracy without social justice is thin; socialism without political freedom is self-defeating.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the thesis, confronts its historical danger and supplies conditions for validity.

## Q1(d). Evaluate the statement that all human beings have certain unalienable rights.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Individual and State](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/individual-and-state.md) → human rights · inalienability · dignity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(d) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Directive: Evaluate  
**Syllabus unit:** Individual and State — Rights  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Locke · natural rights · human dignity · forfeiture and limitation  
**Proposed slug:** `unalienable-human-rights-evaluation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Individual and State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Individual-and-State.md)

**Demand decoding:** Clarify the sense of inalienability, justify universal rights and handle conflicts, limits and cultural objections.

**Argument map:** personhood/dignity → rights not gifts of State → non-transfer/forfeiture distinction → relativist and conflict objections → universal core, contextual specification.

### Model answer

Unalienable rights are claims a person cannot validly surrender or lose merely through another's will. Locke grounded life, liberty and property in natural law; contemporary human-rights theory grounds a basic core in equal dignity, agency and vulnerability. The State recognises rather than creates protection against slavery, torture and arbitrary killing.

Inalienability does not make every exercise unlimited. Liberty may be restricted to prevent harm, and imprisonment can follow fair conviction; nevertheless the offender retains dignity and due process. Nor are all desirable benefits equally fundamental.

**Objection:** rights are culturally constructed, so universal claims impose one tradition.  
**Reply:** institutional forms vary, but bodily security, conscience and equal standing protect the conditions under which cultures themselves can be chosen and criticised.

**Assessment:** certain rights are unalienable as moral status-rights, although their legal specification and balancing require public reasoning. India’s fundamental-rights framework exemplifies universal status mediated through constitutional remedies.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes possession from exercise and defends a qualified universalism.

## Q1(e). “The goal in punishing should be to reform the individual.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Crime and Punishment](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/crime-and-punishment.md) → reformative theory · proportionality · public protection · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(e) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Comment  
**Syllabus unit:** Crime and Punishment — Theories of Punishment  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** rehabilitation · deterrence · retribution · dignity  
**Proposed slug:** `punishment-goal-reform-individual`  
**Canonical owner:** [Crime and Punishment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Crime-and-Punishment.md)

**Demand decoding:** Defend or limit reform as punishment's purpose while relating it to desert, deterrence and safety.

**Argument map:** offender as revisable agent → rehabilitation/reintegration → paternalism and proportionality objections → plural penal aims → reform-centred conclusion.

### Model answer

The reformative theory treats the offender as a responsible but revisable person, not an enemy to be broken. Education, therapy, skill-building and restorative accountability can address causes of crime, reduce recidivism and enable social reintegration. It therefore respects dignity better than vengeance.

Reform cannot be the sole measure. Indeterminate confinement “until cured” may become paternalistic and harsher than proportionate punishment. Victims also deserve recognition, dangerous offenders may require incapacitation, and law must communicate public condemnation.

**Objection:** grave wrongdoing deserves suffering regardless of future benefit.  
**Reply:** proportional censure is compatible with humane conditions; desert sets an upper limit, while reform guides the manner and constructive purpose of punishment.

**Assessment:** reform should be the governing aim within a plural framework of proportionality, deterrence, protection and reparation. Indian prison policy should therefore replace degrading custody with correctional opportunities without erasing accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives reform priority without reducing punishment to therapeutic control.

## Q2(a). Is liberty a positive and equal opportunity of self-realization? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → liberty · positive freedom · equality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(a) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Equality and Liberty  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** T. H. Green · Berlin · capabilities · paternalism  
**Proposed slug:** `liberty-positive-equal-self-realization`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Relate positive liberty to equal enabling conditions, then test the risks of imposed ideals and neglect of non-interference.

**Argument map:** mere absence of restraint → effective capacity → equal social conditions → Berlinian paternalism objection → capabilities plus basic negative liberty.

### Model answer

Negative liberty means an area free from interference. Positive liberty asks whether one actually possesses the power to act autonomously and develop capacities. T. H. Green therefore regards freedom not as doing whatever one desires, but as the effective power to pursue a worthwhile good. On this view, education, health, livelihood and freedom from caste or gender subordination are conditions of self-realisation.

The opportunity must also be equal. Formal permission to compete means little when inherited deprivation determines capability. Amartya Sen's capability approach sharpens the point: equal resources may still yield unequal real freedoms because persons face different social and bodily conditions.

**Objection:** Isaiah Berlin warns that rulers may identify a citizen's “real self” and coerce the empirical person for an alleged higher freedom. Moreover, people reasonably disagree about the good life.  
**Reply:** positive liberty need not impose one perfectionist end. It can secure plural capabilities—education, bodily integrity, political voice—from which persons choose.

**Assessment:** liberty is best understood as protected non-interference plus equal, positive capability for self-direction. India’s reservations and social rights are defensible where they dismantle structural barriers, but remain subject to rights and democratic review.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates liberty and equality while directly answering the paternalism objection.

## Q2(b). On what grounds does Laski criticize Austin’s concept of sovereignty?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → Laski · Austin · pluralism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Directive: Explain grounds of criticism  
**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty — Austin and Laski  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** determinate superior · habitual obedience · associations · moral limits  
**Proposed slug:** `laski-critique-austin-sovereignty`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Austin before listing Laski's empirical, legal, social and normative objections, then assess their reach.

**Argument map:** Austin's command model → constitutional and customary limits → social pluralism → international interdependence → retained need for legal finality.

### Model answer

Austin defines law as the command of a determinate human superior, backed by sanction, who receives habitual obedience but does not habitually obey another. Laski criticises this monistic and unlimited sovereignty on several grounds.

First, no modern State contains a socially omnipotent superior. Governments depend on public opinion, conventions, parties and administrative capacity. Second, law is not exhausted by command: custom, judicial development and constitutional principle shape legal obligation. Third, society is plural. Churches, unions, families, universities and professional associations possess original purposes and loyalties; the State should coordinate them, not absorb them. Fourth, rights and moral personality limit legitimate authority. Finally, international interdependence makes external independence relational rather than absolute.

**Objection:** unless one institution has a final legal word, conflicts among associations become irresolvable.  
**Reply:** Laski need not deny procedural finality; he denies that legal competence entails unlimited moral authority or exclusive social allegiance.

**Assessment:** Laski decisively corrects Austin's empirical and normative absolutism. Yet pluralism still needs a constitutional umpire capable of securing common rights. Sovereignty should therefore mean limited, accountable final authority, not all-embracing supremacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It organises Laski's criticism by distinct grounds and acknowledges the finality problem.

## Q2(c). “The right of free speech implies the genuine independence of the judiciary and its complete separation from the executive.” Evaluate.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Individual and State](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/individual-and-state.md) → free speech · judicial independence · separation of powers · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(c) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Evaluate  
**Syllabus unit:** Individual and State — Rights and Accountability  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Mill · constitutional remedies · judicial review · institutional independence  
**Proposed slug:** `free-speech-judicial-independence-executive-separation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Individual and State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Individual-and-State.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain why speech needs an independent adjudicator, then test whether “complete separation” is necessary or institutionally possible.

**Argument map:** speech checks power → executive conflict of interest → independent courts/remedies → functional overlap objection → independence without isolation.

### Model answer

Free speech protects individual self-development, truth-seeking and democratic accountability. Mill's defence of dissent is ineffective if the same executive criticised by a speaker can define offence, order prosecution and determine guilt. Judicial independence is therefore an institutional implication of the right: an impartial court must test restrictions for legality, necessity and proportionality and provide an enforceable remedy.

Genuine independence requires secure tenure, impartial appointments, financial and decisional autonomy, reasoned judgments and freedom from retaliation. In India, Articles 19(1)(a), 19(2), 32 and 226 make courts central to checking censorship and arbitrary executive action.

The claim of **complete separation**, however, is too strong. Constitutional government involves functional interaction: the executive implements judgments, legislatures define reasonable restrictions, and judicial appointments may include several institutions. Total institutional isolation may reduce accountability or produce judicial supremacy.

**Objection:** any executive role compromises impartiality.  
**Reply:** influence is not control. Transparent, plural appointment and removal procedures can preserve decisional independence while preventing self-perpetuating judicial power.

Speech also needs more than courts—plural media, protection from private intimidation, access to information and affordable litigation. Courts themselves may defer excessively or delay relief.

**Assessment:** free speech logically requires independent and effective judicial review, but not a hermetic separation. The sound ideal is separation of core functions with checks, institutional autonomy and reciprocal accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both the rights logic and the overstatement in “complete separation.”

## Q3(a). Is the State an agency for expressing the will of the ruling classes? Examine.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → Marxism · class State · relative autonomy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Examine  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Marx and Engels · instrumentalism · Gramsci · Poulantzas · democratic autonomy  
**Proposed slug:** `state-agency-ruling-class-will`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Present the Marxian thesis, distinguish versions of class rule, confront counterevidence and reach a qualified institutional verdict.

**Argument map:** property relations → State protects reproduction → direct capture/hegemony/structural dependence → welfare and democracy objection → relative autonomy within unequal structure.

### Model answer

Marx and Engels describe the modern executive as managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie. The thesis is not merely that individual capitalists issue orders. Because the State protects property, contract and the conditions of accumulation, its ordinary functioning tends to reproduce ruling-class power.

Three readings clarify the claim. An **instrumentalist** reading stresses direct elite control through wealth, lobbying and personnel. Gramsci adds **hegemony**: consent is organised through civil society so sectional interests appear universal. Structural Marxists such as Poulantzas grant the State relative autonomy; it may discipline particular firms or concede welfare in order to stabilise capitalism as a whole.

**Objection:** universal franchise, labour law, reservations, progressive taxation and welfare sometimes defeat dominant economic interests. The State also has bureaucratic, nationalist and constitutional purposes irreducible to class.  
**Reply:** concessions can reflect popular struggle and still preserve the wider order; nevertheless their reality disproves a simple puppet model.

In India, unequal campaign resources and corporate access reveal class influence, while elections, courts, social movements and constitutional rights provide countervailing agency. Caste and gender also structure power independently and interact with class.

**Assessment:** the State is neither a neutral umpire nor merely the speaking tube of one class. It is a contested institution with relative autonomy operating inside unequal social relations. The Marxian thesis is strongest as a structural warning and weakest as an exhaustive account.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes Marxian models and tests them against democratic and Indian evidence.

## Q3(b). Can we consider “freedom from domination” as one of the justifications for multiculturalism? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → multiculturalism · non-domination · minority rights · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Take a position and give reasons  
**Syllabus unit:** Humanism; Secularism; Multi-culturalism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** republican non-domination · Kymlicka · Taylor · internal restrictions  
**Proposed slug:** `non-domination-justification-multiculturalism`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Show how cultural recognition prevents arbitrary majority power, while addressing domination within minority groups.

**Argument map:** majority institutions are not neutral → minority vulnerability → recognition/self-government → internal domination objection → dual non-domination test.

### Model answer

Freedom from domination means not merely absence of current interference but security against another's arbitrary power. It provides a strong justification for multiculturalism because ostensibly neutral institutions often reflect majority language, history and customs. A minority forced to depend on majority goodwill for education, worship or cultural reproduction is dominated even when interference is intermittent.

Kymlicka's group-differentiated rights—language protection, representation and limited self-government—can equalise the societal conditions of choice. Taylor's politics of recognition adds that systematic misrecognition damages equal status.

**Objection:** group rights may empower patriarchal or clerical elites to dominate women, dissenters or converts within the community.  
**Reply:** non-domination must operate both **between** groups and **within** them. External protections are defensible; internal restrictions that suppress exit, voice or basic rights are not.

Indian protections for minority culture and tribal self-governance illustrate legitimate insulation from assimilation, while constitutional equality supplies internal safeguards.

**Assessment:** yes, non-domination justifies multicultural accommodation better than mere cultural preservation, because it identifies unequal power. But recognition must remain rights-compatible, participatory and open to internal contestation.

**Why this earns marks:** It offers a reasoned yes while applying the same principle to minorities and their members.

## Q3(c). Is it possible to measure social progress independent of economic development? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Development and Social Progress](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/development-and-social-progress.md) → social indicators · economic growth · capabilities · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Directive: Discuss  
**Syllabus unit:** Development and Social Progress  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** GDP · Sen · capabilities · multidimensional progress  
**Proposed slug:** `measure-social-progress-independent-economic-development`  
**Canonical owner:** [Development and Social Progress](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Development-Social-Progress.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish conceptual independence from causal interdependence and propose defensible measures of social progress.

**Argument map:** GDP is means not end → health/education/equality/agency indicators → resource dependence objection → separate measurement, connected explanation.

### Model answer

Social progress can be measured independently in the sense that it has criteria other than income or output. Longevity, literacy, bodily security, gender and caste equality, political voice, ecological quality and freedom from humiliation are constitutive social achievements. Sen's capability approach asks what persons are actually able to be and do; a rise in GDP that accompanies malnutrition, displacement or repression is not sufficient evidence of progress.

Composite measures such as human-development and multidimensional-poverty indices improve on GDP, while democratic participation and distribution require additional qualitative assessment.

**Objection:** social achievements require economic resources; separating them is artificial and may ignore fiscal capacity.  
**Reply:** causal dependence does not entail identity. Similar-income regions can produce different health or educational outcomes because institutions, distribution and public priorities differ.

Measurement also involves contested values and risks hiding inequalities behind averages. Indicators should therefore be disaggregated and publicly debated.

**Assessment:** social progress is conceptually and measurably distinct from economic development, though not causally independent of it. Economic growth is one enabling means; the evaluative end is expanded, fairly distributed human freedom.

**Why this earns marks:** It resolves the ambiguity in “independent” and supplies a philosophically grounded metric.

## Q4(a). “To me the female sex is not the weaker sex. It is the nobler of the two.” Evaluate this statement of Gandhi.

**Proposed slug:** `gandhi-women-not-weaker-nobler-evaluation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Gender Discrimination](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Gender-Discrimination.md)

**Demand decoding:** Recover the emancipatory intent while criticising idealisation and testing it against equal agency.

**Argument map:** rejection of physical inferiority → courage/non-violence → mobilisation of women → pedestalisation objection → equality without gender essence.

### Model answer

Gandhi's statement rejects the patriarchal inference from physical difference to moral or civic inferiority. He associated women's endurance, care and capacity for self-suffering with non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) and drew women into satyagraha as political agents rather than passive dependants. In a society normalising purdah, child marriage and denial of education, this reversal had emancipatory force.

Yet calling women “nobler” can reproduce the very essentialism it contests. It idealises sacrifice, purity and motherhood, burdens women with superior virtue and may excuse men's violence rather than redistribute power. Feminist equality requires freedom to be assertive, ambitious and fallible—not a pedestal.

**Reply:** strategic revaluation is useful, but only if care and non-violence become human virtues and do not confine women to traditional roles.

**Assessment:** the denial of weakness and affirmation of agency remain powerful; the claim of sex-based nobility should be replaced by equal personhood, capabilities, property rights and bodily autonomy.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances historical emancipation with a precise feminist criticism.

### Model answer

I agree that collective reflection and decision-making are major mechanisms of empowerment. Consciousness-raising converts apparently private experiences—domestic violence, unpaid care, wage discrimination—into publicly intelligible structures. Collective organisation reduces isolation, pools knowledge and resources, and strengthens bargaining power in households, markets and local government.

## Q4(c). How did Ambedkar analyse the caste system from the historical and social perspectives? Explain.

**Metadata:** Official Q4(c) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Explain  
**Syllabus unit:** Caste Discrimination — Gandhi and Ambedkar  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** enclosed class · endogamy · graded inequality · religious legitimation · annihilation  
**Proposed slug:** `ambedkar-historical-social-analysis-caste`  
**Canonical owner:** [Caste: Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Caste-Gandhi-Ambedkar.md)

**Demand decoding:** Present Ambedkar's account of caste formation and its operating social structure, not merely his reform programme.

**Argument map:** class becomes enclosed through endogamy → imitation and multiplication → graded hierarchy/division of labourers → religious sanction and anti-sociality → annihilation.

### Model answer

Ambedkar rejects explanations of caste as merely racial difference, occupation or a benign division of labour. Historically, he asks how an open class became a closed caste. In *Castes in India*, he identifies enforced **endogamy** as the central mechanism. The initial closure of a prestigious group—associated in his reconstruction with Brahmanical practice—was imitated by others, producing multiple self-enclosed units. Practices regulating widows, marriageable men and women, and female sexuality helped maintain the surplus balance required by endogamy.

Socially, caste is a system of **graded inequality**, not a simple binary between rulers and ruled. Every caste can look down upon another, fragmenting solidarity among the oppressed. It is a “division of labourers,” assigning inherited status and occupation while denying mobility, fraternity and common public life. Untouchability represents its extreme exclusion.

Religious texts, purity-pollution and ritual authority legitimate this hierarchy, so economic change alone cannot dissolve it. Caste survives through marriage, social honour and everyday separation even when occupations change.

**Objection:** imitation and scriptural sanction may understate material power, regional diversity and political economy.  
**Reply:** Ambedkar's account is best read as a structural diagnosis of closure and legitimation, compatible with additional economic causes.

**Assessment:** because caste destroys liberty, equality and especially fraternity, reform within hierarchy is insufficient. Its annihilation requires inter-caste association and marriage, education, representation, economic opportunity, constitutional rights and criticism of sacred authority.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates Ambedkar's historical mechanism from his social morphology and reform conclusion.

**Syllabus unit:** Reason, Revelation and Faith  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** propositional model · Aquinas · Barth · contradiction · hermeneutics  
**Proposed slug:** `propositional-revelation-not-against-reason`  
**Canonical owner:** [Reason, Revelation and Faith](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Reason-Revelation-Faith.md)

**Demand decoding:** Assess the propositional conception and explain reason's critical role without reducing revelation to philosophy.

**Argument map:** revelation communicates truth claims → reason tests coherence/interpretation → supra-rational mysteries → encounter-model objection → complementary models.

### Model answer

On the propositional model, revelation communicates divinely authorised truths—about God, creation, obligation or salvation—expressible in statements. This gives doctrine cognitive content and makes agreement, contradiction and interpretation possible.

Reason remains indispensable. It identifies the text or testimony, clarifies concepts, tests logical consistency, compares interpretations and guards against fraud or morally destructive commands. Aquinas's maxim is that truths of faith may be above reason but cannot contradict genuine reason, since truth cannot conflict with truth. Mystery is not the same as contradiction.

## Q6(b). Compare and contrast the relation of man to the world in the oriental religions.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Notions of God](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/notions-of-god.md) → relation of man to the world · Hinduism · Buddhism · Jainism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q6(b) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Compare and contrast  
**Syllabus unit:** Notions of God — Relation to Man and the World; Religion without God  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** karma · rebirth · *saṃsāra* · liberation · non-duality · no-self · plural souls  
**Proposed slug:** `human-world-relation-oriental-religions`  
**Canonical owner:** [Notions of God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Notions-of-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare major Indian/“oriental” traditions on self, world's status, bondage, ethics and liberation while avoiding false homogeneity.

**Argument map:** shared karmic-cyclical horizon → Vedāntic variations → Buddhist process → Jain plural realism → Daoist harmony → convergences and incompatibilities.

### Model answer

“Oriental religions” is a broad and potentially homogenising label. Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Chinese traditions nevertheless often treat the human being as embedded in a morally or causally ordered cosmos rather than as an isolated dominator of nature.

In Hindu traditions, relation to the world varies. Advaita Vedānta regards empirical plurality as dependent appearance (*māyā*) grounded in non-dual Brahman; liberation (*mokṣa*) is knowledge that the self (*ātman*) is Brahman. Viśiṣṭādvaita treats souls and world as real modes of God, making embodied service significant. Jainism is pluralist and realist: souls (*jīvas*) are entangled with karmic matter. Non-violence (*ahiṃsā*), non-possession and many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) discipline conduct toward all living beings. Daoism, by contrast, stresses harmony with the Way (*Dao*) and non-coercive action (*wu wei*).

**Objection:** emphasis on liberation can devalue history and encourage passivity.  
**Reply:** karma-yoga, Buddhist compassion and Jain non-violence generate demanding worldly ethics, though quietist interpretations remain possible.

**Assessment:** common themes include interdependence, disciplined desire and release from egoic attachment. Fundamental contrasts remain over enduring self, reality of the world, God and the exact goal of liberation; no single “oriental” relation is adequate.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares traditions on common axes and preserves their doctrinal differences.

**Argument map:** infinite being not bounded by world → transcends creation → sustaining presence in every being → omnipresence non-spatial → panentheistic balance.

### Model answer

Divine **transcendence** means that God is not one finite item within the universe and is not exhausted by it. **Infinitude** supports this: an unlimited source of being cannot be bounded by space, time or created categories.

Divine **immanence** means that God is intimately present to and active within creation. **Omnipresence** expresses the same relation universally: no creature or place is outside God's sustaining knowledge and power. **Assessment:** the attributes are mutually interpretive, not contradictory, provided “presence” means sustaining relation and “beyond” means ontological non-identity rather than distance.

**Why this earns marks:** It shows the logical connections and answers both collapse and remoteness objections.

## Q7(c). “It would be self-contradictory to say that the most perfect conceivable being lacks the attribute of existence.” Analyse.

**Syllabus unit:** Proofs for the Existence of God — Ontological  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Anselm · Gaunilo · Kant · modal ontological argument  
**Proposed slug:** `perfect-conceivable-being-existence-ontological-argument`  
**Canonical owner:** [Proofs for God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Proofs-for-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the ontological inference and test whether denying existence is logical contradiction or merely denying instantiation.

**Argument map:** greatest conceivable being → existence as greater/perfect → denial contradiction → island and predicate objections → modal reformulation and limits.

### Model answer

The statement expresses Anselm's ontological argument. God is “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.” A being existing in reality is allegedly greater than one existing only in understanding; therefore, if the greatest conceivable being lacked existence, a greater being could be conceived, which is contradictory.

Descartes similarly treats existence as inseparable from a supremely perfect being, as three angles are inseparable from a triangle.

**Objections:** Gaunilo argues that parallel reasoning could conjure a perfect island, suggesting illicit movement from concept to reality. Kant's deeper objection is that existence is not a real predicate adding a perfection to a concept; one hundred real thalers contain no more conceptual properties than one hundred possible thalers. Denying that anything instantiates the divine concept need not contradict the concept itself.

Modal versions claim that if a maximally great being is possible, it exists necessarily in every possible world. But the controversial premise is precisely the possibility of such a being; rival necessary-being concepts may be constructed.

**Assessment:** the argument reveals that God, if existent as maximally perfect, cannot exist contingently. It does not establish actuality from definition alone unless necessary existence is shown to be genuinely possible and uniquely coherent.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs the contradiction claim and identifies exactly where the inference remains disputed.

**Argument map:** omnipotence/goodness/evil tension → free-will defence → soul-making → natural-law order → skeptical theism → horrendous-evil residue.

### Model answer

The problem alleges tension among three claims: God is omnipotent, God is perfectly good, and evil exists. A theist first denies that omnipotence entails doing the logically impossible or abolishing every evil regardless of greater goods and creaturely freedom.

For **moral evil**, Augustine and Plantinga's free-will defence argues that significant freedom includes the possibility of wrongdoing. A world of programmed innocence may lack love and responsibility. Plantinga aims modestly to show logical compatibility, not that every evil is explained.

For **natural evil**, Hick's soul-making theodicy holds that courage, compassion and mature agency require a stable, challenging environment. Regular natural laws also make action, learning and responsibility possible; continual miraculous prevention would destroy predictable order. Some theists add that finite creatures cannot expect to discern all divine reasons—skeptical theism.

**Objections:** freedom does not explain earthquakes or childhood disease; soul-making cannot justify suffering that destroys rather than develops persons; unknown reasons can weaken confidence in moral reasoning. The quantity and distribution of apparently gratuitous suffering make the evidential problem stronger than the strict logical problem.  
**Replies:** communities can exercise solidarity amid natural vulnerability, eschatological fulfilment may defeat suffering, and God may participate compassionately rather than remain a remote planner. These responses, however, must not excuse preventable evil.

Indian karma theories explain suffering through moral causation across lives, but risk victim-blaming and do not by themselves vindicate an omnipotent creator.

**Assessment:** the theist can rebut a formal contradiction through freedom and logically necessary conditions of agency. Rampant horrendous evil remains a grave evidential and existential challenge; honest theism should combine limited theodicy, protest and practical resistance to suffering.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers as a theist while acknowledging the unresolved evidential force of both kinds of evil.

## Q8(b). Among the different views of religious language, which one is more satisfactory and why?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Language](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-language.md) → analogy · symbol · verification · language-game · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Compare, choose and justify  
**Syllabus unit:** Religious Language — Analogical and Symbolic; Cognitivist and Non-cognitive  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Ayer · Flew · Aquinas · Tillich · Wittgenstein · critical realism  
**Proposed slug:** `most-satisfactory-view-religious-language`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Language](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Language.md)

**Demand decoding:** Survey principal views, select one and state criteria for superiority rather than merely listing theories.

**Argument map:** verification/falsification challenge → symbol and language-game preserve use → analogy preserves truth and transcendence → controlled analogical realism.

### Model answer

Logical positivism treats unverifiable God-talk as cognitively meaningless; Flew asks what could count against a religious assertion. These views rightly demand content but impose an overly narrow model of empirical verification. Wittgensteinian language-games explain religious use within a form of life, yet risk insulating claims from external criticism. Tillich's symbols participate in and disclose ultimate reality, but symbolism can become too indeterminate.

Aquinas's **analogical predication** is more satisfactory. Terms such as “good” and “wise” are neither univocal—God is not one finite good agent—nor equivocal—which would make discourse empty. They apply analogically because creaturely perfections depend on and partially resemble their source.

**Objection:** analogy does not specify enough literal content to test truth and may disguise anthropomorphism.  
**Reply:** its content is controlled by causal dependence, coherence, negation of creaturely limits and the practical consequences of the claim; it remains corrigible rather than immune.

Analogical language can also incorporate metaphor and symbol without reducing all religion to attitude. It preserves cognitive aspiration, divine transcendence and critical debate.

**Assessment:** a critical analogical realism is most satisfactory: religious statements genuinely claim truth, but their finite concepts apply to ultimate reality in qualified, non-literal ways.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares alternatives, announces a criterion and defends a clear choice.

## Q8(c). “The natural world is as complexly and manifestly designed as a watch.” Evaluate.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Proofs for God](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/proofs-for-god.md) → teleological argument · Paley · design · evolution · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Directive: Evaluate  
**Syllabus unit:** Proofs for the Existence of God — Teleological  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Paley · Hume · Darwin · fine-tuning · analogy  
**Proposed slug:** `natural-world-watch-design-argument`  
**Canonical owner:** [Proofs for God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Proofs-for-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Paley's watch analogy, test analogy and inference, and consider evolutionary and fine-tuning reformulations.

**Argument map:** organised complexity/function → watchmaker analogy → weak analogy/many designers/evil → evolution → probabilistic fine-tuning residual.

### Model answer

Paley argues that a watch's coordinated parts, adjusted to a purpose, warrant inference to an intelligent watchmaker. Organisms display still greater functional complexity; therefore the natural world allegedly points to a divine designer.

The argument has intuitive abductive force: purposive organisation commonly results from intelligence. Yet Hume's criticisms are substantial. The world is not sufficiently like an artefact; analogy could imply many designers, an apprentice designer or a finite cause rather than the perfect God of theism. Disorder, predation and apparently poor design also qualify the inference.

Darwin supplies a powerful non-intentional explanation of biological adaptation through variation and natural selection. Complexity can accumulate without foresight, so Paley's biological premise no longer establishes a designer.

**Reply:** contemporary defenders shift to cosmic fine-tuning: life-permitting constants appear delicately bounded, and design competes with chance or a multiverse.  
**Objection:** probability assignments are uncertain, observer selection matters, and a divine mind may be no simpler explanatory terminus.

**Assessment:** the watch analogy does not prove God and is undermined as an account of biological complexity. At most, order and fine-tuning provide a defeasible cumulative consideration whose force depends on wider metaphysical evidence; they do not uniquely identify an omnipotent, benevolent creator.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates the classical analogy, Darwinian challenge and contemporary reformulation.
