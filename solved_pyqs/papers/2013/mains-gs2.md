## Q1. The role of individual MPs (Members of Parliament) has diminished over the years and as a result healthy constructive debates on policy issues are not usually witnessed. How far can this be attributed to the anti-defection law which was legislated but with a different intention ?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Anti-defection Law](../../topics/polity/anti-defection-law.md) → Legislative deliberation and party discipline · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Executive accountability, anti-defection and law-making quality](../../../live_sessions/Polity/17-Parliament/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q01-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Assess extent  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Anti-defection law  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Political parties and legislative accountability  
**Canonical owners:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Parliament.md) · [Political Parties](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Political-Parties.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `anti-defection-law-mp-deliberation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the law's original purpose, test its causal role in weakening MPs, identify other causes and give a calibrated reform.

**Compact framework:** Original intent → chilling effect → other causes → narrower whip and fair adjudication.

### Model answer

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the **52nd Amendment (1985)**, sought to stop opportunistic floor-crossing and governmental instability. It disqualifies a member who voluntarily gives up party membership or votes contrary to a party direction without condonation.

It has diminished the individual MP's role because a whip can convert almost every vote into a test of loyalty. Members therefore hesitate to dissent, amend legislation or represent constituency interests. The Speaker's power to decide disqualification can also invite partisan delay. The **91st Amendment (2003)** removed the split exception, further tightening discipline; *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) retained judicial review but did not cure day-to-day conformity.

Yet the law is not the sole cause. Centralised candidate selection, weak committee scrutiny, disruptions, limited research support, criminalisation and executive dominance of the legislative timetable also reduce deliberation. Party cohesion is moreover necessary on confidence and supply.

**Conclusion:** The law is a major, but partial, cause. Whips should be legally confined to confidence motions, money bills and measures central to the government's survival; internal party democracy, stronger committees and time-bound, impartial disqualification decisions should restore responsible dissent without reviving defections.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “how far,” uses the controlling amendments and judgment, separates party stability from excessive whipping, and proposes targeted reform.

## Q2. Discuss Section 66A of IT Act, with reference to its alleged violation of Article 19 of the Constitution.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Online speech and reasonable restrictions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Article 19: six freedoms and six restriction structures](../../../live_sessions/Polity/07-Fundamental-Rights/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q02-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Freedom of speech  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Digital regulation  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `section-66a-online-speech-article-19`

**Demand decoding:** State what Section 66A criminalised and assess its compatibility with Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(2) as the law stood in 2013.

**Compact framework:** Provision → constitutional defects → State rationale → 2013-compliant safeguards/verdict.

### Model answer

Section **66A** of the Information Technology Act, 2000, inserted in 2008, punished sending through a communication service information that was “grossly offensive,” “menacing,” or knowingly false and sent persistently to cause annoyance, inconvenience, insult or ill will. It authorised up to three years' imprisonment.

Its breadth threatened Article **19(1)(a)** because:

- undefined expressions such as “annoyance” and “grossly offensive” gave police excessive discretion;
- the chilling effect extended to legitimate criticism, satire and political discussion;
- unlike Article **19(2)**, the provision did not require a proximate link with public order, defamation, incitement or another enumerated ground;
- identical online and offline expression could attract unequal consequences, raising Article 14 concerns.

The State could legitimately regulate cyberstalking, threats, spam and incitement because online speech is rapid, persistent and borderless. However, convenience of enforcement cannot enlarge Article 19(2). Existing penal provisions and narrowly drafted cyber-offences can address concrete harm.

By 2013, arrests over social-media posts had exposed misuse, prompting an executive advisory requiring senior-police approval; this reduced, but did not remove, statutory vagueness.

**Conclusion:** Section 66A's legitimate aim could not cure its overbreadth. Constitutional regulation required precise offences, harm thresholds and procedural safeguards, not criminalisation of subjective annoyance.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates the provision at the 2013 temporal boundary without importing the later *Shreya Singhal* holding.

## Q3. Recent directives from Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas are perceived by the ‘Nagas’ as a threat to override the exceptional status enjoyed by the State. Discuss in light of Article 371A of the Indian Constitution.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Special Provisions and North-East Governance](../../topics/polity/special-provisions-and-north-east-governance.md) → Article 371A and natural resources · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Article 371A-Nagaland: Consent Shield, Land, Custom, Governor and Tuensang Provisions](../../../live_sessions/Polity/22-Special-Provisions/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q03-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Asymmetric federalism → Nagaland  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Centre–State relations and natural resources  
**Canonical owners:** [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Federal-System.md) · [Centre–State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Centre-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `article-371a-nagaland-petroleum-resources`

**Demand decoding:** Reconcile Nagaland's special constitutional protection over land/resources with Union competence over oil regulation.

**Compact framework:** Article 371A shield → competing Union claim → constitutional reconciliation → negotiated solution.

### Model answer

Article **371A(1)(a)** provides that no Act of Parliament concerning Naga religious or social practices, customary law and procedure, administration of civil and criminal justice according to customary law, or **ownership and transfer of land and its resources** applies to Nagaland unless its Legislative Assembly so resolves.

Nagaland viewed Union objections to its 2012 petroleum regulations as intruding upon a protected resource domain. Its claim draws strength from the provision's express language.

The Union's counter-claim rests on Entry **53 of the Union List**, which covers regulation and development of oilfields and mineral-oil resources, and on national concerns of energy security, safety, environment and inter-State commerce. Article 371A, however, is a specific constitutional exception; ordinary central legislation cannot simply render it ineffective. Nor does it automatically settle every regulatory issue, because ownership, extraction regulation, environmental clearance and revenue may involve distinct constitutional fields.

A judicially untested maximalism by either side would deepen distrust. Parliament's law should apply only through the constitutional route where Article 371A is attracted, while national technical and environmental standards can be secured through consultation.

**Conclusion:** Cooperative federalism requires a negotiated Centre–State framework, Assembly participation, customary-community consent, transparent benefit sharing and, if necessary, authoritative constitutional adjudication.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact protected subject, the competing Union entry and a constitutionally grounded reconciliation.

## Q4. ‘The Supreme Court of India keeps a check on arbitrary power of the Parliament in amending the Constitution.’ Discuss critically.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial review of constitutional amendments · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [The illustrative, case-attributed basic structure](../../../live_sessions/Polity/10-Amendment-and-Basic-Structure/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q04-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss critically  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional amendment → Basic structure review  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Separation of powers  
**Canonical owners:** [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Amendment-and-Basic-Structure.md) · [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `supreme-court-review-amending-power`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the doctrinal check, demonstrate its operation, and evaluate its democratic legitimacy and limits.

**Compact framework:** Article 368 → doctrinal evolution → democratic value → counter-majoritarian risk → restrained verdict.

### Model answer

Article **368** gives Parliament constituent power, but the Supreme Court has held that it is not unlimited. In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the Court evolved the **basic structure doctrine**: any constitutional provision may be amended, but the Constitution's foundational identity cannot be destroyed.

The check has operated through:

- *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, protecting free and fair elections and judicial review;
- *Minerva Mills*, invalidating unlimited amending power and restoring harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles;
- *Waman Rao* and *I.R. Coelho*, subjecting post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions to basic-structure review.

This review prevents a temporary parliamentary majority from abolishing democracy, federalism, secularism, judicial review or constitutional supremacy. It preserves the distinction between amendment and constitutional replacement.

Critically, “basic structure” is not exhaustively defined, allowing judges significant discretion. Judicial supremacy may obstruct democratically supported reform, and close decisions can appear subjective. Yet Parliament controls initiation and ratification, while the Court intervenes only after enactment; ordinary policy choices remain outside the doctrine.

**Conclusion:** The Court is a necessary constitutional sentinel, not a rival constituent assembly. Its legitimacy depends on reasoned, exceptional use focused on structural destruction rather than disagreement with the wisdom of an amendment.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines doctrinal history, named applications, democratic justification and a genuine critique.

## Q5. Many State Governments further bifurcate geographical administrative areas like Districts and Talukas for better governance. In light of the above, can it also be justified that more number of smaller States would bring in effective governance at State level ? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Federal System](../../topics/polity/federal-system.md) → State reorganisation and governance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Article 2 versus Article 3: Admission versus Reorganisation](../../../live_sessions/Polity/05-Union-and-Territory/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q05-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Creation of smaller States  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Administrative decentralisation  
**Canonical owner:** [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Federal-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `smaller-states-effective-governance`

**Demand decoding:** Test whether district-level logic can be extended to State creation, balancing administrative gains against viability and federal costs.

**Compact framework:** Potential gains → limitations → criteria → qualified conclusion.

### Model answer

Smaller administrative units reduce distance between citizens and authority; this logic partly supports smaller States. Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Uttarakhand showed that reorganisation can give neglected regions political voice, sharper policy attention and capitals closer to remote populations.

Possible gains include:

- greater administrative accessibility and monitoring;
- policies better adapted to local ecology, culture and livelihood;
- reduced intra-State regional imbalance;
- stronger representation of previously peripheral communities;
- manageable coordination where a very large State has uneven capacity.

However, a State is not merely a larger district. It needs fiscal viability, institutions, a civil service, infrastructure and settlement of assets, debt, water, power and capital. Smallness may create dependence on Union transfers, elite capture or new inter-State disputes. Goa can govern effectively while some small north-eastern States face capacity constraints; conversely, large States can improve through empowered districts and local bodies.

Article **3** permits Parliament to reorganise States after seeking the affected legislature's views, but political demand should not substitute for objective assessment.

**Conclusion:** Smaller States can improve governance where geography, identity, neglect and administrative overload converge, but size is neither necessary nor sufficient. An independent reorganisation commission should assess viability, consent, resource sharing and alternatives such as regional councils and genuine local decentralisation.

**Why this earns marks:** It challenges the simple district–State analogy and supplies defensible decision criteria.

## Q6. Constitutional mechanisms to resolve the inter-state water disputes have failed to address and solve the problems. Is the failure due to structural or process inadequacy or both ? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre–State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → Inter-State river-water disputes · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Inter-State River-Water Disputes](../../../live_sessions/Polity/13-Centre-State-and-Inter-State-Relations/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q06-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Diagnose; Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Centre–State relations → Water disputes  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Tribunal design and implementation  
**Canonical owner:** [Centre–State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Centre-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `inter-state-water-disputes-structural-process-failures`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish defects in constitutional/statutory architecture from delay and non-compliance in actual procedure.

**Compact framework:** Legal design → structural gaps → process failures → integrated reform.

### Model answer

Article **262** permits Parliament to provide for adjudication of inter-State water disputes and exclude court jurisdiction. Parliament enacted the **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956**; Entry 56 also enables Union regulation of inter-State rivers in the public interest.

Failure is both structural and procedural.

**Structural inadequacy:** tribunals are constituted ad hoc, lack a permanent technical secretariat and have limited machinery to enforce awards. The River Boards Act, 1956 has not produced effective basin institutions. Fragmented control among States encourages competitive extraction, while adjudication allocates shares without continuously managing ecology, groundwater, drought or climate variability.

**Process inadequacy:** the Union often delays constituting tribunals; pleadings, data disputes and vacancies prolong decisions. States politicise compliance, seek repeated clarification and sometimes resist releases during distress years. Non-transparent hydrological data and weak monitoring erode trust. Litigation continues around implementation despite jurisdictional exclusion.

Reform requires a standing tribunal with specialist benches, statutory timelines, a common verified data system and automatic publication of awards. Permanent river-basin organisations should plan conservation, distress-sharing and reservoir operations, supported by an enforceable implementation authority and Inter-State Council mediation.

**Conclusion:** Better procedure alone cannot cure a dispute-only architecture; durable settlement needs basin governance plus timely adjudication and compliance.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “both” analytically and separates institutional design from operational failure.

## Q7. Discuss the recommendations of the 13th Finance Commission which have been a departure from the previous commissions for strengthening the local government finances.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Finance Commission](../../topics/polity/finance-commission.md) → Local-body grants · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Grants Architecture and Local Government Finances](../../../live_sessions/Polity/29-Finance-Commission/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q07-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Finance Commission → Local-government finance  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Fiscal decentralisation  
**Canonical owners:** [Finance Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Finance-Commission.md) · [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/12_Local-Governance-and-Service-Delivery.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `thirteenth-finance-commission-local-finances`

**Demand decoding:** Identify innovations—not merely grants—in the Thirteenth Finance Commission's local-body framework.

**Compact framework:** Predictable pool-linked grants → performance incentives → institutional conditions → significance.

### Model answer

The Thirteenth Finance Commission treated local bodies as a more integral part of fiscal federalism rather than recipients of an ad hoc lump sum.

Its important departures were:

- a **formula-linked basic grant** equal to 1.5% of the previous year's divisible pool, plus a performance grant of 0.5% in 2011–12 and 1% thereafter, improving predictability and buoyancy;
- separate treatment of general and special areas, including Fifth and Sixth Schedule regions;
- performance grants conditional on institutional reform rather than mere expenditure;
- prescribed standards for local-body accounts, audit and timely placement of audit reports;
- incentives for effective State Finance Commissions and action on their recommendations;
- independent local-body ombudsmen, electronic transfer of grants and transparent allocation;
- improvement of property-tax administration and service standards.

Earlier commissions largely recommended fixed amounts; the Thirteenth linked support to the tax base and measurable governance conditions. However, grants still flowed through States, and weak local revenue powers, delayed State Finance Commissions and limited capacity could blunt the design.

**Conclusion:** Its central innovation was combining predictable entitlement with accountable performance. Sustainable local finance additionally requires activity mapping, own-source taxation and timely State devolution.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the distinctive pool-linked and conditional architecture while noting its implementation limits.

## Q8. The product diversification of financial institutions and insurance companies, resulting in overlapping of products and services strengthens the case for the merger of the two regulatory agencies, namely SEBI and IRDA. Justify.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Regulatory Governance and Independent Regulators](../../topics/governance/regulatory-governance-and-independent-regulators.md) → Financial-sector regulatory convergence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Independence is functional, not sovereign: Sectoral Regulators](../../../live_sessions/Governance/11-Regulatory-Governance-and-Independent-Regulators/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q08-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Justify  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Regulators → SEBI and IRDA  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Financial-sector regulation  
**Canonical owner:** [Regulatory Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/11_Regulatory-Governance-and-Independent-Regulators.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `sebi-irda-regulatory-merger-convergence`

**Demand decoding:** Build the case for unified regulation from product overlap, while acknowledging sector-specific risks.

**Compact framework:** Convergence problem → benefits of merger → limits → calibrated institutional design.

### Model answer

Traditional boundaries have blurred: insurers sell unit-linked investment products, banks distribute insurance, and market intermediaries bundle savings, risk and investment. The 2010 **ULIP jurisdiction dispute** between SEBI and IRDA illustrated how entity-based regulation can create uncertainty when one product performs several functions.

A merged regulator could:

- apply the principle “same activity, same risk, same regulation”;
- eliminate regulatory arbitrage and conflicting disclosure norms;
- give consumers a single grievance and compensation architecture;
- supervise conglomerates and systemic risk on a consolidated basis;
- reduce duplication, information gaps and turf disputes;
- support product neutrality and competition.

The case is therefore strong for converged conduct regulation. Yet complete institutional merger is not automatically optimal. Insurance involves actuarial solvency, long-duration liabilities and policyholder protection, whereas securities regulation focuses on market integrity, issuers and intermediaries. A very large regulator may concentrate power and lose specialist expertise. The Financial Stability and Development Council can coordinate systemic issues, but coordination without binding rules may remain weak.

**Conclusion:** Product overlap justifies harmonised law, common consumer protection and consolidated supervision; organisational merger should follow only if specialist insurance and market divisions retain autonomy, expertise and accountability. Functional integration matters more than merely combining offices.

**Why this earns marks:** It justifies convergence with a contemporaneous dispute while qualifying the institutional form.

## Q9. The concept of Mid Day Meal (MDM) scheme is almost a century old in India with early beginnings in Madras Presidency in pre-independent India. The scheme has again been given impetus in most states in the last two decades. Critically examine its twin objectives, latest mandates and success.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → School meals and nutrition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Two paths to better nutrition: Mid-Day Meal and Food Security](../../../live_sessions/Social-Justice/02-Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q09-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q9 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Critically examine  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Nutrition and education → Mid-Day Meal  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → School service delivery  
**Canonical owners:** [Poverty, Hunger and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md) · [Education and Human Resource Development](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/04_Education-and-Human-Resource-Development.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `mid-day-meal-objectives-mandates-performance`

**Demand decoding:** Examine education and nutrition objectives, state the 2013 mandate, and balance achievements against delivery failures.

**Compact framework:** Twin goals → legal/nutritional mandate → achievements → deficits → reform.

### Model answer

The Mid-Day Meal Scheme pursues twin objectives: improve children's nutritional status and increase enrolment, attendance and retention, especially among disadvantaged groups. Eating together can also weaken caste and gender barriers.

Following the Supreme Court's 2001 right-to-food directions, cooked meals replaced dry rations. The national programme covered primary and upper-primary pupils in government and aided schools. Norms aimed at about **450 calories and 12 grams protein** for primary children and **700 calories and 20 grams protein** for upper-primary children. Section 5 of the **National Food Security Act, 2013** converted the meal into a statutory entitlement for children up to class VIII, subject to prescribed standards.

The scheme increased school participation, reduced classroom hunger and transferred income in kind to poor households. Its scale and daily visibility made it an important social-policy platform.

However, irregular supplies, inadequate kitchens, low cook-cum-helper remuneration, caste discrimination and weak hygiene impaired quality. The July 2013 Bihar poisoning tragedy showed failures in procurement, storage and supervision. Calorie provision alone cannot address anaemia, micronutrient deficiency or poor sanitation.

**Conclusion:** MDM is successful as an access-and-hunger intervention but uneven as a nutrition programme. Safe kitchens, local menus, testing, social audit, grievance redress and health convergence are essential.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers both objectives, the latest 2013 legal mandate, measured success and implementation failure.

## Q10. Pressure group politics is sometimes seen as the informal face of politics. With regards to the above, assess the structure and functioning of pressure groups in India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Pressure Groups](../../topics/polity/pressure-groups.md) → Structure and functioning · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [The informal face of politics: Pressure Groups in India](../../../live_sessions/Polity/44-Pressure-Groups/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q10-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Assess  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil society → Pressure groups  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Representation and public policy  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pressure-groups-structure-function-india`

**Demand decoding:** Classify Indian pressure groups, explain their operating methods and judge their democratic contribution and distortions.

**Compact framework:** Structure → methods → democratic gains → asymmetries → verdict.

### Model answer

Pressure groups seek to influence public policy without directly seeking governmental office; hence they are the “informal face” connecting society with formal institutions.

India's structure is plural but uneven:

- business bodies such as FICCI and CII;
- trade unions linked to political parties;
- farmers' organisations and caste/community associations;
- professional bodies, student groups and employees' associations;
- movements on environment, rights, corruption or displacement;
- NGOs and informal mass coalitions.

They function through lobbying ministers and officials, appearing before committees, research and media campaigns, petitions, litigation, demonstrations, strikes and electoral endorsements. Federal politics provides multiple access points; courts and consultations offer additional channels.

Their contribution lies in supplying expertise, aggregating interests, exposing administrative failures and enabling marginal voices to shape agendas. Movements around the Right to Information and forest rights show such democratic value.

Yet access is unequal. Organised capital, public employees and dominant social groups possess more money and continuity than dispersed consumers or informal workers. Opaque lobbying, coercive protest, party capture and narrow rent-seeking can displace the public interest; identity groups may deepen polarisation.

**Conclusion:** Pressure groups enrich representative democracy when participation and lobbying are transparent. Disclosure rules, consultative registers, internal democracy and balanced committee hearings can make influence accountable.

**Why this earns marks:** It assesses both organisational diversity and actual channels of influence rather than merely listing groups.

## Q11. The legitimacy and accountability of Self Help Groups (SHGs) and their patrons, the micro-finance outfits, need systematic assessment and scrutiny for the sustained success of the concept. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → SHG and microfinance accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Self-Help Groups: collective agency, social capital and livelihood linkages](../../../live_sessions/Governance/04-NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q11-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Governance → SHGs → Accountability  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Financial inclusion and microfinance  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `shg-microfinance-legitimacy-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why scrutiny is necessary without undermining community ownership or financial inclusion.

**Compact framework:** Value → legitimacy risks → accountability architecture → balanced conclusion.

### Model answer

SHGs pool savings, extend small loans and create collective agency, especially for rural women. The SHG–Bank Linkage Programme has widened formal finance and supported livelihoods. Their legitimacy rests on voluntary membership, participatory decisions and benefits controlled by members.

Scrutiny is necessary because legitimacy can erode through:

- domination by local elites or sponsoring NGOs;
- fictitious groups, weak books and diversion of revolving funds;
- unequal voice within groups and coercive peer pressure;
- multiple lending, opaque pricing and over-indebtedness;
- aggressive recovery by commercial microfinance institutions (MFIs);
- conflicts where the promoter also selects borrowers, supplies products and evaluates success.

The 2010 Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis exposed these risks. The **Malegam Committee** and RBI's 2011 NBFC-MFI framework responded through borrower-income and indebtedness limits, margin and interest controls, fair-practice requirements and restrictions on multiple lending.

Sustainable accountability should combine audited but simple accounts, public disclosure of effective interest, credit-information sharing, member education, social audit and accessible grievance redress. Federations can provide scale, while banks and NABARD should assess outcomes rather than loan volumes alone. Regulation must distinguish member-owned SHGs from profit-driven MFIs.

**Conclusion:** Proportionate scrutiny protects, rather than weakens, the model by preserving member autonomy while disciplining patrons and lenders.

**Why this earns marks:** It links legitimacy to internal democracy and accountability to the contemporaneous microfinance regulatory response.

## Q12. The Central Government frequently complains on the poor performance of the State Governments in eradicating suffering of the vulnerable sections of the society. **Primary route:** International Relations → [India and the Neighbourhood](../../topics/international-relations/india-and-the-neighbourhood.md) → India–Sri Lanka relations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Sri Lanka: first response, recovery and maritime sensitivity](../../../live_sessions/International-Relations/02-India-and-the-Neighbourhood/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q23-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q23 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Sri Lanka → Domestic determinants  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Federal coalitions and foreign policy  
**Canonical owner:** [India and the Neighbourhood](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/02_India-and-the-Neighbourhood.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-sri-lanka-domestic-foreign-policy`

**Demand decoding:** Show how domestic politics in both countries constrain and shape bilateral choices, not merely list bilateral issues.

**Compact framework:** Indian factors → Sri Lankan factors → policy effects → balancing approach.

### Model answer

India–Sri Lanka relations show that foreign policy is mediated by domestic identity, electoral federalism and regime legitimacy.

In India, Tamil Nadu parties press the Union on accountability for civilian suffering, devolution under the **Thirteenth Amendment**, rehabilitation and fishermen's arrests. Coalition dependence amplified this influence: India voted for UN Human Rights Council resolutions on Sri Lanka in 2012 and 2013, while high-level engagement faced political constraints. Fishermen crossing the maritime boundary create recurring pressure on both governments.

In Sri Lanka, Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism limits provincial autonomy and encourages resistance to perceived Indian interference. Tamil parties demand meaningful devolution and land, police and language rights. The Rajapaksa government's post-war legitimacy rested on military victory, narrowing room for reconciliation. Domestic politics also affected Indian projects.

These pressures can push Colombo towards China and Pakistan for diplomatic and infrastructure support, creating Indian strategic concern. Conversely, excessive strategic indulgence by India risks alienating Tamil citizens and undermining its democratic values.

**Conclusion:** New Delhi must pursue principled pragmatism: support Sri Lanka's unity and economic recovery while pressing through dialogue for reconciliation, provincial elections and constitutional devolution; establish humane fisheries arrangements; and maintain Union–Tamil Nadu consultation so domestic legitimacy strengthens rather than paralyses policy.

**Why this earns marks:** It demonstrates two-way domestic causation and links it to concrete foreign-policy outcomes current in 2013.

## Q24. What is meant by Gujral doctrine ? Does it have any relevance today ? Discuss.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [India and the Neighbourhood](../../topics/international-relations/india-and-the-neighbourhood.md) → Gujral Doctrine · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Neighbourhood First and the Gujral Doctrine](../../../live_sessions/International-Relations/02-India-and-the-Neighbourhood/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q24-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q24 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain; Assess; Discuss  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Neighbourhood policy → Gujral Doctrine  
**Secondary classification:** International Relations → Regional cooperation  
**Canonical owners:** [Foreign Policy Foundations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/01_Foreign-Policy-Foundations-and-Strategic-Autonomy.md) · [India and the Neighbourhood](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/02_India-and-the-Neighbourhood.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `gujral-doctrine-contemporary-relevance`

**Demand decoding:** State the doctrine's principles accurately and judge its continuing utility subject to reciprocity, security and capacity.

**Compact framework:** Five principles → relevance → limitations → updated application.

### Model answer

The Gujral Doctrine, articulated in the 1990s, sought trust-based relations with India's neighbours through five principles:

1. India would not demand reciprocity from smaller neighbours—Bangladesh, Bhutan, Maldives, Nepal and Sri Lanka—but would accommodate them in good faith.  
2. No South Asian country should permit its territory to be used against another.  
3. States should not interfere in one another's internal affairs.  
4. They should respect territorial integrity and sovereignty.  
5. Disputes should be settled peacefully through bilateral negotiations.

Its relevance endures because India's size creates asymmetric fears. Unilateral concessions in trade, transit, disaster relief or development can build confidence, improve regional connectivity and reduce space for hostile external influence. The Ganga water agreement with Bangladesh illustrated accommodation's possibilities.

However, non-reciprocity cannot mean indifference to terrorism, illegal trafficking or treaty non-compliance. Domestic instability in neighbouring States, anti-India politics and growing external-power involvement may prevent goodwill from producing reciprocity later. Indian promises also lose credibility when projects are delayed.

**Conclusion:** The doctrine remains a valuable temperament, not an inflexible rule. India should practise generous asymmetry in public goods and market access, while requiring the reciprocal minimum of sovereign respect, security cooperation and implementation. Consultation and regional institutions should supplement bilateralism.

**Why this earns marks:** It states all five principles and offers a qualified contemporary verdict rather than nostalgia.

## Q25. The World Bank and the IMF, collectively known as the Bretton Woods Institutions, are the two inter-governmental pillars supporting the structure of the world’s economic and financial order. Superficially, the World Bank and the IMF exhibit many common characteristics, yet their role, functions and mandate are distinctly different. Elucidate.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [UN and International Institutions](../../topics/international-relations/un-and-international-institutions-global-governance.md) → Bretton Woods institutions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
**Learning session:** [Bretton Woods reform concerns voice as well as money](../../../live_sessions/International-Relations/12-UN-and-International-Institutions-Global-Governance/Learning-Session-Live-Edition.md#2013-gs-ii-q25-learning)

**Metadata:** 2013 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q25 · 10 marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Elucidate  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → International economic institutions → IMF and World Bank  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Development finance and balance-of-payments support  
**Canonical owners:** [UN and International Institutions](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/12_UN-and-International-Institutions-Global-Governance.md) · [Globalisation and External Policy Effects](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/11_Globalisation-Trade-Agreements-and-External-Policy-Effects.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `imf-world-bank-role-function-mandate`

**Demand decoding:** Compare common institutional features with distinct purposes, instruments, clients and time horizons.

**Compact framework:** Common origin/governance → IMF stabilisation → Bank development → critical distinction.

### Model answer

Both institutions emerged from the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, are owned by member governments, headquartered in Washington, use weighted voting and provide finance with policy advice. Their mandates, however, address different failures.

| Dimension | IMF | World Bank Group |
|---|---|---|
| Core purpose | International monetary stability and orderly exchange arrangements | Long-term development and poverty reduction |
| Main problem | Balance-of-payments and macroeconomic crisis | Infrastructure, human development and institutional capacity gaps |
| Finance | Generally short- to medium-term support from pooled quotas and borrowing | Longer-term project, programme and policy finance through IBRD/IDA and affiliated institutions |
| Clients | Member governments facing external-financing need | Governments; IFC and MIGA also support private-sector investment |
| Instruments | Surveillance, Article IV consultations, conditional lending, technical assistance, SDR allocation | Loans, credits, grants, guarantees, research and sectoral expertise |
| Policy focus | Fiscal, monetary, exchange-rate and financial stability | Health, education, agriculture, infrastructure, governance and social protection |

The distinction is not absolute: IMF conditionality affects development, while Bank policy lending addresses macroeconomic reform. Both face criticism over creditor influence, uniform prescriptions and voting imbalance.

**Conclusion:** The IMF is primarily a guardian of monetary and external stability; the Bank is principally a development financier and knowledge institution. Effective coordination should avoid duplicated or contradictory conditionality.

**Why this earns marks:** It elucidates differences through a direct comparative matrix while acknowledging overlap and governance concerns.
