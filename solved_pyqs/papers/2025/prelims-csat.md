## Q5. Factor-sum enumeration
**Primary route:** CSAT → [Number Systems and Number Sense](../../topics/csat/number-systems-and-number-sense.md) → Factor-sum enumeration · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

A natural number N is such that it can be expressed as N = p + q + r , where p, q and r are distinct factors of N. How many numbers below 50 have this property?

**Options:**
- **(a)** 6
- **(b)** 7
- **(c)** 8
- **(d)** 9

**Metadata:** Family 02 · Number Systems and Number Sense → Factor-sum enumeration · Proposed slug: `factor-sum-enumeration-2025-q05`  
**Canonical owner:** `CSAT/basic/02_Number-Systems-and-Number-Sense.md`

**FINAL answer: (c).**

**Full reasoning:** To solve this, we are looking for numbers where N is the sum of three of its distinct factors. Let the factors be p, q, and r. If we divide both sides of the equation N = p + q + r by N, we get 1 = p/N + q/N + r/N.

Since p, q, and r are factors of N, the fractions p/N, q/N, and r/N must be unit fractions like 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, etc. We need to find three distinct unit fractions that add up to 1. The only set of three distinct unit fractions that sum to 1 is 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6.

This means that for N to satisfy the condition, its three factors must be N/2, N/3, and N/6. For these to be integers, N must be a multiple of the least common multiple of 2, 3, and 6, which is 6.

Therefore, any number that is a multiple of 6 will satisfy this property because N/2 + N/3 + N/6 = (3N + 2N + N) / 6 = 6N / 6 = N.

The multiples of 6 below 50 are 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, and 48. Counting these, we find there are exactly 8 such numbers. Thus, the correct option is C.

The only possible pair for (q, r) where q is at least 3 is (3, 3).

If p is greater than 3, the sum 1/p + 1/q + 1/r will always be less than 1.

The possible values for the sum (p + q + r) are 11, 10, and 9. This gives a total of three distinct possible values. Therefore, the correct option is C.

## Q10. Successive-percentage cycle
**Primary route:** CSAT → [Arithmetic and Commercial Mathematics](../../topics/csat/arithmetic-and-commercial-math.md) → Successive-percentage cycle · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

The price (p) of a commodity is first increased by k% ; then decreased by k% ; again increased by k% ; and again decreased by k% . If the new price is q , then what is the relation between p and q ?

**Options:**
- **(a)** p(10⁴ - k²)² = q × 10⁸
- **(b)** p(10⁴ - k²)² = q × 10⁴
- **(c)** p(10⁴ - k²) = q × 10⁴
- **(d)** p(10⁴ - k²) = q × 10⁸

**Metadata:** Family 03 · Arithmetic and Commercial Math → Successive-percentage cycle · Proposed slug: `successive-percentage-cycle-2025-q10`  
**Canonical owner:** `CSAT/basic/03_Arithmetic-and-Commercial-Math.md`

**FINAL answer: (a).**

**Full reasoning:** Let the original price be p.
When a price is increased by k% and then decreased by k%, the net effect is a decrease.
The new price after one such cycle (increase by k%, then decrease by k%) is given by:
Price' = p * (1 + k/100) * (1 - k/100)
Price' = p * (1 - (k/100)^2)
Price' = p * (1 - k^2/10000)

This process is repeated twice.
First cycle: p becomes p * (1 - k^2/10000)
Second cycle: The price from the first cycle further undergoes the same transformation.
So, the new price q is:
q = [p * (1 - k^2/10000)] * (1 - k^2/10000)
q = p * (1 - k^2/10000)^2

Now, let's simplify the term inside the parenthesis:
1 - k^2/10000 = (10000 - k^2) / 10000

Substitute this back into the equation for q:
q = p * [(10000 - k^2) / 10000]^2
q = p * (10000 - k^2)^2 / (10000)^2
q = p * (10^4 - k^2)^2 / 10^8

To match the options, we rearrange the equation:
q * 10^8 = p * (10^4 - k^2)^2
p * (10^4 - k^2)^2 = q * 10^8

Analyzing the options:
A) p(10^4 - k^2)^2 = q * 10^8 - This matches our derived relation.
B) p(10^4 - k^2)^2 = q * 10^4 - Incorrect power of 10 on the right side.
C) p(10^4 - k^2) = q * 10^4 - Incorrectly missing the square on the left side.
D) p(10^4 - k^2) = q * 10^8 - Incorrectly missing the square on the left side.

The final answer is A

**Option check:** The derived value or relationship satisfies option (a); the other choices fail at least one stated numerical or logical constraint.

**Shortest safe method:** Use a convenient base, apply the change multiplicatively, and compare resulting values rather than percentages verbally.
