## Q1(a). “Corrupt practices reveal an inherent tension between particularistic and universalistic normative standards.” Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons and justification for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Crime and Punishment](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/crime-and-punishment.md) → Corruption · particularistic loyalty versus universal public norms · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(a) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Agreement with reasons  
**Syllabus unit:** Crime and Punishment — Corruption  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** role morality · impartiality · fiduciary public office · conflicts of interest  
**Proposed slug:** `corruption-particularistic-universalistic-norms`  
**Canonical owner:** [Crime and Punishment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Crime-and-Punishment.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain the two normative standards, show why their collision generates corruption, and qualify the claim by recognising systemic incentives and legitimate special duties.

**Argument map:** special loyalties → public-role impartiality → misuse of entrusted power → institutional injury → systemic objection → role-boundary reply → qualified agreement.

### Model answer

I agree, with qualification. **Particularistic norms** give special weight to kinship, caste, friendship or patronage; **universalistic norms** require officials to apply public rules impartially to similarly placed persons. Corruption arises when a person uses an entrusted office to discharge private loyalty—for example, favouring a relative in public allocation—thereby transferring costs to citizens who lack access.

The wrong is not loyalty itself but its exercise in the wrong role. Public office creates fiduciary duties, and favouritism violates equal civic standing, the rule of law and institutional trust.

**Objection:** corruption may result more from secrecy, monopoly and impunity than from moral conflict. **Reply:** structures shape opportunity, but they do not erase agency; disclosure, recusal and audit institutionalise the correct role boundary.

**Assessment:** the tension explains an important moral mechanism of corruption, but not every cause. Corruption is best understood as an ethical breach operating within a systemic ecology.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both standards, identifies the role-conflict mechanism, answers the agreement demand and avoids abolishing legitimate personal loyalty.

## Q1(b). How does gender as a social construct affect individuals’ opportunities, rights, and access to resources? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Gender Discrimination](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/gender-discrimination.md) → gender construction · structural inequality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(b) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand: Critical discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Gender Discrimination  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Beauvoir · Butler · socially assigned roles · public/private structure  
**Proposed slug:** `gender-social-construct-opportunities-rights-resources`  
**Canonical owner:** [Gender Discrimination](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Gender-Discrimination.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain causal mechanisms linking constructed gender roles to distribution. “Constructed” must not mean imaginary, freely chosen or biologically unreal.

**Argument map:** social meanings → repeated roles and sanctions → internalised expectations → institutions distribute opportunity/rights/resources → intersectional objection → anti-essentialist qualification.

### Model answer

Gender is socially constructed when institutions attach roles, capacities and status to perceived sex. As Beauvoir says, one “becomes” a woman: family, school, labour markets, law and media repeatedly produce femininity as care, dependence or limited authority.

These meanings affect **opportunity** through differentiated education, mobility and occupations; **rights** through unequal family authority, bodily autonomy or inheritance; and **resources** through unpaid care, wage gaps, property exclusion and male control of income. Repetition makes hierarchy appear natural and shapes preferences themselves. Butler’s performativity sharpens this: compelled reiteration produces the appearance of a stable gender essence.

**Objection:** constructionism may ignore bodies or dissolve “women” as a political category. **Reply:** bodily differences are real but do not dictate social ranking; categories may be used provisionally for law and mobilisation while remaining internally diverse.

**Assessment:** construction explains patterned inequality, not effortless change. Caste, class and sexuality intersect with gender, so remedies must combine equal rights with transformation of institutions and care relations.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces mechanisms across all three demanded domains and includes the central critical qualification.

## Q1(c). Is the idea of secularism necessarily related to the idea of religious pluralism? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → secularism and religious pluralism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(c) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Necessity test/discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Humanism; Secularism; Multiculturalism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** freedom of conscience · equal citizenship · principled distance · plurality versus pluralism  
**Proposed slug:** `secularism-religious-pluralism-necessary-relation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish plurality, pluralism and secularism; answer the modal word “necessarily” through counterexamples in both directions.

**Argument map:** pluralism creates governance problem → secularism protects conscience/equality → conceptual non-identity → homogeneous dissenters and plural theocracy counterexamples → close practical relation without entailment.

### Model answer

Secularism and religious pluralism are closely related but not logically inseparable. **Religious pluralism** denotes either the presence of several faiths or normative acceptance of their coexistence. **Secularism** governs coercive public power through freedom of conscience, equal citizenship and non-domination.

Pluralism strengthens the practical need for secular institutions: where faiths disagree, state establishment makes non-adherents unequal. Rajeev Bhargava’s “principled distance” therefore allows context-sensitive engagement while refusing religious supremacy.

Yet neither entails the other. A religiously homogeneous society still needs secular safeguards for dissenters, reformers and non-believers. Conversely, a religiously diverse society may remain non-secular if one faith controls sovereignty or others survive merely by permission.

**Objection:** secularism historically arose precisely to manage pluralism. **Reply:** genealogy does not establish conceptual necessity; conscience and limits on coercion have independent force.

**Assessment:** pluralism supplies a major social condition for secularism, while secularism converts coexistence into equal civic standing. Their relation is normatively intimate, not logically necessary.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly tests necessity with two countermodels and preserves the practical connection.

## Q1(d). Comment on Plato’s critique of Democracy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Forms of Government](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/forms-of-government.md) → Plato · democracy’s degeneration into tyranny · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(d) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Critical comment  
**Syllabus unit:** Forms of Government — Democracy  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** ship of state · political expertise · excessive liberty · demagoguery  
**Proposed slug:** `plato-critique-democracy`  
**Canonical owner:** [Forms of Government](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Forms-of-Government.md)

**Demand decoding:** State Plato’s connected reasons and critically comment; do not misrepresent him as merely preferring hereditary monarchy.

**Argument map:** ruling as craft → equality of political voice ignores competence → appetite and unregulated liberty → demagogue → tyranny → elitism objection → epistemic-democratic reply → qualified relevance.

### Model answer

In the *Republic*, Plato places democracy in a degeneration from timocracy and oligarchy toward tyranny. First, ruling is a craft requiring knowledge of justice; the ship-of-state analogy condemns entrusting navigation to the untrained merely because they are numerous. Second, democracy mistakes equal freedom for equal competence and permits appetite rather than reason to order the city. Third, excessive liberty weakens authority and discipline, allowing a demagogue to flatter the many, attack opponents and become a tyrant.

**Objection:** philosopher-rule risks paternalism: who certifies knowledge of the Good, and how are rulers corrected? Democracy’s dispersed experience, opposition and peaceful removal possess epistemic value Plato neglects.

**Reply:** Plato’s warning survives where opinion is manipulated and expertise is despised; democracy itself requires education and institutional restraint.

**Assessment:** his diagnosis of demagoguery is powerful, but his cure undervalues equal political agency. The defensible lesson is knowledgeable, constitutional democracy—not rule without public accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs the degeneration argument and offers a precise democratic qualification.

## Q1(e). Discuss the salient features of equality according to J. S. Mill.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → J. S. Mill · liberal equality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(e) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand: Doctrinal discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Equality  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** equal moral standing · legal equality · women’s equality · individuality  
**Proposed slug:** `js-mill-salient-features-equality`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Identify Mill’s distinctive liberal equality and its limit; do not turn the answer into general utilitarianism.

**Argument map:** equal moral consideration → equality before law/citizenship → attack on inherited privilege and women’s subjection → equal opportunity for self-development → anti-uniformity → structural objection → qualified liberal egalitarianism.

### Model answer

Mill’s equality is liberal and developmental, not compulsory sameness. First, each person has equal moral standing: utility must count everyone’s interests without privilege of birth or station. Second, equality before law and equal citizenship reject inherited hierarchy. Third, *The Subjection of Women* attacks women’s legal and social dependence as manufactured by custom, not proved by nature; equal education, occupation and civil status would release wasted capacities.

Fourth, equality must protect individuality and experiments in living. Equal status and opportunity therefore differ from uniform outcomes or one imposed life-plan. Mill also became open to reforms of property and cooperative labour, though he did not seek complete economic levelling.

**Objection:** formal opportunity cannot overcome structural class power and unequal starting points. **Reply:** Mill radicalises liberalism by exposing custom-made domination, especially gender hierarchy, but his framework needs substantive social supplementation.

**Assessment:** Mill combines equal worth and anti-subordination with diversity; his equality is strongest civically and morally, less complete economically.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the salient features, named textual evidence and a bounded critical assessment.

## Q2(a). Present a detailed account of the debate between Gandhi and Ambedkar on the issue of caste discrimination.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Caste Discrimination: Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/caste-discrimination-gandhi-and-ambedkar.md) → Gandhi and Ambedkar · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Detailed comparative account  
**Syllabus unit:** Caste Discrimination — Gandhi and Ambedkar  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** untouchability versus annihilation · *varṇa* · endogamy · representation · Poona Pact  
**Proposed slug:** `gandhi-ambedkar-caste-discrimination-debate`  
**Canonical owner:** [Caste: Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Caste-Gandhi-Ambedkar.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare diagnosis, normative basis, agency, method, religion and political representation; acknowledge Gandhi’s evolving position and adjudicate the debate.

**Argument map:** shared rejection of untouchability → Gandhi’s moral-religious reform → Ambedkar’s structural annihilation → *varṇa*/scripture/endogamy dispute → Poona Pact and autonomous voice → heart-change versus rights → qualified synthesis.

### Model answer

Gandhi and Ambedkar agreed that untouchability was intolerable, but disputed caste’s nature and the route to emancipation.

**Gandhi:** Untouchability violated truth, non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) and spiritual equality. His earlier writings distinguished an ideal, non-ranked hereditary *varṇa* from corrupt caste; later he moved further against birth barriers and supported inter-caste marriage. His methods were caste-Hindu self-purification, *satyāgraha*, constructive work, temple entry, education and reform from within Hinduism. He feared that political separation would harden social division.

**Ambedkar:** caste itself—not merely untouchability—is graded inequality. In *Castes in India* he identified endogamy as its reproductive mechanism; in *Annihilation of Caste* he attacked scriptural authority, hereditary occupation and denial of fraternity. Reform required rights, education, organisation, inter-marriage, representation, material power and possible exit from caste-sanctioning religion.

The 1932 Poona Pact dramatised their conflict. Gandhi opposed separate electorates as divisive; Ambedkar regarded autonomous political voice as protection against dominant-caste control. The compromise of reserved seats in joint electorates occurred under coercive circumstances.

**Objections and replies:** Gandhi’s heart-change appears paternalistic and structurally weak; his reply is that law without transformed conduct remains evasive. Ambedkar’s institutionalism may seem unable alone to create fraternity; his reply is that fraternity without enforceable equality leaves justice to benevolence.

**Assessment:** Ambedkar offers the stronger diagnosis and non-negotiable rights framework. Gandhi remains valuable where moral transformation supports rather than substitutes for annihilation. The defensible synthesis is Ambedkarite in structure and representation, Gandhian in means only when consistent with equal agency.

**Why this earns marks:** It presents the evolving positions, central philosophical disputes, Poona Pact and a reasoned comparative verdict.

## Q2(b). Evaluate Marxism as a Political Ideology.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → Marxism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Evaluation  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** historical materialism · exploitation · alienation · ideology · revolutionary emancipation  
**Proposed slug:** `evaluate-marxism-political-ideology`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Present Marxism as method, critique, theory of change and political project, then evaluate explanatory power and historical-institutional liabilities.

**Argument map:** material production/class → alienation and surplus appropriation → ideology/state → contradiction and praxis → strengths → reductionism/prediction/authoritarianism objections → democratic reconstruction.

### Model answer

Marxism is simultaneously a materialist method, critique of capitalism, theory of historical change and project of collective emancipation. Historical materialism relates productive forces, relations of production and class struggle; alienation describes labour separated from product, activity, species-being and others; surplus value explains exploitation as structural appropriation rather than individual theft. Ideology makes historically specific domination appear natural, while revolution aims at democratic social control of production and ultimately a classless order.

Its strengths are substantial: it exposes the material conditions beneath formal legal equality, connects economy with political power, and explains how formally free contracts can reproduce dependence. Its conception of praxis also refuses detached interpretation.

**Objections:** economic reductionism underestimates caste, gender, religion and political autonomy; predicted revolutionary trajectories failed; transitional states often entrenched party bureaucracy rather than withering away. **Marxist reply:** the method need not be mechanical, and later theories of hegemony and social reproduction broaden it.

**Assessment:** Marxism remains a powerful critical ideology, but emancipation is not guaranteed by nationalisation or class rule. It is defensible only with civil liberties, plural organisation, anti-caste and feminist analysis, and accountable democratic control.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates the full ideological architecture rather than listing slogans or equating Marxism with later regimes.

## Q2(c). “There is no permanent friend or permanent enemy.” Discuss this statement in the light of Kautilya’s view on Sovereignty.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → Kauṭilya · circle of states and strategic sovereignty · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Applied doctrinal discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty — Kautilya  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** seven limbs (*saptāṅga*) · circle of states (*rājamaṇḍala*) · sixfold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*)  
**Proposed slug:** `kautilya-no-permanent-friend-enemy-sovereignty`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** Treat the sentence as the examiner’s framing, not a verified Kauṭilya quotation. Explain why his doctrines make relations conditional and connect that flexibility to effective sovereignty.

**Argument map:** internal state capacities → relational external environment → changing positions of rival/ally → six policies track relative power → realism’s value → opportunism and modern-law objections → prudential qualification.

### Model answer

The printed maxim captures Kauṭilya’s strategic logic, though it should not be presented as his verified quotation. In the *Arthaśāstra*, sovereignty is not merely a legal title. The seven-limbed state (*saptāṅga*)—ruler, ministers, territory-people, fort, treasury, force and ally—must possess effective capacity.

Externally, the circle of states (*rājamaṇḍala*) places the aspiring ruler (*vijigīṣu*) amid an immediate rival (*ari*), ally (*mitra*), intermediate (*madhyama*) and remote/neutral (*udāsīna*) powers. These are relational positions, not permanent moral identities. The sixfold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*)—treaty, hostility, marching, waiting, seeking shelter and dual policy—allows conduct to change with relative power, security and interest. Hence friendship and enmity are conditional instruments of preserving autonomy.

**Objection:** such realism can legitimise opportunism, aggression and betrayal, and it conflicts with treaty fidelity and sovereign equality. **Reply:** prudence is not arbitrary treachery; welfare, long-term stability and the costs of war constrain policy.

**Assessment:** Kauṭilya illuminates effective sovereignty as capability within interdependence, but modern legitimacy additionally requires law, public accountability and good-faith obligations.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses exact doctrine, controls quotation provenance and links strategic flexibility specifically to sovereignty.

## Q3(a). Can one’s right to life be absolute? Answer with reference to the idea of Capital Punishment.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Crime and Punishment](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/crime-and-punishment.md) → Capital Punishment · right to life · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Modal evaluation with application  
**Syllabus unit:** Crime and Punishment — Capital Punishment  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** absoluteness of rights · retribution · deterrence · dignity · necessity · irreversibility  
**Proposed slug:** `right-to-life-absolute-capital-punishment`  
**Canonical owner:** [Crime and Punishment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Crime-and-Punishment.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish a fundamental right from an exceptionless right; then test whether recognised defensive exceptions justify execution after custody.

**Argument map:** right’s moral basis → self-defence/necessity exceptions → structural difference of execution → retribution/deterrence/public affirmation → fallibility/dignity/inequality/reform → legal illustration → qualified abolitionist assessment.

### Model answer

The right to life is fundamental because life is the condition of agency, dignity and every further right. Yet “fundamental” does not automatically mean exceptionless: proportionate force in immediate self-defence may be justified against an ongoing unjust threat.

Capital punishment is structurally different. The condemned person is secured after adjudication; the state deliberately kills when less final incapacitation is available. Defenders invoke **retribution**—grave culpable killing deserves the gravest proportionate response—**deterrence**, permanent incapacitation and public affirmation of the victim’s worth. Kantian retributivism insists that punishment respects responsible agency rather than using offenders merely as instruments.

**Objections:** execution is irreversible under fallible institutions; unequal defence and discretionary sentencing undermine equal proportionality; deterrence beyond secure imprisonment is an empirical claim, not a presumption; and killing forecloses reform. Using the prisoner to deter others also conflicts with treating persons as ends.

**Reply:** a defender argues that rights may be forfeited through culpable murder and that justice to victims demands proportionate censure. But forfeiture does not establish literal equivalence, nor remove the state’s burden of necessity.

In India, *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) retained death punishment under the “rarest of rare” limitation; Law Commission Report No. 262 (2015) recommended abolition for ordinary crimes while excluding specified terrorism-related and war contexts. These illustrate legal compromise, not philosophical proof.

**Assessment:** the right to life need not be metaphysically absolute, yet defensive exceptions do not automatically justify execution. Under fallibility, unequal administration and available non-lethal protection, capital punishment fails an especially demanding necessity test.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers absoluteness, separates defence from execution, evaluates rival penal theories and reaches a qualified conclusion.

### Model answer

Tagore’s humanism, especially in *The Religion of Man*, reconciles opposites through **creative relation**. The individual is neither dissolved into a collective nor complete in isolation; individuality fulfils itself through love, art, sympathy and participation in universal humanity.

Thus individual and universal are mutually enabling. Reason and spirituality are reconciled because critical intelligence articulates, rather than destroys, lived depth. East and West should meet through reciprocal learning, not imitation or chauvinism. Freedom and community cohere when community nourishes self-expression instead of coercing conformity. The finite person reaches toward the infinite through creative participation in a larger life.

**Assessment:** Tagore offers a powerful non-dual relational ideal, but reconciliation is credible only when asymmetrical power is transformed, not merely spiritually redescribed.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the philosophical mechanism behind the reconciliations and gives a precise limitation.

### Model answer

Reconciliation is possible only if development is understood as expansion of capabilities rather than externally imposed growth. Tribal communities are internally diverse, but many conflicts concern collective control of land, ecological dependence, customary institutions, identity and intergenerational continuity.

A defensible model requires equal citizenship and cultural recognition; informed and inclusive participation in defining benefits; assessment of capabilities destroyed as well as created; fair distribution of burdens, benefits and decision-power; recognition of individual and community rights; ecologically reversible alternatives under uncertainty; and protection of dissent, exit and gender justice within communities. ## Q4(a). How are both equality and liberty inadequate as social and political ideals without justice? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → relation among equality, liberty and justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q4(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Relational philosophical discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Equality, Justice, Liberty  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Berlin · Nozick · Rawls · Sen · Ambedkar · adjudicatory role of justice  
**Proposed slug:** `equality-liberty-inadequate-without-justice`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain the indeterminacy of liberty and equality separately and how theories of justice select, order and rectify their claims; do not merely say justice “balances” them.

**Argument map:** liberty under-specified → equality under-specified → conflicts and background injustice → justice supplies principles/procedure/rectification → competing justice theories → architectonic but contested role.

### Model answer

Liberty and equality are indispensable, yet each is normatively incomplete without justice.

**Liberty** asks what sphere of action should be protected, but not every liberty has equal worth. A proprietor’s liberty to exclude, a worker’s freedom from domination and a citizen’s speech may conflict. Negative non-interference can coexist with poverty or private power that makes choice unusable; positive liberty can become paternalistic if authorities define a person’s “real” good.

**Equality** is equally under-specified. Equality of legal status, opportunity, resources, welfare, capabilities and outcomes yield different institutions. Treating unequals identically may reproduce disadvantage, while levelling every difference may erase choice, need and desert.

**Justice** supplies adjudicatory principles. Rawls gives basic liberties lexical priority, fair opportunity and benefit to the least advantaged. Nozick protects historical entitlement; Sen assesses actual capabilities; corrective justice addresses wrongfully produced starting points. Ambedkar adds that liberty and equality remain unstable without fraternity and annihilation of graded status.

**Objection:** calling justice the higher ideal merely relocates disagreement, since theories of justice conflict. **Reply:** that is true, but the conflict is now explicit: justice asks which freedoms, equalities, procedures and rectifications can be publicly justified.

**Assessment:** justice is not a numerical compromise between liberty and equality. It is the architectonic ideal that specifies their objects, orders competing claims and tests background conditions. Yet justice must itself preserve basic liberty and equal moral standing.

**Assessment:** rights and duties are analytically distinguishable but normatively interdependent. Reject both total separation and the crude formula that every duty maps to one individual claim-right.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the invalid inference, uses exact Hohfeldian distinctions and reaches a qualified conclusion.

**Objections:** Cārvāka denies that imperceptible God follows from a secure concomitance. Mīmāṃsā holds the Veda to be authorless (*apauruṣeya*) and karma self-operating. Buddhism and Jainism explain order without a creator; Sāṃkhya invokes primordial nature (*prakṛti*). The world’s defects also undercut inference to a perfect maker.

**Nyāya reply:** unconscious atoms, karma or nature cannot initiate and coordinate purposive order without intelligence; reliable testimony points to a competent source.

**Assessment:** Nyāya provides a sophisticated cosmological-teleological cumulative case, but the premises underdetermine one omniscient personal God and remain contestable across rival ontologies.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies multiple Nyāya routes, preserves efficient/material causation and stages the Indian inter-school debate.

## Q7(c). Is the concept of immortality of soul a necessary condition for Rebirth? Discuss with reference to the *Bhagavad Gītā*.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Soul, Immortality, Rebirth and Liberation](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/soul-immortality-rebirth.md) → *Bhagavad Gītā* · immortality and rebirth · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q7(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Necessity test within named text  
**Syllabus unit:** Soul — Immortality; Rebirth and Liberation  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** deathless self (*ātman/dehin*) · transmigration · karmic conditions · Buddhist countermodel  
**Proposed slug:** `gita-immortal-soul-necessary-rebirth`  
**Canonical owner:** [Soul, Immortality, Rebirth and Liberation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Soul-Immortality-Rebirth.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish necessity within the Gītā’s substantial-self model from universal logical necessity across traditions; also distinguish necessity from sufficiency.

**Argument map:** Gītā 2.13 continuity → 2.20 unborn self → 2.22 body-changing → 15.8 carried dispositions → within-model necessity → Buddhist no-self counterexample → immortality not sufficient → two-level verdict.

### Model answer

Within the *Bhagavad Gītā*, immortality of the soul is necessary for its account of rebirth. Gītā 2.13 compares bodily stages with passage to another body; 2.20 declares the self unborn and undying; 2.22 compares embodiment to changing garments; and 15.8 describes the embodied self carrying mind and sense-capacities to a new body. Rebirth is therefore the transmigration of the same deathless *dehin* under karmic conditions.

However, immortality is not sufficient. An eternal self need not actually re-embody; karma, desire and unfinished bondage explain renewed birth, while liberation ends rebirth without destroying the self.

**Objection:** Buddhism explains rebirth through causal continuity of aggregates without an immortal self (*anātman*), showing that substantial identity is not universally necessary. **Reply:** this is a genuine cross-traditional countermodel but not the Gītā’s doctrine; it changes the meaning of what continues.

**Assessment:** immortality is necessary within the Gītā’s substantial-self theory, but not for every coherent theory of rebirth. Distinguish internal doctrinal necessity from universal philosophical necessity.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses the named text, separates necessary from sufficient conditions and controls the Buddhist comparison.

## Q8(a). Evaluate the nature and object of Religious Experience as explained by Radhakrishnan in *The Hindu View of Life*.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Experience](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-experience.md) → Radhakrishnan · integral intuition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(a) · 20 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Text-specific exposition and evaluation  
**Syllabus unit:** Religious Experience — Nature and Object (Indian)  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** lived vision (*darśana*) · experience (*anubhava*) · self-certification · Brahman/Bhagavān  
**Proposed slug:** `radhakrishnan-hindu-view-life-religious-experience`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Experience](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Experience.md)

**Demand decoding:** Treat nature and object separately in the order of Radhakrishnan’s argument; self-certifying conviction must be qualified by rational testing and fallibility.

**Argument map:** lived experience prior to creed → whole-person integral apprehension → felt self-certification → logical and practical testing → central spiritual reality as object → personal/supra-personal mediation → diversity/naturalism objections → limited verdict.

### Model answer

In Lecture I of *The Hindu View of Life*, Radhakrishnan places lived vision (*darśana*) and experience (*anubhava*) before fixed creed and ceremony. Religious experience is not a passing feeling but the integrated person’s response: cognition, emotion and will participate in an immediate apprehension that discursive reason later articulates.

Its felt certainty is self-certifying (*svataḥsiddha*) for the subject, but Radhakrishnan does not make it infallible. Spiritual perception must face logical criticism, continuing inquiry, disciplined practice and the test of transformed life; inherited testimony guides but does not replace fresh realisation.

The **object** is central spiritual reality or supreme spirit, not a finite sensory object. Personal God (*Bhagavān*) expresses ultimate reality in relation to devotees, while Brahman names reality beyond exhaustive personal predicates. Conflicting reports are mediated by temperament, culture and conceptual scheme; mediation need not imply invention.

**Objections:** private certainty does not prove an external object. Personal encounter, non-dual identity and Buddhist non-self may be genuinely incompatible, not merely varied descriptions. Further, the same psychological mediation permits naturalistic explanations.

**Reply:** Radhakrishnan invokes convergent spiritual depth, rational correction and moral fruits, while allowing descriptions to be partial.

**Assessment:** his account compellingly portrays religion as integrated, lived and corrigible rather than dogmatic. It gives prima facie warrant to the experiencer, but the inference from diverse experiences to one supra-personal reality remains underdetermined and arguably Advaita-centred.

**Why this earns marks:** It is text-specific, distinguishes nature from object and evaluates both first-person force and public warrant.

## Q8(b). Is it necessary for the normative principles to bear reference to God in order to produce a feeling of obligation in a moral agent? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religion and Morality](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religion-and-morality.md) → God-reference and moral obligation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(b) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Necessity test/critical discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Religion and Morality  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** divine command · felt obligation · Kantian autonomy · non-theistic ethics  
**Proposed slug:** `god-reference-feeling-moral-obligation`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religion and Morality](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religion-and-Morality.md)

**Demand decoding:** The stem concerns production of a felt duty, not merely moral truth. Separate validity, motivation and compliance.

**Argument map:** God as commander/judge/covenantal other → motivational force → Euthyphro and heteronomy → Kantian respect/compassion/vows as counterexamples → neither necessary nor sufficient → believer-specific enrichment.

### Model answer

Reference to God can intensify moral obligation but is not necessary for producing it. Divine-command and covenantal views make duty a response to a personal authority, gratitude or judgement; for believers, this can transform an abstract rule into answerability before God.

Yet the Euthyphro dilemma asks whether an act is right because commanded—risking arbitrariness—or commanded because right—making its standard intelligible independently. Kant further argues that obedience motivated by reward or fear is heteronomous; respect for a law rational agents can universalise can itself produce obligation. Buddhist compassion, Jain vows and secular commitments to dignity also furnish non-theistic counterexamples.

**Objection:** without a supreme lawgiver, moral norms lack categorical motivational force, especially when violation is profitable and hidden. **Reply:** God-reference does not guarantee compliance, while conscience, character, reciprocity and autonomous respect can motivate costly duty.

**Assessment:** God is neither necessary nor sufficient for a feeling of obligation. Religious reference may deepen motivation and communal accountability for believers, but the validity of norms, psychological experience of duty and actual action must remain distinct.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers necessity through counterexamples and preserves the distinction between moral grounding and felt motivation.

## Q8(c). Discuss the Advaitic notion of indescribability (*anirvacanīyatā*) in the context of nature of religious language.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Language](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-language.md) → Advaita · indescribability and indirect indication · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(c) · 15 marks · Difficulty: High · Demand: Contextual doctrinal discussion  
**Syllabus unit:** Nature of Religious Language — Analogical and Symbolic; Cognitivist and Non-cognitive  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** ineffability of Brahman · indescribable status of appearance (*māyā*) · negation · indirect indication (*lakṣaṇā*)  
**Proposed slug:** `advaita-anirvacaniyata-religious-language`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Language](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Language.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish Brahman’s ineffability from the technically indescribable status of *māyā*; explain how language can still teach through negation and indirect indication.

**Argument map:** ordinary words denote qualified objects → Brahman non-objectifiable → “not this, not this” removes limits → great sayings indicate identity → *māyā* neither absolutely real nor unreal → self-defeat objection → pedagogical/sublative reply → qualified cognitive account.

### Model answer

Advaita begins from a limit of ordinary predication: words normally denote qualified objects, whereas non-dual Brahman is not an object with class, quality, action or relation. Hence Brahman is ineffable; “not this, not this” (*neti neti*) removes limiting identifications rather than supplying another finite description.

Language nevertheless has a pedagogical and cognitive role. Upaniṣadic great sayings use indirect indication (*lakṣaṇā*): in “That thou art” (*tat tvam asi*), incompatible connotations of omniscient Lord and finite individual are set aside to indicate consciousness as their non-dual import. Language removes ignorance like a thorn removing a thorn and is itself sublated in realisation.

A distinction is essential. Brahman is beyond literal predication; *māyā* is called indescribable (*anirvacanīya*) because it is neither absolutely real, like Brahman, nor absolutely unreal, like a square circle—it appears and is later sublated.

**Objection:** if all statements about Brahman fail, Advaita’s own teaching becomes self-refuting. **Reply:** its sentences are not exhaustive descriptions but eliminative and indicative instruments whose success culminates beyond propositional grasp.

**Assessment:** Advaita offers a sophisticated middle path between literalism and meaninglessness. Its language is cognitively directed but non-objectifying; the unresolved issue is whether “indication” genuinely refers or merely redescribes transformative experience.

**Why this earns marks:** It controls the Brahman/*māyā* distinction and explains the exact linguistic mechanisms, objection and reply.
