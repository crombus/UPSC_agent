### Q1(a). “Ideas are timeless and spaceless.” Elucidate this statement with reference to Plato.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Plato · Forms · transcendence and immutability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—Ideas; knowledge and opinion · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Plato · Forms · timelessness, spacelessness and participation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `plato-forms-timeless-spaceless`

**Demand decoding:** Explain both predicates, contrast Forms with sensible particulars, connect their immutability to knowledge, and briefly identify the participation problem.

**Argument structure:** sensible particulars become and perish → knowledge requires stable objects → Forms are non-spatial, atemporal universals → particulars participate in them → epistemic gain versus separation difficulty.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Plato’s Ideas or Forms are “timeless and spaceless” because they are intelligible, universal realities rather than physical objects situated in becoming.

A sensible beautiful object occupies a place, begins and ceases to exist, and can become ugly. Beauty itself neither occupies a region nor changes with time. Likewise, the Form of Equality is not one equal stick among others. Forms are eternal, non-composite, self-identical standards apprehended by reason; sensible particulars are changing, many and known through perception. This ontological contrast grounds Plato’s epistemology: stable knowledge (*epistēmē*) concerns Forms, while opinion (*doxa*) concerns the changing visible world.

Particulars are what they are by participation in or imitation of Forms. Yet this creates the principal objection: if Forms are wholly separate from space and time, how do they explain sensible things? The *Parmenides* exposes regress and participation difficulties.

**Evaluation:** Timelessness and spacelessness secure objectivity and universal knowledge, but at the cost of making the relation between intelligible Forms and empirical particulars obscure.

**Why this earns marks:** It elucidates both attributes, links ontology with epistemology, and adds the exact internal criticism.

### Q1(b). “In the empirical world, everything is a compound of Matter and Form.” Evaluate this statement with reference to Aristotle.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Aristotle · hylomorphism · matter and form · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—form and matter; actuality and potentiality · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Aristotle · hylomorphism · scope and exceptions · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aristotle-empirical-hylomorphism`

**Demand decoding:** State hylomorphism, explain the unity of matter and form through examples, and qualify the apparently exceptionless word “everything.”

**Argument structure:** changeable substance → matter as potentiality + form as actuality → unified concrete substance (*synolon*) → explanatory strength → prime matter, artefacts and Unmoved Mover qualify scope.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The statement accurately expresses Aristotle’s dominant account of sensible substance, provided matter and form are understood as correlative principles rather than detachable things.

A bronze statue is bronze as matter and its determinate shape as form; an organism is an organic body whose soul is its first actuality. Matter explains what can receive contrary determinations, while form explains essence, unity and actuality. The concrete substance (*synolon*) is therefore neither bare matter nor a separately existing Platonic Form. Hylomorphism explains change without reducing it either to creation from nothing or to mere rearrangement.

The statement nevertheless needs qualification. Matter is relative: wood is matter for a table but already informed substance. Prime matter is only a limiting principle, never an independently experienced thing. Artefacts possess a weaker unity than natural organisms, and the Unmoved Mover is pure actuality without matter.

**Evaluation:** Hylomorphism is Aristotle’s general ontology of empirical natural substances, not an unrestricted thesis about every reality whatsoever. Its strength is its account of organised unity; its difficulty is the obscurity of prime matter.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates rather than merely repeats the proposition and states its precise scope and limits.

### Q1(c). Explain the difference between being-for-itself and being-in-itself as presented by Sartre.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Sartre · being-for-itself and being-in-itself · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Sartre; freedom and bad faith · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sartre · *pour-soi* and *en-soi* · consciousness, nothingness and freedom · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `sartre-pour-soi-en-soi`

**Demand decoding:** Define both modes, show why their difference is not simply mind versus matter, and derive the consequences for freedom and the failed desire to be God.

**Argument structure:** self-identical being-in-itself → nihilating consciousness-for-itself → facticity and transcendence → freedom → impossible synthesis.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In *Being and Nothingness*, being-in-itself (*en-soi*) is complete self-identical being, whereas being-for-itself (*pour-soi*) is consciousness whose existence consists in distance from itself.

The in-itself “is what it is”: full, opaque, non-conscious and without possibilities or lack. A stone simply coincides with its being. The for-itself, by contrast, introduces nothingness through questioning, negation and projection. It “is not what it is,” because it can transcend its facticity, and “is what it is not,” because it exists toward unrealised possibilities. Consciousness is consequently intentional, temporal and free.

The contrast is not a Cartesian division between two substances. Consciousness is always consciousness of being-in-itself, yet cannot become a thing without bad faith. Human desire seeks the impossible synthesis—being-in-itself-for-itself, self-complete yet conscious—traditionally represented by God.

**Evaluation:** Sartre powerfully connects consciousness with freedom, but making self-coincidence impossible risks depicting human life as permanent lack.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms through Sartre’s formulas and derives freedom, bad faith and the impossible synthesis.

### Q1(d). “The golden mountain is very high.” Discuss this statement in the context of Russell’s theory of descriptions.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → Russell · theory of descriptions · non-denoting expressions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Russell—logical atomism and theory of descriptions · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Bertrand Russell · definite descriptions · existence and uniqueness · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `russell-golden-mountain-description`

**Demand decoding:** Apply Russell’s contextual analysis to a non-denoting description, state the truth-value, and explain the ontological payoff.

**Argument structure:** meaningful but apparently non-referring phrase → existence + uniqueness + predication → existence clause fails → sentence false without a subsisting object.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Russell treats “the golden mountain” not as a name of a mysterious object but as an incomplete symbol eliminated by logical analysis.

The sentence means:

1. at least one object is both golden and a mountain;
2. at most one object is both golden and a mountain; and
3. that object is very high.

Formally: \(\exists x[(Gx \land Mx)\land \forall y((Gy\land My)\rightarrow y=x)\land Hx]\).

Because no golden mountain exists, the existence clause is false; therefore the whole proposition is false, not meaningless. Russell thus avoids Meinong’s claim that a golden mountain must possess some shadowy subsistence merely because it can be discussed. Grammatical subject-predicate form conceals the proposition’s quantified logical form.

**Objection and reply:** Ordinary descriptions can be context-sensitive and need not always imply uniqueness. Russell’s analysis remains compelling for the stipulated definite-description use, though Strawson later argues that failed reference may produce presupposition failure rather than falsity.

**Evaluation:** The example demonstrates significance without ontological inflation.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies the analysis, truth-value, philosophical target and a precise later objection.

### Q1(e). How does Hegel challenge Kant’s distinction between Phenomena and Noumena? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → Hegel · critique of Kantian dualism · thing-in-itself · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Hegel—absolute idealism; Kant—phenomena and noumena · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hegel and Kant · unknowable thing-in-itself · dialectical overcoming · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Secondary route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hegel-kant-phenomena-noumena`

**Demand decoding:** Explain Kant’s distinction, reconstruct Hegel’s immanent criticism, allow Kant’s limiting-concept reply, and evaluate.

**Argument structure:** appearance conditioned by cognition → unknowable noumenon → limit itself thought → asserted unknowable becomes self-contradictory → subject-object opposition sublated in Absolute knowing.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hegel attacks not appearance as such but Kant’s permanent barrier between knowable phenomena and an unknowable thing-in-itself.

Kant restricts theoretical knowledge to appearances structured by sensibility and categories; the noumenon marks what things may be independently of these conditions. Hegel replies that a wholly unknowable beyond is an empty abstraction. To draw a limit to thought is already to think that limit and what is supposedly beyond it. Moreover, calling the thing-in-itself existent or the ground of appearances applies categories while officially prohibiting their trans-empirical use.

For Hegel, finitude and its beyond are dialectically related moments. Thought discovers contradictions within finite categories and sublates them into richer forms until subject and object are comprehended within Absolute Spirit.

Kant can reply that the noumenon is a negative limiting concept, not a known supersensible object. Yet talk of things-in-themselves “affecting” sensibility strains that defence.

**Evaluation:** Hegel exposes the instability of positing an unknowable reality, though his Absolute requires a stronger metaphysics than Kant would permit.

**Why this earns marks:** It presents challenge, Kantian reply and a balanced judgement within 10-mark proportion.

### Q2(a). What are the basic tenets of Rationalism? How does Descartes build a system of Philosophy in consonance with them? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Descartes · rationalist method and system · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Descartes; method and substance · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Descartes · reason, innate ideas, deduction and foundationalism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `descartes-system-rationalist-tenets`

**Demand decoding:** First identify the school’s basic commitments; then map each commitment onto Descartes’ constructive sequence; finally test whether the system attains its own standard of certainty.

**Argument structure:** reason and innate principles → mathematical method → doubt → cogito → God → clear-and-distinct rule → external world and substance dualism → objections to the bridge beyond the cogito.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Rationalism holds that reason can discover necessary and universal truths through innate or self-evident principles and deductive order. Descartes constructs the paradigm rationalist system by moving from methodological doubt to a rationally reconstructed metaphysics.

**Basic tenets**

1. **Primacy of reason:** Sense experience is variable and cannot by itself yield necessity.
2. **Innate structures or ideas:** At least some concepts and principles are not derived from sensation.
3. **Intellectual intuition and deduction:** Self-evident truths ground demonstrative chains.
4. **Mathematical ideal:** Philosophy should display order, clarity and necessity.
5. **Intelligibility of reality:** Reality possesses a rational structure accessible to thought.
6. **Foundationalism:** Knowledge must begin from indubitable premises.

**Descartes’ construction**

In the *Discourse on Method*, Descartes proposes evidence, analysis, synthesis and complete enumeration. The *Meditations* radicalise this procedure through doubt: sensory beliefs may deceive, dreams undermine the present world, and the evil genius threatens mathematics. Doubt itself yields the indubitable cogito—while thinking, “I am, I exist.” The self is then understood as a thinking thing (*res cogitans*).

The idea of an infinite perfect being cannot, Descartes argues, originate from a finite self; God therefore exists. Because a perfect God is no deceiver, clear and distinct perceptions are trustworthy. Mathematics and the external world are restored, while reality is ordered into infinite substance, thinking substance and extended substance. The wax example shows that intellect, not sensation alone, grasps bodily identity. Error arises when unlimited will outruns finite understanding.

**Objections and replies:** The cogito establishes occurring thought more securely than a substantial ego. Arnauld’s Cartesian Circle charges that clear-and-distinct perception proves God while God guarantees that perception. Descartes replies that present clear perception is self-evident and God guarantees only remembered deductions, but the distinction remains contested. Locke rejects innate ideas; Kant later argues that existence cannot be inferred from a concept.

**Evaluation:** Descartes successfully exemplifies rationalist method and secures a powerful first-person foundation. Yet the theological bridge from cogito to world is less certain than the standard from which it begins; his system is rationalist in architecture even where its deductions remain disputable.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately answers the two demands, maps tenets to system-building, uses named texts and objections, and gives a qualified verdict.

### Q2(b). “All determination is negation.” Comment with reference to Spinoza.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Spinoza · determination, negation and infinity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Spinoza; substance, attributes and modes · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Spinoza · *determinatio negatio est* · finite modes and infinite substance · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `spinoza-determination-negation`

**Demand decoding:** Clarify the dictum’s provenance and sense, locate it within substance monism, explain its implications for finite modes, and assess the charge of acosmism.

**Argument structure:** determination means boundary → boundary excludes alternatives → finite mode is limited by other modes → absolutely infinite substance has no external limit → modal dependence does not entail sheer unreality.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Spinoza’s dictum *determinatio negatio est*—“determination is negation”—means that to be this finite thing is to be bounded against what it is not. It expresses finitude and dependence, not the claim that every determinate mode is sheer nothing.

In Letter 50, Spinoza explains that figure is a determination of body and therefore a negation: a finite shape exists only through a boundary excluding further extension. More generally, a finite body is limited by another body and a finite idea by another idea. Each mode expresses only a determinate degree of God or Nature’s power.

Substance, by contrast, is absolutely infinite, self-caused and limited by nothing of the same nature. Attributes express its eternal essence, while modes are affections within it. The dictum therefore supports monism: finite things cannot be self-subsistent substances because their identity includes limitation and relational dependence.

It also has ethical significance. Imagination treats finite things and human choices as isolated; adequate reason sees each as necessitated within the whole, *sub specie aeternitatis*. Freedom is understanding necessity, not exemption from it.

**Objection:** Hegel argues that Spinoza’s infinite is an indeterminate abyss that negates finite individuality—hence “acosmism.” **Reply:** Modes possess positive reality as determinate expressions of divine power; what is negated is their independence, not their existence.

**Evaluation:** The dictum incisively explains why finite determination entails exclusion and dependence. It becomes reductive only if negation of self-subsistence is confused with negation of all positive modal reality.

**Why this earns marks:** It corrects the slogan’s scope, integrates metaphysics and ethics, and adjudicates the Hegelian objection.

### Q2(c). Examine Hume’s refutation of Causal relation and Kant’s response to it.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume and Kant · causation and synthetic a priori · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Hume; Kant—categories and synthetic a priori · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hume and Kant · necessary connection · objective succession · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hume-kant-causation`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Hume’s negative and psychological accounts, show Kant’s transcendental response, and assess what Kant concedes and what he restores.

**Argument structure:** no impression of power → constant conjunction + custom → induction circular → Kant: causal category conditions objective temporal experience → necessity limited to phenomena.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hume refutes causal necessity as an observed or demonstrable relation; Kant responds that causality is not derived from experience but is an a priori condition that makes objective experience possible.

Hume applies the copy principle: every legitimate idea must derive from an impression. In a causal sequence we perceive only spatial contiguity, temporal priority and repeated conjunction—not a necessary power binding cause to effect. Repetition changes the mind, not the objects: custom produces an expectation of B upon perceiving A, and the felt transition is projected as necessary connection. Nor can induction be rationally justified. Uniformity of nature is not demonstrable because its denial is conceivable, and an empirical defence would presuppose the very induction at issue.

Kant accepts that necessity is never sensed but rejects Hume’s psychological conclusion. In the Second Analogy, a merely subjective succession of perceptions becomes cognition of an objective event only when appearances are ordered under a rule: the later state follows the earlier necessarily according to the category of cause. “Every event has a cause” is therefore synthetic a priori—informative, necessary and constitutive of possible experience.

**Objection and reply:** Hume may say Kant converts a habit into an unexplained mental necessity. Kant replies through transcendental deduction: categories are justified by their indispensability to unified self-conscious experience, not by innate metaphysical inspection.

**Evaluation:** Kant restores the objectivity required by science while conceding Hume’s central point that necessity is not copied from impressions. The price is transcendental idealism: causality is valid for phenomena, not demonstrably for things-in-themselves.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives Hume’s two-stage critique, Kant’s exact transcendental reply and its limiting cost.

### Q3(a). “We should look not to an ideal language which derives its meaning from facts and has a precise ‘logical structure but empirically, to the ways in which languages are actually used.” Explain the transition from early views of Wittgenstein to his later views on language and meaning with reference to this statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Later Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/later-wittgenstein.md) → Wittgenstein · picture theory to meaning-as-use · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Early Wittgenstein—picture theory; Later Wittgenstein—meaning and language-games · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Ludwig Wittgenstein · ideal logical language versus ordinary use · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Secondary route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `wittgenstein-picture-to-use`

**Demand decoding:** Use the printed statement as the hinge; explain the early theory, the pressures that displaced it, the later account and the resulting change in philosophical method.

**Argument structure:** proposition pictures fact through shared logical form → ideal analysis → failures of atomism and naming model → plurality of language-games → meaning-as-use within forms of life → philosophy as description and therapy.

#### Model answer

> **Printed-wording note:** The official English line contains an unmatched opening quotation mark before “logical structure”; it is preserved above.

**Thesis:** Wittgenstein’s transition is from a top-down theory in which meaningful language pictures facts through an ideal logical structure to a bottom-up investigation of how expressions function within actual human practices.

In the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, the world is the totality of facts, not things. An elementary proposition pictures a possible atomic fact because names and objects share a logical form. Complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions. Logical analysis should disclose this hidden structure and demarcate meaningful factual discourse from what can only be shown.

Several pressures destabilised this model. The colour-exclusion problem showed that apparently elementary propositions such as “This patch is red” and “This patch is green” are not logically independent. The Augustinian picture of language as naming objects could not explain commands, questions, exclamations, numbers or words such as “if.” Exact logical essence increasingly appeared to be a philosophical demand imposed upon language.

The *Philosophical Investigations* therefore directs us to actual use. For a large class of cases, meaning is use (§43). Words resemble tools whose functions differ. Speaking is part of language-games—ordering, reporting, joking, praying—embedded in forms of life. These games share family resemblances rather than one essence. Rule-following is a public practice, understanding is mastery of a technique, and private ostensive definition cannot by itself establish correctness.

The methodological transition is equally important. Philosophy no longer constructs an ideal notation to mirror reality; it describes ordinary grammar, produces perspicuous representations and dissolves confusions generated when language “goes on holiday.” It “leaves everything as it is.”

**Objection and reply:** The later view may seem descriptively conservative and unable to criticise corrupt practices. Wittgenstein’s reply is methodological: philosophy removes conceptual illusion rather than legislating science or ethics. Nevertheless, the absence of a systematic semantics is a real cost.

**Evaluation:** The later philosophy gains sensitivity to linguistic diversity and practice but relinquishes the early work’s elegant unified account of representation. The continuity is therapeutic: both periods seek to expose the limits created by misunderstanding language, though by opposite methods.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains early doctrine, transition causes, later doctrine and methodological consequence while keeping the quotation central.

### Q3(b). Present an exposition of the verification theory of meaning as propounded by the logical positivists. In this context also differentiate between the “strong” and the “weak” sense of the word “verifiable”.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → verification theory · strong and weak verifiability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Logical Positivism—verification theory and rejection of metaphysics · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Vienna Circle and A. J. Ayer · cognitive meaning · strong/weak verification · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `verification-strong-weak`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the criterion’s purpose and classes of meaningful statements, sharply distinguish strong from weak verification, and assess the dilemma created by both versions.

**Argument structure:** analytic or empirically testable → metaphysics excluded → conclusive verification too strict → probabilistic relevance too permissive → self-application problem.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Logical positivism makes possible verification the criterion of cognitive meaning: a factual statement is meaningful only if experience could count for or against it.

The Vienna Circle—especially Schlick, Carnap and Neurath—and A. J. Ayer distinguish analytic propositions, true by linguistic rules, from synthetic propositions whose meaning lies in their empirical verification conditions. A sentence with no conceivable observational difference is not false but cognitively meaningless; this verdict targets metaphysics, theology and speculative claims, while ethical utterances are often treated emotively. Verification is a criterion of meaning, not a guarantee of truth.

**Strong verifiability** requires that experience conclusively establish a proposition’s truth. This excludes universal laws, historical statements and claims about other minds, for no finite evidence conclusively verifies them. **Weak verifiability** requires only that possible experience be relevant to, or increase the probability of, truth or falsity. It preserves science and distinguishes verification in principle from verification in practice—for example, the once practically inaccessible far side of the Moon.

**Objections and replies:** Strong verification destroys too much meaningful science; weak verification admits too much because almost any claim can be combined with auxiliaries to yield observational consequences. The criterion is also neither analytic nor straightforwardly empirically verifiable. Positivists reply by presenting it as a proposal governing factual discourse, but that weakens its claim to refute metaphysics.

**Evaluation:** Verification usefully demands testable content and exposes empty pseudo-explanation, yet the strong/weak dilemma prevents it from functioning as a precise universal criterion of meaning.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives exposition, exact distinction, examples, objections and a reasoned verdict.

### Q3(c). “Blue is one object of sensation and green is another, and consciousness, which both sensations have in common, is different from either.” Present an account of Moore’s refutation of idealism with reference to this statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → G. E. Moore · refutation of idealism · act-object distinction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Moore—defence of common sense and refutation of idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: G. E. Moore · consciousness and object · diaphanousness · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `moore-blue-green-idealism`

**Demand decoding:** Make the quotation do the argumentative work: separate act from object, explain transparency, identify the idealist inference attacked, and assess the refutation’s scope.

**Argument structure:** blue and green differ → awareness common to both → object is not act → *esse* cannot be identified with *percipi* → negative refutation does not by itself establish full realism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Moore’s quoted contrast refutes idealism by showing that a sensation contains two irreducible elements: consciousness and its object.

Idealism often moves from “blue is experienced” to “blue is nothing but the experience of blue,” captured in Berkeley’s *esse est percipi*. Moore argues in “The Refutation of Idealism” that blue and green are distinct objects of awareness, whereas consciousness is the common element in both experiences. Therefore blue cannot be identical with consciousness: if it were, the allegedly common consciousness would differ whenever its object differed.

Why, then, does the idealist fusion seem plausible? Consciousness is “diaphanous” or transparent. When one introspects the sensation of blue, attention passes through awareness to blue itself; the act is easily overlooked. Yet the very relational structure—awareness **of** blue—requires distinction between act and object. Once distinguished, no contradiction follows from the object’s existing unperceived.

**Objection:** Transparency may establish intentional difference without proving that the object is mind-independent; a sense-datum might remain experience-dependent. **Reply:** Moore’s immediate aim is negative: to block the identity from which idealism is inferred, not to deduce an entire realist ontology.

**Evaluation:** Moore decisively shows that *esse est percipi* is not analytically true. His refutation weakens a central idealist argument, though further premises are required to establish direct realism or an external physical world.

**Why this earns marks:** It centres the quotation, explains diaphanousness, and limits the conclusion accurately.

### Q4(a). How is Husserl’s account of “I think” different from that of Descartes? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Husserl and Descartes · cogito and transcendental ego · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Phenomenology—Husserl; method and intentionality · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Husserl and Descartes · “I think” · substance versus constituting subjectivity · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology (Husserl)](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Secondary route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `husserl-descartes-i-think`

**Demand decoding:** Begin from their shared search for certainty, distinguish doubt from epoché and mental substance from transcendental ego, address world-constitution and intersubjectivity, then evaluate residual idealism.

**Argument structure:** Cartesian doubt → cogito as thinking substance → world recovered through God; Husserlian epoché → intentional stream and transcendental ego → world retained as correlate → intersubjectivity problem and response.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Husserl radicalises the Cartesian turn to subjectivity but rejects Descartes’ conversion of the cogito into a worldless thinking substance. His “I think” is an intentional, constituting field rather than a mental thing.

Both philosophers seek an apodictic starting point and treat the self-givenness of conscious acts as resistant to doubt. Husserl’s *Cartesian Meditations* explicitly honours this common beginning. Their paths then diverge.

First, Descartes’ methodical doubt suspends beliefs in order to discover the indubitable proposition “I think, therefore I am,” from which he infers a thinking substance (*res cogitans*). Husserl’s phenomenological suspension (*epochē*) neither denies nor loses the world; it brackets the natural attitude’s unexamined existential commitment so that how the world is given can be studied.

Second, Descartes’ ego is a substance whose essence is thought and which can, in principle, be conceived apart from body and world. Husserl’s transcendental ego is not another object within experience. It is the pole of intentional acts, habitualities and temporal syntheses through which objects acquire sense. Consciousness is always consciousness **of** something; no isolated cogito precedes all intentional correlation.

Third, Descartes must recover the external world through the veracity of a non-deceiving God. Husserl’s world remains as intentional correlate—perceived through profiles, horizons and syntheses of identity. The issue is constitution, not inference from private ideas to external things.

**Objection:** Does constitution reduce the world and other persons to products of my ego? Husserl addresses transcendental solipsism through embodiment, pairing and analogical appresentation in the Fifth *Cartesian Meditation*: the other is constituted as an alter ego whose first-person life is never originally given to me. Critics such as Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty nevertheless argue that a transcendental ego remains too detached from being-in-the-world and embodiment.

**Evaluation:** Husserl avoids Cartesian substance dualism and the external-world bridge by making intentionality fundamental. Yet his vocabulary of constitution leaves a residual idealist tension: the world is not created by consciousness, but its sense is inseparable from constituting subjectivity.

**Why this earns marks:** It establishes shared ground, develops four precise differences, treats intersubjectivity and gives a critical verdict.

### Q4(b). “We can affirm the truth of any sentence in our total system, in the face of whatever experience, just so long as we are prepared to make adjustments elsewhere.” Discuss this statement in the light of Quine’s ‘Two Dogmas of Empiricism’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → Quine · confirmation holism · web of belief · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Quine—critique of empiricism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: W. V. O. Quine · Two Dogmas · revisability and underdetermination · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `quine-any-sentence-holism`

**Demand decoding:** Interpret the quotation as confirmation holism, connect it to both dogmas, explain how a sentence can be saved, and deny that the thesis licenses arbitrary belief.

**Argument structure:** statements face experience corporately → failed prediction underdetermines culprit → any statement can be protected by auxiliary revision → no statement is unrevisable → pragmatic constraints govern rational choice.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The quotation expresses Quine’s confirmation holism: experience confronts not isolated statements but the total “web of belief,” so any one sentence can be retained if compensating changes are made elsewhere.

In “Two Dogmas of Empiricism,” Quine attacks, first, the analytic–synthetic distinction and, second, reductionism—the belief that each meaningful statement has its own unique experiential confirmation. A hypothesis yields an observational prediction only together with auxiliaries, background assumptions and logic. If the prediction fails, experience shows merely that the conjunction is defective, not which component must be abandoned. Hence even a central principle can logically be retained “come what may” by modifying peripheral beliefs, and no statement is absolutely immune from revision.

This also explains why supposed analytic truths differ from empirical truths by degree of centrality rather than kind. Logical laws lie near the web’s centre and are costly to revise; observation sentences lie nearer its sensory periphery.

**Objection:** If any sentence may be saved, does holism make theory-choice irrational? **Reply:** Quine distinguishes logical possibility from rational economy. Revision is constrained by conservatism, simplicity, predictive success, minimum mutilation and overall explanatory gain. Protecting a claim through ad hoc changes may be possible yet unreasonable.

**Evaluation:** The quotation undermines sentence-by-sentence verification and sharp apriority without implying “anything goes.” Experience constrains systems globally, while pragmatic virtues determine where rational revision should occur.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives the quotation from both dogmas, gives the Duhem–Quine mechanism and answers the relativist objection.

### Q4(c). Explain Berkeley’s doctrine of nominalism and his refutation of Abstract ideas.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Berkeley · nominalism and abstract ideas · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Berkeley; rejection of abstract ideas · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: George Berkeley · particular ideas, general signs and immaterialism · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `berkeley-nominalism-abstract-ideas`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Berkeley’s attack on Locke’s abstraction, explain how generality remains possible without abstract images, and connect nominalism to the critique of matter.

**Argument structure:** every imagined idea is determinate → contradictory abstract image impossible → generality belongs to representative use of signs → abstract material substratum loses intelligibility.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Berkeley’s nominalism denies abstract general ideas while preserving general thought through the representative use of particular ideas and words.

Locke claims that the mind forms an abstract idea of triangle by omitting determinate features. Berkeley replies that no one can imagine a triangle that is neither equilateral, isosceles nor scalene, yet somehow all of them. Every actual idea has determinate features. Abstraction by attending separately to an inseparable aspect may be legitimate, but an indeterminate image allegedly containing incompatible determinations is not.

Generality therefore belongs not to a peculiar abstract entity but to sign-use. A particular drawn triangle can stand indifferently for all triangles when a proof depends only on features common to them. Likewise, the word “triangle” is general through its rule-governed representative function, not because it names an abstract triangle.

This nominalism prepares Berkeley’s immaterialism. If abstract ideas are rejected, one cannot intelligibly conceive “matter” as an unknown substratum stripped of every sensible quality, or existence wholly abstracted from being perceived.

**Objection and reply:** General reasoning appears to require common properties, not arbitrary tokens. Berkeley replies that selective attention and conventional representation suffice. Critics may answer that this explains use while presupposing the similarity classes it seeks to avoid.

**Evaluation:** Berkeley convincingly attacks image-like abstraction and anticipates use-based accounts of generality, though his nominalism does not by itself prove that no mind-independent structure exists.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the refutation, positive nominalist account and its role in immaterialism with a qualification.

### Q5(a). Explain the ground on which Cārvāka rejects inference as a valid source of knowledge.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Cārvāka](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/carvaka.md) → inference · invariable concomitance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Cārvāka—epistemology · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Cārvāka · inference (*anumāna*) · invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Cārvāka](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Carvaka.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `carvaka-rejection-inference`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the foundational problem—establishing universal concomitance—then test perception, inference and testimony as possible grounds, with a fair qualification about practical inference.

**Argument structure:** inference needs *vyāpti* → perception cannot survey all cases → inference of *vyāpti* is circular → testimony depends on inference → hidden conditions defeat certainty → probability versus valid knowledge.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Cārvāka rejects inference (*anumāna*) as an independent means of valid knowledge (*pramāṇa*) because its indispensable universal relation, invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*), cannot be established with certainty.

Perception observes only particular present cases—smoke with fire here and now—not every past, future and distant case. Inferring *vyāpti* from repeated observation is circular because it already uses inference. Testimony cannot ground it, since the reliability of a speaker is itself inferential. Nor can mere non-observation of exceptions exclude an unknown condition (*upādhi*): smoke may accompany fire only under qualifying circumstances.

Thus the move from “observed smoke” to “unobserved fire” exceeds what perception warrants. At best it yields probability useful in ordinary life, not certain knowledge of imperceptible realities such as soul, karma or afterlife.

Nyāya replies that repeated agreement, absence of counter-instance, hypothetical reasoning and removal of conditions establish *vyāpti*. Cārvāka’s challenge nevertheless exposes induction’s fallibility.

**Evaluation:** The critique is strongest against metaphysical certainty, but an absolute rejection is self-undermining because argument and daily action themselves employ inference.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies *vyāpti*, eliminates its alleged sources, and gives both the strongest objection and fair qualification.

**Argument structure:** Nyāya knower–instrument–object–result distinction → Buddhist one momentary cognition with object/subject aspects → production objection → functional-aspect reply → realism versus event economy.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya treats the means of valid knowledge (*pramāṇa*) and its result (*pramāṇaphala*) as distinct causal factors; Buddhist epistemologists treat them as functionally different aspects of one cognitive event.

**Why this earns marks:** It states both theories, their objections and their metaphysical stakes compactly.

### Q5(c). Delineate the main points of difference between the theory of intrinsic validation (svataḥ prāmāṇyavāda) and theory of extrinsic validation (parataḥ prāmāṇyavāda) in classical Indian philosophy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → validity of cognition · intrinsic and extrinsic validation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā—validity of knowledge; Nyāya epistemology · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya · intrinsic validity (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*) versus extrinsic validity (*parataḥ-prāmāṇya*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `intrinsic-extrinsic-validity`

**Demand decoding:** Compare source, cognition and ascertainment of validity, treatment of error, regress objection and practical consequence.

**Argument structure:** cognition arises → Mīmāṃsā presumption of truth until defeated → Nyāya later confirmation by causal excellence and successful activity → trust/regress versus error-control debate.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The disagreement concerns both what makes cognition valid and how its validity is known.

For Mīmāṃsā, cognition is intrinsically valid (*svataḥ-prāmāṇyavāda*): when cognition arises from its normal conditions, it presents its object and carries prima facie authority. Action would be impossible if every cognition awaited certification. Invalidity is discovered later through contradiction, failure or a causal defect; validity does not require a second cognition, thereby avoiding infinite regress.

Nyāya defends extrinsic validation (*parataḥ-prāmāṇyavāda*). Cognition’s truth is not guaranteed merely by occurrence or subjective assurance. It is known through external factors—defect-free causes, subsequent confirming cognition and successful activity (*pravṛtti-sāmarthya*). Nyāya treats both validity and invalidity as extrinsically known.

Mīmāṃsā charges Nyāya with regress: what validates the validating cognition? Nyāya replies that causal and practical confirmation terminates inquiry without presuming every cognition true.

**Evaluation:** Intrinsic validation explains immediate trust and ordinary action; extrinsic validation better foregrounds error-control. A balanced view may treat cognition as prima facie credible but defeasible through external checks.

**Why this earns marks:** It delineates every main axis and avoids the trap that Nyāya is extrinsic only about invalidity.

**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `ramanuja-seven-objections-mayavada`

**Demand decoding:** Name all seven accurately, state the problem each raises, add Advaita’s central reply, and evaluate the strongest pressure points.

**Argument structure:** locus → concealment → nature → indefinability → proof → remover → removal → qualified realism versus sublation.

#### Model answer

Rāmānuja’s seven untenabilities (*sapta-anupapatti*) challenge Advaita’s beginningless ignorance (*avidyā*) or illusion (*māyā*):

1. **Locus (*āśraya*):** it cannot reside in pure Brahman without tainting it, nor in the already ignorant individual without circularity.
2. **Concealment (*tirodhāna*):** self-luminous Brahman cannot be covered.
3. **Nature (*svarūpa*):** ignorance is not coherently identifiable as positive, negative or mixed.

7. **Cessation (*nivṛtti*):** if real it cannot simply vanish; if unreal nothing requires removal.

Advaita replies that ignorance is beginningless but sublatable, empirically evident in “I do not know,” and removed by great-sentence (*mahāvākya*) knowledge as rope-knowledge removes the snake appearance.

**Evaluation:** Rāmānuja exposes genuine tensions concerning locus and proof. Advaita’s levels of reality answer formal contradiction but remain less parsimonious than Rāmānuja’s real qualified unity of Brahman, selves and world.

**Why this earns marks:** It records all seven in order, gives the main defence and identifies the decisive issues.

### Q5(e). Present an exposition of Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika’s theory of causation.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → non-pre-existence of effect · causal types · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—causation; non-pre-existence of effect · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika · new production (*ārambhavāda/asatkāryavāda*) · three causes · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-vaisesika-causation`

**Demand decoding:** Define the doctrine, explain causal relevance and three cause-types, relate production to prior absence, and answer Sāṃkhya’s principal objection.

**Argument structure:** effect absent as effect → indispensable antecedent complex → inhering/non-inhering/efficient causes → prior absence terminated → new whole produced.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika defends the non-pre-existence of the effect (*asatkāryavāda*) or new-production theory (*ārambhavāda*): the effect does not already exist as that effect in its material cause.

A cause is an unconditional, indispensable antecedent whose relevance cannot be explained otherwise (*ananyathāsiddha*). Three types are distinguished:

- **inherent cause (*samavāyi-kāraṇa*):** the material constituents in which the effect inheres, such as threads of cloth;
- **non-inherent cause (*asamavāyi-kāraṇa*):** qualities or relations inhering in the inherent cause, such as the conjunction or colour of threads;
- **efficient cause (*nimitta-kāraṇa*):** the agent and instruments, such as weaver and loom.

Production terminates the effect’s prior non-existence (*prāgabhāva*). The cloth is a real whole inhering in its parts, not a merely revealed latent cloth.

Sāṃkhya objects that what is wholly non-existent cannot arise. Nyāya replies that prior absence is not absolute impossibility; determinate causal powers explain why cloth arises from threads, not from clay.

**Evaluation:** The theory captures novelty and causal plurality, though its real whole and inherence relation invite ontological multiplication.

**Why this earns marks:** It states doctrine, three causes, relevance, prior absence and the central rival exchange.

### Q6(a). Present a detailed account of Gautama’s definition of Perception.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → Gautama · perception · sense-object contact · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika—perception; ordinary and extraordinary perception · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Gautama · definition of perception (*pratyakṣa*) · clauses, classifications and criticism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `gautama-definition-perception`

**Demand decoding:** Analyse every term of the Nyāya-sūtra definition, explain later Nyāya classifications and contact relations, and evaluate Buddhist and internal difficulties.

**Argument structure:** sense-object contact → non-verbal/direct → non-deviant → determinate → ordinary/extraordinary extensions → truth and conceptuality objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Gautama defines perception (*pratyakṣa*) as cognition produced by sense-object contact, non-verbal, non-errant and determinate. The definition combines causal realism with exclusions designed to distinguish perception from testimony, illusion and doubt.

The *Nyāya-sūtra* defines it as *indriyārtha-sannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam*.

1. **Produced by sense-object contact (*indriyārtha-sannikarṣotpanna*):** External senses apprehend appropriate objects, while mind (*manas*) connects the self with internal states. Later Nyāya analyses six ordinary relations: conjunction; inherence in the conjoined; inherence in what inheres in the conjoined; inherence; inherence in what inheres; and qualifier–qualified relation for absence.
2. **Non-verbal (*avyapadeśya*):** Perception does not derive its authority from words. Early interpreters connect this with direct, pre-predicative presentation.
3. **Non-deviant (*avyabhicāri*):** Illusory cognition caused by defect is excluded from valid perception.
4. **Determinate (*vyavasāyātmaka*):** Doubt is excluded. Later Nyāya distinguishes indeterminate perception (*nirvikalpaka*), bare awareness before predication, from determinate perception (*savikalpaka*), such as “this is a blue pot.” Recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) relates a present object to previous cognition.

Ordinary perception is external or internal. Extraordinary perception includes perception through universals (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa*), transferred cognition (*jñānalakṣaṇa*)—used in explaining “fragrant sandalwood” or error—and yogic perception (*yogaja*).

**Objections:** Buddhists restrict perception to non-conceptual awareness of unique particulars (*svalakṣaṇa*) and deny that universals are perceived; conceptual determination is construction. The inclusion of “non-errant” also appears circular: validity seems built into the definition before being tested. Nyāya replies that perception here means valid perception and that successful activity externally confirms truth (*parataḥ-prāmāṇya*). Critics further regard extraordinary perception as an ad hoc expansion of contact.

**Evaluation:** Gautama’s definition gives a sophisticated realist account by identifying causal origin and excluding testimony, error and doubt. Its ordinary core remains powerful; the status of indeterminate awareness, universals and extraordinary contact is more contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It translates and analyses every clause, includes contact relations and classifications, and presents precise objections and replies.

**Demand decoding:** Define both causal functions, support each with scriptural analogies, explain why Advaita denies real transformation, compare rivals and evaluate coherence.

**Argument structure:** non-duality excludes independent matter → Brahman as Īśvara orders creation → Brahman as substrate supports names/forms → apparent transformation preserves immutability → rival objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Advaita regards Brahman as both efficient cause (*nimitta-kāraṇa*) and material cause (*upādāna-kāraṇa*) because non-duality permits neither an external creator nor an independently existing material.

At the empirical level, Brahman conditioned by cosmic illusion (*māyā*) is Īśvara—the omniscient regulator who orders creation and distributes karmic fruits. This is the efficient-cause aspect. As material cause, Brahman is the non-dual substrate upon which all names and forms depend. The spider issuing and withdrawing its web illustrates a source that supplies both agency and material; clay–pot and gold–ornament analogies show the effect’s dependence upon a common basis.

Advaita, however, does not say that Brahman really transforms like milk into curd. Creation is apparent transformation (*vivartavāda*): the world appears upon changeless Brahman as a snake appears on a rope. Thus Brahman is materially immanent without undergoing alteration.

Sāṃkhya separates unconscious material cause, nature (*prakṛti*), from conscious selves; Nyāya makes God efficient cause and atoms material causes. Rāmānuja also makes Brahman both causes but interprets selves and matter as real modes, not mere appearance.

**Objection and reply:** If the world is only appearance, purposive creation seems downgraded. Advaita replies that causation is valid at the empirical (*vyāvahārika*) level while absolute (*pāramārthika*) Brahman remains causeless.

**Evaluation:** The doctrine preserves non-duality and immutability, but its two-level explanation shifts rather than wholly removes the problem of how plurality appears.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both causes, uses suitable examples, distinguishes appearance from transformation and compares rival systems.

**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `bhatta-prabhakara-absence`

**Demand decoding:** Separate the ontological question from the epistemological question, reconstruct both views using an example, expose each view’s burden, and judge.

**Argument structure:** “no jar on floor” → Bhāṭṭa real absence + independent non-cognition → Prābhākara qualified locus perceived without extra entity/pramāṇa → determinacy versus economy.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Bhaṭṭa and Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā agree that negative judgements are meaningful but disagree over whether non-existence (*abhāva*) is an independent object and whether it requires a distinct means of knowledge.

For Kumārila’s Bhaṭṭa school, “the jar is absent on the floor” presents a real negative fact—the absence of a specific counterpositive in a locus. Perception cannot generate this cognition because no present jar contacts the senses; inference lacks an independently known sign. Therefore non-cognition (*anupalabdhi*) is a separate means of valid knowledge: under conditions fit for perceiving a jar, its non-apprehension reveals its absence.

Prābhākara rejects both additions. One directly apprehends the floor as unqualified by a jar—“the floor-alone” or jar-less locus. Absence need not be reified as a separate category, and ordinary perception suffices. The negative expression records the contrast between the perceived locus and an expected qualifier.

**Bhāṭṭa objection:** Mere perception of a floor does not explain the determinate content “absence of this jar”; expectation and non-apprehension do explanatory work. **Prābhākara reply:** Positing a negative entity and a new *pramāṇa* multiplies ontology and epistemology when qualified perception explains practice.

Nyāya accepts *abhāva* as real but generally knows it perceptually through a qualifier–qualified relation, showing that Bhāṭṭa ontology need not entail Bhāṭṭa epistemology.

**Evaluation:** Prābhākara has the advantage of economy; Bhaṭṭa better explains why absence-cognition is object-specific and depends upon suitable conditions. The dispute turns on whether determinacy warrants a distinct negative object and cognitive instrument.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes nature from knowledge, gives both theories, cross-locates Nyāya and evaluates the exact trade-off.

### Q7(a). Why does Śaṅkara consider Sāṃkhya Philosophy as his chief opponent (pradhāna malla)? Examine his arguments against Sāṃkhya Philosophy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sāṃkhya](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/samkhya.md) → Śaṅkara’s critique · primordial nature and dualism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Sāṃkhya—nature and selves; Vedānta—Śaṅkara · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Śaṅkara and Sāṃkhya · chief opponent (*pradhāna-malla*) · unconscious cause, dualism and liberation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Secondary route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `shankara-samkhya-chief-opponent`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why Sāṃkhya, rather than crude materialism, is the strongest rival; organise Śaṅkara’s scriptural and rational arguments; allow Sāṃkhya replies; compare residual problems.

**Argument structure:** shared liberation project + rival world-ground → intelligent Brahman versus unconscious primordial nature → purpose, initiation and relation problems → plurality and bondage difficulties → Advaita’s own illusion burden.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Śaṅkara treats Sāṃkhya as the “chief wrestler” (*pradhāna-malla*) because it offers Advaita’s strongest systematic rival: a comprehensive account of world, bondage and liberation based on unconscious primordial nature (*pradhāna/prakṛti*) and many conscious selves (*puruṣas*).

Sāṃkhya cannot be dismissed as crude materialism. It accepts an unmanifest cause underlying plurality, discriminative knowledge as liberating, and the inadequacy of ordinary ego-consciousness. Its transformation theory (*satkāryavāda* and *pariṇāmavāda*) explains continuity between cause and effect. Precisely this proximity makes it a serious opponent to Brahman-causation.

Śaṅkara’s principal arguments are:

1. **Scriptural:** Upaniṣadic creation passages describe the source as seeing, knowing and willing; they cannot primarily denote unconscious *pradhāna*.
2. **Intelligence and order:** An unconscious equilibrium of three qualities (*guṇas*) cannot purposively generate an ordered cosmos serving experience and liberation.
3. **Initiation problem:** If nature is originally in equilibrium and self-sufficient, what disturbs it? An inactive self cannot act upon it.
4. **Relation problem:** Independent, radically different *puruṣa* and *prakṛti* cannot enter a relation. Conjunction compromises independence; no relation leaves evolution unexplained.
5. **Analogy failure:** Milk flowing for a calf and the blind–lame pair already presuppose organic order, desire or communication absent from the two principles.
6. **Plurality of selves:** Qualitatively identical, attributeless witnesses lack an intrinsic differentiator; bodily differences establish streams of experience, not necessarily numerically distinct consciousnesses.
7. **Bondage–liberation paradox:** If *puruṣa* is eternally inactive and untouched, it is never really bound; if nature alone is bound and released, liberation is misattributed.
8. **Causal limitation:** Real transformation makes the ultimate material cause mutable and non-conscious, whereas Advaita locates changing names and forms at the empirical level.

**Sāṃkhya replies:** Natural transformation need not be consciously planned; the qualities possess inherent causal tendencies. Proximity to consciousness explains manifestation without interaction, as a crystal appears coloured beside a flower. Multiple selves are required by distinct births, deaths and karmic histories. Advaita’s appeal to illusion (*māyā*) may be less intelligible than real transformation.

**Evaluation:** Śaṅkara’s strongest criticism is the coordination problem: Sāṃkhya does not fully explain how an unconscious independent cause evolves for wholly inactive independent selves. Yet Advaita resolves this through empirical appearance, thereby inheriting its own locus and status problems concerning ignorance. Sāṃkhya is the chief opponent because defeating it requires Advaita to defend both intelligent causality and non-duality at their deepest points.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “why chief,” develops eight arguments, gives Sāṃkhya’s best replies and compares the costs of both systems.

### Q7(b). Explain the nature of God and its role in Kaivalya in yoga philosophy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Yoga](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/yoga.md) → special self · devotion to God · isolation/liberation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Yoga—God; liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Patañjali · God (*Īśvara*) · devotion (*Īśvara-praṇidhāna*) and isolation (*kaivalya*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Yoga](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Yoga.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `yoga-isvara-kaivalya`

**Demand decoding:** Define God precisely, distinguish Yoga’s God from creator and liberated selves, trace the practical causal route to liberation, and assess redundancy.

**Argument structure:** special self untouched by affliction/action → timeless teacher and object of sacred syllable → devotion weakens obstacles and deepens absorption → discriminative knowledge liberates → God aids but does not absorb self.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In Pātañjala Yoga, God (*Īśvara*) is a special self (*puruṣa-viśeṣa*) eternally untouched by afflictions, action, karmic fruition and latent impressions; his role in isolation (*kaivalya*) is uniquely efficacious but instrumental.

Unlike ordinary selves, Īśvara was never bound. He contains the unsurpassed seed of omniscience, is the timeless teacher of ancient teachers, and is signified by the sacred syllable (*praṇava*, “Oṃ”). He is neither nature (*prakṛti*) nor simply a previously liberated self. The *Yoga-sūtra* does not straightforwardly make him the material creator or the providential creator-God of Nyāya.

Devotion to God (*Īśvara-praṇidhāna*) calms egoism, removes obstacles, concentrates the mind and facilitates cognitive absorption (*samādhi*). The sequence is:

*devotion → reduced afflictions and distraction → deeper absorption → discriminative discernment (*viveka-khyāti*) → cessation of nature’s purpose for the self → kaivalya.*

God does not fuse with or absorb the individual self. Kaivalya remains the isolation of consciousness from nature through discriminative knowledge and cessation of mental modifications.

**Objection and reply:** If discrimination suffices, Īśvara appears redundant. Yoga replies that multiple practices are admitted, but devotion provides an exceptionally direct support and an ideal of eternally free consciousness.

**Evaluation:** Īśvara distinguishes Yoga from atheistic Sāṃkhya while leaving its dualist liberation structure intact; his soteriological importance is practical rather than constitutively salvific.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines God through the canonical attributes and relates him to, without confusing him with, kaivalya.

### Q7(c). Is Jaina philosophy pluralistic and realistic? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → pluralistic realism · substances, qualities and modes · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Jainism—ontology; relativism and sevenfold predication · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Jaina philosophy · plurality, realism and non-one-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `jaina-pluralistic-realism`

**Demand decoding:** Establish both labels from Jaina ontology, connect perspectival logic to objective reality, answer contradiction and relativism objections, and qualify.

**Argument structure:** many irreducible substances → mind-independent qualities and modes → permanence-with-change → partial standpoints and conditional predication → realism without epistemic absolutism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Jaina philosophy is best described as pluralistic realism with epistemic non-absolutism: reality contains many mind-independent substances, while finite judgements disclose only aspects of them.

It is **pluralistic** because it recognises infinitely many individual living substances (*jīvas*) and five kinds of non-living substance (*ajīva*): matter (*pudgala*), the media of motion and rest (*dharma*, *adharma*), space (*ākāśa*) and time (*kāla*). No single substance reduces the others. It is **realistic** because substances, qualities and modes (*dravya-guṇa-paryāya*) exist independently of cognition. Each real possesses permanence, origination and cessation: substance persists while modes change.

Non-one-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) follows from this complex reality. Standpoints (*naya*) select genuine aspects, and conditioned predication (*syādvāda*) prevents a partial truth from being absolutised. Sevenfold predication (*saptabhaṅgī*) indexes affirmation, negation and inexpressibility to different respects, times or modes; it does not make truth subjective.

**Objections:** Śaṅkara argues that opposed predicates threaten non-contradiction, while Buddhists deny enduring substances beneath modes. A relativist worry asks whether *anekāntavāda* is itself absolutely true. Jainas reply that contradictions disappear once respects are specified and that omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*) comprehends the whole.

**Evaluation:** The system successfully combines objective plurality with intellectual humility. Its unresolved burdens are the coherence of an omniscient standpoint and the metaphysical unity of permanence with continual modal change.

**Why this earns marks:** It proves both classifications, integrates logic and ontology, and critically addresses the strongest objections.

### Q8(a). Discuss the idea of Bimba-pratibimbavāda as presented in Vedānta philosophy along with its soteriological significance.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Advaita · original–reflection theory · self and liberation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Vedānta—Advaita; self, Brahman and liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: later Advaita · original–reflection theory (*bimba-pratibimba-vāda*) · individual, God and liberation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `vedanta-bimba-pratibimba-soteriology`

**Demand decoding:** Explain original, reflection and reflecting media; distinguish individual and cosmic reflections; compare other Advaita models; derive bondage and liberation; assess the analogy’s limits.

**Argument structure:** one consciousness → differently conditioned reflecting media → apparent plurality of God and selves → defects belong to medium, not original → knowledge removes misidentification → already-attained identity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The original–reflection theory (*bimba-pratibimba-vāda*) is an Advaita explanatory model in which one undivided consciousness appears as many finite centres without undergoing real division. Its principal purpose is soteriological: to explain how the apparently bound individual is in truth non-different from Brahman.

The **original** (*bimba*) is pure consciousness, self-luminous and unaffected. The **reflection** (*pratibimba*) is consciousness as manifested through a reflecting medium. In influential later formulations, consciousness reflected in cosmic illusion (*māyā*) appears as Īśvara, omniscient ruler of the empirical order, while consciousness reflected in individual ignorance or internal organ (*avidyā/antaḥkaraṇa*) appears as the finite individual (*jīva*).

The mirror analogy performs several functions:

1. one face appears as many images in many mirrors, explaining plurality without division;
2. every image depends upon the original, while the original does not depend upon any image;
3. differences between individuals arise from differences in reflecting media;
4. defects, movement and impurity belong to the mirror or image, not to the face—preserving Brahman’s non-attachment.

The model should not be attributed uniformly to every Advaitin or treated as physical optics. It differs from limitation theory (*avaccheda-vāda*), where one consciousness appears divided like space in pots, and semblance theory (*ābhāsa-vāda*), which emphasises appearance. These models share the denial that individuality fragments Brahman ultimately.

**Soteriological significance:** Bondage arises when the individual identifies with the limited reflection—body, mind, agency and enjoyership—rather than the original consciousness. Hearing, reflection and contemplation upon great sentences (*mahāvākyas*) remove ignorance. Liberation is therefore not a journey of the image into the original or a new union between two substances. It is recognition of ever-present identity—“attainment of what is already attained” (*prāptasya prāptiḥ*). This supports liberation while living (*jīvanmukti*): bodily and mental conditions may continue, but false ownership is sublated.

**Objections and replies:** Rāmānuja and Madhva argue that the model reduces real individuality, devotion and grace to provisional appearance. It is also unclear how an unconscious medium can “reflect” consciousness or how a reflection itself can be conscious. Advaita replies that the analogy is not causal optics; it expresses dependence, appearance and non-affection. Consciousness is never produced—the mind manifests it.

**Evaluation:** The theory is strongest as a grammar of de-identification: it reconciles one consciousness, empirical diversity and liberation. It is weaker as a literal metaphysical mechanism and must be used as one later Advaita model rather than an uncontested doctrine of Śaṅkara.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains all elements, compares internal models, derives the liberation route and includes historical and philosophical qualifications.

### Q8(b). ‘Both Ascetic and materialist are partial in their negation of each other’. Explain Sri Aurobindo’s integral philosophy in the light of the above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sri Aurobindo](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/sri-aurobindo.md) → integral philosophy · Spirit and Matter · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Sri Aurobindo—evolution, involution and integral yoga · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sri Aurobindo · ascetic denial, materialist denial and integral non-dualism · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Aurobindo](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Aurobindo.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aurobindo-ascetic-materialist-integration`

**Demand decoding:** Identify what each pole sees and denies, reconstruct Aurobindo’s metaphysical reconciliation and practical transformation, then test whether integration is demonstrated.

**Argument structure:** ascetic affirms Spirit/negates world → materialist affirms Matter/reduces Spirit → dynamic existence-consciousness-bliss → involution makes evolution possible → integral yoga transforms life.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Sri Aurobindo, asceticism and materialism are partial because each absolutises one pole of a reality whose truth is the integral relation of Spirit and Matter.

The **ascetic denial** rightly discovers transcendent Spirit and the possibility of release, but treats earthly life, body and multiplicity as obstacles or lower appearance. The **materialist denial** rightly insists upon the reality of embodied nature and empirical process, but reduces consciousness and spiritual experience to products of matter.

Aurobindo’s alternative begins from dynamic existence-consciousness-bliss (*Saccidānanda*). Consciousness is also force (*cit-śakti*); Matter is not an alien second substance but Spirit self-concealed in the Inconscient. **Involution** is the descent or concealment of consciousness through Supermind, mind and life into Matter. **Evolution** is its progressive re-emergence as life, mind and ultimately supramental consciousness. Supermind mediates unity and multiplicity without cancelling either.

Integral Yoga makes this synthesis practical. It unites knowledge, devotion and works; ascent opens the individual to higher consciousness, while descent transforms mind, life and body. Psychic, spiritual and supramental transformations aim not at world-flight but a divine life on earth.

**Objection and reply:** The emergence of mind does not empirically prove prior involution or an inevitable supramental future. Aurobindo replies that evolution becomes intelligible only if higher powers are already involved, though this remains a metaphysical inference.

**Evaluation:** The two positions are not reconciled by compromise but by locating Matter within Spirit’s manifestation. The synthesis is philosophically ambitious and ethically world-affirming, but its teleology exceeds empirical verification.

**Why this earns marks:** It decodes both negations and explains the integral answer through named metaphysical and yogic doctrines.

### Q8(c). Is Buddhist notion of Nirvāṇa in consonance with their conception of Kṣaṇikavāda (momentariness) and Nairātmyavāda (no-soul theory)? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → liberation, momentariness and no-self · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Buddhism—momentariness, no-self and liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Buddhism · cessation (*nirvāṇa*) · momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*) and no-self (*nairātmyavāda*) · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `buddhist-nirvana-momentariness-no-self`

**Demand decoding:** Test compatibility with both doctrines separately, distinguish conditioned from unconditioned or cessation interpretations, answer the “who is liberated?” objection, and add Madhyamaka qualification.

**Argument structure:** conditioned stream without substance → craving and ignorance arise dependently → causes can cease → liberation needs no eternal owner → nirvāṇa must not be reified as permanent self-like entity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nirvāṇa is consistent with momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*) and no-self (*nairātmyavāda*) when understood as cessation of the causal conditions of suffering, not as an eternal soul entering a permanent substance.

Momentariness applies to conditioned phenomena: every event arises dependently and ceases. This does not prevent final cessation; rather, because craving, ignorance and karmic formations are conditioned, removal of their causes can terminate the suffering-series. Early and Abhidharma traditions may call nirvāṇa unconditioned (*asaṃskṛta*), while a cautious philosophical formulation treats it as the cessation of greed, hatred, delusion and renewed becoming—not another enduring entity within the temporal stream.

No-self denies an immutable owner behind the five aggregates, not causal continuity. The conventional person who practises is a dependently connected stream; later wholesome moments arise from earlier effort just as one flame conditions another. Liberation occurs without a numerically identical substance appropriating it.

**Objections:** If nirvāṇa is permanent, it appears to violate momentariness; if momentary, it seems non-final. If there is no self, “who” attains it? **Reply:** The first dilemma wrongly applies a thesis about conditioned existents to cessation or the unconditioned; the second assumes that responsibility and continuity require substance rather than causal dependence.

Madhyamaka adds that both saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are empty of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*). Their distinction is soteriological—ignorance versus insight—not a contrast between two self-existing realms.

**Evaluation:** The three doctrines are coherent within a process ontology. Tension arises only when nirvāṇa is reified as a positive eternal thing or ordinary language about an “attainer” is mistaken for commitment to a permanent self.

**Why this earns marks:** It handles both compatibility tests, the conditioned/unconditioned distinction, personal continuity and Madhyamaka without treating nirvāṇa as annihilation.
