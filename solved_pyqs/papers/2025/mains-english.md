### Q1(a). “The raw material of cinema is life”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Cinema, representation and lived experience · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Reflective essay → Cinema, representation and lived experience  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-cinema-and-life.md`

**Model essay**

Cinema may appear to be made of cameras, actors, scripts, sets and sound. Its deeper raw material, however, is life itself. Human relationships, social conflicts, private dreams, ordinary routines and historical upheavals provide the substance from which films are shaped. Even the most fantastic cinema remains intelligible only because it draws upon emotions and choices that audiences recognise.

Life enters cinema first through observation. A filmmaker notices how people speak, hesitate, celebrate, deceive, endure loss or confront injustice. These details give a film credibility. Indian cinema has repeatedly drawn strength from such observation: the hardship of rural families, the loneliness of migration, the tensions within a joint family, the aspirations of young people and the inequalities of caste, class and gender. When these realities are truthfully rendered, the screen becomes a mirror in which society sees both its familiar face and its concealed wounds.

Yet cinema does not merely copy life. Raw material must be selected, arranged and transformed. Daily existence is continuous and untidy, whereas a film gives it pattern through plot, character, image and rhythm. The filmmaker condenses years into hours, places separate incidents beside one another and uses music or silence to deepen their meaning. Thus, cinema is not a mechanical record of reality; it is an interpretation of reality.

This transformation allows cinema to reveal truths that bare facts may miss. A fictional character can embody the experience of thousands. A domestic quarrel can expose a larger conflict between tradition and freedom. Comedy can uncover social absurdity, while tragedy can make distant suffering emotionally immediate. Documentary cinema works closer to recorded actuality, but it too depends on framing, editing and point of view. Every film therefore carries both life and the maker’s understanding of life.

Cinema also returns to the world from which it came. Films influence speech, fashion, aspiration and public debate. They can create sympathy for neglected groups, question prejudice and preserve memories that might otherwise disappear. At the same time, careless representation can reinforce stereotypes, glorify violence or turn poverty into spectacle. Since audiences may mistake repetition for truth, filmmakers bear a responsibility towards the lives they portray.

The relationship is also historical. Films preserve streets, clothes, gestures and social attitudes that later generations can study. They record how an age imagined romance, authority, work and nationhood, even when the story itself is fictional. What a society permits on screen—and what it censors or refuses to see—can be as revealing as the images it celebrates.

The statement applies even to fantasy and science fiction. Imaginary worlds may contain impossible machines or supernatural beings, but their conflicts usually concern recognisable desires—power, love, fear, freedom, belonging and survival. Without this human foundation, visual spectacle soon becomes empty. The audience enters an invented world because it discovers something of its own life there.

Technological change has expanded cinema’s tools, not replaced its source. Digital effects can create entire universes, but they cannot manufacture emotional truth. A modest film rooted in closely observed experience may endure longer than an expensive production without human insight.

Life is therefore cinema’s inexhaustible quarry. The artist cuts, shapes and polishes what experience provides, but cannot create meaning from nothing. Great cinema begins in life, transforms it through imagination and finally returns it to us with clearer form, deeper feeling and renewed understanding.

**Why this earns marks:** It interprets “raw material” rather than taking it literally, distinguishes representation from imitation, and balances cinema’s social power with artistic mediation.

### Q1(b). “No man is an island”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Interdependence, community and responsibility · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Reflective essay → Interdependence, community and responsibility  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-no-man-is-an-island.md`

**Model essay**

John Donne’s assertion that “No man is an island” expresses a simple but profound truth: no human being is complete or self-sufficient in isolation. Individuality is real, but it develops within relationships. Our language, knowledge, livelihood, security and sense of identity are all formed through contact with others. To deny this dependence is to misunderstand both personal achievement and social life.

The dependence begins at birth. A child survives through care and learns to think through a language inherited from a community. Family, teachers, friends and institutions shape habits and opportunities. Even an apparently self-made person relies on roads, laws, public order, accumulated knowledge and the labour of countless strangers. A farmer, driver, engineer, nurse and sanitation worker may never meet, yet their work sustains one another.

Modern economic life makes this interdependence especially visible. A product sold in one city may depend on raw materials, transport, technology and labour from many regions. A disruption in one country can affect prices and employment elsewhere. Climate change, pandemics and financial crises ignore borders. They demonstrate that private safety cannot be permanently secured amid public neglect. Cooperation is therefore not sentimental generosity; it is practical wisdom.

Emotional life also resists isolation. Human beings need recognition, companionship and a sense of belonging. Success becomes meaningful when shared, while grief becomes bearable through sympathy. Prolonged loneliness can damage both mental and physical health. Digital communication may connect distant people, but it cannot always replace attentive presence. A society crowded with users can still contain isolated persons.

The statement does not mean that individuality should disappear. Solitude can encourage reflection, creativity and moral courage. A person may need to stand against the majority when convention supports injustice. Healthy community depends not on conformity but on distinct individuals who contribute their judgement and talent. The island metaphor warns against severance, not against independence of mind.

Interdependence also creates responsibility. If our choices affect others, freedom cannot mean indifference. Pollution released for private gain burdens a whole community; discrimination denies fellow citizens their rightful place; misinformation weakens shared trust. Conversely, volunteering, paying taxes honestly, respecting public property and helping a neighbour strengthen the common life on which everyone depends.

The natural world extends the argument beyond human society. People depend upon rivers, forests, soil, animals and a stable climate, while their consumption alters these systems. The fantasy of complete mastery over nature produces ecological crises that eventually return as threats to food, health and shelter. Human community must therefore include responsibility towards the living conditions shared by present and future generations.

India’s diversity gives the idea special importance. Languages, faiths, regions and customs may distinguish communities, but constitutional citizenship binds them within a shared political destiny. Floods, epidemics and other crises repeatedly show ordinary people crossing social boundaries to assist one another. Such solidarity does not erase difference; it enables difference to coexist without hostility.

At the global level, the same principle demands cooperation without domination. Nations retain sovereignty, yet peace, trade, public health and environmental survival require common rules. A country cannot prosper indefinitely in a world consumed by conflict and deprivation.

No person is an island because every life is connected to lives seen and unseen. Mature independence consists not in pretending that we need nobody, but in accepting help without servility and giving it without pride. Human dignity reaches its fullest expression when individual freedom and mutual obligation sustain each other.

**Why this earns marks:** It examines familial, economic, emotional, civic, national and global interdependence while qualifying the theme through the value of individuality and solitude.

### Q1(c). “Eating habits and lifestyle in Contemporary India”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Social change, food and public health · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Analytical essay → Social change, food and public health  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-eating-habits-contemporary-india.md`

**Model essay**

Food in India has always reflected geography, season, occupation, faith and community. Contemporary India still possesses this extraordinary culinary diversity, but eating habits are changing rapidly under the influence of urbanisation, rising incomes, migration, advertising, technology and new work patterns. These changes have expanded choice and convenience while also creating serious challenges for health, culture and the environment.

Traditional diets were not uniformly ideal, yet many were adapted to local conditions. Millets in dry regions, rice in areas of abundant rainfall, seasonal vegetables, pulses, fermented foods and diverse spices combined nutrition with ecological knowledge. Meals were often prepared at home and eaten at regular times. Today such practices coexist with packaged snacks, food-delivery applications, restaurant meals and global fast-food chains.

The modern lifestyle helps explain this shift. Long commutes, irregular working hours and smaller households reduce the time available for cooking. Students and migrant workers may lack kitchens or affordable fresh food. Ready-to-eat products and delivery services therefore meet a genuine need. Greater mobility has also widened tastes: regional foods travel across India, and people experiment with unfamiliar cuisines. This exchange can enrich rather than erase food culture.

However, convenience often carries hidden costs. Highly processed foods may contain excessive salt, sugar and unhealthy fats while providing little fibre or micronutrition. Aggressive advertising associates such products with modernity, success and childhood pleasure. Sedentary work, screen-based recreation and reduced physical activity magnify the effects of poor diet. Obesity and lifestyle-related diseases can consequently exist beside anaemia and undernutrition, sometimes even within the same household.

Eating patterns also reveal inequality. One section of society debates organic products and specialised diets, while another struggles to afford sufficient protein, vegetables or safe drinking water. Street vendors and inexpensive canteens are essential to urban workers, yet food safety and sanitation remain uneven. Public policy must therefore avoid treating healthy eating as merely an individual choice; income, time, housing, markets and public services shape that choice.

Changing habits also affect family and culture. Shared meals once offered a regular space for conversation and the transmission of recipes. Different work and school schedules now make such gatherings less frequent, while screens may accompany even the meals eaten together. At the same time, migrants recreate home through food, and online platforms help younger people recover regional dishes. Tradition is therefore being rearranged, not simply lost.

There are encouraging counter-trends. Interest in millets, local produce, fitness and nutrition has grown. Consumers increasingly read labels and question misleading claims. Community kitchens, school meals and workplace canteens can improve access to balanced food. Yet fashionable diet advice on social media may be unscientific, commercially motivated or unsuitable for Indian conditions. Health requires informed moderation, not anxiety or extreme restriction.

Environmental concerns add another dimension. Excess packaging, food waste and resource-intensive consumption place pressure on land and water. Choosing seasonal produce, reducing waste and supporting diverse local crops can benefit both health and ecological resilience.

The desirable path is neither an uncritical return to the past nor blind imitation of global consumer culture. Families, schools and public institutions should promote nutrition literacy, regular exercise and respect for regional food knowledge. Clear labelling and responsible advertising are equally important. Cities must make walking, recreation and fresh food more accessible.

Contemporary India’s food choices express aspiration as well as necessity. The task is to combine convenience with nourishment, diversity with scientific awareness, and personal preference with social and environmental responsibility. A healthy food culture should make the wiser choice not only known, but affordable and practical.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects diet to changing lifestyles, avoids romanticising tradition, and integrates health, inequality, culture and sustainability.

### Q1(d). “Folklore as a knowledge system”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Oral tradition and community knowledge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill:** Expository-analytical essay → Oral tradition and community knowledge  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/essay-folklore-knowledge-system.md`

**Model essay**

Folklore is often treated as entertainment inherited from an unscientific past. Such a view is too narrow. Folk tales, songs, proverbs, riddles, rituals, legends, crafts and performance traditions are ways by which communities preserve, organise and transmit experience. Folklore is therefore not merely a collection of old stories; it is a living knowledge system.

Its first function is social memory. Communities that did not depend entirely on written records carried histories through genealogies, ballads and legends. These forms may combine fact with imagination, yet they preserve how people understood migrations, conflicts, heroes, disasters and moral turning points. They reveal perspectives that official archives, usually produced by institutions and elites, may omit.

Folklore also contains practical knowledge. Agricultural sayings encode observations about seasons, soil, rain and crops. Songs sung during work coordinate collective labour and preserve technique. Traditional stories may identify useful plants, warn against dangerous animals or communicate rules for sharing water and forests. Coastal and pastoral communities often remember environmental signs through narrative and custom. Such knowledge is locally tested and transmitted in memorable forms.

Equally important is moral and psychological knowledge. Tales convert abstract values into concrete choices. Through clever animals, flawed rulers and ordinary heroes, listeners examine courage, greed, loyalty, justice and deception. Proverbs condense social experience into portable judgement, while riddles train attention and inference. Folklore can help children encounter fear, loss and conflict within the safety of imagination.

The system is participatory rather than fixed. A performer adapts a tale to a new audience; each generation preserves some elements and alters others. Variations are not necessarily corruptions. They show that knowledge is being interpreted in changing circumstances. Folklore thus belongs to communities of practice, not simply to a single author.

Its form is part of its intelligence. Rhythm, repetition, rhyme, symbol and dramatic performance make complex information memorable where written storage may be limited. Collective retelling also allows listeners to question, correct and supplement a narrative. Knowledge is preserved through social participation rather than deposited in an impersonal record. This explains both folklore’s resilience and the many versions in which the same underlying pattern survives.

Nevertheless, folklore must not be accepted uncritically. Some traditions reproduce caste prejudice, gender inequality, superstition or hostility towards outsiders. Local knowledge may be valuable without being universally valid. Scientific testing remains necessary where health, safety or causal claims are involved. Respecting folklore does not require freezing it beyond criticism.

Modern institutions can learn from it in two ways. First, researchers can document endangered languages, performances and ecological practices with the informed participation of communities. Second, development programmes can communicate through familiar cultural forms rather than imposing messages in alien language. Ownership and consent are essential, because commercial use without recognition may exploit the very communities that preserved the tradition.

Digital media creates both opportunity and danger. Recordings can carry a song or story to new generations, but a performance detached from its context may become a decorative commodity. Preservation should therefore include the language, occasion, performers and social meaning, not merely the outward form.

India’s vast diversity makes folklore an invaluable archive of plural ways of knowing. It complements written scholarship by preserving memory, ecological observation, ethical reflection and artistic intelligence. Treated critically and respectfully, folklore connects inherited wisdom with present inquiry. It reminds us that knowledge is produced not only in laboratories and libraries, but also in fields, workshops, courtyards and collective memory.

**Why this earns marks:** It establishes folklore as structured knowledge, gives several mechanisms of transmission, and qualifies its value through criticism, validation and community ownership.

<a id="2025-mains-english-q02a"></a>
### Q2(a). “What are the observations of the essayist on books in the passage?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Main ideas and contrast · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill:** Comprehension → Main ideas and contrast  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-books-construction.md`

Loosely constructed books tend to have diffuse paragraphs, forcing readers to search across a chapter for the sentences that make one argument; some books make the search fruitless or discourage it altogether. A good book, by contrast, normally summarizes itself as its argument develops. Chapter, section or final summaries enable an attentive reader to trace the materials assembled into the conclusion. Another defect of bad construction is the omission of argumentative steps. Some omissions are harmless because common knowledge supplies them, but others mislead, especially when a propagandist suppresses a challengeable but relevant premise. Whatever the book’s quality, the reader must identify its arguments and state each compactly.

<a id="2025-mains-english-q02b"></a>
### Q2(b). “Enumerate the points of comparison between a writer and a logician, as proposed in the passage.” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Comparison and inference · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill:** Comprehension → Comparison and inference  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-writer-logician.md`

A logician possesses specialist knowledge of the formal structure and names of arguments. A writer who conveys knowledge also uses arguments, but usually addresses general readers rather than specialists in logic and need not state every argument in compact logical form. Correspondingly, readers do not require a logician’s technical competence to understand such a writer. If they grasp the writer’s terms and propositions, the ordinary operation of the human mind enables them to recognise the reasons and conclusions. Logical labels are secondary; the practical ability to follow and reconstruct the argument is essential.

<a id="2025-mains-english-q02c"></a>
### Q2(c). “What are the helpful rules of reading discussed in the essay?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Ordered extraction of instructions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill:** Comprehension → Ordered extraction of instructions  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-rules-of-reading.md`

The reader should make every step of an argument explicit and reduce each single argument to its essentials. Since an argument contains several statements, the reader should connect reasons with the conclusion: after finding the conclusion, seek its reasons; after finding the reasons, determine where they lead. The reader should distinguish reasoning from particular facts towards a generalization from reasoning through general propositions towards a further generalization—that is, inductive from deductive reasoning. Finally, the reader should identify what the author assumes, what is proved by reasoning or evidence, and what is treated as self-evident.

<a id="2025-mains-english-q02d"></a>
### Q2(d). “What does the essayist mean by the phrase ‘self-evident propositions’?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Contextual definition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill:** Comprehension → Contextual definition  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-self-evident-propositions.md`

Self-evident propositions are starting points that neither writer nor reader can deny and that need not be proved before they support further reasoning. Every proof must begin somewhere because demanding proof for every proposition would create an infinite regress. The passage distinguishes these propositions from assumptions, which may be accepted merely by agreement. It also warns that not every self-evident proposition is a trivial truth produced solely by definition.

<a id="2025-mains-english-q02e"></a>
### Q2(e). “According to the essay, what are ‘tautologies’?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Definition with example · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Language skill:** Comprehension → Definition with example  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/comprehension-tautologies.md`

Tautologies are propositions whose truth is already contained in the definitions of their words. They merely draw out what was put into the terms and may therefore appear trivial or verbally contrived. Thus, “a father of a father is a grandfather” is tautological because “grandfather” is defined as the parent of a parent. The passage contrasts this with “the whole is greater than its parts,” which is presented as self-evident but not merely definitional.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official wording:** “Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give or suggest a title to it. The precis should be written in your own words.”  
**Printed passage length:** 863 words  
**Marks:** 75  
**Language skill:** Précis writing → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement  
**Proposed slug:** `qualifying-english/precis-moral-self-transformation.md`  
**Compression plan:** The target is about 288 words. Preserve the passage’s progression: disciplined transcendence of instinct → conscious human progress → self-transformation and self-discovery → morally significant social conduct → acceptance and elevation of human nature. Remove examples, repetition and rhetorical expansion.

> **Study-only title:** *Self-Transformation and Moral Progress*  
> **Examination instruction:** Do **not** write this or any other title in the answer booklet.

**Model précis (about 295 words; untitled examination response)**

Every person begins as an imperfect being still influenced by animal impulses, but can transform them by accepting spiritual demands and disciplining nature. Such redirection of interest and re-education of the unconscious involve temptation and struggle, yet they are necessary for fulfilled personality. All progress is purposive effort; in human beings, however, the universal movement towards perfection becomes conscious. Unlike subhuman evolution, human progress is deliberately willed. Our awareness of the difference between what we are and what we may become produces a search for a guiding principle.

The world can be improved only through self-improvement. Evolution follows no fixed or economical programme, and wrong choices may lead to failure; humanity can therefore retard or hasten progress. Each unique person can contribute a distinctive good by discovering a proper place in the whole. Reflection, meditation and solitude help us choose a unifying centre for our qualities and gain strength to perform our particular role.

Moral conduct is not instinctive or impulsive action. It gives the raw material of human nature a significant spiritual form and embodies a consciously chosen ideal. Though individuals view life differently and need not follow one conventional pattern, they must understand their ideal, believe it possible, plan for it and pursue it with sacrifice. Conduct is moral only when it supports social preservation and harmony, respects every person’s dignity and adapts to others; whatever destroys humanity or promotes discord is immoral. The world’s goal is a harmonious unity permitting each individual’s distinctive fulfilment.

Moral progress consequently combines acceptance with adventure. Human passions and impulses should not be repudiated but accepted as the foundation on which higher character is built. Since human aspiration seeks more than adjustment, life’s ultimate purpose is not merely to preserve the given condition but to elevate it.

**Why this earns marks:** It is close to one-third of the printed length, keeps every major stage of the argument, uses independent wording and obeys the official no-title rule in the model answer.
