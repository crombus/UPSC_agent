## Q1. Discuss the 'corrupt practices' for the purpose of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Analyze whether the increase in the assets of the legislators and/or their associates, disproportionate to their known sources of income, would constitute 'undue influence' and consequently a corrupt practice.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Corrupt practices and candidate disclosure · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Discuss; Analyse  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Corrupt practices and candidate disclosure  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpa-corrupt-practices-disproportionate-assets`

**Demand decoding:** Define the Section 123 taxonomy; isolate the legal test for undue influence; apply that test to disproportionate assets without converting general illegality into an electoral corrupt practice.

**Compact framework:** Section 123 → free electoral choice → asset-growth test → disclosure/anti-corruption route → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 lists election-linked “corrupt practices”: bribery, undue influence, prohibited identity appeals, promotion of enmity, false personal statements, illegal conveyance, excess expenditure and obtaining government servants’ assistance. When proved through an election petition, these may void the election under Section 100 and attract Section 8A disqualification.

“Undue influence” under Section 123(2) means direct or indirect interference with free exercise of an electoral right. Electoral choice is not free when material financial information is concealed.

In ***Lok Prahari v. Union of India* (2018)**, the Supreme Court held that non-disclosure of candidates’ assets and sources of income, including those of associates, constitutes undue influence and hence a corrupt practice. It directed disclosure of income sources and a permanent mechanism to scrutinise disproportionate asset growth and recommend investigation, prosecution or legislative action.

Thus, a disproportionate increase is a serious trigger for scrutiny; the legally decisive corrupt practice is its non-disclosure to electors, proved through an election petition. *ADR* (2002) and *PUCL* (2003) supply the Article 19(1)(a) right-to-know foundation.

**Conclusion:** Unexplained growth must be investigated, while concealment of that material information can vitiate free electoral choice as undue influence.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies Section 123(2) through the controlling *Lok Prahari* holding while distinguishing suspicious enrichment from the proved electoral wrong.

## Q2. Comment on the need of administrative tribunals as compared to the court system. Assess the impact of the recent tribunal reforms through rationalization of tribunals made in 2021.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Administrative Tribunals](../../topics/polity/administrative-tribunals.md) → Rationalisation and independence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Comment; Assess  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Administrative Tribunals → Rationalisation and independence  
**Canonical owner:** [Administrative Tribunals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Administrative-Tribunals.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `administrative-tribunals-2021-rationalisation`

**Demand decoding:** Compare tribunals’ functional need with ordinary courts, then assess—not merely describe—the 2021 abolition and service-condition reforms.

**Compact framework:** Need → constitutional floor → 2021 changes → gains → independence/capacity costs.

### Model answer

Administrative tribunals provide subject expertise, flexible procedure and faster first-instance adjudication in high-volume fields such as public-service disputes. Articles 323A and 323B constitutionalise tribunal legislation, but *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997) preserved High Court review under Articles 226–227 as part of the basic structure; tribunals supplement, not replace, constitutional courts.

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 abolished several appellate bodies and transferred functions mainly to courts. This can reduce fragmented institutions, duplicated administration and low-volume benches.

Its impact is nevertheless mixed. Abolition may merely shift pendency and specialised disputes to already burdened courts. Common appointment, tenure and service-condition rules also raised independence concerns. The *Madras Bar Association* line of cases repeatedly invalidated executive-dominant or tenure-weak arrangements, demonstrating that efficiency cannot be purchased by weakening adjudicatory autonomy.

**Conclusion:** Rationalisation is sound where caseloads justify consolidation, but succeeds only if jurisdictional transfer is accompanied by judges, staff, records and an independent appointments framework.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances tribunal necessity, judicial-review doctrine, efficiency gains and the institutional costs of the 2021 reform.

## Q3. Compare and contrast the President's power to pardon in India and in the USA. Are there any limits to it in both the countries? What are 'preemptive pardons'?
**Primary route:** Polity → [President & Vice-President (Art 52–73)](../../topics/polity/president-and-vice-president.md) → Clemency powers · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Compare and contrast; Define  
**Primary classification:** Polity → President → Clemency powers  
**Secondary classification:** Comparative Constitution → India and USA  
**Canonical owner:** [President and Vice-President](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/President-and-Vice-President.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-us-presidential-pardon-preemptive-pardons`

**Demand decoding:** Compare source, scope, decision-maker and legal limits; then define preemptive pardon precisely as completed conduct pardoned before charge or conviction.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional source → scope → control/review → limits → preemptive pardon.

### Model answer

India’s Article 72 and the US Constitution’s Article II, Section 2 both confer executive clemency, but their designs differ.

| Dimension | India | USA |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Union-law offences, court-martial and death sentences | Federal offences |
| Real decision-maker | President on binding ministerial advice—*Maru Ram* (1980) | President personally |
| Limits | Limited judicial review for mala fides, irrelevant material or non-application of mind—*Epuru Sudhakar* (2006) | Excludes impeachment and State offences; constitutional review is very narrow |

In *Kehar Singh* (1989), India’s President could reappraise the record. In the United States, clemency may precede prosecution, as recognised in *Ex parte Garland* (1866).

A **preemptive pardon** forgives already completed federal conduct before charge, trial or conviction; it cannot licence future crimes. President Ford’s pardon of Richard Nixon illustrates the device.

**Conclusion:** Both systems provide a constitutional safety valve, but India’s is advised and reviewable, while the American power is personal and broader within the federal sphere.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares like dimensions, states limits in both systems and defines preemptive pardon without implying immunity for future acts.

## Q4. Discuss the nature of Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly after the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganization Act, 2019. Briefly describe the powers and functions of the Assembly of the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Union Territories](../../topics/polity/union-territories.md) → Jammu and Kashmir Legislature · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Union Territories → Jammu and Kashmir Legislature  
**Canonical owner:** [Union Territories](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Union-Territories.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `jammu-kashmir-ut-legislative-assembly`

**Demand decoding:** Establish that this is a statutory UT legislature, distinguish it from both a State and Delhi, and specify legislative, financial and accountability functions with central overrides.

**Compact framework:** 2019 reorganisation → institutional nature → legislative field → executive/financial control → limitation.

### Model answer

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, effective 31 October 2019, divided the former State into J&K **with a legislature** and Ladakh **without one**. J&K’s Assembly is a statutory UT legislature resembling Puducherry’s, not a co-equal State legislature.

It may legislate on State and Concurrent List subjects except **public order and police**. Unlike Delhi, land is not generally excluded. It passes budgets and appropriation laws, authorises taxation within competence, questions ministers and holds the Council of Ministers responsible.

However, Parliament may legislate on any subject for the UT, and parliamentary law prevails in case of repugnancy. The Lieutenant Governor retains significant responsibilities, including on reserved subjects and matters assigned by the Act.

**Conclusion:** The Assembly supplies representative self-government, but within a centrally superior UT framework. *In re Article 370* (2023) also recorded the assurance that J&K statehood would be restored as soon as possible.

**Why this earns marks:** It clearly distinguishes State, Delhi and J&K models while covering both powers and structural limits.

## Q5. "The Attorney General of India plays a crucial role in guiding the legal framework of the Union Government and ensuring sound governance through legal counsel." Discuss his responsibilities, rights and limitations in this regard.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Attorney General & Advocate General](../../topics/polity/attorney-general.md) → Attorney General of India · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional Offices → Attorney General of India  
**Canonical owner:** [Attorney General](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Attorney-General.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `attorney-general-responsibilities-rights-limitations`

**Demand decoding:** Connect Article 76 responsibilities and enabling rights to governance, then assess structural and professional limitations.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional status → duties → rights → restraints → independence tension.

### Model answer

Article 76 makes the Attorney General (AG) India’s highest law officer and the Union’s chief legal adviser.

**Responsibilities:** Appointed by the President from persons qualified to be a Supreme Court judge, the AG advises the Government on referred legal matters, represents the Union in Supreme Court proceedings and Article 143 references, and appears in High Courts when required.

**Rights:** Article 76(3) grants audience in all Indian courts. Under Article 88, the AG may participate in both Houses, joint sittings and relevant committees, but cannot vote; Article 105 protects such participation.

**Limitations:** The AG serves during the President’s pleasure, lacks fixed tenure, is not a Cabinet member and cannot advise or appear against the Union. Restricted private practice creates possible conflicts.

**Conclusion:** The office is powerful within its advisory remit, but its independence rests substantially on professional convention rather than tenure security.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates duties, rights and limits while linking each to constitutional governance.

## Q6. Women's social capital complements in advancing empowerment and gender equity. Explain.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Women and Gender Justice](../../topics/social-justice/women-and-gender-justice.md) → Social capital and empowerment · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Women and Gender Justice → Social capital and empowerment  
**Canonical owner:** [Women and Gender Justice](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/05_Women-and-Gender-Justice.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `womens-social-capital-gender-equity`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the mechanisms through which bonding, bridging and linking networks convert collective association into agency, while recognising exclusion and unequal power within groups.

**Compact framework:** Define → bonding → bridging → linking → equity outcomes → limits.

### Model answer

Women’s social capital comprises networks of trust, reciprocity and collective action. It complements legal rights and public schemes by converting individual entitlements into usable agency.

**Bonding capital** within self-help groups creates savings, emergency credit and solidarity. **Bridging capital** connects women across caste, occupation and locality, enlarging markets, information and voice; SEWA illustrates this mechanism. **Linking capital** connects women to banks, panchayats and departments. DAY-NRLM federations and Kerala’s Kudumbashree improve access to credit, livelihoods and local governance.

These networks advance **empowerment** through bargaining power, mobility, leadership and control over income. They promote **gender equity** by offsetting unequal assets, unpaid-care burdens and weak institutional access.

However, elite capture, unpaid group work, restrictive norms and exclusion of the poorest can reproduce hierarchy.

**Conclusion:** Social capital is an enabling bridge—not a substitute for property rights, safety, care infrastructure and accountable state support.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains three distinct mechanisms with Indian examples and a clear qualification.

## Q7. e-governance projects have a built-in bias towards technology and back-end integration than user-centric designs. Examine.
**Primary route:** Governance → [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../topics/governance/e-governance-models-and-user-centricity.md) → User-centric service design · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Governance → E-Governance → User-centric service design  
**Canonical owner:** [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/05_E-Governance-Models-and-User-Centricity.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `e-governance-technology-bias-user-centric-design`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why the bias is structural, show its citizen-level effects, acknowledge gains from integration, and prescribe design corrections.

**Compact framework:** Structural causes → manifestations → benefits → exclusions → redesign.

### Model answer

The bias is often structural: projects are commissioned as IT procurement, measured by portals, databases and transactions, and built around departmental workflows rather than the citizen’s end-to-end task.

Back-end integration produces benefits: DigiLocker reduces repeated document submission, UMANG aggregates services and interoperable databases shorten verification. Yet authentication failure, English-heavy interfaces, inaccessible design, poor connectivity, repeated data entry and no assisted channel or human appeal shift administrative costs onto citizens. Digitising a defective process creates a digital queue.

DARPG’s **NeSDA** framework corrects this by assessing accessibility, ease of use, privacy, end-service delivery, integrated delivery and status tracking—not merely portal availability.

Projects should begin with user-journey mapping, multilingual and disability-inclusive testing, data minimisation, assisted access through service centres, grievance escalation and outcome metrics such as successful completion and time saved.

**Conclusion:** Technology and integration are necessary infrastructure; user-centricity determines whether they become public service rather than administrative convenience.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the bias’s institutional causes, weighs benefits and uses a named evaluation framework.

## Q8. Civil Society Organizations are often perceived as being anti-State actors than non-State actors. Do you agree? Justify.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → State–civil society relations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Agree/disagree; Justify  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil Society → State–civil society relations  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `civil-society-non-state-not-anti-state`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish a structural category from a political label, justify a graded position through collaborative and adversarial roles, and address accountability.

**Compact framework:** Category distinction → constructive functions → watchdog friction → legitimate regulation → verdict.

### Model answer

I disagree with the generalisation. Civil-society organisations (CSOs) are **non-State** because they operate outside formal government; “anti-State” is a political judgment about particular conduct, not their defining character.

CSOs often strengthen constitutional purposes. SEWA organises informal workers; SHG federations expand financial inclusion; community organisations assist disaster relief; social audits improve welfare accountability. Rights groups expose exclusion and represent voices majorities may overlook.

Protests, environmental litigation or policy criticism can create an anti-State perception. Yet dissent under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(c) is compatible with constitutional democracy. Opaque finance or unlawful conduct nevertheless warrants proportionate scrutiny.

The **FCRA, 2010**, administered by the Ministry of Home Affairs, regulates foreign contributions; it should ensure transparent finance without treating disagreement itself as disloyalty.

**Conclusion:** CSOs range from service partners to watchdogs. Their legitimacy depends on lawful advocacy and accountability, while the State must distinguish dissent from threats to sovereignty.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly resolves the category error and balances civic freedom with financial and legal accountability.

## Q10. "With the waning of globalization, post-Cold War world is becoming a site of sovereign nationalism." Elucidate.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [Globalisation, Trade Agreements and External-Policy Effects](../../topics/international-relations/globalisation-trade-agreements-and-external-policy-effects.md) → Sovereign nationalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Elucidate  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Globalisation → Sovereign nationalism  
**Canonical owner:** [Globalisation, Trade Agreements and External Policy Effects](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/11_Globalisation-Trade-Agreements-and-External-Policy-Effects.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `waning-globalisation-sovereign-nationalism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the causal shift from post-Cold-War hyperglobalisation to state control while resisting the false claim that globalisation has ended.

**Compact framework:** Baseline → shocks → state instruments → geopolitical effects → coexistence.

### Model answer

Post-Cold-War globalisation privileged open trade, mobile capital, global value chains and WTO-centred rules. It is now being reconfigured by sovereign nationalism—states reclaiming control over strategic industries, technology, borders and supply chains.

The 2008 financial crisis weakened faith in unrestricted markets; pandemic disruption exposed dependence on distant suppliers; and US–China rivalry, the Russia–Ukraine war and sanctions converted interdependence into vulnerability. Governments consequently use tariffs, export controls, investment screening, subsidies, “friend-shoring” and domestic-content policies. Semiconductor controls and competing green-industrial subsidies illustrate security-led economics. The WTO Appellate Body’s paralysis and the shift toward bilateral or minilateral trade arrangements further reveal weakened multilateralism.

Nationalism also shapes migration controls, energy security and “strategic autonomy.” Yet globalisation has not disappeared: digital flows, finance, production networks and FTAs continue, often on more selective terms.

**Conclusion:** The emerging order is not deglobalised but **selectively globalised**—economic openness is increasingly subordinated to sovereignty, resilience and geopolitical alignment.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the mechanism through named shocks and instruments, while qualifying the stem’s claim.

## Q11. "Constitutional morality is the fulcrum which acts as an essential check upon the high functionaries and citizens alike...." In view of the above observation of the Supreme Court, explain the concept of constitutional morality and its application to ensure balance between judicial independence and judicial accountability in India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Constitutional morality, independence and accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain and apply  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Constitutional morality, independence and accountability  
**Canonical owner:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `constitutional-morality-judicial-independence-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Define constitutional morality as disciplined fidelity to constitutional values, then show how the same principle protects judges from external pressure and subjects judicial power to transparent, reasoned standards.

**Compact framework:** Concept → independence safeguards → accountability duties → present gaps → reconciliation.

### Model answer

Constitutional morality is fidelity to the Constitution’s values, procedures and limitations even when political or popular morality points elsewhere. Ambedkar treated it as a learned disposition necessary for constitutional government; the Supreme Court invoked it in *Government of NCT of Delhi* (2018) and *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018) to emphasise institutional restraint, liberty and equality.

### Protecting judicial independence

- **Claim:** Courts must decide without executive, legislative or private pressure. **Evidence:** tenure and removal safeguards under Article 124(4), charged expenditure under Articles 125 and 146, the Article 121 bar on discussing judicial conduct except during removal, and Article 50’s separation principle. **Analysis:** these devices protect rights and judicial review. **Qualification:** insulation cannot mean immunity from scrutiny.
- The NJAC judgment (2015) treated judicial independence as part of the basic structure. Yet the collegium’s opacity shows that institutional independence alone does not produce public legitimacy.

### Enforcing judicial accountability

Constitutional morality requires:
- reasoned judgments and faithful precedent;
- transparent collegium criteria and disclosure of reasons;
- reasoned recusal and conflict-of-interest norms;
- judicial asset and ethics disclosure;
- accessible court administration; and
- a fair complaints mechanism with graded sanctions below impeachment.

The in-house procedure (1999) and Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (1997) provide ethical guidance, but are non-statutory; impeachment is deliberately difficult and too blunt for lesser misconduct. Accountability must therefore protect due process and decisional independence, not invite executive retaliation.

**Conclusion:** Constitutional morality reconciles the two through **independence in adjudication and accountability in the exercise of institutional power**. Neither executive control nor judicial self-immunity satisfies constitutional government.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the concept, applies it symmetrically and identifies precise safeguards, gaps and reforms.

## Q12. Indian Constitution has conferred the amending power on the ordinary legislative institutions with a few procedural hurdles. In view of this statement, examine the procedural and substantive limitations on the amending power of the Parliament to change the Constitution.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Amendment of the Constitution & Basic Structure](../../topics/polity/amendment-and-basic-structure.md) → Procedure and basic structure · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional Amendment → Procedure and basic structure  
**Canonical owner:** [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Amendment-and-Basic-Structure.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `article-368-procedural-substantive-limitations`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why Parliament exercises constituent power through ordinary institutions, classify procedural hurdles, then establish the judicially implied substantive ceiling and critically justify it.

**Compact framework:** Nature of power → three amendment routes → procedural limits → basic structure → legitimacy debate.

### Model answer

Article 368 places constituent power in Parliament—the ordinary Union legislature—but makes that power procedurally special and substantively limited. Parliament may amend the Constitution’s text; it cannot destroy its constitutional identity.

### Procedural limitations

1. **Initiation:** An amendment Bill may originate only in either House of Parliament; State legislatures cannot initiate it.
2. **Special majority:** Most amendments require a majority of the total membership of each House plus two-thirds of members present and voting.
3. **Federal ratification:** Amendments affecting specified federal provisions—such as presidential election, Union–State executive power, the Supreme and High Courts, Seventh Schedule lists, States’ representation in Parliament and Article 368—also require ratification by at least half the States.
4. **Separate approval:** There is no joint sitting; each House must pass the Bill independently. State ratification is by simple majority, and Article 368 prescribes no referendum.
5. **Presidential assent:** After the 24th Amendment, the President must assent to a duly passed amendment Bill.

### Substantive limitation

*Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) held that Parliament may amend any provision, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot damage the **basic structure**. *Indira Nehru Gandhi* (1975) protected free elections and judicial review; *Minerva Mills* (1980) made limited amending power itself basic; *I.R. Coelho* (2007) subjected post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions to review; the NJAC judgment (2015) protected judicial independence.

Critics call this judge-made supremacy. However, India follows constitutional—not parliamentary—sovereignty; review prevents a temporary supermajority from abolishing democracy or federalism.

**Conclusion:** Article 368 creates a broad but limited constituent power, restrained procedurally by graded consent and substantively by constitutional identity.

**Why this earns marks:** It classifies every limitation, supports the doctrine through its case sequence and addresses the sovereignty objection.

## Q13. Discuss the evolution of collegium system in India. Critically examine the advantages and disadvantages of the system of appointment of the Judges of the Supreme Court of India and that of the USA.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial appointments · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Discuss; Critically examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Supreme Court → Judicial appointments  
**Secondary classification:** Comparative Constitution → India and USA  
**Canonical owner:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-us-supreme-court-appointments-collegium`

**Demand decoding:** Trace the collegium’s judicial evolution, compare both appointment systems across independence, transparency, democratic input and politicisation, and give a reform-oriented verdict.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional text → Judges Cases → NJAC → comparison → balanced reform.

### Model answer

Articles 124 and 217 originally required presidential appointment after “consultation.” Judicial interpretation transformed consultation into judicial primacy.

### Evolution in India

- *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (First Judges Case, 1981) favoured executive primacy.
- *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association* (Second Judges Case, 1993) made the Chief Justice of India’s institutional opinion decisive and created the collegium.
- The Third Judges Case (1998 Presidential Reference) expanded the Supreme Court collegium to the CJI plus four senior-most judges.
- The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, 2014 introduced a six-member commission, but the Supreme Court struck them down 4:1 in 2015 for impairing judicial independence, a basic feature.

| Test | India: collegium | USA: presidential nomination and Senate confirmation |
|---|---|---|
| Independence | Insulates selection from direct party control | Separate institutions check each other |
| Transparency | Criteria and reasons remain limited | Public hearings and recorded Senate vote |
| Democratic input | Weak | Stronger through elected President and Senate |
| Main risk | Opacity, favouritism, delay and narrow diversity | Ideological polarisation and strategic vacancies |
| Tenure context | Retirement at 65 | Life tenure during good behaviour |

India’s system protects adjudicatory independence, as the NJAC judgment emphasised, but judicial self-selection lacks a permanent secretariat, publicly settled criteria and robust diversity mechanisms. The US system is visibly accountable, yet confirmation battles may make constitutional interpretation an extension of party competition.

**Conclusion:** Neither model is ideal. India should preserve judicial primacy while publishing selection criteria and reasons, institutionalising a secretariat, recording diversity considerations and setting appointment timelines—not import partisan confirmation or restore executive dominance.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines exact evolution with criterion-based comparison and a reform proportionate to each system’s weakness.

## Q14. Examine the evolving pattern of Centre-State financial relations in the context of planned development in India. How far have the recent reforms impacted the fiscal federalism in India?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre-State & Inter-State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → Fiscal federalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Examine; Evaluate extent  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Centre–State Relations → Fiscal federalism  
**Canonical owner:** [Centre-State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Centre-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `centre-state-financial-relations-fiscal-federalism`

**Demand decoding:** Trace the shift from plan-era discretionary finance to Finance Commission–GST institutions, then grade the reforms against autonomy, predictability and vertical/horizontal imbalance.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional base → Planning Commission era → post-2015 reforms → gains → re-centralising pressures.

### Model answer

India’s fiscal federalism combines constitutionally assigned taxes with transfers because States carry major development responsibilities but the Union controls more buoyant revenues—a structural vertical imbalance.

### Evolving pattern

During planned development, the **Planning Commission** and National Development Council channelled plan assistance alongside Finance Commission transfers. This enabled national priorities but expanded discretionary, centrally negotiated grants under Article 282 and blurred the Finance Commission’s Article 280 role.

Since 2015, three reforms changed the architecture:

1. **Planning Commission to NITI Aayog:** ended formula-based plan grants and made NITI an advisory platform; this reduced one discretionary channel but also removed a regular State bargaining forum.
2. **Fourteenth Finance Commission:** raised States’ share in the divisible pool to 42%; the Fifteenth Commission used 41% after J&K’s reorganisation. This strengthened predictable, untied devolution.
3. **GST through the 101st Amendment:** Articles 246A, 269A and 279A pooled indirect-tax sovereignty and created the GST Council. *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022) held its recommendations persuasive, not binding, preserving the federal character of deliberation.

### Extent of impact

Reforms made sharing more rules-based, supported a common market and gave States a collective tax forum. Yet cesses and surcharges under Article 271 remain outside the divisible pool; centrally sponsored schemes impose conditionalities; GST narrowed independent State tax space; compensation disputes and Article 293 borrowing controls sustain Union leverage. Horizontal devolution also produces disputes over population, income distance and performance.

**Conclusion:** Recent reforms improved predictability and institutional bargaining, but fiscal federalism remains incomplete. Fewer cesses, rationalised schemes, transparent borrowing rules and consensus-oriented GST functioning are essential.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces institutional evolution and evaluates both decentralising gains and re-centralising mechanisms.

## Q16. Inequality in the ownership pattern of resources is one of the major causes of poverty. Discuss in the context of 'paradox of poverty'.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Social Justice, Inclusion and Welfare-State Framework](../../topics/social-justice/social-justice-concept-inclusion-and-welfare-state-framework.md) → Resource ownership · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty and Inclusion → Resource ownership  
**Canonical owner:** [Social Justice, Inclusion and Welfare-State Framework](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/01_Social-Justice-Concept-Inclusion-and-Welfare-State-Framework.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `resource-ownership-paradox-of-poverty`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how poverty can persist amid aggregate wealth because control over productive assets, commons, finance and capabilities is unequal; include non-asset causes and policy implications.

**Compact framework:** Define paradox → asset channels → intergenerational mechanism → counter-causes → distributive-capability response.

### Model answer

The “paradox of poverty” is that a society may possess abundant land, minerals, capital, technology or growing national income while large populations lack the command over resources needed for a dignified life. Poverty is therefore not only scarcity; it is unequal ownership and access.

### How ownership inequality produces poverty

- **Land:** Concentrated or insecure holdings deprive small farmers, tenants, women and tribal communities of collateral, bargaining power and stable livelihoods. Land alienation around forests and mines can make resource-rich regions habitation sites of deep deprivation.
- **Capital and credit:** Those without assets face costly informal borrowing and cannot invest in enterprises, irrigation, education or risk protection; wealth then compounds across generations.
- **Human and digital resources:** Unequal access to quality schooling, health, connectivity and information limits Amartya Sen’s capabilities to convert formal opportunity into real freedom.
- **Commons and natural resources:** Loss of forests, grazing lands and water externalises development costs onto communities whose livelihoods depend on them.
- **Political voice:** Ownership often purchases influence over rules, markets and public investment, reinforcing Nancy Fraser’s distribution–recognition–representation cycle.

Yet ownership is not the sole cause. Ill-health, discrimination, unemployment, climate shocks, weak public services and unequal intra-household allocation can cause poverty even where households possess nominal assets. Redistribution without productivity, secure titles, markets or public services may also fail.

Policy must combine land and tenancy security, women’s property rights, Forest Rights Act implementation, accessible institutional credit, quality health and education, social protection and participation in resource decisions.

**Conclusion:** The paradox persists because aggregate resource abundance does not guarantee effective command over resources. Poverty reduction requires redistribution plus capability-building and accountable institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the causal mechanism across several resource types, includes counter-causes and offers a non-reductionist verdict.

## Q17. "In contemporary development models, decision-making and problem-solving responsibilities are not located close to the source of information and execution defeating the objectives of development." Critically evaluate.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Development Processes and the Development Industry](../../topics/governance/development-processes-and-the-development-industry.md) → Subsidiarity and participation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2025 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Critically evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Development Processes → Subsidiarity and participation  
**Canonical owner:** [Development Processes and the Development Industry](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/03_Development-Processes-and-the-Development-Industry.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `development-decision-distance-subsidiarity`

**Demand decoding:** Establish why informational and execution distance causes failure, test the claim against scale, capacity and capture concerns, and propose differentiated subsidiarity rather than indiscriminate decentralisation.

**Compact framework:** Distance mechanism → consequences → decentralised evidence → counter-case → calibrated design.

### Model answer

The statement reflects the principle of **subsidiarity**: decisions should be taken at the lowest level capable of performing the function. Contemporary development chains often separate funders, designers, implementers and beneficiaries, causing information loss and weak accountability.

### Why distance defeats development

- Central ministries, donor boards or CSR committees may standardise solutions despite ecological, gender, caste and livelihood variation.
- Frontline workers transmit data upward, but lack discretion to solve the problem revealed by that data.
- Multiple approval layers delay adaptation and make accountability flow toward funders rather than citizens.
- Target-driven monitoring rewards expenditure or enrolment, not outcomes; intended beneficiaries become data points rather than co-producers.

The 73rd and 74th Amendments, Gram Sabhas, social audits under MGNREGA and community institutions under DAY-NRLM show the alternative: local knowledge can improve beneficiary identification, monitoring and ownership. Kerala’s participatory planning experience similarly demonstrates the value of devolved planning backed by capacity.

### Critical qualification

Not every function should be localised. Epidemic surveillance, river-basin management, national infrastructure and redistribution require scale and inter-jurisdictional coordination. Local bodies may lack staff and finance; caste or gender elites may capture decisions. Donors and central agencies can supply expertise, equalisation and standards that local systems cannot.

Hence the remedy is **differentiated subsidiarity**: central government should set rights, finance and interoperable standards; States should coordinate; local bodies should plan and execute place-specific services. Activity mapping, untied funds, professional support, open data, social audit and downward grievance accountability must accompany devolution.

**Conclusion:** Distance is a major design failure, but proximity alone is insufficient. Development succeeds when authority, information, finance and accountability are aligned at the appropriate level.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both centralisation failure and decentralisation risks, then derives a workable multi-level model.

**Proposed topic slug:** `un-reform-east-west-policy-confrontation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how major-power rivalry blocks reform, but test the stem against North–South, regional and procedural conflicts that cannot be reduced to two alliances.

**Compact framework:** Reform need → East–West confrontation → institutional veto → wider obstacles → pathways and verdict.

### Model answer

UN reform remains unresolved because changing the Security Council redistributes power among states that must approve the change. Its 1945 composition—five permanent members with veto and ten elected members—poorly reflects contemporary Africa, Asia and Latin America.

### East–West confrontation

- The United States and European allies stress a “rules-based order,” while Russia and China criticise selective intervention and seek multipolarity.
- Rival positions on Ukraine, Syria, sanctions, NATO and sovereignty produce vetoes and distrust. Each camp fears expansion may strengthen the other.
- Charter amendment requires a two-thirds General Assembly majority and ratification by two-thirds of UN members **including all P5** under Article 108. Thus every permanent member can block institutional dilution of its privilege.

Ukraine has deepened US–Russia hostility, while US–China competition makes agreement on categories, veto and candidates harder.

### Why the binary explanation is incomplete

Reform also faces:
- **North–South conflict** over under-representation and development priorities;
- competing models—G4 expansion versus **Uniting for Consensus** opposition to new permanent seats;
- regional rivalries concerning G4 candidates;
- Africa’s demand for permanent representation and veto parity under the Ezulwini position; and
- disagreement over whether veto reform should precede or follow expansion.

The Intergovernmental Negotiations and G4 keep reform alive. The **Pact for the Future (22 September 2024)** acknowledged Africa’s under-representation, but endorsement is not Charter amendment.

**Conclusion:** East–West rivalry is a powerful veto-generating obstacle, not the sole cause. Progress requires text-based negotiations, stronger African and Global South representation, intermediate models if necessary, and restraint in veto use—while recognising that reform ultimately depends on P5 consent.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the legal blockage, critically qualifies the alliance thesis and uses current institutional reform mechanisms without claiming achieved reform.
