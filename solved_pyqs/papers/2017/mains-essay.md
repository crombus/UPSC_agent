## Section A — Q1. Farming has lost the ability to be a source of subsistence for majority of farmers in India.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q1`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 1 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Agrarian viability, dignity and structural transformation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `agriculture` `farm-income` `smallholders` `rural-transformation` `dignity` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `farming-subsistence-majority-farmers-india`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Subsistence” means the capacity to sustain a minimally secure and dignified life, not merely to produce enough grain for physical survival. “Lost the ability” points to a structural deterioration in the relation between farm size, costs, prices, risk and household needs. “Majority” requires attention to small and marginal cultivators, tenants, women farmers and rain-fed regions rather than exceptional commercial success.

Misreadings include declaring Indian agriculture universally unviable; blaming farmers for low productivity; reducing the crisis to crop prices alone; romanticising subsistence farming; or assuming that moving everyone out of agriculture is either possible or desirable. The prompt asks why cultivation often fails to sustain cultivators and how viability can be restored during structural transformation.

### Thesis

For a large share of Indian cultivators, farming no longer assures subsistence because fragmented holdings, rising purchased-input costs, water stress, price volatility, weak market power and inadequate non-farm support transfer production risk downward while concentrating value elsewhere. The answer is neither permanent protection of low-productivity agriculture nor abrupt exit, but a farmer-centred transition combining remunerative and diversified production, risk reduction, collective market power, secure rights, ecological renewal and dignified rural non-farm opportunities.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Distinguish food production from a viable farm livelihood.
2. Explain the land, cost, productivity and ecological squeeze.
3. Trace unequal value distribution through credit and markets.
4. Add social dimensions: tenancy, gender, caste and regional inequality.
5. Examine the limits of both price support and migration-only solutions.
6. Build a transition around aggregation, diversification, public goods and non-farm employment.
7. Conclude by making cultivator dignity, not output alone, the test of agricultural policy.

### Counter-view and synthesis

India has moved from chronic food scarcity to large output, created successful dairy and horticultural clusters, and produced many commercially viable farmers. Agriculture therefore has not lost all livelihood capacity. Yet aggregate production can rise while per-cultivator security falls, and islands of success do not negate widespread vulnerability. The synthesis is conditional rather than fatalistic: farming remains viable where institutions reduce risk, farmers capture value and land and water are used sustainably; where these conditions are absent, production may continue while subsistence fails.

### Model essay

A nation may celebrate overflowing granaries while the hands that fill them remain insecure. This paradox captures the contemporary agrarian question. Indian farming has demonstrated remarkable productive capacity, yet for many cultivators the farm does not reliably finance food, health, education, housing and recovery from a bad season. Farming continues as an occupation, but often ceases to function as a secure livelihood.

The distinction between production and subsistence is crucial. A farmer may harvest a crop and still incur a loss after paying for seed, fertiliser, irrigation, machinery, rent and credit. A household may consume some of its produce yet borrow for medical treatment or a daughter’s education. Subsistence in a modern society cannot mean bare caloric survival; it means the capability to live with minimum security and dignity.

The first pressure comes from the arithmetic of land. Repeated subdivision has made many holdings too small to support a family through undiversified cultivation. Smallness itself is not destiny—intensive horticulture, dairying and allied activities can succeed on limited land—but it restricts mechanisation choices, storage, bargaining power and the ability to absorb loss. Informal tenants face an added problem: insecure or unrecorded cultivation can exclude them from institutional credit, insurance and public support.

The second pressure is the cost-risk squeeze. Cultivation increasingly depends on purchased inputs, while returns remain exposed to weather, pests and price movements. Irrigation can stabilise production, but groundwater depletion and energy dependence create new vulnerabilities. Rain-fed farmers face greater climatic uncertainty. A bumper harvest may depress prices; a failed harvest destroys output. Thus the cultivator can lose in opposite seasons for opposite reasons.

Markets often deepen this vulnerability. Perishable produce cannot wait indefinitely for a favourable buyer. Inadequate grading, storage, processing and transport force distress sales. Long value chains may reward processing and retail more reliably than primary production. The individual small farmer negotiates from weakness against better-informed and better-capitalised actors. Indebtedness then becomes not merely a financial fact but a transfer of future choice.

The crisis is also socially differentiated. Women perform major agricultural work but frequently lack recognised title, credit access and decision-making power. Dalit labourers and land-poor households experience the farm economy largely through uncertain wages. Regions connected to irrigation, procurement, roads and markets enjoy opportunities unavailable to dryland districts. The phrase “Indian farmer” can therefore conceal inequalities among owners, tenants, labourers, pastoralists and women cultivators.

Public policy has protected food security and encouraged production, but output-centred policy can miss livelihood viability. Minimum support prices are important where procurement is effective, yet they cannot by themselves reach every crop, region or cultivator. Loan waivers may provide immediate relief but do not correct recurring cost, market and ecological risks. Crop insurance is useful only when assessment is credible and settlement timely. A single instrument cannot repair a system.

Nor is mass exit a sufficient answer. Structural transformation does require fewer people to depend solely on low-productivity cultivation. But migration without skills, affordable housing or secure urban work merely relocates insecurity. Rural youth should be able to choose between viable agriculture and dignified non-farm employment, rather than flee one form of precarity for another.

Renewal begins by treating the farm as part of a livelihood system. Farmer producer organisations and cooperatives can aggregate inputs, knowledge, processing and marketing. India’s dairy cooperatives show how small producers can capture greater value when institutions overcome scale disadvantages. Diversification toward pulses, oilseeds, livestock, fisheries, agroforestry and locally suitable horticulture can spread risk, provided markets and extension support exist.

Public investment must supply what individual cultivators cannot: watershed development, irrigation efficiency, soil health, research, reliable electricity, rural roads, storage, digital and physical market access, and accountable extension. Ecological farming practices should reduce input dependence and restore soil and water, but they must be judged by evidence and supported during transition rather than imposed as slogans.

Finally, secure land records, recognition of tenants and women farmers, accessible institutional credit and transparent markets are matters of justice as well as efficiency. Complementary rural enterprises—food processing, repair, crafts, renewable energy and services—can make the household less dependent on one harvest while retaining links with the land.

Farming has not become inherently incapable of sustaining life. It has become unviable where cultivators bear dispersed risks but capture too little of the value they create. India’s task is not simply to produce more or to move people out; it is to enlarge farmers’ agency over resources, technology and markets while creating genuine alternatives. The true measure of agricultural success is not the height of the grain heap, but whether the cultivator can stand beside it without fear.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes output from livelihood viability, analyses mechanisms across land, costs, ecology, markets and social structure, and offers a qualified transition rather than a single-instrument cure.

## Section A — Q2. Impact of the new economic measures on fiscal ties between the union and states in India.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q2`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 2 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Fiscal federalism, reform and cooperative bargaining · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `fiscal-federalism` `GST` `Finance-Commission` `cooperative-federalism` `accountability` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `new-economic-measures-union-state-fiscal-ties`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“New economic measures” is plural and contextual: the transition to the Goods and Services Tax, changes in tax devolution and grants, restructuring of centrally sponsored schemes, the replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog, and greater reliance on rule-based and digitally administered fiscal systems. “Fiscal ties” includes revenue assignment, transfers, expenditure responsibilities, borrowing, negotiation and accountability.

Misreadings include writing only about GST; treating constitutional federalism as a zero-sum contest; confusing administrative consultation with fiscal autonomy; or assuming greater tax devolution automatically means greater usable resources. The essay must assess both the institutional promise and the distributional frictions of reform.

### Thesis

The new measures have shifted Indian fiscal federalism from plan-mediated bargaining and fragmented indirect taxation toward formula-based devolution, a shared national market and institutionalised negotiation. They have widened some forms of state autonomy while pooling important tax powers and creating fresh dependence on compensation, cesses, data and Council decisions. Their democratic success depends on predictable transfers, genuine voice, transparent accounting and a balance between national coordination and states’ unequal responsibilities.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Establish the constitutional logic of vertical and horizontal fiscal imbalance.
2. Contrast the older Planning Commission and indirect-tax regime with newer institutions.
3. Analyse GST as pooled sovereignty through the GST Council.
4. Examine devolution, grants, scheme restructuring and the rise of cesses.
5. Consider state borrowing, fiscal discipline and asymmetric capacity.
6. Test cooperative federalism against centralisation and accountability concerns.
7. Propose predictable, transparent and genuinely deliberative fiscal federalism.

### Counter-view and synthesis

One view sees a unified tax system and common fiscal rules as necessary for an integrated economy; another sees them as erosion of states’ constitutionally meaningful policy space. Both identify real concerns. Fragmentation can impede commerce and tax compliance, while excessive uniformity can ignore regional conditions. The synthesis is “shared rule with protected voice”: national standards where spillovers are large, state flexibility where local knowledge matters, and neutral institutions to manage disputes and shocks.

### Model essay

Federalism is not sustained by constitutional vocabulary alone. It is sustained by the capacity of each level of government to discharge the responsibilities assigned to it. When revenue powers and expenditure duties are unevenly distributed, fiscal arrangements become the working constitution of the Union.

India necessarily contains both vertical and horizontal imbalances. The Union commands broad and buoyant tax bases, while states deliver many services that citizens encounter directly, including health, education, agriculture and policing. States also differ sharply in income, demography, geography and administrative capacity. Fiscal federalism must therefore reconcile autonomy with redistribution and national integration with regional diversity.

The older system managed this balance through the Finance Commission, the Planning Commission, centrally sponsored schemes and separate indirect taxes. It produced coordination, but also overlapping channels, discretionary plan assistance and bargaining that could blur accountability. Recent measures have attempted to replace parts of this architecture with formula-based transfers, institutional negotiation and a common market.

The Goods and Services Tax is the most visible transformation. By subsuming multiple indirect taxes and establishing the GST Council under Article 279A, it created a framework in which the Union and states jointly decide important elements of taxation. This is neither conventional central taxation nor fully separate state taxation. It is pooled fiscal sovereignty. A producer can operate in a less fragmented national market, cascading can be reduced, and digital matching can strengthen compliance.

Yet pooling power also changes autonomy. States surrendered independent room over several indirect taxes and became participants in a collective decision system. Consensus in the Council can represent cooperative federalism at its best, but dependence on common rates and settlement systems may constrain state-specific responses. Revenue uncertainty, especially during an economic shock, can turn technical disagreements over compensation into questions of federal trust.

Changes in tax devolution sought to give states a larger share of divisible central taxes and greater discretion in spending. This can strengthen accountability: a state government that designs its own programme cannot easily blame a distant planner for every failure. The replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog similarly moved away from the old plan-grant role toward consultation and policy coordination.

However, autonomy must be measured by usable fiscal space, not one headline share. Centrally sponsored schemes can still shape state priorities through design conditions and matching requirements. Cesses and surcharges collected outside the divisible pool affect what is available for sharing. Grants remain essential for local bodies, disaster response, ecological services and states with special constraints. A formal increase in untied resources may coexist with practical obligations and unequal revenue capacity.

Fiscal rules add another dimension. Limits on deficits and borrowing can protect macroeconomic stability and future generations. States cannot treat debt as costless, especially where liabilities are hidden through public enterprises. But uniform restraints may become pro-cyclical during recession or disaster, when states need room to protect lives and demand. Responsible federalism therefore requires transparent liabilities and credible escape clauses, not discipline imposed without regard to circumstance.

The reforms also alter accountability. Digital tax administration can reduce evasion and provide better information, but complex compliance can burden small firms and weaker administrations. A common portal does not erase unequal capacity. Technical failures or delayed settlements affect states differently. Capacity-building must therefore accompany harmonisation.

The deepest issue is trust. Fiscal federalism works when rules are predictable, data are shared, consultations are substantive and adjudication is credible. The Inter-State Council, the GST Council, Finance Commissions and Parliament perform different roles, but all must respect reason-giving. States should have meaningful participation before national policies create large expenditure obligations. The Union, in turn, must retain capacity to equalise opportunities across regions and respond to national crises.

Local governments are the often-neglected third tier. Union–state reform remains incomplete if panchayats and municipalities receive functions without timely funds and functionaries. Strong fiscal federalism should travel downward, because many public goods are delivered closest to citizens.

The new measures have not simply centralised or decentralised India. They have replaced some unilateral powers with negotiated interdependence, some discretion with formulae, and some fragmentation with common systems. Their promise lies in cooperation; their danger lies in cooperation becoming dependence without voice. A mature federation does not ask whether the Union or states should win. It asks whether shared institutions allow each to fulfil its constitutional purpose while remaining answerable to the same citizen.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats reform as a change in institutions and bargaining, not a GST summary, and balances integration, autonomy, equalisation, discipline and three-tier accountability.

## Section A — Q3. Destiny of a nation is shaped in its classrooms.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q3`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 3 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Education, citizenship and national capability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `education` `classroom` `citizenship` `capability` `equality` `nation-building`  
**Proposed slug:** `destiny-nation-shaped-classrooms`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Classrooms” represents the whole formative ecology of education—teachers, peers, curriculum, language, assessment, laboratories, play and institutional culture—not only a physical room. “Destiny” is not predetermined fate; it is the long-term character, capability and direction produced through repeated social choices.

Misreadings include reducing education to employability, attributing all national outcomes to schools, treating children as instruments of state power, or assuming that enrolment equals learning. Families, economies and political institutions also shape destiny, but classrooms mediate inherited inequality and prepare the citizens who will operate those institutions.

### Thesis

Classrooms shape national destiny by developing capability, distributing opportunity, cultivating constitutional citizenship and forming habits of inquiry and cooperation. Their influence is neither automatic nor isolated: unequal, authoritarian or examination-driven classrooms can reproduce social hierarchy. India’s future therefore depends not only on access to schooling but on equitable, humane and intellectually alive education connected to nutrition, teacher support and democratic public life.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Begin with the classroom as a miniature republic and capability workshop.
2. Move from literacy to productivity, science and innovation.
3. Examine equality, language, gender and social mobility.
4. Connect classroom culture to democracy, pluralism and ethics.
5. Address health, nutrition, digital access and teacher conditions.
6. Test educational optimism against family, labour-market and institutional constraints.
7. Conclude with education as freedom rather than credential production.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Economic structure, governance, family background and public health can overwhelm what schools accomplish; educated societies may still produce intolerance or unemployment. This objection rejects educational determinism, not educational significance. Classrooms shape destiny when wider institutions create room to use learning and when education forms judgment rather than credentials alone. The synthesis is education embedded in a just social contract.

### Model essay

Every morning, children carrying unequal inheritances enter a common institutional space. Some bring books, nutrition and confidence; others bring hunger, discrimination or the burden of work. What the classroom does with these differences helps determine what the nation later becomes. In this sense, the classroom is not preparation for national life; it is national life in miniature.

The most visible contribution of education is capability. Literacy and numeracy expand a person’s ability to understand the world, claim rights and participate in economic activity. Scientific learning supports agriculture, medicine, engineering and industry. A society that teaches children to ask why, test evidence and revise error creates the intellectual foundation of innovation. A society that rewards memorisation alone may produce certificates without curiosity.

Classrooms also shape economic destiny through the quality, not merely the quantity, of human capital. India’s demographic scale becomes an advantage only when young people acquire foundational learning, adaptable skills and the capacity to learn throughout life. Technology changes occupations faster than a fixed syllabus can follow. Education must therefore develop communication, reasoning, craftsmanship and problem-solving alongside disciplinary knowledge.

Yet the classroom is equally an institution of distribution. Birth allocates children unequal caste, class, gender, language and regional positions. Public education can interrupt this lottery by providing common learning and social mobility. It can also reproduce it when schools are segregated by income, girls face unsafe access, a child’s home language is treated as inferiority, or discrimination lowers expectations. The arrangement of opportunity inside education becomes the social structure of the next generation.

India’s constitutional destiny is shaped there as well. Democracy requires more than periodic voting. It needs citizens able to listen, disagree without dehumanising, distinguish evidence from rumour and see strangers as equal bearers of rights. A classroom where questions are punished teaches obedience; one where humiliation is normalised teaches hierarchy. Conversely, shared work, fair rules and respectful debate make liberty, equality and fraternity lived practices rather than textbook words.

History and social science carry special responsibility. Education should neither erase national achievement nor convert patriotism into uncritical praise. Mature belonging can acknowledge civilisational creativity, anti-colonial struggle and constitutional aspiration while confronting caste, gender injustice and communal violence. A nation confident enough to examine its failures is stronger than one protected by silence.

The teacher stands at the centre of this project. Buildings and devices matter, but a supported teacher interprets the learner, adapts explanation and models intellectual honesty. Administrative overload, insecure appointments and inadequate professional development weaken this role. Reform that mistrusts teachers while expecting them to repair every social deficit defeats itself.

Learning also depends on conditions outside the lesson. A hungry child cannot be educated by pedagogy alone; school meals therefore join nutrition, attendance and social equality. Health, sanitation and safe transport affect participation, especially for girls. Digital resources can widen access, but unequal devices, connectivity and home support can widen gaps. Technology should assist the teacher and learner, not conceal the absence of institutional care.

Assessment reveals what a system values. High-stakes examinations can motivate effort and allocate scarce opportunities, but excessive dependence on recall narrows teaching, intensifies coaching inequality and turns failure into a judgment on personal worth. Multiple forms of assessment, vocational dignity, arts and sports can recognise varied capacities without abandoning standards.

It would be romantic to say classrooms alone determine destiny. An educated young person still needs fair labour markets, responsive government and freedom from prejudice. Families and media may undo habits of reason learned at school. But these limits increase rather than diminish the importance of education: the classroom is one of the few public places where a society can consciously question the inequalities and assumptions it otherwise inherits.

The nation shaped there must not be imagined only as a more efficient economy. It should be a community of capable persons—productive, certainly, but also compassionate, critical and free. When a child learns to solve a problem, cooperate across difference and speak without fear, the country acquires a resource no budget can instantly purchase. National destiny is shaped in classrooms because the future first appears there, not as fate, but as a young person learning that the world can be understood and changed.

**Why this earns marks:** It interprets the classroom broadly, connects capability with equality and citizenship, and qualifies educational power through material and institutional conditions.

## Section A — Q4. Has the Non-Alignment Movement (NAM) lost its relevance in a multipolar world ?

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q4`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 4 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Non-alignment, strategic autonomy and Global South cooperation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `NAM` `multipolarity` `strategic-autonomy` `Global-South` `multilateralism` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `nam-relevance-multipolar-world`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The prompt asks about the *Movement*, not merely India’s policy, and about relevance in a world with several centres of power rather than two Cold War blocs. Relevance must be judged by purpose, institutional performance and adaptation. Non-alignment did not mean neutrality, isolation or equal distance from every power; at its strongest, it meant independent judgment and collective agency for newly decolonised states.

Misreadings include writing a history of NAM without evaluating the present; assuming multipolarity automatically makes non-alignment redundant; treating every flexible partnership as NAM; or ignoring internal diversity and weak cohesion among members.

### Thesis

Multipolarity has reduced NAM’s old identity as a response to bipolar military blocs, but it has not removed the underlying need for strategic autonomy, sovereign equality and coordinated Global South voice. NAM remains normatively relevant yet institutionally underpowered. Its renewal requires a focused agenda—development finance, technology access, climate justice, health, maritime and cyber governance, and reform of multilateral institutions—rather than ritual declarations or reflexive anti-Westernism.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Recover non-alignment’s original context and principles.
2. Explain how multipolarity changes both constraints and opportunities.
3. Assess continuing relevance for autonomy and issue-based coalitions.
4. Examine NAM’s achievements and current institutional weaknesses.
5. Distinguish Indian multi-alignment from abandonment of autonomy.
6. Address divisions among developing countries and alternative forums.
7. Recast NAM as practical, plural and problem-solving Global South cooperation.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Critics argue that diverse interests, alternative groupings and flexible partnerships make a large consensus movement obsolete. This is persuasive if NAM is understood as a Cold War posture or summit ritual. Yet smaller coalitions cannot always supply a broad moral and negotiating constituency on systemic questions. The synthesis is layered diplomacy: NAM for principles and broad agenda-setting, issue-specific coalitions for implementation, and independent national partnerships without permanent subordination.

### Model essay

Institutions often outlive the circumstances that created them. Their survival is not proof of relevance, but neither does changed context make their founding purpose meaningless. The Non-Alignment Movement arose amid decolonisation and bipolar rivalry. A multipolar world has altered its environment; it has not abolished the problem of unequal power.

Non-alignment was frequently caricatured as passive neutrality. In practice, its deeper claim was that newly independent states should not surrender judgment to military blocs. Bandung and the later movement linked sovereignty with anti-colonialism, racial equality, disarmament and development. India could oppose colonial domination, advocate peaceful coexistence and still take positions on international questions. Non-alignment was intended as autonomy in action, not absence from action.

Today’s distribution of power differs markedly. The United States remains influential, China has risen, Russia retains strategic weight, the European Union exercises economic and regulatory power, and middle powers shape regional outcomes. Economic interdependence cuts across security rivalry. Countries cooperate with one power on trade, another on defence and a third on technology. This fluidity appears to make a formal non-aligned identity unnecessary.

Indeed, NAM suffers serious weaknesses. Its membership is too diverse for easy strategic consensus. Some members are aligned in practice with major powers; others are divided by conflict. Summit declarations can be expansive but operationally thin. Forums such as the G20, BRICS, regional organisations and issue-based coalitions often appear better suited to concrete negotiation. Nostalgia cannot substitute for capacity.

Yet multipolarity can increase the value of autonomy. More poles create more choices, but also more arenas of pressure. Dependence may operate through debt, digital infrastructure, supply chains, energy, sanctions or technology standards rather than formal alliances. Smaller states need space to diversify partnerships and avoid becoming instruments in another power’s contest. The principle of judging each issue on merit remains relevant.

Collective voice also matters. Many developing countries share concerns about unequal representation in global institutions, access to technology and finance, debt vulnerability, climate responsibility and the terms of trade. They do not share identical interests, but neither do members of other negotiating groups. Coalition is not sameness; it is the organisation of overlapping interests.

For India, this logic appears as strategic autonomy. India can participate in the Quad, work with Russia, engage BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, and deepen partnerships with Europe and the Global South without accepting that one relationship determines all others. Such “multi-alignment” differs in method from classic non-alignment, but it preserves the central refusal of automatic bloc discipline. Autonomy, however, should not become ambiguity about aggression or violations of international law. Independent judgment must still be judgment.

NAM’s moral legacy is also valuable. Decolonisation is formally advanced, but hierarchies persist in rule-making, finance and knowledge. The movement can remind international society that sovereign equality is not exhausted by equal flags at the United Nations. However, credibility requires members to apply principles consistently, including to repression and domination within the developing world.

Renewal must therefore be practical. NAM should narrow its agenda to areas where broad numbers matter: reform of the United Nations and global financial institutions; fair climate finance; resilient health systems; affordable medicines; food and energy security; digital public infrastructure with safeguards; and rules for cyber, outer-space and maritime commons. It should support technical networks and common negotiating positions rather than rely solely on summit communiqués.

It should also work in layers. A broad NAM position can establish equity and legitimacy; smaller groups can implement projects; national diplomacy can retain flexible partnerships. This avoids the false choice between a universal movement and effective minilateralism.

NAM has lost relevance if it means preserving the vocabulary of a vanished bipolar order. It retains relevance if it expresses a continuing democratic demand in world politics: that states outside the great-power core possess agency, voice and room for independent choice. Its future depends on converting that demand from memory into capacity. In a multipolar world, non-alignment cannot be a place to stand apart; it must be a method of standing with others without surrendering judgment.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes the movement from neutrality, evaluates changed polarity and institutional weakness, and offers a realistic layered renewal rather than nostalgia or dismissal.

## Section B — Q5. Joy is the simplest form of gratitude.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q5`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 1 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Joy, gratitude and reciprocal flourishing · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `joy` `gratitude` `flourishing` `reciprocity` `suffering` `ethics`  
**Proposed slug:** `joy-simplest-form-gratitude`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

Joy is more than passing pleasure; it is an affirmative openness to existence, relationship or meaningful activity. Gratitude is recognition that life’s goods are not wholly self-produced. “Simplest form” suggests that joy can embody thanks before it becomes speech, ritual or repayment.

Misreadings include demanding cheerfulness from people in pain; treating gratitude as submission to injustice; confusing joy with consumption; or claiming that gratitude eliminates ambition and criticism. Authentic gratitude acknowledges gift and dependence without denying suffering, rights or agency.

### Thesis

Joy is gratitude in its most immediate form because it receives a good without reducing it to possession and recognises, often wordlessly, the relationships and conditions that made it possible. Yet gratitude becomes ethically complete only when joy matures into care, reciprocity and stewardship. It must coexist with grief and resistance, lest appreciation become complacency.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Distinguish joy from pleasure and gratitude from indebtedness.
2. Explain joy as receptive recognition of gift and interdependence.
3. Move through childhood, friendship, work, nature and public life.
4. Explore Indian traditions of contentment, service and celebration without essentialising them.
5. Examine grief, deprivation and the political misuse of gratitude.
6. Convert inward appreciation into reciprocal responsibility.
7. Conclude with joy as a humble “yes” that enlarges rather than closes moral action.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Gratitude may appear deliberate and moral, whereas joy can be spontaneous, selfish or even cruel. People can rejoice at another’s defeat. Therefore not every joy is gratitude. The proposition is strongest when joy is receptive rather than possessive: delight in a good whose value exceeds the self. The synthesis distinguishes grateful joy from gratification and asks it to generate responsibility.

### Model essay

A child receiving rain after summer heat may run outside before finding words of thanks. A musician absorbed in a raga, a farmer watching a field revive, or a family welcoming a recovered patient may express gratitude simply by being fully joyful. Before gratitude becomes a sentence, it can become a way of receiving the world.

Joy differs from pleasure. Pleasure satisfies a desire; joy often enlarges the person beyond desire. Possession says, “This is mine.” Joy says, “How fortunate that this exists.” It can arise from achievement, but also from beauty, friendship, service and another person’s flourishing. This openness explains why joy may be the simplest form of gratitude.

Gratitude begins with a correction to the self-made individual. No one creates the language in which one thinks, the care that sustained infancy, the institutions that make action possible, or the natural systems that support life. Effort matters, but effort operates within received conditions. Joy acknowledges this dependence without necessarily calculating every source.

In personal life, such recognition humanises relationships. A parent’s care, a teacher’s patience or a friend’s presence can never be fully repaid as a transaction. To receive them joyfully affirms that the gift has reached its purpose. Genuine gratitude does not keep a ledger; it responds by sustaining the relationship and becoming capable of similar generosity.

Work can also hold grateful joy. When labour develops skill and contributes to something worthwhile, satisfaction is not merely a wage supplement. The craftsperson’s delight in good work, the nurse’s relief at recovery and the scientist’s wonder at discovery join competence with meaning. Yet this should never be used to romanticise exploitation. Workers deserve justice, not sermons asking them to be grateful for hardship.

Nature provides perhaps the clearest example. A river, monsoon, forest or clear night is not valuable only when converted into a commodity. Joy before nature recognises a good not manufactured by the observer. Such gratitude can become stewardship: one protects what one has learned to receive as more than a resource.

Public life needs this sensibility. National gratitude need not mean uncritical pride. One may rejoice in constitutional freedom, cultural plurality or collective achievement while recognising the sacrifices and institutions that sustain them. The appropriate response is not complacency but citizenship—preserving liberty for others, paying one’s fair share and improving what has been inherited.

Indian social life contains rich forms of shared joy: festivals, harvest celebrations, music, food and collective service. Their value lies not in a claim that one civilisation owns gratitude, but in the way celebration interrupts isolation. Shared joy turns abundance into hospitality. At its best, it widens the circle of belonging; at its worst, it excludes those marked as outsiders. Gratitude must therefore be tested by inclusion.

Suffering complicates the proposition. A grieving or oppressed person should not be commanded to display joy. Forced positivity can deny real loss and protect those responsible for injustice. Gratitude is morally corrupted when the powerful demand it from the vulnerable. Rights are not gifts from rulers, and survival under deprivation is not proof that deprivation is acceptable.

Yet joy need not deny pain. People often preserve songs, humour, affection and celebration amid hardship. Such joy is not approval of suffering; it is resistance to being wholly defined by it. It affirms that oppression does not possess the entire inner life. Grief and gratitude can coexist because love for what was lost is itself a form of valuing.

Nor is every joy grateful. Triumph at humiliation, compulsive consumption or pleasure in domination contracts the moral self. Grateful joy has a different structure: it receives without degrading another and recognises value beyond possession. Its natural movement is outward. Appreciation of education can become teaching; gratitude for care can become service; joy in freedom can become defence of another’s liberty.

The simplest form is not always the final form. Joy begins gratitude by saying “yes” to a good. Ethical gratitude completes it by asking how that good may be shared, protected or passed forward. When joy deepens into reciprocity, it avoids both self-satisfaction and indebted submission.

Human beings cannot be joyful at command, nor should they ignore sorrow. But the capacity for joy prevents achievement from hardening into entitlement and pain from becoming the whole truth of existence. Joy is gratitude in its simplest form because it is the unforced recognition that life contains gifts. Its highest form is to become worthy of them.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes joy from gratification, protects grief and rights from coerced gratitude, and develops appreciation into reciprocity and stewardship.

## Section B — Q6. Fulfilment of ‘new woman’ in India is a myth.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q6`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 2 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Gender modernity, agency and structural inequality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `women` `gender-equality` `agency` `care-work` `intersectionality` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `fulfilment-new-woman-india-myth`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The quoted “new woman” is a social image of the educated, economically active, autonomous and equal modern Indian woman. “Fulfilment” asks whether substantive freedom has been realised, not whether visible pioneers exist. “Myth” may mean a legitimising story that exaggerates transformation, but the proposition itself must also be tested against real gains.

Misreadings include portraying Indian women only as victims; treating women as a homogeneous category; equating employment with emancipation; blaming tradition alone while ignoring modern market and digital harms; or reading “new woman” as rejection of family and culture.

### Thesis

The fulfilled “new woman” is a myth when exceptional visibility is mistaken for universal freedom and when women are expected to combine paid work, unpaid care, respectability and safety without redistribution of power. Yet the myth is not wholly false: constitutional rights, education, collective mobilisation and women’s achievements have enlarged agency. India’s task is to turn symbolic modernity into substantive freedom through bodily autonomy, equal capabilities, economic security, shared care and voice across caste, class, region and community.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Identify the “new woman” as aspiration, image and contested social category.
2. Acknowledge constitutional, educational, political and professional gains.
3. Contrast visible success with labour, property, care and safety constraints.
4. Add caste, class, tribal, disability and regional differences.
5. Examine media and markets that sell empowerment while disciplining appearance.
6. Address family, men, institutions and women’s own organised agency.
7. Recast fulfilment as freedom to choose among plural lives, not conformity to one model.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Calling fulfilment a myth can erase genuine progress and deny women the authority of their achievements. Conversely, celebrating a few leaders can conceal structural inequality. The synthesis treats the “new woman” as a partially realised democratic project: real enough to demonstrate possibility, mythical when presented as an accomplished social fact.

### Model essay

The image of the “new woman” is familiar: educated, employed, technologically connected, articulate and free to choose. She appears in advertising, cinema, corporate offices, elections and public debate. Yet images can describe reality, prescribe conduct or conceal contradiction. The crucial question is not whether such women exist, but whether most Indian women possess the substantive freedom the image promises.

India’s constitutional beginning was radical. Equal citizenship, non-discrimination, universal adult franchise and reform legislation rejected the idea that women must wait for social readiness before becoming political persons. Women have since entered universities, administration, science, entrepreneurship, armed services, sports, local government and social movements. Self-help groups and elected women representatives have altered household and village decisions. These are not decorative exceptions; they are evidence of agency and institutional change.

Nevertheless, formal entry does not guarantee fulfilment. A woman may earn an income yet lack control over it. She may hold a degree yet leave work because transport is unsafe, care services absent or marriage relocates her. She may occupy public office while male relatives attempt to exercise authority in her name. Freedom must therefore be measured by effective choice, not visible presence alone.

The organisation of care is central. The modern woman is often expected to succeed in paid work while remaining chiefly responsible for cooking, cleaning, children, the elderly and emotional labour. Technology may reduce some drudgery, but it does not automatically redistribute obligation. The “double burden” converts empowerment into exhaustion. Unless men, workplaces, markets and the state share care, equality outside the home rests on inequality within it.

Economic autonomy is similarly incomplete. Women’s work in agriculture, family enterprises and homes is frequently under-recognised. Lack of land and asset ownership weakens bargaining power and access to formal finance. Occupational segregation, wage inequality and insecure informal work restrict mobility. Empowerment cannot mean merely adding women to an economy whose rules ignore reproductive and care work.

Bodily integrity forms another boundary. Violence in homes and public spaces, harassment at work, trafficking and pressures surrounding marriage and sexuality constrain the use of every other right. Protective law matters, but policing, health care, judicial responsiveness and social legitimacy determine whether a right can be exercised without intolerable cost.

There is no single Indian woman’s experience. A privileged urban professional and a migrant worker may both face patriarchy, but not in the same form or with the same resources. Caste can expose women to labour and sexual violence; tribal displacement can destroy livelihood autonomy; disability can intensify dependence; poverty can make legal remedy inaccessible. A model centred on consumer choice may therefore call one woman “modern” while overlooking another’s struggle for land, water, wages or dignity.

Modern markets and media carry their own contradictions. They can expand aspiration and provide platforms for speech, yet often package empowerment as consumption, beauty and individual confidence. The woman is invited to be independent but also endlessly self-monitoring; professionally ambitious but never insufficiently feminine; outspoken but not disruptive. The old duty of obedience can be replaced by a new duty to perform effortless success.

The phrase “new woman” can itself become restrictive. Fulfilment should not require every woman to prefer a corporate career, reject care, marry, remain unmarried or imitate a masculine model of achievement. Freedom means the capability to choose among lives without one choice being imposed by violence, dependence or stigma. A homemaker deserves dignity and social security; a working woman deserves shared care; a single woman deserves full citizenship; a rural woman’s collective action is no less modern than metropolitan visibility.

Men and institutions must change as well. Gender justice cannot be a self-improvement project assigned to women. Schools must challenge stereotypes; cities and transport must be designed for safe mobility; workplaces need fair recruitment and care infrastructure; property and inheritance rights must be effective; political parties must create meaningful voice. Men must acquire the freedom and responsibility to care, rather than treating domestic equality as loss.

Women’s movements demonstrate that transformation is not bestowed from above. Campaigns against violence, struggles for wages and land, local collectives and everyday negotiations have expanded what society considers possible. The “new woman” is not a finished product of development; she is an agent contesting development’s terms.

Thus fulfilment is mythical when an aspirational image is presented as universal achievement. It is real where women convert rights into capability and collective voice. India should neither celebrate prematurely nor narrate women only through deprivation. The proper goal is not one “new woman,” but millions of women free to be different without being unequal. The myth will end not when the image disappears, but when its promised autonomy ceases to be exceptional.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances real gains with structural constraints, centres care and effective choice, and replaces a homogeneous ideal with intersectional, plural freedom.

## Section B — Q7. We may brave human laws but cannot resist natural laws.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q7`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 3 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Human agency, natural limits and ecological responsibility · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `natural-law` `human-law` `ecology` `science` `limits` `resilience`  
**Proposed slug:** `human-laws-natural-laws-limits`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Human laws” are enacted norms backed by social authority; they can be obeyed, challenged, reformed or evaded. “Natural laws” are regularities and limits of the physical and biological world; they cannot be repealed by will. “Brave” suggests conscious defiance, not moral approval.

Misreadings include confusing physical laws with the philosophical doctrine of natural law; treating all disasters as punishment; endorsing fatalism; or claiming science conquers nature. Science increases agency precisely by understanding regularities, while technology can alter exposure without abolishing causality.

### Thesis

Human laws derive force from institutions and can therefore be resisted or transformed; natural regularities impose consequences regardless of consent. Human freedom lies not in escaping nature but in understanding it, adapting intelligently and choosing just institutions within ecological limits. The proposition warns against hubris, yet it must not excuse preventable suffering: hazards are natural, while many disasters are socially produced.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Clarify the difference between normative rules and physical regularities.
2. Show how science works with, not against, natural law.
3. Examine ecological limits, climate, health and resource cycles.
4. Distinguish natural hazards from socially distributed disaster.
5. Explore law, power and civil disobedience in contrast to physical consequence.
6. Address technological optimism and fatalistic environmentalism.
7. Conclude with responsible freedom as alignment of institutions with reality.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Human innovation appears repeatedly to defeat natural constraints through medicine, irrigation, flight and engineering. But these achievements do not repeal gravity, evolution or thermodynamics; they arrange forces through knowledge. Conversely, invoking “nature” can naturalise poverty or hierarchy that are human-made. The synthesis is critical naturalism: respect biophysical limits while refusing to mistake unjust institutions for nature.

### Model essay

A legislature can amend a statute, a citizen can violate it, and a movement can overturn it. No parliament can repeal gravity, command a virus to ignore biology or order a river to flow uphill without applied energy. This contrast gives the proposition its force: human authority can be challenged by will, but nature answers action through consequence.

Human laws are normative. They tell people what ought or ought not to be done and depend on legitimacy, enforcement and social acceptance. They may be just or unjust. India’s freedom struggle openly defied colonial laws through civil disobedience, accepting punishment to expose the gap between legality and justice. Such resistance is possible because law is a human institution capable of moral revision.

Natural laws are descriptive regularities. They do not command in a moral voice or punish disobedience. Rather, causes generate effects whether or not people approve. A bridge collapses if design ignores load; disease spreads when conditions permit transmission; soil loses fertility when extraction persistently exceeds renewal. Nature does not negotiate, but it can be understood.

Science therefore does not conquer natural law; it gains freedom through it. Aircraft fly not by defeating gravity but by arranging lift, thrust and materials in accordance with physical principles. Vaccines work by engaging biological processes. Irrigation redirects water but cannot make water availability infinite. Human ingenuity is most powerful when humility before causality replaces magical thinking.

Ecological crisis makes this lesson urgent. Economies may price forests, fossil fuels and waste as though natural systems were limitless, but accounting conventions do not suspend carbon cycles, aquifer recharge or biodiversity dependence. Pollution can be moved away from consumers, not away from the biosphere. Climate change demonstrates delayed consequence: societies can ignore warnings for years, yet accumulated emissions continue to alter risk.

Still, nature must not become an excuse for social failure. A cyclone is a natural hazard; mass death is shaped by housing, warning systems, evacuation, health care and inequality. Odisha’s improvements in cyclone preparedness illustrate how institutions can reduce mortality without controlling the storm. Drought becomes famine through failures of access and governance, not rainfall alone. We cannot resist the existence of hazards, but we can transform exposure and vulnerability.

The distribution of consequence is also political. Those contributing least to ecological damage may live in fragile housing, depend on rain-fed livelihoods or lack insurance. Respect for natural limits must therefore be joined to justice. Conservation imposed by dispossessing local communities can reproduce harm, while community stewardship can align livelihood and ecology.

Human law can help society internalise natural reality. Environmental standards, land-use planning, disaster codes and public-health rules translate scientific knowledge into collective restraint. Yet law fails when enforcement is selective or short-term gain captures institutions. A statute cannot protect a wetland if authorities steadily permit its encroachment; the eventual flood exposes both ecological and legal failure.

Technological optimism offers a partial objection. Desalination, renewable energy, precision agriculture and new materials can expand effective limits. Such innovation is essential, especially for a developing society seeking dignified living standards. But every technology has energy, material and institutional requirements. Efficiency may even increase total consumption if not joined to restraint. Innovation changes the terms of adaptation; it does not grant exemption from systems thinking.

Fatalism presents the opposite error. To say humans cannot resist natural law does not mean they should passively accept disease, disaster or scarcity. Knowledge converts fixed regularities into a wider field of choice. The monsoon cannot be legislated, but water can be harvested; earthquakes cannot be prohibited, but buildings can be made safer; pathogens evolve, but surveillance and medicine can respond.

The distinction also protects freedom. Rulers have often described human hierarchies as “natural” to shield them from challenge. Caste inequality, gender subordination and poverty are not laws of physics. They are social arrangements, and people may rightly brave the laws and customs that sustain them. Humility before nature must coexist with courage before unjust power.

Humanity’s mature freedom is therefore neither domination nor surrender. It is the capacity to know constraints, anticipate consequences and design institutions that preserve possibility for others and for the future. We may brave human laws because justice sometimes requires their transformation. We cannot resist natural laws because reality cannot be overruled by authority. Wisdom begins when courage is directed against injustice and humility is directed toward the conditions of life.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates enacted law, philosophical natural law and scientific regularity, avoids both fatalism and technological hubris, and makes social vulnerability central to disaster analysis.

## Section B — Q8. ‘Social media’ is inherently a selfish medium.

**Stable ID:** `2017-mains-essay-q8`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 4 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Social media, selfhood and platform power · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `social-media` `selfishness` `platform-design` `public-sphere` `attention` `digital-ethics`  
**Proposed slug:** `social-media-inherently-selfish-medium`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Inherently” makes a strong claim: selfishness must arise from the medium’s defining architecture, not merely from some users. “Selfish” may refer to self-presentation, attention-seeking, personalised information, extraction of user data and commercial optimisation. “Medium” directs attention to affordances and incentives rather than individual morality alone.

Misreadings include compiling only benefits and harms; blaming users while ignoring business models; assuming online solidarity is unreal; or treating all platforms and designs as identical. The core inquiry is whether selfish outcomes are necessary, merely encouraged, or capable of institutional redesign.

### Thesis

Social media is not inherently selfish in purpose, because the same networked capacity can organise care, knowledge and collective action. Yet dominant platform architectures systematically privilege the self: profiles turn identity into performance, metrics reward visibility, personalisation narrows attention, and surveillance-based business models monetise engagement. Its selfishness is therefore structurally encouraged rather than technologically inevitable, and can be moderated by design, regulation, digital citizenship and public-interest alternatives.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Define medium, selfishness and the burden created by “inherently.”
2. Analyse self-presentation, validation and personalised feeds.
3. Move from psychology to platform economics and algorithmic incentives.
4. Examine misinformation, polarisation and unequal voice.
5. Present counter-evidence: disaster relief, movements, learning and community.
6. Distinguish network affordance from prevailing business model.
7. Synthesize through rights-respecting design, accountability and user responsibility.

### Counter-view and synthesis

The strongest counter-view is empirical: people use social media for mutual aid, public education, missing-person searches, disaster response and democratic mobilisation. This disproves strict technological inevitability. However, prosocial content often operates inside systems that still reward attention and extract data. The synthesis locates selfishness neither solely in human nature nor in neutral tools, but in the interaction between human vulnerabilities and designed incentives.

### Model essay

A person photographs a meal before sharing it, measures approval through notifications and encounters a world selected around previous preferences. These familiar acts make social media appear like a mirror held permanently before the self. Yet the same device can locate blood donors, reconnect families after disaster and amplify an unheard citizen. Is the medium selfish by nature, or has it been designed to reward selfishness?

The word “inherently” raises the standard of proof. A quality is inherent if it belongs necessarily to the thing. Social media is distinguished by networked creation, circulation and interaction among users. None of these functions logically requires selfishness. But particular architectures can make self-centred conduct the easiest and most rewarded form of participation.

The profile is the first such architecture. It converts identity into a continuous public project. Users select images, opinions and achievements for an imagined audience. This need not be dishonest; self-expression and recognition are human needs. Yet quantified reactions turn recognition into comparison. The person may cease to share an experience and begin to experience in order to share.

Personalisation deepens the inward turn. A feed designed around previous behaviour offers relevance and convenience, but it can make the world appear as an extension of preference. Citizens encounter fewer common reference points and may confuse familiarity with truth. The public sphere fragments into individually curated realities.

The business model matters more than individual vanity. Where revenue depends on attention and behavioural data, platforms have incentives to maximise engagement. Emotionally arousing, divisive or sensational material can travel faster than careful qualification. The user believes the service is free while paying through data, attention and susceptibility to influence. Selfishness then operates at two levels: users compete for visibility, while companies privately capture value from social interaction.

Such incentives have political consequences. Rumour can outrun verification, harassment can silence participation, and coordinated manipulation can imitate popular opinion. In India’s linguistic and social diversity, content moderation faces scale and context problems. Digital speech can democratise voice, but unequal connectivity, literacy and algorithmic visibility mean that presence is not the same as power.

Nevertheless, the medium repeatedly enables solidarity. Citizens have used networks to coordinate relief during floods and other emergencies, locate medical help, support small enterprises and expose abuse. Social movements can bypass traditional gatekeepers. Students access lectures and communities otherwise unavailable. Diasporas maintain relationships across distance. These uses are not accidental exceptions; they arise from the same low-cost connectivity that supports self-promotion.

The apparent contradiction can be resolved by distinguishing affordance from incentive. Social media affords many-to-many communication. Its prevailing commercial design incentivises frequent, measurable and emotionally activating interaction. Human beings bring both narcissism and sympathy to the platform; design amplifies selected tendencies. The medium is therefore not morally neutral in operation, but neither is selfishness technologically predetermined.

Regulation must address structure without turning accountability into censorship. Users need privacy, intelligible terms, control over data and effective remedy. Platforms should face transparency and due-process obligations concerning moderation and automated systems, while independent oversight protects freedom of expression. Competition and interoperability can reduce the power of closed ecosystems. Public-interest and community platforms can demonstrate models not wholly dependent on surveillance advertising.

Design choices can also change conduct. Metrics need not dominate every interaction; forwarding friction can slow impulsive virality; chronological or user-controlled feeds can reduce opaque manipulation; prompts can encourage reading before sharing. No design will remove malice, but architecture can stop rewarding it automatically.

Citizens retain responsibility. Digital literacy is not merely the ability to operate an application; it includes verifying sources, resisting instant outrage, respecting privacy and recognising the human being behind a screen. Deliberate pauses restore agency in an economy built on reaction. Families and schools must teach attention as a civic resource.

Social media also forces a deeper question about the self. The problem is not that people have identities or seek recognition. A democratic society requires voice. Selfhood becomes selfish when recognition demands the invisibility or humiliation of others. A healthy medium should allow persons to appear without turning every relationship into an audience and every public issue into personal branding.

Thus social media is not inherently selfish, but neither is its selfishness a collection of private moral failures. Dominant platforms industrialise the desire for attention and personalise the common world. Their benefits prove that another digital public sphere is possible; their harms show that possibility will not realise itself. The task is to redesign institutions so that connection rewards contribution, not only display. A social medium becomes worthy of its name when it helps the self enter society, rather than reducing society to the self.

**Why this earns marks:** It takes “inherently” seriously, separates technological affordance from business-model incentives, balances solidarity against extraction, and offers a rights-based synthesis rather than a benefit–harm list.
