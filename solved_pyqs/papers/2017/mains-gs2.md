## Q1. “The local self-government system in India has not proved to be effective instrument of governance.” Critically examine the statement and give your views to improve the situation.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Panchayati Raj](../../topics/polity/panchayati-raj.md) → Effectiveness of local self-government · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Critically examine; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Local government → Democratic decentralisation  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Local service delivery  
**Canonical owners:** [Panchayati Raj](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/23_Panchayati-Raj.md) · [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/12_Local-Governance-and-Service-Delivery.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `local-self-government-effectiveness-reforms`

**Demand decoding:** Test the claim against constitutional gains and operational deficits, then give reforms addressing functions, finances and functionaries.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional promise → achievements → binding deficits → 3F reforms.

### Model answer

The 73rd and 74th Amendments constitutionalised elected rural and urban governments through Parts IX and IX-A. Reservations under Articles **243D** and **243T** widened representation, while Gram Sabhas created forums closer to citizens. Local government has thus deepened democracy in several States.

Yet effectiveness remains uneven:

- States incompletely devolve the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedule subjects.
- Parallel agencies and centrally designed schemes bypass elected bodies.
- Weak own revenues and irregular grants undermine autonomy.
- Staff often answer to State departments, not local councils.
- State Finance and planning committees are frequently ineffective.
- Elite capture, proxy representation and limited administrative capacity weaken participation.

Reform requires activity mapping and guaranteed **functions, finances and functionaries**; timely State Finance Commissions and predictable grants; stronger own revenues; local cadres; empowered Gram/Ward Sabhas, social audits and digital accounts; and functional planning committees.

**Conclusion:** Local bodies are not inherently ineffective; incomplete decentralisation has prevented constitutional institutions from becoming governments in substance.

**Why this earns marks:** It qualifies the proposition, identifies institutional causes and links each reform to the 3F deficit.

## Q2. Critically examine the Supreme Court’s judgement on ‘National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014’ with reference to appointment of judges of higher judiciary in India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial appointments and NJAC judgment · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Critically examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Appointment of higher judges  
**Canonical owners:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/18_Supreme-Court.md) · [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Amendment-and-Basic-Structure.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `njac-judgment-judicial-appointments`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the constitutional holding, defend its independence rationale, and assess the collegium-restoration problem.

**Compact framework:** NJAC design → Court’s holding → merits → criticism → balanced reform.

### Model answer

In *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (2015), a 4:1 majority invalidated the **99th Constitutional Amendment** and NJAC Act, 2014. The NJAC comprised the Chief Justice of India, two senior-most judges, the Law Minister and two eminent persons.

The majority held that judicial independence, including judicial primacy in appointments, forms the **basic structure**. Executive participation and the possibility that any two members could veto a recommendation risked political bargaining. It restored the collegium evolved through the Second and Third Judges Cases.

However, striking down the NJAC revived a system criticised for opacity, weak diversity, uncertain criteria and inadequate accountability. Justice Chelameswar’s dissent stressed that judicial primacy need not mean judicial exclusivity.

A sound reform should preserve decisional independence while introducing published criteria, a permanent secretariat, recorded reasons, conflict rules and wider consultation. The Court’s later procedure exercise acknowledged this need.

**Conclusion:** The judgment correctly protected independence, but constitutional legitimacy also requires a transparent and accountable appointment process.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the ratio, engages the dissent and distinguishes independence from unaccountable exclusivity.

## Q3. “Simultaneous election to the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies will limit the amount of time and money spent in electioneering but it will reduce the government’s accountability to the people.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Simultaneous elections · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Simultaneous Lok Sabha and Assembly elections  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `simultaneous-elections-cost-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Balance efficiency gains against democratic and federal accountability costs; do not give an absolute verdict.

**Compact framework:** Benefits → accountability/federal risks → design safeguards.

### Model answer

Simultaneous elections could reduce repeated deployment of personnel, recurrent campaign expenditure and frequent Model Code of Conduct interruptions. A common cycle may allow longer periods for policy implementation.

However, elections are mechanisms of accountability, not merely administrative events:

- staggered polls periodically expose governments to voter judgment;
- national campaigns may overshadow State and local issues;
- fixed synchronisation may require extending or curtailing elected terms;
- premature dissolution requires breaking the cycle or temporary arrangements;
- a constructive vote of no-confidence may provide stability but can also insulate unpopular governments.

Articles **83(2)** and **172(1)** prescribe five-year terms, subject to earlier dissolution; constitutional and statutory amendments would therefore be required. The Law Commission favoured exploration with safeguards.

**Conclusion:** Synchronisation can improve efficiency, but accountability should not be sacrificed to calendar uniformity. Any reform needs political consensus, protected federal issues, constructive no-confidence and a constitutionally legitimate mechanism for mid-term vacancies.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly weighs the proposition and identifies the constitutional design problem behind premature dissolution.

## Q4. How do pressure groups influence Indian political process? Do you agree with this view that informal pressure groups have emerged as more powerful than formal pressure groups in recent years?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Pressure Groups](../../topics/polity/pressure-groups.md) → Formal and informal influence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain; Assess  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Political processes → Pressure groups  
**Canonical owner:** [Pressure Groups](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/44_Pressure-Groups.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pressure-groups-formal-informal-influence`

**Demand decoding:** Identify channels of influence and give a graded verdict on whether informal networks now outweigh organised associations.

**Compact framework:** Influence channels → informal rise → limits → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Pressure groups shape policy without directly seeking office. Trade unions, business associations and professional bodies use lobbying, expert submissions, consultation and litigation. Farmers’ organisations and social movements use demonstrations, petitions and media campaigns.

Informal groups have gained visibility because social media enables rapid mobilisation; issue-based coalitions bypass rigid membership; and caste, religious, regional and corporate networks may secure direct access. Anti-corruption and online campaigns illustrate agenda-setting outside traditional organisations.

Yet “more powerful” is too broad. Formal groups possess durable organisation, finance, expertise and institutional access. Informal mobilisation may be transient, opaque or vulnerable to misinformation.

Both can deepen participation, but unequal resources may produce policy capture. Disclosure of lobbying, transparent consultations, political-finance reform and participatory legislative processes are therefore necessary.

**Conclusion:** Informal influence has expanded, not uniformly displaced formal pressure groups; power varies by issue, resources and access.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both limbs and qualifies the comparison through institutional capacity and democratic risks.

## Q5. Discuss the role of Public Accounts Committee in establishing accountability of the government to the people.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Public Accounts Committee · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Financial committees  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `public-accounts-committee-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how PAC converts legislative control of finance into public accountability, while stating its limits.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → accountability mechanism → limitations → strengthening.

### Model answer

The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) gives effect to Parliament’s control over the public purse. Its **22 members**—15 from Lok Sabha and seven from Rajya Sabha—are elected annually; ministers cannot join. By convention, its chairperson is from the Opposition.

Using CAG reports, the PAC examines whether:

- expenditure conformed to parliamentary grants and legal authority;
- money was spent for the approved purpose;
- reappropriation complied with rules; and
- public resources were used with propriety, economy and effectiveness.

It summons officials, takes evidence and presents reports followed by departmental action-taken notes. Its reports expose irregularities and compel the executive to explain its stewardship.

However, scrutiny is post-facto; recommendations are not binding; backlogs and limited research support weaken follow-up. It ordinarily does not question policy as such.

**Conclusion:** The PAC establishes accountability by linking independent CAG audit to legislative scrutiny. Timely examination, expert staff and mandatory, time-bound government responses would strengthen this chain.

**Why this earns marks:** It specifies composition, audit functions, accountability logic and institutional limitations.

## Q6. “To ensure effective implementation of policies addressing water, sanitation and hygiene needs the identification of the beneficiary segments is to be synchronized with the anticipated outcomes.” Examine the statement in the context of the WASH scheme.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Sanitation, Manual Scavenging and Safai Karamcharis](../../topics/social-justice/sanitation-manual-scavenging-and-safai-karamcharis.md) → Targeting and outcomes in WASH · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Water, sanitation and hygiene → Outcome-oriented targeting  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Policy implementation and evaluation  
**Canonical owners:** [Sanitation, Manual Scavenging and Safai Karamcharis](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/14_Sanitation-Manual-Scavenging-and-Safai-Karamcharis.md) · [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/02_Government-Policy-Design-and-Implementation.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `wash-beneficiary-targeting-outcomes`

**Demand decoding:** Show why WASH beneficiaries must be segmented by deprivation and linked to measurable health and behavioural outcomes, not merely infrastructure outputs.

**Compact framework:** Differentiated needs → outcome chain → implementation safeguards.

### Model answer

WASH policy succeeds only when the intended beneficiary, specific barrier and desired outcome are aligned. A household without a toilet, a schoolgirl lacking menstrual-hygiene facilities, a fluoride-affected habitation and an urban slum without piped water require different interventions.

Accordingly, deprivation mapping should identify women, children, persons with disabilities, Scheduled Castes and Tribes, remote habitations and the urban poor. Inputs must then connect to outcomes:

`safe source → accessible facility → sustained use → handwashing and safe disposal → lower disease and better attendance`.

Counting toilets or water points alone may conceal non-functionality, unsafe water, poor faecal-sludge management or behavioural non-use. Swachh Bharat, school WASH, drinking-water programmes and the National Health Mission therefore need convergence at Gram Panchayat and urban-ward levels.

Community participation, women’s representation, water-quality testing, lifecycle maintenance funds and indicators such as usage, continuity, contamination, diarrhoeal incidence and school attendance should guide monitoring.

**Conclusion:** Precise segmentation prevents exclusion, while outcome-linked design converts physical assets into health, dignity and human-development gains.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains synchronisation through a clear results chain and differentiated beneficiary examples.

## Q7. Does the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 ensure effective mechanism for empowerment and inclusion of the intended beneficiaries in the society? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Disability-Inclusive Policy](../../topics/social-justice/disability-inclusive-policy.md) → Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss; Assess effectiveness  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Persons with disabilities → Rights, accessibility and inclusion  
**Canonical owner:** [Persons with Disabilities](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/11_Persons-with-Disabilities.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpwd-act-2016-empowerment-inclusion`

**Demand decoding:** Assess the Act’s rights-based mechanisms and implementation constraints; avoid treating enactment as assured inclusion.

**Compact framework:** Rights architecture → empowerment mechanisms → implementation gaps → verdict.

### Model answer

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, **2016**, advances India from a welfare approach towards the UNCRPD’s rights-based model. It expands recognised disabilities from seven to **21**, prohibits discrimination, mandates reasonable accommodation and accessibility, and protects legal capacity and community life.

Empowerment mechanisms include:

- at least **4%** reservation in government employment for persons with benchmark disabilities;
- at least **5%** reservation in government and government-aided higher education;
- inclusive education and specified support;
- accessibility duties for buildings, transport and information;
- Special Courts, penalties, grievance officers and Chief/State Commissioners.

Nevertheless, effectiveness depends on certification, accessible infrastructure, trained teachers, assistive technology, budgetary support and vacancies being filled. Exemptions, weak local enforcement and limited awareness can convert rights into paper guarantees. Reservation benefits also focus on “benchmark disability,” while broader non-discrimination must protect all covered persons.

**Conclusion:** The Act supplies a strong enabling framework, but social inclusion requires enforceable accessibility timelines, harmonised rules, participation of disabled persons and accountable implementation.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses exact statutory mechanisms and gives a qualified implementation-based assessment.

## Q8. Hunger and poverty are the biggest challenges for good governance in India still today. Evaluate how far successive governments have progressed in dealing with these humongous problems. Suggest measures for improvement.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Poverty and hunger governance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Evaluate; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty and hunger → Programme performance  
**Canonical owner:** [Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `poverty-hunger-progress-governance`

**Demand decoding:** Give a graded assessment of progress up to 2017 and propose reforms addressing income, nutrition and delivery.

**Compact framework:** Progress → persistent deficits → integrated remedies.

### Model answer

Governments have reduced poverty through growth, employment, food and social protection, but hunger shows that gains remain incomplete.

MGNREGA created a rights-based wage floor; the National Food Security Act, **2013**, expanded grain and maternity entitlements; ICDS and Mid-Day Meals addressed child nutrition; financial inclusion and direct transfers sought to reduce leakage. Poverty declined over the longer term, while PDS reforms improved access in several States.

Yet calorie security is not nutrition security. Informal employment, low wages, exclusion errors, maternal undernutrition, poor sanitation and cereal-heavy diets sustain deprivation. Fragmented databases and weak grievance redress can exclude migrants and other vulnerable households.

Improvement requires diverse food baskets, maternal and child healthcare, WASH, employment-intensive growth, portable entitlements, updated lists, social audits and transparent grievance systems. Agriculture policy should support pulses, millets and local nutrition.

**Conclusion:** India has built a substantial entitlement architecture, but good governance must convert schemes into reliable capabilities—income, health, sanitation and nutritious diets.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates rather than merely lists schemes and connects hunger to multidimensional human development.

## Q11. Explain the salient features of the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016. Do you think it is efficacious enough “to remove cascading effect of taxes and provide for common national market for goods and services”?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre–State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → GST and fiscal federalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Explain; Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fiscal federalism → GST constitutional framework  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Indirect taxation → Common market  
**Canonical owners:** [GST Council](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/30_GST-Council.md) · [Centre–State and Inter-State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/13_Centre-State-and-Inter-State-Relations.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `101st-amendment-gst-common-market`

**Demand decoding:** First state the Amendment’s constitutional architecture; then assess whether design and 2017 implementation remove cascading and create a common market.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional features → anti-cascading/common-market gains → exclusions and implementation limits → verdict.

### Model answer

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, **2016**, created the constitutional basis for a destination-based Goods and Services Tax, operational from 1 July 2017.

**Salient features**

- Article **246A** gives Parliament and State legislatures concurrent power over GST; Parliament has exclusive power over inter-State supplies.
- Article **269A** provides for levy and apportionment of GST on inter-State trade.
- Article **279A** establishes the GST Council, comprising the Union and States, to recommend rates, exemptions, thresholds and model laws.
- Article **366(12A)** defines GST; taxes on petroleum crude, diesel, petrol, natural gas and aviation turbine fuel await Council recommendation.
- Alcohol for human consumption remains outside GST.
- Entry 92A was omitted and several Union/State taxing entries were restructured.
- Parliament could compensate States for revenue loss for five years.

GST reduces cascading by permitting input-tax credit across goods and services and replacing multiple central and State levies. IGST supports seamless inter-State credit, while removal of check-post barriers and harmonised rules aid a common market.

However, efficacy is qualified. Multiple rate slabs, exclusions such as petroleum, blocked credits and compliance complexity interrupt the credit chain. Real-estate stamp duty and electricity duties remain outside. A common portal cannot by itself cure classification disputes, refund delays or administrative duality.

**Conclusion:** The Amendment is a transformative enabling framework, not a self-executing guarantee. Simpler rates, broader coverage, stable rules and cooperative GST Council decision-making are required to realise fully the anti-cascading and common-market objectives.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately separates constitutional design from implementation and tests both quoted objectives.

## Q12. Examine the scope of Fundamental Rights in the light of the latest judgement of the Supreme Court on Right to Privacy.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Right to privacy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Privacy, dignity and liberty  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `puttaswamy-privacy-fundamental-rights-scope`

**Demand decoding:** Use the nine-judge *Puttaswamy* ruling to explain the doctrinal expansion, horizontal implications and permissible restrictions on Fundamental Rights.

**Compact framework:** Holding → enlarged rights architecture → restriction test → implications and limits.

### Model answer

In *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India* (2017), a unanimous nine-judge Bench held that privacy is a constitutionally protected Fundamental Right. It overruled contrary propositions in *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh*.

The judgment enlarges the understanding of Fundamental Rights in four ways:

1. **Integrated rights:** Privacy arises primarily from life and personal liberty under Article **21**, but also informs freedoms under Article **19** and equality under Article **14**. Rights are not isolated silos.
2. **Dignity and autonomy:** Privacy protects bodily integrity, decisional autonomy, family, marriage, procreation, sexual orientation, home and communications.
3. **Informational privacy:** State and private data processing can affect liberty; the Court called for a robust data-protection regime.
4. **Living Constitution:** Technological change does not freeze liberty at the Constitution’s original factual setting.

Privacy is not absolute. Any invasion must have **legality**, pursue a **legitimate State aim**, and satisfy **proportionality**; procedural safeguards against abuse are integral. Restrictions may support security, crime prevention, welfare delivery or public health, but mass or indiscriminate intrusion cannot rest on administrative convenience.

The judgment also strengthens later adjudication on sexuality, choice and dignity. Yet private actors’ power and surveillance require legislation because constitutional remedies traditionally operate chiefly against the State.

**Conclusion:** *Puttaswamy* shifts Fundamental Rights towards dignity-centred, technology-aware liberty while preserving carefully justified regulation. Its lasting value lies in making State power answerable to legality, necessity and proportionality.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the ratio, maps the expanded scope and explains the precise limitation framework.

## Q13. The Indian Constitution has provisions for holding joint session of the two Houses of the Parliament. Enumerate the occasions when this would normally happen and also the occasions when it cannot, with reason thereof.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Joint sitting of Parliament · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Enumerate; Explain reasons  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Legislative procedure and deadlocks  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `joint-sitting-parliament-occasions-exclusions`

**Demand decoding:** State each Article 108 deadlock, then distinguish Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills and explain why joint sitting is unavailable.

**Compact framework:** Trigger conditions → procedure → excluded Bills → rationale and examples.

### Model answer

Article **108** permits the President to summon a joint sitting of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha to resolve a deadlock over an ordinary Bill. It may normally occur when, after one House passes a Bill and transmits it to the other:

1. the other House **rejects** the Bill;
2. the Houses **finally disagree** on amendments; or
3. more than **six months** elapse without the other House passing it.

In calculating six months, periods when the receiving House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days are excluded. The Speaker of Lok Sabha normally presides. The Bill is passed by a majority of members of both Houses present and voting. A dissolution of Lok Sabha after the President has notified the intention to summon a joint sitting does not terminate the process.

Joint sitting is unavailable in two important cases:

- **Money Bills:** Under Articles **109–110**, Lok Sabha has primacy; Rajya Sabha can only recommend changes within fourteen days. Hence no constitutional deadlock arises.
- **Constitution Amendment Bills:** Article **368** requires each House separately to pass the Bill by the prescribed special majority. Federal and constituent legitimacy cannot be obtained by allowing the numerically larger Lok Sabha to override Rajya Sabha.

It is also unnecessary when a Bill lapses on Lok Sabha’s dissolution before the Article 108 notification.

Joint sittings have been rare—the Dowry Prohibition Bill, Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill and Prevention of Terrorism Bill illustrate their exceptional character.

**Conclusion:** Article 108 resolves ordinary legislative deadlock while preserving Lok Sabha’s financial primacy and each House’s independent constituent role.

**Why this earns marks:** It enumerates all triggers and exclusions, explains their constitutional logic and adds procedurally relevant qualifications.

## Q14. To enhance the quality of democracy in India the Election Commission of India has proposed electoral reforms in 2016. What are the suggested reforms and how far are they significant to make democracy successful?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Proposed electoral reforms, 2016 · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Identify; Evaluate significance  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Electoral reform  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `eci-2016-electoral-reforms-democracy`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the ECI’s 2016 reform proposals and assess how they address criminalisation, money power, transparency and institutional autonomy.

**Compact framework:** Reform clusters → democratic significance → limitations and complementary measures.

### Model answer

The Election Commission’s **Proposed Electoral Reforms, 2016** consolidated measures intended to improve integrity, equality and informed choice.

**Important proposals included:**

- constitutional protection for all Election Commissioners’ removal and an independent secretariat charged to the Consolidated Fund;
- disqualification upon framing of charges, at least six months before an election, for serious offences punishable with imprisonment of five years or more;
- making false affidavits a ground for disqualification and increasing punishment;
- permanent disqualification for corruption and heinous offences;
- greater transparency of political contributions, including disclosure and reform of anonymous donations;
- placing paid news and electoral bribery on a stronger legal footing;
- empowering the ECI to countermand elections affected by bribery;
- registration and de-registration reforms for political parties;
- prohibition on government-sponsored advertisements before elections and stronger controls on misuse of official machinery;
- totaliser machines to protect booth-wise voter secrecy and strengthening voting safeguards.

These measures are significant because they protect electoral management, deter criminalisation and money power, improve voter information, and create a more level playing field. They reinforce Article **324** and free-and-fair elections, recognised as part of the basic structure.

However, electoral law alone cannot make democracy successful. Premature disqualification must respect due process; party finance requires auditable accounts and internal democracy; appointments to the ECI need a credible process; and enforcement needs timely courts, investigation and citizen scrutiny.

**Conclusion:** The proposals address major distortions and are democratically significant, but their value depends on careful legislation, independent enforcement and reform within political parties.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies concrete 2016 proposals and evaluates both their democratic purpose and rule-of-law constraints.

## Q15. Is the National Commission for Women able to strategize and tackle the problems that women face at both public and private spheres? Give reasons in support of your answer.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Women and Gender Justice](../../topics/social-justice/women-and-gender-justice.md) → National Commission for Women · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Assess; Give reasons  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Women → Institutional protection and NCW  
**Canonical owner:** [Women and Gender Justice](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/05_Women-and-Gender-Justice.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `national-commission-women-capacity-public-private`

**Demand decoding:** Evaluate the NCW’s statutory mandate against its actual powers and capacity across workplace/public harms and family/private harms.

**Compact framework:** Mandate and contribution → public/private reach → structural constraints → reforms and verdict.

### Model answer

The National Commission for Women (NCW), established under the **National Commission for Women Act, 1990**, has a broad mandate to review safeguards, investigate complaints, advise government, fund studies and inspect custodial institutions. Its civil-court powers during inquiry help it summon persons and documents.

It can address both spheres. In the **public sphere**, it takes complaints involving workplace harassment, trafficking, cyber abuse, unequal opportunity and custodial violence, recommends legal reform and conducts awareness programmes. In the **private sphere**, it handles domestic violence, dowry, desertion, maintenance and matrimonial disputes, often through counselling, hearings and coordination with police or State commissions. Its reviews and interventions can place neglected gender harms on the policy agenda.

However, its capacity to “tackle” them is limited:

- it is a statutory, mainly recommendatory body without independent enforcement power;
- appointments may lack a transparent, expertise-based process;
- inadequate staff, regional reach and investigation capacity constrain follow-up;
- dependence on police and State authorities delays relief;
- fragmented data and overlap with State commissions impede strategy;
- social norms and women’s limited access to legal aid make private violence under-reported.

Strengthening requires transparent appointments, an independent investigation and research wing, time-bound government responses, integrated complaint tracking, district-level access and formal coordination with police, legal-services authorities and workplace committees.

**Conclusion:** The NCW is an important agenda-setting and grievance institution, but it cannot by itself overcome gender inequality. Greater autonomy, capacity and enforceable follow-up are needed, alongside functioning courts and frontline services.

**Why this earns marks:** It tests statutory powers against delivery constraints and separately addresses public and private harms.

## Q16. “The emergence of the Self-Help Groups (SHGs) in contemporary times points to the slow but steady withdrawal of the State from developmental activities.” Examine the role of the SHGs in developmental activities and the measures taken by the Government of India to promote the SHGs.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → SHGs and development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Examine; Explain measures  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Development processes → Self-Help Groups  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Women’s economic empowerment  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `shgs-development-state-withdrawal-promotion`

**Demand decoding:** Test the “State withdrawal” thesis, explain SHGs’ developmental functions, and identify Government measures current to 2017.

**Compact framework:** Thesis qualified → SHG roles → promotion architecture → safeguards.

### Model answer

Self-Help Groups are small, affinity-based collectives that pool savings, access credit and undertake livelihood or social action. Their growth can appear to shift welfare burdens to communities, but it more accurately reflects a transition from direct State delivery towards **State-enabled collective action**.

**Developmental role**

- savings and collateral-free internal lending reduce dependence on moneylenders;
- bank linkage supports micro-enterprises, agriculture and consumption smoothing;
- women’s participation strengthens mobility, household bargaining and political voice;
- federations improve procurement, marketing, insurance and access to schemes;
- groups support nutrition, sanitation, financial literacy, social audits and action against violence;
- peer monitoring lowers transaction costs and can improve repayment.

Yet SHGs cannot replace public goods, redistributive finance or accountable administration. Excessive debt, weak markets, unpaid care burdens, elite capture and pressure for repayment can undermine empowerment.

**Government promotion**

- NABARD’s **SHG–Bank Linkage Programme** connects groups with formal banking.
- **DAY–National Rural Livelihoods Mission (Aajeevika)** mobilises poor women into SHGs and federations, provides capacity building, revolving funds and community investment support.
- Interest subvention and bank-linkage initiatives expand affordable credit.
- The **National Urban Livelihoods Mission** supports urban poor groups.
- Training, livelihood missions, financial-inclusion programmes and the Mahila Kisan Sashaktikaran Pariyojana strengthen skills and women farmers.

**Conclusion:** SHGs should complement, not substitute for, the State. Their developmental potential is greatest when public investment supplies credit, capabilities, markets, social protection and accountable services.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly qualifies the withdrawal thesis and combines developmental roles with named government mechanisms.

## Q17. “Poverty alleviation programmes in India remain mere showpieces until and unless they are backed up by political will.” Discuss with reference to the performance of the major poverty alleviation programmes in India.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Poverty alleviation and political will · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty → Programme performance and political commitment  
**Canonical owner:** [Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `poverty-programmes-political-will-performance`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how political commitment affects design, funding, implementation and accountability; evaluate major programmes rather than merely list them.

**Compact framework:** Why will matters → programme record → structural constraints → institutionalised commitment.

### Model answer

Political will converts anti-poverty promises into budgets, capable institutions and enforceable entitlements. Without it, programmes may become visible announcements with weak implementation.

India’s record is mixed:

- **MGNREGA** created a legal employment guarantee, rural assets and a fallback wage. Its performance improves where work is opened on demand and wages are timely, but rationing, delayed payments and weak unemployment allowance dilute the right.
- The **Public Distribution System/NFSA 2013** protects food access; computerisation and doorstep delivery reduced leakage in some States. Outdated lists, exclusion and limited dietary diversity remain problems.
- **DAY-NRLM** builds women’s SHGs and livelihoods, but credit without skills, markets and risk protection yields uneven income gains.
- **Housing, pensions and financial-inclusion programmes** create assets and security, yet low benefits, identification errors and fragmented delivery reduce impact.
- Earlier self-employment schemes often suffered from target-driven lending, poor training and weak market linkage.

Political commitment matters in selecting beneficiaries transparently, providing adequate funds, filling frontline vacancies, resisting local capture and acting on audits. Kerala’s decentralised planning and States with stronger PDS systems show that institutions and sustained leadership can improve outcomes.

Nevertheless, poverty also reflects land, education, health, gender, caste and labour-market structures; no scheme can substitute for broad-based employment and quality public services.

**Conclusion:** Political will is necessary but should be institutionalised through legal entitlements, predictable finance, social audits, portable benefits, grievance redress and outcome evaluation, so poverty policy survives changes of government.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates named programmes causally and qualifies political will with structural and institutional determinants.

## Q18. Initially Civil Services in India were designed to achieve the goals of neutrality and effectiveness, which seems to be lacking in the present context. Do you agree with the view that drastic reforms are required in Civil Services? Comment.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Civil Services](../../topics/governance/civil-services.md) → Neutrality, effectiveness and reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Comment; Assess  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil services → Reform and accountability  
**Canonical owner:** [Civil Services and Mission Karmayogi](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/09_Civil-Services-and-Mission-Karmayogi.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `civil-services-neutrality-effectiveness-reforms`

**Demand decoding:** Give a qualified agreement, diagnose institutional—not merely individual—causes, and prescribe reforms without weakening impartial permanence.

**Compact framework:** Continuing value → neutrality/effectiveness deficits → reform package → safeguards.

### Model answer

India’s permanent civil service was expected to provide political neutrality, continuity and competent implementation. It still supplies institutional memory and nationwide administrative capacity, especially during elections and disasters. Therefore, the deficit is serious but not universal.

**Why reform is required**

- arbitrary transfers and politicised postings weaken impartial advice;
- generalist dominance and frequent rotation reduce domain expertise;
- process compliance often displaces citizen outcomes;
- opaque appraisal, weak accountability and limited protection for honest decisions encourage risk aversion;
- departmental silos obstruct complex, cross-sectoral policy;
- corruption, vacancies and inadequate frontline capacity impair delivery;
- colonial secrecy and hierarchy persist despite democratic expectations.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission recommended ethics management, civil-service boards, performance orientation and citizen-centric administration. The Supreme Court in *T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India* (2013) supported minimum tenure, Civil Services Boards and written directions.

Reforms should include merit-based and transparent postings; fixed minimum tenure; specialisation with planned career paths; rigorous mid-career training; outcome-sensitive appraisal with multi-source evidence; lateral entry for identified gaps under fair selection; protection for bona fide decisions alongside swift disciplinary action; digital process simplification; and stronger grievance redress. A statutory civil-services code should clarify political direction and professional responsibility.

However, “drastic” must not mean contractual insecurity or corporate-style targets that undermine constitutional neutrality, federal diversity and service to vulnerable citizens.

**Conclusion:** Deep systemic reform is necessary, but its purpose must be to strengthen an impartial, skilled and accountable public service—not to weaken its permanence or independence.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a qualified verdict anchored in the 2nd ARC and *T.S.R. Subramanian*, with balanced institutional reforms.

## Q19. The question of India’s Energy Security constitutes the most important part of India’s economic progress. Analyze India’s energy policy cooperation with West Asian countries.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [West Asia, Energy Security and Connectivity](../../topics/international-relations/west-asia-energy-security-and-connectivity.md) → Energy cooperation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2017 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q19 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Analyse  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → West Asia → Energy security  
**Canonical owner:** [West Asia, Energy Security and Connectivity](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/06_West-Asia-Energy-Security-and-Connectivity.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-west-asia-energy-cooperation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why West Asia matters across supply, investment and strategy; assess vulnerabilities and diversification rather than merely describe oil imports.

**Compact framework:** Energy-growth nexus → cooperation pillars → constraints → resilient policy.

### Model answer

Reliable and affordable energy underpins India’s industrialisation, transport, inflation control and fiscal stability. West Asia is central because it supplies a large share of India’s crude oil and liquefied petroleum gas and holds major hydrocarbon reserves close to Indian sea routes.

**Cooperation has several dimensions:**

- long-term crude contracts with Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait and Iran provide supply diversity;
- Qatar is a major LNG partner under long-term arrangements;
- Indian public-sector firms pursue upstream stakes and service contracts, while Gulf national oil companies are courted for refining, petrochemicals and storage investment;
- producer participation in India’s strategic petroleum reserves can combine commercial stock with emergency security;
- buyer–seller dialogues seek favourable pricing, flexible contract terms and stable supply;
- Chabahar and engagement with Iran connect energy interests with continental access.

This interdependence is reinforced by trade, investment and the Indian diaspora. Yet it is vulnerable to conflict, sectarian rivalry, sanctions on Iran, maritime chokepoints such as Hormuz, price volatility and competition from other Asian buyers. Heavy import dependence exposes the current account and domestic prices.

India should therefore maintain balanced relations across Saudi Arabia, Iran, Israel and the Gulf States; obtain equity oil and diversified LNG contracts; expand strategic reserves; improve naval and evacuation preparedness; and use diplomacy for secure sea lanes. Domestic renewables, efficiency, electric mobility and diversified suppliers must reduce—not end—West Asian dependence.

**Conclusion:** West Asian cooperation remains indispensable, but energy security requires converting buyer–seller ties into investment and strategic partnerships while building domestic and geographic resilience.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses cooperation as a multidimensional energy relationship and qualifies it through geopolitical and import-dependence risks.

**Primary route:** International Relations → [Indian Diaspora](../../topics/international-relations/indian-diaspora.md) → South-East Asia · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Proposed topic slug:** `indian-diaspora-southeast-asia-role`

**Demand decoding:** Assess economic and social contributions to host countries and India–ASEAN relations while acknowledging diversity, historical tensions and limits.

**Compact framework:** Historical diversity → economic role → social/strategic role → qualifications.

### Model answer

South-East Asia’s Indian-origin communities arose through ancient trade and cultural contact, colonial-era labour migration, and modern professional mobility. They are therefore diverse in class, citizenship and relationship with India.

**Economic contribution**

- Indian-origin entrepreneurs have built enterprises in trade, plantations, textiles, finance, technology and services, particularly in Singapore, Malaysia, Myanmar and Thailand.
- Professionals in medicine, education, engineering and information technology add human capital to regional economies.
- Business networks connect Indian firms with ASEAN markets, investment and supply chains.
- Migrant workers support construction and services, while remittances and tourism strengthen people-to-people commerce.

**Social and diplomatic contribution**

- temples, language associations, festivals, cuisine and arts enrich multicultural societies and preserve civilisational links.
- Diaspora institutions support education, philanthropy and community welfare.
- Prominent public figures and business leaders can improve understanding of India and support the **Act East Policy**.
- Cultural familiarity provides soft-power foundations for India–ASEAN relations beyond government diplomacy.

An appraisal must avoid treating the diaspora as a uniform strategic instrument. Most members are loyal citizens or residents of host States, and their interests may not mirror New Delhi’s. Sections of plantation-origin communities have faced socioeconomic disadvantage, documentation problems or ethnic tension; high-profile success can obscure these vulnerabilities. Diaspora engagement must therefore respect sovereignty and avoid domestic political mobilisation abroad.

India should emphasise consular protection, skills and qualification partnerships, cultural exchange and transparent business networks rather than instrumentalising communities.

**Conclusion:** The diaspora is an important economic bridge and social connector, but its durable value lies in inclusive host-country citizenship combined with voluntary cultural and commercial links to India.

**Why this earns marks:** It appraises both contribution and limitation, differentiating historical communities from newer professionals and workers.
