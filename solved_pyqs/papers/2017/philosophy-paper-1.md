### Q1(a). Elaborate Hegel’s dictum: “All identity is identity and difference.”
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → identity-in-difference · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Elaborate · Syllabus unit: Hegel—dialectical method · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Slug:** `hegel-identity-and-difference`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why determinate identity includes negation and relation, without reducing Hegel to the crude formula “thesis–antithesis–synthesis.”

**Argument map:** empty self-identity (`A=A`) says nothing determinate → to be this is not to be others → difference is internal to determinacy → opposition is dialectically preserved and overcome → concrete identity is unity-through-difference.

#### Model answer

Hegel rejects the abstract understanding’s principle that identity excludes difference. Bare `A=A` gives only an empty tautology. A determinate thing is what it is by excluding and relating itself to what it is not: “red” is intelligible within a field of contrasting colours, and masterhood exists only through relation to bondsmanhood. Difference is therefore not merely attached from outside; it belongs to identity’s content.

Dialectical reason shows that identity passes into difference, difference into opposition, and opposition into a higher unity that preserves its moments—sublation (*Aufhebung*). The higher identity does not erase difference. An organism, for example, remains one precisely through differentiated organs whose functions mediate the whole.

**Objection:** If identity contains difference, contradiction seems licensed indiscriminately. **Reply:** Hegel does not deny formal consistency within fixed contexts; he argues that finite determinations become contradictory when treated as self-sufficient.

**Evaluation:** The dictum replaces inert sameness with concrete, relational identity. Its strength is explanatory dynamism; its danger is obscurity if every contrast is too quickly declared “dialectical.”

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes abstract from concrete identity and explains negation, relation and sublation.

### Q1(b). Explain Husserl’s reasons for advocating phenomenological reductionism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Phenomenology](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/phenomenology.md) → phenomenological reduction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Husserl—phenomenological reduction · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology—Husserl](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Slug:** `husserl-phenomenological-reduction`

**Demand decoding:** State what is reduced or bracketed, why the natural attitude is suspended, and what philosophical field thereby becomes accessible.

**Argument map:** natural attitude naïvely posits world → sciences inherit this presupposition → epoché brackets existential commitment → reduction redirects inquiry to intentional appearing → essences and transcendental subjectivity become describable → philosophy gains presuppositionless rigour.

#### Model answer

Husserl advocates phenomenological reduction to make philosophy a rigorous, presuppositionless science. In the “natural attitude” we simply take the world and its objects as existing independently. Natural sciences explain entities within that assumed world, but do not examine how objectivity is given to consciousness.

The suspension or epoché (*epochē*) neither denies the world nor doubts it in a Cartesian manner. It brackets the existential thesis and redirects attention from the object simply posited to the object-as-intended. Phenomenological reduction thus reveals intentionality: every consciousness is consciousness *of* something, correlated with modes of givenness. Eidetic variation then discloses essential structures, while transcendental reduction uncovers the constituting activity of transcendental subjectivity.

**Objection:** Reduction imprisons inquiry inside a private mind. **Reply:** Husserl analyses intersubjective constitution and the shared life-world; the aim is not psychological introspection but the conditions of objective meaning.

**Evaluation:** Reduction powerfully exposes hidden assumptions, though the transition to transcendental idealism remains contested.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains natural attitude, epoché, intentionality and the transcendental purpose of reduction.

### Q1(c). How is the statement, “I met a man”, semantically problematic for Russell? How does he account for the meaningfulness of this statement?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → incomplete symbols and descriptions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Russell—logical atomism and descriptions · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Slug:** `russell-i-met-a-man`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the apparent constituent “a man” and show how contextual analysis removes the need for a corresponding indefinite object.

**Argument map:** grammatical form suggests subject-term “a man” → no unique object is denoted → treating it as a name creates ontological/semantic confusion → quantify proposition existentially → meaning rests on propositional function and bound variable.

#### Model answer

For Russell, surface grammar misleadingly makes “a man” look like a name standing for some indefinite entity. But names must directly designate particulars, whereas “a man” neither identifies a unique individual nor denotes a peculiar object called “an-any-man.” If it were a constituent, the proposition’s reference would remain indeterminate.

Russell’s theory of descriptions treats the phrase as an incomplete symbol whose meaning appears only in the whole sentence. “I met a man” is analysed as:

`There exists at least one x such that x is a man and I met x.`

Symbolically, `∃x (Man(x) ∧ Met(I,x))`. Unlike a definite description, no uniqueness condition is required. The sentence is meaningful because it asserts that a propositional function is satisfied, without making “a man” a logically proper name or independent constituent.

**Objection:** Ordinary speakers refer to a particular man. **Reply:** Context may pragmatically identify him, but the sentence’s minimal logical content remains existential.

**Evaluation:** The analysis separates grammatical appearance from logical form and avoids unnecessary entities.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the semantic problem and supplies Russell’s exact quantificational solution.

### Q1(d). In what sense can ideas be both immanent and transcendent? Discuss in this context Plato’s theory of universals and particulars.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Forms, participation and immanence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Plato—Ideas; Aristotle’s critique · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Slug:** `plato-forms-immanent-transcendent`

**Demand decoding:** Reconcile, or expose the tension between, separate Forms and their presence in particulars.

**Argument map:** Forms are transcendent as non-spatial, eternal paradigms → particulars participate in or imitate them → Form is immanent as intelligible character present in many → separation creates relation problem → participation cannot be literal part-whole sharing → qualified dual-aspect reading.

#### Model answer

Plato’s Forms are **transcendent** because Beauty itself, Equality itself or Humanity itself exists independently of sensible instances: each is eternal, non-spatial, immutable and intelligible. Particular beautiful things arise and perish, whereas the Form supplies their objective standard and the object of knowledge.

Yet a Form is also **immanent** insofar as particulars are what they are by “participating in,” “imitating,” or “having the presence of” it. The same universal character is instantiated in many particulars without being exhausted by any one. Thus the Form is ontologically independent but explanatorily present.

The relation is problematic. If the whole Form is in each thing, it appears separated from itself; if only a part is present, the Form becomes divisible. The *Parmenides* develops this and the Third Man regress. Aristotle consequently locates form in substances, though intellect may abstract it.

**Evaluation:** Plato needs transcendence to secure stable knowledge and immanence to explain predication. Participation names this dual role but does not fully analyse its mechanism.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both senses and identifies the participation and regress difficulties.

### Q1(e). Show how Hume’s analysis of experience leaves no ground for belief in any permanent reality either physical or mental.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume on substance and self · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Show · Syllabus unit: Hume—substance and self · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Slug:** `hume-no-permanent-reality`

**Demand decoding:** Apply the impression principle separately to material substance and mental self, then qualify the sceptical result.

**Argument map:** meaningful ideas derive from impressions → experience gives qualities/perceptions, never substratum → causal continuity is habit → introspection finds changing perceptions, never self → memory and imagination feign identity → permanence is natural belief, not rationally established reality.

#### Model answer

Hume’s copy principle requires every legitimate simple idea to derive from an impression. Regarding bodies, experience presents colours, shapes, resistance and other perceptions, never an imperceptible material substratum supporting them. Nor can continuous external existence be inferred through causation, because causal knowledge itself rests on observed conjunction and habit, while the alleged external cause is never observed.

Regarding mind, introspection always encounters a particular perception—heat, pleasure, thought or pain—never a simple, enduring self behind them. The mind is therefore a “bundle or collection” of perceptions in perpetual flux. Memory discovers relations among perceptions, while imagination, aided by resemblance and causal continuity, mistakes a sequence for strict identity.

**Objection:** Hume must presuppose a subject that unifies the bundle. **Reply:** He concedes that personal identity presents a deep difficulty but finds no experiential warrant for a substance-self.

**Evaluation:** Hume undermines rational proof of permanent physical or mental substance, not everyday belief: nature irresistibly returns us to belief in bodies and selves.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies Hume’s empiricism symmetrically to object and subject and states the qualified scepticism.

### Q2(a). How is ‘all bodies are extended’ an analytic judgement but ‘all bodies are heavy’ a synthetic judgement? Is ‘every event has a cause’ an analytic or a synthetic judgement? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → analytic/synthetic and synthetic a priori judgements · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Kant—possibility of synthetic a priori judgements · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Slug:** `kant-body-extension-causality`

**Demand decoding:** Apply Kant’s containment test, distinguish analyticity from apriority, and establish the status and possibility of causality.

**Argument map:** analytic judgement explicates concept → extension belongs to body-concept → heaviness adds a predicate not contained in it → causal predicate not contained in event-concept → nevertheless causality is a priori category → principle is synthetic a priori and conditions objective temporal experience.

#### Model answer

Kant calls a judgement analytic when the predicate is already contained in the subject-concept; its denial is contradictory and analysis suffices to know it. A body is conceived as something occupying space. Hence “all bodies are extended” merely explicates what is thought in “body.” “All bodies are heavy,” by contrast, adds the relation of gravitational force, which is not contained in the bare concept of an extended body. Experience must connect predicate and subject; it is synthetic a posteriori.

“Every event has a cause” is also **synthetic**, because the concept of an event—something beginning or occurring—does not analytically contain the concept of a determinate antecedent that necessitates it. One can analyse “event” indefinitely without extracting “cause.” Yet for Kant the proposition is **a priori** and necessary. It is not an empirical generalisation, since experience shows sequences but cannot yield strict universality and necessity.

In the Second Analogy, subjective succession of perceptions becomes experience of an objective event only if appearances are ordered by a rule: the later state must follow an antecedent according to a necessary connection. Causality is therefore a category of understanding schematised in time. The understanding “legislates” this rule to possible experience.

**Objection:** Hume argues that necessity is merely projected habit. **Kant’s reply:** Hume explains expectation after repeated experience, but not the prior possibility of representing an objective alteration as distinct from an arbitrary sequence of perceptions.

**Qualification:** Causality validly applies only to appearances within possible experience, not to things in themselves. It cannot prove a first cause beyond experience.

**Evaluation:** Kant convincingly separates conceptual containment from transcendental conditions. Whether his transcendental deduction establishes necessity without circularity remains disputed.

**Why this earns marks:** It classifies all three examples and explains, rather than merely labels, synthetic a priori causality.

### Q2(b). What is Wittgenstein’s picture theory of meaning? What are his reasons for giving up this theory and suggesting the use theory of meaning?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → picture theory and transition to use · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain reasons · Syllabus unit: Early and later Wittgenstein · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Secondary route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Slug:** `wittgenstein-picture-to-use`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct the *Tractatus* account and show why linguistic diversity, rule-following and context motivate the later view.

**Argument map:** elementary proposition pictures atomic fact through shared logical form → names correlate with objects → truth is agreement/disagreement with reality → model privileges representation → commands, questions, avowals and jokes resist truth-functional picturing → ostensive definition and simples fail → meaning is use in language-games and forms of life.

#### Model answer

In the *Tractatus*, the world is the totality of facts, not things. An elementary proposition is a logical picture of a possible atomic fact: its simple names correspond to objects, and their arrangement mirrors the possible arrangement of objects. Picture and pictured reality share logical form, which cannot itself be said but is shown. A proposition is meaningful when it represents a possible state of affairs; it is true if that state obtains. Complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions.

The later Wittgenstein abandons this as a universal theory. First, language performs many activities—ordering, questioning, thanking, praying, joking, calculating—not all of which picture facts or possess truth-values. Second, the ideal of final simple names and atomic objects lacks an independent criterion; what counts as “simple” varies with purpose. Third, ostensive definition cannot fix meaning by itself, because pointing is interpretable only within an already mastered practice. Fourth, logical form does not explain how rules are learned and continued publicly.

In *Philosophical Investigations*, meaning is therefore ordinarily the use of an expression in a language-game embedded in a form of life. Rule-governed practices, context and family resemblances replace a single representational essence.

**Objection:** Use theory neglects truth and reference. **Reply:** It denies only that all meaning reduces to picturing; representational discourse remains one important language-game.

**Evaluation:** The transition is not from truth to relativism but from one rigid model to functional pluralism. The later theory better fits linguistic diversity, though “use” requires norms and cannot mean mere frequency.

**Why this earns marks:** It presents the picture theory precisely and supplies philosophical—not biographical—reasons for the transition.

### Q2(c). Explain Aristotle’s theory of form and matter. How does it help him resolve the problem of change and permanence?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → hylomorphism, actuality and potentiality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Aristotle—matter and form; causation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Slug:** `aristotle-form-matter-change`

**Demand decoding:** Define hylomorphic substance and use potentiality/actuality to mediate Heraclitean flux and Parmenidean denial of change.

**Argument map:** concrete substance = matter + form → matter is potentiality for contrary forms → form is actuality/organising essence → change retains subject while exchanging form or determination → substantial and accidental change differ → permanence is structured continuity, not changelessness.

#### Model answer

Aristotle holds that ordinary primary substances are composites of matter (*hylē*) and form (*morphē/eidos*). Matter is not an independently existing stuff but that out of which something is made and the principle of potentiality. Form is the organising actuality by which the composite is the kind of thing it is. A bronze statue, for example, has bronze as proximate matter and its determinate configuration as form.

This hylomorphism resolves the ancient dilemma. Parmenides inferred that change is impossible because being cannot arise from non-being; Heraclitean flux threatens stable identity. Aristotle distinguishes absolute non-being from privation and potential being. A seed is not actually a tree but has the potentiality to become one. Change is the actuality of the potential insofar as it is potential.

In accidental change, an enduring substance acquires a contrary property: Socrates remains Socrates while becoming educated. In substantial change, prime matter underlies generation and corruption while one substantial form succeeds another. Thus there is a persisting subject or capacity and a genuinely new actuality.

**Objection:** Prime matter without form seems inconceivable. **Reply:** Aristotle treats it as a limiting explanatory principle, never as separately existing matter.

**Evaluation:** The theory makes change intelligible without turning permanence into an inert substratum. Its teleological forms explain organised development, though modern science disputes whether natural processes require intrinsic final causes.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects form/matter directly with privation, potentiality/actuality and two kinds of change.

### Q3(a). Elaborate Kant’s theory of space and time. How does this theory enable him to explain how mathematical propositions can be both synthetic and a priori?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → transcendental aesthetic and mathematics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Elaborate and explain · Syllabus unit: Kant—space, time and synthetic a priori knowledge · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Slug:** `kant-space-time-mathematics`

**Demand decoding:** Establish space and time as pure forms of intuition, then derive geometrical and arithmetical necessity and restrict their scope.

**Argument map:** spatial/temporal order presupposed by experience → neither empirical concept → singular infinite representation, hence intuition → a priori forms of outer/inner sense → construction adds predicates not analytically contained → geometry constructs in space; arithmetic synthesises succession in time → necessity applies to appearances.

#### Model answer

Kant’s Transcendental Aesthetic argues that space and time are neither properties of things in themselves nor empirical concepts abstracted from experience. They are **pure a priori forms of sensible intuition**.

Space cannot be learned from outer experience because representing things as outside and beside one another already presupposes space. It is a singular, all-embracing intuition, not a discursive concept assembled from instances; particular spaces are limitations of one space. Space is the form of outer sense. Time similarly precedes the representation of succession and simultaneity, is one unlimited intuition, and is the form of inner sense. Since every representation is an inner state, time mediately conditions all appearances.

This “transcendental ideality” coexists with “empirical reality”: space and time validly structure every possible human experience but cannot be attributed to things as they are independently of our sensibility.

Mathematical judgements are **a priori** because their necessity derives from these pure forms rather than induction. They are **synthetic** because construction in intuition extends knowledge beyond conceptual analysis. The concept of a straight line does not contain “shortest distance”; drawing it in pure spatial intuition connects the predicates. Similarly `7+5=12` is reached by successively adding units in pure temporal intuition; “12” is not extracted merely by analysing the concepts seven and five.

Geometry constructs its concepts in pure space, while arithmetic depends on homogeneous successive synthesis, fundamentally temporal. Because appearances must conform to our forms of intuition, such constructions apply necessarily to possible objects of experience.

**Objection:** Non-Euclidean geometries and modern physics challenge Kant’s claim that Euclidean geometry is uniquely necessary. **Reply:** They weaken his specific identification of pure space with Euclidean structure, but preserve the deeper question of how formal frameworks condition empirical representation.

**Evaluation:** Kant explains mathematical necessity without rationalist innate objects or empiricist generalisation. The account is revolutionary, though its philosophy of geometry requires revision.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives both metaphysical and transcendental expositions and explicitly derives synthetic apriority.

### Q3(b). What, according to Descartes, is a ‘clear and distinct idea’? What is the epistemological status of clear and distinct ideas? Does this account help Descartes prove that material objects exist? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Descartes’ criterion and external world · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain and assess · Syllabus unit: Descartes—method, knowledge and external world · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Slug:** `descartes-clear-distinct-material-objects`

**Demand decoding:** Define clarity/distinctness, explain their certainty and divine guarantee, then test their role in the proof of bodies.

**Argument map:** clear = present and manifest to attentive mind → distinct = sharply separated into evident contents → cogito supplies paradigm → clear/distinct perception is self-evident while attended → non-deceiving God guarantees memory/general rule → involuntary sensory ideas plus divine veracity support bodies → only mathematically conceived primary qualities securely belong to them.

#### Model answer

Descartes calls an idea **clear** when it is present and manifest to an attentive mind, as an object is clear to sight; it is **distinct** when it is so precisely separated from other ideas that it contains only what is evident. The *cogito* is the paradigm: while thinking it, the mind cannot doubt its existence.

The rule “whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive is true” has immediate certainty during attentive intuition. Its stable, general epistemological status, however, depends on proving a perfect, non-deceiving God. God would not create us so that properly used reason systematically deceives us. This generates the “Cartesian Circle” objection: the proof of God employs clear and distinct premises while their reliability is supposedly guaranteed by God. Descartes replies that present intuitions are self-validating; divine veracity secures recollection and extended reasoning.

The account contributes to proving material objects. Sensory ideas arrive involuntarily, and we possess a strong natural inclination to refer them to external bodies. If no bodies existed, God would allow a systematic deception without providing a corrective faculty. Hence bodies exist. Clear and distinct geometrical ideas establish their essence as extension, figure and motion.

Yet sensory qualities such as colour or taste need not resemble bodies; they signify effects on us. Moreover, divine veracity—not clarity alone—carries the external-world inference.

**Evaluation:** Descartes secures a rational route from self to world, but the circle and the gap between involuntary ideas and mind-independent causes leave the proof contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms, explains certainty and addresses the exact external-world question with qualifications.

### Q3(c). What are the two dogmas of empiricism that Quine attacks? What are his arguments against what he calls the second dogma?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → two dogmas and confirmation holism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: State and argue · Syllabus unit: Quine—critique of empiricism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Slug:** `quine-second-dogma-reductionism`

**Demand decoding:** Name both dogmas, but concentrate on why statement-by-statement reduction to experience fails.

**Argument map:** first dogma = analytic/synthetic cleavage → second = each meaningful statement reducible to immediate experience → verification never tests isolated sentence → auxiliary assumptions mediate prediction → recalcitrant experience confronts whole web → revision can occur anywhere with differing pragmatic costs → empiricism becomes holistic and naturalistic.

#### Model answer

Quine identifies two dogmas underlying modern empiricism: **(1)** a fundamental distinction between analytic truths grounded in meanings and synthetic truths grounded in fact; and **(2)** **reductionism**, the belief that every meaningful statement is individually translatable into, or confirmable by, a construction from immediate experience.

The radical form appears in phenomenalism; a moderated form survives in verification theory by assigning each statement its own empirical confirmation conditions. Quine’s central objection is that scientific statements do not face experience one by one. A prediction follows only from a network comprising laws, mathematics, observational assumptions, boundary conditions and background beliefs. When prediction fails, logic alone does not identify which sentence must be surrendered—the Duhem–Quine point.

Accordingly, “the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science.” Our beliefs form a web touching experience at its edges. Peripheral observation sentences are normally revised first, while logic and mathematics lie centrally because altering them would disrupt much else; nevertheless no statement is unrevisable in principle. Conversely, almost any statement may be retained if compensating revisions are made elsewhere.

**Objection:** Holism makes evidence too indeterminate and erases the special role of observation. **Reply:** Quine admits graded proximity to sensory stimulation and pragmatic constraints—simplicity, conservatism and predictive success—without restoring unique sentence-level reduction.

**Evaluation:** The attack decisively weakens atomistic verification. It does not show that evidence is irrelevant; it relocates confirmation from isolated sentences to interconnected theory.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies both dogmas and reconstructs the second-dogma critique through underdetermination and the web of belief.

### Q4(a). How does Locke draw a distinction between primary and secondary qualities? Does he also draw a distinction between the idea of primary qualities and primary qualities as well as the idea of secondary qualities and secondary qualities? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Locke’s qualities and ideas · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Locke—primary and secondary qualities · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Slug:** `locke-qualities-ideas-resemblance`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish qualities from ideas throughout and explain the asymmetric resemblance thesis, its arguments and difficulties.

**Argument map:** quality = power in object; idea = perception in mind → primary qualities inseparable and intrinsic → their ideas resemble them → secondary qualities are powers grounded in primary texture → their ideas do not resemble object → relativity and causal science support distinction → Berkeley challenges resemblance and abstraction.

#### Model answer

Locke carefully distinguishes a **quality**, a power in an object to produce an idea, from an **idea**, the immediate object of perception in the mind. Primary qualities—solidity, extension, figure, motion/rest and number—are inseparable from a body through all alterations. Divide a grain repeatedly and each part remains extended, shaped and solid. Secondary qualities—colours, sounds, tastes, smells, heat and cold—are powers to produce sensations in perceivers by the size, figure, motion and texture of imperceptible particles.

Locke therefore makes two related distinctions:

1. **Ideas versus primary qualities:** The idea is a mental representation, not the bodily quality itself. Nevertheless, ideas of primary qualities **resemble** their causes: an idea of shape represents shape in the object.
2. **Ideas versus secondary qualities:** The sensation and the objective power are categorically different. Nothing like redness or sweetness exists in bodies as perceived. Bodies possess microstructural powers that cause those ideas under suitable conditions.

His arguments include inseparability, perceptual relativity and causal explanation. Lukewarm water feels hot to one hand and cold to another, so both sensations cannot resemble one intrinsic temperature-quality. Crushing an almond changes no basic corpuscular properties but produces a new taste and colour, showing that sensible qualities depend on arrangements and perceivers.

**Berkeley’s objection:** Primary qualities are as perception-relative as secondary ones—size and shape vary with distance and perspective. Further, an idea can resemble only another idea, not an unperceived material quality. Locke may reply that variation concerns appearances under conditions, while measurable structural relations remain in bodies.

**Qualification:** Locke sometimes calls all qualities “powers,” including primary qualities, creating textual tension. The intended asymmetry is that primary powers are grounded in intrinsic features resembling our ideas, whereas secondary powers produce non-resembling sensations.

### Q4(b). Does the sentence, ‘Whatever is coloured is extended’, satisfy the criterion of meaningfulness proposed by the logical positivists? Explain.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → verification and analytic meaning · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Logical positivism—verification theory of meaning · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Slug:** `positivism-coloured-extended`

**Demand decoding:** Classify the sentence under different positivist formulations and address whether it is analytic, empirical or a mixed conceptual rule.

**Argument map:** meaningful statements are analytic or empirically verifiable → colour can occur only over spatial field → if conceptual containment, analytic and meaningful → if treated as universal empirical law, no finite conclusive verification → weak verification gives experiential testability → sentence is meaningful, though status depends on analysis.

#### Model answer

Logical positivists initially held that a cognitively meaningful proposition is either analytically true/false by linguistic rules or empirically verifiable. “Whatever is coloured is extended” is not metaphysics: it connects concepts with identifiable conditions of application.

On a common analysis it is **analytic**. To call something coloured is to locate colour over some visual field or surface; an unextended patch of colour is not intelligible within the grammar of these terms. The predicate “extended” is thus contained in, or follows from rules governing, “coloured.” The statement is meaningful without requiring a separate confirming observation, like “all bachelors are unmarried.”

If interpreted instead as a universal hypothesis about all coloured physical objects, strict verification is impossible because infinitely many cases cannot be surveyed. Yet Ayer’s weaker criterion asks whether possible experience can render a proposition probable. Observing spatially extended coloured items and specifying what would count against the generalisation provides empirical content.

**Objection:** A point of coloured light may seem unextended. **Reply:** Either it occupies a minimal visual region or the case prompts revision of the conceptual rule; it does not make the sentence nonsensical.

**Evaluation:** The sentence satisfies positivist meaningfulness, most plausibly as analytic. Its ambiguous status also exposes the difficulty of drawing a sharp analytic/synthetic boundary later pressed by Quine.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies both strong/weak verification and the analytic alternative rather than merely asserting meaningfulness.

#### Model answer

Praśastapāda characterises a category or knowable referent (*padārtha*) by three common marks:

1. **Existence (*astitva*):** it has its own objective being or character and is not sheer fiction.
2. **Nameability (*abhidheyatva*):** it can be denoted by a word.
3. **Knowability (*jñeyatva*):** it can become an object of valid cognition.

Later interpreters therefore treat *astitva* as having a determinate objective status or own nature, not merely positive existence; alternatively, knowability and nameability become the widest marks.

**Evaluation:** The triad joins ontology, epistemology and language, but its extension to negative facts requires a broader notion of reality.

**Why this earns marks:** It states all three marks accurately and addresses the seventh-category qualification.

### Q5(c). Explain the conditions of valid hetu as propounded in Nyāya philosophy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → five marks of a valid reason · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain · Syllabus unit: Nyāya—inference (*anumāna*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Slug:** `nyaya-valid-hetu-five-marks`

**Demand decoding:** State all five conditions, demonstrate them through a standard inference, and connect failures to fallacies.

**Argument map:** reason present in subject → present in similar cases → absent from dissimilar cases → not contradicted → no equally strong counter-reason → satisfies pervasion and relevance → otherwise pseudo-reason (*hetvābhāsa*).

#### Model answer

A valid inferential reason (*hetu*) must possess five characteristics:

1. **Presence in the subject (*pakṣasattva*):** smoke must be present on the hill.
2. **Presence in positive instances (*sapakṣasattva*):** smoke occurs in similar cases where fire exists, such as a kitchen.
3. **Absence from negative instances (*vipakṣāsattva*):** smoke is absent where fire is absent, such as a lake.
4. **Non-contradiction (*abādhitatva*):** stronger knowledge must not defeat the conclusion; “fire is cold because it is a substance” is perceptually contradicted.
5. **Absence of a counterbalancing reason (*asatpratipakṣatva*):** no sound opposing reason should prove the negation of the proposed conclusion.

Together these establish that the reason belongs to the subject and is pervaded by the probandum (*sādhya*) through invariable concomitance (*vyāpti*). Their violation yields fallacies (*hetvābhāsa*) such as the unestablished, inconclusive, contradicted or counterbalanced reason.

**Evaluation:** The list integrates formal relevance with epistemic defeat, making Nyāya inference richer than a merely verbal syllogism.

**Why this earns marks:** It names, translates and applies all five conditions.

### Q5(e). Elucidate the role of catuṣkoṭi in explaining the nature of reality according to Mādhyamika school of thought.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → four-cornered negation and emptiness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Elucidate · Syllabus unit: Mādhyamika—emptiness and two truths · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Slug:** `madhyamaka-catuskoti-reality`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the four alternatives and their therapeutic function without treating emptiness as a fifth metaphysical position.

#### Model answer

The four-cornered schema (*catuṣkoṭi*) considers that a thing: (1) exists, (2) does not exist, (3) both exists and does not exist, or (4) neither exists nor does not exist. Nāgārjuna uses it dialectically, often rejecting all four when they purport to describe ultimate reality.

Absolute non-existence cannot explain conventional appearance and causal efficacy. “Both” combines opposed extremes; “neither,” if reified, becomes another thesis. The fourfold negation therefore dismantles conceptual positions generated by essentialism, not ordinary conventional discourse.

Its result is emptiness (*śūnyatā*): phenomena exist dependently and conventionally, but none possesses self-established essence. Emptiness itself is empty and must not become a hidden Absolute. Through the two truths, conventional practices and causation remain valid while ultimate analysis finds no intrinsic entity.

**Evaluation:** *Catuṣkoṭi* is a therapeutic logic of the Middle Way, avoiding both eternalism and annihilationism; it explains reality by exposing the limits of reifying predication.

**Why this earns marks:** It states all four alternatives and connects their rejection to dependent origination, emptiness and two truths.

### Q6(a). Explain the early Nyāya definition of perception. Why is this definition considered inadequate by the later Naiyāyikas?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → definition of perception · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Explain and criticise · Syllabus unit: Nyāya—perception (*pratyakṣa*) · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Slug:** `nyaya-perception-definition`

**Demand decoding:** Expound Gautama’s definition term-by-term, identify over/under-inclusion and show how later Nyāya shifts to direct cognition.

**Argument map:** perception arises from sense-object contact → non-verbal, non-errant, determinate → terms exclude testimony/error/doubt → divine, yogic and internal awareness challenge contact → memory and doubt complicate adjectives → later definition: cognition not instrumentally produced by another cognition; direct apprehension.

#### Model answer

The *Nyāya Sūtra* defines perception as cognition “produced by the contact of a sense-organ with its object, unnameable/non-verbal, non-errant and determinate” (*indriyārthasannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam*).

Each element performs a task. Sense-object contact marks perception’s immediate causal basis and excludes inference and testimony. “Non-verbal” (*avyapadeśya*) excludes cognition generated by words and, on one interpretation, designates pre-predicative indeterminate perception (*nirvikalpaka*). “Non-errant” excludes illusion; “determinate” (*vyavasāyātmaka*) excludes doubt and accommodates classificatory perception (*savikalpaka*).

Later Naiyāyikas find the definition inadequate:

- **Under-inclusion:** God’s omniscient perception, yogic perception and some self-awareness do not arise through ordinary sense-object contact. Mental perception and extraordinary relations also strain a narrow contact model.
- **Internal tension:** If perception is “non-verbal” in the sense of indeterminate, calling it determinate seems inconsistent. Later analysis distinguishes stages rather than placing both predicates straightforwardly together.
- **Redundancy/dispute:** Valid cognition is already non-errant; adding *avyabhicāri* may be unnecessary. Memory can be clear and non-verbal yet is not perception, so causal immediacy must be specified more carefully.
- **Ordinary cognition:** Much mature perception is concept-laden and linguistically expressible, though not produced by testimony.

Gaṅgeśa’s Navya-Nyāya defines perception broadly as cognition whose instrumental cause is not another cognition (*jñānākaraṇakaṃ jñānam*). In inference, prior cognition of pervasion functions instrumentally; in verbal knowledge, cognition of words does so. Perception is direct even where its auxiliary causes vary.

**Objection:** The later negative definition says what perception is not. **Reply:** It captures epistemic directness across ordinary and extraordinary cases, while detailed subtypes provide positive causal accounts.

**Evaluation:** The early definition is phenomenologically and causally rich but tied too closely to normal sensory episodes. The later account gains extension at the cost of intuitive specificity.

**Why this earns marks:** It translates every clause and explains precise reasons for later revision.

### Q6(b). “The Jaina metaphysics is relativistic and realistic pluralism.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → many-sided realism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Jainism—reality, *anekāntavāda* and *syādvāda* · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Slug:** `jaina-relativistic-realistic-pluralism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain all three descriptors while distinguishing perspectival relativity from subjective relativism.

**Argument map:** many real substances → souls and non-soul categories retain identity → each has infinite attributes/modes → permanence as substance plus change as modes → finite judgement grasps standpoint → *anekāntavāda*, *naya* and *syādvāda* qualify claims → realism constrains perspectives.

#### Model answer

Jaina metaphysics is **realistic** because objects exist independently of cognition and possess determinate characteristics. It is **pluralistic** because reality comprises indefinitely many individual souls (*jīva*) and non-soul entities (*ajīva*): matter, motion-medium, rest-medium, space and, in Digambara classification, time. No single substance absorbs the rest.

It is called **relativistic** because every real has infinitely many aspects and modes. A substance is permanent with respect to underlying identity yet changes through origination and cessation of modes. Finite knowers grasp it from limited standpoints (*naya*). Many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) therefore requires conditional predication (*syādvāda*): “in some respect” a pot exists, in another it does not—for example, it exists presently as clay-pot but did not exist as pot before production.

The sevenfold predication (*saptabhaṅgī*) combines affirmation, negation and inexpressibility under specified standpoints. It prevents one-sided dogmatism without asserting that contradictions are true in the same respect.

**Objection:** If every claim is relative, *anekāntavāda* itself is only relatively true. **Reply:** Jainas distinguish the comprehensive regulative insight that objects exceed partial judgements from any particular limited description.

**Evaluation:** “Perspectival realism” is more accurate than subjective relativism. Standpoints are constrained by a plural, mind-independent world.

**Why this earns marks:** It justifies each term and preserves the difference between qualified contradiction and arbitrary relativism.

### Q6(c). What are the arguments of the Buddhists to establish Kṣaṇikavāda? Do they necessarily lead to kṛtanāśa and akṛtābhyupagama?

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Argue and assess · Syllabus unit: Buddhism—momentariness, no-self and causation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Slug:** `buddhist-momentariness-karma-objections`

**Demand decoding:** State major proofs of momentariness, then answer the “destroyed deed/unperformed result” objection through causal-series continuity.

**Argument map:** existence = causal efficacy (*arthakriyākāritva*) → enduring entity cannot act successively without changing → produced things perish immediately → similarity creates appearance of duration → objection: doer perishes, so deed lost; successor did not act → reply: later moment is causally conditioned heir, neither identical nor wholly other.

#### Model answer

Buddhists defend momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*) chiefly through causal efficacy. To exist is to possess the capacity for effective action (*arthakriyākāritva*). If an entity endured unchanged through several moments, either it would produce all its effects at once, since its complete causal nature was present, or it would require new auxiliaries and therefore was not independently the same sufficient cause. Genuine causal action entails a new state; what is produced is therefore momentary.

Further, conditioned things arise through causes. Once their causal task is complete, no separate cause of destruction is needed: perishability belongs to the produced entity. Apparent duration is a rapid series of similar moment-events, like a flame-stream.

Critics allege **destruction of the deed** (*kṛtanāśa*): the acting moment perishes before receiving its fruit; and **acquisition of the unperformed** (*akṛtābhyupagama*): a later moment receives a result for an act it did not perform.

The Buddhist replies that moral responsibility requires causal continuity, not numerical identity of a substance-self. Each mental moment conditions its successor and transmits karmic potency within a continuum (*santāna*). The fruit belongs to the same causally connected series: the later person is neither strictly identical with nor wholly different from the earlier agent.

**Objection:** Mere causal connection may not ground desert. **Reply:** Even substance theories must explain psychological continuity; Buddhist dependent origination supplies a non-arbitrary transmission relation without an immutable self.

**Evaluation:** The two consequences do not logically follow if series-continuity is accepted, though whether continuity suffices for robust moral responsibility remains philosophically disputable.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the causal-efficiency proof and directly resolves both named objections.

### Q7(a). How do Mādhyamikas, Yogācāravādins and Sarvāstivādins differ among themselves concerning the nature of reality? How do Sarvāstivādins differ among themselves with regard to knowability of reality?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → Buddhist schools on reality and knowledge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Compare · Syllabus unit: Buddhism—schools · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Slug:** `buddhist-schools-reality-knowability`

**Demand decoding:** Compare ontologies without collapsing them, then explain the Vaibhāṣika–Sautrāntika epistemological division often grouped around Sarvāstivāda.

**Argument map:** Sarvāstivāda plural realism of dharmas across three times → Vaibhāṣika direct realism → Sautrāntika critical/representative realism and present momentariness → Yogācāra consciousness-only, three natures → Madhyamaka emptiness of all intrinsic nature and two truths → different targets and levels.

#### Model answer

The three traditions reject a permanent self but differ sharply about what remains ultimately real.

**Sarvāstivāda:** Reality consists of discrete conditioned and unconditioned factors (*dharmas*) possessing own-character (*svalakṣaṇa*). “All exists” (*sarvam asti*) means dharmas of past, present and future have ontological status, though only present dharmas exercise current causal efficacy. This temporal realism explains memory, prediction and karma without a substance-self.

**Yogācāra:** External objects independent of consciousness are rejected or bracketed as unwarranted. What appears as subject and object is “representation-only” (*vijñaptimātratā*), structured by store consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*) and karmic seeds. Its three natures distinguish the imagined subject-object duality (*parikalpita*), dependent causal flow (*paratantra*) and perfected absence of duality (*pariniṣpanna*). This is not a permanent individual mind but a dependently arisen stream.

**Madhyamaka:** Nāgārjuna denies intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) to external objects, consciousness and dharmas alike. Because all arise dependently, they are empty (*śūnya*). Conventionally, persons and causes function; ultimately, analysis yields no self-established entity. Emptiness is not another substance.

Regarding knowability, an important intra-Abhidharma contrast is:

- **Vaibhāṣika direct realism:** External particulars are directly perceived through sense-consciousness; cognition apprehends the object itself, though conceptual construction may follow.
- **Sautrāntika representational or critical realism:** External momentary objects exist, but perception immediately apprehends a mental image or aspect caused by them; the external cause is inferred. The object may have vanished by the time cognition occurs.

Strictly, Sautrāntikas criticised the full Sarvāstivādin three-time ontology, so “among Sarvāstivādins” should be read as the broad scholastic dispute emerging within/against that Abhidharma milieu, not as doctrinal identity.

**Comparison:** Sarvāstivāda preserves external dharma realism; Yogācāra relocates experienced duality within consciousness; Madhyamaka deconstructs intrinsic being in both. Their claims therefore cannot be flattened into one idealism.

**Evaluation:** Each solves a predecessor’s problem at a price: Sarvāstivāda risks reifying dharmas, Yogācāra risks idealism, and Madhyamaka must explain how radical emptiness preserves conventional knowledge.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately states ontology, epistemology, temporal status and the historical qualification.

### Q7(b). How does Sri Aurobindo’s integral yoga help in the realization of supramental consciousness? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sri Aurobindo](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/sri-aurobindo.md) → integral Yoga and Supermind · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Sri Aurobindo—evolution and integral Yoga · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Aurobindo](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Aurobindo.md) · **Slug:** `aurobindo-integral-yoga-supermind`

**Demand decoding:** Show the mechanism by which integral Yoga transforms rather than escapes nature, including ascent, descent and triple transformation.

**Argument map:** evolution is return from involution → ordinary mind is divided ignorance → aspiration/rejection/surrender open whole being → ascent to higher planes plus descent of force → psychic, spiritual, supramental transformations → Supermind integrates unity and multiplicity → transformed life, not private trance.

#### Model answer

Sri Aurobindo understands evolution as the progressive manifestation of consciousness already involved in matter. Mind is not the terminus: because it knows through division, it cannot fully reconcile spirit and world. Supermind is truth-consciousness, knowledge by identity that simultaneously holds unity and multiplicity.

Integral Yoga aids its realisation by transforming the whole being rather than securing an escape of an inner soul. Its practical movement combines aspiration for the Divine, rejection of egoistic and ignorant movements, and surrender to the Divine Śakti. An **ascent** opens consciousness beyond ordinary mind to higher mind, illumined mind, intuition and overmind; a corresponding **descent** brings peace, light and power into mind, life and body.

The process has three transformations:

1. **Psychic transformation:** the inner soul or psychic being comes forward and reorients nature toward truth.
2. **Spiritual transformation:** cosmic and transcendent consciousness replaces ego-centred awareness.
3. **Supramental transformation:** truth-consciousness descends and changes even the instruments of life and body.

**Objection:** Higher experience may be subjective and unverifiable. **Reply:** Aurobindo proposes disciplined, cumulative transformation and intersubjective yogic testimony, though not laboratory proof.

**Evaluation:** Integral Yoga uniquely unites liberation and terrestrial transformation. Its ambition is philosophically powerful, but the supramental stage remains difficult to publicly validate.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects the method, ascent/descent and all three transformations to supramental realisation.

### Q7(c). Distinguish between vivartavāda and pariṇāmavāda with reference to causation and explain how in the light of these theories Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja differ on the status of the world.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → apparent and real transformation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Distinguish and explain · Syllabus unit: Advaita and Viśiṣṭādvaita Vedānta · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Slug:** `vivarta-parinama-world-status`

**Demand decoding:** Define both causal models, apply them accurately to Brahman-world relations, and avoid saying Advaita treats the world as absolute non-being.

**Argument map:** transformation theory = cause really becomes effect → apparent transformation = cause unchanged while effect appears through ignorance → Śaṅkara: Brahman immutable; world empirically real but ultimately sublatable → Rāmānuja: Brahman qualified by real souls/matter; world real transformation of modes/body → unity differs.

#### Model answer

Transformation theory (*pariṇāmavāda*) holds that the effect is a real modification of its material cause, as milk becomes curd. Apparent-transformation theory (*vivartavāda*) holds that the cause does not really change; an effect appears through misapprehension, as a rope appears as a snake.

For Śaṅkara, attributeless Brahman (*nirguṇa Brahman*) is immutable, non-dual reality. A real transformation would compromise its changelessness and divide the Absolute. Through beginningless ignorance (*avidyā*) or appearance-power (*māyā*), names and forms are superimposed upon Brahman. The world is not sheer non-being like a square circle: it has transactional reality (*vyāvahārika-sattā*) and supports moral and cognitive practice. Yet it is sublatable by knowledge of Brahman and lacks ultimate reality (*pāramārthika-sattā*).

Rāmānuja rejects an indefinable world-appearance. Brahman, qualified by conscious souls (*cit*) and matter (*acit*), is the material and efficient cause. The world is a real manifestation or transformation of Brahman’s modes, often expressed through the body-soul relation: souls and matter constitute Brahman’s body while Brahman remains their inner controller. The divine essential nature is not corrupted when its dependent modes change.

**Advaitin objection:** Real transformation makes Brahman mutable. **Rāmānuja’s reply:** Change belongs to dependent modes, not to Brahman’s perfection. **Rāmānuja’s objection:** Illusory creation undermines scripture and devotion. **Advaitin reply:** empirical validity persists until liberating knowledge.

**Evaluation:** Śaṅkara safeguards absolute immutability through graded reality; Rāmānuja safeguards world, devotion and plurality through qualified unity. Their dispute concerns the kind of dependence, not simply “unreal versus real.”

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes causal theories and gives each school’s nuanced world-status with objections and replies.

### Q8(a). Explain the theory of Validity of Knowledge (prāmāṇyavāda) according to Mīmāṃsakas. How did they criticize the Nyāya theory of Validity?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → intrinsic validity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Directive: Explain and criticise · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya—validity of knowledge · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Slug:** `mimamsa-intrinsic-validity-nyaya`

**Demand decoding:** Separate production and ascertainment of validity/error, distinguish Bhāṭṭa and Prābhākara refinements, and reconstruct the regress/circularity critique of Nyāya.

**Argument map:** cognition presents object and normally authorises action → validity intrinsic (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*) → falsity known extrinsically by defect or sublation → Nyāya: both validity and invalidity extrinsic, inferred from successful activity/causal excellence → Mīmāṃsā: prior validation causes regress and cannot explain first action → later failure defeats initial entitlement.

#### Model answer

Mīmāṃsā defends the intrinsic validity of cognition (*svataḥ-prāmāṇyavāda*). A cognition, when produced, presents its object as such and is prima facie entitled to acceptance; it does not await certification by a second cognition. Knowledge motivates action immediately. Invalidity is extrinsic (*parataḥ-aprāmāṇya*): it is discovered later through awareness of a causal defect or through sublation, as when approaching “silver” reveals shell.

The schools formulate self-disclosure differently. The Bhāṭṭa theory of knownness (*jñātatā*) holds that cognition is inferred from the object’s acquired character of being-known, while its truth is presumed unless defeated. The Prābhākara doctrine of triple presentation (*tripuṭī-pratyakṣa*) says cognition simultaneously manifests knower, known and itself; all cognition is intrinsically valid qua cognition, and “error” is a failure to discriminate presented elements.

Nyāya holds validity and invalidity to be extrinsic (*parataḥ*). A cognition’s truth is known by inference from successful activity (*pravṛtti-sāmarthya*) and from defect-free causal conditions; error is known through failure or defects.

Mīmāṃsā criticises this:

1. If cognition must be validated before action, another cognition must validate the validator, generating infinite regress.
2. If successful action establishes truth, one must already trust cognitions of the action and success, risking circularity.
3. Ordinary agents act directly on cognition, showing an initial presumption of truth.
4. Doubt requires a positive defeater; universal initial doubt would make inquiry impossible.

Nyāya can reply that extrinsic ascertainment need not precede every action; it becomes explicit when truth is questioned. Mīmāṃsā nevertheless distinguishes entitlement from reflective confirmation more clearly.

**Evaluation:** Intrinsic validity is best understood as defeasible prima facie authority, not infallibility. It explains cognition’s default role while allowing later correction.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers production/knowledge of validity, both Mīmāṃsā schools, Nyāya’s alternative and the regress critique.

### Q8(b). Explain Rāmānuja’s conception of God and examine the difficulties he faced in explaining the relation of God to matter and spirit.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Viśiṣṭādvaita God-world relation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Explain and examine · Syllabus unit: Rāmānuja—God, soul and matter · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Slug:** `ramanuja-god-matter-spirit`

**Demand decoding:** Present qualified non-dualism and test whether body-soul and mode-substance relations preserve divine perfection, finite agency and unity.

**Argument map:** Brahman/Nārāyaṇa has infinite auspicious qualities → souls and matter are real, dependent attributes/modes/body → God is inner ruler and material-efficient cause → organic unity without identity → problems of evil, mutability, agency and category of embodiment → replies through dependence, karma and modal change.

#### Model answer

Rāmānuja’s Viśiṣṭādvaita affirms one Brahman—Nārāyaṇa—possessing infinite auspicious qualities, free from defects and accessible through devotion. Non-duality is **qualified**: conscious souls (*cit*) and unconscious matter (*acit*) are real and eternally dependent upon Brahman. They are Brahman’s modes or attributes and, through the body-soul relation (*śarīra-śarīrin-bhāva*), constitute the divine body whose inner ruler is God. Brahman is both material and efficient cause of the cosmos.

This raises difficulties. If matter and suffering souls are God’s body, evil seems to contaminate God. If the world is a real transformation, divine immutability appears threatened. If souls are wholly controlled modes, moral freedom becomes obscure; if genuinely independent, non-duality weakens. Finally, unlike an ordinary body, the cosmos is not spatially external and instrumentally moved, so the analogy may seem stretched.

Rāmānuja replies that bodily defects belong to dependent modes, not to the inner self; God remains pure while sustaining them. Creation is a transition of souls and matter from subtle to gross condition, not alteration of God’s essential perfection. Souls possess real but dependent agency, while inequalities follow beginningless karma rather than divine partiality. “Body” is defined functionally as what is completely supported, controlled and exists for another’s purpose.

**Evaluation:** The organic model preserves real plurality better than strict monism and deeper unity than dualism. Yet the evil and freedom problems remain pressures on any view making all finite reality God’s body.

**Why this earns marks:** It presents the conception positively before examining four specific relational difficulties and replies.

### Q8(c). Given its theory of causation, is it possible for Sāṃkhya to explain the presence of consciousness in the world? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sāṃkhya](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/samkhya.md) → causation and consciousness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Syllabus unit: Sāṃkhya—*satkāryavāda*, Puruṣa and Prakṛti · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Slug:** `samkhya-causation-consciousness`

**Demand decoding:** Test whether effect-pre-existence permits unconscious nature to generate consciousness, and explain Sāṃkhya’s dualist solution plus its interaction problem.

**Argument map:** effect pre-exists in material cause (*satkāryavāda*) → nature (*prakṛti*) is unconscious → consciousness cannot emerge from it → posit many conscious Puruṣas → proximity illuminates intellect and activates evolution → reflection causes apparent agency/enjoyment → dualism explains presence but not causal relation fully.

#### Model answer

Sāṃkhya accepts effect-pre-existence (*satkāryavāda*): an effect is a real manifestation of what already exists latently in its material cause. Nature (*prakṛti*), constituted by the three qualities (*guṇas*), evolves into intellect, ego, mind, senses and elements through real transformation (*pariṇāma*). But Prakṛti is intrinsically unconscious. If consciousness were produced from it, the effect would contain a character absent from its cause, contradicting *satkāryavāda*.

Sāṃkhya therefore does not derive consciousness from material evolution. It posits Puruṣa: many eternal, inactive, contentless centres of pure consciousness. The subtle intellect (*buddhi*) is transparent and reflects Puruṣa’s consciousness; consequently mental states appear conscious, while Puruṣa appears to think, act and enjoy. The proximity of Puruṣa disturbs the equilibrium of Prakṛti—illustrated by blind and lame partners or iron moved near a magnet—so evolution serves experience and liberation.

This explains consciousness’s **presence** without treating it as a material product. Yet it creates difficulties: how can inactive Puruṣa affect Prakṛti? How can mere proximity relate radically different principles? If consciousness is qualityless and plural, individuation is obscure.

Sāṃkhya replies that no literal transfer or efficient action occurs; beginningless conjunction and reflection suffice, and plurality is inferred from distinct births, deaths and liberations.

**Evaluation:** Given its causal theory, Sāṃkhya consistently requires consciousness as an independent principle. It explains irreducibility but leaves the relation between consciousness and nature less satisfactory than the principles separately.

**Why this earns marks:** It makes *satkāryavāda* do argumentative work and gives both Sāṃkhya’s solution and the interaction objection.
