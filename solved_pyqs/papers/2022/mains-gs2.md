## Q1. “The most significant achievement of modern law in India is the constitutionalization of environmental problems by the Supreme Court.” Discuss this statement with the help of relevant case laws.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Environmental constitutionalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Discuss with case laws  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Environmental constitutionalism  
**Secondary classification:** Environment and Ecology → Environmental regulation and remedies  
**Canonical owners:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/18_Supreme-Court.md) · [Environmental Impact Assessment and NGT](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Environment-and-Ecology/basic/16_Environmental-Impact-Assessment-and-NGT.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `supreme-court-environmental-constitutionalism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how constitutional interpretation converted environmental protection into enforceable public law, illustrate the doctrines through cases, and qualify judicial achievement with institutional limits.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional text → Article 21/PIL expansion → doctrines and remedies → cases → limits.

### Model answer

The Constitution originally contained no express enforceable environmental right. The Supreme Court constitutionalised the field by reading environmental quality into **Article 21**, linking it with Articles **48A and 51A(g)**, and relaxing standing through public-interest litigation.

In *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra* (1985), it protected ecology against destructive quarrying. *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (Oleum Gas Leak, 1987) evolved **absolute liability** for hazardous industries. *Subhash Kumar* (1991) recognised pollution-free water and air as part of life while warning against private-interest PILs. *Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum* (1996) treated sustainable development, the precautionary principle and polluter-pays principle as part of Indian environmental law. Continuing mandamus in *T.N. Godavarman* strengthened forest governance.

Thus, the Court transformed broad constitutional values into rights, duties, regulatory doctrines and effective remedies. Yet prolonged judicial supervision cannot replace scientifically capable regulators, democratic policy choices or consistent executive enforcement.

**Conclusion:** Environmental constitutionalism is a major judicial achievement, but its durable success depends on institutions implementing—not merely courts declaring—ecological rights.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces the constitutional method, accurately pairs doctrines with cases, and offers a separation-of-powers qualification.

## Q2. “Right of movement and residence throughout the territory of India are freely available to the Indian citizens, but these rights are not absolute.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Movement and residence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Articles 19(1)(d) and 19(1)(e)  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/07_Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `movement-residence-rights-reasonable-restrictions`

**Demand decoding:** State the freedoms, distinguish internal movement from travel abroad, explain Article 19(5), and test restrictions for legality and proportionality.

**Compact framework:** Right-holders and scope → permitted grounds → examples → reasonableness safeguard.

### Model answer

Articles **19(1)(d)** and **19(1)(e)** guarantee citizens the freedoms to move freely and to reside and settle anywhere within India. Travel abroad, however, falls under Article 21, not Article 19(1)(d).

Under **Article 19(5)**, the State may impose reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public or for protecting the interests of any Scheduled Tribe. Accordingly, proportionate restrictions may support:

- public order, health and safety, including quarantine, curfew or lawful externment;
- protection of tribal land, culture and resources through entry or settlement controls;
- regulation of movement in ecologically or strategically sensitive areas.

In *N.B. Khare v. State of Delhi* (1950), the Court treated reasonableness as a judicial question. Restrictions cannot be arbitrary, excessive or indefinite merely because a permitted ground is invoked.

**Conclusion:** The freedoms are constitutionally broad, but Article 19(5) reconciles mobility with collective interests; legality ultimately depends on necessity, proportionality and fair procedure.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact clauses, correct restriction grounds and the often-missed distinction between internal and foreign travel.

## Q3. To what extent, in your opinion, has the decentralisation of power in India changed the governance landscape at the grassroots?
**Primary route:** Governance → [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../topics/governance/local-governance-and-service-delivery.md) → Grassroots decentralisation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: To what extent  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Local Governance → Democratic and administrative decentralisation  
**Canonical owner:** [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/12_Local-Governance-and-Service-Delivery.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `decentralisation-grassroots-governance-landscape`

**Demand decoding:** Make a graded assessment: identify constitutional and practical changes, then explain why incomplete devolution limits transformation.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional change → democratic/service gains → devolution gap → qualified judgment.

### Model answer

The **73rd and 74th Amendments** transformed local bodies from discretionary State creations into constitutionally recognised institutions with regular elections, reservations for women and disadvantaged groups, Gram Sabhas and State Finance Commissions. Parts IX and IX-A, with the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules, widened the space for local planning and accountability.

This has changed grassroots governance substantially: millions gained representative experience; women and marginalised groups entered public decision-making; local bodies became platforms for selecting beneficiaries, implementing schemes, maintaining civic assets and conducting social audits. Kerala’s People’s Plan and participatory Gram Sabhas show the potential of local priority-setting.

However, Articles **243G and 243W** leave actual devolution largely to State legislation. In many States, functions remain with line departments or parastatals, finances are tied and unpredictable, staff answer upward, and SFC recommendations are delayed. PESA implementation is also uneven.

**Conclusion:** Decentralisation has democratised representation more decisively than it has decentralised administration. The landscape will be fully transformed only when the **3Fs—functions, funds and functionaries—**follow electoral authority.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “to what extent” with a balanced distinction between political inclusion and effective governing capacity.

## Q4. Discuss the role of the Vice-President of India as the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
**Primary route:** Polity → [President and Vice-President](../../topics/polity/president-and-vice-president.md) → Rajya Sabha chairmanship · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Vice-President → Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha  
**Canonical owner:** [President and Vice-President](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/15_President-and-Vice-President.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `vice-president-rajya-sabha-chairman-role`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the constitutional position, procedural and disciplinary functions, impartiality requirement and limits of the office.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional basis → conduct of business → order and committees → neutrality → limit.

### Model answer

Under Articles **64 and 89(1)**, the Vice-President is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. Though not a member of the House, the Chairman is its principal presiding authority and guardian of deliberative procedure.

The Chairman:

- presides over sittings, recognises members and allocates debate time;
- interprets the Constitution and Rules of Procedure within the House;
- decides admissibility of questions, motions and points of order;
- maintains decorum and may name disorderly members;
- refers matters to committees and nominates members to specified committees;
- decides disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review;
- has no ordinary vote but exercises a **casting vote** when votes are equally divided under Article 100.

The office requires political detachment so opposition voices receive fair space. When a resolution for the Vice-President’s removal is considered, the Chairman cannot preside (**Article 92**).

**Conclusion:** The Vice-President’s Rajya Sabha role is not ceremonial; impartial use of procedural authority is central to federal deliberation and parliamentary legitimacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers powers, constitutional limits and the democratic purpose of impartial chairmanship.

## Q5. Discuss the role of the National Commission for Backward Classes in the wake of its transformation from a statutory body to a constitutional body.
**Primary route:** Polity → [National Commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes](../../topics/polity/national-commissions-sc-st-bc.md) → National Commission for Backward Classes · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional Bodies → National Commission for Backward Classes  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Statutory and constitutional institutional design  
**Canonical owners:** [National Commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/National-Commissions-SC-ST-BC.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `ncbc-constitutional-status-role`

**Demand decoding:** Contrast the earlier statutory remit with Article 338B functions, explain enhanced authority, and assess continuing constraints.

**Compact framework:** Statutory origin → 102nd Amendment → functions and powers → 105th Amendment context → limits.

### Model answer

The NCBC was created under the **NCBC Act, 1993** after *Indra Sawhney* (1992), principally to examine requests for inclusion in or exclusion from the Central OBC list. The **102nd Constitutional Amendment, 2018** gave it constitutional status through **Article 338B**.

Its role includes monitoring safeguards; inquiring into complaints; advising on socio-economic development; evaluating progress; and reporting to the President. It has civil-court powers for investigation, and governments must consult it on major policy matters affecting backward classes, except State-list identification under Article 342A(3). Constitutional status improves visibility and continuity.

After the Maratha-reservation judgment, the **105th Amendment, 2021** restored States’ power to identify SEBCs for their own purposes, while the NCBC remains central to safeguards and the Central List.

Yet its advice is generally recommendatory; enforcement capacity, reliable caste data and timely governmental action remain weak.

**Conclusion:** Constitutionalisation broadened the NCBC from list-management towards rights oversight, but effective backward-class justice still requires evidence-based policy and executive compliance.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately distinguishes the 1993, 102nd-Amendment and 105th-Amendment stages.

## Q6. The Gati-Shakti Yojana needs meticulous coordination between the government and the private sector to achieve the goal of connectivity. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../topics/governance/government-policy-design-and-implementation.md) → PM Gati Shakti coordination · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Policy Implementation → Integrated infrastructure planning  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Infrastructure, logistics and PPPs  
**Canonical owners:** [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/02_Government-Policy-Design-and-Implementation.md) · [Infrastructure, PPPs, Logistics and Public Investment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pm-gati-shakti-government-private-coordination`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why multimodal connectivity is a coordination problem, specify complementary public/private roles, and identify safeguards.

**Compact framework:** Integrated objective → government role → private role → coordination failures → institutional remedy.

### Model answer

PM Gati Shakti is a GIS-enabled National Master Plan intended to integrate roads, railways, ports, airports, waterways, mass transport and logistics infrastructure. Connectivity therefore depends not on isolated projects but on synchronised networks.

Government must provide interoperable spatial data, coordinated pipelines, clearances, trunk infrastructure, stable regulation and fair contracts. Ministries, States and local bodies must sequence utilities and prevent repeated digging or missing last-mile links.

Private firms contribute capital, construction capacity, logistics technology and demand information. Early consultation improves route design; sound PPPs allocate risks to the party best able to manage them.

Coordination may fail through departmental silos, opaque bidding, weak contract enforcement, data-security concerns or social and environmental shortcuts. Common standards, independent appraisal, transparent risk-sharing, time-bound dispute resolution and public dashboards are therefore essential.

**Conclusion:** Gati Shakti succeeds when the State coordinates the network and safeguards public value while private participation supplies innovation and execution—not when either substitutes for the other.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the mechanism of coordination rather than merely praising infrastructure investment.

## Q7. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 remains only a legal document without intense sensitisation of government functionaries and citizens regarding disability. Comment.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Disability-Inclusive Policy](../../topics/social-justice/disability-inclusive-policy.md) → Sensitisation and implementation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Persons with Disabilities → Rights-based implementation  
**Canonical owner:** [Persons with Disabilities](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/11_Persons-with-Disabilities.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpwd-act-sensitisation-implementation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the Act’s rights-based promise, show why attitudes determine implementation, and clarify that sensitisation must accompany enforcement and resources.

**Compact framework:** Legal entitlements → attitudinal barriers → administrative effects → combined remedy.

### Model answer

The **Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016**, aligned with the UNCRPD, expanded recognised disabilities, prohibited discrimination, required reasonable accommodation and accessibility, and provided reservations for persons with benchmark disabilities in government employment and higher education.

Yet rights are mediated by officials, employers, teachers, health workers, families and citizens. A charity or medical view of disability can produce humiliating certification, inaccessible offices and examinations, denial of accommodation, institutionalisation and exclusion from work or education. Unaware officials may treat accessibility as optional; social stigma can prevent families from claiming even available entitlements.

Sensitisation should therefore include disability-rights training, interaction with organisations of persons with disabilities, accessible communication, curriculum reform and accountability of departmental nodal officers.

However, awareness alone cannot implement the Act. Accessible budgets and procurement, reliable data, enforceable timelines, trained Commissioners, grievance remedies and universal-design audits are equally necessary.

**Conclusion:** The Act becomes a living rights instrument only when informed attitudes and capable institutions jointly replace paternalism with autonomy, dignity and reasonable accommodation.

**Why this earns marks:** It validates the claim while avoiding the trap of treating sensitisation as a substitute for enforcement.

## Q8. Reforming the government delivery system through the Direct Benefit Transfer Scheme is a progressive step, but it has its limitations too. Comment.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../topics/governance/government-policy-design-and-implementation.md) → Direct Benefit Transfer · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Public Service Delivery → Direct Benefit Transfer  
**Canonical owner:** [Public Finance and Service Delivery Tools](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/13_Public-Finance-and-Service-Delivery-Tools.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `direct-benefit-transfer-gains-limitations`

**Demand decoding:** Balance delivery gains against exclusion, infrastructure and design risks, distinguishing cash-transfer efficiency from welfare adequacy.

**Compact framework:** Delivery reform → benefits → exclusion/design limits → safeguards → judgment.

### Model answer

Direct Benefit Transfer sends benefits to identified bank accounts, often using the JAM architecture. It is progressive because it can reduce intermediaries and duplicate records, create an auditable payment trail, deliver money quickly and give beneficiaries greater choice.

Its limitations are substantial:

- authentication, seeding or database errors can exclude genuine claimants;
- weak connectivity, distant banking correspondents and cash-out costs shift burdens onto citizens;
- dormant or male-controlled accounts may not empower the intended woman beneficiary;
- inaccurate eligibility lists are digitised rather than corrected;
- cash may be unsuitable where functioning markets do not exist or where in-kind nutrition and health guarantees are important;
- centralised personal data raises privacy and security concerns;
- failed transactions often lack timely, human grievance redress.

DBT also cannot compensate for inadequate benefit amounts or weak public services.

**Conclusion:** DBT should be an accountable delivery option, not a compulsory technology test. Offline alternatives, minimal-data design, social audits, updated registries and accessible appeals are essential to combine efficiency with inclusion.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both administrative efficiency and the citizen-side risks that determine actual delivery.

## Q10. Do you think that BIMSTEC is a parallel organisation like the SAARC? What are the similarities and dissimilarities between the two? How are Indian foreign policy objectives realized by forming this new organisation?
**Primary route:** International Relations → [Regional, Global and Minilateral Groupings](../../topics/international-relations/regional-global-and-minilateral-groupings.md) → BIMSTEC and SAARC · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Assess; Compare; Explain  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Regional Groupings → BIMSTEC and SAARC  
**Canonical owner:** [Regional, Global and Minilateral Groupings](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/10_Regional-Global-and-Minilateral-Groupings.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `bimstec-saarc-comparison-indian-objectives`

**Demand decoding:** Answer all three limbs: reject or qualify “parallel”, compare institutional and geographic features, and connect BIMSTEC to specific Indian objectives.

**Compact framework:** Qualified position → similarities → differences → Indian objectives → limit.

### Model answer

BIMSTEC is not a replica of SAARC; it is a complementary Bay of Bengal grouping that also gives India an avenue for cooperation when SAARC is paralysed.

Both are intergovernmental, consensus-based organisations with secretariats and agendas covering economic, social and technical cooperation. However, **SAARC** comprises eight South Asian States, includes Pakistan and Afghanistan, and has been constrained by India–Pakistan conflict. **BIMSTEC** links five South Asian States with Myanmar and Thailand, excludes Pakistan, bridges South and Southeast Asia, and gives greater emphasis to connectivity, security, trade and the blue economy. Its **2022 Charter** strengthened institutional identity.

For India, BIMSTEC connects **Neighbourhood First**, **Act East** and **SAGAR**; develops its Northeast and regional transport corridors; deepens ties with Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Thailand; enables counter-terrorism and disaster-management cooperation; and supports energy and supply-chain integration around the Bay of Bengal.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers comparison and foreign-policy utility within a compact, explicitly qualified structure.

## Q11. Discuss the procedures to decide the disputes arising out of the election of a Member of the Parliament or State Legislature under The Representation of the People Act, 1951. What are the grounds on which the election of any returned candidate may be declared void? What remedy is available to the aggrieved party against the decision? Refer to the case laws.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Election petitions under the RPA, 1951 · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Discuss; Identify; Explain remedy  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Election disputes under the Representation of the People Act, 1951  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpa-1951-election-petitions-void-grounds-remedy`

**Demand decoding:** Present the statutory procedure, exhaustively organise Section 100 grounds, identify the appellate remedy, and use cases for the jurisdictional principles.

**Compact framework:** Article 329(b) → election petition procedure → Section 100 grounds → High Court orders → Supreme Court appeal.

### Model answer

Article **329(b)** requires an election to Parliament or a State legislature to be challenged only through an election petition presented in the manner prescribed by law. Under the **Representation of the People Act, 1951**:

1. **Sections 80 and 80A:** an election petition lies to the jurisdictional High Court; no ordinary civil suit is maintainable.
2. **Section 81:** a candidate or elector must ordinarily present it within **45 days** from the election of the returned candidate.
3. The petition must plead material facts, join parties under Section 82 and satisfy special requirements for corrupt-practice allegations.
4. The High Court tries it substantially according to civil-trial procedure and may dismiss it, declare the returned candidate’s election void, or in a proper case declare another candidate elected (**Sections 98 and 101**).

Under **Section 100**, election may be void because:

- the returned candidate lacked qualification or incurred disqualification;
- the returned candidate, election agent, or another person with their consent committed a corrupt practice;
- a nomination was improperly rejected;
- the result was materially affected by improper acceptance of nomination, specified corrupt practice, improper reception/refusal/rejection of votes, or non-compliance with the Constitution, Act or rules.

An aggrieved party may appeal to the **Supreme Court under Section 116A**, ordinarily within 30 days.

In *N.P. Ponnuswami* (1952) and *Mohinder Singh Gill* (1978), the Court stressed that elections should not be interrupted midstream; the election petition is the post-election remedy. *Jyoti Basu* (1982) affirmed that election disputes are creatures of statute.

**Conclusion:** The scheme combines electoral continuity with specialised, time-bound judicial review after completion of the election.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers procedure, grounds and remedy separately while accurately locating the High Court and Supreme Court jurisdictions.

## Q12. Discuss the essential conditions for exercise of the legislative powers by the Governor. Discuss the legality of re-promulgation of ordinances by the Governor without placing them before the Legislature.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Governor and Chief Minister](../../topics/polity/governor-and-cm.md) → Ordinance-making power · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Discuss; Assess legality  
**Primary classification:** Polity → State Executive → Governor’s legislative and ordinance powers  
**Canonical owner:** [Governor, Chief Minister and State Council](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/19_Governor-CM-State-Council.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `governor-legislative-powers-ordinance-repromulgation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the Governor’s principal legislative powers and constitutional conditions, then decide the legality of repeated ordinances using controlling cases.

**Compact framework:** Legislative functions → Article 213 conditions → duration and laying → re-promulgation doctrine → conclusion.

### Model answer

The Governor’s legislative role is constitutionally structured, not a personal law-making authority. The Governor summons, prorogues and may dissolve the Assembly under **Article 174**; addresses and sends messages to the Legislature under Articles **175–176**; and under **Article 200** assents to, returns a non-Money Bill, withholds assent, or reserves a Bill for the President.

The exceptional power under **Article 213** may be exercised when the Legislature is not fully in session and circumstances require **immediate action**. An ordinance has the force of a State law but:

- must lie within the State Legislature’s competence and respect Fundamental Rights;
- is ordinarily issued on ministerial advice;
- requires prior presidential instructions in constitutionally specified cases;
- must be laid before the Legislature and ceases six weeks after reassembly, unless earlier withdrawn or disapproved.

Re-promulgation without legislative consideration subverts these temporal and democratic safeguards. In *D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987), the Supreme Court called serial re-promulgation a fraud on the Constitution. A seven-judge Bench in *Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017) held that laying an ordinance before the Legislature is a mandatory constitutional obligation and that re-promulgation to bypass debate is unconstitutional. Ordinance satisfaction and constitutional compliance are judicially reviewable.

Only a genuinely fresh necessity followed by prompt legislative accountability could justify a new ordinance; routine renewal cannot.

**Conclusion:** Article 213 bridges an urgent legislative gap, but re-promulgation without placement before the House converts an exception into executive legislation and is constitutionally impermissible.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers both ordinary legislative functions and the precise legality of re-promulgation through controlling precedents.

## Q13. “While the national political parties in India favour centralisation, the regional parties are in favour of State autonomy.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Federal System](../../topics/polity/federal-system.md) → Party system and State autonomy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Political parties and centralisation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Political parties → National and regional incentives  
**Canonical owners:** [Political Parties](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/43_Political-Parties.md) · [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/12_Federal-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `national-regional-parties-centralisation-state-autonomy`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why party scale creates different federal incentives, test the proposition historically and institutionally, and reject it as an absolute rule.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional setting → national-party incentives → regional-party incentives → counter-evidence → qualified judgment.

### Model answer

India’s Constitution creates a federal polity with a strong Union. Party competition influences how this design operates, giving the statement considerable—but not universal—validity.

**Why national parties tend towards centralisation**

- A nationwide mandate encourages uniform programmes, common markets and centrally sponsored schemes.
- National leadership seeks coordinated responses on security, macroeconomic stability and inter-State externalities.
- A party controlling both levels may use party discipline, Governors or fiscal instruments to align States with the Union.

**Why regional parties demand autonomy**

- Their legitimacy rests on State-specific language, culture, resources and development claims.
- They seek larger untied fiscal transfers, limits on gubernatorial discretion, greater say in concurrent subjects and protection against centrally imposed schemes.
- Regional parties in Union coalitions after 1989 strengthened negotiated federalism and made forums such as the Inter-State Council politically relevant.

Yet the binary is incomplete. National parties may champion decentralisation when in State opposition; regional parties often centralise power within their own States and seek strong Union action that benefits them. Coalition partners also exchange autonomy claims for national influence.

The Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions’ emphasis on consultation, restraint in Article 356 and cooperative institutions addresses structural tensions beyond party labels.

**Conclusion:** Party scale shapes federal preferences, but office, coalition arithmetic and the issue involved often matter more than whether a party is “national” or “regional.” Institutionalised consultation is therefore safer than relying on partisan federalism.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the proposition’s causal logic, supplies counter-evidence and reaches a properly qualified conclusion.

## Q14. Critically examine the procedures through which the Presidents of India and France are elected.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → Presidential elections in India and France · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Critically examine  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitution → Election of Presidents  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → President and Vice-President → Indian presidential election  
**Canonical owners:** [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md) · [President and Vice-President](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/15_President-and-Vice-President.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-france-presidential-election-comparison`

**Demand decoding:** Compare electorates, voting systems, mandate and supervision, then evaluate whether each procedure fits its constitutional executive.

**Compact framework:** India procedure → France procedure → comparison → democratic strengths and limits → institutional fit.

### Model answer

India and France are republics, but their Presidents are elected differently because they occupy different constitutional positions.

| Dimension | India | France |
|---|---|---|
| Electorate | Elected MPs and elected MLAs of States, Delhi and Puducherry under Article 54 | Citizens through direct universal suffrage |
| Method | Proportional representation by single transferable vote; secret ballot | Two-round majority system; if no candidate wins an absolute majority, the leading two contest the run-off |
| Vote value | MLA votes are weighted for inter-State uniformity; MP votes balance Union and States under Article 55 | Each citizen’s vote has equal value |
| Supervision/disputes | Election Commission conducts; Supreme Court decides disputes under Article 71 | Constitutional Council supervises, rules on disputes and proclaims results |
| Mandate | Indirect, federal and broadly representative | Direct popular mandate |

India’s system fits a parliamentary federation: weighting protects the federal principle and preferential voting encourages a broad coalition, while avoiding a rival popular mandate to the Council of Ministers. However, party arithmetic may make the result predictable and citizens participate only indirectly.

France has used direct election since the 1962 reform. Its run-off confers majority legitimacy appropriate to a powerful President in a semi-presidential system and permits voter choice between finalists. Yet it can personalise politics, marginalise smaller candidates after the first round and create institutional conflict during “cohabitation.”

**Conclusion:** Neither method is inherently superior. India prioritises federal-parliamentary balance; France prioritises direct majoritarian legitimacy. Each procedure is intelligible only in relation to the office’s actual powers.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares equivalent procedural dimensions and critically links electoral design to constitutional function.

## Q15. Discuss the role of the Election Commission of India in the light of the evolution of the Model Code of Conduct.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Model Code of Conduct · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Election Commission → Model Code of Conduct  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `election-commission-model-code-evolution-role`

**Demand decoding:** Trace the MCC’s development, connect enforcement to Article 324, assess ECI tools and achievements, and examine the non-statutory code’s limitations.

**Compact framework:** Evolution → constitutional basis → enforcement role → adaptation → limits and reform.

### Model answer

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) evolved from a code used in the **1960 Kerala Assembly election**. The Election Commission circulated a minimum code in 1968, expanded it in 1979 to regulate the party in power, and enforced it more assertively from the 1990s. It remains a consensus-based, non-statutory code, operating from announcement of the election schedule until completion.

Under **Article 324**, the ECI uses the MCC to secure a level playing field. It:

- restrains misuse of official machinery, public advertising, grants and policy announcements;
- regulates campaign speech, processions, meetings, polling-day conduct and manifestos;
- seeks reports, issues notices, censures leaders, advises campaign restrictions and orders transfer of officials;
- refers conduct violating election or criminal law for prosecution;
- adapts instructions to social media, paid news and digital campaigning.

In *Union of India v. Harbans Singh Jalal* (2001), application of the MCC from announcement of elections was upheld. In *S. Subramaniam Balaji* (2013), the Supreme Court directed ECI guidelines on manifestos while noting gaps in the existing corrupt-practice law.

The MCC’s speed and flexibility are strengths, but non-statutory status, delayed or inconsistent orders, limited sanctions and opaque reasoning can weaken deterrence. Full criminalisation could invite litigation that paralyses campaigns.

**Conclusion:** The better course is a transparent, time-bound ECI enforcement framework, statutory backing for serious violations, and proportionate sanctions with prompt review.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats evolution, constitutional authority, enforcement practice and reform as connected parts of the demand.

## Q16. Besides the welfare schemes, India needs deft management of inflation and unemployment to serve the poor and the underprivileged sections of the society. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Economy → [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) → Inflation, employment and welfare · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Welfare and Inclusion → Macroeconomic foundations  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Inflation and employment  
**Canonical owners:** [Inflation, Price Indices and Business Cycles](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/03_Inflation-Price-Indices-and-Business-Cycles.md) · [Employment, Labour Codes, Skills and Demographic Dividend](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `inflation-unemployment-welfare-poor`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why inflation and unemployment can neutralise welfare, propose calibrated management of both, and preserve welfare as a necessary social floor.

**Compact framework:** Welfare limits → inflation transmission → unemployment transmission → policy mix → qualification.

### Model answer

Welfare schemes protect minimum consumption and capabilities, but they cannot substitute for stable prices and productive work. Inflation and unemployment directly determine whether poor households can convert entitlements into dignity.

**Inflation** is regressive because food, fuel and rent absorb a larger share of poor households’ budgets. It erodes fixed cash transfers, savings and real wages. Management requires credible RBI monetary policy alongside fiscal and supply measures: timely release of food stocks, better storage and logistics, calibrated trade policy, competition against hoarding, and targeted support rather than untargeted subsidies that enlarge demand and deficits.

**Unemployment and underemployment** deprive households of income, skills and bargaining power. Policy must stimulate labour-intensive manufacturing, construction and services; improve MSME credit and market access; align ITIs, apprenticeships and skilling with employer demand; strengthen MGNREGA during rural distress; and reduce women’s employment barriers through safe transport and care services. Public investment in health, education and infrastructure raises both capability and job demand.

The objectives can conflict: abrupt monetary tightening may reduce inflation but weaken employment. “Deft management” therefore requires coordinated, data-responsive monetary, fiscal, trade and labour policy, with special protection against food inflation.

Welfare nevertheless remains indispensable for children, older persons, persons with disabilities and workers facing shocks; employment alone does not ensure access to health or education.

**Conclusion:** Inclusion rests on a three-part compact—social protection for security, price stability for purchasing power, and decent employment for durable economic agency.

**Why this earns marks:** It establishes causal links, offers a balanced policy mix and avoids falsely opposing welfare to macroeconomic management.

## Q17. Do you agree with the view that increasing dependence on donor agencies for development reduces the importance of community participation in the development process? Justify your answer.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Development Processes and the Development Industry](../../topics/governance/development-processes-and-the-development-industry.md) → Donor dependence and community participation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Take a position; Justify  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Development Process → Donors and community participation  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Participatory governance  
**Canonical owners:** [Development Processes and the Development Industry](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/03_Development-Processes-and-the-Development-Industry.md) · [Participatory Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/14_Participatory-Governance.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `donor-dependence-community-participation-development`

**Demand decoding:** Take a qualified position, explain mechanisms by which donor dependence can crowd out participation, show counter-possibilities, and specify an accountable partnership model.

**Compact framework:** Qualified agreement → crowding-out mechanisms → counter-case → safeguards → judgment.

### Model answer

Increasing donor dependence can reduce community participation, but the outcome follows from programme design rather than foreign funding automatically.

**Crowding-out risks include:**

- donor priorities and measurable “deliverables” may displace needs identified by Gram Sabhas or neighbourhood groups;
- short project cycles and tied funds create upward accountability to financiers instead of downward accountability to citizens;
- consultants and parallel implementation units may bypass Panchayats, local knowledge and State capacity;
- communities may be treated as beneficiaries invited for token consultation after key decisions;
- externally sustained services may collapse when funding exits.

Participation matters because it improves problem definition, local contribution, beneficiary selection, monitoring and ownership. Constitutional decentralisation, PESA Gram Sabhas, self-help groups and social audits demonstrate institutional routes for such agency.

However, donors can strengthen participation by financing community organisations, technical capacity, transparent social audits and innovations that governments later scale. Nor is participation inherently democratic: elite capture, exclusion of women or marginal groups, and limited technical capacity require safeguards.

A sound model therefore makes donors **enablers**, not agenda owners: communities should help set priorities and monitor outcomes; elected local bodies should hold authority; government should ensure rights and continuity; and donor funds should be transparent, flexible and supported by an exit strategy.

**Conclusion:** Dependence reduces participation when money centralises knowledge and accountability. It can enhance participation when finance is deliberately subordinated to representative local institutions and inclusive community control.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a justified, non-binary answer and distinguishes genuine participation from donor-managed consultation.

**Metadata:** 2022 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Analyse  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Education → RTE, incentives and social awareness  
**Canonical owner:** [Education and Human Resource Development](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/04_Education-and-Human-Resource-Development.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rte-act-incentives-awareness-schooling`

**Demand decoding:** Separate the RTE’s rights-and-supply design from incentive schemes, analyse why awareness shapes demand, and show why neither incentives nor awareness can replace school quality.

**Compact framework:** RTE guarantee → incentive gap → awareness mechanism → implementation deficits → integrated remedy.

### Model answer

Article **21A** and the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** make elementary education for children aged 6–14 a legal entitlement. The Act mandates neighbourhood access, prescribed school and teacher norms, non-discrimination, and 25% entry-level reservation in specified private unaided schools under Section 12(1)(c).

Yet it is primarily a rights-and-supply statute, not a complete incentive architecture. Meals, scholarships, bicycles, hostels or conditional support arise from other policies. Such incentives offset poverty, distance and opportunity costs, but cannot by themselves create sustained educational demand.

These rulings illustrate that legal design must still be translated into social legitimacy and administrative capacity.

**Conclusion:** RTE needs a threefold strategy: enforceable quality, targeted support that lowers attendance costs, and community awareness that treats schooling as a right and public good.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses the exact relationship among statutory entitlement, incentives, awareness and learning quality.
