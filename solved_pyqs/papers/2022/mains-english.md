### Q1(a). “Mathematics: A mirror of modern civilization.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Mathematics: A mirror of modern civilization.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → mathematics, technology and social organisation · `qualifying-english/essay-mathematics-modern-civilization.md`

**Model essay**

Civilizations reveal themselves through their buildings, institutions, arts and machines. Beneath all these visible forms lies a quieter language: mathematics. Numbers measure what a society values; geometry shapes its spaces; statistics guide its governments; and algorithms increasingly organise its daily life. Mathematics is therefore not merely a school subject or an abstract science. It is a mirror in which the ambitions, achievements and contradictions of modern civilization can be seen.

The modern world depends upon measurement. Clocks divide time precisely, maps locate every place, and accounts record production and exchange. Governments estimate populations, income, disease and unemployment through numerical data. Without such measurement, a large and complex society could neither plan services nor compare outcomes. Mathematics converts scattered experience into patterns that institutions can understand and act upon.

Science and technology make this dependence still clearer. Physics expresses natural laws through equations; engineering uses calculation to turn those laws into bridges, aircraft and power systems. Modern medicine relies on dosage, imaging, epidemiology and clinical statistics. Weather forecasts, satellite navigation and telecommunications all require mathematical models. Even an ordinary mobile-phone call rests upon coding, probability and signal processing. Civilization’s technological confidence is thus inseparable from its mathematical capacity.

Mathematics also mirrors the economic structure of the age. Banking, taxation, insurance and global trade depend on calculation. Digital payments translate value into electronic records, while businesses use data to predict demand and manage supply chains. Algorithms recommend products, approve loans and select information for users. This increases speed and convenience, but it also raises questions. A mathematical model may appear neutral while reproducing biased assumptions or excluding what cannot easily be counted.

The democratic state likewise uses numbers. Election results determine representation; budgets express public priorities; census figures influence welfare and delimitation. Reliable statistics can expose inequality and test whether policy has worked. Yet figures can also be selectively presented. An average may conceal disparities, and a percentage without context may mislead. A numerate citizen must therefore ask not only whether a calculation is correct, but what was measured, what was omitted and who interprets the result.

Mathematics reflects modern civilization’s desire for order, prediction and control. Models help anticipate epidemics, climate change and traffic flows. They allow society to prepare for risks that no individual can directly observe. Nevertheless, reality is more complex than any model. Human beings possess motives, emotions and moral claims that cannot be reduced completely to variables. When efficiency alone determines education, healthcare or justice, numerical precision may coexist with human insensitivity.

The history of mathematics also challenges the idea that civilization is the achievement of one region. Indian contributions such as the decimal place-value system and zero, developments in West Asia, Greek geometry and later European advances became part of a shared intellectual inheritance. Modern science grew through transmission, translation and improvement across cultures. Mathematics is universal not because it belongs nowhere, but because many societies have contributed to it.

Education should therefore teach mathematics as a mode of reasoning rather than a collection of procedures. Logical thinking, estimation and the ability to interpret data protect citizens from fraud and misinformation. At the same time, mathematical training should be joined with history and ethics so that technical power remains accountable to human purposes.

Mathematics mirrors modern civilization because it displays both our creative intelligence and our preference for quantification. It has enlarged freedom by making complex cooperation possible, yet it can become an instrument of surveillance or exclusion when treated as infallible. The image in the mirror is therefore mixed. Mathematics shows a civilization capable of extraordinary reason, but still responsible for deciding wisely what its calculations should serve.

**Why this earns marks:** It develops the metaphor through science, economy, governance and ethics, recognises mathematics as a multicultural inheritance, and qualifies numerical power with human judgement.

### Q1(b). “New frontiers of science need to be explored in the current times.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “New frontiers of science need to be explored in the current times.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → scientific exploration, public purpose and responsibility · `qualifying-english/essay-new-frontiers-science.md`

**Model essay**

Science advances when human curiosity moves beyond the boundary of established knowledge. Earlier explorations of electricity, microbes and space transformed life in ways their pioneers could not fully predict. The present age faces climate change, emerging diseases, resource pressure and rapid technological disruption. Exploring new scientific frontiers is therefore not a luxury. It is necessary for survival, prosperity and a deeper understanding of nature, provided that discovery remains guided by ethics and public purpose.

One urgent frontier is clean energy. Societies need abundant power, but continued dependence on fossil fuels intensifies pollution and global warming. Better energy storage, safer nuclear technologies, green hydrogen and efficient renewable systems can support development without repeating an environmentally destructive path. Scientific work must also improve buildings, transport and industrial processes, because the cleanest unit of energy is often the one not wasted.

Medicine presents another frontier. Recent epidemics have shown how quickly a local outbreak can become a global emergency. Research in vaccines, diagnostics, genomics and public-health modelling can shorten response time and enable more precise treatment. Equally important are neglected diseases, antimicrobial resistance and affordable medical devices. A frontier is socially valuable not merely because it is technologically impressive, but because it addresses suffering that markets may overlook.

Space and ocean research expand both knowledge and practical capacity. Satellites support communication, navigation, weather forecasting and disaster management. Planetary exploration helps explain the origin of the solar system and may reveal resources or conditions beyond Earth. The deep ocean, still imperfectly known, influences climate and contains remarkable ecosystems. Exploration, however, should not become an excuse for territorial rivalry or uncontrolled extraction. International cooperation and environmental safeguards must accompany scientific capability.

Artificial intelligence, quantum technology and advanced materials may reshape production and communication. They can help analyse complex data, design medicines and improve services. Yet they may also displace workers, concentrate power and weaken privacy. Scientific possibility does not itself settle social desirability. Researchers, lawmakers and citizens must examine bias, safety, accountability and access before systems become too deeply embedded to govern.

Fundamental research deserves protection even when no immediate application is visible. Theories developed from pure curiosity have often produced later technologies. A society that funds only short-term commercial projects may become efficient at imitation but weak in original discovery. Universities require intellectual freedom, stable support and collaboration across disciplines. Failure must be accepted as part of genuine research, because an experiment that disproves an assumption may still advance knowledge.

Exploration must also become more inclusive. Talent is lost when scientific education is inaccessible to rural students, women or disadvantaged communities. Diverse researchers ask different questions and notice different needs. Scientific knowledge should be communicated in clear language so that public debate is informed rather than divided between blind worship and irrational fear.

Ethics is not an obstacle placed after discovery; it is part of responsible exploration. Gene editing, autonomous weapons and mass data collection can affect human dignity and future generations. Transparent regulation, independent review and international norms are essential. The benefits of publicly supported research should not be confined to those who can pay the highest price.

The new frontiers of science must be explored because old methods cannot solve every new problem. But progress should not be measured only by distance travelled, speed achieved or data collected. Its true measure is whether knowledge enlarges human capability while protecting justice and the natural world. Curiosity gives science its movement; responsibility must give it direction.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects contemporary scientific frontiers with social needs, defends fundamental inquiry, and integrates inclusion, regulation and ethical limits.

### Q1(c). “Education is a means of shaping character and of social change.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “Education is a means of shaping character and of social change.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → education, character and social transformation · `qualifying-english/essay-education-character-social-change.md`

**Model essay**

Education is often judged by examinations, degrees and employment. These are important outcomes, but they do not exhaust its purpose. Education introduces a person to ways of thinking, standards of conduct and experiences beyond the immediate self. At the same time, by widening knowledge and opportunity, it can challenge inherited inequality. It is thus both a means of shaping character and an instrument of social change.

Character is formed through repeated choices. A good education develops the ability to distinguish evidence from assertion, long-term good from immediate temptation and legitimate disagreement from personal hostility. Literature enlarges sympathy by allowing students to enter unfamiliar lives. History reveals the consequences of courage, prejudice and public choices. Science cultivates honesty before evidence, while games and collective work can teach discipline, fairness and cooperation.

Character education should not mean forcing students to repeat moral slogans. Values become meaningful when institutions practise them. A school cannot teach dignity while humiliating weaker pupils, or equality while tolerating discrimination. Teachers shape character through fairness, patience and intellectual integrity. Students also need opportunities to debate, serve their communities, accept responsibility and learn from failure.

Education promotes social change by enabling people to question arrangements once accepted as natural. Literacy allows citizens to understand laws, claim rights and participate in public life. Education can weaken caste prejudice, gender discrimination and superstition by exposing learners to constitutional values and reasoned inquiry. For girls and marginalised groups, schooling may increase autonomy, delay forced dependence and open occupations previously denied to them.

Economic transformation is also linked to education. Knowledge and skill improve productivity and allow workers to adapt to new technology. Yet education should not merely fit individuals into the existing economy. It should equip them to improve it—to create enterprises, solve local problems and demand decent conditions. Vocational learning, digital competence and foundational literacy are complementary rather than rival goals.

The promise of education can, however, be defeated by unequal access. A child studying in an overcrowded school without books does not possess the same opportunity as one supported by skilled teachers, technology and a secure home. Language, disability, poverty and social prejudice can turn formal admission into practical exclusion. Social change therefore requires quality and inclusion, not enrolment alone.

There is also a danger that education reproduces conformity. Excessive memorisation rewards obedience instead of curiosity. Competition may teach students to view classmates only as rivals, while commercialisation can convert learning into a private commodity. An educated person may possess technical skill without public responsibility. Character must therefore include humility, constitutional morality, concern for others and the courage to question unjust authority.

Education changes society gradually because its effects travel through generations. A student who learns equality may practise it at home; a trained nurse improves community health; a thoughtful voter strengthens democracy. Nevertheless, schools cannot by themselves remove unemployment, violence or structural discrimination. Their influence is greatest when supported by just laws, responsive institutions and social movements.

The deepest aim of education is to enlarge freedom—the freedom to think clearly, choose responsibly and participate fully in society. It shapes character when knowledge becomes judgement and conduct. It produces social change when individual capability is joined with equal opportunity and public purpose. Education is therefore not preparation for life alone; it is one of the principal ways in which both persons and societies remake themselves.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats character as practised judgement, connects education to equality and economic agency, and recognises institutional limits and access gaps.

### Q1(d). “The role of literature in a common man’s life.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → “The role of literature in a common man’s life.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → literature, empathy and everyday experience · `qualifying-english/essay-literature-common-life.md`

**Model essay**

Literature is sometimes treated as a refined pleasure reserved for scholars. Yet stories, songs, poems, plays and proverbs have always belonged to ordinary life. They are heard in childhood, remembered during hardship, shared at festivals and used to express emotions for which daily speech seems inadequate. Literature matters to the common person because it gives shape to experience, enlarges sympathy and preserves a community’s memory.

The first role of literature is companionship. A reader may encounter in a character the same fear, ambition, loneliness or hope that seemed private and inexpressible. Recognition reduces isolation. A novel cannot remove poverty or grief, but it can show that suffering has been understood by another mind. Humour and satire also provide relief, allowing people to endure difficulty without surrendering their critical sense.

Literature enlarges the world available to a person. One may travel to another age, region or social group without leaving home. Through imaginative identification, a reader sees that strangers possess inner lives as complex as one’s own. This does not automatically make everyone virtuous, but it can disturb prejudice. The farmer, migrant, widow, soldier or child ceases to be a category and becomes a person with motives and dignity.

In everyday society, literature preserves memory. Folk tales, epics and oral songs carry histories, beliefs and local knowledge across generations. Written works record the voices of those whom official documents may ignore. They also keep language alive by preserving its rhythms, idioms and expressive possibilities. When people lose access to their literature, they lose part of the vocabulary through which they understand themselves.

Literature can question power. A story may expose injustice more vividly than a report because it reveals how a rule enters a household or wounds an individual. Satire punctures the claims of the powerful; drama stages conflicting viewpoints; poetry condenses protest into memorable language. Literature rarely changes society alone, but it can alter moral imagination and provide words around which reform gathers.

It also develops judgement. Serious reading requires attention to motive, context, irony and ambiguity. Life itself contains such complexity. A person accustomed only to slogans may divide the world too easily into heroes and villains. Literature shows that good intentions can have harmful consequences and flawed persons can act nobly. This habit of nuanced understanding is valuable in family life, citizenship and work.

The common person need not approach literature through difficult classics alone. Popular fiction, regional writing, theatre, cinema scripts and oral performance can all possess literary value. Accessibility should not be confused with shallowness. At the same time, digital entertainment and hurried consumption can weaken sustained attention. Schools and libraries must therefore make varied literature affordable and inviting rather than presenting reading as punishment.

Literature has limits. It cannot substitute for food, healthcare, employment or justice. Nor is every work humane; literature can repeat prejudice or glorify domination. Readers must engage critically, asking whose voice is present and whose is absent. Its value lies not in automatic moral improvement but in the possibilities of reflection it opens.

In a common person’s life, literature names emotions, preserves belonging and offers imagined alternatives to present reality. It helps people remember, question, laugh and hope. Far from being ornamental, it is one of the ordinary means by which human beings make experience shareable and life intelligible.

**Why this earns marks:** It grounds literature in ordinary experience while covering companionship, empathy, memory, dissent, judgement, access and critical reading.

<a id="2022-mains-english-q02a"></a>
### Q2(a). “What does the author say about earlier assumptions regarding evolution?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Earlier assumptions about primate evolution · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Skill/slug:** Identifying a superseded claim · `qualifying-english/comprehension-human-ape-relationships.md`

**Answer:** Earlier accounts devoted substantial space to primate fossils because scientists assumed that, at each evolutionary stage, one fossil primate could be recognised as the direct ancestor of modern humans. The author says that this assumption is no longer accepted: for several reasons, many such fossil taxa are now considered highly unlikely to be ancestral to living higher primates. Attention has consequently shifted from constructing a simple chain of direct fossil ancestors to examining the evolution and relationships of the great apes.

<a id="2022-mains-english-q02b"></a>
### Q2(b). “According to the author, how are modern humans and apes related?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Human–ape evolutionary relationship · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Skill/slug:** Synthesising anatomical and molecular evidence · `qualifying-english/comprehension-human-ape-relationships.md`

**Answer:** The passage presents modern humans as very closely related to the great apes, especially the African apes. Huxley found the anatomical differences between humans and chimpanzees or gorillas to be smaller than those separating the African apes from the orangutan. Darwin therefore inferred that human ancestors were more likely to be found in Africa. Later molecular studies strengthened the relationship: human, chimpanzee and gorilla haemoglobin peptide patterns were indistinguishable, and human and chimpanzee albumins were so similar that they could not be told apart structurally.

<a id="2022-mains-english-q02c"></a>
### Q2(c). “What later developments took place in the twentieth century in investigating the relationship of apes and humans?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Twentieth-century molecular investigation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Skill/slug:** Tracing a methodological development · `qualifying-english/comprehension-human-ape-relationships.md`

**Answer:** During the first half of the twentieth century, advances in biochemistry and immunology enabled researchers to move beyond traditional anatomical comparison to the “morphology of molecules.” Early protein-based attempts began just after the turn of the century, while a new generation of analyses produced results in the early 1960s. Linus Pauling named this field “molecular anthropology.” Studies published in 1963 then compared haemoglobin peptides and albumin in humans, apes and monkeys, providing crucial molecular evidence of close relationships.

<a id="2022-mains-english-q02d"></a>
### Q2(d). “What were the attempts made to use proteins to determine primate relationships?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Protein comparison in molecular anthropology · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Skill/slug:** Extracting experimental method and result · `qualifying-english/comprehension-human-ape-relationships.md`

**Answer:** Protein studies began shortly after the start of the twentieth century and became decisive in the 1960s. Emile Zuckerkandl broke haemoglobin into peptide components and separated them with a small electric current; the resulting patterns from a human, chimpanzee and gorilla were indistinguishable. Morris Goodman used immunological techniques to compare the serum protein albumin from humans, apes and monkeys. He found human and chimpanzee albumins so alike in structure that they could not be distinguished. These experiments used molecular similarity to infer evolutionary relationship.

<a id="2022-mains-english-q02e"></a>
### Q2(e). “In what way does the latest research prove the relationship between apes and humans?” — 15 marks
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Reading Comprehension](../../topics/qualifying-english/reading-comprehension.md) → Molecular reconstruction of evolutionary history · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Skill/slug:** Explaining the passage’s concluding evidence · `qualifying-english/comprehension-human-ape-relationships.md`

**Answer:** The latest research described in the passage studies small variations in the amino-acid sequences of proteins. Because such substitutions record changes in molecular structure, Vince Sarich and Allan Wilson used them to reconstruct the evolutionary history of the molecules and, by inference, of the primate taxa from which the samples came. Their work in the 1960s and 1970s, together with the earlier haemoglobin and albumin results, showed that modern humans and the African apes are very closely related.

**Examiner takeaway:** Each response answers only what the passage supports, distinguishes anatomy from molecular evidence, and avoids adding outside claims about species divergence or genetics.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official wording:** “Make a précis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give a title to it. The précis should be written in your own language:”  
**Marks:** 75  
**Source length:** 750 words · **Target:** about 250 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Précis writing → faithful compression without title · `qualifying-english/precis-computers-error-discovery.md`

**Compression logic**

- Retain the central paradox: errors are usually blamed on people, yet computers are extensions of error-prone human thought.
- Preserve the argument that error enables learning, debate and scientific discovery.
- Remove the long lists of billing mistakes, machine-room imagery and laboratory examples while retaining their function.
- Maintain the author’s reflective tone and sequence.
- Supply **no title**, because the paper expressly prohibits one.

**Model précis — intentionally untitled**

Computer errors have become familiar in banking, billing and public services. Although machines issue apologies for such mistakes, people generally treat error as abnormal in a computer and attribute it to human interference. Yet a computer is an extension of the human brain, enhanced in speed and power but derived from a mind that is itself fallible.

Computers may calculate, play chess and even produce verse, though whether they possess consciousness is uncertain. Their unexplained mistakes nevertheless resemble an unconscious tendency inherited from their makers. Human thinking depends fundamentally upon the capacity to be wrong. We progress by choosing between alternatives, and wrong choices are as necessary to learning as right ones; hence the truthful expression “trial and error.”

The same principle operates in scientific work. A well-run laboratory normally follows rules and produces expected results, but an accidental departure—a wrong solution, misplaced decimal, altered temperature or misread instruction—may reveal something new. The initial mistake is valuable only if the investigator notices the unexpected result and uses it as the starting point for discovery.

New thought and artistic creation similarly require an internal argument between competing possibilities. Action becomes possible because one side may be right and the other wrong. The faculty of wrongness enables the mind to cross accumulated information and reach an unforeseen conclusion. Error is therefore not merely a defect to be eliminated; when recognised and intelligently pursued, it is a basic condition of learning, invention and human progress.

**Length:** Approximately 245 words (about one-third of the stated source length).

**Examiner takeaway:** The précis is approximately one-third of the stated 750-word passage, uses independent wording, preserves the full argumentative movement, and correctly omits a title.
