### Q1(a). “There is a red chair.” How would Plato explain this statement with the use of his theory of forms? Examine.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Forms, particulars and predication · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—Ideas; knowledge and opinion · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Plato · theory of Forms · predication of redness and chairhood · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `plato-forms-red-chair-predication`

**Demand decoding:** Apply, rather than merely describe, the Forms theory; distinguish the sensible chair from intelligible universals and assess participation.

**Argument map:** changing particular chair → participates in Chairhood and Redness → Forms ground stable predication and knowledge → participation/separation problem.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Plato would analyse the sentence as predicating of a sensible particular properties whose intelligibility depends on Forms.

The chair before us is a spatial, changing object apprehended by sense and therefore belongs to the realm of becoming. It is a chair because it participates in or imitates the Form of Chairhood, and red because it participates in Redness. The Forms are not merely private concepts: they are universal, intelligible and stable standards that explain why many different objects can truly receive the same predicate. Sense supplies this red chair; intellect grasps what chair and red are.

**Objection:** If Chairhood and Redness exist separately, “participation” remains unexplained and may generate the Third Man regress. **Reply:** Plato can treat participation as asymmetric dependence, not physical sharing, though its exact mechanism stays obscure.

**Evaluation:** Forms explain universal predication and objective classification, but multiply entities and struggle to connect intelligible standards with concrete particulars.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies Forms directly to both subject and predicate and evaluates the central separation difficulty.

### Q1(b). “Potentiality is indefinable” according to Aristotle. Explain the relationship between potentiality and actuality with reference to the above philosophical position by taking the example of a “wooden table”.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → potentiality and actuality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—form and matter; actuality and potentiality · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Aristotle · change · priority of actuality · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aristotle-potentiality-actuality-wooden-table`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why potentiality is grasped relationally through actuality and use the wooden-table example to integrate matter, form and causation.

**Argument map:** wood has qualified capacity → carpenter actualises it under table-form → actuality defines the relevant potential → actuality is prior in account and end.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Potentiality is not definable as an isolated property; it is a determinate capacity understood through the corresponding actuality.

Wood is potentially a table, but not potentially anything whatever. Its material structure makes it capable of receiving table-form. The carpenter, as efficient cause, works for the end of a usable table; when the form is realised in the wood, what existed potentially exists actually. Change is therefore the actualisation of a potential **as potential**.

Actuality is prior in definition because “table-making capacity” is identified by the completed table; prior in end because production aims at actuality; and often prior in substance because the mature form explains the process. Yet potentiality is real: it distinguishes possible development from mere logical possibility.

**Objection:** The account can appear circular—potentiality is defined by actuality and actuality through prior potential. **Reply:** Aristotle treats them as correlative but gives explanatory priority to realised form.

**Evaluation:** The distinction intelligibly mediates being and non-being in change, though artefact examples depend heavily on external design.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses the prescribed table example to connect capacity, form, causation and Aristotle’s priority of actuality.

### Q1(c). “Sensible things are those only which are immediately perceived by sense.” Explain Berkeley’s theory of knowledge with reference to the above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Berkeley · esse est percipi · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Berkeley; rejection of material substance · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Berkeley · ideas and spirits · sensible objects · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `berkeley-sensible-things-immediate-perception`

**Demand decoding:** Derive immaterialism from immediate perception while distinguishing ideas, perceiving spirits and divine coordination.

**Argument map:** senses immediately present ideas → no material substratum is perceived → sensible object is ordered idea-complex → to be is to be perceived → God secures continuity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Berkeley holds that sensible objects are collections of ideas immediately perceived by minds, not mind-independent material substances hidden behind them.

Colour, hardness, shape and motion are ideas given in perception. We never perceive an inert substratum supporting them; abstracting a material object from all perceivable qualities is unintelligible. Hence the table is the coordinated complex of visual and tactile ideas: for sensible things, “to be is to be perceived” (*esse est percipi*). Ideas are passive; active perceivers are spirits, known through reflexive notion rather than as sensory ideas.

Objects do not vanish whenever finite persons look away. Their regularity and continued availability are secured by God, the omnipresent spirit who orders sensory ideas independently of our wills.

**Objection:** Immaterialism seems to collapse reality into illusion. **Reply:** Berkeley distinguishes vivid, orderly, involuntary ideas of sense from faint ideas of imagination; he denies matter, not public reality.

**Evaluation:** The view exposes the representational gap but makes divine perception carry substantial explanatory weight.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives immaterialism from immediate sensible ideas while distinguishing passive ideas, active spirits and divine continuity.

### Q1(d). Examine the concept of personal identity by Locke.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Locke · personal identity and memory · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Locke; theory of knowledge · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Locke · consciousness, memory and forensic personhood · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `locke-personal-identity-consciousness-memory`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish person from human organism and substance; explain consciousness-continuity and test it against classic objections.

**Argument map:** same person = same continuing consciousness → memory appropriates past action → identity differs from body/soul identity → circularity and transitivity objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Locke locates personal identity not in sameness of body or immaterial substance but in continuity of consciousness.

A “man” is the same living human organism; a “person” is a rational, self-conscious, accountable being. Personal identity extends as far backward as present consciousness can appropriate past thoughts and actions through memory. Thus a prince’s consciousness transferred to a cobbler’s body would carry the prince’s personhood. This makes identity forensic: reward and punishment attach to actions owned through consciousness.

**Objections:** Butler argues that memory presupposes rather than constitutes identity; Reid’s brave-officer case shows apparent failure of transitivity when an old general remembers bravery but not childhood. **Reply:** Locke can appeal to overlapping chains of consciousness rather than occurrent recall, but this revises his strict formulation.

**Evaluation:** Locke decisively separates moral personhood from substance, yet memory is better treated as evidence and a psychological connector than as the sole criterion of numerical identity.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes person, organism and substance and tests the memory criterion against Butler and Reid.

### Q1(e). “The relation between cause and effect is one of constant conjunction”. Examine Hume’s criticism of causation in the light of the above statement.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume · causation and necessary connection · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Hume; causation and scepticism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hume · constant conjunction · custom · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hume-causation-constant-conjunction`

**Demand decoding:** Explain Hume’s negative critique of necessary power and positive psychological account; assess whether regularity is sufficient.

**Argument map:** no impression of necessary tie → observation gives succession and conjunction → repetition creates customary expectation → necessity projected by mind.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hume reduces the experienced basis of causation to regular succession and explains necessity as a habit-generated expectation.

In a single collision we perceive contiguity, temporal priority and motion, but no binding power forcing the effect. The effect cannot be deduced a priori because alternatives remain conceivable. Repeated observation supplies only constant conjunction: events of type A are regularly followed by events of type B. Custom then determines the imagination to expect B upon seeing A; this felt transition produces the idea of necessary connection.

**Objection:** Constant conjunction cannot distinguish causation from accidental regularity—night follows day without causing it—and scientific explanation invokes mechanisms and counterfactual dependence. **Reply:** Hume’s purpose is epistemic: no necessity beyond patterns and mental inference is directly given.

**Evaluation:** Hume exposes the inductive leap from sequence to necessity, but a pure regularity account underdescribes causal powers and intervention.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs both the negative critique of necessary power and the positive custom-based account of causal belief.

### Q2(a). Discuss Hegel’s Dialectical method. Explain how his dialectical method leads him to the Absolute Idealism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → dialectic and Absolute idealism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Hegel—dialectical method; absolute idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hegel · determinate negation, sublation and Absolute Spirit · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hegel-dialectic-absolute-idealism`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct dialectic as immanent development, not a mechanical triad; show why finite oppositions culminate in self-knowing Absolute Spirit.

**Argument map:** finite category claims independence → internal contradiction → determinate negation → sublation (*Aufhebung*) → increasingly comprehensive unity → subject–object reconciliation in Absolute knowing.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hegel’s dialectic is the self-development of thought and reality through internal contradiction; its completion is Absolute idealism, in which reality is an intelligible whole that knows itself through Spirit.

A finite determination excludes its opposite and thereby reveals its own insufficiency. Dialectical negation is determinate: it preserves what was true while overcoming one-sidedness. Sublation (*Aufhebung*) simultaneously cancels, preserves and elevates. In the *Logic*, pure Being, being wholly indeterminate, collapses into Nothing; their truth is Becoming. The movement is therefore not an externally imposed “thesis–antithesis–synthesis” formula.

The same structure governs consciousness in the *Phenomenology*. Sense-certainty proves mediated by universals; self-consciousness requires recognition; reason becomes ethical Spirit. Each shape fails by its own criterion and is incorporated into a richer standpoint. Nature is Idea in externality, while finite minds progressively return from this otherness through art, religion and philosophy.

Absolute idealism follows because no finite object or subject is self-sufficient. Their opposition is intelligible only within the whole process whereby the Idea objectifies and comprehends itself. The Absolute is not a static substance beyond differences but self-mediating Subject.

**Objections and replies:** Kierkegaard says the system absorbs concrete existence; Marx says material conflict is idealistically inverted; analytic critics question whether contradiction can generate reality. Hegel can reply that dialectical contradiction means unstable conceptual opposition, not formal inconsistency, and that the whole preserves finite difference.

**Evaluation:** Dialectic powerfully models relational and historical development, but the claimed necessary culmination in Absolute Spirit risks teleology and totalisation.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains determinate negation and sublation and shows, rather than merely asserts, their culmination in Absolute idealism.

### Q2(b). What according to Logical Positivists are “pseudostatements”? How does one identify “pseudostatements”? Critically discuss with examples.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → verification and elimination of metaphysics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Logical Positivism—verification theory of meaning; rejection of metaphysics · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Vienna Circle and A. J. Ayer · cognitive meaning · pseudostatements · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `logical-positivism-pseudostatements-verification`

**Demand decoding:** Define pseudostatements, supply syntactic and verification tests, distinguish them from false statements, and critically evaluate the criterion.

**Argument map:** meaningful assertion is analytic or empirically verifiable → failure through unverifiable vocabulary/grammar → metaphysical sentence is cognitively meaningless → criterion faces self-refutation and science objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Logical Positivists, a pseudostatement has declarative grammar but lacks cognitive meaning because no possible experience or logical rule determines its truth or falsity.

The verification principle recognises two classes of meaningful propositions: analytic propositions true by linguistic rules, and synthetic propositions whose truth is at least in principle empirically testable. “Water boils at 100°C under standard pressure” is testable; “the Absolute enters evolution timelessly” supplies no observation-conditions. Carnap also identified logical-syntactic violations, as in “Caesar is a prime number,” where a numerical predicate is misapplied. A pseudostatement differs from a false claim: evidence can refute “all swans are white,” whereas no evidence bears on an unverifiable metaphysical utterance. Ethical and religious sentences were often treated as expressions of attitude rather than factual reports.

**Objections and replies:** The verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable; universal laws and historical claims are not conclusively verified. Ayer’s weak verification—some possible evidence is relevant—avoids strictness but admits too much. Popper’s falsifiability addresses science, not meaning.

**Evaluation:** The test usefully demands public conditions of assertion, but cannot consistently dismiss all metaphysics without becoming either self-defeating or excessively permissive.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies both identification tests, examples and the strongest self-application and scientific-law objections.

### Q2(c). Explain how Cartesian formulation of ontological argument is criticised by Kant.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → critique of ontological proof · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Kant—critique of speculative metaphysics; Ideas of pure reason · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Descartes and Kant · existence as non-predicate · necessary existence · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Secondary route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-critique-cartesian-ontological-argument`

**Demand decoding:** State Descartes’ proof fairly, then present Kant’s predicate, concept-instantiation and synthetic-existence objections.

**Argument map:** supremely perfect being includes all perfections → existence alleged perfection → denial said contradictory → Kant: existence adds no predicate → concepts do not entail instantiation.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant argues that Descartes illegitimately moves from the concept of a supremely perfect being to its existence.

Descartes claims that necessary existence belongs to God’s essence as the equality of three angles to two right angles belongs to a triangle: a being lacking existence would not be supremely perfect. Kant answers, first, that one may reject the subject together with all its predicates without contradiction. “God does not exist” does not posit God and then remove a property; it denies that the concept is instantiated.

Second, existence is not a real predicate adding content to a concept. One hundred real thalers contain no more conceptual predicates than one hundred possible thalers; actuality posits the object corresponding to the concept. Therefore an existential judgement is synthetic, not obtainable by analysing “God.”

Calling God “necessarily existent” does not help: necessity characterises a judgement under conditions, and no concept alone guarantees an object outside thought.

**Objection:** Modal versions argue that possible necessary existence entails actual existence. **Reply:** Kant would challenge the premise that such real possibility is established merely conceptually.

**Evaluation:** Kant decisively blocks definition-to-existence inference, though defenders contend that necessary existence concerns a unique mode of being rather than an ordinary property.

**Why this earns marks:** It states Descartes fairly and develops Kant’s non-predicate, instantiation and modality objections.

### Q3(a). What are the main arguments put forward by Moore in his paper “A Defence of Common Sense” to prove that there are possible propositions about the world that are known to be true with certainty? Do you think Moore’s arguments provide a sufficient response to objections presented by the sceptic against the possibility of knowledge? Give reasons in support of your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → common sense and scepticism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Moore, Russell and early Wittgenstein—defence of common sense; refutation of idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: G. E. Moore · certainty, external world and scepticism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `moore-common-sense-certainty-scepticism`

**Demand decoding:** Identify Moore’s common-sense truisms and argumentative reversal, then decide whether knowledge claims answer or merely resist sceptical hypotheses.

**Argument map:** ordinary propositions are more certain than sceptical premises → incompatible philosophical theory must surrender → “here is a hand” supplies proof → sceptic challenges justification/possibility.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Moore establishes a powerful anti-sceptical standpoint by treating common-sense knowledge as more certain than the premises used to deny it, but he does not fully explain how such knowledge survives radical sceptical possibilities.

In “A Defence of Common Sense,” Moore lists truisms he claims to know: a human body existed before the paper was written; it was born, has lived near other bodies and the earth long pre-existed it; other persons have had similar knowledge. He also knows that many others have known corresponding propositions. Any idealist or sceptical theory implying that no external objects, past facts or other minds are known is therefore less credible than these Moorean facts.

His method reverses the sceptic’s modus ponens:

1. If I do not know I am not dreaming, I do not know I have hands.  
2. I know I have hands.  
3. Therefore I know I am not dreaming.

The later “proof of an external world”—raising each hand and saying “Here is one hand, and here is another”—is valid if its premises are known, concerns external objects and yields a conclusion distinct from them. Moore need not know a proof of his premises in order to know them.

**Objections and replies:** The sceptic asks what justifies the premises under dream or evil-demon hypotheses; simply asserting knowledge may beg the question. Moore replies that philosophical doubt has no greater warrant than ordinary knowledge. Yet this is dialectically inconclusive because both sides reject the other’s premise. Wittgenstein’s hinge approach improves Moore by treating some certainties as framework conditions rather than evidence-based conclusions.

**Evaluation:** Moore successfully shifts the burden and exposes the implausible cost of scepticism. He gives a rational stance against scepticism, but not a non-circular proof capable of compelling the radical sceptic.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs Moore’s truisms and argumentative reversal before giving a reasoned verdict on dialectical sufficiency.

### Q3(b). What according to Strawson are basic particulars? What reasons does Strawson offer to believe that ‘material bodies’ and ‘persons’ are basic particulars? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → descriptive metaphysics and basic particulars · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Quine and Strawson—basic particulars and persons · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: P. F. Strawson · identification and reidentification · material bodies and persons · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `strawson-basic-particulars-bodies-persons`

**Demand decoding:** Define “basic” through public identification, explain the spatiotemporal framework and the primitive person-concept, then test exclusions.

**Argument map:** reference requires shared identification → public spatiotemporal particulars anchor discourse → material bodies endure and are reidentifiable → persons are bodies plus primitive mental/physical predicates.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Strawson calls particulars “basic” when identification of other particulars ultimately depends upon them, while their own identification does not depend comparably on the latter.

Material bodies are basic because they occupy a single public spatiotemporal framework, endure through change and can be reidentified by continuous routes. Sounds, pains and private experiences are located and identified through the bodies or persons whose states they are. Purely private Cartesian egos cannot supply publicly shared criteria.

Persons are also basic particulars. A person is not a body plus an independently identified mind; the concept is primitive. Both material predicates (“weighs sixty kilograms”) and psychological predicates (“is anxious”) apply to the same individual. Self-ascription of experience is learned alongside other-ascription, whose criteria include behaviour, expression and circumstances. This dissolves the mind–body problem generated by treating minds as separate substances.

**Objections and replies:** Events or organisms may appear equally basic, and bodily criteria can misidentify persons in transplant cases. Strawson can answer that descriptive metaphysics reconstructs our actual conceptual scheme, not every imaginable ontology.

**Evaluation:** The account powerfully explains public reference and embodied agency, but “basicness” may reflect our scheme rather than metaphysical necessity.

**Why this earns marks:** It derives the basic status of bodies and persons from public identification, reidentification and primitive person-predication.

### Q3(c). Critically examine Quine’s postulate of empiricism without the dogmas with reference to his ‘Two Dogmas of Empiricism’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → critique of empiricism and confirmation holism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Quine and Strawson—critique of empiricism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: W. V. O. Quine · analytic–synthetic distinction · reductionism · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `quine-empiricism-without-dogmas`

**Demand decoding:** Identify both dogmas, show how their collapse yields holistic pragmatism, and assess whether analyticity can nevertheless be reconstructed.

**Argument map:** analyticity definitions become circular → statements lack unique experiential reductions → experience tests web of belief → revision is holistic and pragmatic.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Quine retains empiricism while abandoning the analytic–synthetic boundary and atomistic reduction of each meaningful statement to immediate experience.

The first dogma says analytic truths hold solely by meaning. Attempts to explain analyticity through synonymy, definition, interchangeability or semantic rules become circular: each presupposes the very notion needing explanation. The second dogma holds that every meaningful statement has a unique set of confirming experiences. Quine argues instead that predictions confront experience only with auxiliary assumptions; “the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science.”

Knowledge is therefore a web whose edges face sensory stimulation. Any statement can be retained if compensating revisions are made elsewhere, while even logic may in principle be revised. Conservatism, simplicity and explanatory power guide adjustment. Ontological objects are posits within the web, differing in degree rather than kind from theoretical constructs.

**Objections:** Grice and Strawson argue that established use distinguishes analytic from synthetic; later semantics explains necessity without crude definitions. **Reply:** Quine can allow practical differences in revisability while denying a sharp, philosophically foundational boundary.

**Evaluation:** Holism transformed epistemology and philosophy of science, but total rejection of analyticity overstates the failure of local semantic explanation.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies both dogmas, explains confirmation holism and evaluates Quine’s stronger rejection of analyticity.

### Q4(a). Present a critical exposition of Husserl’s criticism of ‘natural attitude’. How does Husserl propose to address the problems involved in natural attitude through his phenomenological method?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Phenomenology](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/phenomenology.md) → natural attitude, epoché and reduction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Phenomenology—Husserl; method and intentionality · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Husserl · natural attitude · phenomenological reduction and constitution · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Phenomenology (Husserl)](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `husserl-natural-attitude-phenomenological-method`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the natural attitude’s unnoticed existential commitment, then trace epoché, reductions, intentional analysis and remaining idealist difficulty.

**Argument map:** everyday/scientific consciousness posits world as simply there → presuppositions obscure conditions of givenness → epoché brackets positing → reduction discloses intentional correlation → essences and constitution.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Husserl criticises the natural attitude not because ordinary belief is false, but because it naïvely presupposes a ready-made world without examining how objects acquire sense and validity for consciousness.

In everyday life and the positive sciences, the world is accepted as simply existing “out there.” Consciousness is then treated as one natural object among others. This attitude conceals intentionality—every act is consciousness **of** something—and encourages naturalism and psychologism: meanings and logical norms are reduced to causal mental events. It also leaves the subject–object relation unexplained.

Husserl’s method proceeds through:

1. **Phenomenological suspension (*epoché*):** neither deny nor affirm worldly existence; bracket the natural thesis.  
2. **Phenomenological reduction:** redirect attention from objects simply posited to how they are given in acts of consciousness.  
3. **Eidetic reduction:** vary examples imaginatively to intuit invariant essences.  
4. **Intentional analysis:** correlate the act-side (*noesis*) with the intended sense or object-as-meant (*noema*).  
5. **Constitution:** disclose syntheses of identity, time, embodiment and horizon through which an object appears as the same across profiles.

The result is transcendental subjectivity, not the empirical ego: the field in which worldly sense and objectivity are constituted. Intersubjective constitution is meant to prevent solipsism.

**Objections and replies:** Bracketing may be psychologically impossible; constitution seems to recreate idealism; access to other subjects remains difficult. Husserl replies that epoché changes reflective stance rather than erasing belief, and constitution means disclosure of sense, not causal creation.

**Evaluation:** The method reveals hidden structures of experience and avoids naïve realism, but its transition from description to transcendental idealism remains controversial.

**Why this earns marks:** It links the exact defect in the natural attitude to each methodological stage and retains the idealism and intersubjectivity problems.

### Q4(b). “I can always choose, but I ought to know that if I do not choose, I am still choosing”. Critically discuss Sartre’s conception of choice and responsibility in the light of above statement.

### Q4(c). What does Wittgenstein mean by the statement — “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent?” Critically discuss.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** The closing proposition of the *Tractatus* requires silence where language cannot picture a possible state of affairs; it protects the boundary between factual saying and what can only show itself.

A meaningful proposition has constituents arranged so as to share logical form with a possible fact. Natural science therefore says how the world is. Logic, however, cannot be represented as another fact because it is the form enabling representation. Likewise ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life, the metaphysical subject and the mystical concern the world’s value or limit, not facts within it. Attempts to state them transgress logical syntax. The *Tractatus* itself functions as a ladder: once it clarifies the limit, its own elucidatory sentences are recognised as nonsensical and thrown away.

**Objections and replies:** The doctrine appears self-refuting because it states what allegedly cannot be said and may impoverish moral discourse. A resolute reading treats the book therapeutically, not as ineffable metaphysical theory. Later Wittgenstein abandons a single pictorial essence for plural language-games.

**Evaluation:** The maxim disciplines speculative language and distinguishes fact from value, but its early criterion is too narrow for the meaningful diversity of actual linguistic practice.

**Why this earns marks:** It grounds the maxim in picture theory and saying/showing and addresses self-refutation and Wittgenstein’s later revision.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Inseparability (*ayutasiddhatva*) is a necessary but not by itself sufficient defining condition of inherence (*samavāya*).

**Objection:** Postulating inherence invites a regress requiring another relation to connect it. **Reply:** Vaiśeṣika treats *samavāya* as self-linking and sui generis.

**Evaluation:** Necessary, not independently sufficient, is the safer logical verdict.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the requested logical verdict, tests it across standard inherence pairs and answers the regress objection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In Yoga, afflictions (*kleśas*) are cognitive-affective distortions rooted in ignorance; removing their gross and subtle forms ends karmic bondage and permits the seer’s isolation (*kaivalya*).

Patañjali names five:

1. **Ignorance (*avidyā*):** mistaking impermanent, impure, painful and non-self for permanent, pure, pleasant and self.

**Evaluation:** Liberation is not production of a new self but cessation of false conjunction between consciousness and nature (*prakṛti*). With the qualities’ purpose fulfilled, *puruṣa* abides in its own nature: *kaivalya*.

**Why this earns marks:** It names and causally orders all five afflictions and traces their attenuation and seed-destruction to isolation.

**Argument map:** Nyāya: real elsewhere-object mislocated through defective perception and memory → Advaita: absent silver cannot be perceptually present here → appearance has temporary efficacy → neither real nor unreal → sublatable, indescribable projection.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Advaita rejects Nyāya’s claim that illusion is cognition of one real thing as another and explains the presented object as empirically manifest yet ultimately sublatable—neither real nor unreal.

Nyāya’s misapprehension theory (*anyathā-khyāti*) analyses shell-silver as perception of “this” shell combined with memory-mediated cognition of real silver elsewhere. Advaitins object:

1. The subject seems to perceive silver **here**, not remember distant silver.  
2. Memory cannot by itself produce the immediate “this is silver” presentation.  
3. A real elsewhere-silver cannot enter ordinary perceptual relation with the shell.  
4. If the silver were wholly unreal, like a square circle, it could not appear or motivate action.

The appearance is not real (*sat*), since corrective shell-cognition sublates it; not unreal (*asat*), since it appears and prompts reaching. It is indescribable as either. The shell supplies the locus, while prior impressions determine the projected content. This local error models the world’s superimposition upon Brahman: empirical reality persists until knowledge sublates it.

**Objections and replies:** Nyāya charges that “neither real nor unreal” violates bivalence and that *avidyā* is obscure. Advaita replies that levels of reality explain experienced yet sublatable objects; “indescribable” marks ontological dependence, not formal contradiction.

**Evaluation:** Advaita better captures illusion’s phenomenal immediacy and sublatability, but its positive account depends on the difficult metaphysics of beginningless ignorance.

**Why this earns marks:** It presents Nyāya fairly, gives four targeted Advaita objections and establishes the neither-real-nor-unreal conclusion without evading criticism.

### Q7(b). If everything is momentary then how do the Buddhists explain the problem of memory and personal identity? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Buddhism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/buddhism.md) → momentariness, memory and personal continuity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Classification route:** [Buddhism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Buddhism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `buddhism-momentariness-memory-personal-identity`

**Demand decoding:** Reconcile the denial of endurance with recognition, memory, karma and practical identity through causal continuity; assess whether continuity presupposes a subject.

**Argument map:** each phenomenon perishes instantly → preceding cognition leaves causal disposition → later cognition reproduces past content → stream is neither identical nor wholly different → conventional person.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Buddhism explains memory and personal identity through an ordered causal continuum of momentary events, not through a numerically identical enduring self.

Each cognition perishes after conditioning its successor and deposits a disposition (*saṃskāra/vāsanā*) in the mental stream (*santāna*). When appropriate conditions occur, a later cognition arises with the form “I experienced that.” The rememberer is not the earlier subject numerically preserved, but belongs to the same causally continuous series. The flame-to-flame and milk-to-curd analogies express continuity without immutable identity.

The “person” is a conventional designation upon the five aggregates. This supports karma: the later recipient is neither strictly the same as nor wholly different from the earlier agent. Madhyamaka further treats both moments and streams as dependently arisen and empty.

**Objection:** Recognition appears to require one subject comparing past and present; causal succession alone may not explain first-person ownership. **Reply:** Buddhists argue that ownership is itself a constructed feature of memory-cognition; positing a changeless witness adds no causal explanation.

**Evaluation:** The stream theory avoids a substantial self and accommodates psychological continuity, but its account of unity and responsibility remains vulnerable unless causal connectedness is specified robustly.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains memory, first-person continuity and karmic responsibility through one causal stream and directly confronts the enduring-subject objection.

### Q7(c). Explain the Jaina view of seven-fold (sapta-bhaṅgī) ‘Naya’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → conditioned predication and sevenfold scheme · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Jainism—many-sidedness; standpoint and conditioned predication · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Jaina philosophy · sevenfold predication (*sapta-bhaṅgī*) · standpoints (*naya*) · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `jaina-sevenfold-predication-naya`

**Demand decoding:** Relate standpoint theory to many-sided reality, enumerate all seven predications accurately, illustrate and answer the contradiction charge.

**Argument map:** reality has infinite aspects → finite judgement uses a standpoint → “in some respect” qualifies assertion → seven combinations of affirmation, negation and inexpressibility.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Jaina sevenfold predication (*sapta-bhaṅgī*) systematises how apparently opposed judgements can be conditionally true of a many-sided reality.

A standpoint (*naya*) grasps one aspect; conditioned predication (*syādvāda*) marks it as “in some respect.” Of a jar:

1. in some respect it exists (*syād asti*)—as present clay-jar;  
2. in some respect it does not exist (*syān nāsti*)—as cloth or at another time/place;  
3. in some respect it exists and does not exist (*syād asti nāsti*)—successively under different conditions;  
4. in some respect it is inexpressible (*syād avaktavyaḥ*)—when simultaneous complexity defeats one simple assertion;  
5. it exists and is inexpressible;  
6. it does not exist and is inexpressible;  
7. it exists, does not exist and is inexpressible.

The scheme expresses many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) without claiming every view is equally adequate.

**Objection:** Combining being and non-being violates non-contradiction. **Reply:** predicates differ by substance, mode, place, time or standpoint; they are not affirmed in the same respect.

**Evaluation:** It promotes logical pluralism and intellectual non-absolutism, though “inexpressibility” and the exact necessity of seven forms remain contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It lists all seven predications, supplies a single controlled example and blocks contradiction through standpoint qualification.

### Q8(a). According to Sri Aurobindo, ‘the awakening of the psychic being and its gradual prominence over all other parts of the being is the first step in the conscious evolution of man’. Explain and examine.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Sri Aurobindo](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/sri-aurobindo.md) → psychic transformation and conscious evolution · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Sri Aurobindo—evolution; integral yoga · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sri Aurobindo · psychic being · triple transformation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Sri Aurobindo](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Aurobindo.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aurobindo-psychic-being-conscious-evolution`

**Demand decoding:** Define the psychic being, explain why its awakening is first, place it within involution/evolution and the triple transformation, then evaluate.

**Argument map:** Divine consciousness involved in matter → evolves through life and mind → inner soul grows through lives → psychic emergence reorders outer nature → spiritual and supramental transformations follow.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Aurobindo, the psychic being is the evolving soul-personality behind the surface mind, life and body; its emergence is the first secure step from unconscious natural evolution to conscious integral transformation.

Cosmic evolution is possible because consciousness was first involved in matter. It emerges as life and mind, but ordinary mentality remains divided, ego-centred and governed by desire. Behind it stands the psychic being—not the immutable individual self alone, but the developing representative of the Divine that assimilates the essence of experience across births.

Its awakening brings an intrinsic orientation toward truth, beauty, love and surrender. As it comes forward, it distinguishes genuine aspiration from vital ambition, purifies emotion, disciplines mind and body, and harmonises the personality. Hence psychic transformation is first: premature spiritual powers without psychic guidance may inflate ego or produce imbalance.

It prepares two further movements. **Spiritual transformation** widens consciousness into silence, peace, universality and the higher planes above mind. **Supramental transformation** brings truth-consciousness down to transform mental, vital and physical nature. Integral yoga therefore seeks divine life in the world, not escape from embodiment.

**Objections and replies:** The psychic being and rebirth lack public verification, while the stages risk elitist teleology. Aurobindo replies that sustained yogic practice provides convergent experiential evidence and that evolution is an invitation, not automatic historical inevitability.

**Evaluation:** The doctrine offers a sophisticated moral psychology linking inner purification to cosmic evolution. Its practical account of ego-transformation is compelling, but supramental and reincarnational claims remain philosophically underdetermined.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the psychic being, proves why its awakening comes first and locates it accurately within triple transformation.

### Q8(b). Compare and contrast the views of Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja regarding the status of the world.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → world in Advaita and qualified non-dualism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Vedānta—Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja; Brahman, world and liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Advaita and Viśiṣṭādvaita · ontological status of the world · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `sankara-ramanuja-status-of-world`

**Demand decoding:** Compare reality-level, causation, Brahman–world relation, scripture and liberation without calling Advaita simple nihilism.

**Argument map:** both accept Brahman as cause → Śaṅkara: appearance through ignorance, empirically real but sublatable → Rāmānuja: real transformation of modes/body of Brahman → non-duality interpreted differently.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Śaṅkara grants the world dependent empirical reality but denies it ultimate status; Rāmānuja treats it as a real mode and body of Brahman within an organic qualified unity.

For Śaṅkara’s non-dualism (*Advaita*), attributeless Brahman alone is ultimately real. Under beginningless ignorance (*avidyā*), name-and-form are superimposed and the world appears through indefinable appearance (*māyā*). It is neither absolutely real nor sheer non-being: it supports ordinary knowledge and morality at the empirical level but is sublated by liberating knowledge. Brahman is the apparent material cause without undergoing real modification (*vivarta*).

For Rāmānuja’s qualified non-dualism (*Viśiṣṭādvaita*), matter and selves are real attributes or modes constituting God’s body. Brahman is their inner controller; creation is a real manifestation or transformation of dependent nature, not illusion. Liberation removes bondage, not the world’s reality.

**Objections and replies:** Rāmānuja says Advaita makes scripture, devotion and liberation themselves provisional. Śaṅkara replies that empirical means validly remove empirical ignorance. Advaita asks how imperfections can belong to God’s body; Rāmānuja answers that dependent modes do not compromise divine sovereignty.

**Evaluation:** Śaṅkara secures uncompromising non-duality but faces the status-of-ignorance problem; Rāmānuja better preserves plurality and devotion but must reconcile a perfect Brahman with a suffering world.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares ontological level, causation, Brahman–world relation and liberation while giving objections from both sides.

### Q8(c). Explain the status of jīva and jagat in the philosophy of Madhvācārya.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Madhva’s dualism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Vedānta—Madhva; God, self, world, bondage and liberation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Madhvācārya · dualism (*Dvaita*) · dependent reality of selves and world · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `madhva-jiva-jagat-status`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the reality, dependence, plurality and hierarchy of selves and world through fivefold difference; connect ontology to bondage and liberation.

**Argument map:** Viṣṇu alone independent → selves and matter real but dependent → five differences eternal → souls plural and graded → liberation preserves individuality and world-realism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Madhva’s dualism (*Dvaita*) regards both individual selves (*jīvas*) and world (*jagat*) as eternally real but wholly dependent upon Viṣṇu, the sole independent reality.

Reality is structured by fivefold difference (*pañcabheda*): God–self, God–matter, self–self, self–matter and matter–matter. The world is neither illusion nor an indescribable appearance; its objects, causal order and scriptural distinctions are real. God is its efficient cause and sovereign regulator, while primordial matter is the dependent material cause.

Selves are conscious, atomic, plural and intrinsically distinct from God and one another. Their agency is real but dependent. Madhva also affirms gradation (*taratamya*): souls possess unequal capacities, and traditions of the school classify their destinies differently. Bondage arises from ignorance of dependence and karmic attachment. Correct knowledge of difference, devotion and divine grace lead eligible souls to liberation.

**Objection:** Eternal difference and hierarchy compromise divine unity and fairness. **Reply:** Madhva argues that unity means God’s unrivalled independence, not identity of all beings; grace perfects rather than erases distinct natures.

**Evaluation:** Dvaita strongly secures realism, devotion and personal immortality, but eternal inequality and the dependent-yet-eternal status of matter create serious theological tensions.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both self and world through dependence, fivefold difference, soul-gradation, bondage and liberation.
