### Q1(a). “Social Media: A Challenge to Societal Harmony”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(a). “Social Media: A Challenge to Societal Harmony” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → communication, polarisation and civic responsibility · `qualifying-english/essay-social-media-societal-harmony.md`

**Model essay**

Social media has transformed communication from a largely one-way activity into a continuous public conversation. It enables families to remain connected, citizens to obtain information and neglected voices to reach a wide audience. Yet the same systems can amplify suspicion, hostility and falsehood with exceptional speed. Social media is therefore not inherently opposed to societal harmony, but its incentives and patterns of use pose a serious challenge to it.

Harmony does not require complete agreement. A diverse society remains harmonious when disagreement is expressed without denying another person’s dignity or belonging. Social media often weakens this restraint. Face-to-face conversation supplies tone, context and immediate human response; an online post can reduce a complex person to a label. Distance and anonymity encourage insults that many users would avoid in ordinary life.

The design of platforms deepens the problem. Content that provokes anger or fear attracts attention and is rapidly shared. Algorithms learn from such engagement and may repeatedly show users material resembling what they already approve. People can consequently enter “echo chambers” in which their own assumptions seem universally accepted and opponents appear foolish or dangerous. Political, religious, regional and caste differences then harden into mutually hostile identities.

Misinformation is another threat. A fabricated message, altered image or clipped video can travel through trusted family and community networks before verification catches up. Repetition creates familiarity, and familiarity may be mistaken for truth. Rumours can damage reputations, intensify communal tension or produce panic. The harm is especially severe when users forward material because it confirms prejudice rather than because they have examined its source.

Social comparison also affects harmony at the personal level. Carefully edited displays of wealth, beauty and success can generate inadequacy, resentment and anxiety. Online approval becomes a measure of worth, while constant exposure reduces reflective attention. Families may occupy the same room yet remain absorbed in separate screens. A technology designed to connect people can thus diminish patient listening and companionship.

Nevertheless, blaming technology alone would be mistaken. Social media has organised disaster relief, reunited missing persons, supported social movements and allowed citizens to question authority. Marginalised groups can narrate experiences ignored by established media. During isolation or emergency, digital communities provide information and emotional support. The task is not to silence social media but to shape it toward responsible communication.

Platforms must act transparently against coordinated manipulation, incitement and fraudulent content while protecting legitimate criticism. Their recommendation systems should not reward harmful engagement without accountability. Governments require clear, proportionate laws and independent oversight; vague censorship can suppress dissent in the name of harmony. Fact-checkers, journalists and civil-society organisations should respond quickly in accessible languages.

The deepest defence is digital citizenship. Users must pause before forwarding, distinguish evidence from assertion, read beyond headlines and engage with disagreement civilly. Schools should teach media literacy alongside language and civics. Public figures bear a special duty not to convert every issue into an occasion for division.

Social media magnifies both the generosity and the prejudice already present in society. If profit, speed and outrage remain its governing principles, it will continue to strain social trust. If design, law and individual conduct are guided by truth, dignity and dialogue, it can strengthen rather than fracture community. Societal harmony in the digital age depends not on avoiding difference, but on learning to communicate across it responsibly.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines harmony, analyses platform design and user behaviour, balances harms with public benefits, and proposes proportionate institutional and civic remedies.

### Q1(b). “The Role of NGOs in Social Change”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(b). “The Role of NGOs in Social Change” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → civil society, participation and accountable change · `qualifying-english/essay-ngos-social-change.md`

**Model essay**

Social change rarely results from government action or market forces alone. It also grows through citizens who organise around needs, rights and shared responsibilities. Non-governmental organisations occupy this civil-society space. They vary from small community groups to large professional institutions, but at their best they connect public policy with lived experience and enable people to participate in changing their own conditions.

One important role of NGOs is to reach communities that formal institutions may overlook. Remote settlements, migrant workers, persons with disabilities and socially marginalised groups often face barriers of distance, information or discrimination. An NGO rooted in the locality can identify these obstacles, provide immediate services and help eligible persons approach public authorities. Its flexibility may permit experimentation where a large department must follow uniform procedures.

Service delivery, however, is only one dimension. Durable social change requires agency. Organisations that merely distribute benefits may create dependence, whereas those that build collectives, train local leaders and spread legal awareness enlarge people’s capacity to act. Women’s groups, self-help associations and disability-rights organisations can turn apparently private hardships into public questions of equality and justice.

NGOs also act as bridges of knowledge. They translate technical policy into accessible language and carry community experience back to administrators and researchers. Work in public health, education, environment or livelihoods can reveal why a well-designed scheme fails during implementation. Pilot projects may demonstrate new methods which governments can later adopt at scale. Civil society thus contributes practical evidence, not merely criticism.

Advocacy is equally significant. Democratic institutions function better when official claims are independently examined. NGOs conduct surveys, assist citizens in using transparency laws, pursue public-interest litigation and draw attention to violations. They may give continuity to issues that receive only brief media attention. Environmental protection, child rights and access to food or work have all benefited from organised civic engagement.

During disasters and emergencies, NGOs can mobilise volunteers and local networks rapidly. Yet speed must be joined with coordination. Unplanned charity may duplicate supplies in visible areas while neglecting less accessible communities. Professional standards, accurate needs assessment and cooperation with public agencies are therefore essential.

NGOs themselves are not beyond criticism. Some may be dependent on donors, unrepresentative of the communities they claim to serve or insufficiently transparent about finances and outcomes. External funding can distort priorities; a project may follow a donor’s fashionable theme rather than local need. Organisations may also substitute temporary relief for structural reform. Good intention cannot replace competence, accountability or respect for democratic institutions.

Their work must also be judged by outcomes rather than publicity. A moving campaign may attract donations, yet careful monitoring is needed to learn whether the intended community actually gained lasting capacity, access or protection.

The answer is neither uncritical praise nor indiscriminate restriction. NGOs should disclose funding, governance and results, follow lawful financial standards and create meaningful community oversight. Governments should regulate them fairly without treating criticism as disloyalty. Partnerships must preserve the state’s constitutional responsibility rather than outsource essential duties permanently.

The finest NGO works not as a benefactor above the people but as a facilitator beside them. It combines service with empowerment, evidence with advocacy and innovation with accountability. Social change becomes lasting when communities acquire voice, institutions become responsive and successful local practice influences wider policy. NGOs cannot replace elected government, but a healthy democracy needs their energy, independence and closeness to society.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers service, empowerment, knowledge, advocacy and emergency work while candidly addressing representation, funding and accountability.

### Q1(c). “Education as a Means to Serve Humanity”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(c). “Education as a Means to Serve Humanity” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → knowledge, character and public service · `qualifying-english/essay-education-serve-humanity.md`

**Model essay**

Education is often pursued for examination results, employment and social advancement. These aims are legitimate, especially for families seeking security. Yet education fulfils its highest purpose only when personal capability contributes to the well-being of others. To educate is not merely to fill the mind with information; it is to cultivate knowledge, judgement and sympathy that can be placed at the service of humanity.

The first service education performs is liberation from ignorance. Literacy allows a person to understand rights, health advice, public notices and the wider world. Scientific education replaces fear and superstition with inquiry. Historical and social knowledge helps citizens recognise how inequality is created and sustained. Education therefore enlarges freedom: people become better able to make choices rather than simply accept conditions imposed upon them.

Knowledge serves humanity most visibly through professions. A doctor relieves suffering, an engineer provides safe infrastructure, a teacher develops future generations and a scientist expands the range of possible solutions. But technical skill alone is insufficient. A brilliant professional without ethical judgement may exploit patients, design unsafe systems or manipulate data. Education must unite competence with responsibility.

The humanities are equally important. Literature enables readers to enter lives unlike their own; history exposes the consequences of prejudice and power; philosophy trains the mind to examine purposes rather than merely means. Such study nurtures empathy and moral imagination. A society may possess advanced technology yet remain inhumane if it cannot recognise the dignity of the weak.

Education also prepares citizens for democratic service. A reasoned public culture requires the ability to test evidence, listen to opposing views and distinguish criticism from hatred. Educated citizens can monitor institutions, resist misinformation and participate constructively in local affairs. Service need not always take the form of a heroic career; honest work, informed voting, mentoring a child or helping a neighbour are everyday expressions of educated responsibility.

Access to education is itself a humanitarian obligation. Poverty, caste, gender, disability, language and geography still restrict opportunity. When quality education is confined to the privileged, knowledge reinforces hierarchy instead of serving humanity. Inclusive schools, scholarships, accessible materials and well-supported teachers are therefore not acts of charity but investments in equal human potential.

The method of education matters. Excessive memorisation may produce obedient candidates rather than thoughtful persons. Competition without cooperation can teach students to treat classmates as obstacles. Service learning, discussion, experimentation and community projects connect knowledge with real needs. Students should encounter environmental problems, public-health challenges and local crafts not as abstract chapters alone but as fields requiring respectful engagement.

Education must also teach humility. Every discipline has limits, and every expert can be corrected by evidence or by the experience of those directly affected. Humanity is not served when educated people speak for communities without listening to them. Genuine service works with people, not merely for them.

The worth of education is finally measured by the uses to which knowledge is put. It should enable individuals to earn with dignity, but also to act with conscience; to succeed, but also to share; to question inherited wrongs, yet remain open to correction. Education serves humanity when the trained mind and the compassionate heart work together. Its highest graduate is not simply the person who knows more, but the person whose knowledge makes life more just, free and humane.

**Why this earns marks:** It joins literacy, professional competence, humanities, citizenship, inclusion and humility to a clear thesis about knowledge in public service.

### Q1(d). “The Crying Need for Embracing Minimalism”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(d). “The Crying Need for Embracing Minimalism” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Reflective essay → consumption, attention and sustainable well-being · `qualifying-english/essay-embracing-minimalism.md`

**Model essay**

Modern life offers an unprecedented variety of goods, information and experiences. Yet abundance has not automatically produced contentment. Homes become crowded, schedules overloaded, attention fragmented and natural resources depleted. Minimalism—the deliberate choice to keep and pursue what is genuinely useful or meaningful—has therefore become a crying need. It is not compulsory poverty or rejection of comfort; it is freedom from excess.

Consumer culture often equates identity with possession. Advertising creates dissatisfaction by presenting every purchase as a step toward beauty, status or belonging. Goods that still function are discarded because a newer version appears. The result is financial anxiety for individuals and enormous material waste for society. Minimalism interrupts this cycle by asking whether an object serves a real need or merely a passing impulse.

The environmental case is urgent. Every product embodies raw materials, energy, transport and eventual disposal. Excessive consumption accelerates extraction, pollution and climate change. Repairing, sharing, reusing and buying durable goods reduce this pressure. Minimalism cannot by itself replace sound environmental policy or responsible industry, but it aligns everyday choices with ecological limits.

Excess is not confined to objects. Digital life fills each idle moment with messages, alerts and endless streams of content. Constant stimulation weakens concentration and leaves little space for reflection. A minimalist approach to technology might involve fewer applications, protected periods without screens and deliberate selection of reliable information. The aim is not isolation but the recovery of attention.

Time also becomes cluttered. People may accept every invitation, ambition and obligation until activity crowds out purpose. Minimalism requires choosing priorities and refusing commitments that do not serve them. A simpler schedule can make room for sleep, relationships, exercise and civic participation. Doing fewer things carefully may be more valuable than performing many things hurriedly.

Minimalism also has a social dimension. Competitive display intensifies inequality because dignity appears dependent on consumption. A culture that respects moderation can reduce the pressure to imitate lifestyles beyond one’s means. It can shift admiration from conspicuous ownership toward skill, character and contribution. Traditional practices of conservation and repair offer useful lessons, though poverty itself must never be romanticised as simplicity.

This distinction is essential. For a family lacking food, shelter or healthcare, “living with less” is not a lifestyle choice but deprivation. Minimalism is most relevant to those who possess enough yet continue to consume without limit. Social justice still requires expanding the basic capabilities of the poor. The ethical principle is sufficiency for all rather than austerity for some and extravagance for others.

Minimalism need not produce bare rooms or identical lives. One person may value books, another tools or family heirlooms. The test is intentionality: possessions should support life rather than govern it. Nor should simplicity become another fashionable competition in which expensive “minimalist” products advertise superiority.

Its practice can begin modestly: delaying an unnecessary purchase, repairing an old object, clearing an overfilled calendar or protecting an hour from digital interruption. Repeated choices make simplicity durable.

To embrace minimalism is to distinguish need from appetite, value from price and experience from display. It lightens debt, waste and distraction while restoring attention to relationships and purposes. In an age of ecological strain and psychological overload, restraint is not backwardness. It is a mature form of freedom—the capacity to say that enough is enough, and to use the space thereby created for a richer human life.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines minimalism carefully, applies it to goods, environment, technology and time, and distinguishes chosen sufficiency from involuntary poverty.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression, coherence and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Compression logic**

- Retain the three kinds of scholarship and the three shared limitations.
- Preserve the contrast between colonial and nationalist interpretations.
- Keep the criticism of the dominant functionalist focus.
- Compress, but do not erase, the scale, distribution and regional patterns of overseas Indians.
- Supply **no title**, because the paper expressly prohibits one.

**Model précis — intentionally untitled**

Recent writing on Indians abroad is mainly historical, diplomatic or anthropological. It respectively describes phases of emigration and early conditions, reports their status and problems in particular countries, or studies cultural continuity and change. Despite this variety, most studies remain country-specific rather than comparative, descriptive rather than analytical, and strongly shaped by a colonial perspective.

Two broad interpretations dominate. The colonial account portrays Indians as voluntary migrants driven by poverty or greed, culturally rigid, quarrelsome and eager to displace local people; it treats their difficulties as self-created. The nationalist account argues instead that many Indians were transported under colonial arrangements, deceived rather than predatory, and industrious contributors to their host societies. According to this view, they were tolerated while subordinate but opposed when they asserted rights, and their later problems arose from neo-colonial politics.

Research generally follows a functionalist concern with cultural identity and integration while neglecting class, power and alternative perspectives. Yet overseas Indians deserve wider scholarly and civic attention. Although fewer than overseas Chinese or British, they form substantial communities across South, Southeast and Southwest Asia, eastern and southern Africa, Western Europe, North America and the Caribbean. Communities of at least 1,500 exist in fifty-three countries, and Indians form majorities or near-majorities in several.

Their origins also reveal broad regional links: South Indians, especially Tamils, predominate in parts of Asia and South Africa; eastern Indians in the West Indies; and Punjabis and Gujaratis in Africa, Europe and North America. These patterns are significant without excluding Indians of other origins.
