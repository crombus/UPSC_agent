## Q1. ‘Constitutional Morality’ is rooted in the Constitution itself and is founded on its essential facets. Explain the doctrine of ‘Constitutional Morality’ with the help of relevant judicial decisions.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Salient Features and Constitutional Parts](../../topics/polity/salient-features-and-constitutional-parts.md) → Constitutional morality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain with judicial decisions  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutionalism → Constitutional morality  
**Canonical owner:** [Salient Features](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/03_Salient-Features.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `constitutional-morality-doctrine`

**Demand decoding:** Define the doctrine from constitutional text and values, identify its operative facets, and demonstrate its judicial application without equating it with judges’ personal morality.

**Compact framework:** Text and structure → core values → institutional conduct → cases → democratic restraint.

### Model answer

Constitutional morality means fidelity to the Constitution’s text, structure and transformative values rather than prevailing social morality or official preference. It requires institutions and citizens to respect liberty, equality, dignity, fraternity, rule of law, constitutional procedures and limitations on power.

In *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (2018), the Supreme Court linked constitutional morality to representative government, federal balance and institutional respect. *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018) held that constitutional morality protects individual dignity and sexual autonomy against majoritarian social morality. *Joseph Shine* (2018) invalidated the adultery offence because patriarchal notions cannot override equality and autonomy. In *Indian Young Lawyers Association* (Sabarimala, 2018), the plurality invoked dignity and equality, though the case also shows that the doctrine must remain anchored in identifiable constitutional provisions.

**Conclusion:** Constitutional morality is a discipline of limited, rights-respecting government. Its legitimacy lies in enforcing constitutional commitments—not in authorising courts or governments to substitute subjective morality for law.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the doctrine, identifies its constitutional anchors, uses decisions for distinct applications and adds a principled limitation.

## Q2. Discuss the desirability of greater representation to women in the higher judiciary to ensure diversity, equity and inclusiveness.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial diversity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss desirability  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Diversity in judicial appointments  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Women and gender justice  
**Canonical owners:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/18_Supreme-Court.md) · [Women and Gender Justice](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/05_Women-and-Gender-Justice.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `women-representation-higher-judiciary`

**Demand decoding:** Establish why representation matters to judicial legitimacy and justice, while avoiding the claim that women judges decide uniformly or that diversity replaces merit.

**Compact framework:** Democratic legitimacy → substantive gains → pipeline barriers → appointment reforms → qualification.

### Model answer

Appointments under Articles **124 and 217** should make greater representation of women in the Supreme Court and High Courts a constitutional and institutional objective.

- **Equality:** Articles 14 and 16 require equal opportunity; an opaque pipeline should not reproduce historical exclusion.
- **Legitimacy:** A judiciary drawn from the society it serves strengthens public confidence and the appearance of impartial justice.
- **Institutional perspective:** Diverse experience can expose gendered effects in bail, family, workplace and service-law disputes. *Vishaka* (1997) illustrates recognition of structural inequality.
- **Talent pool:** Excluding women through late elevation, unequal briefing opportunities, unsafe workplaces or assumptions about mobility wastes merit.

Reform should widen consideration of women advocates and district judges, publish appointment criteria, improve workplace safety and childcare, and record diversity as a relevant constitutional factor.

**Conclusion:** Diversity must supplement, not dilute, professional competence and integrity. Women are not a homogeneous judicial bloc; the objective is equal access to the bench and a more representative institution.

**Why this earns marks:** It ties representation to constitutional equality, decisional quality and legitimacy while preserving the merit and individual-independence qualification.

## Q3. How have the recommendations of the 14th Finance Commission of India enabled the States to improve their fiscal position?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Finance Commission](../../topics/polity/finance-commission.md) → Fourteenth Finance Commission · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain effects  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fiscal Federalism → Finance Commission  
**Canonical owner:** [Finance Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/29_Finance-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `fourteenth-finance-commission-state-finances`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the Commission’s principal recommendations and explain the mechanism through which each increased State fiscal space, autonomy or predictability.

**Compact framework:** Higher devolution → untied autonomy → grants → local bodies/disasters → qualification.

### Model answer

The Fourteenth Finance Commission, constituted under **Article 280** and covering 2015–20, strengthened fiscal federalism principally by raising the States’ share in the divisible pool of Union taxes from **32% to 42%**.

This enabled States to:

- receive larger, predictable, formula-based tax devolution;
- gain greater **untied fiscal space**, allowing expenditure according to State priorities rather than centrally designed schemes;
- address horizontal disparities through a formula using income distance, population, area, forest cover and demographic change;
- bridge assessed post-devolution gaps through **revenue-deficit grants**;
- strengthen panchayats and municipalities through sizeable basic and performance grants;
- finance disaster response through recommended State Disaster Response Fund arrangements.

The Commission also encouraged fiscal discipline and transparent borrowing while recommending fewer sector-specific grants, thereby favouring autonomy over micromanagement.

**Conclusion:** The recommendations improved State finances through both more resources and greater spending discretion. Their effect, however, depended on buoyant divisible-pool revenues and could be weakened by the Union’s growing reliance on cesses and surcharges, which are not shareable.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the exact devolution change and explains fiscal capacity, autonomy, equalisation and the cess-related limitation.

## Q4. To what extent, in your view, the Parliament is able to ensure accountability of the executive in India?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Executive accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: To what extent  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Executive accountability  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `parliament-executive-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Make a graded assessment by explaining constitutional and procedural controls, then identify why party government often weakens their practical operation.

**Compact framework:** Collective responsibility → scrutiny tools → financial/committee control → constraints → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Parliament possesses extensive accountability instruments. Under **Article 75(3)**, the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Question Hour, debates, calling-attention devices, adjournment and no-confidence motions require explanation and can impose political costs. Budget approval, cut motions and scrutiny of CAG reports protect the power of the purse. Department-related Standing Committees, the Public Accounts Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings permit detailed, relatively non-partisan examination.

Its effectiveness is nevertheless uneven. A stable government majority, party whips and the Tenth Schedule reduce independent legislative voting. Disruptions and short sittings compress debate; many Bills receive limited committee scrutiny; delegated legislation and ordinances expand executive law-making. Committees lack binding powers and often face delayed governmental responses.

**Conclusion:** Parliament can expose, question and ultimately remove the executive, but day-to-day accountability is only partial. More sittings, systematic committee referral, protected opposition time, timely answers and narrower whips would convert formal responsibility into sustained scrutiny.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “to what extent” by distinguishing strong constitutional tools from their party-political and procedural limitations.

## Q5. “Pressure groups play a vital role in influencing public policy making in India.” Explain how the business associations contribute to public policies.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Pressure Groups](../../topics/polity/pressure-groups.md) → Business associations · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain contribution

**Canonical owner:** [Pressure Groups](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/44_Pressure-Groups.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `business-associations-public-policy`

**Demand decoding:** Explain concrete channels and constructive functions through which organised business influences policy, while acknowledging risks of unequal access and regulatory capture.

**Compact framework:** Information → consultation → implementation → representation → accountability safeguards.

### Model answer

Business associations such as **FICCI, CII and ASSOCHAM** aggregate dispersed enterprise interests and translate them into policy inputs.

They contribute by:

- submitting pre-Budget memoranda and evidence on taxation, trade, credit and regulation;
- participating in ministry consultations, expert groups and standards-setting bodies;
- commissioning research and sector reports that reduce information gaps;
- communicating implementation problems faced by MSMEs and industries;
- building consensus on reforms and disseminating new rules among firms;
- supporting skilling, technology adoption, export promotion and public-private partnerships;
- representing Indian industry internationally.

Such participation can improve regulatory feasibility and anticipate economic consequences. During crises, industry networks also help coordinate supplies and operational feedback.

However, concentrated resources may produce privileged access, opaque lobbying or regulatory capture, marginalising labour, consumers and informal firms.

**Conclusion:** Business associations are legitimate policy intermediaries when consultation is transparent, disclosures and conflict-of-interest rules apply, and government tests their claims against wider public interest and competing stakeholder evidence.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains identifiable policy channels and balances expertise-based participation against capture risks.

## Q6. “Besides being a moral imperative of a Welfare State, primary health structure is a necessary precondition for sustainable development.” Analyze.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage](../../topics/social-justice/health-systems-public-health-and-universal-health-coverage.md) → Primary healthcare · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Analyze  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Health → Primary healthcare and sustainable development  
**Canonical owner:** [Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/03_Health-Systems-Public-Health-and-Universal-Health-Coverage.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `primary-healthcare-sustainable-development`

**Demand decoding:** Establish both limbs: the State’s normative obligation and the causal contribution of accessible primary care to economic, social and environmental sustainability.

**Compact framework:** Welfare obligation → prevention and equity → development effects → system design → conclusion.

### Model answer

A welfare State must protect life and dignity through accessible healthcare. **Article 47** makes improvement of public health a primary State duty, while Article 21 jurisprudence connects health with the right to life.

Primary healthcare converts this obligation into sustainable development because it:

- prevents disease through immunisation, nutrition, sanitation and health education;
- detects and manages illness early, reducing costly hospitalisation and out-of-pocket impoverishment;
- improves maternal and child survival, learning and productivity;
- supplies surveillance and community trust needed against epidemics;
- corrects rural, gender and income inequalities;
- integrates mental health and non-communicable-disease care close to households.

Thus it advances SDGs on health, poverty, education and gender. The **Alma-Ata** approach and Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centres reflect community-based care.

**Conclusion:** Hospitals alone cannot produce sustainable development. Adequately staffed sub-centres and primary health centres, essential medicines, referral networks and local accountability create a healthier population while preventing medical expenditure from reversing development gains.

**Why this earns marks:** It links constitutional duty to specific preventive, equity and productivity mechanisms rather than treating health only as welfare spending.

## Q7. “Earn while you learn” scheme needs to be strengthened to make vocational education and skill training meaningful. Comment.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Education and Human Resource Development](../../topics/social-justice/education-and-human-resource-development.md) → Work-linked vocational education · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Education and Skills → Apprenticeship and vocational learning  
**Canonical owner:** [Education and Human Resource Development](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/04_Education-and-Human-Resource-Development.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `earn-while-you-learn-vocational-skills`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why paid work-integrated learning is valuable, diagnose weaknesses in vocational training, and specify reforms that preserve education and worker protection.

**Compact framework:** Rationale → current gaps → strengthened design → safeguards.

### Model answer

“Earn while you learn” combines classroom instruction with paid workplace experience. It can reduce dropout caused by financial hardship, remove the stigma attached to vocational education and give learners job-relevant skills, work discipline and employer networks.

Its promise is weakened by low stipends, few quality employers, obsolete curricula, weak trainer capacity, poor industry–institute coordination and certificates that do not support credit mobility. Some placements risk becoming cheap labour without structured learning.

Strengthening requires:

- expansion of apprenticeships under the **Apprentices Act, 1961** and NAPS, especially among MSMEs;
- competency standards aligned with the **National Skills Qualification Framework**;
- jointly designed curricula, trained mentors and assessed learning outcomes;
- adequate, timely stipends with safety and anti-harassment protection;
- academic credits and pathways between vocational and general education, as envisaged by **NEP 2020**;
- placement tracking and independent quality audits.

**Conclusion:** Payment makes training accessible, but only certified competencies, educational mobility and decent-work safeguards make it meaningful.

**Why this earns marks:** It comments critically, names the relevant institutional framework and distinguishes genuine apprenticeship from subsidised casual work.

## Q8. Can the vicious cycle of gender inequality, poverty and malnutrition be broken through microfinancing of women SHGs? Explain with examples.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → Women’s self-help groups · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Assess and explain with examples  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Self-Help Groups → Women’s financial and social empowerment  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Gender, poverty and nutrition  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `women-shg-microfinance-poverty-malnutrition`

**Demand decoding:** Trace the causal pathway from finance to agency, income and nutrition; support it with Indian examples; and qualify microcredit as an enabling but insufficient intervention.

**Compact framework:** Credit and collectives → income/agency → nutrition effects → examples → limits.

### Model answer

Women’s SHG microfinance can weaken this vicious cycle because credit is combined with collective agency. The **SHG–Bank Linkage Programme**, initiated by NABARD in 1992, and **DAY-NRLM** connect savings groups to formal finance and livelihoods.

Small loans can fund livestock, food processing or retail, diversifying household income. Group meetings improve financial literacy, mobility and bargaining power; women controlling income often prioritise food, healthcare and children’s education. Federations can also deliver nutrition awareness and monitor entitlements. **Kudumbashree** in Kerala integrates women’s livelihoods with local development; NRLM groups have supported community kitchens and nutrition campaigns.

Yet credit alone cannot cure unequal wages, unpaid care, anaemia, poor sanitation or weak health services. High-cost multiple borrowing and enterprises without market access may deepen distress.

**Conclusion:** SHG finance can break key links in the cycle when paired with skills, affordable credit, producer collectives, markets, property rights, childcare, public nutrition and healthcare.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the mechanism, provides named examples and avoids presenting microfinance as a standalone cure.

**Primary route:** International Relations → [India, China, Major Powers and Resilient Supply Chains](../../topics/international-relations/india-china-major-powers-and-resilient-supply-chains.md) → US–China strategic competition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Primary classification:** International Relations → Major Powers → US–China competition  
**Canonical owner:** [India–China, Major Powers and Resilient Supply Chains](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/03_India-China-Major-Powers-and-Resilient-Supply-Chains.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain why China presents a broader challenge than the USSR, compare economic, technological and military dimensions, and qualify the term “existential.”

### Model answer

China challenges US primacy across more dimensions than the Soviet Union did.

Unlike the isolated USSR, China is a manufacturing and trading hub embedded in global supply chains and US markets. It converts economic scale into technology, infrastructure finance and influence through the Belt and Road Initiative. Its military modernisation and anti-access systems contest US power in the western Pacific, especially around Taiwan. ## Q11. The jurisdiction of the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) regarding lodging an FIR and conducting probe within a particular State is being questioned by various States. However, the power of the States to withhold consent to the CBI is not absolute. Explain with special reference to the federal character of India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [CVC & CBI — Anti-Corruption Bodies](../../topics/polity/cvc-and-cbi.md) → State consent under the DSPE Act · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Explain with federal reference  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Investigative Agencies → CBI jurisdiction and State consent  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Distribution of police powers  
**Canonical owners:** [CVC and CBI](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/CVC-and-CBI.md) · [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Federal-System.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cbi-state-consent-federalism`

**Demand decoding:** State the DSPE Act consent rule, explain the constitutional reason for it, identify recognised exceptions, and reconcile investigative efficacy with federal autonomy.

**Compact framework:** Statutory basis → federal principle → general/specific consent → limits and exceptions → cooperative safeguards.

### Model answer

The CBI derives ordinary police powers from the **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946**, not from a general constitutional police power. Under Section **5**, the Union may extend DSPE powers to a State; Section **6** requires that State’s consent. This respects “police” and “public order” in the State List.

Consent may be **general**, covering notified classes of offences, or **specific**, case by case. A State may withdraw general consent prospectively, requiring consent for new cases. Withdrawal does not ordinarily invalidate investigations already validly commenced.

Yet State control is not absolute:

1. The Supreme Court and High Courts may order a CBI investigation under Articles **32 and 226** without State consent. In *State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights* (2010), a Constitution Bench held that this judicial power forms part of constitutional review and does not violate federalism.
2. Constitutional courts may intervene where a credible, impartial investigation is necessary to enforce fundamental rights.
3. State withdrawal cannot extinguish the constitutional jurisdiction of courts or automatically obstruct proceedings already authorised according to law.

The judicial exception must be used sparingly; routine displacement of State police would erode federal balance. Conversely, blanket withdrawal can impede inter-State investigations.

**Conclusion:** Indian federalism protects State control over policing but remains subject to constitutional rights and judicial review. A statutory charter for the CBI, transparent case-selection standards and institutional consultation would reduce consent disputes while preserving both impartial investigation and federal comity.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately distinguishes statutory consent, prospective withdrawal and the constitutional-court exception through controlling precedent.

## Q12. Though the Human Rights Commissions have contributed immensely to the protection of human rights in India, yet they have failed to assert themselves against the mighty and powerful. Analyzing their structural and practical limitations, suggest remedial measures.
**Primary route:** Polity → [CVC & CBI — Anti-Corruption Bodies](../../topics/polity/cvc-and-cbi.md) → Q12. Though the Human Rights Commissions have contributed immensely to the protection of human rights in India, yet they have failed to assert themselves against the mighty and powerful. Analyzing their structural and practical limitations, suggest remedial measures. · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Primary route:** Polity → Human Rights Commissions → NHRC and SHRC · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Analyze; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Statutory Bodies → Human Rights Commissions  
**Canonical owners:** [NHRC and SHRC](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/NHRC-and-SHRC.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `human-rights-commissions-limitations-reforms`

**Demand decoding:** Acknowledge contributions, separate design defects from operational weaknesses, explain why these matter against powerful actors, and match each diagnosis with a remedy.

**Compact framework:** Contributions → structural limits → practical limits → institutional remedies → calibrated conclusion.

### Model answer

The NHRC and SHRCs under the **Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993** have investigated custodial abuse, recommended compensation, inspected institutions, promoted awareness and kept rights violations in public view. Their civil-court powers under Section 13 aid inquiry.

**Structural limitations**

- Recommendations under Section **18** are generally not binding; compliance depends on the same governments scrutinised.
- The one-year inquiry bar under Section **36(2)** can exclude delayed complaints.
- Under Section **19**, the NHRC’s role regarding armed forces is largely confined to seeking a Union report and making recommendations.
- Appointment processes are executive-dominated and insufficiently transparent; vacancies and limited diversity weaken independence.
- SHRC jurisdiction, staffing and resources remain uneven.

**Practical limitations**

Dependence on government investigators risks conflict of interest. Case backlogs, inadequate field capacity, delayed action-taken reports and weak follow-up reduce deterrence. Commissions may address individual compensation without correcting systemic causes.

**Remedies**

Appointments should follow transparent criteria, public consultation and plural representation consistent with the **Paris Principles**. Commissions need independent investigation cadres, adequate budgets and timely filling of vacancies. Governments should be statutorily required to give reasoned, time-bound responses, with non-compliance reported to legislatures. Parliament should reconsider the limitation period and armed-forces procedure while retaining legitimate security safeguards. SHRCs and district-level access should be strengthened; systemic recommendations should be monitored through public dashboards.

**Conclusion:** Human Rights Commissions should remain recommendatory watchdogs rather than courts, but independence, investigative capacity and enforceable governmental accountability are essential if their voice is to constrain powerful institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes legal design from implementation and proposes remedies directly corresponding to each limitation.

## Q13. Analyze the distinguishing features of the notion of Right to Equality in the Constitutions of the USA and India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Comparative equality law · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Comparative analysis  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Equality in India and the USA  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/07_Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `right-to-equality-india-usa`

**Demand decoding:** Compare constitutional text, scope, tests, affirmative action and judicial doctrines, showing similarities as well as distinctive Indian commitments.

**Compact framework:** Common principle → textual design → standards of review → substantive equality → judicial development.

### Model answer

Both Constitutions reject arbitrary discrimination, but they structure equality differently.

| Dimension | United States | India |
|---|---|---|
| Text | Fourteenth Amendment: no State shall deny “equal protection of the laws”; Fifth Amendment due process applies equality constraints to the federal government | Articles **14–18** create a detailed equality code: equality before law, equal protection, non-discrimination, public-employment equality, abolition of untouchability and titles |
| Reach | Primarily restrains governmental “state action” | Primarily binds the State; Articles **15(2), 17 and 23** also reach specified private conduct |
| Review | Classifications receive rational-basis, intermediate or strict scrutiny depending on the ground | Article 14 uses reasonable classification—intelligible differentia and rational nexus—and also prohibits manifest arbitrariness |
| Corrective equality | Affirmative action developed through constitutional interpretation and legislation, but is closely judicially scrutinised | Articles **15(3), 15(4), 15(5), 15(6), 16(4), 16(4A) and 16(6)** expressly permit special provisions or reservations |

US equality jurisprudence dismantled racial segregation in *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954). India combines anti-classification with substantive social transformation: *State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar* developed the classification test; *E.P. Royappa* (1974) linked equality with non-arbitrariness; *Indra Sawhney* (1992) elaborated reservation principles.

India’s formulation “equality before law” draws on the British rule-of-law idea, while “equal protection” resembles the US clause. Yet India constitutionally recognises caste hierarchy and untouchability and therefore more explicitly authorises group-differentiated remedies.

**Conclusion:** US doctrine is centred on equal protection and graded judicial scrutiny; India’s equality code is broader and expressly transformative, joining formal equality with affirmative measures to achieve substantive equality.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares text, application, doctrine and affirmative action with named provisions and leading cases.

## Q14. Explain the constitutional provisions under which Legislative Councils are established. Review the working and current status of Legislative Councils with suitable illustrations.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Q14. Explain the constitutional provisions under which Legislative Councils are established. Review the working and current status of Legislative Councils with suitable illustrations. · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Primary route:** Polity → State Legislature → Legislative Councils · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2021 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Explain; Review  
**Primary classification:** Polity → State Legislature → Legislative Councils  
**Canonical owner:** [State Legislature](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/20_State-Legislature.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `state-legislative-councils-provisions-working`

**Demand decoding:** Explain creation, composition and powers accurately, then review actual utility, weaknesses and the 2021 institutional position with examples.

**Compact framework:** Article 169 procedure → Article 171 design → limited powers → performance debate → status and reform.

### Model answer

Under **Article 169**, Parliament may create or abolish a State Legislative Council by ordinary law after the Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by a majority of its total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Such a law is not deemed a constitutional amendment under Article 368.

Article **171** limits Council strength to one-third of the Assembly, subject to a minimum of 40. Broadly, one-third are elected by local authorities, one-twelfth each by graduates and teachers, one-third by MLAs, and the remainder are nominated by the Governor for distinction in specified fields. It is a continuing chamber: one-third retire every two years.

Councils provide revision, continuity, specialist voices and representation to local bodies. They can delay and reconsider ordinary Bills and scrutinise administration. However, under Articles **197 and 198**, the Assembly can ultimately override them; a Money Bill can be retained for only 14 days. The Council neither forms nor removes the government.

Critics cite indirect and narrow electorates, low public visibility, expense, partisan rehabilitation and obstruction. Andhra Pradesh illustrates instability: its Council was abolished in 1985 and revived in 2007. As of the 2021 examination context, Councils functioned in **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh**; Jammu and Kashmir’s Council had been abolished through the 2019 reorganisation.

**Conclusion:** Councils are useful only when they operate as expert revising chambers rather than patronage forums. Broader constituencies, transparent nominations, stronger committee work and reasoned legislative review would improve their value.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately covers creation, composition, powers, status and a balanced institutional assessment.

**Demand decoding:** Test the strong claim by explaining committee design and achievements, then weigh practical weaknesses and propose reforms.

**Compact framework:** Institutional design → accountability contributions → examples → limitations → reform and verdict.

### Model answer

The Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee system, established in 1993 and expanded to **24 committees**, enables sustained legislative scrutiny beyond the floor of the Houses. Constituted under parliamentary rules framed pursuant to **Article 118**, these committees examine Demands for Grants, referred Bills, annual reports and long-term policy documents of allocated ministries.

They keep administration “on its toes” by:

- questioning secretaries and experts in a less partisan setting;
- identifying expenditure gaps before budget approval;
- obtaining written governmental explanations and monitoring action-taken reports;
- subjecting technically complex Bills to stakeholder evidence;
- building cross-party policy knowledge and institutional memory.

The Health Committee’s **123rd Report (2020)** examined COVID-19 management, public-health spending and infrastructure gaps. The Public Accounts framework is distinct, but together with DRSC examination it deepens financial accountability.

The anti-defection law does not directly control committee recommendations, yet party majorities still matter.

Reform should require reference of most non-Money Bills, allow adequate consultation time, strengthen expert research staff, publish reasoned governmental responses and track implementation. Chairs should be allocated fairly and adequate scrutiny time guaranteed.

**Conclusion:** DRSCs do not command executive obedience, but they create informed, continuous and reputational accountability. Their capacity to inspire respect for Parliament depends on systematic referral and serious governmental follow-up.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both oversight mechanisms and real institutional constraints instead of treating committee existence as proof of control.

**Compact framework:** Two-layer divide → sectoral effects → reinforcing inequalities → initiatives → corrective framework.

### Model answer

Digital exclusion has two interacting dimensions: lack of reliable devices, electricity and connectivity, and lack of the skills needed to use digital services safely. Together they hinder rural development even when services formally move online.

**Development effects**

- Farmers without connectivity or literacy cannot easily access weather, extension, market-price, insurance and e-NAM services.
- Students lose access to digital content and remote teaching, as the pandemic exposed.
- Telemedicine, online appointments and health information remain inaccessible.
- Digital-only welfare authentication, banking or grievance systems can convert a technological failure into exclusion from entitlements.
- MSMEs and workers miss e-commerce, digital payments, skilling and employment opportunities.

Digital-service standards should measure successful citizen outcomes, not merely enrolment or connections.

**Conclusion:** Digital illiteracy and inaccessible ICT do not merely reflect underdevelopment; they reproduce it by restricting capabilities and public-service access. Technology becomes developmental only when physical access, skills, trust and grievance redress advance together.

### Model answer

Women’s achievements coexist with patriarchal norms that define authority, property, care work, sexuality and public space as male domains. Feminism is consequently caricatured as anti-family rather than understood as a claim to equal citizenship. Change therefore requires interventions beyond beneficiary schemes.

1. **Equal rights in practice:** enforce inheritance, domestic-violence, dowry, workplace-harassment and sexual-offence laws through gender-sensitive police, prosecutors and courts. *Vishaka* (1997) and the POSH Act require safe institutions, not paper compliance.

**Canonical owner:** [Indo-Pacific, Indian Ocean and Maritime Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/International-Relations/basic/04_Indo-Pacific-Indian-Ocean-and-Maritime-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `aukus-strength-impact-regional-partnerships`

**Demand decoding:** Answer supersession explicitly, identify AUKUS’s distinctive capability contribution, and assess strategic, diplomatic and non-proliferation effects on the plural Indo-Pacific architecture.

**Compact framework:** Nature of pact → comparative institutional roles → strengths → positive/negative impacts → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Announced in September **2021**, AUKUS links Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States in a high-technology security partnership. Its centrepiece is assistance for Australia to acquire **conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines**, alongside cooperation in cyber capabilities, artificial intelligence, quantum technologies and undersea systems.

AUKUS is unlikely to supersede existing arrangements because they perform different functions:

- **ANZUS** remains a defence alliance framework.
- **Five Eyes** focuses on intelligence sharing.
- The **Quad** is a wider, non-alliance partnership covering maritime awareness, resilient supply chains, technology, health and regional public goods.
- **ASEAN-led institutions** provide inclusive diplomatic forums and regional legitimacy.

AUKUS’s strength lies in trusted intelligence relationships, advanced defence-industrial capacity, long-range undersea deterrence and interoperability. It signals durable Anglo-American commitment and can complicate coercive Chinese naval expansion.

Its impact is mixed. It may improve the regional balance and indirectly benefit India by dispersing Chinese military attention. Conversely, China may intensify military modernisation; some ASEAN States fear bloc rivalry. France’s exclusion from Australia’s submarine decision exposed coordination costs among partners. Transfer of naval nuclear-propulsion material to a non-nuclear-weapon State also creates a sensitive safeguards precedent, even though the submarines are not nuclear-armed.

Long delivery timelines and high costs mean immediate deterrent effects are more political than operational.

**Conclusion:** AUKUS adds a hard-capability layer to a networked Indo-Pacific order; it does not replace the Quad, ASEAN or alliances. Its success depends on credible delivery, IAEA-compatible safeguards and reassurance that balancing China will not erode regional autonomy or trigger uncontrolled escalation.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly rejects supersession, distinguishes institutional functions and evaluates military, diplomatic and non-proliferation consequences.
