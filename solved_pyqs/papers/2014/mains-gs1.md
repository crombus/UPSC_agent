## Q1. To what extent has the urban planning and culture of the Indus Valley Civilization provided inputs to the present day urbanization? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Ancient History → [Harappan Civilization](../../topics/ancient-history/harappan-civilization.md) → Urban planning and cultural legacy · **Canonical owners:** [Harappan Civilization](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ancient-Indian-History/basic/06_Harappan-Civilization.md) · [Architecture Foundations and Harappan Urbanism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Art-and-Culture/basic/01_Architecture-Foundations-and-Harappan-Urbanism.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss extent  
**Primary classification:** Ancient History → Harappan Civilization → Urban planning  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Architectural and civic legacy  
**Proposed topic slug:** `indus-urban-planning-culture-inputs-present-urbanization`  
**Demand:** Identify genuine continuities in civic planning and urban culture, distinguish broad lessons from demonstrable historical transmission, and give a graded verdict.

**Compact framework:** Harappan features → modern parallels → cultural continuities → limits of direct descent → verdict.

### Model answer

Harappan cities offer early principles of organized urbanism, although modern Indian cities are not their direct institutional descendants.

- **Dholavira’s** zoning, reservoirs and channels show water-sensitive planning relevant to rainwater harvesting.
- **Mohenjo-daro’s** grid, covered drains, soak-pits and household bathing connected private sanitation with civic infrastructure.
- Standardized bricks and weights, craft quarters and storage structures suggest regulation, modular construction and an integrated economy.
- Wells, courtyards and the Great Bath indicate concern for water, cleanliness and collective space; specialized crafts and trade remain familiar urban functions.

However, Harappan urbanism declined after c. 1900 BCE, and no continuous municipal code links it to present cities. Contemporary urbanization derives more immediately from later Indian towns, colonial planning, industrialization, motor transport and democratic government. Many modern settlements also violate Harappan lessons through poor drainage and unplanned growth.

**Conclusion:** Harappan urbanism supplies an indigenous precedent and sustainable planning principles, not a demonstrably unbroken blueprint.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses named sites and mechanisms, answers “to what extent,” and avoids claiming unsupported direct continuity.

## Q2. Gandhara sculpture owed as much to the Romans as to the Greeks. Explain.
**Primary route:** Indian Art and Culture → [Sculpture, Pottery and Iconography](../../topics/art-and-culture/sculpture-pottery-and-iconography.md) → Gandhara sculpture · **Canonical owner:** [Sculpture, Pottery and Iconography](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Art-and-Culture/basic/06_Sculpture-Pottery-and-Iconography.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain  
**Primary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Sculpture → Gandhara school  
**Secondary classification:** Ancient History → Kushana-age cultural interaction  
**Proposed topic slug:** `gandhara-sculpture-roman-and-greek-influences`  
**Demand:** Separate Greek/Hellenistic and Roman contributions, connect formal features to each, and retain the Indian-Buddhist content of the synthesis.

**Compact framework:** Historical setting → Greek form → Roman technique/iconography → Indian content → synthesis.

### Model answer

Gandhara art, flourishing under the Kushanas in the north-west, was a cosmopolitan Buddhist idiom, not merely “Greek art in India.”

- Its **Greek-Hellenistic** inheritance appears in idealized anatomy, wavy hair, sharp facial modelling and motifs such as Corinthian capitals, acanthus and Atlas figures.
- Roman influence was comparable: heavy toga-like drapery, deeply drilled realistic folds, individualized portraiture and muscular modelling resemble imperial sculpture.
- Architectural frames, vine scrolls, garland-bearers and narrative panels recall Roman decorative and sarcophagus traditions.
- Indo-Roman trade and the circulation of craftsmen and visual models made such transmission plausible.

Yet content and function remained Indian-Buddhist: Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, Jatakas, mudras and life-scenes served monasteries and devotion. Some “Roman” features also belonged to a wider Hellenistic-West Asian vocabulary, so exact origins cannot always be isolated.

**Conclusion:** Greek idealism and Roman naturalism supplied complementary forms that Gandharan artists transformed into a distinctive Buddhist language.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies distinct Roman and Greek traits while qualifying a mechanically derivative interpretation.

## Q3. Taxila university was one of the oldest universities of the world with which were associated a number of renowned learned personalities of different disciplines. Its strategic location caused its fame to flourish, but unlike Nalanda, it is not considered as a university in the modern sense. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Ancient History → [Crafts, Commerce and Urban Growth](../../topics/ancient-history/crafts-commerce-urban-growth.md) → Taxila as an educational centre · **Canonical owners:** [Crafts, Commerce and Urban Growth](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ancient-Indian-History/basic/19_Crafts-Commerce-Urban-Growth.md) · [Sources of Ancient Indian History](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Ancient-Indian-History/basic/02_Sources-of-Ancient-Indian-History.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Difficult · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Ancient History → Education and urban centres → Taxila  
**Secondary classification:** Ancient History → Comparative institutions → Taxila and Nalanda  
**Proposed topic slug:** `taxila-strategic-location-learning-not-modern-university`  
**Demand:** Explain Taxila’s fame, test the role of location, and clarify why the conventional label “university” differs from a centralized modern or Nalanda-type institution.

**Compact framework:** Location → intellectual range → famous associations → institutional form → Nalanda comparison → verdict.

### Model answer

Taxila was a celebrated centre of advanced learning, but “university” is a retrospective label.

- At the junction of routes linking the Gangetic plain, Central Asia and Iran, it attracted merchants, rulers, ideas and students. Achaemenid, Mauryan, Indo-Greek and Kushana phases reinforced its cosmopolitanism.
- Buddhist texts associate it with the Vedas, grammar, medicine, statecraft, archery and crafts. **Panini**, **Kautilya** and physician **Jivaka** are traditionally linked to it, though all associations are not equally verifiable.
- Teaching occurred through autonomous masters and residential circles; pupils selected teachers and paid fees or rendered service.

Unlike **Nalanda**, Taxila had no known unified campus, corporate administration, common curriculum, endowed monastery, central library or degree system. Bhir Mound and Sirkap establish an urban-intellectual milieu, not one educational corporation.

**Conclusion:** Strategic connectivity made Taxila a distributed knowledge hub—university-like in range and reputation, but not a centralized university in the Nalanda or modern sense.

**Why this earns marks:** It addresses location, personalities, disciplines and the institutional comparison while qualifying traditional claims.

## Q4. The third battle of Panipat was fought in 1761. Why were so many empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat?
**Primary route:** Medieval History → [Provincial and Regional Kingdoms](../../topics/medieval-history/provincial-and-regional-kingdoms.md) → Panipat and the contest for Delhi · **Canonical owners:** [Decline of the Mughal Empire](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/01_Decline-of-the-Mughal-Empire.md) · [Indian States and Society in the Eighteenth Century](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/02_Indian-States-and-Society-18th-Century.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain why  
**Primary classification:** Medieval History → Political geography → Battles of Panipat  
**Secondary classification:** Modern History → Eighteenth-century state system  
**Proposed topic slug:** `why-empire-shaking-battles-fought-at-panipat`  
**Demand:** Explain Panipat’s repeated strategic selection through terrain, routes, logistics and proximity to Delhi, supported by the three battles.

**Compact framework:** Gateway geography → battlefield terrain → logistics → Delhi objective → three historical confirmations.

### Model answer

Panipat repeatedly became decisive because geography made it the northern military gateway to Delhi.

- Armies entering through the Khyber–Punjab corridor advanced along the open Lahore–Sirhind–Delhi route. Panipat, about 90 km north of Delhi, allowed interception before the capital.
- Its broad alluvial plain suited cavalry, artillery and infantry deployment better than broken terrain farther north or Delhi’s congested surroundings.
- Nearby roads, settlements, the Yamuna system and fertile doab supplied water, fodder and provisions.
- Delhi conferred imperial legitimacy and access to the Indo-Gangetic heartland, making control of its approach strategically valuable.

Thus Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi in **1526**, Akbar’s forces defeated Hemu in **1556**, and Ahmad Shah Abdali checked Maratha expansion in **1761**. Each occurred when fragmentation made Delhi contestable. Yet victory did not guarantee durable empire: Abdali withdrew.

**Conclusion:** Panipat combined favourable battlefield terrain with command of Delhi’s principal invasion corridor.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains recurrence causally and uses all three battles without reducing the answer to chronology.

## Q5. Sufis and medieval mystic saints failed to modify either the religious ideas and practices or the outward structure of Hindu / Muslim societies to any appreciable extent. Comment.
**Primary route:** Medieval History → [Bhakti and Sufi Movements](../../topics/medieval-history/bhakti-and-sufi-movements.md) → Social and religious impact · **Canonical owners:** [Bhakti and Sufi Movements](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Medieval-Indian-History/advanced/10_Bhakti-and-Sufi-Movements.md) · [Religion, Philosophy and Cultural Synthesis](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Art-and-Culture/basic/13_Religion-Philosophy-and-Cultural-Synthesis.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Difficult · Directive: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Medieval History → Bhakti and Sufism → Religious and social change  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Society → Caste, community and cultural synthesis  
**Proposed topic slug:** `sufis-mystic-saints-religious-social-impact-limits`  
**Demand:** Assess the strong claim of failure by separating change in devotional culture from limited structural transformation.

**Compact framework:** Religious innovations → social-cultural effects → structural persistence → regional variation → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

The proposition is valid about structural transformation, but understates changes in religious experience and culture.

- Chishti Sufis such as **Nizamuddin Auliya** emphasized love and service; khanqahs and dargahs created accessible devotional spaces beyond formal juristic institutions.
- Bhakti voices—**Kabir**, **Ravidas**, **Guru Nanak**, Chaitanya and the Alvars—privileged devotion, criticized ritual status and used vernaculars.
- Poetry, music, pilgrimage and shrine cultures widened participation and produced durable traditions including Sikhism, nirguna bhakti and qawwali.
- Low-status devotees could claim spiritual dignity and alternative communities.

Yet caste and patriarchy survived; several sects later acquired hereditary leadership and caste distinctions. Bhakti rarely redistributed land or power. Sufi orders sometimes accommodated rulers and hierarchy, while orthodox Brahmanical and Islamic institutions remained strong. Effects varied by region and order.

**Conclusion:** Mystic movements appreciably transformed devotional language, ethics and Hindu-Muslim cultural interaction, but only partially altered society’s outward socio-economic structure.

**Why this earns marks:** It comments on both religious and structural dimensions and reaches a calibrated judgement.

## Q6. Examine critically the various facets of economic policies of the British in India from mid-eighteenth century till independence.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Economic Impact of British Rule](../../topics/modern-history/economic-impact-of-british-rule.md) → Colonial economic policy · **Canonical owner:** [Economic Impact of British Rule](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/07_Economic-Impact-of-British-Rule.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Examine critically  
**Primary classification:** Modern History → Colonial economy → British policies  
**Secondary classification:** Modern History → Nationalist economic critique  
**Proposed topic slug:** `british-economic-policies-mid-eighteenth-century-independence`  
**Demand:** Cover the principal policy facets across the whole period, explain their colonial logic and effects, and qualify both continuity and limited modernization.

**Compact framework:** Revenue → trade and industry → agriculture → infrastructure and finance → late change → critical balance.

### Model answer

British policy reorganized India chiefly to finance rule and metropolitan accumulation.

- After the **Diwani (1765)**, Permanent Settlement, ryotwari and mahalwari systems varied institutionally but imposed heavy cash demands, encouraging debt and insecure cultivation.
- Company monopoly and later free trade turned India from a major manufactures exporter into a supplier of cotton, jute, indigo, opium and foodstuffs; Lancashire imports damaged artisans.
- Commercialization lacked adequate credit, irrigation and famine protection, magnifying rural vulnerability.
- Railways, ports, telegraph and canals integrated markets, but guarantees, freight patterns and port orientation primarily served troops, exports and British capital.
- Currency, tariffs and “Home Charges” sustained the nationalist-described **drain of wealth**.

Indian mills, late protective tariffs and wartime demand produced limited industrial growth; infrastructure later aided national integration. Nevertheless, poverty, weak employment and low human development persisted.

**Conclusion:** Colonialism introduced modern instruments but subordinated them to imperial needs, producing dependent and uneven rather than autonomous development.

**Why this earns marks:** It spans the period, links policies to effects, and critically distinguishes modernization from developmental purpose.

## Q7. In what ways did the naval mutiny prove to be the last nail in the coffin of British colonial aspirations in India?
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Post-War Upsurge, INA, RIN Mutiny and Cabinet Mission](../../topics/modern-history/post-war-upsurge-ina-rin-mutiny-and-cabinet-mission.md) → Royal Indian Navy mutiny · **Canonical owner:** [Post-War Upsurge, INA, RIN Mutiny and Cabinet Mission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/26_Post-War-Upsurge-INA-RIN-Mutiny-Cabinet-Mission.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain ways  
**Primary classification:** Modern History → National movement → Royal Indian Navy mutiny  
**Secondary classification:** Modern History → British withdrawal and post-war crisis  
**Proposed topic slug:** `naval-mutiny-last-nail-british-colonial-aspirations`  
**Demand:** Explain how the 1946 revolt affected the coercive basis and political calculations of empire, while avoiding monocausal claims about independence.

**Compact framework:** Scale → cross-community solidarity → civilian spread → coercive reliability → British calculation → qualification.

### Model answer

The Royal Indian Navy revolt of February 1946 was a “last nail” because it exposed uncertainty within the coercive apparatus sustaining British rule.

- Beginning at **HMIS Talwar** over racial abuse, it spread to about 20,000 ratings, ships and shore establishments.
- Ratings raised nationalist demands and displayed Congress, League and red flags together, challenging communal polarization.
- Bombay strikes joined military disaffection with popular unrest.
- Following the **INA trials**, Air Force strikes and wartime politicization, it suggested that Indian servicemen could no longer reliably suppress nationalism.
- War-exhausted Britain could retain India only through an expensive European garrison.

However, the brief revolt lacked unified leadership and ended after leaders urged surrender. Mass movements, negotiations, British weakness and decolonization also mattered.

**Conclusion:** The mutiny did not independently secure freedom, but it undermined the coercive credibility and affordability of continued empire, accelerating withdrawal.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the mutiny’s causal mechanism and significance while rejecting the metaphor as a complete explanation.

## Q8. What were the major political, economic and social developments in the world which motivated the anti-colonial struggle in India?
**Primary route:** Modern History → [National Movement: Organisations and Leaders](../../topics/modern-history/national-movement-organisations-and-leaders.md) → Global influences on Indian nationalism · **Canonical owners:** [Foundation of INC and Moderate Phase](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/14_Foundation-of-INC-and-Moderate-Phase.md) · [Decolonization of Africa and Asia](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/World-History/basic/18_Decolonization-of-Africa-and-Asia.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Difficult · Directive: Identify and explain  
**Primary classification:** Modern History → Indian nationalism → Global influences  
**Secondary classification:** World History → Revolutions, wars and anti-imperialism  
**Proposed topic slug:** `global-developments-motivating-indian-anti-colonial-struggle`  
**Demand:** Organize major global developments into political, economic and social-intellectual categories and show how each influenced Indian anti-colonial action.

**Compact framework:** Political models → economic critiques → social-intellectual currents → Indian transmission → qualification.

### Model answer

Indian nationalism developed through global circulation of ideas and examples.

**Political:** The American and French Revolutions popularized sovereignty and rights. Italian and German unification demonstrated consolidation. Japan’s defeat of Russia in **1905** challenged European invincibility. The Russian Revolution, self-determination and Irish, Turkish and Chinese struggles widened possibilities.

**Economic:** Imperial trade exposed colonial subordination; socialism sharpened critiques of exploitation. The Great Depression discredited imperial laissez-faire and encouraged labour and agrarian organization.

**Social-intellectual:** Liberalism, socialism, Marxism and international labour movements supplied languages of citizenship, class and equality. Pan-Asian, Pan-Islamic and diasporic networks circulated activists and propaganda; the **Ghadar movement** exemplified this transnational channel.

These influences were adapted. India’s drain, racial discrimination and land-revenue burdens supplied immediate grievances.

**Conclusion:** World developments furnished precedents, ideologies and confidence; Indian conditions converted them into a distinctive mass anti-colonial struggle.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers all three categories and explicitly links world developments to Indian mobilization.

## Q9. What were the events that led to the Suez Crisis in 1956? How did it deal a final blow to Britain’s self-image as a world power?
**Primary route:** World History → [Decolonization of Africa and Asia](../../topics/world-history/decolonization-of-africa-and-asia.md) → Suez Crisis · **Canonical owners:** [Decolonization of Africa and Asia](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/World-History/basic/18_Decolonization-of-Africa-and-Asia.md) · [Cold War and International Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/World-History/basic/15_Cold-War-and-International-Relations.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q9 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain events and assess significance  
**Primary classification:** World History → Decolonization → Suez Crisis  
**Secondary classification:** World History → Decline of European great powers  
**Proposed topic slug:** `suez-crisis-events-final-blow-british-world-power-image`  
**Demand:** Reconstruct the immediate sequence to war and explain why forced withdrawal revealed Britain’s post-war dependence and diminished status.

**Compact framework:** Background → nationalization → collusion and invasion → superpower pressure → British consequences.

### Model answer

The crisis arose from Egyptian nationalism colliding with Britain’s residual imperial interests.

- Britain agreed in **1954** to leave the Canal Zone, while **Gamal Abdel Nasser** championed Arab nationalism.
- After the USA and Britain withdrew Aswan High Dam finance, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company on **26 July 1956**.
- Under the secret **Sèvres Protocol**, Israel invaded Sinai; Britain and France issued an ultimatum and attacked to secure the canal.
- Egypt blocked the canal. The USSR threatened intervention; the United States applied UN and financial pressure, straining sterling.

Eden accepted withdrawal without removing Nasser. Britain displayed military capacity but no strategic autonomy against Washington. Nasser’s prestige rose and Britain increasingly aligned with the USA.

**Conclusion:** Suez exposed the gap between imperial self-image and financial-diplomatic dependence, confirming a world dominated by the American and Soviet superpowers.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the causal sequence and explains precisely how withdrawal damaged Britain’s status.

## Q10. The New Economic Policy – 1921 of Lenin had influenced the policies adopted by India soon after independence. Evaluate.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Colonial Legacy and Foundations of the Republic](../../topics/modern-history/colonial-legacy-and-foundations-of-the-republic.md) → Post-independence mixed economy · **Canonical owners:** [Colonial Legacy and Foundations of the Republic](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/29_Colonial-Legacy-and-Foundations-of-the-Republic.md) · [Economy, Land, Society and State: A Post-Independence Synthesis](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/38_Economy-Land-Society-and-State-A-Post-Independence-Synthesis.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Difficult · Directive: Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Post-Independence History → Economic policy → Mixed economy and planning  
**Secondary classification:** World History → Soviet New Economic Policy  
**Proposed topic slug:** `lenin-new-economic-policy-influence-post-independence-india`  
**Demand:** Compare NEP and early Indian policy, identify plausible influence, and distinguish it from Soviet planning and India’s independent intellectual-institutional sources.

**Compact framework:** NEP essentials → Indian parallels → important differences → alternative influences → verdict.

### Model answer

Lenin’s **New Economic Policy (1921)** replaced War Communism with a tactical mixture: the state retained the “commanding heights,” while markets, peasant trade and small private enterprise revived production.

Early India showed parallels. The **Industrial Policy Resolution, 1948** accepted a mixed economy; strategic infrastructure and heavy industry entered the public sector while private firms continued. Planning allocated capital without abolishing markets or property. Agrarian policy rejected collectivization.

NEP demonstrated that public control could coexist with private activity. However, India did not treat this as a temporary socialist retreat. Democracy, federalism and constitutional rights differed fundamentally. The **Bombay Plan**, Gandhian ideas, wartime controls, Keynesian economics and Soviet planning also influenced policy. The Mahalanobis-led Second Plan resembled Soviet planning more than NEP.

**Conclusion:** NEP was a useful mixed-economy precedent, but India selectively adapted several traditions to democratic developmental needs.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates influence through comparison and avoids confusing NEP with the later Soviet command economy.

## Q11. How does patriarchy impact the position of a middle class working woman in India?
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Women, Gender and Social Distress](../../topics/indian-society/women-gender-and-social-distress.md) → Working women and patriarchy · **Canonical owner:** [Women and Women’s Organisations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Society/basic/07_Women-and-Womens-Organisations.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q11 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain impacts  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Women → Patriarchy and paid work  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Society → Family, class and labour  
**Proposed topic slug:** `patriarchy-impact-middle-class-working-woman-india`  
**Demand:** Show how patriarchy operates simultaneously in household, workplace and public space, while recognizing agency and class-specific buffers.

**Compact framework:** Household → workplace → mobility and respectability → intersection and agency → verdict.

### Model answer

Paid employment gives a middle-class woman income and public identity, but patriarchy often determines their use.

- At home, she commonly bears the “double burden” of paid work plus cooking, childcare and eldercare, while male employment is treated as primary.
- Family authority may shape occupation, location, hours and control over earnings. Marriage and motherhood interrupt careers through a “motherhood penalty.”
- At work, recruitment bias, glass ceilings, harassment and mobility assumptions restrict advancement.
- Safety concerns and reputational surveillance constrain commuting, travel and evening work.
- Domestic help reduces some drudgery but can transfer it to poorer women rather than redistribute care between genders.

Education, income, supportive spouses and childcare increase bargaining power; class privilege moderates patriarchy. Yet caste and religion differentiate experience.

**Conclusion:** The working woman’s position is negotiated—economically empowered, but still constrained by gendered care, authority, workplace organization and respectability.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains mechanisms across private and public spheres and qualifies a uniform victimhood narrative.

## Q12. Why do some of the most prosperous regions of India have an adverse sex ratio for women? Give your arguments.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Population and Associated Issues](../../topics/indian-society/population-and-associated-issues.md) → Adverse sex ratio · **Canonical owners:** [Population and Associated Issues](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Society/basic/06_Population-and-Associated-Issues.md) · [Women and Women’s Organisations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Society/basic/07_Women-and-Womens-Organisations.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q25 · Official Q12 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Explain and argue  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Population → Sex ratio and son preference  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Society → Gender inequality and development paradox  
**Proposed topic slug:** `prosperous-regions-adverse-sex-ratio-women`  
**Demand:** Explain why income growth may coexist with or facilitate son preference, using regional evidence and distinguishing sex ratio from gender justice generally.

**Compact framework:** Prosperity paradox → family system → technology and fertility → economic valuation → migration/measurement → qualification.

### Model answer

Prosperity can lower mortality without ending son preference; it may increase families’ capacity to act upon it.

- In patrilineal Punjab and Haryana, sons inherit property, continue lineage and provide old-age support; daughters leave.
- Dowry and status competition can make daughters appear costly despite rising income.
- Ultrasound access enabled prenatal sex selection despite the **PCPNDT Act**.
- Fertility decline creates a “small-family, at-least-one-son” pressure: fewer desired births can intensify selection.
- Male land ownership reinforces inheritance bias; education alone may not change bargaining power.
- Male labour in-migration can depress an overall ratio locally, but cannot explain a low child sex ratio.

Kerala shows prosperity need not cause this; female education, health and reform matter.

**Conclusion:** Adverse ratios reflect patriarchy and technology-mediated choices, not wealth itself. Development must enlarge daughters’ security and agency.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the paradox causally, uses regional comparison and separates demographic effects from structural discrimination.

## Q13. The life cycle of a joint family depends on economic factors rather than social values. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Family, Marriage, Kinship and Socialisation](../../topics/indian-society/family-marriage-kinship-and-socialisation.md) → Joint-family life cycle · **Canonical owner:** [Family, Marriage and Kinship](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Indian-Society/basic/04_Family-Marriage-and-Kinship.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)
