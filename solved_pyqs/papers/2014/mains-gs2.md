## Q1. Starting from inventing the ‘basic structure’ doctrine, the judiciary has played a highly proactive role in ensuring that India develops into a thriving democracy. In light of the statement, evaluate the role played by judicial activism in achieving the ideals of democracy.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial activism and constitutional democracy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Judicial activism  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Basic structure and Fundamental Rights  
**Canonical owners:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Supreme-Court.md) · [Amendment and Basic Structure](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Amendment-and-Basic-Structure.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `judicial-activism-democratic-ideals`

**Demand decoding:** Judge judicial activism by its contribution to constitutional democracy, while distinguishing rights-protecting intervention from judicial overreach.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional restraint → democratic gains → institutional risks → bounded verdict.

### Model answer

Judicial activism uses interpretation and remedies proactively to enforce constitutional values when ordinary institutions fail. Its democratic contribution began with *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), which made Parliament’s amending power subject to the **basic structure**.

It has advanced democracy by:

- protecting liberty through the expansive Article **21** doctrine in *Maneka Gandhi*;
- widening access through public-interest litigation, as in *Hussainara Khatoon* on speedy trial;
- converting Directive Principles into enforceable protections through right-to-food and environmental cases;
- filling legal vacuums, notably the *Vishaka* guidelines on workplace sexual harassment;
- strengthening accountable government through *D.K. Basu*, *Vineet Narain* and electoral-disclosure rulings;
- checking arbitrary allocation of public resources, including the 2G and coal-block cases;
- preserving federalism and secularism through *S.R. Bommai*.

However, unelected judges lack administrative expertise and direct electoral accountability. Continuing mandamus, policy prescriptions and judicial appointments can blur separation of powers; PIL may also become publicity litigation.

**Conclusion:** Activism deepens democracy when it protects rights, representation and legality against institutional default. It weakens democracy when courts substitute their policy preference for constitutionally competent authorities. Reasoned restraint and effective legislative follow-up are therefore essential.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates concrete democratic gains with named cases, acknowledges legitimacy and capacity concerns, and reaches a graded verdict.

## Q2. Though the federal principle is dominant in our Constitution and that principle is one of its basic features, but it is equally true that federalism under the Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong Centre, a feature that militates against the concept of strong federalism. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Federal System](../../topics/polity/federal-system.md) → Centralising features · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Strong-Centre federation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Centre–State relations  
**Canonical owner:** [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Federal-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `indian-federalism-strong-centre`

**Demand decoding:** Establish India’s federal character, identify its centralising provisions and decide whether they negate or qualify federalism.

**Compact framework:** Federal elements → Union tilt → rationale and misuse → cooperative balance.

### Model answer

India is federal because the Constitution is supreme, powers are divided by the Seventh Schedule, governments operate directly upon citizens, and an independent judiciary adjudicates disputes. In *S.R. Bommai* (1994), federalism was recognised as part of the basic structure.

Yet the design gives the Union unusual strength:

- Parliament has residuary power under Article **248** and a larger Union List;
- it may legislate on State subjects under Articles **249, 250, 252 and 253**;
- Parliament can alter State boundaries under Article **3**;
- emergency provisions, especially Articles **352 and 356**, centralise authority;
- Governors are Union appointees;
- All India Services and integrated audit, election and judicial systems create administrative unity;
- States depend substantially on centrally collected taxes and grants;
- Rajya Sabha representation is population-based, not equal;
- Union law prevails over repugnant Concurrent List law under Article **254**.

This tilt reflected Partition, integration, uneven capacity and development needs. It can coordinate national markets and crises, but partisan use of Governors, Article 356 or tied finance undermines State autonomy.

**Conclusion:** India is not a weak federation but a holding-together, cooperative federation with central safeguards. Strong federalism requires constitutional restraint, Inter-State Council consultation, impartial Governors and predictable fiscal devolution.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconciles the basic-structure status of federalism with specific centralising provisions and a balanced institutional conclusion.

## Q3. The ‘Powers, Privileges and Immunities of Parliament and its Members’ as envisaged in Article 105 of the Constitution leave room for a large number of un-codified and un-enumerated privileges to continue. Assess the reasons for the absence of legal codification of the ‘parliamentary privileges’. How can this problem be addressed?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Parliamentary privileges · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Assess; Suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Privileges  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights and judicial review  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `parliamentary-privileges-codification`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why Parliament has avoided codification, assess the resulting constitutional uncertainty and propose a workable solution.

**Compact framework:** Purpose → reasons for non-codification → costs → calibrated codification.

### Model answer

Article **105** protects parliamentary speech, voting and such other privileges as Parliament may define by law; until then, historically inherited privileges continue. They exist to secure legislative independence, not personal status.

Codification has been avoided because:

- Houses fear that a fixed list may omit powers needed against unforeseen obstruction;
- broad privilege preserves institutional flexibility and autonomy;
- legislation would expose precise boundaries to ordinary judicial interpretation;
- political consensus is difficult because privilege intersects with press freedom, contempt and members’ conduct;
- conventions inherited from Westminster were considered adaptable;
- each House claims primary authority to judge breach and contempt.

Non-codification, however, creates uncertainty for citizens and journalists, permits inconsistent punishment and generates conflict with Articles **19 and 21**. *Searchlight*, the *Keshav Singh* reference and *Raja Ram Pal* show that privilege is substantial but not beyond constitutional review.

Parliament should enact a privileges law defining core privileges, contempt, defences, limitation and proportionate penalties. It should protect fair and accurate reporting, require notice, hearing, reasoned decisions and appeal or judicial review on constitutional grounds. A joint parliamentary committee could periodically update the code.

**Conclusion:** Functional autonomy needs protection, but predictable law—not undefined power—best reconciles legislative dignity with the rule of law.

**Why this earns marks:** It addresses both causes and remedies while locating privilege within constitutional, rather than absolute, authority.

## Q4. What do you understand by the concept “freedom of speech and expression”? Does it cover hate speech also? Why do the films in India stand on a slightly different plane from other forms of expression? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Fundamental Rights](../../topics/polity/fundamental-rights.md) → Speech, hate speech and film certification · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Explain; Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Freedom of speech  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Media and film regulation  
**Canonical owner:** [Fundamental Rights](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Fundamental-Rights.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `speech-hate-speech-film-certification`

**Demand decoding:** Define Article 19(1)(a), distinguish offensive speech from punishable hate speech, and explain the constitutional basis for prior film certification.

**Compact framework:** Scope of freedom → Article 19(2) limits → hate-speech threshold → film distinction.

### Model answer

Article **19(1)(a)** protects the freedom to express and receive ideas through speech, writing, art, press, silence and electronic media. It sustains individual autonomy, democratic criticism and the public’s right to know.

The freedom is not absolute. Article **19(2)** permits reasonable restrictions for sovereignty and integrity, State security, friendly relations, public order, decency, morality, contempt, defamation and incitement to an offence.

“Hate speech” is not protected merely because it is expression. Speech promoting enmity, deliberate religious insult or public mischief may be punished under IPC sections **153A, 295A and 505**, but only within Article 19(2). Mere unpopular, shocking or offensive opinion should not be equated with incitement; restriction requires a proximate connection with the specified harm.

Films stand differently because their audio-visual impact, mass reach and capacity for simultaneous emotional appeal are considered stronger than print. The **Cinematograph Act, 1952** therefore provides prior certification. *K.A. Abbas* upheld pre-censorship with procedural safeguards. Yet *S. Rangarajan* held that expression cannot be suppressed because of threatened demonstrations unless danger is proximate and unavoidable.

**Conclusion:** Films may receive classification and narrowly tailored prior scrutiny, but neither cinema nor hate-speech regulation permits vague moral censorship or a heckler’s veto.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates constitutional protection, statutory offences and the special certification rationale while preserving the proportionality of restrictions.

## Q5. Instances of President’s delay in commuting death sentences has come under public debate as denial of justice. Should there be a time limit specified for the President to accept/reject such petitions? Analyse.
**Primary route:** Polity → [President and Vice-President](../../topics/polity/president-and-vice-president.md) → Mercy jurisdiction · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Analyse  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Executive clemency → Death-sentence mercy petitions  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Article 21 and judicial review  
**Canonical owner:** [President and Vice-President](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/President-and-Vice-President.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `presidential-mercy-petitions-time-limit`

**Demand decoding:** Balance careful executive clemency with the Article 21 injury caused by unexplained delay, and decide whether a rigid deadline is desirable.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional power → case for deadline → difficulties → enforceable procedure.

### Model answer

Article **72** empowers the President, acting on ministerial advice, to pardon or commute sentences, including death sentences. Clemency is a constitutional safeguard for circumstances beyond the judicial record, not another appeal.

Delay is grave because prolonged uncertainty and solitary confinement inflict severe psychological suffering upon the prisoner and family. It also makes punishment arbitrary and erodes finality. In *Triveniben* the Supreme Court permitted judicial scrutiny of post-sentence delay; in *Shatrughan Chauhan* (2014), unexplained, inordinate delay was recognised as a ground for commutation, alongside procedural safeguards.

A reasonable time framework would promote equality, administrative diligence and Article **21** fairness. Yet an inflexible deadline may produce mechanical rejection where records, State reports, mental-health evidence or multiple connected petitions require examination. The Constitution deliberately specifies no period, and silence cannot automatically grant clemency.

The better course is a statutory or executive schedule: prompt forwarding of records, a defined period for ministerial advice, reasons for delay, notice of decision, legal aid and periodic judicially reviewable status. Departure should require recorded exceptional reasons.

**Conclusion:** There should be an enforceable reasonable-time framework rather than automatic disposal on a rigid date. Compassionate scrutiny must remain careful, but executive inaction cannot become additional punishment.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses the controlling constitutional provision and pre-2015 judgments to propose a balanced, rights-compatible procedure.

## Q6. The size of the cabinet should be as big as governmental work justifies and as big as the Prime Minister can manage as a team. How far is the efficacy of a government then inversely related to the size of the cabinet? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Prime Minister and Council of Ministers](../../topics/polity/prime-minister-and-council-of-ministers.md) → Cabinet size and effectiveness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Assess extent; Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Executive → Council of Ministers  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Coordination and accountability  
**Canonical owner:** [Prime Minister and Council of Ministers](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/PM-and-Council-of-Ministers.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cabinet-size-government-efficacy`

**Demand decoding:** Test, rather than assume, an inverse relationship between ministry size and governmental effectiveness.

**Compact framework:** Benefits of compactness → need for adequate representation/workload → constitutional cap → qualified determinants.

### Model answer

A compact ministry can improve efficacy by shortening communication, clarifying responsibility and enabling the Prime Minister to enforce collective responsibility. Oversized ministries may create overlapping portfolios, coordination costs, patronage posts and fragmented accountability.

This concern led the **91st Constitutional Amendment, 2003** to insert Article **75(1A)**, capping the Union Council of Ministers at 15% of Lok Sabha strength. A similar ceiling, with a minimum of twelve, applies to States under Article 164.

Yet efficacy is not mechanically inverse to size:

- a large and diverse federation requires specialised portfolios;
- coalition management and regional, social and gender representation strengthen legitimacy;
- combining unrelated departments can overload ministers and weaken supervision;
- Ministers of State can distribute work without necessarily impairing Cabinet unity;
- war, economic transformation or welfare expansion may increase executive workload.

The decisive distinction is between the **Cabinet**, the smaller policy core, and the wider Council of Ministers. Cabinet committees, the secretariat and clear transaction-of-business rules can coordinate a larger ministry.

**Conclusion:** Beyond a functional threshold, size can reduce coherence; below it, concentration may impair expertise and representation. Government efficacy depends more on portfolio design, ministerial competence, stable tenure, collective discipline and administrative support than on numbers alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates both managerial efficiency and representative governance while using the applicable constitutional ceiling.

## Q7. Though 100 percent FDI is already allowed in non-news media like a trade publication and general entertainment channel, the Government is mulling over the proposal for increased FDI in news media for quite some time. What difference would an increase in FDI make? Critically evaluate the pros and cons.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Regulatory Governance and Independent Regulators](../../topics/governance/regulatory-governance-and-independent-regulators.md) → News-media foreign investment · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Critically evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Media regulation → FDI in news media  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Foreign investment  
**Canonical owner:** [Regulatory Governance and Independent Regulators](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/11_Regulatory-Governance-and-Independent-Regulators.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `fdi-news-media-pros-cons`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how higher foreign ownership could affect capital, quality, plurality and editorial sovereignty, then specify safeguards.

**Compact framework:** Potential gains → democratic and security risks → regulatory conditions → verdict.

### Model answer

Higher FDI in news media could bring patient capital, digital technology, global reporting practices and managerial competition to an industry facing high distribution and modernisation costs.

**Potential gains**

- investment in digital newsrooms, regional coverage and training;
- improved production quality and access to international networks;
- new entrants challenging domestic concentration;
- formal employment and transparent corporate governance;
- reduced dependence on politically connected domestic finance.

**Risks**

- foreign owners may influence editorial priorities on security, diplomacy or economic policy;
- profit pressures can produce sensationalism, paid content and closure of unprofitable public-interest reporting;
- global capital may acquire distressed outlets and increase concentration rather than plurality;
- beneficial ownership may be obscured through layered companies;
- smaller Indian-language media could be crowded out;
- news is a democratic public good, unlike ordinary entertainment content.

The relevant issue is therefore not foreign origin alone but control. Any increase should retain Indian editorial control, disclose ultimate ownership and funding, cap cross-media concentration, protect journalist independence, enforce competition law and subject security-sensitive investment to transparent scrutiny.

**Conclusion:** Carefully regulated FDI can improve capacity and plurality, but unrestricted ownership could compromise agenda-setting autonomy. Capital liberalisation must be subordinate to editorial freedom, diversity and national democratic accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It critically links ownership to both industry capability and democratic risks instead of treating FDI as purely financial.

## Q8. The setting up of a Rail Tariff Authority to regulate fares will subject the cash strapped Indian Railways to demand subsidy for obligation to operate non-profitable routes and services. Taking into account the experience in the power sector, discuss if the proposed reform is expected to benefit the consumers, the Indian Railways or the private container operators.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Regulatory Governance and Independent Regulators](../../topics/governance/regulatory-governance-and-independent-regulators.md) → Rail tariff regulation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Discuss; Assess distributional effects  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Regulators → Rail Tariff Authority  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Public-service obligations and cross-subsidy  
**Canonical owner:** [Regulatory Governance and Independent Regulators](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/11_Regulatory-Governance-and-Independent-Regulators.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rail-tariff-authority-stakeholder-impact`

**Demand decoding:** Use the power-sector analogy to assess whether transparent, cost-reflective tariffs help each named stakeholder and under what conditions.

**Compact framework:** Existing distortion → stakeholder benefits → power-sector warning → design safeguards.

### Model answer

Indian Railways historically kept passenger fares low by overcharging freight, weakening finances and shifting cargo to roads. The **Rail Tariff Authority created in 2014** was intended to recommend rational fares and freight rates, though its advisory status limited independence.

**Consumers:** Transparent costing can reduce arbitrary hikes, improve service and protect targeted passengers through explicit subsidies. However, cost-reflective fares may initially burden poor and suburban users unless public-service obligations are directly funded.

**Indian Railways:** Independent recommendations can depoliticise pricing, reveal the cost of non-remunerative routes and justify budgetary compensation. Reduced freight cross-subsidy may recover traffic and finance maintenance. Gains disappear if governments ignore recommendations or promise services without paying.

**Private container operators:** Rational, predictable haulage charges and non-discriminatory access can improve investment certainty. Conversely, an incumbent-controlled information base or selective concessions may preserve an uneven field.

Power-sector regulators show both possibilities: transparent tariffs and subsidy accounting improved discipline, but political tariff freezes, regulatory capture and unpaid State subsidies kept utilities distressed.

**Conclusion:** The reform can benefit all three groups only if the authority is statutory, expert and transparent; subsidies are targeted and paid from budgets; service standards accompany fare revision; and track access treats public and private operators fairly.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the stakeholder-specific demand and derives conditional lessons, rather than superficial analogy, from electricity regulation.

## Q9. National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) in India can be most effective when its tasks are adequately supported by other mechanisms that ensure the accountability of a government. In light of the above observation assess the role of NHRC as an effective complement to the judiciary and other institutions in promoting and protecting human rights standards.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Constitutional Bodies](../../topics/polity/constitutional-bodies.md) → NHRC and human-rights accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q9 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Assess  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Statutory bodies → NHRC  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Government accountability  
**Canonical owner:** [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `nhrc-complement-judiciary-accountability`

**Demand decoding:** Assess NHRC’s distinctive value, statutory limitations and dependence on courts, legislatures, commissions and executive compliance.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → complementary functions → limits → accountability ecosystem.

### Model answer

The NHRC, established under the **Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993**, complements courts by providing a specialised, comparatively accessible forum for investigating violations by public servants.

It can act on complaints or suo motu, seek reports, visit jails, intervene with court permission, review safeguards, promote research and recommend compensation, prosecution or reform. Its inquiries into custodial deaths, encounter allegations, bonded labour and displacement can create records and relief without the cost of ordinary litigation. Courts may use its expertise, while its advisories help governments prevent recurrence.

Its effectiveness is limited because recommendations are not binding; it ordinarily cannot inquire after one year; its armed-forces jurisdiction is restricted to seeking Central reports; vacancies and dependence on government investigators affect capacity; and State Commissions are uneven. It cannot replace writ jurisdiction, criminal prosecution or legislative oversight.

Effectiveness therefore requires complementary mechanisms: prompt government action-taken reports, parliamentary committee scrutiny, independent police investigation, functioning State Commissions, legal-services authorities, vigilant media and civil society, and judicial enforcement where authorities disregard rights.

**Conclusion:** NHRC’s strength is preventive scrutiny, expertise and moral-public authority. It becomes an effective rights guarantor only within an accountability chain in which executive agencies comply, legislatures supervise and courts supply enforceable remedies.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately distinguishes recommendatory commission functions from judicial power and directly answers the “effective complement” proposition.

## Q10. The penetration of Self Help Groups (SHGs) in rural areas in promoting participation in development programmes is facing socio-cultural hurdles. Examine.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → Rural SHG participation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q10 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Governance → SHGs → Socio-cultural barriers  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Women’s participation  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil-Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rural-shgs-socio-cultural-hurdles`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how social relations—not merely finance or administration—restrict SHG formation, voice and development participation.

**Compact framework:** Participation mechanism → social barriers → consequences → enabling response.

### Model answer

SHGs convert small savings and regular meetings into credit access, collective voice and participation in programmes such as the National Rural Livelihoods Mission. Their spread, however, is filtered through rural power relations.

Major socio-cultural hurdles are:

- patriarchal control over women’s mobility, time, income and bank interaction;
- unpaid care work and seasonal labour reducing meeting attendance;
- low literacy, numeracy and confidence in official procedures;
- caste segregation and resistance to mixed-group leadership;
- exclusion of landless persons, migrants, widows, minorities and the poorest who cannot save regularly;
- elite capture by dominant families or local intermediaries;
- purdah, early marriage and fear of social sanction;
- distrust of formal banks and previous experiences of debt;
- male appropriation of loans taken in women’s names;
- language and tribal-cultural distance from standard programme design.

These barriers can make membership nominal and allow better-off women to monopolise credit or contracts.

Remedies include participatory identification, flexible savings, doorstep banking, literacy and leadership training, childcare-compatible meetings, federations across caste groups, women facilitators, social audits and convergence with Panchayats. Banks and officials should measure women’s control over decisions, not merely loan recovery.

**Conclusion:** SHGs are not automatically transformative; they succeed when programmes alter the social conditions governing voice, mobility and resource control.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces specific cultural barriers to participation outcomes and proposes corresponding institutional correctives.

## Q11. Do government’s schemes for up-lifting vulnerable and backward communities by protecting required social resources for them, lead to their exclusion in establishing businesses in urban economies?
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Welfare State](../../topics/social-justice/welfare-state.md) → Protection, mobility and urban enterprise · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: High · Directive: Assess  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Vulnerable communities → Economic inclusion  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Urban entrepreneurship and informality  
**Canonical owner:** [Social Justice, Inclusion and Welfare State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/01_Social-Justice-Concept-Inclusion-and-Welfare-State-Framework.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `protective-schemes-urban-business-exclusion`

**Demand decoding:** Test whether place- or identity-based protection itself excludes vulnerable groups from urban enterprise, separating design effects from structural discrimination.

**Compact framework:** Purpose of protection → possible lock-in effects → larger urban barriers → integrated remedy.

### Model answer

Protective schemes do not inherently exclude vulnerable communities from urban business. Articles **15(4), 16(4) and 46**, land safeguards and targeted finance seek to correct unequal command over education, land, credit and public employment.

However, poorly integrated protection can produce unintended lock-in:

- benefits tied to village residence, land or local lists may become difficult to access after migration;
- tribal land-transfer restrictions protect against dispossession but cannot themselves provide movable capital;
- welfare databases and certificates may not be portable across States or municipalities;
- excessive focus on subsistence assets can neglect skills, networks and urban market access;
- beneficiaries may fear losing entitlements upon formal registration or higher recorded income.

The deeper causes of exclusion are discrimination, lack of collateral, insecure housing, weak education, informality, costly licences and absent business networks. Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe, minority and women entrepreneurs may therefore remain outside supply chains despite protective programmes.

Policy should make entitlements portable, combine protection with institutional credit, procurement access, incubation and skills, and implement the **Street Vendors Act, 2014** fairly. Development-finance corporations require wider outreach and simpler procedures.

**Conclusion:** Social-resource protection is a necessary floor, not a ceiling. Exclusion results when protection is detached from mobility, capability and non-discriminatory urban institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a nuanced answer to the causal claim and links constitutional protection to concrete urban-enterprise constraints.

## Q12. An athlete participates in Olympics for personal triumph and nation’s glory; victors are showered with cash incentives by various agencies, on their return. Discuss the merit of state sponsored talent hunt and its cultivation as against the rationale of a reward mechanism as encouragement.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Sports Governance, Institutions and Major Tournaments](../../topics/governance/sports-governance-institutions-and-major-tournaments.md) → Talent development and rewards · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Discuss; Compare  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Sports policy → Talent identification  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Equal opportunity in sport  
**Canonical owner:** [Sports Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/16_Sports-Governance-Institutions-and-Major-Tournaments.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `sports-talent-cultivation-versus-rewards`

**Demand decoding:** Compare upstream public investment with post-victory cash awards and explain the proper role of each.

**Compact framework:** Limits of rewards → merits of talent pipeline → design risks → complementary model.

### Model answer

Post-victory rewards recognise excellence, provide financial security and create role models. They may encourage athletes who bear high training costs and uncertain careers. Yet rewards occur after success and cannot substitute for the system that produces it.

State-sponsored talent identification has stronger developmental merit:

- it searches schools, rural and tribal areas beyond affluent sporting networks;
- scientific assessment can match aptitude to disciplines;
- sustained coaching, nutrition, equipment, competition and sports medicine convert potential into performance;
- scholarships and hostels reduce dropout caused by poverty or gender norms;
- long-term support creates a broad medal pipeline rather than dependence on exceptional individuals.

Programmes such as **PYKKA/Rajiv Gandhi Khel Abhiyan**, Sports Authority of India centres, the National Sports Development Fund and the 2014 **Target Olympic Podium Scheme** reflected parts of this approach.

Talent schemes can nevertheless suffer opaque selection, age fraud, political patronage, unsafe hostels and excessive pressure on children. Rewards too become inequitable when governments announce arbitrary sums only for visible sports.

## Q14. Has the Cadre based Civil Services Organization been the cause of slow change in India? Critically examine.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Civil Services](../../topics/governance/civil-services.md) → Cadre system and administrative reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2014 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 12½ marks · 200 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Directive: Critically examine  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil services → Cadre organisation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → All India Services  
**Canonical owner:** [Civil Services](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/09_Civil-Services-and-Mission-Karmayogi.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cadre-civil-services-slow-change`

**Demand decoding:** Examine whether cadre structure itself causes inertia, separate its strengths from personnel-management failures, and identify reforms.

**Compact framework:** Cadre rationale → sources of rigidity → countervailing benefits → reforms.

### Model answer

Cadre-based services organise officers by service and State allocation, providing a permanent, politically neutral administrative spine. They have supported national integration, district administration and continuity across elected governments.

They can nevertheless slow change when:

- generalist careers reward seniority over specialised competence;
- rigid service silos obstruct interdepartmental teams;
- frequent transfers prevent institutional memory and accountability;
- cadre control and deputation rules mismatch skills with posts;
- assured progression weakens performance incentives;
- resistance to lateral expertise and technology protects organisational turf;
- the same elite service dominates policy formulation and implementation.

Yet the cadre is not the sole cause. Political interference, unstable priorities, procedural laws, weak local capacity and risk-averse vigilance systems also produce delay. Career officials have successfully implemented elections, disaster relief and major welfare programmes when mandates and authority are clear.

**Conclusion:** Cadres provide valuable neutrality and federal linkage, but closed, generalist and transfer-driven personnel management creates inertia. Fixed tenure, domain postings, mid-career training, transparent appraisal, lateral entry and inter-service mobility can retain continuity while accelerating adaptation.

**Why this earns marks:** It critically separates structural value from dysfunctional cadre management and anchors reform in contemporary institutional recommendations.

### Model answer

As of 2014, Aadhaar was a voluntary UIDAI identity project providing a biometric-based number to residents, while the National Population Register was compiled under the **Citizenship Act, 1955** and 2003 Rules as a compulsory register of usual residents linked to citizenship administration.

**Demand decoding:** State the WTO’s functions and legal effect, then evaluate India’s 2014 public-stockholding strategy, including both justification and negotiation costs.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → binding character → India’s objection → critical assessment and settlement.

### Model answer

The WTO administers trade agreements, provides a negotiating forum, settles disputes, reviews trade policies and offers technical assistance. Its rules cover goods, services and intellectual property.

Accepted agreements are binding. Decisions are ordinarily by consensus; dispute rulings, once adopted by the Dispute Settlement Body, require compliance, compensation or authorised retaliation. The WTO cannot rewrite domestic law, but non-compliance carries international consequences.

At Bali in 2013, members adopted a “peace clause” protecting developing-country public stockholding programmes from certain challenges pending a permanent solution. India argued that subsidy calculation uses outdated **1986–88 reference prices**, inflating measured support, and that procurement and the Public Distribution System are essential for the **National Food Security Act, 2013**.

India’s insistence on a durable safeguard was justified by food security, farmer livelihoods and policy space. However, withholding consent to the Trade Facilitation Agreement protocol in July 2014 attracted criticism because it linked two negotiations and risked India’s coalition credibility.

The November 2014 India–US understanding clarified that protection would continue until a permanent solution, enabling movement on trade facilitation.

**Conclusion:** India rightly defended legitimate food programmes, but should pair firmness with transparent stocks, anti-leakage reform and coalition-based negotiation to distinguish food security from trade-distorting surplus disposal.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers mandate, enforceability and the complete 2013–14 negotiation sequence with a balanced judgment of India’s strategy.
