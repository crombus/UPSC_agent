## Q1. Services-led structural transformation
**Primary route:** Economy → [Services, Digital Economy, Fintech and Platform Markets](../../topics/economy/services-digital-economy-fintech-and-platform-markets.md) → Services-led growth · **Secondary route:** [Industrial Policy, 1991 Reforms, PSUs and Disinvestment](../../topics/economy/industrial-policy-1991-reforms-psus-and-disinvestment.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 1  
**Official question:** Normally countries shift from agriculture to industry and then later to services, but India shifted directly from agriculture to services. What are the reasons for the huge growth of services vis-à-vis industry in the country? Can India become a developed country without a strong industrial base?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Structural Transformation → Services versus Manufacturing  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/services-digital-economy-fintech-and-platform-markets`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Explain causes + Assess proposition  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/24_Services-Digital-Economy-Fintech-and-Platform-Markets.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/24_Services-Digital-Economy-Fintech-and-Platform-Markets.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain India’s atypical sectoral transition, then judge whether services can substitute for manufacturing in development, employment and technological capability.

### Compact framework
`English-speaking skills + telecom/reforms + global outsourcing → services surge; mass employment + tradables + learning still require industry`

### Model answer
India’s services surge arose from a distinctive conjunction rather than a completed structural transformation.

- Post-1991 deregulation, telecommunications reform and external opening expanded finance, trade, transport and business services.
- A large English-speaking pool of engineers and graduates, time-zone advantage and low communication costs enabled software and business-process exports.
- Public investment in higher technical education supported skill-intensive services, while domestic demand rose with urbanisation and a growing middle class.
- Industry remained constrained by unreliable power, logistics, land acquisition, small firm scale, restrictive regulation and inadequate vocational skills.
- Unlike factories, information technology could enter global markets without first overcoming India’s physical-infrastructure deficit.

However, services cannot wholly replace a strong industrial base. Modern services are productive and exportable, but many employ educated workers and cannot absorb the large, less-skilled workforce leaving agriculture. Manufacturing creates dense supplier networks, learning-by-doing, machinery capability, mass formal employment and exports; it also supports transport, design, finance and after-sales services.

India can develop through a larger services share than earlier industrialisers, but not through services alone. A balanced path requires labour-intensive manufacturing, competitive infrastructure and MSME upgrading alongside high-value services. Development depends on productive employment and technological depth, not merely sectoral GDP shares.

### Examiner rationale
The answer explains both the services pull and industrial constraints, rejects a mechanical Western sequence, and gives a qualified verdict centred on jobs, capabilities and linkages.

## Q2. Demographic dividend and employability
**Primary route:** Economy → [Employment, Labour Codes, Skills and Demographic Dividend](../../topics/economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend.md) → Employability and job creation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 2  
**Official question:** “While we flaunt India’s demographic dividend, we ignore the dropping rates of employability.” What are we missing while doing so? Where will the jobs that India desperately needs come from? Explain.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Employment → Skills and Demographic Dividend  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/employment-labour-codes-skills-and-demographic-dividend`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate-High · Diagnose + Explain employment sources  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/22_Employment-Labour-Codes-Skills-and-Demographic-Dividend.md)

### Demand decoding
Identify why a youthful population is not automatically an economic dividend and specify realistic sectors and enabling reforms for employment.

### Compact framework
`youth bulge − health/education/skills/jobs = demographic burden; jobs require labour-intensive structural transformation`

### Model answer
A demographic dividend is conditional: a large working-age population raises growth only when workers are healthy, educated, skilled and productively employed. India’s celebration often misses four deficits.

First, learning outcomes and vocational exposure remain weak; degrees do not ensure communication, numeracy or workplace competence. Second, training is poorly matched with local industry and apprenticeships. Third, low female labour-force participation wastes talent. Fourth, growth has been concentrated in capital- or skill-intensive activities, while most workers remain informal and agriculture retains disguised labour.

Jobs must come from a broad employment ecosystem:

- labour-intensive manufacturing—textiles, garments, leather, food processing, electronics assembly and light engineering;
- construction and infrastructure, including housing, roads, railways and sanitation;
- modernised agriculture and allied dairy, fisheries, storage, logistics and agro-processing;
- MSMEs and clusters supplied with credit, power, technology and market access;
- care, tourism, retail, transport and other labour-absorbing services; and
- entrepreneurship, without treating self-employment as a substitute for decent wage work.

The National Skill Development Corporation offered a base, but training requires employer-designed standards, apprenticeships and certification. India must combine industrial policy, easier formalisation, portable social security and women-friendly workplaces. Otherwise, the youth bulge may magnify unemployment and social stress.

### Examiner rationale
The response converts “employability” into measurable capability deficits and identifies sector-specific job sources instead of offering training as a stand-alone cure.

## Q3. APMCs, agricultural development and food inflation
**Primary route:** Economy → [APMC, e-NAM, FPOs and Agricultural Supply Chains](../../topics/economy/apmc-e-nam-fpos-and-agricultural-supply-chains.md) → Regulated markets and price transmission · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 3  
**Official question:** There is also a point of view that Agricultural Produce Market Committees (APMCs) set up under the State Acts have not only impeded the development of agriculture but also have been the cause of food inflation in India. Critically examine.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → APMC Reform and Food Inflation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/apmc-e-nam-fpos-and-agricultural-supply-chains`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Critically examine causation  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/13_APMC-e-NAM-FPOs-and-Agricultural-Supply-Chains.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/13_APMC-e-NAM-FPOs-and-Agricultural-Supply-Chains.md)

### Demand decoding
Assess both the original rationale and actual distortions of APMCs, while distinguishing their contribution to inflation from other supply and macroeconomic causes.

### Compact framework
`regulated-market protection → cartelised intermediation where competition fails; inflation is multi-causal, not an APMC-only outcome`

### Model answer
APMCs were created to protect cultivators from itinerant traders through notified yards, open auctions, standard weights and dispute settlement. Their performance, however, has often departed from that purpose.

Compulsory sale through licensed mandis, buyer-entry barriers, commissions and State levies fragment markets. Licence scarcity can produce trader cartels; agents may combine credit, auction and payment functions, weakening farmer bargaining. Restrictions on direct purchase and private markets discourage processors, storage and cold-chain investment. These costs widen farm-retail spreads and impede diversification.

APMC design can therefore contribute to food inflation, especially when repeated handling, opaque auctions and weak logistics amplify scarcity. Yet it is not the sole cause. Weather shocks, rising protein demand, minimum-support operations, export-import decisions, fuel costs, hoarding, poor storage and slow supply response also affect prices. Abolishing regulated yards without alternatives could expose small farmers to private monopsony.

Reform should follow the **Model APMC Act, 2003**: unified licences, direct marketing, private markets, transparent auctions, rationalised fees and jurisdictional competition. Producer organisations, assaying, warehouse receipts, cold chains and enforceable payment are equally necessary.

Thus, unreformed APMCs impede price discovery, but food inflation reflects the entire production-to-retail chain. The objective is regulated pluralism—not replacing a public cartel with a private one.

### Examiner rationale
The answer treats APMCs as one causal link, preserves their protective rationale, and offers period-appropriate reforms rather than attributing inflation monocausally.

## Q4. Cooperative credit and technology-enabled rural finance
**Primary route:** Economy → [Irrigation, Inputs, Credit, Insurance and Sustainable Agriculture](../../topics/economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture.md) → Agricultural credit · **Secondary route:** [Banking Structure, NBFCs and Financial Regulation](../../topics/economy/banking-structure-nbfcs-and-financial-regulation.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 4  
**Official question:** “In the villages itself no form of credit organization will be suitable except the cooperative society.” — All India Rural Credit Survey. Discuss this statement in the background of agricultural finance in India. What constraints and challenges do financial institutions supplying agricultural finance face? How can technology be used to better reach and serve rural clients?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Agriculture → Rural Credit and Financial Inclusion  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/irrigation-inputs-credit-insurance-and-sustainable-agriculture`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Discuss claim + Diagnose constraints + Suggest technology  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/14_Irrigation-Inputs-Credit-Insurance-and-Sustainable-Agriculture.md)

### Demand decoding
Evaluate the cooperative advantage without romanticising it, identify lender and borrower constraints, and show specific technology applications.

### Compact framework
`local knowledge + member ownership favour cooperatives; governance/cost/risk gaps require plural institutions and digital last-mile delivery`

### Model answer
The **All India Rural Credit Survey (1954)** favoured cooperatives because member ownership, local knowledge and proximity could reduce information costs and dependence on moneylenders. The logic remains valuable, but cooperatives alone proved insufficient. Political interference, poor recovery, weak capital and uneven capacity impaired many societies; commercial banks, regional rural banks and self-help-group linkages are also necessary.

Lenders face dispersed loans, high transaction costs, seasonal cash flows, covariant drought and price risks, defective land records and informal tenancy. Branch distance, inadequate collateral and financial illiteracy exclude borrowers. Loan waivers can weaken repayment culture, while bankers may avoid remote regions.

Technology can improve both reach and risk management:

- Aadhaar-enabled identification and no-frills accounts can reduce duplication and simplify access;
- business correspondents with micro-ATMs can provide doorstep cash-in/cash-out services;
- mobile banking and SMS can deliver balances, reminders and market information;
- digitised land records and Kisan Credit Card histories can support appraisal;
- remote sensing and weather data can improve crop monitoring and insurance; and
- interoperable payment systems can transfer benefits directly.

Technology must not replace assistance: poor connectivity, biometric failure and low literacy require grievance redress and assisted channels. A plural credit system, anchored locally, combines inclusion with prudence.

### Examiner rationale
The response historicises the quotation, evaluates cooperative limitations, and links each technology to a concrete financial-friction problem.

## Q5. Land acquisition, rehabilitation and development
**Primary route:** Economy → [Land Reforms, Green Revolution and Cropping Systems](../../topics/economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems.md) → Land acquisition and agrarian rights · **Secondary route:** [Industrial Policy, 1991 Reforms, PSUs and Disinvestment](../../topics/economy/industrial-policy-1991-reforms-psus-and-disinvestment.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 5  
**Official question:** The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 has come into effect from 1st January, 2014. What are the key issues which would get addressed with the Act in place? What implications would it have on industrialization and agriculture in India?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Land → Acquisition, Compensation and Rehabilitation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/land-reforms-green-revolution-and-cropping-systems`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate-High · Identify provisions + Assess implications  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/11_Land-Reforms-Green-Revolution-and-Cropping-Systems.md)

### Demand decoding
Explain what deficiencies of the 1894 regime the 2013 Act addresses and balance rights gains against effects on projects, land use and farming.

### Compact framework
`fair compensation + consent/SIA + R&R + safeguards → legitimacy; higher time/cost but lower conflict and arbitrary conversion`

### Model answer
The 2013 Act replaces the coercive **Land Acquisition Act, 1894** with a rights-based framework. It provides market-linked compensation—up to four times market value in rural areas and twice in urban areas—plus solatium. Social Impact Assessment, public hearings and disclosure improve transparency. Consent applies to specified private and public-private projects; rehabilitation covers livelihood losers, not only titleholders. Safeguards for Scheduled Areas, food security and return of unutilised land address displacement and speculation.

For industrialisation, these provisions raise acquisition time and project cost, especially where records are unclear. Yet predictable rules, legitimacy and rehabilitation can reduce litigation, protests and stranded projects. Industry is encouraged to use brownfield sites and voluntary purchase efficiently.

For agriculture, the Act restrains indiscriminate conversion of fertile multi-cropped land, recognises dependent labourers and provides resources for rehabilitation. Conversely, high compensation may accelerate sale, fragment agrarian communities or leave recipients without durable livelihoods; rigid protection can also obstruct essential rural infrastructure.

The Act’s success therefore depends on credible land records, time-bound assessments, independent valuation and livelihood-based rehabilitation. Its central trade-off is not development versus rights, but arbitrary acquisition versus socially sustainable development.

### Examiner rationale
The answer names the Act’s operative safeguards and assesses both transaction costs and conflict-reduction benefits without adopting a one-sided pro-industry or pro-agriculture position.

## Q6. Capitalism and inclusive growth
**Primary route:** Economy → [Poverty, Inequality, Social Sector and Inclusive Growth](../../topics/economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth.md) → Inclusive capitalism · **Secondary route:** [Growth, Development, HDI, IHDI and MPI](../../topics/economy/growth-development-hdi-ihdi-and-mpi.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 6  
**Official question:** Capitalism has guided the world economy to unprecedented prosperity. However, it often encourages short-sightedness and contributes to wide disparities between the rich and the poor. In this light, would it be correct to believe and adopt capitalism for bringing inclusive growth in India? Discuss.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Inclusive Growth → Markets, State and Inequality  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/poverty-inequality-social-sector-and-inclusive-growth`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Discuss and reach balanced judgment  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/23_Poverty-Inequality-Social-Sector-and-Inclusive-Growth.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/23_Poverty-Inequality-Social-Sector-and-Inclusive-Growth.md)

### Demand decoding
Distinguish capitalism’s productive strengths from unregulated outcomes and specify an Indian institutional model for inclusion.

### Compact framework
`markets create wealth and innovation; unequal assets/externalities exclude; democratic regulation and capabilities make growth inclusive`

### Model answer
Capitalism’s prices, private initiative and competition can mobilise savings, reward innovation and allocate resources dynamically. India’s post-1991 growth, telecommunications expansion and enterprise demonstrate these gains. Growth also enlarges the fiscal base for social investment.

Yet capitalism does not automatically produce inclusion. Wealth and education confer unequal starting power; firms may externalise pollution, underprovide public goods or suppress labour bargaining. Monopoly and regulatory capture convert competition into rent seeking. Unequal land ownership, caste and gender barriers, informality and regional disparities may reproduce disadvantage.

India should therefore adopt a **socially regulated market economy**, not laissez-faire capitalism:

- competition law and transparent regulation to curb monopoly and crony allocation;
- macroeconomic stability, infrastructure and credit for MSMEs;
- quality public health, education, nutrition and skilling to equalise capabilities;
- labour rights, social insurance and progressive taxation;
- land and financial inclusion, with targeted support for disadvantaged groups; and
- environmental pricing and accountable corporate governance.

Thus capitalism is a powerful engine, not a distributive ethic. Inclusive growth requires markets to create opportunity, the State to supply capabilities and correct failures, and democratic institutions to prevent concentrated economic power from governing policy.

### Examiner rationale
The answer neither rejects markets nor equates growth with inclusion; it supplies an institutional synthesis tailored to India’s structural inequalities.

## Q7. Intergenerational liabilities in PPP infrastructure
**Primary route:** Economy → [Infrastructure, PPPs, Logistics and Public Investment](../../topics/economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment.md) → PPP risk allocation · **Secondary route:** [Union Budget, Fiscal Policy and Deficit Indicators](../../topics/economy/union-budget-fiscal-policy-and-deficit-indicators.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 7  
**Official question:** Explain how Private Public Partnership arrangements, in long gestation infrastructure projects, can transfer unsustainable liabilities to the future. What arrangements need to be put in place to ensure that successive generations’ capacities are not compromised?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Infrastructure → PPPs and Intergenerational Fiscal Risk  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Explain mechanism + Prescribe safeguards  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md)

### Demand decoding
Show how off-budget promises and poor risk allocation create future burdens, then provide fiscal, contractual and regulatory safeguards.

### Compact framework
`hidden guarantees + optimistic demand + renegotiation → deferred public liability; disclose, cap, price and allocate risks`

### Model answer
Long-gestation PPPs can create infrastructure while postponing payment and risk. Governments may promise annuities, minimum revenue, exchange-rate cover or termination compensation. These contingent liabilities remain outside the headline deficit until demand disappoints. Optimistic forecasts, restrained tariffs and aggressive bidding trigger renegotiation, bank stress or public takeover. Future taxpayers pay, while poor projects lock resources into obsolete assets.

Intergenerational protection requires:

- value-for-money and public-sector-comparator tests before choosing PPP over public procurement;
- independent demand, environmental and fiscal-risk appraisal;
- allocation of construction, demand, financing and regulatory risks to the party best able to manage each;
- disclosure and valuation of guarantees, annuities and termination obligations in budget documents;
- statutory ceilings, provisioning and a central contingent-liability register;
- competitive bidding, standard contracts and published concession terms;
- independent regulators for predictable tariffs and service standards;
- time-bound dispute resolution and tightly defined renegotiation;
- performance-linked payments, escrow and periodic audits; and
- lifecycle maintenance and hand-back standards.

Projects should pass affordability and resilience tests. PPP does not eliminate public cost; it rearranges financing and risk. Transparent accounting and disciplined democratic project selection ensure infrastructure expands future productive capacity rather than mortgaging it.

### Examiner rationale
The answer identifies concrete liability channels and matches each with an institutional safeguard, keeping the focus on future fiscal and productive capacity.

## Q8. Moving people under the National Urban Transport Policy
**Primary route:** Economy → [Infrastructure, PPPs, Logistics and Public Investment](../../topics/economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment.md) → Urban transport · **Secondary route:** [Urbanisation, Problems and Remedies](../../topics/indian-society/urbanisation-problems-and-remedies.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 8  
**Official question:** National Urban Transport Policy emphasises on ‘moving people’ instead of ‘moving vehicles’. Discuss critically the success of the various strategies of the Government in this regard.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Economy → Infrastructure → Sustainable Urban Mobility  
**Proposed topic slug:** `economy/infrastructure-ppps-logistics-and-public-investment`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate-High · Critically assess implementation  
**Canonical owner:** [`Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/18_Infrastructure-PPPs-Logistics-and-Public-Investment.md)

### Demand decoding
State the policy’s people-centred logic, evaluate major instruments by 2014, and explain why modal shift remained incomplete.

### Compact framework
`public transport + walking/cycling + integrated land use > road widening; gains exist but car-oriented incentives persist`

### Model answer
The **National Urban Transport Policy, 2006** shifted the objective from vehicle speed to safe, affordable access. It promoted buses and mass transit, walking and cycling, land-use integration, unified planning, traffic management and restraint on private vehicles.

There were gains. Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission assistance financed buses and reforms. Delhi Metro demonstrated high-capacity public transport. Bus rapid transit experiments, pedestrian facilities and city mobility plans introduced multimodal planning.

Success was nevertheless partial:

- metro construction was expensive and often weakly integrated with buses and last-mile access;
- bus fleets, depots and service quality remained inadequate in many cities;
- BRT suffered from poor design, enforcement and public communication;
- footpaths and cycle tracks were discontinuous or encroached;
- fragmented municipal, development and traffic agencies prevented unified decisions;
- cheap or unpriced parking and continued road widening subsidised car use; and
- peripheral, low-density development increased travel distances.

Government should prioritise reliable buses, common ticketing, safe non-motorised networks, transit-oriented development, parking pricing and metropolitan transport authorities. Performance should measure accessibility, safety and modal share—not flyover length.

Thus the policy changed doctrine and created successful projects, but fiscal and institutional practice had not yet displaced vehicle-centred urban planning.

### Examiner rationale
The response distinguishes policy success from system-wide modal shift and uses only strategies and projects established by 2014.

## Q9. Liberalised FDI in defence
**Primary route:** Science and Technology → [Defence Indigenization, Atmanirbhar and Procurement](../../topics/science-and-technology/defence-indigenization-atmanirbhar-and-procurement.md) → FDI and defence manufacturing · **Secondary route:** [Industrial Policy, 1991 Reforms, PSUs and Disinvestment](../../topics/economy/industrial-policy-1991-reforms-psus-and-disinvestment.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 9  
**Official question:** Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) in the defence sector is now set to be liberalized. What influence this is expected to have on Indian defence and economy in the short and long run?  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Science and Technology → Defence Technology → FDI and Indigenisation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `science-and-technology/defence-indigenization-atmanirbhar-and-procurement`  
**Difficulty / directive:** Moderate · Assess short- and long-run effects  
**Canonical owner:** [`Science-and-Technology/basic/07_Defence-Indigenization-Atmanirbhar-and-Procurement.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Science-and-Technology/basic/07_Defence-Indigenization-Atmanirbhar-and-Procurement.md)

### Demand decoding
Evaluate the 2014 liberalisation across military capability, technology, industry, employment and strategic dependence, separated by time horizon.

### Compact framework
`capital + technology + competition may localise production; ownership alone cannot ensure transfer, design capability or autonomy`

### Model answer
In 2014 India raised the normal foreign-investment ceiling in defence from 26 to **49 per cent**, with higher investment possible for state-of-the-art technology. This may bring joint ventures, capital and competition to a public-sector-dominated industry. Local assembly can shorten supply chains, create skilled work and reduce imports. Forces may gain better spares and lifecycle support.

Short-run gains, however, may be modest. Global original-equipment manufacturers may protect core intellectual property, while uncertain orders, slow procurement, offsets and licensing can deter investment. Import of kits for assembly may raise domestic value only superficially.

Long-term, stable procurement and genuine technology partnerships can build vendors, testing, exports and dual-use spillovers into electronics, materials and precision engineering. Competition can discipline public producers.

Risks remain: dependence may shift from finished imports to foreign-controlled components; strategic supply can be disrupted; profits may be repatriated; and weak security screening can expose sensitive technology.

FDI must complement indigenous research. Transparent acquisition, technology-transfer benchmarks, offset enforcement, security screening, MSME support, and DRDO–industry–university collaboration are essential. Success means durable Indian design, manufacturing and upgrade capability, not merely nominal foreign equity inflows.

### Examiner rationale
The answer is anchored in the 2014 policy ceiling, differentiates assembly from capability, and balances economic benefits with strategic autonomy.

## Q10. Declining scientific research in universities
**Primary route:** Science and Technology → [Scientific Discoveries, Nobel Prizes and Scientists](../../topics/science-and-technology/scientific-discoveries-nobel-prizes-and-scientists.md) → Research ecosystem · **Secondary route:** [Education and Human Resource Development](../../topics/social-justice/education-and-human-resource-development.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Identity:** 2014 · Mains · General Studies III · Compulsory Question 10  
**Official question:** Scientific research in Indian universities is declining, because a career in science is not as attractive as are business professions, engineering or administration, and the universities are becoming consumer-oriented. Critically comment.  
**Marks / limit:** 12½ / 200 words  
**Classification:** Science and Technology → Research Ecosystem → Universities and Scientific Careers  
**Proposed topic slug:** `science-and-technology/scientific-discoveries-nobel-prizes-and-scientists`  
**Difficulty / directive:** High · Critically comment on diagnosis  
**Canonical owner:** [`Science-and-Technology/basic/24_S-and-T-Institutions-DST-CSIR-ANRF.md`](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Science-and-Technology/basic/24_S-and-T-Institutions-DST-CSIR-ANRF.md)

### Demand decoding
Assess the stated causes but broaden the explanation to institutions, finance, incentives and the teaching-research divide; propose remedies.

### Compact framework
`career incentives + weak university research conditions + teaching-commercial pressures → talent exit; reform careers, grants and autonomy`

### Model answer
The proposition is substantially valid but incomplete. Higher salaries, clearer career paths and social prestige in engineering, management and administration draw strong students away from research. Scientific careers involve long training, uncertain fellowships, delayed recruitment and limited mobility.

Universities face high teaching loads, obsolete laboratories, fragmented grants, bureaucratic procurement, vacancies and weak mentoring. Separating research institutes from universities deprives students of research cultures. Fee-dependent colleges may encourage “consumer” expectations—credentials and placement rather than inquiry—while publication counts reward quantity.

Decline is not universal. IISc, TIFR, IISERs and leading universities show that talent responds to funding and autonomy. Low industrial research and limited university collaboration are additional constraints.

Reform requires:

- predictable, peer-reviewed competitive grants through agencies such as DST, CSIR and SERB;
- timely fellowships, tenure-track careers and transparent recruitment;
- shared laboratories and professional research administration;
- reduced teaching burden for active researchers;
- undergraduate research and mobility between universities, laboratories and industry;
- stronger doctoral mentoring and integrity standards; and
- evaluation based on quality, replication and social relevance rather than publication numbers.

India needs universities that teach through discovery. Scientific careers become attractive with autonomy, infrastructure, dignity and credible prospects.

### Examiner rationale
The answer critically expands the prompt beyond salaries and commercialisation, uses period-appropriate institutions, and connects reforms to identified causes.

## Q11. Antibiotic misuse and antimicrobial resistance

### Model answer
These rights protect different kinds of knowledge and arise differently.

| Feature | Copyright | Patent | Trade secret |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject | Original expression—literary, artistic, musical works, films and software code | A novel, inventive and industrially applicable product or process | Valuable confidential information such as a formula, method or customer list |

Firms may combine protections—patenting a machine, copyrighting its software and keeping manufacturing know-how secret. Sound policy balances creation incentives against competition, access and cumulative innovation.

### Compact framework
`territorial sovereignty governs airspace; outer space is non-appropriable; undefined vertical boundary complicates dual-use threats`

### Model answer
Airspace is the column of atmosphere above a State’s land territory and territorial waters. Under the **Chicago Convention, 1944**, every State has complete and exclusive sovereignty over it; foreign civil aircraft require permission according to applicable rules, while State aircraft need specific authorisation.

Outer space follows a different regime. The **Outer Space Treaty, 1967** treats exploration as available to all and prohibits national appropriation. - unauthorised aircraft or drones may conduct surveillance, smuggling or attack;
- missiles traverse airspace and near-space at high speed;
- civilian and military objects are hard to distinguish quickly;
- anti-satellite or counter-space activity can impair communications and navigation;
- mistaken identification and escalation; and
- dense civil traffic requires military–civil coordination.

India requires layered radar and satellite surveillance, Airborne Warning and Control Systems, integrated command, interceptors and civil–military sharing. Drone registration, protected-zone enforcement and countermeasures should address low-altitude threats. Space situational awareness and confidence-building are needed above conventional airspace.

Rules of engagement must ensure proportionality around civil aircraft. Since technology outruns the legal boundary, capability and diplomacy must advance together.

The response accurately contrasts the Chicago and Outer Space regimes, identifies the delimitation gap, and ties remedies to distinct altitude and technology threats.
