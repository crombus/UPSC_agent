## Q1(a). If caste discrimination has continuity and hierarchy, which principle of justice can dissolve this problem?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Caste Discrimination, Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/caste-discrimination-gandhi-and-ambedkar.md) → graded inequality · social justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(a) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Identify and justify · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Caste Discrimination — Gandhi and Ambedkar  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Ambedkar · graded inequality · substantive equality · fraternity  
**Proposed slug:** `caste-continuity-hierarchy-principle-justice`  
**Canonical owner:** [Caste, Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Caste-Gandhi-Ambedkar.md)

**Demand decoding:** Name a principle able to attack both inherited continuity and ranked status, and explain its institutional implications.

**Argument map:** hereditary hierarchy → formal equality inadequate → substantive equality plus fraternity → reverse-discrimination objection → transformative justice.

### Model answer

The appropriate principle is **transformative social justice**, combining substantive equality with fraternity. Caste is not merely unequal treatment; Ambedkar calls it graded inequality sustained through endogamy, inherited occupation and denial of social intercourse. Therefore equal rules applied to unequally placed groups reproduce hierarchy.

Substantive equality requires compensatory measures—reservations, anti-discrimination law, equal education and redistribution—to dismantle accumulated disadvantage. Fraternity is equally necessary because legal opportunity alone cannot dissolve notions of purity, pollution and ranked human worth. Ambedkar’s constitutional morality joins liberty, equality and fraternity as mutually sustaining principles.

**Objection:** group-sensitive remedies may perpetuate caste identities and violate individual merit.  
**Reply:** temporary, reviewable remedies recognise caste to remove its effects; “merit” itself reflects unequal access to social goods.

**Assessment:** distributive justice repairs material exclusion, while recognitional justice rejects caste status. Their union in transformative equality can interrupt caste’s continuity and hierarchy without replacing one permanent hierarchy with another.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies one integrated principle and connects it to caste’s precise structure and remedies.

## Q1(b). How does multiculturalism redefine liberal notions like identity, freedom and equality and reformulate its assumptions?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → multiculturalism · liberalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(b) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Multiculturalism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Taylor · Kymlicka · recognition · differentiated citizenship  
**Proposed slug:** `multiculturalism-redefines-liberal-identity-freedom-equality`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Show how multiculturalism revises, rather than simply rejects, liberal individualism.

**Argument map:** abstract individual → culturally situated identity → freedom through cultural options → equality as accommodation → internal-domination objection → rights-compatible pluralism.

### Model answer

Classical liberalism often pictures the person as an abstract chooser and equality as identical treatment. Multiculturalists argue that identity is dialogically formed within languages, histories and communities. Taylor therefore stresses recognition: cultural misrecognition can injure agency. Kymlicka adds that freedom requires a secure “societal culture” supplying meaningful options.

Equality is consequently reformulated as differentiated rather than uniform citizenship. Minority-language rights, representation or exemptions may correct majority advantage. Liberal neutrality is also questioned because public institutions inevitably privilege some language, calendar and norms.

**Objection:** group rights may essentialise cultures and empower patriarchal elites.  
**Reply:** Kymlicka distinguishes legitimate external protections from oppressive internal restrictions; members must retain voice, dissent and exit.

**Assessment:** multiculturalism retains liberal concern for freedom and equality but makes their social conditions visible. India’s linguistic federalism and minority educational rights illustrate accommodation, while constitutional rights limit internal domination. Liberalism becomes context-sensitive, recognitional and group-differentiated without surrendering equal personhood.

**Why this earns marks:** It reformulates all three named notions and supplies a principled liberal limit.

## Q1(c). How do we distinguish Liberal humanism and Marxist humanism?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → humanism · **Secondary route:** [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(c) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Distinguish · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Syllabus unit:** Humanism; Political Ideologies — Liberalism and Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Locke · Mill · Marx · individuality · species-being  
**Proposed slug:** `liberal-humanism-marxist-humanism-distinction`  
**Canonical owner:** [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism.md)

**Demand decoding:** Contrast their conceptions of the person, freedom, alienation, rights and emancipation while noting common ground.

**Argument map:** common human dignity → liberal autonomous individual → Marxian social species-being → rights versus material emancipation → synthesis.

### Model answer

Both humanisms affirm human agency against theological or authoritarian subordination, but they locate emancipation differently. Liberal humanism, represented by Locke, Kant and Mill, treats the person primarily as an autonomous rights-bearer. It protects conscience, expression, property and a private sphere through limited government; freedom is chiefly secure choice and self-development.

Marxist humanism, especially the early Marx, sees persons as social, productive beings whose “species-being” is realised through unalienated cooperative activity. Formal rights are valuable but insufficient where private ownership separates workers from product, process, others and self. Freedom therefore requires transforming exploitative material relations.

**Objection to liberalism:** its abstract individual conceals class dependence. **Objection to Marxism:** collective emancipation may subordinate actual individuals to an alleged historical subject.

**Assessment:** liberalism better secures plural rights; Marxism better exposes socio-economic conditions of agency. A defensible humanism combines inviolable civil liberty with democratic control of domination and material capabilities.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares anthropology, freedom, institutions and characteristic limitations.

## Q1(d). Explain the significance of John Austin’s theory of sovereignty. How does it differ from that of Hobbes?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → Austin · Hobbes · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(d) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Explain and distinguish · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty — Hobbes and Austin  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** command theory · determinate superior · social contract · indivisibility  
**Proposed slug:** `austin-sovereignty-significance-difference-hobbes`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** State Austin’s jurisprudential significance and distinguish its method and foundation from Hobbes’s political theory.

**Argument map:** law as sovereign command → legal positivism → Hobbesian contract and security → common absolutism → different grounds and scope.

### Model answer

Austin defines the sovereign as a determinate human superior habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and not habitually obeying another. Positive law is the sovereign’s general command backed by sanction. Its significance lies in separating law “as it is” from morality and systematising analytical legal positivism: legal validity depends on pedigree, not ethical merit.

Hobbes also makes sovereignty absolute, indivisible and legally final, but reaches it through a political argument. In the state of nature insecurity makes rational persons authorise a sovereign through covenant to secure peace. Hobbes’s sovereign may be an assembly; Austin seeks the empirically identifiable lawgiver behind constitutional forms.

**Objection:** both understate custom, constitutional limitation and divided authority.  
**Reply:** they illuminate legal finality, though not the whole basis of obligation.

**Assessment:** Austin converts Hobbes’s justificatory theory of political authority into a jurisprudential test of positive law. Hobbes asks why sovereignty is needed; Austin asks whose commands count as law.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the shared monism but sharply distinguishes purpose, method and foundation.

## Q1(e). Can we say that racial supremacy is the main reason for genocide? Give reasons for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) → fascism · racism · genocide · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(e) · 10 marks · about 150 words · Directive: Take a position and give reasons · Difficulty: Moderate-high  
**Syllabus unit:** Political Ideologies — Fascism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** racial supremacy · dehumanisation · totalitarian power · genocide  
**Proposed slug:** `racial-supremacy-main-reason-genocide`  
**Canonical owner:** [Political Ideologies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Political-Ideologies.md)

**Demand decoding:** Assess racial supremacy as a powerful but non-exhaustive cause of genocide.

**Argument map:** superior/inferior classification → dehumanisation → elimination → non-racial genocides objection → ideology plus institutions and crisis.

### Model answer

Racial supremacy can be a major cause because it naturalises hierarchy and converts a population into an allegedly contaminating “other.” Nazi antisemitism joined pseudo-biological superiority to dehumanisation, making exclusion, dispossession and extermination appear purificatory. Such ideology weakens ordinary moral restraints and supplies perpetrators with a collective mission.

It is not, however, the universal or sufficient cause of genocide. Groups may be targeted by ethnicity, religion, nationality or politics; mass killing also requires state capacity, propaganda, obedience, war or crisis, and organised impunity. Colonial ambition and material seizure may exploit racial doctrine rather than merely follow it.

**Objection:** because ethnicity is often racialised, supremacy remains the underlying cause.  
**Reply:** this stretches “race” until it explains every hostile identity and obscures institutional mechanisms.

**Assessment:** racial supremacy is a paradigmatic genocidal ideology, not a complete causal law. Prevention must oppose dehumanising identities while protecting plural citizenship, accountable institutions and minority security.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a qualified answer and separates ideological motivation from enabling conditions.

## Q2(a). Which theory of punishment, retributive or restorative, do you recommend and why?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Crime and Punishment](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/crime-and-punishment.md) → retributive and restorative justice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(a) · 20 marks · Directive: Recommend and justify · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Crime and Punishment — Theories of Punishment  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Kant · desert · restorative justice · proportionality · victims  
**Proposed slug:** `recommend-retributive-or-restorative-punishment`  
**Canonical owner:** [Crime and Punishment](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Crime-and-Punishment.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare the moral logic of both theories, recommend one, answer hard-case objections and specify limits.

**Argument map:** retribution restores moral balance → restoration repairs relationships → objections on leniency/coercion → restorative priority within proportional safeguards.

### Model answer

Retributivism holds that a responsible offender deserves proportionate punishment for a past wrong. Kant objects to using offenders merely as instruments of deterrence: punishment respects agency by responding to their culpable choice. It expresses public condemnation and prevents serious wrongdoing from being trivialised.

Restorative justice instead asks who was harmed, what needs arose and who must repair them. Through voluntary, safeguarded dialogue, restitution and community reintegration, it gives victims voice, requires offenders to confront consequences and addresses fractured relationships. It is especially suitable for juvenile and many non-violent offences and resonates with reintegration rather than social exclusion.

I recommend **restorative justice as the governing orientation**, subject to retributive proportionality. Pure retribution can become backward-looking suffering, neglect victims’ practical needs and deepen criminal identity. Yet restoration alone faces objections: remorse may be strategic; unequal parties may be pressured to reconcile; grave violence implicates public wrong, not merely a private relationship.

The reply is a hybrid. Desert supplies an upper limit and public censure; due process protects both parties; restoration determines constructive response where consent and safety permit. Serious offenders may require incapacitation, but even imprisonment can include accountability, treatment and reparation.

In India, victim compensation, juvenile justice and community-based sanctions can advance this model, provided caste, gender and economic power do not coerce settlements.

**Assessment:** punishment should condemn proportionately but aim beyond suffering toward repair, reintegration and reduced recurrence. Restorative justice is therefore preferable, not as permissiveness, but as responsibility made socially productive.

**Why this earns marks:** It makes a reasoned recommendation, preserves desert and public protection, and handles unequal-power cases.

## Q2(b). “One is not born a woman, but she becomes a woman.” Critically comment on it.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Gender Discrimination](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/gender-discrimination.md) → Beauvoir · sex and gender · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(b) · 15 marks · Directive: Critically comment · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Gender Discrimination  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Simone de Beauvoir · becoming · Other · social construction  
**Proposed slug:** `beauvoir-not-born-woman-becomes-woman`  
**Canonical owner:** [Gender Discrimination](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Gender-Discrimination.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain Beauvoir’s existential-social thesis, its emancipatory force and major qualifications.

**Argument map:** biological sex ≠ social destiny → socialisation produces femininity → freedom under situation → material/intersectional objections → qualified constructionism.

### Model answer

Beauvoir’s statement in *The Second Sex* distinguishes biological embodiment from the social production of “woman” as a subordinate gender. Patriarchal institutions, myths, education and economic dependence train females into passivity, care and immanence, while man is treated as the neutral Subject and woman as the “Other.” Femininity is therefore neither an eternal essence nor an unavoidable biological fate.

The thesis is liberating because what is historically made can be remade. Existential freedom, however, is situated: women choose within material and symbolic constraints, not from nowhere. Indian norms concerning marriage, unpaid care, property and honour illustrate such gendered becoming.

**Objection:** an exclusive emphasis on construction may neglect pregnancy, bodily vulnerability and sex-based violence; speaking of “woman” may also universalise the experience of privileged women. Black, Dalit and postcolonial feminists stress intersecting race, caste and class.

**Reply:** Beauvoir need not deny bodies; she denies that biological facts carry a fixed social meaning. Intersectional analysis extends her anti-essentialism.

**Assessment:** the aphorism correctly exposes gender hierarchy as produced rather than natural, but emancipation requires transforming both cultural meanings and material, embodied conditions.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains “becoming,” engages embodiment and intersectionality, and reaches a qualified verdict.

## Q2(c). By eliminating alienation can we bring social progress? Critically analyse.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Development and Social Progress](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/development-and-social-progress.md) → alienation · social progress · **Secondary route:** [Political Ideologies](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/political-ideologies.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q2(c) · 15 marks · Directive: Critically analyse · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Development and Social Progress; Marxism  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Marx · alienated labour · recognition · capabilities  
**Proposed slug:** `eliminating-alienation-social-progress`  
**Canonical owner:** [Development and Social Progress](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Development-Social-Progress.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain why overcoming alienation advances progress, then test whether it is sufficient or fully eliminable.

**Argument map:** alienated labour and relations → lost agency/community → democratic work and recognition → plural sources of injustice objection → necessary but insufficient.

### Model answer

For Marx, capitalist labour alienates the worker from the product, productive activity, other persons and human “species-being.” Work becomes externally compelled rather than self-realising. Eliminating such domination through control over production, meaningful labour and cooperative relations would enlarge agency, solidarity and creative development—important dimensions of social progress.

Alienation also appears in bureaucratic power, caste exclusion, consumerism and the loss of recognition. Democratic workplaces, social security and participation can therefore improve well-being beyond growth in income.

**Objection:** not every unpleasant or specialised task can become self-expression; complex societies require division of labour. Moreover, eliminating workplace alienation does not automatically end patriarchy, caste, ecological destruction or political repression. Historical regimes invoking Marx sometimes abolished private ownership while intensifying bureaucratic alienation.

**Reply:** elimination should mean reducing avoidable powerlessness and objectification, not romanticising a society without mediation or constraint.

**Assessment:** overcoming alienation is a necessary component of progress because progress must expand meaningful agency and social recognition. It is not sufficient: rights, capabilities, equality, ecological sustainability and plural democratic institutions are also required.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs Marx’s concept and avoids treating one emancipatory condition as the whole of progress.

## Q3(a). Analyse the statement with reasons that “Power corrupts, absolute power corrupts absolutely”.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Sovereignty](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/sovereignty.md) → absolute power · accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(a) · 20 marks · Directive: Analyse with reasons · Difficulty: Moderate-high  
**Syllabus unit:** Sovereignty; Forms of Government  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Lord Acton · domination · checks and balances · virtue  
**Proposed slug:** `power-corrupts-absolute-power-analysis`  
**Canonical owner:** [Sovereignty](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Sovereignty.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain causal and moral mechanisms of corruption, test the universal claim and derive institutional implications.

**Argument map:** discretionary power → temptation and insulation → rationalisation/fear → counterexamples objection → probabilistic warning and constitutional remedy.

### Model answer

Lord Acton’s maxim is not a psychological law that every officeholder becomes venal. It is a warning that unchecked power predictably damages both rulers and institutions. Power expands opportunity for private gain, reduces dependence on others’ judgement and enables the powerful to suppress evidence of failure. Subordinates then flatter rather than correct; impunity converts occasional abuse into a system.

The corruption is broader than bribery. It includes epistemic arrogance—the belief that one’s will defines truth—and moral corruption, where persons become instruments of policy. Plato’s tyrant is enslaved by appetite; Montesquieu therefore argues that power must check power. Arendt’s account of total domination shows how concentrated authority can normalise participation in evil.

**Objection:** benevolent rulers, judges or emergency leaders sometimes exercise extensive power without corruption; weak fragmented authority can itself enable private domination. Power may also reveal, rather than cause, prior character.

**Reply:** the maxim states a heightened risk, not inevitability. Even virtuous rulers lack perfect knowledge, and institutions cannot safely depend on virtue alone. Accountability also improves information and legitimacy.

India’s constitutional separation of functions, judicial review, elections, federalism, audit and free speech disperse authority. Yet unelected economic, social and digital power likewise needs scrutiny.

**Assessment:** absolute power “corrupts absolutely” insofar as it removes external correction and recasts personal will as public reason. Effective government requires power, but legitimate power must be limited, transparent, answerable and contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses several mechanisms, answers counterexamples and converts the maxim into a qualified institutional principle.

## Q3(b). What are the basic differences between Gandhi and Ambedkar regarding caste system in India?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Caste Discrimination, Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/caste-discrimination-gandhi-and-ambedkar.md) → Gandhi · Ambedkar · caste · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(b) · 15 marks · Directive: Compare · Difficulty: Moderate-high  
**Syllabus unit:** Caste Discrimination — Gandhi and Ambedkar  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** varṇa · untouchability · annihilation of caste · political safeguards  
**Proposed slug:** `gandhi-ambedkar-basic-differences-caste`  
**Canonical owner:** [Caste, Gandhi and Ambedkar](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Caste-Gandhi-Ambedkar.md)

**Demand decoding:** Compare diagnosis, normative goal, method, religion and political safeguards without caricaturing either thinker.

**Argument map:** shared rejection of untouchability → Gandhi’s moral reform → Ambedkar’s structural annihilation → representation dispute → limited convergence.

### Model answer

Gandhi and Ambedkar both condemned untouchability, but differed fundamentally on caste’s structure and remedy.

Gandhi initially distinguished an idealised *varṇa* division of function from hierarchical caste and sought purification of Hindu society through change of heart, inter-dining, service and moral appeal. He later became more critical of caste and supported inter-caste marriage, yet emphasised social unity and reform from within. His language of *Harijan* expressed care but was criticised as paternalistic.

Ambedkar held that caste itself—not merely untouchability—is a system of graded inequality sustained by endogamy and religious authority. It is a “division of labourers,” denying liberty, equality and fraternity. Hence caste must be annihilated through rights, education, political representation, economic independence, inter-caste marriage and rejection of scriptural authority; conversion to Buddhism marked exit from oppressive Hindu structures.

Their conflict over separate electorates and the Poona Pact captures the difference: Gandhi feared permanent communal division; Ambedkar sought independent Dalit political voice.

**Assessment:** Gandhi mobilised moral conscience and attacked untouchability; Ambedkar supplied the deeper structural diagnosis and constitutional programme. Contemporary justice needs social fraternity, but on Ambedkarite foundations of equal power and dignity rather than paternal reform.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares diagnosis, means and political agency while acknowledging development and overlap.

## Q3(c). Discuss Amartya Sen’s principle of Nīti as a critique of Nyāya.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Social and Political Ideals](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/social-and-political-ideals.md) → justice · Sen · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q3(c) · 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Social and Political Ideals — Justice  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Amartya Sen · *nīti* · *nyāya* · realisation-focused comparison  
**Proposed slug:** `sen-niti-critique-nyaya`  
**Canonical owner:** [Social and Political Ideals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Social-Political-Ideals.md)

**Demand decoding:** Answer the printed direction by showing how institutional *nīti* can criticise unstructured outcome-oriented *nyāya*, while clarifying that Sen’s own principal emphasis runs in the reverse direction.

**Argument map:** *nīti* as fair rules/institutions → critique of discretionary outcome judgement → Sen’s reverse warning against institutional fetishism → mutual correction.

### Model answer

*Nīti* concerns proper institutions, rules and conduct; *nyāya* concerns justice actually realised in people’s lives. Read in the printed direction, *nīti* criticises an unstructured appeal to outcomes: fair procedures, settled rights, predictability and equal public rules prevent officials from labelling whatever consequence they prefer “just.” Courts, constitutional liberties and due process constrain discretionary consequentialism and protect minorities even when majorities claim beneficial results.

Sen’s own principal argument, however, runs conversely. In *The Idea of Justice* he criticises justice reduced to ideal *nīti* or “transcendental institutionalism.” Formally excellent rules can coexist with *matsyanyāya*—the strong devouring the weak—if actual capabilities remain unequal. Public reasoning must compare feasible alternatives and remove manifest injustice.

**Objection:** outcome-sensitive *nyāya* lacks a complete ideal and may sacrifice rights.  
**Reply:** Sen does not discard *nīti*; he makes institutions answerable to effective freedom and actual conduct.

**Assessment:** each corrects the other. *Nīti* supplies rights, procedure and non-arbitrariness; *nyāya* tests whether they produce freedom from caste, gender and capability deprivation. Justice needs fair institutions evaluated by realised lives.

**Why this earns marks:** It resolves the terminology carefully and presents Sen’s institutional-realisation distinction with qualification.

## Q4(a). “All human rights are centred on individual rights.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Individual and State](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/individual-and-state.md) → human rights · individual and collective rights · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q4(a) · 20 marks · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Individual and State — Rights  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** dignity · collective rights · peoples · group-differentiated rights  
**Proposed slug:** `human-rights-centred-individual-rights`  
**Canonical owner:** [Individual and State](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/socio-political/Individual-and-State.md)

**Demand decoding:** Assess whether individuals are the ultimate subjects of human rights while explaining genuinely collective dimensions.

**Argument map:** individual dignity → universal rights → social conditions and group harms → collective-rights objection → individual-centred but socially embedded account.

### Model answer

Human rights are centrally rights of persons: every individual possesses equal moral status independently of citizenship, caste, sex or community. Rights against torture, arbitrary detention and coercion protect embodied individuals; freedom of conscience and expression secure personal agency. This individual centre also prevents rulers or communities from sacrificing members to “collective good.”

Yet human beings are socially embedded. Language, culture, family and political membership shape agency, while some harms target persons precisely as group members. Minority-language education, indigenous control of ancestral resources and peoples’ self-determination cannot be fully described as isolated individual claims. Socio-economic rights likewise require collective institutions and shared resources.

**Collectivist objection:** communities themselves persist across generations and therefore possess rights irreducible to current members’ preferences.  
**Liberal reply:** collective rights are justified when they protect members’ equal agency and a group’s fair standing, not when leaders suppress dissent. Kymlicka’s distinction between external protections and internal restrictions is decisive.

India’s cultural and educational rights protect minorities, but cannot validate untouchability, forced conformity or gender subordination. Similarly, tribal community rights can protect livelihood and identity while retaining individual voice and remedy.

**Assessment:** human rights are individual-centred because persons are the ultimate bearers of dignity, but they are not individualistic. Their effective exercise can require collective goods, institutions and group-differentiated protections. The correct model is socially situated personhood under a non-domination test.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes moral foundation, legal bearer and social conditions instead of choosing simplistic individualism or collectivism.

## Q4(b). Explain descriptive and normative perspectives on multiculturalism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Humanism, Secularism and Multiculturalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/humanism-secularism-and-multiculturalism.md) → descriptive and normative multiculturalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Argument map:** contingent/dependent reality → sufficient reason/first cause → necessary being → regress and special-pleading objections → limited explanatory inference.

### Model answer

Cosmological arguments reason from dependent features of the world to an ultimate ground. Aquinas’s Ways move from change and efficient causation to a first unmoved mover or uncaused cause; Leibniz asks why there is something rather than nothing and invokes the principle of sufficient reason. Since contingent beings do not contain the reason for their existence, the totality allegedly requires a necessary being.

**Assessment:** the argument gives a serious demand for ultimate explanation, strongest in its contingency form. It supports, at most, a necessary ground; further argument is needed to identify that ground with the God of religion.

**Why this earns marks:** It reconstructs more than one version and separates first cause from full theism.

## Q8(a). Elucidate the status of man in the realm between ‘immanence’ and ‘transcendence’.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Notions of God](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/notions-of-god.md) → immanence · transcendence · human status · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(a) · 20 marks · Directive: Elucidate · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Notions of God — Relation to Man and the World  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** theism · pantheism · panentheism · participation · freedom  
**Proposed slug:** `human-status-between-immanence-transcendence`  
**Canonical owner:** [Notions of God](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Notions-of-God.md)

**Demand decoding:** Explain how different God-world models determine human dependence, dignity, freedom and religious relation.

**Argument map:** divine immanence grounds presence → transcendence preserves otherness → extremes of absorption/deism → mediating participation → relational personhood.

### Model answer

Divine **immanence** means that the sacred is present and active within the world; **transcendence** means that ultimate reality exceeds finite nature and human concepts. Human status is shaped by holding these poles together.

If immanence is exclusive, as in a crude pantheism, persons may appear modes of the whole and freedom or personal relation may be weakened. Yet immanence grounds the sanctity of embodied life, ecological responsibility and the possibility of immediate religious experience. Advaita locates the deepest self (*ātman*) in identity with *Brahman*, though empirical individuality belongs to the conventional level.

If transcendence is exclusive, as in remote deism, God becomes external and religious intimacy is difficult. Classical theism preserves creator-creature distinction, dependence and accountability, but can make humanity seem merely heteronomous. Biblical “image of God” and Islamic stewardship combine dependence with dignity and agency.

Panentheistic and qualified non-dual models mediate the poles: the world exists in God, while God exceeds it. Rāmānuja treats souls and world as the body of *Brahman*: genuinely distinct yet inseparable and dependent. Human beings then participate in divine reality without being identical to or abandoned by it.

**Objection:** mediation may be conceptually unstable—either finite persons are genuinely other or ultimately absorbed.  
**Reply:** relation itself can unite without identity; dependence need not erase relative autonomy.

**Assessment:** humanity is best understood as finite, embodied and responsible, yet open to self-transcendence and participation in ultimate reality. Immanence secures presence and worth; transcendence secures otherness, critique and humility.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects metaphysical models directly to freedom, dignity and religious life.

## Q8(b). Can reason be used to justify faith?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Reason, Revelation and Faith](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/reason-revelation-and-faith.md) → faith and reason · justification · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(b) · 15 marks · Directive: Assess · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Reason, Revelation and Faith  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Aquinas · Kierkegaard · James · Plantinga · cumulative warrant  
**Proposed slug:** `reason-justify-faith`  
**Canonical owner:** [Reason, Revelation and Faith](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Reason-Revelation-Faith.md)

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish proof, rational permissibility and existential commitment, then state reason’s proper role and limits.

**Argument map:** evidential reason → natural theology → limits/leap/proper basicality → fideism objection → critical, cumulative justification.

### Model answer

Reason can justify faith if justification means showing it to be coherent, evidentially supported or rationally permissible; it may not compel faith by demonstration. Aquinas holds that natural reason can infer God’s existence, while revelation concerns truths beyond unaided reason. Historical testimony, religious experience, moral experience and cosmological considerations may form a cumulative case.

Kierkegaard stresses that faith is an existential commitment under objective uncertainty, not the conclusion of a detached proof. James permits commitment in a genuine option when evidence cannot decide and postponement itself loses a possible good. Plantinga argues that belief in God may be “properly basic,” though still defeasible by objections.

**Objection:** without sufficient public evidence, faith becomes wishful thinking; contradictory faiths could claim equal immunity.  
**Reply:** rational justification need not be certainty, but faith must remain answerable to coherence, evidence, moral consequences and counter-evidence.

**Assessment:** reason can criticise, interpret and provide cumulative warrant for faith, but cannot manufacture trust or eliminate risk. Faith beyond proof can be rational; faith against decisive reason becomes fideism.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes demonstration, warrant and commitment while avoiding both rationalism and irrationalism.

## Q8(c). Discuss the conflicting nature of religious experiences with special reference to Buddhism and Jainism.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper II → [Religious Experience](../../topics/philosophy-paper-ii/religious-experience.md) → conflicting claims · Buddhism · Jainism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q8(c) · 15 marks · Directive: Discuss · Difficulty: High  
**Syllabus unit:** Religious Experience — Nature and Object  
**Thinker/doctrine/subproblem:** Buddhist *anātman* · Jain *jīva* · omniscience · many-sidedness  
**Proposed slug:** `conflicting-religious-experience-buddhism-jainism`  
**Canonical owner:** [Religious Experience](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-2/philosophy-of-religion/Religious-Experience.md)

**Demand decoding:** Identify doctrinally conflicting experiential interpretations, common transformative features and their evidential implications.

**Argument map:** similar discipline/ineffability → Buddhist no-self insight → Jain purified self insight → perennialist/contextualist debate → defeasible and tradition-shaped evidence.

### Model answer

Religious experiences often share discipline, inward transformation and claims of direct insight, yet their apparent objects conflict. Buddhist meditative insight discloses impermanence (*anitya*), suffering and no-self (*anātman*); liberation (*nirvāṇa*) ends craving through insight into dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*). It rejects an eternal substance-self.

Jain experience, by contrast, culminates in the manifestation of the soul’s (*jīva*) intrinsic consciousness when karmic matter is removed. The liberated being retains perfected individuality and may attain omniscience (*kevala-jñāna*). Thus Buddhism experiences the absence of enduring self where Jainism affirms its purified reality.

Perennialists may interpret both as culturally different descriptions of detachment and liberation. Contextualists such as Katz reply that concepts and practices partly constitute experience; doctrinal conflict cannot simply be translated away. Jain many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) encourages perspectival humility, but cannot make “an eternal self exists” and “no permanent self exists” literally identical.

**Assessment:** shared ethical transformation supplies some cross-traditional credibility, while conflicting ontologies prevent any experience from serving as self-authenticating proof. Religious experience is defeasible evidence interpreted within traditions and must be tested by coherence, conduct and comparative inquiry.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the exact Buddhist-Jain conflict without erasing substantial common ground.
