## Q1. In the light of recent controversy regarding the use of Electronic Voting Machines (EVM), what are the challenges before the Election Commission of India to ensure the trustworthiness of elections in India?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Electoral integrity and EVM trust · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Identify challenges  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Election Commission and electoral integrity  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `evm-trust-electoral-integrity`

**Demand decoding:** Focus on the Election Commission’s institutional, technical and communicative challenges—not on proving or disproving partisan allegations.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional duty → trust deficits → safeguards → qualified conclusion.

### Model answer

Article **324** entrusts the Election Commission of India (ECI) with superintendence, direction and control of elections. EVMs therefore pose not only a technical-security challenge but also a democratic-confidence challenge.

- The ECI must protect the machines’ stand-alone design, chain of custody, randomisation, sealing and storage.
- It must make verification intelligible through VVPAT slips, mock polls, candidate representatives and credible audits.
- Misinformation and opaque technical language can magnify suspicion even without proof of manipulation.
- Nationwide procurement and replacement require capacity and uniform protocols.
- Perceived partisanship in enforcement or complaint handling can contaminate confidence in sound technology.

In *Subramanian Swamy v. ECI* (2013), the Supreme Court treated the voter-verifiable paper audit trail as indispensable for electoral transparency. The Conduct of Elections Rules were consequently amended for VVPAT use.

**Conclusion:** Trustworthiness requires demonstrable security, statistically meaningful verification, procedural transparency and visibly impartial administration; institutional credibility cannot rest on technological assurance alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates technical reliability from public verifiability and anchors the ECI’s response in Article 324 and VVPAT jurisprudence.

## Q2. Whether National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) can enforce the implementation of constitutional reservation for the Scheduled Castes in the religious minority institutions? Examine.
**Primary route:** Polity → [National Commissions for SC, ST and BC](../../topics/polity/national-commissions-sc-st-bc.md) → NCSC powers and minority institutions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine legal competence  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional bodies → National Commission for Scheduled Castes  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Fundamental rights → Minority educational institutions  
**Canonical owners:** [National Commissions—SC, ST and BC](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/National-Commissions-SC-ST-BC.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `ncsc-reservation-minority-institutions`

**Demand decoding:** Reconcile the NCSC’s Article 338 mandate with Articles 15(5) and 30(1), and distinguish inquiry/recommendation from enforceable adjudication.

**Compact framework:** Competing provisions → NCSC powers → constitutional exception → verdict.

### Model answer

The NCSC cannot itself compel religious-minority educational institutions to implement Scheduled Caste reservation contrary to the Constitution.

Article **338** empowers the NCSC to investigate safeguards, inquire into complaints and report to the President. During inquiry it has civil-court powers, but its recommendations are not judicial decrees.

Article **15(5)** permits special provisions for admission of socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs even in private educational institutions, but expressly excludes minority educational institutions under Article **30(1)**. In *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014), the Supreme Court protected this minority-institution exception in the context of the Right to Education framework.

The NCSC may still investigate discrimination, monitor applicable safeguards and recommend legislative or executive action. General regulatory laws may apply if they do not extinguish minority character.

**Conclusion:** The Commission can protect SC interests through inquiry and constitutional advice, but cannot override Article 15(5)’s express exception or convert its recommendations into a reservation mandate.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the precise constitutional exception and correctly limits the NCSC’s enforcement capacity.

## Q3. Under what circumstances can the Financial Emergency be proclaimed by the President of India? What consequences follow when such a declaration remains in force?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Salient Features and Constitutional Parts](../../topics/polity/salient-features-and-constitutional-parts.md) → Financial Emergency · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: State condition; Explain consequences  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Emergency provisions → Financial Emergency  
**Canonical owner:** [Emergency Provisions](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/14_Emergency-Provisions.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `financial-emergency-circumstances-consequences`

**Demand decoding:** Give Article 360’s trigger, approval and duration, followed by its federal and service-related consequences.

**Compact framework:** Trigger → parliamentary control → Union directions → safeguards.

### Model answer

Under Article **360**, the President may proclaim a Financial Emergency when satisfied that India’s financial stability or credit, or that of any part of its territory, is threatened.

The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within **two months**. If the Lok Sabha is dissolved, Rajya Sabha approval preserves it until thirty days after the new Lok Sabha’s first sitting. Once approved, it continues until revoked; no maximum duration is prescribed.

While it operates:

- the Union may direct States to observe specified canons of financial propriety;
- it may require State Money Bills and other financial Bills to be reserved for presidential consideration;
- directions may reduce salaries and allowances of persons serving the Union or a State, including Supreme Court and High Court judges.

**Conclusion:** Article 360 creates exceptional central supervision to restore public credit, but its sweeping impact on federalism and institutional independence explains why it has never been invoked.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both limbs with the exact trigger, approval rules and constitutional consequences.

## Q4. Why do you think the committees are considered to be useful for parliamentary work? Discuss, in this context, the role of the Estimates Committee.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Parliamentary committees and Estimates Committee · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Explain utility; Discuss role  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Financial committees  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `parliamentary-committees-estimates-committee`

**Demand decoding:** First explain why committees improve legislative scrutiny; then specify the Estimates Committee’s composition, functions and limits.

**Compact framework:** Committee utility → Estimates Committee mandate → limitation.

### Model answer

Parliamentary committees make legislative control continuous and informed. Smaller, relatively less partisan forums can examine documents, hear experts and scrutinise matters for which floor debates lack time.

The **Estimates Committee**, constituted annually under Lok Sabha rules, has **30 Lok Sabha members** elected by proportional representation; ministers cannot be members. It:

- examines budget estimates after they are voted;
- suggests economies, organisational improvements and administrative efficiency;
- recommends alternative policies consistent with the estimates;
- checks whether money is well laid out within policy limits; and
- proposes the form in which estimates should be presented.

It cannot question settled policy or enforce recommendations. Unlike the Public Accounts Committee, its scrutiny is prospective and efficiency-oriented, not an audit of expenditure already incurred.

**Conclusion:** Committees constitute Parliament’s “workshop”; the Estimates Committee converts voting of supply into informed examination of economy and administrative performance.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects the general value of committees to the Estimates Committee’s distinctive prospective function.

## Q5. “The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has a very vital role to play.” Explain how this is reflected in the method and terms of his appointment as well as the range of powers he can exercise.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comptroller and Auditor General](../../topics/polity/comptroller-and-auditor-general.md) → Independence and audit mandate · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain institutional design  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional bodies → CAG  
**Canonical owner:** [CAG](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/32_CAG.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cag-independence-powers`

**Demand decoding:** Relate appointment and tenure safeguards to the breadth of audit powers; do not claim that the appointment method itself is collegial.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional status → safeguards → statutory audit range → accountability.

### Model answer

Under Articles **148–151**, the President appoints the CAG. Independence is protected by removal in the same manner and on the same grounds as a Supreme Court judge; salary and administrative expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India; service conditions cannot be varied to the CAG’s disadvantage; and the incumbent is ineligible for further government office. The CAG’s Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service Act, **1971** prescribes a six-year term or age sixty-five, whichever is earlier.

The CAG audits Union and State expenditure, receipts, stores, government companies and substantially financed bodies as authorised by the Constitution and the 1971 Act. Reports submitted to the President or Governor are laid before the legislature and examined by financial committees.

However, executive appointment without consultation and dependence on timely records can affect perceived autonomy.

**Conclusion:** Security of tenure and a wide audit mandate enable the CAG to make executive spending answerable to legislatures; appointment transparency can be strengthened.

**Why this earns marks:** It links each independence safeguard to the CAG’s constitutional accountability function and avoids overstating its powers.

## Q6. “Policy contradictions among various competing sectors and stakeholders have resulted in inadequate ‘protection and prevention of degradation’ to environment.” Comment with relevant illustrations.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../topics/governance/government-policy-design-and-implementation.md) → Policy coherence and environmental protection · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Comment with illustrations  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Policy design → Inter-sectoral coordination  
**Secondary classification:** Environment → Environmental governance  
**Canonical owners:** [Government Policy Design and Implementation](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/02_Government-Policy-Design-and-Implementation.md) · [Environmental Impact Assessment and NGT](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Environment-and-Ecology/basic/16_Environmental-Impact-Assessment-and-NGT.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `policy-contradictions-environmental-degradation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how conflicting development mandates weaken prevention and protection, illustrate the mechanism, and give a balanced institutional remedy.

**Compact framework:** Contradiction → illustrations → governance failures → coherence reforms.

### Model answer

Environmental degradation often arises not from absence of law but from incoherence among sectoral policies.

- Coal, highways and industrial-corridor policies seek rapid clearances, while the Environment (Protection) Act, **1986**, Forest (Conservation) Act, **1980**, and EIA process require precaution and appraisal.
- Irrigation and power incentives encourage groundwater extraction even as water policy seeks aquifer conservation.
- Minimum-support and procurement patterns can favour water-intensive paddy and sugarcane in stressed regions.
- Urban construction, mining and tourism revenues may conflict with wetlands, floodplains and carrying-capacity limits.

Different ministries optimise separate targets; cumulative impacts cross jurisdictions, regulators face capacity and conflict-of-interest problems, and affected communities enter late. *Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum* recognised the precautionary and polluter-pays principles, yet weak appraisal and enforcement dilute them.

**Conclusion:** India needs strategic environmental assessment, cumulative-impact studies, independent regulators, transparent data, inter-ministerial coordination and ecological indicators in budgeting. Development and protection must be jointly designed, not reconciled only after damage.

**Why this earns marks:** It shows the causal link between policy silos and degradation through specific sectoral examples and legal principles.

## Q7. Appropriate local community-level healthcare intervention is a prerequisite to achieve ‘Health for All’ in India. Explain.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage](../../topics/social-justice/health-systems-public-health-and-universal-health-coverage.md) → Community-level healthcare · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain necessity  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Health → Primary and community healthcare  
**Canonical owner:** [Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/03_Health-Systems-Public-Health-and-Universal-Health-Coverage.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `community-healthcare-health-for-all`

**Demand decoding:** Establish why universal health cannot be achieved through hospitals alone and identify functions and enabling conditions at community level.

**Compact framework:** Access logic → community functions → institutional support → qualification.

### Model answer

“Health for All” requires universal access to promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative care. A hospital-centric system reaches illness late; community care acts at its social and epidemiological roots.

ASHA workers, Anganwadi workers, auxiliary nurse midwives, sub-centres and primary health centres can:

- provide immunisation, antenatal care and screening;
- detect tuberculosis, malaria, non-communicable diseases and outbreaks early;
- improve nutrition, sanitation, contraception and health literacy;
- ensure referral, treatment adherence and follow-up;
- adapt services to tribal, gender and local disease contexts; and
- build trust and community surveillance.

The Alma-Ata approach and the National Health Mission emphasise comprehensive primary healthcare. Articles **21** and **47** support the State’s responsibility for health.

Community intervention nevertheless needs reliable medicines, trained personnel, diagnostics, referral transport, digital continuity and adequate public financing; unpaid or under-supported workers cannot substitute for a health system.

**Conclusion:** Strong local care is the equitable first contact and prevention platform on which universal health coverage must rest.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the functional necessity of community care while qualifying it with system-level requirements.

## Q8. E-governance is not only about utilization of the power of new technology, but also much about critical importance of the ‘use value’ of information. Explain.
**Primary route:** Governance → [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../topics/governance/e-governance-models-and-user-centricity.md) → Information use value · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain proposition  
**Primary classification:** Governance → E-governance → Information and public value  
**Canonical owner:** [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/05_E-Governance-Models-and-User-Centricity.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `e-governance-use-value-information`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish digitisation from useful, actionable and rights-respecting information; demonstrate citizen and administrative value.

**Compact framework:** Technology versus value → dimensions of useful information → safeguards.

### Model answer

E-governance creates public value only when technology converts reliable information into better decisions, services and accountability.

Information has “use value” when it is accurate, timely, interoperable, intelligible and actionable:

- citizens can track entitlements, applications, budgets and grievances;
- administrators can identify exclusion, leakages and regional gaps;
- open data enables social audit and evidence-based policy;
- integrated records reduce repeated documentation and transaction costs; and
- real-time dashboards can improve disaster, health and welfare responses.

Merely computerising a defective process can reproduce opacity or exclusion. Inaccurate databases, digital divides, non-speaking rejection orders and inaccessible portals diminish value. Excessive data collection also threatens privacy. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission therefore linked e-governance with business-process re-engineering, while *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017) requires legality, legitimate purpose and proportionality in data use.

**Conclusion:** E-governance is not hardware-led administration; it is citizen-centred governance in which trustworthy information improves agency, outcomes and accountability under privacy safeguards.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines “use value,” gives operational examples and distinguishes meaningful reform from superficial digitisation.

## Q11. Whether the Supreme Court Judgement (July 2018) can settle the political tussle between the Lt. Governor and elected government of Delhi? Examine.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Union Territories](../../topics/polity/union-territories.md) → Delhi government and Lieutenant Governor · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine capacity and limits  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Union Territories → National Capital Territory of Delhi  
**Canonical owner:** [Union Territories](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/25_Union-Territories.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `delhi-lg-elected-government-2018-judgement`

**Demand decoding:** State the July 2018 Constitution Bench principles, explain how they reduce conflict, and assess why legal doctrine alone may not end political tussles.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional scheme → holdings → conflict-reducing effect → unresolved areas → verdict.

### Model answer

In *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (2018), the Supreme Court clarified the constitutional relationship under Article **239AA**, but could not by itself eliminate every political or administrative conflict.

**What the judgment settled**

- Delhi has a representative government; constitutional morality and collaborative federalism require respect for its democratic mandate.
- The Lieutenant Governor is generally bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers on transferred subjects.
- The LG has no general independent decision-making power and cannot refer every disagreement to the President.
- The exception covers **public order, police and land**, which remain outside the Delhi Assembly’s legislative competence.
- Referral of a genuine “difference of opinion” must be exceptional, reasoned and not a device to paralyse administration.

These principles rejected an interpretation that reduced the elected government to a subordinate department and established a norm of communication and constitutional restraint.

**Why tussles could persist**

Article 239AA creates an asymmetric arrangement: Delhi is neither a full State nor an ordinary Union Territory. Ambiguity can remain over services, control of officials, commissions and the boundary between reserved and transferred subjects. Parliament also retains extensive legislative power, while political rivalry can convert legal discretion into institutional confrontation.

Subsequent disputes over “services” demonstrate that adjudication may clarify one boundary while legislation and new factual controversies generate others.

**Conclusion:** The 2018 judgment supplied an authoritative cooperative-governance framework and could settle the governing principle, but lasting peace depends on constitutional conventions, precise legislation and restraint by both the LG and elected government.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes the legal issues actually decided from the structural and political causes that adjudication cannot finally remove.

## Q12. How far do you agree with the view that tribunals curtail the jurisdiction of ordinary courts? In view of the above, discuss the constitutional validity and competency of the tribunals in India.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Administrative Tribunals](../../topics/polity/administrative-tribunals.md) → Tribunal jurisdiction and constitutional validity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Assess; Discuss validity and competence  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Tribunals → Judicial review and institutional competence  
**Canonical owners:** [Administrative Tribunals](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Administrative-Tribunals.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `tribunals-jurisdiction-validity-competence`

**Demand decoding:** Give a graded response: tribunals replace ordinary courts in defined fields, but cannot exclude the constitutional courts’ basic-structure jurisdiction; then evaluate competence.

**Compact framework:** Rationale → curtailment → constitutional settlement → capacity issues → reforms.

### Model answer

Tribunals do curtail the **initial jurisdiction** of ordinary civil courts in specialised fields, but constitutionally they cannot extinguish judicial review by High Courts and the Supreme Court.

The **42nd Amendment** inserted Articles **323A** and **323B**, enabling administrative and other tribunals. Parliament and State legislatures have also created sectoral tribunals for tax, environment, companies and services. Their justification is expertise, speed, flexible procedure and reduced court arrears.

However, tribunalisation creates constitutional concerns:

- exclusion clauses may deny ordinary judicial forums;
- executive control over appointments, tenure, finance and infrastructure can compromise independence;
- short tenure and non-uniform procedures weaken expertise;
- multiple appellate routes may add, rather than reduce, litigation.

In *S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987), a tribunal was accepted as an effective alternative institutional mechanism. The decisive settlement came in *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997): judicial review under Articles **226/227** and **32** is part of the basic structure; tribunal decisions are therefore subject to High Court scrutiny, and tribunals act as courts of first instance in their fields. The *Madras Bar Association* line of cases further insists on judicially compatible selection, tenure and independence.

Tribunals are thus constitutionally valid when they provide competent, independent adjudication and supplement—not supplant—constitutional courts.

**Conclusion:** The claim is only partly correct. Tribunals may lawfully channel original jurisdiction, but cannot become executive-controlled substitutes for the judiciary or exclude basic-structure review.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “how far,” traces the controlling cases and evaluates both validity and practical competence.

## Q13. India and USA are two large democracies. Examine the basic tenets on which the two political systems are based.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → India and United States · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comparative examination  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitution → India and United States  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Parliamentary and presidential systems  
**Canonical owners:** [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md) · [Parliamentary System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/11_Parliamentary-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-usa-political-systems-tenets`

**Demand decoding:** Identify shared democratic foundations, compare institutional tenets, and avoid reducing the answer to isolated constitutional differences.

**Compact framework:** Common foundations → structured comparison → functional implications → synthesis.

### Model answer

India and the United States share popular sovereignty, written constitutional supremacy, representative government, federalism, bicameralism, enforceable rights, an independent judiciary and judicial review. Their institutional tenets, however, embody different democratic designs.

| Dimension | India | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Parliamentary; real executive is collectively responsible to Lok Sabha | Presidential; separately elected President is not responsible to Congress |
| Separation of powers | Functional overlap with checks and balances | Relatively stricter separation with checks and balances |
| Federalism | Strong Centre, single Constitution, emergency centralisation and integrated services | Dual polity with constitutionally strong States and enumerated federal powers |
| Citizenship | Single citizenship | Dual citizenship in federal practice |
| Legislature | Rajya Sabha gives unequal State representation; government usually controls legislative agenda | Senate gives equal State representation; executive is institutionally separate |
| Rights | Fundamental Rights plus Directive Principles and reasonable restrictions | Bill of Rights with strong negative-liberty tradition |
| Judiciary | Integrated hierarchy; advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 | Dual federal-State court structure; no advisory opinions |
| Amendment | Multiple procedures under Article 368 | Rigid Article V process requiring supermajorities and State ratification |

India’s model prioritises responsible government and accommodation in a diverse developmental federation; the US model prioritises separated institutions, fixed tenure and stronger horizontal checks. Both systems have evolved through judicial interpretation and political conventions.

**Conclusion:** The two democracies rest on constitutionalism and consent, but translate them differently: India through parliamentary responsibility and cooperative federalism, the US through presidentialism and institutionally separated federal power.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares governing principles and explains their consequences rather than merely listing features.

## Q14. How is the Finance Commission of India constituted? What do you know about the terms of reference of the recently constituted Finance Commission? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Finance Commission](../../topics/polity/finance-commission.md) → Constitution and Fifteenth Finance Commission terms · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Explain constitution; Discuss terms of reference  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Fiscal federalism → Finance Commission  
**Canonical owner:** [Finance Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/29_Finance-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `fifteenth-finance-commission-constitution-terms`

**Demand decoding:** Explain Article 280 and the Finance Commission Act, then discuss the Fifteenth Finance Commission’s 2017 terms of reference as understood when the question was set.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional design → standard duties → Fifteenth FC terms → federal concerns.

### Model answer

Article **280** requires the President to constitute a Finance Commission every five years or earlier. It consists of a Chairperson and four other members. Parliament determines qualifications and selection through the Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, **1951**. The President specifies its terms of reference.

Its core constitutional duties are to recommend:

- distribution of the divisible tax pool between Union and States and its inter-State allocation;
- principles governing grants-in-aid under Article **275**;
- measures to augment State funds for Panchayats and Municipalities; and
- other matters referred in the interests of sound finance.

The **Fifteenth Finance Commission**, constituted in November 2017 under N.K. Singh, was asked to make recommendations for 2020–25. Its terms included:

- tax devolution and grants, considering Union and State finances;
- review of fiscal consolidation, debt and deficit levels;
- use of **2011 population data**;
- performance-based incentives relating to GST, population control, ease of doing business, sanitation, power-sector reform and direct-benefit transfer; and
- assessment of the impact of GST and abolition of the Plan/non-Plan expenditure distinction.

The ToR widened attention from equalisation to performance and macro-fiscal discipline. States nevertheless feared that 2011 population would penalise demographic success and that conditional incentives could narrow fiscal autonomy.

**Conclusion:** The Commission is a constitutionally independent periodic mediator of fiscal federalism; its ToR must balance national priorities, equity, efficiency and predictable State autonomy.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates constitutional composition and duties from the specific Fifteenth Commission mandate and its federal implications.

## Q15. Assess the importance of the Panchayat system in India as a part of local government. Apart from government grants, what sources the Panchayats can look out for financing developmental projects?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Panchayati Raj](../../topics/polity/panchayati-raj.md) → Democratic decentralisation and local finance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Assess importance; Identify finance sources  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Local government → Panchayati Raj finance  
**Canonical owner:** [Panchayati Raj](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/23_Panchayati-Raj.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `panchayats-importance-own-source-finance`

**Demand decoding:** Assess democratic and developmental importance, acknowledge devolution deficits, and classify non-grant financing realistically.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional role → developmental value → constraints → revenue basket and safeguards.

### Model answer

The **73rd Amendment** constitutionalised Panchayats through Part IX, Gram Sabhas, regular elections, reservation and State Finance Commissions. Panchayats deepen democracy by moving planning, monitoring and accountability closer to citizens.

**Importance**

- They enable participation and social inclusion, especially of women, SCs and STs under Article **243D**.
- Local knowledge improves targeting of water, sanitation, roads, housing and welfare.
- Gram Sabhas permit social audit and direct accountability.
- The Eleventh Schedule provides a framework for integrated rural development.

Their potential is limited by incomplete transfer of **funds, functions and functionaries**, weak staff and dependence on tied grants.

**Non-grant finance**

1. **Own taxes:** property/house tax, profession or entertainment levies where State law authorises them.
2. **User charges and fees:** water, sanitation, markets, fairs, building permissions, licences and local services, with protection for poor households.
3. **Income from assets:** leasing ponds, markets, shops, community halls, commons and commercial property.
4. **Borrowing:** pooled finance, bank credit or bonds for bankable projects where law, repayment capacity and State oversight permit.
5. **Partnerships:** public-private partnerships, producer/user groups and corporate social responsibility support without surrendering public accountability.
6. **Community contribution:** labour, beneficiary contribution and local philanthropy for suitable projects.

Better tax mapping, digital collection, audited accounts and empowered State Finance Commissions are prerequisites.

**Conclusion:** Panchayats are the constitutional foundation of rural self-government; diversified own revenue can increase autonomy, but cannot replace predictable fiscal devolution for public and redistributive services.

**Why this earns marks:** It assesses both democratic significance and institutional weakness, then provides a feasible, classified financing basket.

## Q16. Multiplicity of various commissions for the vulnerable sections of the society leads to problems of overlapping jurisdiction and duplication of functions. Is it better to merge all commissions into an umbrella Human Rights Commission? Argue your case.
**Primary route:** Polity → [National Commissions for SC, ST and BC](../../topics/polity/national-commissions-sc-st-bc.md) → Specialised commissions and institutional design · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Argue a position  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional and statutory bodies → Vulnerable-section commissions  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Institutional protection of vulnerable groups  
**Canonical owners:** [NHRC and SHRC](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/NHRC-and-SHRC.md) · [National Commissions—SC, ST and BC](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/National-Commissions-SC-ST-BC.md) · [Statutory, Regulatory and Quasi-Judicial Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `vulnerable-section-commissions-umbrella-body`

**Demand decoding:** Weigh overlap and efficiency against constitutional status, specialised expertise, voice and intersectionality; defend a clear institutional choice.

**Compact framework:** Case for merger → case against → preferred coordination model.

### Model answer

India has specialised bodies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, backward classes, women, minorities, children and persons with disabilities, alongside the NHRC. Their mandates can overlap where discrimination is intersectional, producing parallel notices, forum shopping and administrative duplication.

**Case for an umbrella commission**

- common investigation, research and regional infrastructure could reduce cost;
- a single complaint window could prevent jurisdictional confusion;
- integrated analysis would recognise, for example, the distinct vulnerability of a Dalit woman or disabled child;
- uniform procedures could improve monitoring and data.

**Why complete merger is undesirable**

- Articles **338, 338A and 338B** give constitutional status and group-specific mandates to the NCSC, NCST and NCBC; ordinary institutional restructuring cannot erase these safeguards.
- Different communities face distinct histories, laws and policy questions requiring specialised expertise.
- Dedicated commissions provide political voice and visibility that may be diluted in a large bureaucracy.
- The NHRC under the Protection of Human Rights Act, **1993**, already faces workload, vacancy and recommendatory-power constraints; enlargement alone may worsen access.
- Dominant or high-volume claims could crowd out smaller groups.

The better model is **coordination without homogenisation**: a common digital intake and referral system, statutory rules against duplicate proceedings, joint investigations for intersectional cases, shared regional offices and databases, and a council of commission heads. Clear jurisdiction, timely appointments and enforceable government response obligations matter more than structural consolidation.

**Conclusion:** Functional convergence is desirable, but wholesale merger would sacrifice constitutional guarantees, expertise and representative voice. A federated umbrella for coordination is preferable to one omnibus commission.

**Why this earns marks:** It argues a defensible middle position grounded in the distinct constitutional status of specialised commissions.

## Q17. How far do you agree with the view that the focus on lack of availability of food as the main cause of hunger takes the attention away from ineffective human development policies in India?
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Hunger and human development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Evaluate proposition  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty and hunger → Food access, utilisation and human development  
**Canonical owner:** [Poverty, Hunger, Food and Nutrition Security](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/02_Poverty-Hunger-Food-and-Nutrition-Security.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `hunger-food-availability-human-development`

**Demand decoding:** Give a graded agreement; distinguish aggregate availability from access, nutrition, absorption and agency, while acknowledging local supply shocks.

**Compact framework:** Availability thesis → entitlement/capability critique → policy failures → integrated response.

### Model answer

The proposition is substantially correct. National food availability is necessary, but hunger depends on whether people can **access, afford, absorb and equitably share** nutritious food.

Amartya Sen’s entitlement approach shows that people may starve despite available food when employment, wages, prices or social claims fail. In India:

- poverty, informal work and unequal land or asset ownership restrict food access;
- poor maternal health, sanitation and drinking water cause disease and reduce nutrient absorption;
- gender and caste hierarchies distort intra-household distribution and service access;
- inadequate public health, early-childhood care and education perpetuate intergenerational malnutrition;
- cereal-heavy support may ensure calories without sufficient pulses, eggs, milk and micronutrients;
- exclusion errors, weak grievance redress and irregular delivery dilute entitlements.

The Supreme Court’s right-to-food orders under Article **21**, the National Food Security Act, **2013**, ICDS and Mid-Day Meal framework recognise that hunger is an entitlement and human-development issue, not merely a production problem. Articles **39(b)** and **47** reinforce this orientation.

Nevertheless, availability cannot be dismissed: drought, conflict, supply-chain disruption, ecological stress and regional food deficits can raise prices and intensify deprivation.

**Conclusion:** Policy should combine resilient production and distribution with employment, maternal care, diverse diets, universal-quality health, water and sanitation, empowered women and accountable local delivery. Hunger is best understood as a failure of capabilities and public action, with food supply as one component.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly evaluates “how far,” uses the entitlement framework and integrates availability with access and absorption.

## Q18. The Citizens’ Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own limitations. Identify the limitations and suggest measures for greater effectiveness of the Citizens’ Charter.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Transparency, Accountability, Grievance Redress and Social Audit](../../topics/governance/transparency-accountability-grievance-redress-and-social-audit.md) → Citizens’ Charters · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2018 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tags: Identify limitations; Suggest measures  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Citizen-centric administration → Citizens’ Charter  
**Canonical owner:** [Citizen-Centric Administration](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/07_Citizen-Centric-Administration.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `citizens-charter-limitations-effectiveness`

**Demand decoding:** Diagnose design, legal, organisational and user-side weaknesses, then pair them with implementable reforms.

**Compact framework:** Promise → limitations → matched remedies → accountability conclusion.

### Model answer

A Citizens’ Charter publicly states service standards, timelines, responsible offices and grievance channels. It can convert vague administrative responsibility into a measurable promise, but most Indian charters remain weakly enforceable declarations.

**Limitations**

- standards are often drafted internally without citizen consultation and use vague language;
- charters are poorly displayed, outdated and unknown to users;
- they rarely specify individual responsibility, compensation or consequences for delay;
- grievance redress may remain within the same hierarchy;
- unrealistic standards ignore staff, process and infrastructure constraints;
- one uniform charter may disregard local language, disability and digital-access needs;
- departments monitor inputs or disposal rather than user outcomes;
- charters do not automatically cure corruption, discretion or defective procedures.

**Measures**

1. Co-design service standards with users and frontline staff after process mapping.
2. Specify measurable timelines, documents, fees, responsible officers and escalation levels.
3. Link charters to public-service-delivery laws, time-bound grievance redress, compensation and disciplinary accountability.
4. Create independent appeal and ombudsman arrangements.
5. Publish multilingual, accessible physical and digital versions with application tracking.
6. Conduct third-party audits, citizen report cards and periodic revision based on complaint data.
7. Train and resource staff; integrate charter outcomes with performance appraisal without encouraging mechanical disposal.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission advocated a citizen-centric, participative and enforceable charter approach, including the **Sevottam** model of standards, grievance redress and capability.

**Conclusion:** A charter becomes effective only when public promise, process capacity, enforceable remedy and citizen feedback form one accountability chain.

**Why this earns marks:** It pairs each major weakness with a practical institutional correction and adds the ARC/Sevottam framework.

## Q19. What are the key areas of reform if the WTO has to survive in the present context of ‘Trade War’, especially keeping in mind the interest of India?
**Primary route:** International Relations → [Globalisation, Trade Agreements and External Policy Effects](../../topics/international-relations/globalisation-trade-agreements-and-external-policy-effects.md) → WTO reform and Indian interests · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Proposed topic slug:** `wto-reform-trade-war-india`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the institutional crisis caused by unilateral trade measures and propose reforms that preserve multilateralism, development space and India’s specific interests.

**Compact framework:** Crisis → reform pillars → India’s safeguards → negotiating posture.

### Model answer

The US–China trade war exposed the WTO’s inability to discipline unilateral tariffs, subsidies and technology restrictions quickly. Survival requires restoring both legitimacy and effectiveness.

**Key reforms**

- **Dispute settlement:** preserve a binding two-tier system, fill Appellate Body vacancies, enforce timelines and address concerns over judicial overreach.
- **Negotiating function:** make consensus workable through transparent plurilateral agreements that remain open to all and do not erode members’ rights.
- **Development:** retain meaningful special and differential treatment, but use objective and issue-specific criteria rather than a crude developed/developing binary.
- **Agriculture:** correct asymmetries in historical farm subsidies; secure a permanent solution for public stockholding, transparent special safeguards and policy space for food security.
- **New distortions:** strengthen disciplines and notification on industrial subsidies, state enterprises, forced technology practices and export restrictions.
- **Emerging trade:** negotiate e-commerce and data rules without prematurely eliminating developing-country revenue or digital-industrial policy space.

For India, multilateral rules protect against power-based bargaining, but reform must preserve livelihood concerns, affordable medicines under TRIPS flexibilities, services mobility, infant digital sectors and policy autonomy. India should support coalition-building with developing countries while accepting greater transparency and targeted differentiation where capability justifies it.

**Conclusion:** WTO reform should restrain unilateralism without replacing unequal old rules with exclusive new clubs. India’s interest lies in a rules-based, development-sensitive and enforceable multilateral order.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects the trade-war context to institutional reforms and consistently tests each proposal against Indian interests.

**Proposed topic slug:** `us-iran-jcpoa-india-national-interest`

**Demand decoding:** Analyse the 2018 US withdrawal from the JCPOA across energy, connectivity, finance, security and diplomacy, then prescribe a strategic-autonomy response.

**Compact framework:** Context → interest matrix → calibrated response → strategic verdict.

### Model answer

The controversy intensified when the United States withdrew in May **2018** from the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)** and restored sanctions, although the arrangement had placed Iran’s nuclear programme under negotiated limits and IAEA verification.

**Effects on India**

- **Energy security:** sanctions threaten Iranian crude supplies, favourable payment arrangements and diversification, while instability raises oil prices and inflation.
- **Connectivity:** restrictions can delay Chabahar port and India’s access to Afghanistan and Central Asia, weakening an alternative to routes through Pakistan.
- **Trade and finance:** secondary sanctions complicate banking, insurance, shipping and rupee-based settlement.
- **Strategic balance:** escalation could destabilise the Gulf, endanger the Indian diaspora and disrupt remittances and sea lanes.
- **Diplomacy:** India faces pressure between its strategic partnership with the US, ties with Israel and Arab States, and longstanding relations with Iran.
- **Non-proliferation:** collapse of the agreement could encourage nuclear escalation and weaken confidence in negotiated regimes.

**India’s response**

India should preserve strategic autonomy: support diplomacy, IAEA verification and peaceful compliance with UN-backed non-proliferation norms rather than unilateral regime change. It should seek sanctions waivers and explicit protection for Chabahar, diversify oil suppliers and strategic reserves, develop lawful payment and insurance mechanisms, and coordinate evacuation and maritime contingencies. Continued engagement with Iran, the US, EU, Russia and Gulf partners can prevent any one relationship from becoming hostage to another.

**Conclusion:** India should neither ignore sanctions exposure nor abandon Iran. A calibrated, law-conscious and diversified policy best protects energy, connectivity and regional stability.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both limbs through a national-interest matrix and a realistic strategic-autonomy response.
