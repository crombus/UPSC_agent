## Section A — Q1. Lending hands to someone is better than giving a dole.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q1`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 1 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Agency, welfare and enabling solidarity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `agency` `welfare` `capability` `solidarity` `social-protection` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `lending-hands-better-than-dole`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Lending hands” means enabling a person to recover agency through skills, credit, public services, fair opportunity, collective support and dignified work. A “dole” is passive, one-way relief that may sustain life without changing the conditions of dependence. The comparison concerns the quality of assistance, not whether emergency transfers should exist.

Misreadings include condemning all welfare as dependency, romanticising self-help despite structural barriers, or assuming that livelihood programmes can replace food, pensions and disaster relief. The real distinction is between assistance that merely manages deprivation and support that joins immediate security to capability and voice.

### Thesis

Enabling help is generally superior because it respects dignity, builds capability and converts a recipient into an agent; nevertheless, a hand can be grasped only by someone protected from hunger, illness and catastrophe. A just society therefore combines a reliable social minimum with education, health, assets, skills, credit, institutional access and decent work. The objective is not welfare versus self-reliance, but welfare designed as a bridge to substantive freedom wherever such transition is possible.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Distinguish charity, relief, capability and structural justice.
2. Explain why agency and participation preserve dignity.
3. Connect enabling support to education, health, work, assets and collective organisation.
4. Use Indian evidence: MGNREGA, self-help groups, cooperatives and rights-based welfare.
5. Examine emergencies, disability, old age and care where continuing transfers remain necessary.
6. Address paternalism, market exclusion and the limits of skill-only solutions.
7. Synthesize a social floor with ladders of mobility and institutional voice.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Critics of doles correctly warn that discretionary handouts can breed patronage, fiscal waste and dependence. Yet the opposite slogan can blame people for poverty produced by ill health, caste, gender, landlessness or absent demand. Moreover, cash and food support may themselves enable job search, schooling and risk-taking. The synthesis is “floor plus ladder”: unconditional protection where life and dignity require it, and enabling investments that expand choices rather than compel the vulnerable to accept any work.

### Model essay

When a person falls into a river, a lecture on swimming is cruelty; when the person has been rescued, permanent confinement to the shore is another kind of cruelty. Humane assistance must first secure life and then restore the capacity to act. This is why lending a hand is ordinarily better than giving a dole: it treats the vulnerable person not as a permanent object of charity but as a citizen capable of shaping a future.

A dole relieves an immediate want through food, cash or some other transfer. Relief is indispensable when hunger, disaster, unemployment, disability or old age leaves no time for gradual empowerment. Yet if assistance stops at repeated relief, the original barriers remain. Lending a hand goes further. It may mean schooling that develops ability, healthcare that restores function, credit that unlocks enterprise, a job guarantee that creates bargaining power, or an institution through which people act collectively.

The moral difference lies in dignity. Charity often establishes a vertical relationship between giver and receiver; enabling solidarity recognises equal citizenship. A person who receives the tools and opportunity to earn, decide and contribute acquires not only income but self-respect. This does not imply that worth depends on economic productivity. Children, older persons and persons with severe disabilities possess equal dignity independent of work. It means that where agency can be enlarged, assistance should not freeze people in a subordinate identity.

The economic case is equally strong. Consumption support protects demand today, while capabilities raise productivity tomorrow. Nutrition permits a child to learn; public health prevents a family from selling assets; education and apprenticeships connect effort to opportunity. Amartya Sen’s capability approach clarifies the point: resources matter because of the real freedoms they create. Identical cash cannot produce identical freedom where one person faces disability, discrimination or inaccessible institutions.

India’s experience illustrates the continuum between relief and empowerment. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act does not merely transfer money; it creates a statutory claim to work and can build water-conservation and rural assets. Its promise is weakened when work is unavailable or wages are delayed, showing that a right requires administrative capacity. Self-help groups, including Kerala’s Kudumbashree, enable women to pool savings, obtain credit and enter local decision-making. Dairy cooperatives demonstrate how organisation can improve the bargaining position of small producers. In each case, the “hand” is institutional, not a moment of private generosity.

Education is perhaps the most durable enabling hand, but only when it produces learning rather than enrolment alone. A child cannot convert schooling into mobility if hunger, unsafe transport, discrimination or poor teaching blocks participation. Similarly, skill programmes fail when training is detached from employers and local demand. Empowerment rhetoric becomes hollow if the economy offers no decent work.

Assets and voice also matter. Land records, inheritance rights, accessible finance and digital connectivity can turn labour into bargaining power. Yet a loan without market access may become debt, and technology without literacy may create exclusion. The poor do not merely lack effort; they often face unequal prices, weak information and institutions designed without them. Lending a hand must therefore alter opportunity structures rather than simply improve individual attitudes.

There are also dangers in paternalism. A helper may decide what another person ought to become, impose conditions unrelated to public purpose or withdraw support after declaring someone “empowered.” Genuine assistance expands choice and involves beneficiaries in design. It is accountable, time-sensitive where appropriate and respectful of local knowledge.

The case for a social minimum remains decisive. Food support during distress, pensions in old age, scholarships, maternity benefits and disability assistance are not moral failures. They insure citizens against risks that individuals cannot bear alone and prevent irreversible losses of health and education. Even temporary cash can be enabling when it gives a worker time to seek safer employment or allows a woman greater control over household decisions.

The correct policy principle is therefore neither indiscriminate handout nor abandonment in the name of self-reliance. It is a floor beneath every citizen and ladders that are genuinely climbable: nutrition, health, education, care services, skills, credit, infrastructure, fair markets, social security and voice. Outcomes must be judged by whether people gain durable freedom, not by how quickly government can remove them from a beneficiary list.

A dole asks, “What can be given to this person?” A helping hand asks, “What prevents this person from standing, choosing and participating?” The first may save a day; the second can transform a life. Justice requires both compassion in the moment of need and institutions that make dependence less necessary. Assistance becomes worthy of a democracy when it protects without patronising and enables without abandoning.

**Why this earns marks:** It preserves the value of emergency welfare while defending agency, uses Indian institutions analytically, and replaces the false welfare-versus-work binary with a capability-based “floor plus ladder” synthesis.

## Section A — Q2. Quick but steady wins the race.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q2`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 2 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Speed, constancy and adaptive action · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `speed` `steadiness` `adaptation` `governance` `development` `ethics`  
**Proposed slug:** `quick-but-steady-wins-race`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The altered proverb rejects the choice between haste and patience. “Quick” signifies timely decision, initiative and responsiveness; “steady” signifies consistency, direction, discipline and the capacity to learn without abandoning purpose. Winning requires velocity joined to endurance.

Misreadings include glorifying hurried action, treating steadiness as rigidity, or reducing success to competition against others. The “race” may be personal development, public reform, scientific progress or national transformation; in each, sustainable speed matters more than either impulsive acceleration or complacent gradualism.

### Thesis

Durable achievement comes from acting early and energetically while maintaining coherent purpose, institutional continuity and corrective feedback. Speed without steadiness produces error, exclusion and reversal; steadiness without speed becomes inertia in the face of urgent need. The winning rhythm is rapid learning within a stable moral and strategic direction.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Interpret the modified proverb as a union of urgency and endurance.
2. Examine individual habit, leadership and institutional performance.
3. Apply it to development, democracy, disaster response and technology.
4. Show how pilots, feedback and course correction reconcile speed with care.
5. Test the maxim against emergencies and deliberative legitimacy.
6. Distinguish steadiness from rigidity and speed from haste.
7. Conclude with purposeful, adaptive persistence.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Some circumstances reward slowness: constitutional change, ecological intervention and irreversible technology require deliberation. Others demand immediate action: rescue, epidemic control and financial panic. The proverb remains valid only if “quick” means timely rather than reckless and “steady” means reliable rather than inflexible. Good judgment selects pace according to risk while preserving direction and feedback.

### Model essay

The familiar fable praises the slow and steady tortoise. The altered maxim—“quick but steady wins the race”—belongs to a world in which delay can itself be failure. A vaccine developed too late, justice delivered after decades or climate action postponed beyond a threshold cannot be redeemed by good intentions. Yet speed that abandons accuracy, consent or continuity may run swiftly in the wrong direction. Success therefore demands quickness disciplined by steadiness.

Quickness is the ability to recognise an opportunity or danger and act before circumstances close the window. Steadiness is the capacity to continue, coordinate and correct action without being diverted by novelty or panic. The two are complementary. Quickness provides momentum; steadiness converts momentum into distance.

At the individual level, this union appears as disciplined initiative. A student who understands rapidly but studies irregularly may lose to one who learns consistently. Yet consistency without improved method can repeat error. Excellence comes from frequent effort, timely feedback and willingness to adjust. The same is true of entrepreneurship: entry at the right moment matters, but trust is built through reliable quality and patient learning.

Institutions face a harder version of the problem. Democratic governments are criticised both for acting too slowly and for deciding without consultation. A crisis may require an executive response within hours, while durable policy requires law, finance, trained personnel and public legitimacy. The answer is not permanent emergency government. It is preparedness: institutions built steadily before crisis can respond quickly during it.

Disaster management offers a clear example. Early warning and evacuation must be rapid; resilient housing, local training, ecological restoration and communication systems require sustained investment. Odisha’s improvement in cyclone preparedness demonstrates how repeated institutional learning can make emergency speed possible. A dramatic rescue cannot substitute for years of readiness, but readiness that does not trigger timely action is equally futile.

Economic development also requires this dual rhythm. India’s Green Revolution responded to food insecurity with technological and institutional urgency. Its success in raising grain production was substantial, while groundwater depletion, chemical stress and regional concentration revealed the cost of pursuing speed through a narrow metric. The lesson is not that rapid transformation was mistaken, but that speed must be accompanied by steady ecological monitoring and course correction.

In public administration, campaign-style mobilisation can break inertia—vaccination drives, sanitation missions or financial inclusion can expand reach quickly. However, announcements and initial enrolment do not guarantee lasting outcomes. Toilets need water and behavioural change; bank accounts need autonomous use; school access needs learning. Steady maintenance and local capacity are what convert a launch into a public good.

Science similarly advances through quick imagination and steady verification. Discovery often begins with a bold conjecture, but evidence must survive replication and criticism. During a health emergency, excessive delay costs lives, while untested claims destroy trust. Adaptive trials, transparent data and post-deployment surveillance reconcile urgency with rigour.

The political temptation is to mistake visible speed for effective government. Demolition is faster than institution-building; central orders are faster than federal consultation; numerical targets are faster than changing capability. Conversely, procedure can become a refuge for avoiding decisions. Democratic steadiness requires due process and participation, but these should be designed efficiently rather than worshipped as delay.

Steadiness must also be moral. Means shape ends. A state cannot pursue development quickly by treating rights, displaced people or nature as obstacles outside the race. Constitutional values supply the lane within which speed acquires legitimacy. At the same time, invoking perfect consensus to postpone action on hunger, pollution or violence protects the status quo.

The best model is adaptive persistence. Set a clear public purpose, act early, begin at workable scale, measure results, hear affected people and correct without losing momentum. Such governance is neither a rigid five-year march nor improvisation by headline. It learns quickly and commits steadily.

Not every race has the same finish line, and society should not glorify speed for its own sake. Care, reflection and relationships resist acceleration. But where action is necessary, the winning quality is sustained responsiveness. The hare’s energy and the tortoise’s constancy need not remain enemies. Quick but steady action wins because it respects both the urgency of time and the endurance required for transformation.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the modified proverb rather than repeating the original, applies its tension across domains, and develops adaptive persistence as a qualified governing principle.

## Section A — Q3. Character of an institution is reflected in its leader.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q3`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 3 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Leadership, institutional culture and constitutional restraint · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `leadership` `institutions` `ethics` `accountability` `constitutionalism` `culture`  
**Proposed slug:** `institution-character-reflected-leader`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The topic claims a relationship, not an identity. Leaders embody priorities, reward conduct, shape appointments and signal what an institution tolerates; therefore their behaviour reveals much about institutional character. Yet institutions also select, constrain and sometimes outlast leaders.

Misreadings include a personality-centred “great man” essay, assuming every institutional failure originates at the top, or denying the role of rules and professional norms. The question invites analysis of reciprocal causation: leaders shape institutions, while institutional culture produces and disciplines leaders.

### Thesis

A leader is the most visible expression of an institution’s character because authority amplifies example and converts values into incentives. However, a mature institution cannot depend on personal virtue alone. Its true character is revealed both in the leader it enables and in its capacity to constrain, correct and survive flawed leadership through law, professional ethics, transparency and distributed responsibility.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Define institutional character as repeated norms, incentives and public purpose.
2. Explain leadership by example, appointments, priorities and crisis conduct.
3. Examine public administration, corporations, universities and civil society.
4. Show how institutions select and constrain leaders.
5. Use Indian constitutional examples of office, restraint and accountability.
6. Address charismatic leadership, bureaucratic inertia and collective complicity.
7. Synthesize ethical leadership with resilient institutional design.

### Counter-view and synthesis

An institution may remain corrupt despite an honest leader or preserve integrity under a poor one because routines, staff and external checks matter. This objection limits leader-centric analysis but does not eliminate it: leaders control signals, sanctions and succession at precisely the points where culture can change. The synthesis is that leadership is a diagnostic mirror and a causal force, while institutional character is tested by whether integrity becomes systemic rather than personal.

### Model essay

When an organisation confronts a crisis, written rules seldom answer every question. Employees watch what the leader notices, whom the leader protects and whether inconvenient truth is punished. In those moments, the leader becomes a public text from which the institution’s real values are read. Thus the character of an institution is often reflected in its leader.

Institutional character is more than a mission statement. It consists of recurring habits: whether merit defeats favour, whether dissent can travel upward, whether mistakes are concealed or corrected, and whether power is exercised for purpose or privilege. Leaders influence each habit because formal authority gives their conduct multiplier effects. A minor ethical compromise at the top can become permission throughout the hierarchy; an act of accountability can establish a demanding precedent.

Leadership operates first through example. A head who accepts scrutiny makes transparency safer for others. One who humiliates subordinates teaches fear regardless of official claims about teamwork. Second, leaders shape personnel. Appointments and promotions tell an institution whether competence, loyalty or integrity is rewarded. Third, they allocate attention and resources. What is measured and funded gradually becomes the organisation’s practical purpose.

The relationship is especially important in public institutions. The Constitution distributes authority among offices so that public power remains impersonal and accountable. A civil servant, minister, judge or police chief does not own the office; each holds it in trust. Lal Bahadur Shastri’s personal simplicity acquired institutional meaning because it aligned public office with restraint. E. Sreedharan’s association with professional execution in major transport projects similarly shows how leadership can create norms of timeliness and responsibility. Such examples should not become personality worship; their value lies in the standards they help institutionalise.

Crises expose this character most clearly. A leader who shares accurate information, admits uncertainty and protects frontline staff can strengthen trust. One who suppresses bad news may produce short-term calm but destroys learning. Corporate disasters often reveal whether safety warnings could challenge production targets. In universities, the treatment of unpopular inquiry indicates whether scholarship or conformity is the real value.

Institutional character is also visible in the treatment of the least powerful. A leader may deliver impressive targets while silencing complainants, bypassing procedure or transferring costs to contractual workers. Results matter, but the means used to obtain them disclose whose dignity counts. Ethical leadership therefore joins performance with fairness; it does not use noble ends to excuse arbitrary power.

Yet leaders do not act on blank pages. Institutions recruit and socialise them. A corrupt system can isolate an honest head through manipulated information and entrenched interests. A strong professional service can maintain standards despite erratic leadership. Rules, independent audit, legislative oversight, courts, free media, whistle-blower protection and organised employees distribute the defence of integrity.

This qualification is central to constitutional democracy. If institutional virtue depends entirely on one good individual, succession becomes a lottery. Charismatic leaders can mobilise change, but charisma may personalise authority and weaken procedure. The leader who respects limits displays greater institutional character than one who claims that personal popularity makes limits unnecessary. Restraint is not weakness; it is loyalty to an office larger than its temporary holder.

Nor should followers escape responsibility. Organisational wrongdoing often depends on silence, routine compliance and diffusion of blame. Ethical culture requires channels through which professionals can question unlawful or harmful orders. The leader sets the climate, but every member reproduces or resists it. Institutional character is collective conduct under structured incentives.

The reverse relationship also matters: the leader reflects what the institution is willing to choose and tolerate. A governing board that repeatedly rewards short-term results despite ethical breaches reveals its own priorities. Citizens who demand arbitrary shortcuts from public officials cannot later treat institutional erosion as a leader’s private defect. Leadership is partly society viewed through concentrated authority.

Reform must therefore work on both person and structure. Selection should test competence and integrity; tenure and procedure should protect independent judgment; decisions should be recorded; outcomes and conflicts of interest should be transparent; misconduct should meet credible sanction; and succession should preserve mission without preserving a personality cult. Leaders must invite truthful feedback and cultivate successors capable of disagreeing with them.

The finest leader leaves behind not dependence but a stronger institution. The truest reflection of character is not the photograph in the office but what happens when the leader is absent: whether rules remain fair, truth remains speakable and public purpose survives. A leader is the institution’s most visible mirror, but a worthy institution also holds a mirror to its leader.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats leadership as both reflection and cause, uses constitutional and organisational evidence, and makes institutional resilience—not personality worship—the final test.

## Section A — Q4. Education without values, as useful as it is, seems rather to make a man more clever devil.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q4`  
**Anchor:** Section A, printed Question 4 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Knowledge, moral agency and humane education · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `education` `values` `ethics` `knowledge` `citizenship` `technology`  
**Proposed slug:** `education-without-values-clever-devil`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The topic does not deny the usefulness of education; it warns that knowledge and skill increase the power to pursue whatever ends a person already values. Without moral judgment, empathy and responsibility, education can make exploitation more efficient. “Values” should mean reasoned constitutional and humane commitments, not indoctrination into one sectarian morality.

Misreadings include opposing science to ethics, romanticising ignorance, equating values with religious instruction, or assuming educated persons are naturally virtuous. The question asks how education can unite competence with conscience while preserving inquiry and pluralism.

### Thesis

Education is an amplifier of human agency: it can cure disease, deepen democracy and enlarge freedom, but it can also rationalise domination, engineer deception and exploit nature. Its public purpose must therefore include ethical reasoning, constitutional values, empathy, truthfulness and responsibility alongside knowledge and skill. Values cannot be added as sermons; they must be practised through pedagogy, institutional conduct and service.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Distinguish information, intelligence, wisdom and character.
2. Explain why skill magnifies both good and harmful purposes.
3. Examine science, business, administration, media and technology.
4. Locate Indian educational ideals in constitutional citizenship and plural traditions.
5. Address the risks of indoctrination and moral hypocrisy.
6. Propose experiential ethics, critical inquiry and institutional example.
7. Conclude with education as disciplined freedom directed toward human flourishing.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Schools cannot manufacture virtue, and overloading education with moral instruction may suppress independent thought. Families, communities, law and incentives also shape conduct. Yet value-neutral education is impossible: what institutions reward, exclude and permit already teaches values. The synthesis is not compulsory conformity but ethical literacy—the capacity to reason about consequences, rights, duties and competing goods in a plural democracy.

### Model essay

A trained engineer can build a bridge or design a mechanism of destruction; a skilled accountant can protect public money or conceal its theft. Education expands the range and precision of human action, but it does not by itself choose the end. When power grows faster than conscience, the educated wrongdoer may indeed become a more clever devil.

Education is useful because it transmits knowledge, cultivates skill and frees people from superstition and inherited limitation. It supports health, productivity, scientific discovery and democratic participation. The warning in the topic is therefore not against learning. It is against confusing intelligence with wisdom and certification with character.

Knowledge answers what is possible; values ask what is worthy. Technical reasoning can identify the most efficient means, while ethical reasoning examines whether the end respects dignity, justice and nature. The distinction is visible in modern history. Science has produced vaccines and weapons, communications and surveillance, agricultural abundance and ecological stress. The same intellectual capacity has served liberation and domination.

Professional life makes the problem concrete. A doctor requires scientific competence, but also consent, confidentiality and concern for the vulnerable. A civil servant needs administrative skill, yet must resist treating citizens as files. A business leader can innovate while exploiting information asymmetry or labour. An algorithm designer may optimise engagement without asking whether addiction and misinformation are being monetised. Competence without responsibility does not remain neutral; it gives harm greater reach.

Values education, however, cannot mean reciting moral slogans. Students quickly detect the contradiction when honesty is taught but cheating is tolerated, equality praised but discrimination practised, and environmental concern celebrated in institutions that waste resources. The hidden curriculum—the actual distribution of respect, voice and punishment—often teaches more powerfully than a textbook.

India’s constitutional framework offers a democratic foundation. Justice, liberty, equality and fraternity provide shared civic commitments without erasing religious or philosophical diversity. Fundamental Duties encourage scientific temper, harmony and protection of the environment. Tagore’s educational vision joined freedom and creativity with a relationship to nature; Gandhi’s *Nai Talim* connected learning, productive work and character. Their approaches differed, but both rejected education as mere credential accumulation.

Critical thinking is itself a value. It enables students to question prejudice, propaganda and unjust authority. Hence ethical education must not become obedience training. A plural society should teach learners to give reasons, hear disagreement, distinguish fact from assertion and recognise the humanity of those unlike themselves. Empathy without reason may become sentimentality; reason without empathy may become manipulation.

The pressure of competitive examinations and employability can narrow education into a race for private advancement. Employment is a legitimate purpose, especially for first-generation learners. The error lies in treating it as the only purpose. Universities and schools also prepare citizens, neighbours, professionals and custodians of common resources. A society of highly employable people who cannot cooperate across difference is not well educated.

Digital education sharpens the dilemma. Learners now receive unprecedented access to information, yet also face misinformation, surveillance, distraction and automated persuasion. Memorising more content is a weak defence. Students need media literacy, respect for privacy and the habit of checking evidence. The technologically capable citizen must also ask who designed a system, whose interests it serves and who bears its errors.

Assessment practices must reflect the same purpose. If examinations reward only recall and rank, students learn that the correct answer matters more than honest inquiry. Collaborative projects, arts, debate and reflective work can recognise judgment and cooperation, though evaluation must remain fair. Values grow when educational success includes the quality of one’s reasoning and relationships, not marks alone.

How can values be cultivated without indoctrination? Through discussion of real dilemmas; literature that enlarges moral imagination; history that confronts injustice; science taught with its social consequences; sports and group work that join competition with fairness; community service linked to reflection; and institutional procedures that model equality and accountability. Teachers require autonomy and example, not merely a prescribed chapter on ethics.

Law and incentives remain necessary. No curriculum can substitute for professional regulation, transparent institutions and punishment of fraud. Nor should every moral failure be blamed on schooling. Families, media, markets and politics shape desire and conduct. Education’s responsibility is more modest but indispensable: to equip persons to examine these influences rather than become their clever instruments.

The phrase “clever devil” captures intelligence severed from moral purpose. The answer is not less education, but fuller education—one that joins the hand, head and heart; skill with service; liberty with responsibility; and ambition with constitutional fraternity. Education reaches its highest usefulness when it teaches not only how to succeed in the world, but how to ensure that one’s success does not diminish the world.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes ethical literacy from indoctrination, integrates professional and constitutional dimensions, and explains education as an amplifier whose ends require reflective values.

## Section B — Q5. Technology cannot replace manpower.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q5`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 1 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Technology, work and irreducible human agency · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `technology` `work` `automation` `human-agency` `employment` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `technology-cannot-replace-manpower`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

“Manpower” refers to human labour, judgment, creativity, care and responsibility. The absolute “cannot replace” should be tested: machines can replace particular tasks and occupations, but not the entire human role in defining ends, exercising moral responsibility, building relationships and adapting to unstructured contexts.

Misreadings include denying automation, opposing technology to employment, or celebrating human uniqueness without examining displacement. The essay must distinguish substitution of tasks from replacement of persons and ask how gains are distributed.

### Thesis

Technology repeatedly replaces routine human tasks and can radically reduce labour demand in specific sectors, but it does not abolish the need for human purpose, judgment, care, creativity and accountability. The desirable future is neither technological resistance nor passive displacement, but human-centred augmentation: machines performing what is dangerous, repetitive and data-intensive while institutions equip people for complementary work and share productivity gains fairly.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Separate tools, tasks, jobs and the human purposes behind production.
2. Trace substitution from mechanisation to artificial intelligence.
3. Identify irreducible roles in judgment, care, creativity and legitimacy.
4. Examine Indian employment, skills, agriculture, services and digital public systems.
5. Address inequality, deskilling and algorithmic control.
6. Test the counterclaim that sufficiently advanced machines may replace most labour.
7. Conclude with augmentation, transition security and democratic control.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Technological optimists note that earlier innovations created new occupations after destroying old ones; pessimists argue that digital systems may now substitute cognitive as well as manual work. Neither historical reassurance nor technological fatalism is sufficient. The scale and speed of transition matter. Society must shape incentives, education and social protection so that task automation enlarges human freedom rather than making people economically redundant.

### Model essay

The loom replaced some movements of the hand, the tractor replaced much animal and human effort, and software now performs calculations that once occupied entire offices. Technology clearly can replace labour in particular tasks. Yet every machine embodies a human purpose, operates within a social system and leaves questions of judgment, responsibility and meaning unresolved. Technology may replace work; it cannot simply replace humanity at work.

The first distinction is between a task and a job. Jobs are bundles of activities. A machine may sort documents, detect patterns or lift heavy material while humans frame the problem, handle exceptions, communicate and accept responsibility. Automation therefore changes the composition of work before it eliminates occupations. Bank tellers, factory workers, farmers and teachers increasingly work with technologies that remove some tasks and create others.

Substitution has enormous benefits. Mechanisation can spare workers dangerous mining, toxic exposure and exhausting manual labour. Precision tools improve surgery; digital systems speed payments; remote sensing supports agriculture and disaster management. India’s space and digital public infrastructure show that technology can extend state and market capacity at scale. Rejecting such gains in order to preserve every existing task would protect drudgery rather than dignity.

Nevertheless, displacement is real. A power loom can reduce demand for particular artisans; automated production can expand output without proportionate employment; software can eliminate clerical roles. Workers bear transition costs long before society receives diffuse benefits. Age, location, education and family obligations limit easy reskilling. The statement must therefore not become a comforting denial of job loss.

Human labour remains distinctive in several domains. Care requires more than correct procedure. A nurse’s presence, a teacher’s encouragement and a social worker’s trust respond to vulnerability in ways difficult to reduce to calculation. Machines can assist diagnosis or personalise exercises, but care involves reciprocal recognition. Replacing every human encounter with an interface may increase efficiency while impoverishing the service.

Judgment is another boundary. Algorithms learn from past data and can detect patterns beyond unaided perception, yet social decisions involve values, context and contestable trade-offs. A credit model or policing system may reproduce historical discrimination. Someone must decide which errors are acceptable, explain outcomes and provide remedy. Responsibility cannot disappear into “the computer decided.”

Creativity also exceeds novelty generated by recombination. Technology can support design and produce useful outputs, but human communities determine significance. Art, scientific inquiry and entrepreneurship arise from curiosity, lived experience and purposes that are socially interpreted. Even where machines generate content, humans choose why it matters and how it should be used.

The economic issue is distribution. Technology can make each worker more productive, creating wealth sufficient for shorter hours, safer work and higher living standards. But if ownership is concentrated and labour institutions are weak, gains accrue mainly to capital while workers face insecurity. Technology then appears to replace manpower because institutions make human beings cheaper to discard than to retrain.

India’s challenge is especially complex. A young labour force needs large-scale employment even as firms compete through automation. The answer is not to prohibit modernisation. It is to build complementary capabilities: foundational education, apprenticeships, lifelong learning, portable social security and support for labour-intensive sectors where India has potential. Health, education, care, tourism, green restoration, construction and small-enterprise services can absorb human effort while technology raises quality.

Policy must also guide design. Worker participation can identify technologies that remove drudgery without removing livelihoods. Competition policy can prevent digital monopolies from capturing gains. Data and algorithmic decisions require transparency. Public investment can direct innovation toward assistive devices, clean energy, agricultural resilience and accessible services rather than automation for its own sake.

Perhaps future systems will perform an extraordinary range of physical and cognitive tasks. Even then, the social question will remain: for whose benefit, under whose authority and toward what conception of a good life? These are human and political judgments. If machines can produce abundance, society must decide whether people receive leisure and security or exclusion and dependence.

Technology cannot replace manpower in the deepest sense because persons are not merely inputs into production. They are bearers of dignity, creators of purpose and citizens entitled to shape change. The wise objective is not to keep humans doing what machines can safely do better, but to ensure that machines enlarge human capability and that no worker is treated as obsolete. Technology should replace drudgery, not human worth.

**Why this earns marks:** It concedes real task substitution, identifies irreducible human functions, and turns the prompt into a balanced argument about augmentation, transition and distributive institutions.

## Section B — Q6. Crisis faced in India – moral or economic.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q6`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 2 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Moral economy, institutions and development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `morality` `economy` `institutions` `inequality` `corruption` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `india-crisis-moral-or-economic`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The punctuation invites a choice, but the strongest answer examines interaction. An economic crisis concerns livelihoods, productivity, distribution and public capacity; a moral crisis concerns the erosion of duties, trust, fairness and regard for human dignity. In India, material deprivation can pressure conduct, while unjust conduct and captured institutions reproduce deprivation.

Misreadings include preaching about individual character, reducing morality to corruption, treating GDP growth as proof that no economic crisis exists, or blaming poverty on deficient values. The essay must connect personal conduct, institutional incentives and structural inequality.

### Thesis

India does not face a purely moral or purely economic crisis. Its central challenge is a crisis of moral economy: growth and democratic institutions coexist with deprivation, inequality, ecological stress and unequal access to justice because public and private power often separate economic decisions from constitutional responsibility. Moral renewal without material reform becomes sermon; economic reform without ethics can make exploitation efficient. India needs productive inclusion governed by justice, accountability and fraternity.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Define moral and economic crisis without exaggerating either.
2. Examine poverty, employment, agrarian stress and unequal opportunity.
3. Analyse corruption, violence, discrimination and normalisation of injustice.
4. Show reciprocal causation between scarcity, power and institutional ethics.
5. Use constitutional morality as an Indian standard.
6. Address growth achievements and everyday solidarity as counter-evidence.
7. Synthesize ethical institutions with inclusive economic transformation.

### Counter-view and synthesis

One view argues that prosperity will reduce social conflict and corruption; another that only moral regeneration can repair public life. Material security can widen choice, but wealth does not automatically eliminate prejudice or capture. Moral exhortation can inspire service, but cannot compensate for missing jobs, schools or healthcare. The synthesis joins incentives and values: fair economic structures make ethical conduct feasible, while constitutional ethics determines what growth is for.

### Model essay

India can launch spacecraft while children remain malnourished, build digital markets while workers lack security, and celebrate constitutional equality while caste and gender still shape life chances. Such contrasts make it tempting to ask whether the nation’s crisis is economic or moral. The deeper answer is that the two have become intertwined.

An economic crisis need not mean national bankruptcy. It may appear as chronic inability to provide productive employment, affordable health, quality education and resilient livelihoods to all. Aggregate growth can coexist with household insecurity. Farmers may produce yet remain indebted; graduates may possess degrees without employable capability; cities may generate wealth while housing workers in unsafe settlements. The crisis lies in the distance between economic potential and lived opportunity.

A moral crisis is not simply the presence of bad individuals. Every society contains selfishness and virtue. Crisis begins when wrongdoing becomes normal, when public office is treated as private property, when violence against the weak is rationalised, and when citizens cease expecting fairness. Corruption is one expression, but discrimination, indifference to suffering and willingness to profit from ecological destruction are equally moral questions.

The economic can produce moral pressure. Persistent unemployment may make bribery appear necessary for access; insecure work can silence protest; unequal schooling converts inherited privilege into apparent merit. Poverty does not cause moral inferiority—indeed the poor often sustain remarkable solidarity—but deprivation restricts choice and increases vulnerability to exploitation. Asking the hungry to practise heroic virtue while institutions deny basic opportunity is itself morally evasive.

The moral, in turn, shapes economic outcomes. Tax evasion weakens public capacity. Cronyism directs credit and contracts away from productive use. Discrimination wastes talent. Unsafe construction and adulterated goods convert the pursuit of profit into public harm. Ecological irresponsibility records present gain while transferring costs to future citizens. Trust, honesty and rule-bound administration are therefore forms of economic infrastructure.

India’s Constitution supplies a standard for judging this relationship. Justice—social, economic and political—appears alongside liberty, equality and fraternity. Constitutional morality asks institutions and citizens to respect procedures, rights and the equal status of persons, especially when prejudice or majority sentiment points elsewhere. It makes economic policy answerable not only to output but to human dignity.

Yet an essay of crisis must not become a portrait of national decay. India has expanded democratic participation, reduced many forms of deprivation, built scientific and administrative capability and created traditions of public service. Self-help groups, cooperatives, community kitchens, disaster volunteers and rights movements demonstrate moral resources within society. The question is why these capacities remain uneven and why institutions sometimes reward their opposite.

Market reform alone cannot settle the issue. Competition can discipline inefficiency and enterprise can create opportunity, but markets do not decide the fair distribution of health, education, bargaining power or environmental risk. Nor can the state claim moral superiority merely because it acts in the public name. Opaque discretion, delayed justice and poor service can reproduce domination. Both market and state require accountability.

Moral renewal must likewise avoid sermonising. Ethics becomes credible when embodied in institutional design: transparent political finance, reasoned public decisions, independent audit, speedy and equal justice, protection for whistle-blowers, professional codes and citizen grievance systems. Schooling should cultivate critical empathy and constitutional citizenship, while leaders must demonstrate restraint rather than merely demand sacrifice from others.

Economic reform should enlarge decent work, public health, learning, social security and access to assets. It should enable small enterprises, recognise unpaid care and ensure that technological progress does not discard workers. Environmental costs must enter economic calculation because responsibility to future generations is both moral duty and prudent development.

India’s crisis is thus not a choice between empty pockets and empty consciences. It is the failure to align wealth creation with justice and public power with service. Economics without morality can count growth while overlooking the people and nature that bear its cost. Morality without economics can praise dignity while leaving the conditions of indignity intact.

The path forward is a moral economy: productive enough to expand opportunity, distributive enough to sustain fraternity and accountable enough to preserve trust. India will overcome its crisis when success is measured not only by what the nation can produce, but by whether its institutions enable every citizen to live and act with dignity.

**Why this earns marks:** It rejects a false binary, uses constitutional morality to connect institutions and livelihoods, and balances evidence of crisis with India’s democratic and social capacities.

## Section B — Q7. Dreams which should not let India sleep.

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q7`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 3 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → National aspiration, constitutional duty and restless citizenship · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `India` `aspiration` `constitutional-values` `development` `citizenship` `future`  
**Proposed slug:** `dreams-should-not-let-india-sleep`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The topic asks for urgent collective aspirations, not a wish list or a prediction. Dreams that “should not let India sleep” are morally compelling unfinished tasks whose neglect should disturb public conscience and mobilise sustained action.

Misreadings include listing schemes, celebrating national power without human development, treating development and justice as separate dreams, or using patriotic rhetoric without priorities and mechanisms. The essay should unite constitutional ideals, material capability and responsibility to future generations.

### Thesis

India’s sleepless dream should be a republic in which every person possesses the capability to live with dignity: freedom from hunger and discrimination, excellent education and health, meaningful work, equal citizenship, ecological security and institutions worthy of trust. Scientific strength and economic power matter because they enable this constitutional transformation, not because greatness can be measured apart from people. The dream demands restless but democratic action.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Ground national dreams in the Constitution and freedom struggle.
2. Prioritise human capability: nutrition, health, learning and work.
3. Extend equality to caste, gender, region, disability and minority citizenship.
4. Join prosperity with science, innovation and ecological responsibility.
5. Examine clean institutions, federal cooperation and citizen duty.
6. Reject both complacent triumphalism and cynical despair.
7. Conclude with disciplined, inclusive national ambition.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Grand national dreams can become coercive when one vision silences plural aspirations or when symbolic projects displace ordinary welfare. Yet a democracy without shared ambition can fragment into short-term interests. The synthesis is a constitutional dream broad enough to include diverse life plans but specific about equal dignity, capability, sustainability and accountable means.

### Model essay

A dream usually brings sleep; the most important national dreams should take it away. They should trouble complacency, expose the distance between promise and reality, and summon effort across generations. For India, such dreams cannot be fantasies of prestige alone. They must arise from the unfulfilled pledge of freedom: that every citizen will possess equal dignity and a real opportunity to flourish.

The Constitution gives this dream its clearest vocabulary—justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. Political independence transferred authority to the people; the continuing task is to make citizenship equally valuable in everyday life. A nation cannot sleep peacefully while birthplace, caste, gender or wealth determines whether a child will be nourished, educated and heard.

The first sleepless dream is freedom from avoidable deprivation. Hunger is not merely lack of food; it weakens learning, health and agency. Public health cannot remain a privilege purchased after illness. Safe water, sanitation, nutrition, primary care and financial protection are foundations of freedom. India’s scientific and pharmaceutical achievements show its capability; the dream is to make that capability universal.

Education must move from enrolment to understanding. Every child should gain literacy, numeracy, curiosity, constitutional values and the confidence to ask questions. Universities should produce knowledge rather than credentials alone. An India whose demographic strength lacks learning risks converting promise into frustration. Teachers, libraries, digital tools and vocational pathways must work together, with technology supporting rather than substituting human attention.

The dream of meaningful work follows. Growth deserves celebration when it creates livelihoods of dignity and productivity. Farmers should not bear every climatic and market risk; small enterprises should be able to scale; women should not be kept from paid work by unsafe mobility and unequal care burdens; young people should encounter apprenticeships and opportunity rather than an endless queue for scarce posts. Self-reliance should mean capability, not abandonment.

Equality must become social experience. The annihilation of caste remains unfinished wherever occupation, violence or exclusion follows birth. Gender justice requires bodily autonomy, property, education, work and political voice. Persons with disabilities need accessible institutions, not sympathy without access. Religious and linguistic diversity must be protected through equal citizenship. Fraternity is the discipline of seeing another’s dignity as compatible with one’s own identity.

India should also dream scientifically. Space missions, digital public systems, clean energy, biotechnology and advanced manufacturing can enlarge national capacity. But innovation must answer public needs—affordable health, resilient agriculture, safe transport and accessible technology. Scientific temper includes the courage to test claims and change course. It opposes both superstition and technocratic arrogance.

No dream of prosperity can ignore ecology. Polluted air, dying rivers, degraded soil and climate vulnerability mortgage the future. Development and environmental protection are not competing luxuries; ecological stability supports food, health and livelihoods. India should pursue energy access and industrialisation while accelerating clean technology, efficient cities, watershed restoration and climate adaptation with justice for affected workers and communities.

Institutions determine whether aspiration becomes reality. Justice delayed, arbitrary administration, corruption and opaque political finance weaken faith in the republic. India needs police and judicial reform, professional civil services, empowered local governments, transparent regulation and cooperative federalism. Strong institutions do not obstruct decisive leadership; they ensure that public energy survives changes of leader.

Citizenship carries duties as well as claims. Public cleanliness cannot be outsourced entirely to sanitation workers; tax compliance, respect for women, rejection of caste prejudice, scientific temper and care for common resources begin in social conduct. Yet citizen responsibility must not become an excuse for state failure. Public authority possesses resources and coercive power and therefore bears a special duty of accountability.

There are dangers in national dreaming. A single imposed vision can erase India’s plurality. Spectacular projects can conceal weak schools; patriotic emotion can silence criticism. True national confidence welcomes scrutiny because correction protects the dream. Nor should India imitate another country’s model mechanically. Its path must draw strength from constitutional democracy, federal diversity and local knowledge.

India should not be kept awake by anxiety alone. It should be animated by achievable moral ambition: a well-nourished child, a fearless woman, a farmer with security, a worker with dignity, a river restored, a scientist free to inquire and a citizen able to obtain justice without influence. These are not small dreams. Together they define greatness.

The dream worth losing sleep over is not merely that India become powerful, but that Indian power become humane—capable of protecting freedom, expanding opportunity and preserving the earth for those not yet born. Such a dream forbids both despair and complacency. It asks each generation to inherit the republic unfinished and leave it more just.

**Why this earns marks:** It builds a coherent constitutional vision rather than a scheme list, integrates capability, equality, science, ecology and institutions, and qualifies national ambition through pluralism and democratic means.

## Section B — Q8. Can capitalism bring inclusive growth ?

**Stable ID:** `2015-mains-essay-q8`  
**Anchor:** Section B, printed Question 4 · 125 marks · about 1000–1200 words  
**Primary route:** Essay → [Philosophical Prompts](../../topics/essay/philosophical-prompts.md) → Markets, inclusion and democratic social contract · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Tags:** `capitalism` `inclusive-growth` `markets` `inequality` `welfare-state` `India`  
**Proposed slug:** `capitalism-inclusive-growth`

### Interpretation and possible misreadings

The question asks about possibility and conditions, not whether capitalism automatically produces inclusion. Capitalism denotes private ownership, markets, profit and accumulation; inclusive growth means expanding productivity and income while broadening access to decent work, assets, public goods, security and voice.

Misreadings include equating capitalism with any market exchange, treating redistribution after growth as sufficient, or dismissing all private enterprise as exploitation. The essay must examine how markets create wealth, why gains concentrate, and which institutions can align accumulation with inclusion.

### Thesis

Capitalism can contribute powerfully to inclusive growth through innovation, investment, competition and employment, but inclusion is not its automatic outcome. Unregulated markets tend to reproduce unequal ownership, bargaining power and opportunity, and may externalise social and ecological costs. Inclusive capitalism is therefore a political achievement: competition, labour rights, universal capabilities, social protection, progressive taxation, public goods and democratic accountability must make markets serve broad freedom.

### Multidimensional outline with transitions

1. Define capitalism and substantive inclusion.
2. Explain wealth creation through incentives, investment and innovation.
3. Examine employment, enterprise, consumers and structural transformation.
4. Analyse concentration, inherited advantage, informality and externalities.
5. Use India’s mixed-economy and post-1991 experience.
6. Compare laissez-faire, welfare capitalism and state-dominated alternatives.
7. Synthesize dynamic markets with universal capabilities and countervailing power.

### Counter-view and synthesis

Defenders argue that growth ultimately lifts living standards and that state intervention creates inefficiency and rent-seeking. Critics contend that profit depends on exploitation and capitalism cannot become inclusive. Markets have indeed reduced costs and generated innovation, while states can fail. Yet historical capitalist societies became more inclusive through political struggle, regulation and social provision, not spontaneous diffusion. The synthesis is neither market worship nor state monopoly, but democratic governance of a productive market economy.

### Model essay

Capitalism possesses an extraordinary ability to transform private initiative into large-scale production. It can also transform unequal ownership into unequal power. The question is therefore not whether capitalism creates growth—it often does—but whether the process can include those who begin without capital, skill, voice or security.

Capitalism coordinates economic activity through private property, prices, competition and the expectation of profit. Its dynamism comes from decentralised experimentation. Entrepreneurs risk resources, firms seek better methods and consumers signal demand. Innovation in telecommunications, pharmaceuticals, transport and finance has lowered costs and created entirely new possibilities. A system that suppresses initiative and price information may sacrifice both freedom and productivity.

Growth can be inclusive through several channels. Expanding firms create direct and indirect employment. Competition can make goods affordable. Credit and market access can allow small producers to grow. Structural transformation can move workers from low-productivity activity into more productive industry and services. India’s post-1991 economy expanded sectors such as information technology, telecommunications and modern services, demonstrating the gains from greater competition and integration.

Yet none of these channels operates automatically. Ownership determines who receives profits, while bargaining power shapes wages and conditions. Capital can wait, move and diversify more easily than a worker dependent on the next wage. Where unemployment is high and labour organisation weak, productivity gains need not translate into secure work. Informal workers may participate in capitalist growth without pensions, insurance or a safe workplace.

Opportunity is also inherited. A child with nutrition, good schooling, networks and collateral enters the market differently from one facing discrimination and poor public services. Market competition among unequal participants can legitimise inherited advantage as merit. Inclusive growth therefore requires capabilities before the market transaction begins.

Capitalism also tends toward concentration. Successful firms benefit from scale, finance, data and networks; they may then influence regulation or exclude competitors. Profit encourages innovation under competition but can become rent when monopoly controls access. Strong competition policy, transparent regulation and limits on cronyism are not anti-market interventions; they preserve the conditions under which markets remain productive.

Externalities present another limit. A firm may profit while pollution costs are borne by villagers, public health systems or future generations. Natural resources and unpaid care can be consumed without appearing fully in market prices. Inclusive growth must count those who bear costs as well as those who receive income. Environmental liability, labour standards and recognition of care are necessary corrections.

India’s constitutional order does not require a choice between private enterprise and social justice. The Directive Principles seek welfare, livelihood and prevention of harmful concentration, while fundamental freedoms protect lawful occupation and association. The Indian economy has always been mixed: state, market, cooperatives, households and communities jointly produce welfare. The relevant question is which institution is suited to which task and how each is made accountable.

Public goods are indispensable. Markets underprovide universal primary health, foundational education, sanitation and basic research because returns are diffuse or purchasing power is unequal. These services are not merely redistribution after growth; they create the healthy and educated citizens on whom productive capitalism depends. Social protection similarly allows workers to change jobs and take risks without catastrophe.

Taxation must finance this social contract without extinguishing enterprise. Progressive and predictable taxes, effective compliance and prudent expenditure can share the gains of growth. Redistribution alone, however, is insufficient if primary distribution remains deeply unequal. Decent wages, access to assets, quality schooling, collective bargaining, small-enterprise finance and wider ownership allow people to participate in creating value.

Alternatives also require scrutiny. State ownership can direct investment toward national goals, but monopoly bureaucracy may become inefficient and unaccountable. Cooperative forms can align producers and users, though they too need professional governance. A plural economy can use private innovation, public provision and collective ownership rather than forcing every need into one model.

Capitalism can bring inclusive growth, but the verb “can” carries the entire argument. Inclusion emerges when democratic institutions civilise markets: dispersing opportunity, restraining domination, protecting labour and nature, and guaranteeing a social minimum. Without these conditions, growth may be rapid yet socially brittle, generating wealth beside exclusion.

The aim is not to make every outcome equal or to punish success. It is to ensure that success arises from value creation rather than capture and that every citizen possesses the capability to participate, adapt and live with dignity. Markets are powerful servants but poor authors of justice. Capitalism becomes inclusive only when society democratically defines the purposes and limits within which capital may grow.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the conditional “can,” explains both wealth creation and concentration, uses India’s mixed constitutional economy, and synthesises dynamic markets with capabilities and countervailing institutions.
