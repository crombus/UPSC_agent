## Q1. Right to privacy relating to self-identity is very dear to every human being and well protected under Article 21 of the Constitution. In this context, examine the effect of the amendment in 2026, to the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Transgender Persons](../../topics/social-justice/transgender-persons.md) → Self-identification, privacy and legal recognition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Transgender Persons → Self-identification, privacy and legal recognition  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Article 21 autonomy and informational privacy  
**Proposed topic slug:** `transgender-self-identification-privacy-2026-amendment`  
**Demand:** Establish the *NALSA–Puttaswamy* autonomy baseline, identify the exact statutory changes, assess their privacy and inclusion effects, present the State’s justification, and avoid prejudging the pending challenge.

**Compact framework:** Article 21 baseline → 2026 changes → autonomy/privacy effects → State justification → sub judice verdict.

### Model answer

Article 21 protects decisional and informational privacy. *NALSA v. Union of India* (2014) recognised self-identified gender, while *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017) located identity and autonomy within privacy.

The 2026 Amendment reverses the 2019 Act’s self-declaration orientation: it deletes Section 4(2), narrows the statutory definition and makes a designated medical board’s recommendation necessary for a District Magistrate’s identity certificate. A revised certificate after surgery also entails hospital-to-authority reporting.

This medicalises an intimate identity, expands circulation of sensitive health data and excludes some self-identifying trans, non-binary and genderqueer persons from the Act’s anti-discrimination and welfare protections. The State, however, invokes verifiability, prevention of benefit misuse and stronger protection against forced transition.

**Conclusion:** The amendment pursues legitimate protective aims but uses an intrusive classification-and-certification route whose proportionality under Articles 14, 19 and 21 is doubtful. The Supreme Court challenge remains pending; the Act has not been stayed or struck down.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the precise amendment, links privacy to autonomy and data flows, states the competing justification, and preserves judicial-status discipline.

## Q2. “Women as a class neither belong to a minority group nor are they regarded as forming a backward class.” In the light of this statement, discuss the evolution of women’s reservation from local bodies to Parliament.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Women and Gender Justice](../../topics/social-justice/women-and-gender-justice.md) → Political representation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Women and Gender Justice → Political representation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Parliament and Local Government → Reservation of seats  
**Proposed topic slug:** `womens-reservation-local-bodies-to-parliament`  
**Demand:** Explain why women’s reservation rests on under-representation rather than minority/backward-class status, trace the constitutional progression, and qualify formal representation with implementation and substantive-agency concerns.

**Compact framework:** Equality rationale → 73rd/74th Amendments → State experience → 106th Amendment → operational limits.

### Model answer

Women’s reservation is a remedy for structural political exclusion across social groups, not recognition of women as a “minority” or homogeneous backward class.

The **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992)** inserted Articles **243D and 243T**, reserving at least one-third of seats and chairperson offices in Panchayats and Municipalities for women, including within SC/ST quotas. Several States later raised this to 50 per cent. This created a leadership pipeline and normalised women’s public authority, though rotation, proxy control and unequal capacity sometimes weakened substantive agency.

At the Union and State levels, repeated reservation Bills failed until the **Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023** inserted Articles **330A, 332A and 334A**, providing one-third reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, including SC/ST-reserved seats.

The amendment commenced on **16 April 2026**, but reservation awaits publication of the relevant first post-commencement census figures and a consequent delimitation exercise.

**Conclusion:** India has moved from grassroots affirmative representation to constitutionalised legislative reservation; its success will depend on timely operationalisation, stable opportunity and women’s actual influence within parties and legislatures.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the correct constitutional sequence and distinguishes enactment, commencement and electoral operation.

## Q3. Examine whether the constitutional office of the Lok Sabha Speaker has become vulnerable to partisan politics under the current anti-defection regime. What institutional changes are required to ensure the neutrality of the Lok Sabha Speaker in managing a polarised House?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Lok Sabha Speaker · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine and recommend  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Lok Sabha Speaker  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Anti-Defection Law → Adjudicatory neutrality  
**Proposed topic slug:** `lok-sabha-speaker-neutrality-anti-defection`  
**Demand:** Test partisan vulnerability through the Speaker’s dual political-adjudicatory position and propose workable reforms without ignoring parliamentary accountability.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional role → Tenth Schedule incentive → judicial evidence → institutional reforms → balanced verdict.

### Model answer

The Speaker is expected to be the House’s impartial guardian, yet is elected on a party ticket and decides politically consequential disqualification petitions under the **Tenth Schedule**.

Partisan vulnerability arises from selective delay, recognition of party leadership and control over procedure. *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) upheld the Speaker’s jurisdiction but subjected decisions to judicial review. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020) prescribed an ordinary three-month norm and recommended an independent tribunal; *Subhash Desai* (2023) clarified that the recognised whip must reflect the political party, not merely a legislative faction. These controls often operate only after political consequences have matured.

Reforms should:
- transfer defection adjudication to an independent tribunal or the Election Commission acting on a constitutional reference;
- prescribe a statutory decision period with reasoned orders;
- adopt a Westminster-style convention of post-election resignation from the party;
- strengthen all-party business consultation and transparent rulings; and
- narrow compulsory whips to confidence and core financial matters.

**Conclusion:** The office is not inherently partisan, but its present incentive structure makes neutrality fragile. Separating adjudication from floor management would best protect institutional legitimacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines doctrine, incentive analysis and feasible reforms rather than merely alleging bias.

## Q4. Is the right to vote a fundamental right? Discuss the position of the Election Commission of India while undertaking the revision of electoral rolls. Can it also examine the question of citizenship of voters?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Electoral-roll revision · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Legal discussion  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Election Commission → Electoral-roll revision  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Citizenship → Eligibility and adjudication  
**Proposed topic slug:** `right-to-vote-electoral-roll-revision-citizenship`  
**Demand:** Distinguish constitutional suffrage from the statutory right to vote, explain ECI roll powers, and draw the boundary between eligibility inquiry and final citizenship adjudication.

**Compact framework:** Legal status → ECI authority → limited citizenship check → due process → conclusion.

### Model answer

Article **326** constitutionalises universal adult suffrage, but the individual right to vote is exercised subject to the **Representation of the People Act, 1950**; the Supreme Court has generally characterised voting as a statutory right, while free and fair elections remain a constitutional basic feature.

Under Article **324** and Sections **21–23, RPA 1950**, the Election Commission may direct intensive or summary revision, correct entries and include or delete names. Citizenship is relevant because Section 16 disqualifies a non-citizen from registration.

In *Association for Democratic Reforms v. ECI* (2026), the Supreme Court upheld the Bihar Special Intensive Revision and allowed a **limited citizenship inquiry for electoral eligibility**. However, the ECI cannot conclusively determine or terminate citizenship under the **Citizenship Act, 1955**; unresolved cases must go to the competent statutory authority.

Notice, hearing, reasoned decisions and accessible appeals are essential because documentation burdens can disproportionately disenfranchise migrants and poorer citizens. Aadhaar establishes identity, not citizenship.

**Conclusion:** The ECI may verify citizenship incidentally to roll integrity, but cannot become the final citizenship tribunal.

**Why this earns marks:** It precisely separates the right’s status, revision power and citizenship-jurisdiction boundary.

## Q5. Do constitutional bodies like the National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) possess adequate enforcement powers to secure substantive social equality? Suggest measures to enhance their institutional effectiveness.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Constitutional Bodies](../../topics/polity/constitutional-bodies.md) → NCSC and NCST · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Evaluate and suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Constitutional Bodies → NCSC and NCST  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → SC/ST safeguards → Substantive equality  
**Proposed topic slug:** `ncsc-ncst-enforcement-institutional-effectiveness`  
**Demand:** Compare broad constitutional mandates and civil-court inquiry powers with recommendatory enforcement, then propose institutionally credible improvements.

**Compact framework:** Mandate → powers → enforcement gap → reforms → verdict.

### Model answer

Articles **338 and 338A** empower the NCSC and NCST to investigate safeguards, inquire into complaints, advise on planning and report to the President. During inquiry they possess civil-court powers such as summoning witnesses and requiring documents.

These are important oversight powers, but not adequate enforcement powers. Their recommendations are ordinarily non-binding; they cannot themselves prosecute, invalidate administrative action or compel budgetary compliance. Delayed appointments, uneven regional presence, limited research capacity and weak action-taken follow-up reduce impact. Substantive equality also depends on police, courts, departments and legislatures acting on findings.

Effectiveness can improve through:
- time-bound government responses to reports and recommendations;
- mandatory consultation on laws and major policies affecting SCs/STs;
- independent investigation, legal and data units with adequate staffing;
- public compliance dashboards and stronger parliamentary committee scrutiny;
- empowered regional offices and accessible complaint systems; and
- formal referral protocols with vigilance, police and prosecuting authorities.

**Conclusion:** The commissions are constitutional watchdogs, not courts. Their legitimacy requires stronger follow-up and institutional access, while coercive adjudication should remain with legally accountable authorities.

**Why this earns marks:** It avoids equating civil-court inquiry powers with binding adjudication and gives targeted reforms.

## Q6. Does India’s tribal development policy reflect the aspirations for a socially grounded and equity-based governance? Justify your answer.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Scheduled Tribes and PVTGs](../../topics/social-justice/scheduled-tribes-and-pvtgs.md) → Tribal development policy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Justify  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Scheduled Tribes and PVTGs → Tribal development policy  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Scheduled and Tribal Areas → Self-governance and forest rights  
**Proposed topic slug:** `tribal-development-equity-self-governance`  
**Demand:** Give a graded judgement by testing rights, self-governance and welfare architecture against implementation, displacement and participation.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional vision → rights/welfare instruments → implementation failures → participation-centred reform → verdict.

### Model answer

India’s policy partly reflects socially grounded governance because it recognises that tribal development requires community, territory and culture—not welfare delivery alone.

The **Fifth and Sixth Schedules**, **PESA, 1996** and **Forest Rights Act, 2006** protect differentiated governance, Gram Sabha participation and individual/community forest rights. **PM-JANMAN**, Eklavya schools and Van Dhan initiatives address PVTG services, education and forest-based livelihoods. *Orissa Mining Corporation* (2013) affirmed Gram Sabha determination of specified cultural and religious forest claims at Niyamgiri.

Yet implementation remains uneven: delayed forest-right recognition, land alienation, displacement without durable rehabilitation, weak PESA rule-making, poor health and education access, and top-down projects that reduce consultation to procedure. Tribes also differ greatly across central India, the North-East, islands and pastoral regions; a uniform beneficiary model is inequitable.

**Conclusion:** The legal architecture embodies equity and self-governance, but administrative practice remains substantially welfare-centric. Mapping habitat rights, strengthening Gram Sabhas, ensuring prior consultation, protecting land and measuring outcomes community-wise would align policy with tribal aspirations.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances named rights-based institutions against concrete implementation deficits and regional diversity.

## Q7. Malnutrition in India is not merely a public health concern; it is also a challenge of social equity, human development and effective welfare governance. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Food and Nutrition Security](../../topics/social-justice/food-and-nutrition-security.md) → Malnutrition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Food and Nutrition Security → Malnutrition  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Welfare convergence → Lifecycle delivery  
**Proposed topic slug:** `malnutrition-equity-human-development-governance`  
**Demand:** Explain the health pathway but give equal attention to distribution, capability formation and multi-sector delivery.

**Compact framework:** Health burden → equity causes → human-development effects → governance chain → integrated response.

### Model answer

Malnutrition arises from inadequate diet, disease and care, but its distribution reveals deeper inequalities of income, gender, caste, location and public-service access.

**Social equity:** Foodgrain availability does not ensure dietary diversity. Women’s unequal nutrition, anaemia, unsafe water and exclusion of migrants or remote tribal households reproduce deprivation. **ONORC** improves ration portability, but documentation and authentication failures can still exclude.

**Human development:** Stunting, wasting and micronutrient deficiency impair immunity, cognition, school participation and adult productivity, creating an intergenerational poverty–malnutrition cycle. **NFHS-6 (2023–24)** reported under-five stunting at 29.3%, showing progress but continuing deprivation.

**Governance:** **NFSA**, **Poshan 2.0/ICDS**, **PM POSHAN**, health services and WASH are administered by different institutions. Weak convergence, Anganwadi capacity, growth monitoring, referral and grievance correction turn scheme coverage into uncertain outcomes.

**Conclusion:** Malnutrition requires a lifecycle strategy—from adolescent girls and the first 1,000 days to school meals—integrating diverse diets, health, sanitation, social protection and accountable local monitoring. It is therefore a test of substantive citizenship, not only medicine.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates equity, capability and named programme-delivery mechanisms within the required length.

## Q8. Analyse the advantages and limitations of the generalist structure of the Indian Civil Services in contemporary governance.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Civil Services](../../topics/governance/civil-services.md) → Generalist-specialist debate · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Analyse  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil Services → Generalist-specialist debate  
**Proposed topic slug:** `civil-services-generalist-specialist-hybrid-model`  
**Demand:** Explain why generalism remains useful, identify where contemporary complexity exposes its limits, and offer a hybrid rather than binary reform.

**Compact framework:** Generalist strengths → complexity limits → incentive failures → hybrid reform → verdict.

### Model answer

India’s generalist civil service supplies a transferable administrative core: constitutional orientation, district-level coordination, crisis response, interdepartmental perspective and continuity across changing governments. Rotational exposure can help officers reconcile technical advice with political, fiscal and social constraints.

However, contemporary governance increasingly requires sustained expertise in digital systems, climate, health, finance, regulation and infrastructure. Frequent transfers weaken institutional memory; seniority-based progression may not reward domain competence; and a generalist can become dependent on consultants without the capacity to interrogate them. Conversely, specialists working in silos may neglect distributional and federal consequences.

Reform should therefore create a **hybrid model**:
- domain-based career tracks after a common foundational phase;
- minimum assured tenure and transparent postings;
- multidisciplinary teams with specialists in decision roles;
- calibrated lateral entry with conflict-of-interest safeguards; and
- **Mission Karmayogi/iGOT** training linked to workplace performance, not course completion alone.

**Conclusion:** Generalism remains valuable for coordination, but cannot substitute for expertise. Contemporary governance needs “specialised generalists” and administratively literate specialists under clear accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses both capability and incentive mechanisms and avoids a simplistic replacement argument.

## Q9. “IPMDA (Indo-Pacific Partnership for Maritime Domain Awareness) bridges the gap between India’s SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region) vision and the Quad’s collective Indo-Pacific strategy.” Make a critical assessment of the statement focusing on IPMDA.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [Indo-Pacific and Indian Ocean](../../topics/international-relations/indo-pacific-and-indian-ocean.md) → Maritime domain awareness · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q9 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Critical assessment  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Indo-Pacific and Indian Ocean → Maritime domain awareness  
**Proposed topic slug:** `ipmda-sagar-quad-maritime-domain-awareness`  
**Demand:** Define IPMDA’s architecture, demonstrate the institutional bridge, and qualify capability, geography, dependency and strategic-perception limits.

**Compact framework:** Architecture → SAGAR bridge → Quad value → limitations → graded verdict.

### Model answer

Launched at the **2022 Quad Tokyo Summit**, IPMDA seeks a near-real-time, integrated maritime picture using unclassified commercial AIS and satellite radio-frequency data shared through existing regional fusion centres.

The “bridge” is institutionally real. In the Indian Ocean, Quad-funded data flows through India’s **Information Fusion Centre–Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR)**, operationalising SAGAR’s cooperative-security objective while connecting it to a Pacific–Southeast Asia–Indian Ocean common picture. It supports detection of dark shipping, illegal fishing, trafficking and disaster response. The 2026 Quad initiative to develop a comprehensive Common Operating Picture further deepens this function.

However, IPMDA provides awareness, not enforcement: smaller states may lack patrol assets. Sensor and financing dependence creates capability asymmetry. SAGAR is India-led and development-oriented in the Indian Ocean, whereas the Quad is a wider minilateral; some partners may accept data while avoiding anti-China alignment.

**Conclusion:** IPMDA is a practical bridge through IFC-IOR, but not a merger of SAGAR and Quad doctrines. Its legitimacy depends on local capacity-building, data governance and an inclusive public-goods orientation.

**Why this earns marks:** It names the technology and institution that make the bridge concrete while critically limiting the claim.

## Q10. “BRICS acts as a powerful counterweight in global governance, actively amplifying the voice and influence of the Global South.” Explain the role of BRICS in projecting itself as an alternative to other groupings.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [Regional, Global and Minilateral Groupings](../../topics/international-relations/regional-global-and-minilateral-groupings.md) → BRICS · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Explain  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → Regional and Global Groupings → BRICS  
**Proposed topic slug:** `brics-global-south-alternative-global-governance`  
**Demand:** Explain BRICS’ alternative role through representation, agenda, institutions and expansion, while distinguishing reformist counterweight from coherent replacement order.

**Compact framework:** Representation → agenda-setting → institutional alternatives → expansion → limits.

### Model answer

BRICS projects an alternative less by replacing the existing order than by widening bargaining power within it.

First, its expanded membership brings major emerging economies from Asia, Africa, West Asia and Latin America into a common platform, strengthening demands for reform of the **UN Security Council, IMF and World Bank**. Second, summit coordination frames development finance, climate justice, technology access and multilateral reform from Global South priorities.

Third, the **New Development Bank** supplies development finance with greater emerging-economy ownership, while the **Contingent Reserve Arrangement** offers a supplementary financial-safety mechanism. Local-currency cooperation seeks to reduce transaction vulnerability, although it is not a common currency or full substitute for dollar-centred finance.

BRICS’ alternative credentials are constrained by China–India tensions, different political systems, unequal economic weight and consensus-based, relatively light institutionalisation. Expansion increases legitimacy but also coordination costs.

**Conclusion:** BRICS is a consequential reform coalition and supplementary institution-builder, not yet a cohesive rival bloc. Its counterweight lies in agenda pluralisation and additional policy space for the Global South.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains concrete institutions and distinguishes an alternative platform from an overstated replacement order.

## Q11. Discuss the position of the Governor in the federal polity of India. What is the nature of his power while giving assent to a bill passed by the State Legislature? Is he bound by the aid and advice of his Council of Ministers in all his functions?
**Primary route:** Polity → [State Executive](../../topics/polity/state-executive.md) → Governor · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Discuss and distinguish  
**Primary classification:** Polity → State Executive → Governor  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Assent to State Bills  
**Proposed topic slug:** `governor-federal-role-article-200-assent`  
**Demand:** Cover the Governor’s dual federal position, Article 200 options and current judicial doctrine, then answer the aid-and-advice question with a qualified “generally yes, but not always.”

**Compact framework:** Federal position → advice default → Article 200 choices → current judicial limits → reform-oriented conclusion.

### Model answer

The Governor is the constitutional head of a State under Articles **153–162**, ordinarily acting through a Council of Ministers responsible to the Legislative Assembly. Yet appointment by the President and limited discretionary functions make the office a federal link—and a recurring site of Union–State tension.

**Aid and advice:** Under Article **163** and *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974), ministerial advice is the default; the Governor is not a parallel political executive. Discretion survives only where the Constitution expressly or necessarily implies it: government formation, an objective floor test, specified Article 200 choices, Article 356 reporting and specially assigned responsibilities.

**Assent under Article 200:** On presentation of a Bill, the Governor may assent, reserve it for the President, or withhold assent and return a non-Money Bill with comments. If the Legislature re-passes a returned Bill, the constitutional text says assent shall not thereafter be withheld, though reservation may arise where constitutionally required.

The five-judge advisory opinion in **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025** clarified that the Governor chooses among Article 200 options in discretion and is not bound by State-Cabinet advice for that choice. Courts cannot impose rigid timelines, create “deemed assent” or review the merits. Nevertheless, prolonged, unexplained inaction is not a constitutional pocket veto; a limited mandamus may require discharge of the function within a reasonable time.

**Qualification:** Discretion is constitutionally bounded, not personal. *S.R. Bommai*, *Rameshwar Prasad* and *B.P. Singhal* show that gubernatorial action remains subject to constitutional purpose, federalism and limited judicial review.

**Conclusion:** The Governor is normally an advised head but exceptionally a constitutional sentinel. Transparent reasons, prompt action and adherence to Sarkaria/Punchhi norms are necessary to prevent discretion from becoming partisan obstruction.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers all three limbs, uses the controlling 2025 assent position and distinguishes discretion from unreviewable political power.

## Q12. What is meant by judicial legislation? In this context, discuss the verdicts of the apex court allowing “passive euthanasia” and a “living will”.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → Judicial legislation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Define and discuss  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Supreme Court → Judicial legislation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Fundamental Rights → Dignified death and advance directives  
**Proposed topic slug:** `judicial-legislation-passive-euthanasia-living-will`  
**Demand:** Define court-made gap-filling norms, trace the euthanasia jurisprudence, explain safeguards, and evaluate legitimacy through rights protection versus separation of powers.

**Compact framework:** Definition → constitutional basis → *Gian Kaur/Aruna/Common Cause* line → safeguards → legitimacy and limits.

### Model answer

Judicial legislation means courts formulating generally applicable norms while interpreting the Constitution or filling a legal vacuum. Under Articles **32, 141 and 142**, such norms may protect rights, but they cannot permanently replace legislative policy; *Vishaka* (1997) is the classic gap-filling model.

Indian euthanasia law evolved cautiously. *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996) rejected a general “right to die” but recognised that a dignified natural death differs from unnatural curtailment of life. In *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011), the Supreme Court permitted passive euthanasia—withdrawal of futile life-sustaining treatment—under court-supervised safeguards, while maintaining the prohibition on active euthanasia.

In ***Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018)**, a Constitution Bench held that the Article 21 right to life with dignity includes a dignified dying process. It legalised passive euthanasia and recognised an **advance medical directive or living will**, enabling a competent adult to state treatment preferences for future incapacity.

The Court created procedural safeguards: voluntary execution before witnesses, medical-board scrutiny and review before withdrawal. In **2023**, it simplified the cumbersome regime by permitting attestation by a notary or gazetted officer and streamlining the two-board process.

**Analysis:** The verdict protects bodily autonomy and prevents non-beneficial treatment; independent medical review guards against coercion, inheritance pressure and diagnostic error. Yet questions of palliative care, capacity, record accessibility and unequal hospital infrastructure require legislation and health-system support.

**Conclusion:** This is defensible judicial legislation because it operationalises dignity in a statutory vacuum with safeguards. Its long-term democratic legitimacy, however, requires Parliament to enact a clear, accessible end-of-life-care framework.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the doctrine, accurately traces three cases, explains safeguards and gives a separation-of-powers qualification.

## Q13. How far has the Indian federal framework been successful in accommodating regional and cultural diversities? Highlight the role of asymmetric federalism and suggest measures to make dispute resolution mechanisms more effective.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Federal System](../../topics/polity/federal-system.md) → Diversity accommodation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Evaluate, highlight and suggest  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federal System → Diversity accommodation  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Special Provisions → Asymmetric federalism  
**Proposed topic slug:** `federalism-diversity-asymmetry-dispute-resolution`  
**Demand:** Evaluate accommodation outcomes, explain asymmetric devices, identify limits and reform adjudicatory as well as bargaining institutions.

**Compact framework:** Successes → asymmetry instruments → persistent tensions → dispute-resolution reform → graded verdict.

### Model answer

India’s “holding-together” federation has substantially accommodated diversity by converting many identity claims into constitutionally recognised territorial and political arrangements, though success remains uneven.

**Accommodation record:** Linguistic reorganisation after 1956 allowed language to become a basis of Statehood without becoming a claim to sovereignty. Elected State governments, fiscal transfers and the Seventh Schedule enable regional priorities. Creation of States such as Nagaland, Mizoram, Jharkhand and Telangana demonstrates adaptive territorial federalism.

**Asymmetric federalism:** Differentiated arrangements allow unequal treatment for equality-sensitive purposes:
- the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** protect tribal areas through special administrative and autonomous-council mechanisms;
- Articles **371A–371J** safeguard matters such as Naga customary law, Mizoram’s religious/social practices, land, law and order, and regional development;
- Union Territories and special institutional arrangements recognise distinct strategic or administrative conditions.

This asymmetry can integrate communities by assuring identity, land and voice. However, weak implementation, overlapping jurisdictions and perceptions of exceptional privilege can create fresh disputes. Article 370’s 2019 reconfiguration and continuing Statehood questions illustrate that asymmetry is politically contingent.

**Persistent strains:** Inter-State river disputes, boundary claims, fiscal grievances, Governor–State conflict and centrally designed schemes reveal bargaining deficits. Judicial settlement is authoritative but often slow and cannot replace political negotiation.

**More effective resolution requires:**
- regularising the **Inter-State Council under Article 263** with agenda follow-up;
- strengthening Zonal Councils and issue-specific mediation;
- time-bound, technically supported river tribunals with implementation monitoring;
- transparent Finance Commission and GST Council deliberation;
- neutral gubernatorial practice; and
- early negotiated settlements backed by enforceable compliance.

**Conclusion:** Indian federalism has succeeded most when it treats diversity as a design input rather than a threat. Asymmetry must be rule-based, consultative and reviewable; disputes require continuous bargaining institutions, not episodic litigation alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates outcomes, names exact asymmetric devices and connects each proposed reform to a dispute-resolution failure.

## Q14. Examine how the separation of powers is practised in India compared to the rigid presidential model of the United States of America. In this context, compare the actual authority of the Indian Prime Minister with that of the President of the USA.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → India and USA · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine and compare  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitutional Schemes → India and USA  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Parliamentary System → Real executive authority  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-us-separation-powers-pm-president-authority`  
**Demand:** Compare institutional design and actual power, avoiding the simplistic claim that either system has complete separation or unrestricted executive supremacy.

**Compact framework:** India’s functional separation → US stricter separation → PM authority → US President authority → contextual verdict.

### Model answer

India practises functional separation with constitutional checks; the United States adopts a structurally stricter presidential separation, but neither system creates watertight compartments.

| Dimension | India | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Executive–legislature relation | Fusion: ministers are ordinarily legislators and collectively responsible to Lok Sabha | Separately elected President; cabinet cannot sit in Congress |
| Tenure | Government survives while it retains House confidence | Fixed presidential and congressional terms |
| Law-making | Executive dominates agenda; ordinances and delegated legislation, subject to review | Presidential veto and executive orders; Congress legislates and may override veto |
| Accountability | Questions, confidence motions, committees and elections | Impeachment, congressional oversight, appropriations and investigations |
| Judiciary | Judicial review plus basic-structure limitation | Judicial review under a rigid written Constitution |

**Actual authority of the Indian Prime Minister:** The PM is the real executive under Articles **74–75**, leads the Council of Ministers, controls cabinet coordination, legislative agenda and senior appointments, and may advise dissolution. A disciplined parliamentary majority and anti-defection whips can make the PM legislatively powerful. Yet coalition arithmetic, federalism, courts, party organisation and loss of confidence constrain authority.

**Actual authority of the US President:** The President is both head of State and government, Commander-in-Chief, conducts foreign policy and possesses veto, appointment and executive-order powers. However, Congress independently controls legislation and appropriations; the Senate confirms major appointments and treaties; courts review executive action; and divided government can block the President’s programme.

**Qualification:** The US model is “rigid” institutionally, not politically absolute; bargaining is indispensable. India’s formal fusion does not erase judicial, federal and bicameral checks, but majority government often enables faster agenda control.

**Conclusion:** The Indian PM may exercise greater day-to-day legislative authority when commanding a stable majority, while the US President possesses greater independent tenure and executive identity but faces stronger institutional separation.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares design and practical authority across identical dimensions and supplies a qualified, non-formulaic verdict.

## Q15. To what extent do pressure groups, social movements and corporate lobbies deepen pluralistic democracy in India by representing excluded interests? Analyse whether the growing convergence of corporate wealth and political power poses a threat to the autonomy of formal democratic institutions.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Pressure Groups](../../topics/polity/pressure-groups.md) → Pluralism and capture · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Extent and threat analysis  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Pressure Groups → Pluralism and capture  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Transparency and accountability → Political finance and lobbying  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pressure-groups-corporate-lobbies-democratic-capture`  
**Demand:** Evaluate participatory benefits and unequal-resource risks, then assess institutional capture without assuming all business participation is illegitimate.

**Compact framework:** Pluralist contribution → examples → wealth-power convergence → institutional threat → transparency reforms → verdict.

### Model answer

Pressure groups and movements deepen democracy by supplying continuous representation between elections, but pluralism is democratic only when access is sufficiently open and influence is visible.

**Democratic contribution:** Farmer and labour organisations aggregate dispersed interests; women’s and disability movements convert lived exclusion into legal reform; environmental movements such as the Narmada Bachao Andolan place displacement and ecological costs on the public agenda. Business associations contribute sectoral knowledge, investment constraints and implementation feedback. Articles **19(1)(a), (b) and (c)** protect speech, assembly and association, subject to reasonable restrictions.

Their value is therefore causal: affected-group testimony improves problem definition; mobilisation lowers collective-action barriers; consultation can make policy feasible; and litigation subjects decisions to legal review.

**Unequal pluralism:** Corporate actors possess finance, expertise, media access and repeated contact unavailable to informal workers or dispersed citizens. Opaque lobbying, revolving doors, concentrated media ownership, procurement influence and campaign finance can shift agendas before formal debate begins. The Supreme Court’s **2024 electoral-bonds judgment** invalidated anonymous bond-based political funding, linking voter information to meaningful electoral choice.

Capture threatens institutional autonomy when regulators depend on regulated industries, parties become financially beholden, consultation excludes countervailing voices, or legislatures merely ratify privately negotiated outcomes. Yet wealth–policy interaction alone does not prove capture; transparent expertise can improve governance.

**Reforms:** India needs a general lobbying-disclosure framework covering clients, meetings and expenditure; transparent political donations; conflict-of-interest and cooling-off rules; plural pre-legislative consultation under the **2014 policy**; reasoned publication of accepted/rejected submissions; and stronger independent regulators and parliamentary committees.

**Conclusion:** Organised interests are democratic infrastructure, not democratic substitutes. They deepen pluralism when participation is inclusive and reasons are public; they erode it when wealth purchases hidden access and disables institutional independence.

**Why this earns marks:** It links representation and capture to mechanisms, evidence and qualified reforms rather than treating all lobbying as corruption.

## Q16. “Transparency and accountability in governance are not about controlling corruption but about creating the trust of stakeholders in the policy process by following the Rule of Law and Participatory Governance.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Transparency, Accountability, Grievance Redress and Social Audit](../../topics/governance/transparency-accountability-grievance-redress-and-social-audit.md) → Trust and legitimacy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Transparency and Accountability → Trust and legitimacy  
**Proposed topic slug:** `transparency-accountability-trust-participatory-governance`  
**Demand:** Accept the wider trust proposition while showing that anti-corruption remains a necessary component and that participation without legal accountability can be tokenistic.

**Compact framework:** Beyond corruption → rule-of-law trust → participation trust → accountability chain → limitations → verdict.

### Model answer

The statement is substantially correct: corruption control is one outcome of transparency and accountability, whereas their deeper purpose is to make public power explainable, contestable and predictable.

**Rule of Law creates reliability.** Published rules, equal application, recorded reasons and independent review reduce arbitrary discretion. The **RTI Act, 2005**, especially proactive disclosure under Section 4, enables citizens to know the basis of decisions. Audit by the **CAG**, legislative committees and judicial review connects authority to legal limits. This builds trust even when a stakeholder dislikes the final policy.

**Participation creates procedural legitimacy.** Pre-legislative consultation, Gram Sabha deliberation, public hearings and **MGNREGA social audit under Section 17** allow affected persons to contribute knowledge and challenge implementation. The **Meghalaya Community Participation and Public Services Social Audit Act, 2017** extends participatory verification beyond one scheme. Participation can reveal exclusion that financial audit misses.

**Accountability completes the chain:** information → answerability → correction or sanction. **CPGRAMS**, departmental appeals and grievance authorities matter only when complaints receive reasoned, time-bound remedies. A dashboard or disclosure without correction may increase distrust.

**Qualification:** Transparency can invade privacy or overwhelm decision-making; exemptions and data minimisation are legitimate when narrowly applied. Participation can be captured by organised elites, and consultation cannot displace elected responsibility. Anti-corruption investigation and punishment remain essential because trust without integrity is unsustainable.

**Conclusion:** Trust arises not from publicity alone but from citizens seeing lawful procedure, genuine voice, reasoned decisions and correctable error. Thus, transparency and accountability are institutions of democratic legitimacy whose anti-corruption effect is necessary but not exhaustive.

**Why this earns marks:** It tests the quotation through named legal mechanisms and explains how each produces—or fails to produce—trust.

## Q17. Can the constitutional mandate of rights-based welfare be effectively realised in the context of non-integrated governance and minimal public investment? Examine.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Welfare State](../../topics/social-justice/welfare-state.md) → Rights-based welfare · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Examine  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Welfare State → Rights-based welfare  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Scheme convergence → Public investment and delivery architecture  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rights-based-welfare-convergence-public-investment`  
**Demand:** Explain why legal entitlement is necessary but insufficient, trace fragmentation and underinvestment through the rights-to-capability chain, and state institutional and fiscal conditions for effective realisation.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional mandate → enforceable rights → fragmentation failure → investment failure → integrated reform → verdict.

### Model answer

Rights-based welfare converts beneficiaries into claim-holders and the State into a duty-bearer, but a legal entitlement cannot produce capabilities without coordinated institutions and adequate public resources.

The constitutional basis lies in dignity and equality under Articles **14 and 21**, read with Directive Principles such as Articles **38, 39, 41, 45 and 47**. Statutes including **MGNREGA, the NFSA, RTE Act** and rights recognised through health and shelter jurisprudence translate parts of this mandate into enforceable claims.

**Non-integrated governance breaks the delivery chain.** Nutrition depends on food, health, water, sanitation and care, yet these sit in separate departments and databases. A child may possess an entitlement but face an absent Anganwadi worker, unsafe water and weak referral care. Migrants encounter non-portable records; Centre–State and rural–urban fragmentation creates accountability gaps. Multiple portals may duplicate beneficiaries while excluding those with authentication or documentation failures.

**Minimal investment hollows out rights.** Schools without trained teachers, health centres without personnel, underfunded social assistance and delayed wage payments reduce a right to nominal coverage. Private substitution then makes access depend on income, contradicting substantive equality. Expenditure, however, is not itself an outcome; procurement quality, frontline incentives and local capacity determine conversion.

**Realisation requires:** outcome-linked but adequate public financing; common beneficiary and service pathways with privacy safeguards; portability; district-level convergence teams; decentralised planning; interoperable—not indiscriminately merged—data; social audits, grievance redress and independent evaluation; and transparent Centre–State responsibility.

**Conclusion:** Rights can survive fragmented and underfunded governance on paper, but not in lived experience. Effective welfare requires a rights-to-capability chain joining law, finance, personnel, convergence, participation and remedy.

**Why this earns marks:** It links constitutional and statutory claims to concrete fiscal and administrative failure mechanisms, while qualifying spending with governance quality.

## Q18. Should education be treated primarily as a welfare obligation of the state or as a strategic investment for building a globally competitive, knowledge-driven nation? Critically evaluate.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Education and Human Resource Development](../../topics/social-justice/education-and-human-resource-development.md) → Rights and strategic investment · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Critical evaluation  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Education and Human Resource Development → Rights and strategic investment  
**Proposed topic slug:** `education-welfare-right-strategic-investment`  
**Demand:** Evaluate both frames, expose their risks and resolve the apparent binary through an equity-first knowledge-capability strategy.

**Compact framework:** Welfare/right case → strategic-investment case → dangers of each → synthesis across stages → verdict.

### Model answer

Treating education as either welfare or investment creates a false dichotomy. Its public purpose is simultaneously equal citizenship, individual capability and national productive capacity.

**Welfare obligation:** Article **21A** and the **RTE Act, 2009** make free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14 a legal duty. Articles **45 and 46** reinforce early-childhood and weaker-section concerns. Public provision corrects market exclusion by income, gender, caste, disability, language and geography. School meals under **PM POSHAN** also connect education with nutrition and attendance.

Yet a welfare-only frame may prioritise enrolment and infrastructure while tolerating weak learning. Attendance is not literacy, and certification is not capability.

**Strategic investment:** Foundational learning under **NIPUN Bharat**, teacher capacity, vocational pathways, universities, research, digital infrastructure and industry–academia links raise productivity, innovation and adaptability. **NEP 2020** seeks multidisciplinary education and research orientation. Global competitiveness built on unequal foundational schooling is neither efficient nor legitimate. Digital delivery without devices, language support or disability access can widen gaps.

**Synthesis:** The State should guarantee an equitable quality floor—early childhood care, foundational literacy, trained teachers, inclusion, safe schools and affordable higher education—while strategically investing in research, skills and innovation. Public finance should be paired with learning measurement, institutional autonomy and accountability, not reduced to either spending targets or rankings.

**Conclusion:** Education is a welfare obligation in access and equality, and a strategic investment in quality and knowledge creation. The former supplies legitimacy; the latter supplies transformative capacity.

**Why this earns marks:** It critically tests both frames, uses constitutional and programme evidence, and resolves the binary through a stage-sensitive synthesis.

### Model answer

India’s diaspora can convert mobility and cultural familiarity into economic and diplomatic connectivity, but it is an autonomous, heterogeneous population—not an instrument the Indian State can command.

Remittances stabilise household consumption and external finances. Entrepreneurs and professionals also connect Indian firms to capital, technology and markets; however, remittances are private transfers, not proof of productive investment.

## Q20. “China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has transformed South Asia from a regional space into a theatre of great power competition.” Analyse the strategic implications of the BRI for India’s security and regional influence in South Asia.
**Primary route:** International Relations → [India, China, Major Powers and Resilient Supply Chains](../../topics/international-relations/india-china-major-powers-and-resilient-supply-chains.md) → BRI and South Asia · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-II · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Strategic analysis  
**Primary classification:** International Relations → India–China Relations → BRI and South Asia  
**Proposed topic slug:** `bri-india-security-regional-influence-south-asia`  
**Demand:** Explain BRI instruments, differentiate country experiences, analyse security and influence effects, and avoid deterministic “debt trap” or “string of pearls” claims.

**Compact framework:** BRI mechanism → CPEC sovereignty → country differentiation → security effects → influence effects → Indian response.

### Model answer

BRI turns infrastructure deficits into strategic competition because Chinese finance, state-owned construction capacity, ports, energy and digital systems can simultaneously generate development gains and durable political or security access.

**Direct security implications**
- **Sovereignty:** The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor passes through territory in the former State of Jammu and Kashmir under Pakistan’s control, making India’s objection categorical.
- **Two-front and maritime pressure:** Deeper China–Pakistan integration and **Gwadar** connect continental rivalry with Arabian Sea access.
- **Dual-use risk:** Ports, airports, grids, cables and surveillance systems may support logistics or intelligence. Dual-use potential warrants scrutiny, but does not itself prove militarisation.
- **Technology dependence:** Chinese telecom and digital infrastructure can create cybersecurity, data and vendor-lock-in concerns.

**Regional influence:** Visible project delivery gives South Asian governments alternatives to Indian finance and transit, increasing their bargaining space. Sri Lankan port and urban projects, Maldivian infrastructure, Bangladeshi power and transport links, and proposed Nepal connectivity illustrate different national calculations. These States hedge and diversify; they are not passive pieces in an India–China contest.

The “debt-trap” label is therefore too broad. Debt stress reflects project viability, domestic choices, exchange rates and external shocks; nevertheless, opaque, foreign-currency and low-return projects can create restructuring dependence and sovereignty sensitivity.

**India’s response** should be competitive rather than prohibitory: timely, demand-driven neighbourhood projects; grants and transparent Lines of Credit; **Neighbourhood First, SAGAR/MAHASAGAR, BIMSTEC and BBIN**; stronger maritime-domain awareness; and viable alternatives such as Chabahar, while recognising that proposed corridors such as IMEC are not yet substitutes for operating infrastructure.

**Conclusion:** BRI has intensified great-power competition, but India’s influence will depend less on demanding exclusivity than on reliability, consultation, financial sustainability and regional public goods.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates sovereignty, dual-use, debt and partner-agency questions and converts diagnosis into a credible Indian strategy.
