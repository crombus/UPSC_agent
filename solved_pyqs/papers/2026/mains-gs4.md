## Q1(a). AI-generated Ph.D. evaluation
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Emerging Ethics—AI](../../topics/ethics/emerging-ethics-ai.md) → Non-delegable judgment, accountability and academic integrity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Owing to paucity of time, a university professor generates a Ph.D. evaluation report using Artificial Intelligence and submits it with some modifications. Discuss this from the perspective of accountability and integrity. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Emerging Ethics—AI · Subtopic: Non-delegable judgment, accountability and academic integrity · Proposed slug: `ai-phd-evaluation-accountability-integrity` · Demand: Discuss

**Demand:** Separate accountability from integrity; avoid a generic bias answer.

### Model answer

AI may assist clerical work, but evaluating a doctorate is a **non-delegable scholarly judgment**. Accountability needs an identifiable actor, standard, explanation, review and correction; an AI cannot defend reasons or bear sanctions. The professor retains responsibility; automation merely conceals or diffuses it.

Integrity is independently breached. Minor edits do not transform machine output into the examiner’s careful reading. Submitting it as personal evaluation misrepresents authorship and violates fidelity to the candidate and university. UNESCO’s 2023 generative-AI guidance similarly retains human accountability; the ICMJE accountability criterion is a useful analogy, not governing law.

Time pressure explains but cannot justify the act. Ethical alternatives were an extension, reduced workload, declining the assignment, or disclosed AI assistance limited by institutional rules. Even where permitted, the professor must read the thesis, verify every claim, document assistance and independently own the reasons.

**Conclusion:** Efficiency cannot replace the personal judgment for which academic authority was entrusted.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies two distinct wrongs, adds a precise institutional anchor and offers realistic alternatives.

## Q1(b). Forest-community displacement
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Environmental and Administrative Ethics](../../topics/ethics/environmental-and-administrative-ethics.md) → Displacement, consent and forest rights · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** A business house is working on a project that could displace a forest community from their habitat. As the administrative officer of that district, what ethical challenges are you likely to face? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Environmental and Administrative Ethics · Subtopic: Displacement, consent and forest rights · Proposed slug: `forest-community-displacement-ethical-challenges` · Demand: Identify challenges

### Model answer

The officer faces development benefits—jobs, revenue and infrastructure—against rights that cannot be reduced to aggregate utility.

Challenges include **procedural justice**, because information gaps, illiteracy or pressure may manufacture consent; **distributive justice**, because benefits are dispersed while losses of land and livelihood are concentrated; and **recognition justice**, because compensation cannot recreate sacred geography, identity or community relations. Ecological and intergenerational duties also arise.

The officer is simultaneously project facilitator, statutory-rights protector and law-and-order authority. Corporate or political pressure may threaten impartiality; delay can harm investment, while haste can cause irreversible injustice. The Forest Rights Act, Gram Sabha powers, PESA consultation where applicable, the 2013 land-acquisition law’s social-impact architecture and the Niyamgiri precedent anchor a fair process.

The ethical deliverable is **process integrity**: informed participation, independent assessment, fair rehabilitation, disclosure, reasoned decision and a genuine possibility of modification or rejection.

**Why this earns marks:** It classifies procedural, distributive, cultural and role conflicts rather than prematurely deciding the project.

## Q2(a). Untested supplement for an endangered tribe
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Bioethics and Administrative Ethics](../../topics/ethics/bioethics-and-administrative-ethics.md) → Vulnerable populations and unproven interventions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** An endangered tribe has developed a severe skeletal deformity. A university research has identified a mineral supplement as a possible remedy, though clinical trials are yet to be conducted. Should the District Magistrate (DM) use this research on the tribe? Discuss from the perspective of medical and administrative ethics. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Bioethics and Administrative Ethics · Subtopic: Vulnerable populations and unproven interventions · Proposed slug: `untested-supplement-tribe-ethics` · Demand: Decide and discuss

### Model answer

The DM should **not order population-wide use**. Systematic administration of an unvalidated intervention is research disguised as welfare, and the DM lacks therapeutic authority.

Medical ethics requires scientific equipoise, informed and culturally intelligible consent, non-maleficence, fair selection and independent review. Isolation, dependence on the state and endangered status create a vulnerability multiplier; apparent consent may reflect authority pressure or therapeutic misconception. Good intent cannot replace evidence.

Administrative ethics adds legality, role competence, recorded reasons and protection from exploitation of a “vulnerable and available” population. The ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017), Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethics scrutiny provide the route.

The DM should urgently arrange diagnosis, nutrition and established care, seek specialist/regulator advice, and facilitate an ethics-approved trial only with community engagement, individual consent, monitoring, compensation and post-trial access. Urgency justifies accelerating the lawful pathway—not bypassing it.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes treatment from research and combines bioethical thresholds with administrative competence.

## Q2(b). National security and human rights
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Security Ethics](../../topics/ethics/security-ethics.md) → Proportionality, non-derogable rights and oversight · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Discuss how national security can be balanced with concerns of human rights. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Security Ethics · Subtopic: Proportionality, non-derogable rights and oversight · Proposed slug: `security-human-rights-proportionality` · Demand: Discuss

### Model answer

Security and rights are mutually reinforcing: security protects liberty, while rights-compliant security preserves legitimacy and community cooperation.

Restrictions should pass **legality**, legitimate aim, necessity/least-restrictive means and proportionality *stricto sensu*. A non-derogable core—life, dignity and freedom from torture—cannot be traded away. The ICCPR’s derogation discipline and Siracusa Principles support this approach.

Surveillance, detention and communication restrictions require written reasons, narrow targeting, time limits, data minimisation, independent authorisation where feasible, judicial/parliamentary scrutiny and remedies. Sunset clauses and periodic review prevent emergency powers becoming permanent. *D.K. Basu* safeguards illustrate that procedure strengthens lawful policing.

Unrestrained coercion produces alienation, poor intelligence and impunity; excessive restraint may expose citizens to violence. The ethical balance is therefore calibrated and reviewable state action—not compromise between legality and expediency.

**Why this earns marks:** It turns “balance” into an executable test and explains why rights can improve security.

## Q3(a). Ambedkar and civil disobedience
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Indian Moral Thinkers](../../topics/ethics/indian-moral-thinkers.md) → Constitutional morality and civil disobedience · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Dr. B. R. Ambedkar had cautioned that employing civil disobedience within a constitutional democracy equates to endorsing ‘anarchy’. How should modern democracies navigate the ethical dilemmas posed by civil disobedience movements that aim to promote social justice against policies that may raise genuine concerns? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Indian Moral Thinkers · Subtopic: Constitutional morality and civil disobedience · Proposed slug: `ambedkar-civil-disobedience-democracy` · Demand: Navigate ethical dilemmas

### Model answer

On 25 November 1949, Ambedkar condemned unconstitutional methods as the “Grammar of Anarchy” **where constitutional methods are open**, while recognising justification when such channels are absent. Legality alone therefore cannot settle the ethics.

A movement gains moral credibility when it addresses clear, substantial injustice; exhausts normal appeals in good faith; remains public and non-violent; uses proportionate disruption; and accepts legal consequences. These broadly reflect Rawls’s conditions and Martin Luther King Jr.’s sequence of fact-finding, negotiation, self-purification and direct action.

The state has a reciprocal duty: keep courts, legislatures, consultation and protest spaces genuinely accessible; facilitate peaceful dissent; negotiate; give reasoned policy responses; and use least-restrictive policing. Violence may be prosecuted, but dissent must not be criminalised by association.

**Conclusion:** Democracy should neither romanticise lawbreaking nor fetishise order; it must assess both the justice of the claim and the openness of remedies.

**Why this earns marks:** It accurately qualifies Ambedkar and allocates duties to movement and state.

## Q3(b). Gandhian trusteeship in administration
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Indian Moral Thinkers](../../topics/ethics/indian-moral-thinkers.md) → Trusteeship as administrative practice · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** In what ways can an Indian Administrative Officer apply Gandhi’s notion of ‘trusteeship’ to ensure fairness in governance? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Indian Moral Thinkers · Subtopic: Trusteeship as administrative practice · Proposed slug: `gandhian-trusteeship-governance` · Demand: Apply

### Model answer

Gandhian trusteeship rejects morally absolute ownership: power and surplus resources are held in stewardship for social welfare, while permitting an honourable livelihood. An administrator should treat office—not citizens—as the officer’s asset.

Application means using **public money** as a trust corpus through outcome budgeting and audit; exercising discretion by published criteria; sharing information through disclosure and reasoned orders; conserving natural resources for present communities and future generations; using Gandhi’s talisman to prioritise Antyodaya; preventing conflicts of interest; and leaving transparent records and stronger institutions to successors.

For example, rehabilitation funds should follow participation, social audit and grievance review, not official benevolence. The officer renders account to beneficiaries as well as superiors.

Trusteeship’s limitation is its voluntary moral basis: without RTI, audit, entitlements and conflict-of-interest rules, it can become paternalism. Moral stewardship therefore needs institutional scaffolding.

**Why this earns marks:** It operationalises the thinker and adds a structural qualification.

## Q3(c). Just intention and force
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Governance and International Ethics](../../topics/ethics/governance-and-international-ethics.md) → Just intention, legitimate force and accountability · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Shakespeare in Henry IV says, “The arms are fair when the intent of bearing them is just”. What ethical implications does this have in governance? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Governance and International Ethics · Subtopic: Just intention, legitimate force and accountability · Proposed slug: `just-intention-force-governance` · Demand: Explain implications

### Model answer

The line from *Henry IV, Part 1* (V.ii), spoken by Hotspur before his failed rebellion, warns against allowing power to certify its own virtue. Just intention is relevant but cannot alone make coercion fair.

Force must also satisfy **legitimate authority, just cause, necessity, last resort, reasonable prospect of success, proportionality and discrimination**. In policing this requires minimum force, recorded reasons, medical/procedural safeguards, reporting and independent review. The doctrine of double effect asks whether foreseeable collateral harm is unintended, proportionate and minimised—not merely relabelled.

Otherwise benevolent claims become alibis: preventive detention “for order”, censorship “for harmony”, or excess “for safety”. Conversely, lawful authority without public-regarding intention becomes repression.

**Conclusion:** Intent supplies direction; externally testable standards and accountability supply legitimacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses the quotation’s dramatic irony and translates just-war criteria into governance.

## Q4(a). Paternalism and beneficence
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories and Bioethics](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories-and-bioethics.md) → Autonomy, beneficence, paternalism and undue influence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Citing the serious adverse effects of long-term medication, a doctor convinces the family of a female patient for surgery. This led the patient to reluctantly consent for the surgery, though her original choice was to opt for medication. Explain the concepts of paternalism and beneficence by analysing the doctor’s action. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Moral Theories and Bioethics · Subtopic: Autonomy, beneficence, paternalism and undue influence · Proposed slug: `paternalism-beneficence-reluctant-consent` · Demand: Explain by analysis

### Model answer

**Beneficence** is the duty to promote welfare; **paternalism** overrides or constrains autonomous choice for a person’s supposed good. They may share motive but differ on respect for autonomy.

The doctor fulfils beneficence by explaining medication risks and recommending surgery. Persuading the **family** to pressure a competent adult, however, risks hard paternalism and undue influence. “Reluctant consent” is a warning, not automatic invalidity: capacity, disclosure of alternatives and risks, voluntariness and freedom from manipulation must be tested.

*Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda* (2008) supports the competent patient as the locus of consent and disclosure of alternatives; it does not make every reluctant choice invalid. A defensible process counsels the patient directly, keeps family supportive rather than substitutive, allows time and a second opinion, documents reasons, and respects informed refusal unless capacity is impaired.

**Conclusion:** Beneficence advises; unjustified paternalism substitutes.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact autonomy boundary and uses precedent cautiously.

## Q4(b). Efficiency, effectiveness and productivity
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Work Culture and Public Funds](../../topics/ethics/work-culture-and-public-funds.md) → Efficiency-effectiveness-productivity bridge · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Efficiency is doing things right, while effectiveness is doing the right thing. How do you strike a balance between the two to enhance productivity? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Work Culture and Public Funds · Subtopic: Efficiency-effectiveness-productivity bridge · Proposed slug: `efficiency-effectiveness-productivity` · Demand: Explain balance

### Model answer

As Drucker put it, efficiency concerns execution; effectiveness concerns objectives. Public productivity rises when **effectiveness sets the numerator**—valuable outcomes—and **efficiency disciplines the denominator**—time, money and effort—while equity constrains both.

First validate the objective through citizen need, legality and a theory of change; then simplify processes and reduce waste. Use a balanced dashboard combining outputs, outcomes, quality, timeliness, inclusion and user experience. Outcome budgeting and CAG performance audit test whether savings improved public value.

An efficient but ineffective office may rapidly process applications while excluding eligible citizens—the costliest failure because dashboards show success. Goodhart’s and Campbell’s laws warn that single targets invite gaming; Merton’s goal displacement turns means into ends. Field verification, plural indicators and periodic revalidation are essential.

**Conclusion:** Do the right public task first, then do it economically—never economise the purpose out of the programme.

**Why this earns marks:** It supplies the productivity bridge, named traps and administrative instruments.

## Q5(a). Remote schools: rules and educational rights
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Service Delivery and Regulatory Ethics](../../topics/ethics/service-delivery-and-regulatory-ethics.md) → Responsive regulation in education · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Usually schools, working in remote areas, do not fully comply with government regulations. However, if the rules are enforced strictly, it would lead to most schools closing down. How should an administrator strike a balance between enforcement of rules and educational rights of children? **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Service Delivery and Regulatory Ethics · Subtopic: Responsive regulation in education · Proposed slug: `remote-schools-compliance-education-rights` · Demand: Balance

### Model answer

The conflict is not rules versus children, but the child’s right to **safe, quality education** versus continued **access**. Blanket closure extinguishes access; blanket forbearance hollows out quality.

Use risk-based triage. Structural safety, drinking water, toilets, child protection and minimum teacher competence are non-negotiable. Remediable capacity gaps should receive time-bound improvement plans, grants, shared facilities and training. Enforcement should escalate: counselling → warning → improvement notice → proportionate penalty → suspension/withdrawal for persistent or grave breach.

Compliance status and timelines should be public and independently inspected. Closure must follow assured placement of every child in an accessible neighbourhood school. The RTE framework and NEP’s “light but tight” philosophy support firm standards with facilitative implementation.

**Conclusion:** Ethical regulation is strict about harm, supportive about capacity and uncompromising about the child—not mechanically about paperwork.

**Why this earns marks:** It rejects both extremes and gives a workable triage-and-escalation framework.

## Q5(b). Ethics and strategic interests
**Primary route:** Ethics → [International Ethics](../../topics/ethics/international-ethics.md) → Realism, national interest and restraint · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** In international relations, ethical considerations are often subjugated to the strategic interests of nation-states. Discuss this from an ethical perspective. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: International Ethics · Subtopic: Realism, national interest and restraint · Proposed slug: `international-ethics-strategic-interest` · Demand: Discuss

### Model answer

The claim is descriptively strong: international anarchy encourages self-help, and governments have a fiduciary duty to protect citizens. Kautilyan prudence and realism therefore prioritise survival.

Yet prudence is not amorality. Strategic action remains bounded by civilian immunity, treaties and proportionality; unavoidable “dirty hands”, in Walzer’s sense, should be acknowledged rather than redefined as virtue. Ethical reliability also creates strategic assets—credibility, soft power, coalition access and reciprocal compliance. Humanitarian law, multilateral review and reputational costs make breach materially consequential.

India’s Panchsheel, disaster relief and strategic autonomy illustrate an ethical middle: independent pursuit of interest without accepting that any means is permissible. Moral rhetoric without consistency, however, becomes selective idealism.

**Conclusion:** Ethics does not replace national interest; it disciplines means, broadens the time horizon and makes interest legitimate and durable.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances realism, constraints and interest convergence.

## Q6(a). Empathy versus transfer rules
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Emotional Intelligence and Ethical Dilemmas](../../topics/ethics/emotional-intelligence-and-ethical-dilemmas.md) → Institutionalised empathy and conflict of interest · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** On the request of his Personal Assistant (PA), the District Magistrate (DM) stops the transfer of a schoolteacher who takes private tuition for his daughter diagnosed with learning disability. In this context, discuss the aspects of empathy versus compliance with rules. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Emotional Intelligence and Ethical Dilemmas · Subtopic: Institutionalised empathy and conflict of interest · Proposed slug: `empathy-transfer-rules-conflict-interest` · Demand: Discuss

### Model answer

Empathy recognises the child’s genuine need; it does not authorise personalised exception. The intervention has three defects. The tutoring relationship is extraneous to transfer policy; the requester is the DM’s own PA, creating an **apparent conflict of interest**; and RTE Act section 28 prohibits private tuition by a teacher, subject to the Act’s applicability to the school.

A sound test is: Would the DM grant this to a stranger? Is there a lawful route? Can the reason be publicly recorded? Compassion should instead be institutionalised through assessment, reasonable accommodation, remedial/resource support under the RPwD Act’s inclusive-education duties, and Samagra Shiksha. If school functioning genuinely warrants retention, a transparent, criteria-based exception should go to the competent transfer authority and apply to comparable cases.

The DM should revoke the personal favour, disclose/recuse where appropriate, and secure lawful support for the child.

**Why this earns marks:** It exposes conflict of interest and replaces favouritism with universal accommodation.

## Q6(b). Duty versus charity
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Moral Theories](../../topics/ethics/moral-theories.md) → Positive duties, charity and supererogation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official question:** Some moral philosophers argue that spending some of your resources—without significantly affecting your financial stability—to help other people who badly suffer is our moral duty and not merely a matter of charity. Justify this claim by explaining the distinction between duty and charity. **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Moral Theories · Subtopic: Positive duties, charity and supererogation · Proposed slug: `moral-duty-charity-supererogation` · Demand: Justify

### Model answer

A **duty** is obligatory: unjustified omission attracts blame. Charity is ordinarily voluntary generosity; **supererogation**, clarified by J. O. Urmson, is admirable action beyond duty.

Singer’s moderate principle in “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” (1972) holds that if serious suffering can be prevented without sacrificing anything comparably significant, one ought to act. His drowning-child analogy shows that distance and other available helpers do not erase responsibility. The question’s qualifier—without significantly affecting financial stability—answers the strongest demandingness objection. Kant’s imperfect duty of beneficence and Rawls’s natural duty of mutual aid independently support obligation with latitude about its discharge.

Special obligations, aid effectiveness and personal projects still matter. Thus reasonable, effective help is duty; exceptional sacrifice remains supererogatory. Gandhian trusteeship and Antyodaya provide an Indian register, while dignified giving avoids dependence.

**Why this earns marks:** It defends the moderate claim with thinkers while preserving a qualified category beyond duty.

## Q7. Lata, Dr Mansi and hysterectomy
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Case Studies—Bioethics](../../topics/ethics/case-studies-bioethics.md) → Therapeutic consent, bodily integrity and paternalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official case stem:** Lata, a mother of two children, was admitted to a hospital for acute abdominal pain. Her sister-in-law, Sujatha, accompanied her. Dr. Mansi examined Lata and recommended a diagnostic laparoscopy. Lata’s consent was taken to conduct the medical procedure under general anesthesia. During the laparoscopy, Dr. Mansi’s team discovered a tumor in Lata’s uterus. A closer examination suggested that the tumor could be malignant. One option before Dr. Mansi was to extract a sample for biopsy. In that case, if the tumor was malignant, Lata would have to undergo another surgery for removal of the uterus. An alternative was to remove the uterus immediately. Dr. Mansi had to take a quick decision. As Lata was under general anesthesia, Dr. Mansi explained the situation to Sujatha. Sujatha agreed with Dr. Mansi’s recommendations for a hysterectomy, wherein Lata’s uterus would be removed to avoid the risk and pain of undergoing another surgery. Dr. Mansi removed Lata’s uterus after receiving Sujatha’s consent in writing. Lata was informed of this the next day. She was very upset and felt betrayed as she had not consented to the removal of her uterus. Lata complained to the police who tried to convince her that Dr. Mansi had acted with good intention to help a patient. Sujatha was of the same opinion, however Lata was not convinced and decided to approach the court.

**Official demands:** **(a)** Discuss the ethical issues involved in this case. **(b)** Discuss the moral conduct of the doctor in this situation. **(Answer in 250 words) 20**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: Case Studies—Bioethics · Subtopic: Therapeutic consent, bodily integrity and paternalism · Proposed slug: `lata-hysterectomy-consent` · Demand: Issues + appraisal

<a id="2026-mains-gs4-q7a"></a>
### Q7(a). Stakeholders and issues
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Case Studies—Bioethics](../../topics/ethics/case-studies-bioethics.md) → Therapeutic consent, bodily integrity and paternalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

<a id="2026-mains-gs4-q7b"></a>
### Q7(b). Appraisal, action and safeguards
**Primary route:** Ethics → [Case Studies—Bioethics](../../topics/ethics/case-studies-bioethics.md) → Therapeutic consent, bodily integrity and paternalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

The motive was benevolent, but the act was **strong paternalism**: good intention did not create authority. Kantian ethics shows treatment of Lata as an object to optimise; virtue ethics credits decisiveness but finds deficient humility and fidelity. Late disclosure was necessary; using family/police to “convince” her compounded the wrong.

The correct option was biopsy/frozen section where available, closure, disclosure and Lata’s specific consent. The hospital should apologise, preserve grievance routes, counsel Lata and conduct clinical-ethics review.

**Safeguards:** prospective extended-procedure choices; contemporaneous emergency documentation; specific consent; consent-form audit and training.

**Qualification:** Not malicious, but a serious autonomy violation; bona fides may mitigate culpability, not justify the act.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates motive, act and aftermath and adds systemic prevention.

## Q8. Ravi and predictive policing
**Primary route:** Ethics → [AI and Policing](../../topics/ethics/ai-and-policing.md) → Feedback-loop bias, contestability and proportional surveillance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Official case stem:** Ravi is a senior police officer with vast experience in riot control and cyber-policing. Since one year, he has been the Superintendent of Police (SP) of a district with a history of frequent rioting. Last year, Ravi had sought installation of an AI enabled software for predictive policing. This system has been operational for approximately six months. This new system employs advanced algorithms for capturing the biometric data of persons in a crowd and swiftly relating it to a data library. This has enabled the police to identify the persons involved in various crimes. The system has identified an immigrant and low-income neighbourhood as a centre for gang violence and drug trafficking. Aided by this AI analysis, the local police has focused its patrolling, preventive detentions and establishing checkposts. Consequently, public order and law enforcement has visibly improved. Last week, some community leaders, civil rights lawyers and human rights activists visited Ravi’s office. They submitted a memorandum that the new system is faulty as it is based on incorrect historical data caused by social biases and discriminatory policing. The memorandum also alleges that the increased surveillance has created a climate of tension amongst residents. This feeling is aggravated by the fact that the residents are not aware of the data noted against their names.

**Official demands:** **(a)** What are the ethical issues including biases involved in the use of AI in data-driven policing? **(b)** Place yourself in Ravi’s role and discuss the alternatives available. Justify the action that optimises compliance with ethics. **(Answer in 250 words) 20**

**Metadata:** Subject: Ethics · Topic: AI and Policing · Subtopic: Feedback-loop bias, contestability and proportional surveillance · Proposed slug: `predictive-policing-bias` · Demand: Issues + options

<a id="2026-mains-gs4-q8a"></a>
### Q8(a). Stakeholders and issues
**Primary route:** Ethics → [AI and Policing](../../topics/ethics/ai-and-policing.md) → Feedback-loop bias, contestability and proportional surveillance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Stakeholders:** residents/detainees; victims; police/Ravi; vendor; civil-rights groups; future technology users.

Issues include dirty-data feedback loops—past over-policing creates more records and future policing; proxy discrimination through locality, poverty or immigrant status; automation bias and discretion-laundering; opacity and inability to correct records; privacy, dignity and function creep; anticipatory coercion based on prediction; and unequal distribution of safety benefits and surveillance burdens. Accountability remains with police, not the algorithm.

<a id="2026-mains-gs4-q8b"></a>
### Q8(b). Options, decision and safeguards
**Primary route:** Ethics → [AI and Policing](../../topics/ethics/ai-and-policing.md) → Feedback-loop bias, contestability and proportional surveillance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

Retaining unchanged entrenches untested discrimination; total shutdown discards genuine gains; captive internal review lacks credibility. I choose **calibrated suspension plus independent audit**: pause person-level automated targeting and biometric matching; retain evidence-based place/time deployment; review AI-derived detentions; require written human reasons; audit data provenance and group error rates; and provide notice, record access, correction and appeal.

Legality/non-discrimination are hard thresholds; proportionality and contestability favour this option. Opacity itself prevents residents proving bias.
