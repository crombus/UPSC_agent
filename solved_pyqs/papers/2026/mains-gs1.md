## Q1. Analyze the importance of Ashokan inscriptions for reconstructing Mauryan history.
**Primary route:** Ancient History → [Ashokan Inscriptions and Mauryan History](../../topics/ancient-history/ashokan-inscriptions-mauryan-history.md) → Epigraphy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Analysis · Ancient History → Sources → Epigraphy  
**Topic:** [Ashokan inscriptions and Mauryan reconstruction](../../topics/ancient-history/ashokan-inscriptions-mauryan-history.md)  
**Demand:** “Analyze” requires explaining what inscriptions establish, how they establish it, and where their evidentiary limits require corroboration.

**Compact framework:** Identity/chronology → imperial geography → administration and *dhamma* → society/welfare → source criticism.

### Model answer

Ashokan inscriptions are the most extensive contemporary royal evidence for the Mauryan Empire, converting an otherwise literary reconstruction into a dated and geographically grounded history.

Minor Rock Edicts at **Maski and Gujarra** connect the name Ashoka with *Devanampiya Piyadasi*. **Major Rock Edict XIII** anchors the Kalinga conquest in his eighth regnal year and reveals remorse alongside power to punish *atavikas*: policy change, not total pacifism.

Their distribution from **Shahbazgarhi-Mansehra** to **Dhauli-Jaugada** and **Brahmagiri** maps communication zones and differentiated imperial reach. Greek-Aramaic records at Kandahar and Kharosthi records in the north-west show adaptation to audiences.

References to *dhamma-mahamatras*, tours, sectarian restraint in Rock Edict XII and welfare measures illuminate administration and political ethics.

However, edicts express royal intention, omit ordinary dissent and cannot mark exact frontiers. They must therefore be triangulated with archaeology, the *Arthashastra*, Megasthenes and Buddhist traditions.

**Conclusion:** Their value is foundational precisely when read as official, selective evidence rather than a complete social chronicle.

**Why this earns marks:** It links named edicts and sites to historical inferences while explicitly qualifying royal bias and uneven geographical survival.

## Q2. Discuss the impact of Macaulay’s Minute on colonial administration and education in India.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Macaulay’s Minute and Colonial Education](../../topics/modern-history/macaulays-minute-colonial-education.md) → Education · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discussion · Modern History → Colonial Policy → Education  
**Topic:** [Macaulay’s Minute and colonial education](../../topics/modern-history/macaulays-minute-colonial-education.md)  
**Demand:** “Discuss” calls for administrative purpose, educational change, wider consequences and exclusionary limits.

**Compact framework:** Anglicist victory → administrative intermediary class → higher-education system → public sphere/nationalism → limited mass access.

### Model answer

Macaulay’s Minute of **1835**, accepted through Bentinck’s resolution, decisively shifted state patronage from Oriental learning towards Western knowledge through English.

Administratively, it supported an English-educated intermediary class for clerical, legal and subordinate governmental work. It complemented, rather than created, the colonial bureaucracy; the **Macaulay Committee of 1854** concerned civil-service recruitment and was distinct.

Educationally, the Anglicist victory privileged higher education and “downward filtration.” **Wood’s Despatch (1854)** subsequently institutionalised departments, grants-in-aid and universities; Calcutta, Bombay and Madras universities followed in **1857**. Thus, 1835 set direction, while 1854 built the system.

Its consequences exceeded colonial intent. English opened science, law and liberal ideas to an interregional educated public whose lawyers, teachers and journalists developed the nationalist critique.

Yet benefits remained concentrated among urban, male and socially advantaged groups; vernacular and mass primary education were underfunded.

**Conclusion:** The Minute efficiently served administrative economy but unintentionally created an articulate anti-colonial intelligentsia.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates the 1835 education decision from the 1854 civil-service committee and balances intention, institutional development and unintended nationalism.

## Q3. The significance of the Gandhian movements lay in the masses they mobilized. Elucidate.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Gandhian Movements and Mass Mobilisation](../../topics/modern-history/gandhian-mass-mobilisation.md) → Mass Mobilisation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Elucidation · Modern History → National Movement → Mass Mobilisation  
**Topic:** [Gandhian movements and mass mobilisation](../../topics/modern-history/gandhian-mass-mobilisation.md)  
**Demand:** “Elucidate” requires showing how mobilisation changed nationalism, with social/regional examples and qualifications.

**Compact framework:** Methods and organisation → widening social base → local appropriation → political transformation → unevenness.

### Model answer

The chief significance of Gandhian movements was that they transformed nationalism from elite petition into repeated mass participation, though mobilisation was never socially uniform.

The **Non-Cooperation Movement** linked urban boycott with peasants, artisans and Muslims through Khilafat. The **Nagpur Congress (1920)** created linguistic committees and low-fee membership, turning Congress into a mass organisation. Yet **Eka** peasants under Madari Pasi pursued autonomous agrarian demands.

The **Civil Disobedience Movement** politicised salt, tax and forest laws and widened women’s participation. **Quit India (1942)** penetrated rural districts after leaders were arrested; underground networks and parallel governments showed local initiative.

Gandhian methods—satyagraha, boycott, constructive work and simple symbols—converted everyday acts into politics and eroded colonial legitimacy.

Withdrawals, regional variation, reduced Muslim participation after Non-Cooperation and unequal inclusion qualify the claim.

**Conclusion:** Gandhi’s durable achievement was not continuous victory but making millions political actors whose consent colonial rule could no longer presume.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses three movements and named social examples while distinguishing mobilisation from uniform control or immediate success.

## Q4. What is the Fujiwhara effect? Explain its impact on the movement and intensity of tropical cyclones.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Fujiwhara Effect](../../topics/geography/fujiwhara-effect.md) → Tropical Cyclones · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explanation · Geography → Climatology → Tropical Cyclones  
**Topic:** [Fujiwhara effect](../../topics/geography/fujiwhara-effect.md)  
**Demand:** Define the binary-vortex interaction, then separate its reliable track effects from its conditional intensity effects.

**Compact framework:** Definition → controls → movement outcomes → intensity pathways → forecast qualification.

### Model answer

The **Fujiwhara effect**, named after Sakuhei Fujiwhara, is the interaction of two sufficiently close cyclonic vortices whose circulation fields make them rotate around a common centroid. It is mutual steering, not necessarily a collision.

Movement depends on separation, relative strength, structure and background steering. The pair may orbit, loop, stall, deflect, merge or separate. **Hurricanes Hilary and Irwin (2017)** illustrated mutual rotation and track alteration.

Intensity effects are less deterministic. Outflow interaction, shear, dry air and asymmetric convection can weaken or deform a cyclone. Absorption may leave a larger circulation but not necessarily a stronger one; intensification still depends on ocean heat and inner-core organisation. **Seroja and Odette (2021)** showed substantial track modification.

Because each cyclone’s predicted position affects the other’s forecast, small errors compound.

**Conclusion:** Unusual track behaviour is the clearest Fujiwhara signature; intensity change is indirect, variable and environmentally conditioned.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly distinguishes movement from intensity and rejects the common “always merge and strengthen” error.

## Q5. “Tundra regions are ecologically fragile but economically important.” Examine this statement critically.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Tundra Fragility and Economic Importance](../../topics/geography/tundra-fragility-economic-importance.md) → Tundra · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Critical examination · Geography → Climatology/Biomes → Tundra  
**Topic:** [Tundra fragility and economic importance](../../topics/geography/tundra-fragility-economic-importance.md)  
**Demand:** “Examine critically” requires establishing both propositions, exposing trade-offs and giving a conditional verdict.

**Compact framework:** Fragility mechanisms → economic uses → externalities and Indigenous interests → governance verdict.

### Model answer

Tundra is a low-energy, treeless biome whose slow ecological recovery makes even limited disturbance consequential, yet its resources and location give it major economic value.

Fragility arises from **permafrost**, a short growing season and slow nutrient cycling. Vehicles, pipelines and mining can cause lasting damage. Warming lowers albedo and thaws permafrost, releasing carbon and undermining infrastructure; sea-ice loss disrupts habitats and Indigenous subsistence.

Arctic tundra and adjoining shelves contain hydrocarbons and minerals; poleward-moving fisheries and longer ice-free seasons increase economic interest and remote employment.

Yet mobile ice, sparse ports, high insurance and weak rescue capacity constrain shipping. Extraction risks spills, fragmentation and disruption of Inuit, Sami and Nenets livelihoods.

**Conclusion:** Tundra’s economic importance is real, but its low resilience makes precautionary, Indigenous-participatory and ecosystem-based regulation indispensable; “development first, restoration later” is ecologically untenable here.

**Why this earns marks:** It links permafrost and albedo mechanisms to named economic sectors, then qualifies shipping and extraction claims.

## Q6. Discuss the role of aeolian processes in desertification and land degradation.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Aeolian Processes and Land Degradation](../../topics/geography/aeolian-processes-land-degradation.md) → Aeolian Processes · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discussion · Geography → Geomorphology → Aeolian Processes  
**Topic:** [Aeolian processes, desertification and land degradation](../../topics/geography/aeolian-processes-land-degradation.md)  
**Demand:** Explain how wind erosion, transport and deposition reduce productivity, while distinguishing geomorphic process from desertification and identifying human amplification.

**Compact framework:** Preconditions → deflation/abrasion/transport/deposition → productivity effects → feedbacks → controls.

### Model answer

Aeolian processes degrade land when wind erosion and deposition operate on dry, exposed and poorly bound surfaces. They are agents of desertification, not its sole cause.

**Deflation** removes fine fertile particles, leaving coarse pavement and lowering water retention. **Abrasion** by saltating sand damages crusts and crops. Through **saltation and suspension**, wind exports dust and moves dunes that can bury fields and roads. Deposition shifts material into dunes or distant loess.

Overgrazing, vegetation removal, repeated tillage and bare fallow reduce roughness and root binding. Drought then creates a feedback: erosion lowers fertility, weaker vegetation exposes more soil and productivity declines. The Thar’s fringes illustrate this interaction even where irrigation stabilises dunes.

Controls must combine shelterbelts, vegetative cover, controlled grazing, conservation tillage and watershed-based moisture management.

**Conclusion:** Wind converts climatic aridity into persistent degradation chiefly where land use removes the surface protections that normally limit erosion.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces the complete erosion-transport-deposition chain and avoids equating desertification with advancing sand deserts.

## Q7. “Water resources are both an asset and a source of conflict in South Asia.” Examine this statement giving examples.
**Primary route:** International Relations/Geography → [South Asian Water Resources](../../topics/international-relations/south-asia-water-resources.md) → Transboundary Water · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Examination with examples · International Relations/Geography → South Asia → Transboundary Water  
**Topic:** [South Asian water resources](../../topics/international-relations/south-asia-water-resources.md)  
**Demand:** Examine developmental benefits and conflict mechanisms with basin examples, then identify conditions for cooperation.

**Compact framework:** Asset channels → conflict drivers → examples → climate/federal complications → basin governance.

### Model answer

South Asia’s rivers are shared natural infrastructure: they support irrigation, drinking water, hydropower, navigation and ecosystems, but upstream-downstream asymmetry converts interdependence into political risk.

India-Bhutan hydropower cooperation, illustrated by **Mangdechhu**, links Bhutanese revenue with Indian electricity. The Ganga-Brahmaputra system supports agriculture, connectivity and settlements.

Conflict concerns timing, quantity, storage and benefit-sharing. The **Indus Waters Treaty (1960)** created durable India-Pakistan rules, yet project disputes persisted; India announced on **23 April 2025** that it was holding the treaty in abeyance. The **Ganga Water Treaty (1996)** contrasts with unresolved **Teesta**, where Indian federal interests affect diplomacy. India-Nepal cooperation combines hydropower potential with inundation and unequal-benefit concerns.

Climate variability, glacier change, sediment and extreme floods further reduce the value of fixed volumetric assumptions.

**Conclusion:** Water becomes conflictual when treated as divisible flow rather than a shared basin. Joint data, flood forecasting, ecological-flow protection and transparent benefit-sharing can convert asymmetry into managed interdependence.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses three named basin relationships and explains the precise mechanisms linking water cooperation and conflict.

## Q8. Unity in diversity remains the defining feature of Indian society despite the challenges from communalism and regionalism. Comment.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Unity in Diversity, Communalism and Regionalism](../../topics/indian-society/unity-diversity-communalism-regionalism.md) → Social Cohesion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment · Indian Society → Diversity → Social Cohesion  
**Topic:** [Unity in diversity, communalism and regionalism](../../topics/indian-society/unity-diversity-communalism-regionalism.md)  
**Demand:** “Comment” requires a reasoned judgement: explain integrative foundations, assess threats, and qualify resilience.

**Compact framework:** Sources of unity → communal challenge → regional challenge → constitutional/social correctives → verdict.

### Model answer

India’s unity is not cultural uniformity but an institutional and civilisational capacity to accommodate overlapping identities of language, religion, caste and region.

Integration occurs through markets, migration, elections, schools and **Bhakti-Sufi** syncretism. Equal citizenship, rights, linguistic recognition and federalism give difference legitimate space. Linguistic reorganisation after **1956** shows that recognising regional identity can strengthen integration.

Communalism converts religious difference into antagonistic blocs, eroding trust. Regionalism becomes divisive when recognition or resource demands turn into nativism against migrants or secession. Misinformation and uneven development intensify both.

Yet electoral alternation, interstate mobility, composite cultures and constitutional accommodation repeatedly reconnect identities to a common polity.

**Conclusion:** Unity in diversity remains India’s defining feature, but it is an achievement continually reproduced through federal accommodation, impartial institutions, intercommunity contact and reduction of regional inequality—not an automatic civilisational guarantee.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes accommodative regionalism from exclusionary nativism and treats unity as resilient but conditional.

## Q9. How do you understand disability? Substantiate the need for inclusive policy framework in India in this regard.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Disability and Inclusive Policy](../../topics/social-justice/disability-inclusive-policy.md) → Inclusion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q9 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Concept and substantiation · Social Justice → Disability → Inclusion  
**Topic:** [Disability and inclusive policy](../../topics/social-justice/disability-inclusive-policy.md)  
**Demand:** Define disability through the social/rights model and substantiate why cross-sector inclusion, not charity alone, is necessary.

**Compact framework:** Definition/model → barriers → legal basis → policy pillars → implementation qualification.

### Model answer

Disability is not merely an individual impairment. Under the **Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016**, it arises when long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment interacts with environmental and attitudinal barriers to restrict equal participation.

This rights-based understanding requires inclusion. Equality needs **reasonable accommodation**—such as screen readers or extra examination time. In *Vikash Kumar v. UPSC*, the Supreme Court treated accommodation as substantive equality.

Exclusion is multidimensional: inaccessible transport restricts mobility; inflexible schools and workplaces block capability; digital barriers and stigma silence participation. Hence the Act’s 21 specified disabilities, reservation, accessibility duties and commissioners must operate together.

Universal design should guide infrastructure and ICT, while individual accommodation addresses residual barriers. **UDID** improves certification portability; Accessible India covers built environment, transport and ICT. Registration alone does not prove inclusion.

**Conclusion:** Policy must move from welfare delivery to autonomy, accessibility, education, work, social protection and enforceable grievance redress, designed with persons with disabilities themselves.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines disability correctly, cites a statute and case, and connects barriers to specific inclusive-policy instruments.

## Q10. Do you think digital technology promotes social empowerment? Explain with examples.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Digital Technology and Social Empowerment](../../topics/indian-society/digital-technology-social-empowerment.md) → Digital Inclusion · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Conditional explanation · Indian Society → Social Empowerment → Digital Inclusion  
**Topic:** [Digital technology and social empowerment](../../topics/indian-society/digital-technology-social-empowerment.md)  
**Demand:** Give a conditional judgement by tracing how technology expands capability and voice, then test exclusion and control risks.

**Compact framework:** Information → services/resources → voice/collective action → livelihood → divides/risks → conditional verdict.

### Model answer

Digital technology promotes social empowerment when access is converted into capability and agency; connectivity alone is insufficient.

It lowers information barriers: **DIKSHA** widens educational reach and **eSanjeevani** connects remote patients to clinicians. **Direct Benefit Transfer** and **DigiLocker** can reduce distance and discretion. **UPI** enables small merchants and women’s collectives to receive payments, while social media helps marginal groups organise claims.

Gains remain unequal. Affordability, connectivity, language, literacy, disability access and gendered phone control determine use. Authentication failure or incorrect databases turn digitisation into exclusion. Surveillance, misinformation, abuse and opaque algorithms may weaken autonomy; digital voice may not secure institutional response.

**Conclusion:** Digital technology is an enabling infrastructure, not empowerment by itself. Its social value depends on affordable access, assisted and offline alternatives, accessible design, data protection, grievance correction and the ability of citizens to convert information into meaningful choice.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives named Indian examples while applying the capability lens and qualifying access, exclusion and surveillance.

## Q11. Vijayanagara architecture exemplifies the amalgamation of past traditions with contemporary practices. Elucidate with examples.
**Primary route:** Indian Art and Culture → [Vijayanagara Architecture](../../topics/art-and-culture/vijayanagara-architecture.md) → Vijayanagara · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Elucidation with examples · Art and Culture → Architecture → Vijayanagara  
**Topic:** [Vijayanagara architecture](../../topics/art-and-culture/vijayanagara-architecture.md)  
**Demand:** “Elucidate” requires identifying inherited traditions, contemporary innovations and their synthesis through named sacred, urban and secular monuments.

**Compact framework:** Dravida inheritance → Deccan/local synthesis → ritual-urban innovations → secular Indo-Islamic vocabulary → qualified conclusion.

### Model answer

Vijayanagara architecture was not an eclectic mixture without grammar; it renewed the Dravida temple tradition while adapting Deccan craft, imperial ceremony, urban commerce and contemporary Islamicate forms.

**Inherited sacred grammar:** Temples retained the Dravida complex—enclosures, *mandapas*, axial planning and monumental gateways—drawing upon Chola, Pandya, Chalukya and Hoysala precedents. At **Virupaksha Temple, Hampi**, Sangama foundations were expanded under Krishnadevaraya; its monumental **Raya Gopuram** demonstrates the late-medieval shift of visual emphasis from the sanctum’s *vimana* to the entrance tower.

**Contemporary ritual and social practice:** The **Vitthala complex** combined a Vishnu shrine with a non-monolithic stone chariot, musical pillars in the Ranga Mandapa and a **Kalyana Mandapa** for ceremonial marriage and festivals. Wide chariot streets lined with pillared mandapas integrated temple ritual with markets and processions. Yali-bearing composite pillars show continuity that later informed Nayaka architecture. The **Hazara Rama Temple**, with Ramayana reliefs, connected royal ideology and sacred narrative.

**Imperial urbanism:** Sacred Hampi coexisted with a planned royal-ceremonial landscape. The **Mahanavami Dibba** functioned as a platform for festival, army review and kingship rather than as a temple.

**Contemporary cosmopolitanism:** Indo-Islamic arches and domes appear most clearly in secular buildings—the **Lotus Mahal**, Queen’s Bath and elephant stables—while temples remained predominantly Dravidian.

**Qualification:** The synthesis varied by building function; it is inaccurate to project Indo-Islamic form uniformly onto Hampi’s temples.

**Conclusion:** Vijayanagara’s originality lay in making inherited temple forms serve a new imperial city of ceremony, commerce and cosmopolitan court culture.

**Why this earns marks:** It names monuments and architectural elements, distinguishes sacred from secular synthesis, and explains rather than merely lists influences.

## Q12. Linguistic reorganization of Indian States was driven by popular pressure from below. Comment.
**Primary route:** Post-Independence History → [Linguistic Reorganisation of States](../../topics/post-independence-history/linguistic-reorganisation.md) → Language · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment · Post-Independence History → State Reorganisation → Language  
**Topic:** [Linguistic reorganisation of states](../../topics/post-independence-history/linguistic-reorganisation.md)  
**Demand:** Test the “from below” proposition against popular movements, elite/state caution and commission-led institutionalisation.

**Compact framework:** Pre-independence roots → 1948 resistance → Andhra trigger → SRC settlement → later movements → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Popular pressure was the decisive trigger of linguistic reorganisation, but the final map emerged from interaction between movements from below and constitutional statecraft from above.

The demand had deep organisational roots: the Congress had itself used linguistic provincial committees, creating expectations that administration should follow language. Yet after Partition, national leaders prioritised security. The **Dar Commission (1948)** and **JVP Committee (1948)** advised postponement, fearing that linguistic states might intensify fragmentation.

The policy reversal was compelled from below. **Potti Sriramulu’s** fast and death after 58 days in December 1952 generated mass agitation; the Union created **Andhra State in 1953**, before the States Reorganisation Commission. Thus, popular mobilisation established the principle that official committees had resisted.

However, pressure alone did not design a viable federation. The **Fazl Ali Commission (1953–55)** assessed language alongside administrative, economic and security considerations, and the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** produced 14 states and 6 Union Territories. Later, the **Samyukta Maharashtra** and **Mahagujarat** movements led to Bombay’s division in 1960; the Punjabi Suba mobilisation preceded Punjab’s reorganisation in 1966.

The process also exposed limits: border and capital disputes, linguistic minorities within new states, and non-linguistic tribal reorganisation in the North-East.

**Conclusion:** Reorganisation was driven by popular pressure in agenda-setting and timing, but commissions, Parliament and federal bargaining converted agitation into a general settlement. Its success lay in recognising language without conceding sovereignty.

**Why this earns marks:** It supports the proposition with chronology while qualifying it through the SRC, Parliament and non-linguistic considerations.

## Q13. The model of planned economy was adopted in India to address the regional imbalances left behind by colonial rule. Comment.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Planned Economy and Regional Imbalances](../../topics/geography/planned-economy-regional-imbalances.md) → Planning · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Comment/evaluation · Geography → Regional Development → Planning  
**Topic:** [Planned economy and regional imbalance](../../topics/geography/planned-economy-regional-imbalances.md)  
**Demand:** Explain the colonial spatial legacy, evaluate planning instruments and achievements, and assess persistent or new imbalance.

**Compact framework:** Colonial legacy → rationale → spatial instruments → achievements → persistence/new polarization → verdict.

### Model answer

Reducing inherited regional imbalance was a major rationale for India’s planned economy, although planning also pursued growth, industrialisation and self-reliance.

Colonial development was spatially selective. Port cities, railway corridors and plantation/mineral enclaves served imperial trade, while many interior, tribal, hill and drought-prone regions lacked infrastructure and social investment. This produced what Myrdal later described as cumulative advantage: capital and skills moved towards already connected cores.

Planning attempted a territorial correction. The **Five-Year Plans (1951–2017)** directed public investment into multipurpose river-valley projects, irrigation, transport and social services. Public-sector plants such as **Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur** were located partly to create industrial growth poles beyond old presidency centres. Hill-area, tribal sub-plan, drought-prone and backward-area programmes recognised distinct planning regions. Fiscal transfers and nationally financed schemes sought minimum service provision across states.

These interventions built heavy industry, widened infrastructure and improved social indicators in many lagging regions. Yet spread effects were incomplete. Growth poles sometimes remained enclaves; the Green Revolution initially favoured irrigated north-western regions, while mineral-rich central-eastern districts often remained income-poor. Agglomeration around metropolitan and coastal corridors generated fresh intra-state disparities.

Moreover, administrative boundaries often poorly match functional regions such as river basins or drought belts, weakening delivery.

**Conclusion:** Planning was a necessary response to colonial spatial inequality, but public investment could not erase differences in institutions, human capital, connectivity and agglomeration. Balanced development requires continued district- and region-specific policy, not equal allocation everywhere.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects named planning instruments to spatial theory, achievements and enclave/backwash limitations.

## Q14. Compare the Loo, Chinook and Foehn winds with respect to their regions of prevalence, their nature and climatic impacts.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Loo, Chinook and Foehn Winds](../../topics/geography/loo-chinook-foehn.md) → Local Winds · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comparison · Geography → Climatology → Local Winds  
**Topic:** [Loo, Chinook and Foehn winds](../../topics/geography/loo-chinook-foehn.md)  
**Demand:** A classify-first comparison across region, season, mechanism, nature and impacts; avoid treating Loo as a Foehn wind.

**Compact framework:** Classification → comparison table → shared/different mechanisms → impacts → conclusion.

### Model answer

All three are hot, dry local winds, but only Chinook and Foehn share an orographic-descent mechanism; the Loo is generated by intense surface heating over plains.

| Dimension | Loo | Foehn | Chinook |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Region** | North-western and northern Indian plains | Leeward Alpine valleys, especially Switzerland-Austria | Eastern/leeward Rockies, Canadian Prairies and US Great Plains |
| **Season** | Pre-monsoon summer, mainly May-June | Mainly winter-spring | Mainly winter-early spring |
| **Nature** | Hot, dry, gusty afternoon wind | Warm, dry downslope wind | North American regional name for a Foehn-type wind |
| **Mechanism** | Strong continental heating creates a steep local pressure gradient; no mountain descent | Moist air rises, cools and precipitates windward; dried air descends and warms adiabatically | Same windward rainout and leeward adiabatic warming as Foehn |
| **Major impacts** | Heat stress, dehydration, crop desiccation and fire risk | Rapid temperature rise, snowmelt, avalanche and fire-weather risk | “Snow-eater”: exposes pasture, causes rapid thaw, possible ice-jam/flood risk |

For Foehn and Chinook, air cools at the saturated adiabatic rate while rising and losing moisture, then warms faster at the dry adiabatic rate during descent. It therefore reaches the leeward foot warmer and drier. The Loo’s heat, by contrast, comes directly from intensely heated continental surfaces and is strongest during summer afternoons.

**Conclusion:** Their similar thermal character should not obscure causal difference: Chinook and Foehn are one orographic family under regional names, whereas Loo is a plain-heating wind.

**Why this earns marks:** The table answers every comparison axis and foregrounds the core mechanistic distinction.

## Q15. Analyze the role of satellite-based technologies in achieving climate-smart agriculture and food security in India.
**Primary route:** Science and Technology → [Satellites, Climate-Smart Agriculture and Food Security](../../topics/science-and-technology/satellite-climate-smart-agriculture.md) → Agriculture · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Analysis · Science and Technology → Satellites → Agriculture  
**Topic:** [Satellites, climate-smart agriculture and food security](../../topics/science-and-technology/satellite-climate-smart-agriculture.md)  
**Demand:** Trace satellite data from observation to farm and food-security outcomes, while assessing adoption, accuracy and governance limits.

**Compact framework:** Observation/navigation/weather functions → resilience/productivity/resource efficiency → system-level food security → constraints → integration.

### Model answer

Satellite technology advances climate-smart agriculture when Earth observation, meteorology and navigation are converted into timely field decisions that raise productivity, resilience and resource efficiency.

**Anticipation and resilience:** The **INSAT-3D/3DR/3DS** meteorological series supports cloud, rainfall and severe-weather observation. Combined with IMD advisories, this can guide sowing, irrigation and harvest timing and improve warning against drought, flood or cyclone risk.

**Monitoring production:** **Resourcesat**-type multispectral imagery can estimate crop acreage and condition, identify stress and support drought assessment. Platforms such as **Bhuvan** and the **Krishi Decision Support System** integrate geospatial and administrative layers for crop-weather monitoring and planning. Repeated observation improves early production assessment, enabling procurement, storage and import decisions.

**Efficient inputs:** Satellite-derived soil-moisture, water-body and vegetation information can target irrigation and crop protection. **NavIC/GNSS** enables geotagging and precision operations, while remote sensing can support transparent crop-insurance loss assessment.

**Food-system contribution:** Better forecasts reduce crop loss; acreage/yield intelligence improves buffer-stock and supply-chain planning; mapping helps protect land and water resources needed for long-term food security.

However, cloud cover, revisit time, spatial resolution and similar crop signatures create inference errors. Satellite signals require ground truth, local agronomy and last-mile extension. Smallholders, tenants and women cultivators may be excluded by weak connectivity or inaccurate land records; automated insurance decisions therefore need appeal mechanisms.

**Conclusion:** Satellites are an information infrastructure, not a substitute for irrigation, extension, credit or resilient seeds. Their climate-smart value emerges from an inclusive space-ground-user ecosystem.

**Why this earns marks:** It links named Indian systems to productivity, resilience and food governance, then qualifies remote-sensing inference and last-mile exclusion.

## Q16. “The centre of global trade is gradually shifting from the Atlantic region to the Indo-Pacific region.” Examine this statement.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Atlantic-to-Indo-Pacific Trade Shift](../../topics/geography/atlantic-indo-pacific-trade-shift.md) → Indo-Pacific · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Examination · Geography → World Trade → Indo-Pacific  
**Topic:** [Atlantic-to-Indo-Pacific trade shift](../../topics/geography/atlantic-indo-pacific-trade-shift.md)  
**Demand:** Define the dimensions of “centre,” establish the historical baseline, show drivers/evidence, and qualify the incomplete institutional-financial shift.

**Compact framework:** Atlantic baseline → production/market shift → sea lanes/chokepoints → institutions/strategy → counterargument → graded verdict.

### Model answer

The statement is broadly correct for the geography of production, merchandise flows and shipping, but less complete for finance, currency and rule-making.

The Industrial Revolution and post-war order made the **North Atlantic**—Western Europe and North America—the principal manufacturing, shipping and institutional axis. Bretton Woods institutions and transatlantic investment reinforced this concentration.

The centre of gravity has since moved eastward. Sequential industrialisation from Japan and the Asian Tigers to China, ASEAN and India relocated manufacturing and regional value chains. **RCEP** and ASEAN-centred networks deepen intra-Asian trade, while “China+1” diversification spreads assembly across Vietnam, India and other Indo-Pacific economies. Asia’s population, growing consumer markets, Indian services and East Asian electronics add demand and high-value trade.

Geographically, the Malacca-Indian Ocean-South China Sea corridor now connects major factories, energy suppliers and markets. The **Straits of Malacca and Hormuz**, Bab-el-Mandeb, Sunda and Lombok have therefore acquired systemic chokepoint importance. Strategic initiatives—the BRI, Quad, IPEF and India’s Act East policy—are political responses to the same economic geography.

However, “shift” does not mean Atlantic decline into irrelevance. US-EU trade and investment remain enormous; dollar-centred finance, deep capital markets, technology standards and institutions such as the IMF continue to reflect Atlantic weight. Indo-Pacific flows also depend on markets and finance outside the region.

**Conclusion:** Global trade’s operational centre has moved towards the Indo-Pacific in production and sea-lane terms, while its financial and institutional centre remains more hybrid. The transition is gradual, uneven and interdependent rather than a clean regional replacement.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines separate flow and governance dimensions, names chokepoints and institutions, and provides a balanced spatial verdict.

## Q17. Analyze the major drivers of human-induced land-use changes in India and their geographical consequences.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Human-Induced Land-Use Change in India](../../topics/geography/human-induced-land-use-change.md) → Land-Use Change · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Causal analysis · Geography → Contemporary Issues → Land-Use Change  
**Topic:** [Human-induced land-use change in India](../../topics/geography/human-induced-land-use-change.md)  
**Demand:** Pair distinct conversion/intensification drivers with spatially specific consequences and identify scale-sensitive responses.

**Compact framework:** Definition → driver-region pairs → consequence mechanisms → distributional/scale issues → planning response.

### Model answer

Human-induced land-use change is the conversion or intensification of forest, wetland, grassland or cropland into built-up, mining, plantation, aquaculture or more intensive agricultural use. In India it is spatially concentrated, not uniform.

**Major drivers and locations**
- Food demand, irrigation and price incentives intensify cultivation in command areas and forest fringes.
- Urban migration, real estate and expressways expand built-up land around NCR, Bengaluru, Pune and Hyderabad.
- Mineral and energy demand drives mining in the Odisha-Jharkhand-Chhattisgarh belt.
- Dams, highways and transmission lines transform river valleys, forests and Himalayan corridors.
- Export-oriented aquaculture converts coastal wetlands and mangrove fringes; plantation and tourism demand alter the Western Ghats, North-East, hills and islands.

**Geographical consequences**
Vegetation removal accelerates sheet and gully erosion, while unsustainable irrigation causes waterlogging or salinisation. Forest conversion fragments wildlife corridors and reduces carbon sinks. Wetland loss and impervious urban surfaces increase runoff and flooding; loss of vegetation and water bodies intensifies urban heat islands. Hill cutting and deforestation amplify landslide risk. Mangrove conversion weakens storm-surge protection, while mining and reservoirs displace forest-dependent and tribal livelihoods.

These impacts operate at watershed, landscape and coastal-cell scales, often mismatching district or state jurisdictions. Yet conversion also supplies housing, food, minerals and connectivity; policy must manage rather than deny this trade-off.

**Conclusion:** Land-use change is not wholly reversible, but its rate and damage can be reduced through zoning, secure tenure, cumulative EIA, wetland/CRZ enforcement, wildlife corridors and watershed-based planning.

**Why this earns marks:** It pairs drivers, locations and mechanisms instead of presenting unrelated environmental lists.

## Q18. Is caste disappearing in urban India? Illustrate your answer with examples.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Caste in Urban India](../../topics/indian-society/caste-in-urban-india.md) → Urban Dynamics · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Evaluation with illustrations · Indian Society → Caste → Urban Dynamics  
**Topic:** [Caste in urban India](../../topics/indian-society/caste-in-urban-india.md)  
**Demand:** Test disappearance across occupational, ritual, marital, residential, labour-market and political arenas; illustrate change of form and variation.

**Compact framework:** What weakens → what persists/reappears → mechanisms/examples → intersectional variation → verdict.

### Model answer

Caste is not disappearing in urban India; it is changing register—from overt ritual hierarchy and hereditary occupation towards marriage boundaries, social networks, residential sorting and political identity.

Urbanisation weakens several traditional features. Anonymous public spaces, credential-based employment, cash markets and occupational mobility dilute jajmani ties and public purity-pollution practices. A Dalit engineer and an upper-caste wage worker may share a workplace without their occupations being caste-assigned. Intercaste contact in universities, offices and neighbourhoods also expands.

Yet the reproduction mechanisms persist. **Endogamy** remains caste’s strongest boundary: matrimonial advertisements and digital marriage platforms commonly filter by caste, even among educated urban families. Housing searches can be shaped by surname, food practice and community networks, producing informal residential segregation. In labour markets, alumni, kin and jati associations operate as network capital for referrals and business credit; the same networks can exclude outsiders.

Caste also acquires modern organisational forms. Urban caste associations run hostels, scholarships and welfare bodies and mobilise electoral claims. M.N. Srinivas’s concepts help explain the shift: occupational linkage weakens, but status assertion and politicisation continue. The 2024 UPSC premise that some intercaste marriages grow where socio-economic parity exists itself shows selective erosion, not disappearance.

Variation is crucial. Class, gender, region and migration status mediate caste: economic mobility may reduce dependence without eliminating discrimination, while stigmatised occupations remain disproportionately inherited.

**Conclusion:** Urban India makes caste less publicly ritualised but often more networked and privately reproduced. Its form is modernising faster than its marital and discriminatory boundaries are dissolving.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates multiple social arenas, uses a sociological mechanism and illustrates both erosion and persistence without unsupported prevalence claims.

## Q19. Critically examine the challenges of demographic transition in contemporary India.
**Primary route:** Geography → [India’s Demographic Transition](../../topics/geography/indias-demographic-transition.md) → Demographic Transition · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q19 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Critical examination · Geography → Population → Demographic Transition  
**Topic:** [India’s demographic transition](../../topics/geography/indias-demographic-transition.md)  
**Demand:** Identify the transition’s uneven stages and critically assess both youthful-dividend and ageing challenges, including gender, migration and federal dimensions.

**Compact framework:** Uneven transition → jobs/skills → health/gender → ageing/care → migration/urbanisation → federal policy differentiation.

### Model answer

India’s demographic transition is not one national sequence but simultaneous youthfulness, slowing growth and ageing across different states. This unevenness is the central policy challenge.

First, a large working-age population is only a potential **demographic dividend**. Inadequate job creation, learning deficits, skill mismatch and low-quality informal work can convert it into unemployment and social stress. Female education has contributed to fertility decline, but unequal care burdens and labour-market access restrict women’s economic participation.

Second, population momentum means growth and demand for schools, housing, health services and urban infrastructure continue even after fertility falls. Younger states in the north and east face stronger employment and service pressures, while migration towards industrial and service centres strains affordable housing, transport and water.

Third, low-fertility southern and western states are ageing earlier. Longer life expectancy raises demand for geriatric care, pensions, chronic-disease management and community support. A shrinking family-care base particularly affects elderly women. Pro-natalist incentives alone have shown limited capacity internationally to reverse deep fertility decline.

Fourth, inter-state divergence creates federal dilemmas: younger states supply labour to older states, while portability and migrant inclusion shape integration; representation and fiscal allocation can generate contestation.

Finally, national averages conceal district, rural-urban, caste, gender and class differences. Census 2011 remains the latest completed all-India Census baseline; later estimates require dates.

**Conclusion:** India needs differentiated policy—jobs, skills and reproductive health in youthful regions; care systems and higher labour participation in ageing regions; and portable social protection for migrants. Demography supplies the opportunity and burden; institutions determine the outcome.

**Why this earns marks:** It critically integrates dividend, momentum, ageing, migration and federal variation while maintaining data discipline.

## Q20. Evaluate the impact of globalization on Indian youths with reference to social, political, economic and cultural spheres.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Globalisation and Indian Youth](../../topics/indian-society/globalisation-indian-youth.md) → Youth · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2026 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q20 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Four-sphere evaluation · Indian Society → Globalisation → Youth  
**Topic:** [Globalisation and Indian youth](../../topics/indian-society/globalisation-indian-youth.md)  
**Demand:** Evaluate distinct social, political, economic and cultural channels, with benefits, risks, uneven distribution and a graded judgement.

**Compact framework:** SPEC matrix—Social → Political → Economic → Cultural → intersectional qualification.

### Model answer

Globalisation has redistributed opportunity, identity and voice among Indian youth, but its effects are uneven by skill, gender, class, region and digital access.

**Socially**, global education, migration and digital networks widen peer communities and can increase autonomy in work, lifestyle and partner choice. Skilled young women’s urban migration may expand mobility and income, yet family ties are usually renegotiated through remittances and digital contact rather than simply severed. Online networks also expose youth to cyberbullying and thinner support systems.

**Politically**, global rights, climate and gender discourses enter domestic debate through digital media. Issue-based mobilisation—from citizenship-law protests to climate campaigns—can organise rapidly outside party structures. However, online visibility is not sustained formal participation; misinformation, echo chambers and polarisation can weaken deliberation.

**Economically**, IT-BPO, global value chains, start-ups and remote work broaden aspirations and labour markets. Platform work lowers entry barriers but transfers demand risk and social-security gaps to workers. Gains favour those with language, digital and professional skills, widening intra-youth inequality.

**Culturally**, streaming media, global brands and English-language aspiration encourage some homogenisation and consumer status competition. Yet **glocalisation** produces regional-language content, hybrid music, fashion and India-specific products; youth are active cultural adapters, not passive recipients.

The same platform can therefore create livelihood, political voice and cultural creativity while concentrating data power and insecurity.

**Conclusion:** Globalisation has neither uniformly liberated nor culturally uprooted Indian youth. Its net effect depends on capabilities, labour protection, media literacy, affordable connectivity and the continued strength of local institutions and identities.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately evaluates all four demanded spheres and connects each claim to an Indian mechanism, example and counterpoint.
