### Q1(a). How does Spinoza explain the relationship between states of mind and bodily processes?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Rationalism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/rationalism.md) → Spinoza · psychophysical parallelism · mental causation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Rationalism—Spinoza; substance and mind–body relation · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Spinoza · psychophysical parallelism · mental causation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `spinoza-mind-body-parallelism`

**Demand decoding:** Explain Spinoza’s positive relation between mind and body, contrast it with Cartesian interaction, and briefly assess whether identity without cross-attribute causation preserves mental efficacy.

**Argument map:** one substance → thought and extension as attributes → mind as idea of body → identical order without interaction → elegance versus epiphenomenal worry.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Spinoza dissolves, rather than solves, Cartesian interactionism: mind and body are not two substances exchanging causal influence but one finite mode expressed under the attributes of Thought and Extension.

In *Ethics* II, Proposition 7, “the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.” The human mind is the idea of the human body (*Ethics* II, P13). Thus a decision and its bodily process are not two events linked across an ontological gulf; they are the same modal occurrence understood mentally and physically. Causal explanation remains attribute-bound: ideas are explained through ideas, bodies through bodily causes.

This parallelism avoids Descartes’ problem of how the unextended could move the extended. However, it appears to weaken autonomous mental causation and ordinary free will. Spinoza replies that both descriptions express one necessary immanent order; freedom is adequate self-understanding, not uncaused choice.

**Qualified evaluation:** The account is metaphysically economical, but its success depends on accepting Spinoza’s one-substance, two-attribute framework.

**Why this earns marks:** It states identity, parallel order, textual anchors, anti-interactionism and the principal objection within 10-mark proportion.

### Q1(b). Explain the distinction between Intuitive knowledge and Demonstrative knowledge as propounded by Locke.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Locke · degrees of knowledge · immediacy and mediation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Locke; theory of knowledge · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Locke · degrees of knowledge · immediacy and mediation · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `locke-intuitive-demonstrative-knowledge`

**Demand decoding:** Define Locke’s conception of knowledge, distinguish the two degrees by mode and certainty, explain why demonstration is a chain of intuitions, and locate sensitive knowledge.

**Argument map:** agreement of ideas → immediate perception = intuition → intermediate proofs = demonstration → derivative certainty → memory/error qualification.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Locke distinguishes intuitive and demonstrative knowledge by whether the agreement of ideas is perceived immediately or through intermediate ideas, not by assigning certainty to one and probability to the other.

For Locke, knowledge is perception of the agreement or disagreement of ideas (*Essay* IV.i.2).

**Intuitive knowledge** occurs when the mind immediately perceives agreement or disagreement without an intervening idea—for example, white is not black, three exceeds two, or one’s own existence. It is irresistible, instantaneous and Locke’s clearest degree of certainty.

**Demonstrative knowledge** uses intermediate ideas or “proofs,” as in mathematics or Locke’s demonstration of God. Every link is intuitively perceived; demonstration is therefore a chain of intuitions. It remains knowledge, not probability, but is less luminous because it depends on attention and memory.

Locke also recognises **sensitive knowledge** of the present existence of external particulars, ranked lowest and somewhat awkwardly related to his definition.

**Objection and reply:** If all links are intuitive, demonstration may seem no distinct degree. Locke locates the distinction in mediate, successive access, though fallible memory strains its certainty.

**Evaluation:** The doctrine persuasively distinguishes luminosity of evidence while keeping both forms within knowledge.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives Locke’s definition, mechanism, examples, hierarchy and a precise critical qualification.

### Q1(c). Explain Hegel’s idea that the concept of the Absolute as being is the concept of the Absolute as becoming as a process of self-development.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Hegel](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/hegel.md) → Hegel · Being–Nothing–Becoming · Absolute as subject · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Hegel—dialectical method and absolute idealism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hegel · Being–Nothing–Becoming · Absolute as subject · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hegel-absolute-being-becoming`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish pure Being at the beginning of the *Logic* from the completed Absolute, explain the dialectical transition, and show why self-development is constitutive rather than temporal.

**Argument map:** indeterminate Being → indistinguishable Nothing → Becoming → determinate negation/sublation → Being–Essence–Concept → Absolute as result and self-return.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Hegel, the Absolute is not inert Being behind change; it is Being that becomes intelligible only through its own mediated development.

The *Science of Logic* begins with **pure Being** (*Sein*), wholly indeterminate. Because it contains no differentiating content, it is indistinguishable from pure Nothing (*Nichts*). Their truth is **Becoming** (*Werden*), the unity of coming-to-be and ceasing-to-be. This is not a temporal cosmogony but the instability of trying to think either abstraction separately.

The same dialectical movement proceeds through determinate negation and **sublation** (*Aufhebung*), which cancels one-sidedness, preserves valid content and raises it into a richer unity. The completed Absolute is therefore the whole path—Being through Essence to Concept and Absolute Idea. As the *Phenomenology* says, substance must also be understood as subject: the Absolute differentiates itself and returns to itself in self-conscious Spirit.

**Qualification:** The first transition illustrates Hegel’s method but does not by itself prove every later transition necessary.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates beginning from result, explains becoming and sublation, and avoids treating the Absolute as a changing cosmic person.

### Q1(d). Compare and contrast the notion of entelechy as presented in Aristotle’s and Leibniz’s philosophy.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Aristotle and Leibniz · entelechy · actuality versus monad · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Aristotle—actuality/potentiality; Rationalism—Leibnizian substance · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Aristotle and Leibniz · entelechy · actuality versus monad · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Secondary route:** [Rationalism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Rationalism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aristotle-leibniz-entelechy`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the shared emphasis on intrinsic activity, then expose the category shift from Aristotelian actuality to Leibnizian simple substance.

**Argument map:** intrinsic completion shared → Aristotle: form actualising matter → soul as first actuality → Leibniz: monad as entelechy → perception/appetition → hylomorphic compound versus immaterial unity.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Both philosophers use **entelechy** for intrinsic completion and activity, but Aristotle primarily means actuality within a hylomorphic compound, whereas Leibniz means an immaterial simple substance.

For Aristotle, *entelecheia* means actuality or realised potentiality. Form actualises matter. In *De Anima* II.1, soul is the “first actuality” of an organic body potentially alive—its living organisation, not normally a separate substance; second actuality is exercised capacity.

Leibniz transforms the term. In *Monadology* §§18–19, simple monads are “entelechies,” internally active through perception and appetition. They are immaterial, indivisible and “windowless”; bodies are well-founded phenomena coordinated through pre-established harmony.

Thus Aristotle’s entelechy is primarily what a hylomorphic substance **is in act**; Leibniz’s entelechy is the simple substance itself. Both reject passive substance, but Leibniz substantialises what Aristotle treats as immanent actuality.

**Objection and reply:** Leibniz may seem merely to rename substantial form. Yet a monad neither informs matter nor forms a compound with it; the category is transformed.

**Evaluation:** Leibniz revives Aristotle’s anti-passivist insight but abandons Aristotle’s matter–form unity.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives the precise continuity and decisive difference, supported by both primary texts.

### Q1(e). “I can prove now, for instance, that two human hands exist.” How does Moore use this illustration to prove the existence of an external world? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → G. E. Moore · external-world proof · scepticism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 10 marks · about 150 words · Syllabus unit: Moore—defence of common sense · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: G. E. Moore · external-world proof · scepticism · Difficulty: Moderate  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `moore-proof-external-world`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Moore’s proof and its criteria, then assess the sceptical charge that the premise is precisely what needs proof.

**Argument map:** here is right hand + here is left hand → two external objects → external world → proof conditions → burden reversal → question-begging objection.

#### Model answer

In “Proof of an External World” (1939), Moore raises his right hand—“Here is one hand”—then his left—“and here is another.” Since hands are things encountered in space, at least two external objects exist; therefore an external world exists.

Moore claims a rigorous proof needs: (i) premises different from the conclusion; (ii) premises known, not merely believed; and (iii) a conclusion validly following from them. His demonstration satisfies these conditions. Crucially, inability to analyse “material object” or further prove “here is a hand” does not cancel ordinary knowledge. Moore reverses the sceptic’s burden: any argument denying hands must use premises more certain than the commonsense proposition denied.

The strongest objection is dialectical: the sceptic doubts precisely Moore’s claim to **know** the premises, so the gesture may beg the question rather than answer scepticism. Moore replies that known premises need not themselves always be proved.

**Evaluation:** The proof powerfully challenges sceptical standards but does not compel someone who rejects Moore’s comparative certainty claim.

**Why this earns marks:** It includes the three proof conditions, philosophical strategy, objection, reply and bounded verdict.

### Q2(a). What, according to Aristotle, is the fundamental problem of metaphysics? Discuss this in relation with Aristotle’s account of the essence of the world, where he rejects the views of both the Atomists and Plato.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Plato–Aristotle](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/plato-aristotle.md) → Aristotle · being qua being · substance · anti-atomism and anti-separation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Plato and Aristotle—substance, form/matter, causation, actuality/potentiality · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Aristotle · being qua being · substance · anti-atomism and anti-separation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Plato–Aristotle](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Plato-Aristotle.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `aristotle-substance-atomists-plato`

**Demand decoding:** Establish Aristotle’s fundamental question, present his positive hylomorphic account, test both rival ontologies, and assess whether his middle path really explains unity and change.

**Argument map:** being qua being → focal priority of substance → hylomorphic individual → four causes and potentiality/actuality → Atomists explain too little → Plato locates form wrongly → qualified middle path.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Aristotle’s fundamental metaphysical problem is “What is being?” reduced, through the focal priority of substance (*ousia*), to “What is substance?” A satisfactory answer must preserve individual reality, intelligible essence and genuine change.

In *Metaphysics* IV, first philosophy studies being **qua being** and first principles. Because “being” is said in many ways, Aristotle orders quality, quantity and relation to primary substance. In the central books, sensible substance is a **hylomorphic** compound: matter supplies potentiality; immanent form is essence and actuality. Change is the actualisation of a potency, while complete explanation requires material, formal, efficient and final causes.

**Against the Atomists:** Leucippus and Democritus explain composites through indivisible bodies differing in shape, order and position within void. Aristotle grants their attempt to preserve permanent constituents, but argues:

1. atoms and impact provide, at most, material and efficient causes, not formal unity or final function;
2. eternal motion is assumed rather than explained;
3. extended yet indivisible magnitudes strain continuity;
4. rearrangement explains aggregates better than generation of a new substance;
5. regular organic functioning “always or for the most part” is not adequately explained by chance;
6. a heap of atoms does not explain why an organism is one *this-something*.

**Against Plato:** Aristotle accepts the demand for stable essences but rejects separated Forms. Separation duplicates entities; “participation” is metaphorical; changeless Forms cannot explain motion or generation; self-predication produces the Third Man regress; and an essence apart from a thing cannot make that individual what it is. The universal, predicable of many, cannot be primary individual substance.

Aristotle’s alternative places form **in** the particular. The Atomists retain particulars but lose form; Plato retains form but separates it from particulars; Aristotle unites them through hylomorphism and potentiality–actuality.

**Objection and reply:** Immanent form may appear merely to relocate Plato’s problem, and prime matter remains obscure. Aristotle replies that location is explanatory: form is the actuality and internal cause of this compound. Modern mechanism weakens his physics, yet functional organisation remains philosophically important.

**Qualified evaluation:** Aristotle provides a powerful anti-reductionist theory of organised beings, though the individuation of forms and teleology remain contested.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the one problem, two rejections and positive solution with named texts, arguments, objections and proportionate evaluation.

### Q2(b). How does Hume account for ideas of reason? How does Kant respond to Hume’s views in this regard? Critically discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Empiricism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/empiricism.md) → Hume and Kant · relations of ideas, causality and synthetic a priori · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Empiricism—Hume; Kant—synthetic a priori and categories · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Hume and Kant · relations of ideas, causality and synthetic a priori · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Empiricism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Empiricism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `hume-kant-ideas-of-reason`

**Demand decoding:** Avoid equating Hume’s “relations of ideas” with Kant’s technical Ideas of Reason; reconstruct Hume’s fork and causal scepticism, then show Kant’s synthetic-a-priori response and its limits.

**Argument map:** impressions → ideas → relations of ideas/matters of fact → necessity absent from experience → custom → Kant’s missing third class → categories condition experience → phenomena-only limitation.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Hume confines demonstrative reason to relations of ideas and explains factual expectation psychologically; Kant responds that objective experience itself presupposes synthetic a priori forms and categories.

Hume’s **copy principle** derives simple ideas from impressions, subject to the missing-shade qualification. His Fork distinguishes **relations of ideas**—a priori, necessary and demonstrable, as in mathematics—from **matters of fact**, whose contraries remain conceivable. Causal necessity is neither sensed nor demonstrable. Repeated conjunction produces custom, and custom generates expectation; reason cannot non-circularly prove that nature will remain uniform.

Kant accepts Hume’s challenge but rejects the exhaustiveness of the Fork. Mathematics and “every event has a cause” are **synthetic a priori**: informative yet necessary. Through the Copernican turn, space and time structure sensibility, while categories such as causality synthesise the manifold. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*’s Second Analogy, objective succession—distinguished from my subjective order of apprehension—requires rule-governed causal order. This necessity applies to phenomena, not things in themselves.

Kant’s technical **Ideas of pure reason**—soul, world and God—seek the unconditioned but yield no constitutive knowledge; they must not be confused with Humean relations of ideas.

**Objection:** Hume may ask whether experience requires Kant’s specific categorical apparatus rather than learned regularity. Conversely, Hume’s psychology seems unable to explain objective necessity.

**Evaluation:** Kant identifies a genuine third possibility and explains lawful objectivity, but purchases it with a demanding transcendental idealism whose deduction remains contestable.

**Why this earns marks:** It controls the terminology, reconstructs both arguments and gives a balanced critical verdict.

### Q2(c). Describe Sartre’s notion of consciousness as ‘nothing’. How is it different from Husserl’s notion of consciousness? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Sartre and Husserl · intentional consciousness · nothingness and transcendental ego · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Phenomenology and Existentialism · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Sartre and Husserl · intentional consciousness · nothingness and transcendental ego · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Secondary route:** [Phenomenology—Husserl](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Phenomenology-Husserl.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `sartre-consciousness-nothing-husserl`

**Demand decoding:** State the shared phenomenological basis, explain Sartrean nothingness positively, contrast the status of ego, constitution and freedom, and qualify the opposition historically.

**Argument map:** intentionality shared → no inner contents → ego as reflected object → for-itself as non-self-identical → nihilation/freedom → Husserlian constituting ego → objections and qualified divergence.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Sartre radicalises Husserlian intentionality: consciousness is “nothing” not because it is unreal, but because it has no thing-like content, fixed essence or self-identical ego within it.

Both philosophers hold that consciousness is intentional—always consciousness **of** something—and reject the model of ideas as inner pictures. Both recognise pre-reflective self-awareness. Sartre, however, argues in *The Transcendence of the Ego* that the ego is not an inhabitant or transcendental source within consciousness; it is a transcendent object constituted in reflection. Pre-reflective consciousness is non-positionally aware of itself while directed toward the world.

In *Being and Nothingness*, the **for-itself** (*pour-soi*) lacks the plenitude of being-in-itself. It “is what it is not and is not what it is”: it distances itself from facticity, projects possibilities and introduces negation through **nihilation** (*néantisation*). Pierre’s experienced absence in the café shows that negation structures experience rather than merely linguistic judgment. This lack of fixed being grounds freedom and makes bad faith possible.

Husserl’s mature transcendental phenomenology, by contrast, retains constituting subjectivity or a pure ego, together with inner time-consciousness and habitualities, to explain the unity and constitution of noematic sense. Sartre treats the object and intentional field as sufficient unifiers and shifts from constitution to an ontology of freedom.

**Objection and reply:** Husserl can answer that the pure ego is not an inner object but a formal pole of acts, solving diachronic unity that Sartre must explain. Moreover, early Husserl was less egological; hence Sartre may be read, with Dan Zahavi, as a reorientation within phenomenology rather than a simple refutation.

**Evaluation:** Husserl explains how the world has sense for consciousness; Sartre explains how non-coinciding consciousness opens nothingness and freedom.

**Why this earns marks:** It begins from shared ground, states the exact difference, uses primary texts/examples and supplies a scholarly qualification.

### Q3(a). Why are states of consciousness ascribed to anything at all and why are they ascribed to the same things to which we also ascribe physical properties? How does Strawson answer these questions in his work Individuals?
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Quine–Strawson](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/quine-strawson.md) → P. F. Strawson · person as primitive · M- and P-predicates · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Strawson—theory of basic particulars and persons · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: P. F. Strawson · person as primitive · M- and P-predicates · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Quine–Strawson](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Quine-Strawson.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `strawson-person-consciousness-ascription`

**Demand decoding:** Answer two distinct “why” questions: why experiences require subjects, and why their subjects are the same publicly identifiable bearers of physical predicates.

**Argument map:** identification/re-identification → experiences not free-floating → persons as subjects → M/P predicates → self-ascription and other-ascription reciprocity → dualism/no-ownership rejected → descriptive-metaphysical limit.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In *Individuals* (1959), Strawson argues that the concept of a **person** is logically primitive: experiences are intelligible only as states of identifiable subjects, and the same embodied subjects must bear both psychological and physical predicates.

First, why ascribe consciousness to anything? Pains, memories and intentions are not free-floating particulars that can be counted and re-identified independently of owners. To distinguish my pain from yours or this memory from another requires criteria for identifying a continuing subject. The “no-ownership” view says experiences are merely causally dependent on bodies, but then cannot explain which body uniquely organises which experiences without covertly reintroducing the person.

Second, why ascribe consciousness to the same things that have physical properties? Strawson distinguishes:

- **M-predicates**, applicable to material bodies—“is in the room,” “weighs 70 kg”;
- **P-predicates**, psychological—“is in pain,” “intends to leave.”

Both are predicated of one entity, the person. P-predicates possess a dual grammar: I self-ascribe pain without observing my behaviour, while I ascribe pain to others through public behavioural and situational criteria. Yet both uses belong to the same predicate and presuppose embodied subjects identifiable in a shared spatio-temporal framework.

This reciprocity blocks Cartesian dualism. An independently unidentifiable ego cannot ground other-ascription; nor is psychological predication merely inference from movement to a hidden substance. It also blocks reduction to the body, because first-person avowal is not behavioural observation. The person is neither mind plus body nor a third substance, but a primitive concept under which both predicate classes apply.

**Objection:** A metaphysical dualist may accept ordinary person-talk while claiming it conceals an underlying mind. Strawson’s reply is descriptive: revisionary theories cannot replace the personal scheme they already presuppose.

**Qualified evaluation:** Strawson establishes the conceptual priority of persons and dissolves a false starting point, but he does not provide a causal theory of consciousness or prove physicalism.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates both burdens, reconstructs the self/other-ascription argument and states the exact anti-dualist but non-physicalist conclusion.

### Q3(b). Explain Heidegger’s conception of authenticity and inauthenticity as modes of Dasein’s being.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Existentialism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/existentialism.md) → Heidegger · Dasein · authenticity/inauthenticity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Existentialism—Heidegger · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Heidegger · Dasein · authenticity/inauthenticity · Difficulty: Moderate-high  
**Classification route:** [Existentialism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Existentialism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `heidegger-authenticity-inauthenticity`

**Demand decoding:** Explain both modes without moralising them, connect inauthenticity to the “they” and authenticity to conscience, death and resoluteness, then assess formal emptiness.

**Argument map:** being-in-the-world/being-with → falling into the “they” → idle talk, curiosity, ambiguity → call of conscience → being-toward-death → anticipatory resoluteness → modified everydayness.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** In *Being and Time*, authenticity (*Eigentlichkeit*) and inauthenticity are existential modes of Dasein’s self-relation, not moral virtue and vice.

Dasein is constitutively being-in-the-world and being-with others. “Initially and for the most part,” it is dispersed in **the “they”** (*das Man*): what “one” thinks, says and does. This **falling** (*Verfallen*) appears as idle talk, which circulates unappropriated opinion; curiosity, which seeks novelty without dwelling; and ambiguity, in which everything seems understood but nothing is genuinely owned. Inauthenticity is therefore not an accidental fall from purity but ordinary absorption in public possibilities.

Authenticity arises when the **call of conscience** recalls Dasein from the “they” to responsibility for its thrown projection. Conscience supplies no moral code; it discloses Dasein’s null, groundless choosing. **Being-toward-death** individualises because death is one’s ownmost, non-relational, certain yet temporally indeterminate possibility. Instead of evasion—“one dies”—Dasein adopts **anticipatory resoluteness** (*vorlaufende Entschlossenheit*), taking over finite possibilities as its own.

Authenticity does not abolish social existence or facticity. It modifies everyday being-with by owning rather than anonymously inheriting possibilities.

**Objection:** Because Heidegger gives no substantive ethical criteria, an authentic choice could be morally abhorrent. His defence is that fundamental ontology analyses ownership of existence, not ethics; yet this formalism limits its normative guidance.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses Heidegger’s exact structures, avoids the moralistic trap and provides a relevant critical qualification.

### Q3(c). Can words be viewed as the ideas in the mind of the person who uses them? Answer with reference to the philosophy of later Wittgenstein.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Later Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/later-wittgenstein.md) → Wittgenstein · ideational theory of meaning · normativity · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Later Wittgenstein—meaning and use, language-games, private language · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Wittgenstein · ideational theory of meaning · normativity · Difficulty: Moderate-high  
**Classification route:** [Later Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Later-Wittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `later-wittgenstein-words-and-mental-ideas`

**Demand decoding:** Reject ideas as a universal meaning-bearer without denying mental imagery; show why interpretation, diverse grammar and rule-governed use defeat a private foundation.

**Argument map:** Augustinian picture → same image permits multiple uses → tool analogy/language-games → understanding as technique → private “S” lacks criterion → images as optional samples.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Later Wittgenstein denies that words generally mean private ideas in a speaker’s mind; meaning is constituted by norm-governed use in shared language-games.

In *Philosophical Investigations* §43, “for a large class of cases” the meaning of a word is its use. A mental cube-image does not by itself determine whether “cube” names a solid, drawing, toy or arithmetical object; the image itself requires interpretation within a practice. Moreover, words such as “if,” “five” and “pain” have radically different grammatical functions, so no uniform inner presentation can explain them.

Understanding is mastery of a technique—the ability to “go on” appropriately after training and correction—not the occurrence of a private episode. Ostensive definition also presupposes a language-game: pointing at two red squares does not independently decide whether colour, shape or number is meant.

The private-language argument sharpens the point. If “S” is fixed solely by inwardly attending to a sensation, there is no independent standard separating correct recurrence from merely seeming correct. Where whatever seems right is right, the distinction between right and wrong disappears.

**Objection:** Silent understanding may occur without behaviour. **Reply:** a capacity need not be exercised on every occasion; Wittgenstein gives a grammatical criterion, not crude behaviourism.

**Evaluation:** Images and ideas may accompany speech or function as public-type samples, but cannot autonomously ground word meaning.

**Why this earns marks:** It targets the exact thesis, uses §43 and §258 logic, and preserves mental life without making it semantically foundational.

### Q4(a). “Wittgenstein’s difficulty about not being able to say anything within a given language about the structure of that language can be met by the idea of a hierarchy of languages.” Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons and justifications for your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/moore-russell-early-wittgenstein.md) → Wittgenstein, Russell and later Tarski · metalanguage hierarchy · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Early Wittgenstein—picture theory and saying/showing; Russell/analytic philosophy · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Wittgenstein, Russell and later Tarski · metalanguage hierarchy · Difficulty: Very high  
**Classification route:** [Moore–Russell–Early Wittgenstein](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Moore-Russell-EarlyWittgenstein.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `wittgenstein-hierarchy-of-languages`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct why language cannot state its own logical form, present Russell’s hierarchy accurately, distinguish formal-semantic success from transcendental failure, and give a reasoned verdict.

**Argument map:** picture shares form with fact → form shown, not said → *Tractatus* self-problem → Russell L1/L2/L3 → Tarskian vindication → regress/open hierarchy → ethics and form remain unsayable → qualified disagreement.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** I agree only in a restricted formal-semantic sense. A hierarchy of object-language and metalanguage handles self-reference and truth-definition, but it does not overcome Wittgenstein’s deeper claim that logical form, as the condition of representation, cannot itself be represented.

In the *Tractatus*, a proposition pictures a possible state of affairs because both share logical form. Yet a picture “cannot depict its pictorial form: it displays it” (2.172). Propositions **say** contingent facts; logical form, internal relations and the language–world relation are **shown** (4.1212). Hence philosophy clarifies rather than states doctrines. The work’s own sentences generate a self-referential difficulty, answered through the ladder metaphor at 6.54.

Russell’s 1922 Introduction proposes:

1. language L1 cannot describe its own structure;
2. metalanguage L2 can describe L1;
3. L3 describes L2, and so on without a final language.

This proposal later receives formal support from Tarski: an adequate truth-definition for an object-language requires a richer metalanguage, preventing semantic paradox.

However, three limitations remain. First, each metalanguage possesses a form it cannot characterise internally; the difficulty moves upward rather than disappears. Second, an open hierarchy never reaches a standpoint from which the total limits of language can be stated. Third, Wittgenstein’s target is not merely missing syntactic vocabulary but the form common to representation as such. Ethical value, the metaphysical subject and the mystical are not made factual by ascent to another formal language.

**Objection:** If L2 says what L1 merely shows, the distinction is dissolved. **Reply:** L2 can state structural facts about L1, but its own form remains shown, and no level exits representation altogether.

The traditional ineffability reading treats the *Tractatus* as gesturing toward unsayable truths; the resolute reading treats its apparent doctrines therapeutically. On the latter, Russell’s hierarchy answers a doctrinal problem Wittgenstein did not intend.

**Conclusion:** The hierarchy solves a formal-semantic problem but evades the transcendental one: it converts one unsayable limit into indefinitely many limits.

**Why this earns marks:** It attributes the hierarchy to Russell, controls the Tarski anachronism, distinguishes two problems and defends a qualified verdict.

### Q4(b). Analyze and discuss the different ways in which Kant and Hegel conceived the relationship between reality and our thinking about reality.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Kant](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/kant.md) → transcendental idealism versus absolute idealism · thought–reality relation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Kant and Hegel · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: transcendental idealism versus absolute idealism · thought–reality relation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Kant](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Kant.md) · **Secondary route:** [Hegel](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Hegel.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `kant-hegel-thought-reality`

**Demand decoding:** Give two positive accounts before comparison; explain their shared anti-copy insight, then distinguish Kantian restriction from Hegelian dialectical identity-in-difference.

**Argument map:** Kant: intuitions/categories constitute phenomena → noumenal limit → Hegel: finite categories self-negate → thought and being co-develop → critique of unknowable in-itself → reciprocal objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Kant and Hegel both reject knowledge as passive copying, but Kant limits constitutive thought to possible experience, whereas Hegel treats the thought–reality divide itself as a finite determination overcome within the self-development of the Absolute.

Kant’s Copernican turn asks how objects **as objects of possible experience** conform to our cognitive conditions. Space and time are forms of sensibility; categories such as substance and causality synthesise intuitions into objective experience. Thus understanding “makes nature” formally—the law-governed unity of appearances—not sensory matter, empirical laws or things in themselves. Kant is an empirical realist but transcendental idealist. The negative noumenon marks what cannot be theoretically known independently of our sensible conditions.

Hegel accepts that thought is constitutive but rejects Kant’s fixed phenomenon/noumenon opposition. Through immanent dialectic, a finite category reveals its own inadequacy, undergoes determinate negation and is sublated into a richer determination. Reality is not an alien given facing thought; it is intelligible as the developing Concept, culminating in the Absolute as substance and subject. This is not a private mind inventing reality.

Hegel objects that positing an unknowable in-itself while identifying its limit already relates thought to it. Kant can reply that a **negative** noumenon is only a boundary-concept, not positive knowledge. Conversely, Kant may charge Hegel with converting a demand for intelligibility into unwarranted knowledge of the Absolute; showing instability in a distinction does not prove the entire system.

**Evaluation:** Kant secures critical objectivity by restraint; Hegel secures systematic unity by overcoming restraint. The gain of each is the other’s cost.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives both accounts, the common premise, reciprocal objections and a genuinely comparative conclusion.

### Q4(c). How did the logical positivists try to make philosophy a handmaid to science and establish a unified language of science? Why did their project fail? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Logical Positivism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/logical-positivism.md) → 2026-philosophy-p1-q4c · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Classification route:** [Logical Positivism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/western/Logical-Positivism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `logical-positivism-unified-science-failure`

**Demand decoding:** Explain philosophy’s reduced clarificatory role, the analytic/empirical programme and unified language, then identify multiple internal and external reasons for failure without dismissing all its achievements.

**Argument map:** logical analysis → verification → metaphysics excluded → observation/protocol basis → physicalist public language → universal laws/theoretical terms/protocol dispute/self-application/Quine → residual legacy.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Logical positivists subordinated philosophy to science by making it logical clarification rather than speculative world-description; their stronger unification project failed because neither meaning nor science could be reduced to one verification rule and observation language.

The Vienna Circle—Schlick, Carnap and Neurath, popularised by Ayer—divided meaningful propositions into analytic truths and empirically testable claims. Philosophy would expose pseudo-problems produced by misleading grammar, clarify scientific concepts and formal inference, and eliminate metaphysics rather than rival science.

For unity, early reconstructionist projects sought to translate scientific discourse into elementary experience-statements. Neurath and Carnap moved toward a public **physicalist language** and a coordinated “encyclopedia” of science. Shared protocols were expected to make claims across disciplines intersubjectively comparable.

The stronger programme failed because:

1. strict verification excludes universal scientific laws; weak or indirect confirmation blurs the promised demarcation;
2. theoretical terms derive testability from whole systems of laws and auxiliaries, not one-to-one translation into observations;
3. Schlick’s incorrigible momentary affirmations and Neurath’s revisable public protocols supplied no agreed foundation;
4. the verification principle appears neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, unless treated merely as a proposal;
5. Quine challenged both the analytic–synthetic distinction and sentence-by-sentence reduction;
6. different sciences resist complete reduction to one vocabulary even while remaining mutually coordinated.

**Objection and reply:** Positivists can recast verification as a methodological proposal and unity as coordination rather than reduction. This saves a weaker programme but concedes the original claim to a uniquely decisive criterion and language.

**Qualification:** Carnap’s principle of tolerance already softened the claim to a uniquely privileged language.

**Evaluation:** The reductive unity project failed, but logical analysis, formal semantics, intersubjective testing and scientific cooperation survived as enduring contributions.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects the positive programme to distinct failure mechanisms and avoids confusing Circle verificationism with Popper’s falsification.

**Argument map:** sign in subject → presence in positive instances → absence in negative instances → no stronger contradiction → no counter-reason → valid inference.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya validates an inferential sign by testing its presence, concomitance and undefeatedness through five characteristics.

In Nyāya, inference (anumāna) arises through a sign or reason (hetu) known to be pervaded by the probandum (sādhya). A valid hetu has five characteristics:

1. **Presence in the subject (pakṣadharmatā):** smoke is on the hill.

Together they test subject-presence, invariable concomitance (vyāpti) and undefeatedness. Their failures appear as fallacies of reason (hetvābhāsa), though not in a rigid one-to-one mapping.

**Objection and reply:** Cārvāka denies that finite observations establish vyāpti. Nyāya invokes repeated observation, removal of hidden conditions and reasoning (tarka), though the issue remains contested.

**Evaluation:** The scheme is a powerful diagnostic ideal, but its success depends on Nyāya’s account of grasping vyāpti.

**Why this earns marks:** It names, explains and exemplifies all five while adding a technically accurate qualification.

**Classification route:** [Sāṃkhya](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Samkhya.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `samkhya-mango-seed-causation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the example through all principal arguments for effect-pre-existence, distinguish latent presence from miniature preformation, and reject both Nyāya beginning-theory and Advaita apparent transformation fairly.

**Argument map:** determinate seed selected → effect latent → five reasons → guṇa transformation → Nyāya novelty objection/reply → Advaita appearance objection/reply → qualified verdict.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Sāṃkhya explains the mango tree through **effect-pre-existence (satkāryavāda)** and **real transformation (pariṇāmavāda)**: the effect is latent in its material cause and becomes manifest through suitable conditions; it is neither newly created from non-being nor merely apparent.

A mango seed is chosen rather than sand or another seed because its material constitution and potency are specifically ordered to a mango tree. The tree is not spatially hidden as a miniature object; its determinate effect-form exists unmanifest in the causal continuum. Soil, water, heat and time enable real transformation of the guṇic material basis.

Sāṃkhya offers five arguments:

1. **Non-production of the non-existent (asadakaraṇāt):** sheer non-being cannot be produced; oil never comes from sand.
2. **Selection of a material cause (upādāna-grahaṇāt):** one takes mango seed for mango, milk for curd and threads for cloth.
3. **Not everything arises from everything (sarvasambhavābhāvāt):** absolute non-being cannot explain causal specificity.
4. **A competent cause produces only what it can (śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt):** determinate potency points to a determinate latent effect.
5. **The effect has the nature of its cause (kāraṇa-bhāvāt):** material continuity persists through changed configuration.

Production is therefore manifestation (**abhivyakti**) through a new state (**avasthā**), not useless repetition. Sāṃkhya’s account is real transformation: unlike apparent transformation (**vivartavāda**), Prakṛti genuinely evolves.

**Against Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika:** beginning-theory (**asatkāryavāda/ārambhavāda**) says the tree as a new whole was previously absent (**prāgabhāva**) and has novel properties. Sāṃkhya replies that absence from perception is not absolute non-being and that causal specificity is best explained by latent effect. Nyāya can counter that cause-specific powers explain selectivity without identity between cause and effect; Sāṃkhya must therefore justify equating potency with pre-existence.

**Against Advaita:** apparent transformation locates empirical change within ignorance while Brahman remains unchanged. Sāṃkhya argues that seed/tree specificity and material continuity require real transformation. Advaita replies that real empirical causation is admitted conventionally; it denies only its ultimate status.

**Evaluation:** The mango case strongly supports determinate material continuity and rejects creation ex nihilo, but it does not conclusively prove that causal potency is identical with a pre-existent effect.

**Why this earns marks:** It applies all five arguments to the example, reconstructs two rivals accurately and ends with a critical rather than sectarian verdict.

### Q6(c). Bring out the main points of contestation between Naiydyikas and Prabhakara Mimarhsakas with reference to knowledge through words (Sabdabodha).
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → 2026-philosophy-p1-q6c · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā—verbal knowledge · Schools/doctrine/subproblem: Naiyāyika and Prābhākara · sentence cognition (śābdabodha) · connected meaning · Difficulty: Very high  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Secondary route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-prabhakara-shabdabodha`

**Demand decoding:** Identify the shared problem and sentence conditions, then contrast isolated-first and connected-denotation accounts, including their strongest objections and replies.

**Argument map:** successive words/unified meaning → four conditions shared → Nyāya word meanings then connection → Prābhākara connected meaning from outset → language-learning argument → novelty/stability dispute.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Naiyāyikas and Prābhākara Mīmāṃsakas agree that verbal cognition (**śābdabodha**) is a unified sentence-meaning, but disagree over where syntactic connection enters cognition.

Both recognise four conditions: expectancy (**ākāṅkṣā**), semantic fitness (**yogyatā**), proximity (**sannidhi/āsatti**) and intended purport (**tātparya**).

For Nyāya, words first present their own meanings through denotative power (**śakti**). These meanings are retained in memory and then combined, under the four conditions, into a qualifier–qualified cognition (**viśeṣaṇa–viśeṣya anvayabodha**). This is an *abhihitānvaya*-type account, but should not be simply identified with the Bhāṭṭa theory, which invokes an additional semantic power.

Prābhākara defends **connected-denotation theory (anvitābhidhānavāda)**: a word denotes its meaning only as already related to other word-meanings. His language-learning (**vyutpatti**) argument notes that a child hears whole commands—“bring the cow”—and learns through observing action and substitutions; isolated meanings are abstractions from sentence-use. Vedic injunction and action-guidance (**kārya/niyoga**) provide the paradigm.

Nyāya objects that Prābhākara faces circularity, unstable lexical meaning and difficulty with novel sentences or single-word utterances. Prābhākara replies that learners acquire functional roles holistically and that “Fire!” supplies an elliptical sentential context. Prābhākara objects that Nyāya’s extra combination stage is not experienced. Nyāya replies that causal stages need not be introspectable and become evident when separate meanings are grasped but unity fails through lack of expectancy or fitness.

**Evaluation:** Nyāya better explains lexical stability and novelty; Prābhākara better captures contextual acquisition and the immediacy of sentence understanding. Neither is Bhartṛhari’s indivisible sentence doctrine.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the shared floor, exact rival mechanisms, learning argument, reciprocal criticisms and technical cautions.

### Q7(a). Critically explain the arguments presented by the Akhyativadins for explaining erroneous cognition. Evaluate the arguments against Akhyativadins by the Anyathakhyativadins.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Mīmāṃsā](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/mimamsa.md) → 2026-philosophy-p1-q7a · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya—theories of error · Schools/doctrine/subproblem: Prābhākara non-apprehension (akhyāti) versus Nyāya misapprehension (anyathākhyāti) · Difficulty: Very high  
**Classification route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Secondary route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `akhyati-anyathakhyati-error-debate`

**Demand decoding:** Reconstruct Prābhākara’s positive explanatory argument and its link to intrinsic validity, then assess—not merely list—Nyāya’s objections and alternative.

**Argument map:** shell perception valid + silver memory valid → difference not apprehended → erroneous action without false cognition → Nyāya: unitary positive “this is silver” → real silver mislocated → presentation mechanism → reciprocal difficulties.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Prābhākara’s **non-apprehension theory (akhyāti or vivekākhyāti)** explains illusion as failure to discriminate two individually valid cognitions; Nyāya’s **misapprehension theory (anyathākhyāti)** argues that the phenomenology and action of error require a genuinely unitary false cognition.

In the shell–silver case, Prābhākara analyses the episode as follows:

1. defective conditions produce a valid but incomplete perception of “this shining object”;
2. similarity awakens a valid memory of previously encountered silver;
3. the subject fails to apprehend the difference between present perception and past memory;
4. because the remembered silver is not marked as absent and past, the subject acts toward the shell as silver.

No constituent cognition is false: presentation of “this” is valid, and memory preserves genuine past silver. Error lies only in non-discrimination. This protects Prābhākara’s doctrine of **intrinsic validity (svataḥ-prāmāṇya)**: cognition is valid in itself unless later defeated; illusion need not contain an intrinsically false cognition.

Nyāya objects:

1. The subject experiences and asserts one positive judgment—“this is silver”—not two cognitions plus a missing discrimination.
2. Mere non-apprehension cannot explain positive silver-content or goal-directed movement to pick it up.
3. If both cognitions are valid, their fusion should not yield false action without a further defective synthesis.
4. Correction—“this is shell, not silver”—appears to negate a prior positive cognition.

Nyāya’s alternative is **anyathākhyāti**: both “this” and silver are real, but silver known elsewhere is presented here through an extraordinary cognition associated with prior knowledge (**jñānalakṣaṇa-pratyakṣa**). Error is mislocation or wrong synthesis, not apprehension of a nonentity.

The Prābhākara can reply that Nyāya multiplies entities and processes unnecessarily: memory plus present appearance already explain action, and “unitary” phenomenology need not prove a single cognition. Moreover, how can spatially absent silver become perceptually present without making extraordinary perception ad hoc? Nyāya replies that realism requires a real content for the positive presentation, while akhyāti cannot adequately distinguish error from mere uncertainty.

**Evaluation:** Akhyāti elegantly preserves intrinsic validity and explains the contribution of memory; anyathākhyāti better captures positive misidentification and corrective negation. Nyāya’s gain in phenomenological adequacy is purchased by the controversial mechanism of extraordinary presentation. The debate turns on whether error is fundamentally **failure to discriminate** or **positive misplacement**.

**Why this earns marks:** It develops both theories as arguments, links them to wider epistemology, evaluates reciprocal objections and supplies a discriminating verdict.

### Q7(b). Explain the meaning of seven steps of judgement (Saptabhanginaya) and its application in Jaina’s Syadvada for advocating a philosophy of non-absolutism (Anekantavada).
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Jainism](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/jainism.md) → 2026-philosophy-p1-q7b · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Jainism—non-one-sidedness, standpoint and conditional predication · School/doctrine/subproblem: sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī), syādvāda, anekāntavāda · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Jainism](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Jainism.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `jaina-saptabhangi-syadvada-anekantavada`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish ontology, standpoint and logic; state all seven predications in order; apply one example with explicit respects; answer the contradiction and self-reference objections.

**Argument map:** many-sided reality → finite standpoint → conditional “syāt” → seven forms → jar application → no same-respect contradiction → non-absolutist realism.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Jaina sevenfold predication (**saptabhaṅgī**) is the logical expression of non-one-sided reality, not seven truth-values or sceptical hesitation.

**Non-one-sidedness (anekāntavāda)** holds that reality has indefinitely many aspects. **Standpoint theory (nayavāda)** explains why finite judgments grasp only selected aspects. **Conditional predication (syādvāda)** therefore prefixes claims with **syāt**—“in a certain respect”—to prevent a partial truth from becoming absolute.

The seven predications are:

1. **syād asti**—in some respect, it is;
2. **syād nāsti**—in some respect, it is not;
3. **syād asti ca nāsti ca**—in different respects, it is and is not;
4. **syād avaktavyam**—in some respect, it is indescribable;
5. **syād asti ca avaktavyam**—it is and is indescribable;
6. **syād nāsti ca avaktavyam**—it is not and is indescribable;
7. **syād asti ca nāsti ca avaktavyam**—it is, is not and is indescribable.

Take one clay jar. It exists here, now and in jar-form; it does not exist elsewhere, before production or as cloth. Affirmation and negation are both valid across distinct substance–place–time–mode qualifications. “Indescribable” means that opposed qualified aspects cannot be expressed in one simultaneous, unqualified predicate; it is not mystical ignorance.

Critics charge violation of non-contradiction. Jainism replies that contradiction arises only when affirmation and negation occur in the same respect, time and sense. A deeper objection asks whether the universal rule “all claims are conditioned” becomes absolute. The Jaina may consistently qualify that rule too; this limits dogmatism without making all claims equally true.

**Evaluation:** Saptabhaṅgī gives rigorous form to epistemic humility and plural realism, though its proliferating qualifications risk reduced explanatory economy.

**Why this earns marks:** It lists all seven accurately, distinguishes the three doctrines, applies them and handles the strongest objections.

### Q7(c). Do you agree with Nimbarka’s explanation of the nature of God, Souls and Matter? Justify your answer.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Nimbārka · dualistic non-dualism (dvaitādvaita) · God, conscious selves and matter · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Schools of Vedānta · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Nimbārka · dualistic non-dualism (dvaitādvaita) · God, conscious selves and matter · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nimbarka-god-souls-matter`

**Demand decoding:** Present all three realities and their difference–non-difference relation, then give a defended first-person verdict rather than neutral exposition.

**Argument map:** Brahman independent ground → selves conscious finite dependents → matter real non-conscious dependent → difference in properties + non-difference in dependence → devotion/liberation → contradiction objection → qualified agreement.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** I agree with Nimbārka’s dualistic non-dualism (**dvaitādvaita**) as a coherent account of dependent plurality, provided “non-difference” means inseparable dependence rather than numerical identity.

God—Brahman or Kṛṣṇa—is the independent controller and ground. Conscious selves (**cit**) are real, many, finite knowers and agents capable of devotion; liberation does not erase their individuality. Non-conscious reality (**acit**) is also real and includes ordinary primordial matter (**prākṛta**), non-prakritic divine substance (**aprākṛta**) and time (**kāla**) in a standard classification.

Souls and matter differ from God in nature and capacity: the finite knower is not the omniscient Lord, and unconscious matter is not consciousness. Yet they are non-different because they cannot exist, function or be understood independently of Brahman. The sun–rays analogy conveys dependent unity with persistent distinction. Creation is a real transformation of dependent powers, while devotion and grace culminate in communion rather than Advaitic identity.

The main objection is logical: how can the same entities be both different and non-different? Nimbārka replies that the predicates apply in different respects—difference of intrinsic properties, non-difference of ontological dependence. This avoids flat contradiction. However, Dvaita may argue that dependence does not establish real non-difference, while Advaita may deny that ultimate plurality is compatible with non-duality.

**Verdict:** Nimbārka preserves world-reality, individuality and devotion without positing a second independent absolute. Its coherence rests on a relational—not identity-based—reading of non-difference, which I find defensible but not demonstratively compulsory.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers God, souls and matter, explains the central relation, presents rival objections and explicitly justifies a qualified agreement.

### Q8(a). Present an account of Upamana as an instrument of knowledge (Pramana). How is Nyaya view of Upamana different from Mimarhsa view? Discuss.
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/nyaya-vaisesika.md) → 2026-philosophy-p1-q8a · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 20 marks · Syllabus unit: Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā—means of valid knowledge · Schools/doctrine/subproblem: comparison (upamāna) · cognitive result and irreducibility · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Nyaya-Vaisesika.md) · **Secondary route:** [Mīmāṃsā](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Mimamsa.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `nyaya-mimamsa-upamana`

**Demand decoding:** Define comparison as an independent knowledge-source, narrate both versions of the gavaya case, identify their different cognitive fruits and evaluate reciprocal reduction arguments.

**Argument map:** known cow/resemblance → Nyāya prior testimony + present animal → word–referent relation → Mīmāṃsā present resemblance + absent cow → new reverse similarity → irreducibility and objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā both treat **comparison (upamāna)** as an independent means of valid knowledge (**pramāṇa**), but Nyāya makes its result semantic—the word–object relation—whereas Mīmāṃsā makes it relational—new knowledge of similarity in an absent remembered object.

For Nyāya, *Nyāya-sūtra* 1.1.6 defines comparison through similarity to something well known (**prasiddha-sādharmya**). The classical case proceeds:

1. a reliable person says, “A **gavaya** (wild ox) resembles a cow”;
2. the hearer later perceives a cow-like animal in the forest;
3. he remembers the statement;
4. he cognises, “This is what the word ‘gavaya’ denotes.”

The result (**upamiti**) is knowledge of the name–named relation (**saṃjñā–saṃjñi-sambandha**). It is not perception, which shows the animal but not linguistic denotation; not testimony, which did not identify this animal; not memory, which only reproduces the statement; and not inference, because no universal concomitance (**vyāpti**) or probans is used. Testimony, perception and memory are auxiliary; perceived resemblance is operative.

Mīmāṃsā changes the result and does not require prior testimony. Having seen a cow, one later perceives a gavaya as similar and newly cognises: “The previously seen cow resembles this gavaya.” The cow is absent, so this is not perception; that particular relation was never previously known, so it is not memory; and no vyāpti makes it inference.

The difference is therefore:

- Nyāya: familiar cow → unfamiliar gavaya; new semantic knowledge; testimony essential.
- Mīmāṃsā: present gavaya → absent familiar cow; new reverse-similarity knowledge; testimony unnecessary.

Mīmāṃsā objects that Nyāya decomposes into testimony, perception and memory. Nyāya replies that none yields denotation, even jointly, until similarity activates the naming cognition. Nyāya objects that Mīmāṃsā tells us something about an already known cow and can be reduced to memory or inference. Mīmāṃsā replies that the specific relation is novel and that inferential reconstruction presupposes the similarity-cognition it seeks to reduce.

**Qualification:** Bhāṭṭas commonly analyse similarity through shared qualities, while Prābhākaras may treat it as a distinct category; their epistemic contrast with Nyāya remains.

**Evaluation:** Nyāya better explains vocabulary acquisition; Mīmāṃsā more sharply identifies a novel cognition involving an absent object. Both show that the debate turns on precisely **what is newly known**.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives both mechanisms, irreducibility arguments, comparison axes, internal qualification and balanced evaluation.

### Q8(b). Critically explain Ramanuja’s conception of Brahman with special reference to the difficulties in explaining the relation of Brahman to Spirit (Cit) and Matter (Acit).
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → qualified non-dualism (viśiṣṭādvaita) · cit–acit relation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Vedānta—Rāmānuja, Brahman and inseparable dependence · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: qualified non-dualism (viśiṣṭādvaita) · cit–acit relation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `ramanuja-brahman-cit-acit`

**Demand decoding:** Explain qualified Brahman through inseparable existence and the body–soul model, then critically test divine perfection against material change and creaturely agency.

**Argument map:** Brahman as qualified whole → cit/acit real modes → apṛthaksiddhi → body–soul dependence → material/efficient causality → impurity/change objection → freedom/control objection → qualified verdict.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** Rāmānuja conceives Brahman as one qualified whole whose real conscious and non-conscious modes—spirit (**cit**) and matter (**acit**)—exist inseparably in it without being identical to it.

In qualified non-dualism (**viśiṣṭādvaita**), Brahman is personal, omniscient and possessed of auspicious qualities. Cit consists of many conscious selves; acit is non-conscious and mutable matter. Through **inseparable existence (apṛthaksiddhi)** they cannot exist independently of Brahman, yet remain real and distinct. The **body–soul relation (śarīra–śarīrī-bhāva)** explains this unity: selves and world are Brahman’s “body,” wholly supported and controlled by the indwelling divine self and existing for its purposes. Brahman is consequently both material and efficient cause through the transformations of dependent modes.

Two difficulties arise. First, if the body changes, suffers and contains impurity, does Brahman undergo mutation and defect? Rāmānuja replies that changes belong to the modes, especially acit, not to Brahman’s essential perfection; the analogy expresses dependence, not divine embodiment in a literal fleshly sense. Yet critics ask whether real modes of one whole can change without qualifying the whole.

Second, if conscious souls are God’s body under complete control, their freedom and moral responsibility seem threatened. Rāmānuja retains genuine agency, karma and devotion while insisting that every capacity is sustained by God. Dependence need not mean identity or mechanical compulsion, but reconciling exhaustive divine control with robust creaturely agency remains difficult.

**Evaluation:** Apṛthaksiddhi successfully avoids both Advaitic world-denial and independent pluralism, but its central strength—organic unity—is also its pressure point when imperfection and agency are considered.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains Brahman positively and critically tests both cit and acit rather than repeating the body–soul analogy.

### Q8(c). Why does the Dualistic School of Vedanta consider that differences (Bhedas) have separate existence and constitute the unique nature of things? Answer with reference to the theory of five-fold difference (Paficavidhabheda).
**Primary route:** Philosophy Paper I → [Vedānta](../../topics/philosophy-paper-i/vedanta.md) → Dualism (Dvaita) · real difference (bheda) · individuation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 15 marks · Syllabus unit: Vedānta—Madhva and fivefold difference · Thinker/doctrine/subproblem: Dualism (Dvaita) · real difference (bheda) · individuation · Difficulty: High  
**Classification route:** [Vedānta](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Philosophy/paper-1/indian/Vedanta.md) · **Proposed topic slug:** `madhva-fivefold-difference`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why difference is constitutive and real, enumerate all five differences, show the work each performs, and distinguish irreducible identity from independent existence.

**Argument map:** difference directly disclosed → identity requires exclusion → five differences → God’s independence/non-divine dependence → anti-Advaita case → unity and hierarchy objections.

#### Model answer

**Thesis:** For Madhva’s Dualism (**Dvaita**), difference (**bheda**) is not a comparison imposed by the mind or an ignorance-produced appearance; it belongs to the intrinsic nature (**svarūpa**) of real entities and makes determinate identity possible.

The doctrine of **fivefold difference (pañcavidhabheda)** affirms:

1. **soul–God difference (jīva–Īśvara-bheda);**
2. **soul–soul difference (jīva–jīva-bheda);**
3. **soul–matter difference (jīva–jaḍa-bheda);**
4. **God–matter difference (Īśvara–jaḍa-bheda);**
5. **matter–matter difference (jaḍa–jaḍa-bheda).**

Each performs indispensable work. God–soul difference protects Viṣṇu’s independence against identifying the finite embodied knower with the omniscient Lord. Soul–soul difference secures distinct karmic histories and spiritual destinies. Soul–matter difference preserves consciousness from reduction to insentient matter. God–matter difference safeguards divine sovereignty. Matter–matter difference grounds determinate objects and causal roles.

“Separate existence” does not make all entities equally independent. Only God is absolutely independent (**svatantra**); souls and matter are real but dependent (**paratantra**). Their difference is nevertheless irreducible and eternal.

Advaita objects that difference is empirically given but sublated in non-dual knowledge. Madhva replies that sublation cannot legitimately cancel a repeatedly and validly disclosed structure of reality; treating difference as ignorance undermines knowledge and devotion. Critics further ask whether eternal differences and gradation compromise ultimate unity or moral equality. Dvaita answers the first through dependence on one supreme God, though the ethical concern about hierarchy remains.

**Evaluation:** Pañcavidhabheda offers a robust realist basis for identity, devotion and plurality, but achieves unity through dependence rather than ontological non-duality.

**Why this earns marks:** It lists all five, explains their necessity, clarifies “separate,” engages Advaita and gives a qualified philosophical verdict.
