### Q1(a). “The rise of Indian women in sports.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(a). “The rise of Indian women in sports.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(a) · 100 marks · About 600 words · Language skill: Essay writing → Social change and sports · Proposed slug: `qualifying-english/essay-indian-women-sports.md`

**Model treatment**

For a long time, Indian sport was narrated largely through male achievement. Women participated, excelled and sometimes won international honours, but their success was often treated as an exception. That picture has changed. Indian women are now visible not merely as participants but as champions, captains, record-breakers and role models. Their rise reflects sporting excellence as well as a wider social transformation.

The journey has been gradual. Pioneers such as P. T. Usha showed that Indian women could compete with the world despite limited facilities. Karnam Malleswari’s Olympic medal gave women’s sport a new national visibility. Later, Mary Kom, Saina Nehwal, Sania Mirza, Sakshi Malik, P. V. Sindhu, Mirabai Chanu, Lovlina Borgohain and Manu Bhaker widened the range of disciplines in which Indian women could aspire to the highest level. The success of the women’s cricket and hockey teams has also shifted attention from isolated stars to sustained team performance.

Several forces have enabled this advance. Families are increasingly willing to support daughters who choose demanding sporting careers. Better coaching, sports science, scholarships, institutional employment and specialised training centres have made achievement more feasible. Television and digital media have taken women athletes into Indian homes, while commercial sponsorship has begun to recognise their appeal. Public programmes and rewards have reduced, though not removed, the financial insecurity associated with sport.

The social meaning of these achievements is profound. Many champions have emerged from small towns, rural families or economically modest backgrounds. Their success challenges the belief that strength, ambition and public competition are masculine qualities. A girl who sees a woman boxer, wrestler or weightlifter on an Olympic podium receives a powerful message: her body is not merely to be protected or judged; it can be trained, disciplined and celebrated.

Their victories also change sporting institutions. Women coaches, physiotherapists, administrators and commentators gain greater visibility, while success in one discipline encourages investment in others. International achievement creates a virtuous circle: recognition attracts resources, resources widen participation and a larger talent pool produces more credible contenders. The transformation is therefore cultural as well as competitive.

Yet celebration must not conceal continuing inequality. Girls still receive less access to playgrounds, nutrition and coaching. Early marriage, safety concerns, domestic responsibilities and social prejudice cause many to leave sport. Women athletes face unequal publicity and pay, intrusive comments about appearance and, in some institutions, insecure grievance mechanisms. Success at elite competitions cannot compensate for the absence of everyday sporting opportunities for millions of schoolgirls.

The next stage must therefore convert individual triumph into a broad sporting culture. Schools need safe grounds, trained coaches and equal playing time. Federations require transparent selection and credible safeguards against harassment. Talent identification must reach villages and disadvantaged communities. Media and sponsors should support women’s leagues consistently, not only after a medal.

The rise of Indian women in sports is already one of modern India’s most inspiring stories. Its lasting achievement, however, will be measured not only by medals but by whether every girl gains the freedom, health, confidence and opportunity to play.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines chronology, causes, social significance, constraints and a forward-looking conclusion without becoming a list of athletes.

### Q1(b). “A thing of beauty is a joy forever.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(b). “A thing of beauty is a joy forever.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(b) · 100 marks · About 600 words · Language skill: Essay writing → Reflective/literary composition · Proposed slug: `qualifying-english/essay-beauty-enduring-joy.md`

**Model treatment**

John Keats’s famous line, “A thing of beauty is a joy forever,” expresses more than delight in an attractive object. It suggests that genuine beauty outlives the moment in which it is first experienced. It enters memory, enlarges the imagination and becomes a lasting source of consolation. Time may alter the object, but the experience of beauty can continue to nourish the mind.

Nature offers the clearest illustration. A sunrise lasts only minutes, a flower fades and a season passes, yet their images remain. The memory of rain on dry earth or mountains under snow may comfort a person years later in a crowded city. Such beauty is not dependent on possession. Its joy lies in attentive experience and in the sense that life contains harmony larger than our immediate anxieties.

Art gives beauty another form of endurance. Civilisations vanish, but their poems, music, paintings and monuments continue to move later generations. The Ajanta murals, a raga, a finely told folk tale or a line of poetry can cross centuries because they express emotions recognisable to human beings everywhere. Art does not deny sorrow; often it transforms sorrow into insight. A tragic play may leave us saddened, yet also clarified and strengthened.

Beauty also resides in human conduct. Courage without cruelty, kindness offered without reward and dignity maintained in adversity possess a moral beauty deeper than physical appearance. The memory of someone who helped us in difficulty may remain a source of faith long after the act itself. In this sense, beauty is linked with truth and goodness: it reveals what human beings can become.

There is also beauty in ideas and institutions that bring order to collective life. A scientific explanation can be beautiful because it unites many observations through a simple principle. A just law, an elegant bridge or a thoughtfully planned garden combines purpose with proportion. Such beauty is not an escape from usefulness; it shows that usefulness itself can be humane and imaginative.

The statement, however, should not encourage a shallow worship of appearances. Standards of physical beauty change across societies and are often manipulated by advertising. An attractive surface can conceal injustice, while age and disability do not diminish a person’s worth. Nor does every beautiful object literally last forever. Keats’s insight concerns the continuing power of authentic beauty, not the permanence of material form.

Beauty also demands receptiveness. A hurried or prejudiced mind may pass it unnoticed. Education in literature, music and the arts refines attention, but ordinary life offers equal opportunities: a well-kept public space, careful craftsmanship, a child’s curiosity or neighbours cooperating during a crisis. When beauty is treated as a common human need rather than a luxury, it improves both private life and public culture.

Human existence includes loss, conflict and routine. Beauty does not abolish these realities. It gives us reasons to endure them by reminding us that life is capable of order, wonder and grace. A beautiful thing becomes a “joy forever” because its physical moment is converted into memory, meaning and renewed hope.

**Why this earns marks:** It interprets the quotation, moves through nature, art and moral beauty, introduces a qualification and returns to the central idea.

### Q1(c). “Growth of influencer culture in India.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(c). “Growth of influencer culture in India.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(c) · 100 marks · About 600 words · Language skill: Essay writing → Contemporary analytical composition · Proposed slug: `qualifying-english/essay-influencer-culture-india.md`

**Model treatment**

The rapid spread of smartphones, inexpensive data and social-media platforms has created a new public figure in India: the influencer. Unlike the traditional celebrity, an influencer may begin with no institutional position or mass-media backing. By regularly producing content and cultivating an audience, an ordinary user can acquire visibility, trust and commercial value. Influencer culture has democratised expression, but it has also blurred the boundaries between personal advice, entertainment and advertising.

Its growth is closely connected with India’s digital expansion. Audiences now consume short videos, live streams, reviews and tutorials in numerous Indian languages. Creators speak about food, fashion, education, finance, fitness, travel, technology and public issues. Their conversational style creates a sense of intimacy that conventional advertisements often lack. Businesses therefore use them to reach specialised communities, while small creators and local enterprises gain access to markets once dominated by large firms.

The culture has positive possibilities. It has opened creative and entrepreneurial careers, especially for young people outside established media centres. Regional-language creators can represent local humour, crafts and customs. Teachers simplify difficult concepts; farmers share techniques; doctors and lawyers can spread basic awareness; consumers receive demonstrations before buying products. At its best, the influencer economy connects expertise and experience with audiences in accessible forms.

However, influence without accountability creates serious risks. A recommendation that appears to be friendly advice may actually be paid promotion. Followers may buy unsuitable financial products, health remedies or cosmetics because they trust the creator rather than the evidence. Edited lifestyles can intensify anxiety, materialism and unrealistic standards of beauty or success. Algorithms reward attention, so outrage, imitation and sensational claims may travel faster than careful information.

The political effects are equally important. Influencers can make public questions understandable and bring neglected voices into debate, but they can also circulate rumours or reduce complex issues to emotionally charged clips. When repeated familiarity is mistaken for expertise, personal loyalty may displace verification. Digital influence therefore affects not only consumption but the quality of citizenship and public reasoning.

Children and adolescents are particularly vulnerable because they may not recognise commercial persuasion. Creators themselves face unstable incomes, constant pressure to remain visible and online harassment. Privacy also becomes fragile when family life, relationships and even children are converted into content.

The answer is neither hostility to new media nor unrestricted promotion. Paid partnerships should be clearly disclosed. Claims about health, finance and education require special care and, where applicable, professional accountability. Platforms must act against fraud and harmful content while preserving legitimate expression. Schools should teach digital and advertising literacy so that users ask who created a message, why it was created and what evidence supports it. Influencers, for their part, must recognise that trust is not merely a commercial asset but an ethical responsibility.

Influencer culture reflects a wider change in authority: people increasingly listen to personalities they feel they know. This can broaden participation and opportunity, but popularity cannot substitute for truth. India will benefit from the new creator economy only when creativity is joined with transparency, critical thinking and responsible influence.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the phenomenon, its causes, benefits and harms, and offers a balanced regulatory and educational response.

### Q1(d). “War is a problem, not a solution.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Q1(d). “War is a problem, not a solution.” · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** Official Q1(d) · 100 marks · About 600 words · Language skill: Essay writing → Argumentative composition · Proposed slug: `qualifying-english/essay-war-problem-not-solution.md`

**Model treatment**

War is usually presented as a means of settling a dispute: defeating aggression, securing territory, protecting honour or compelling an opponent to yield. It may produce a military decision, but a decision is not necessarily a solution. War destroys lives and institutions, multiplies grievances and often leaves the causes of conflict unresolved. It is therefore better understood as a problem that may sometimes be forced upon a state, but must never be mistaken for a complete remedy.

Its immediate cost is human. Soldiers die or return injured; civilians lose homes, livelihoods and family members. Hospitals, schools, water systems and transport networks are damaged. Children suffer interrupted education and lasting trauma. Even after fighting ends, mines, displacement and disability continue. The language of victory cannot erase these consequences.

War also imposes economic and political costs. Resources needed for health, employment and climate resilience are diverted to destruction and reconstruction. Trade breaks down, prices rise and insecurity crosses borders through refugees, arms and extremism. Emergency powers may weaken civil liberties. Hatred created during conflict can harden identities for generations, making reconciliation more difficult than the original disagreement.

Its environmental damage is also lasting. Explosions, fires and attacks on industrial or energy facilities pollute soil, air and water. Military operations consume enormous resources, while ruined farmland and displaced communities increase pressure on safer regions. Thus, a conflict begun for strategic advantage can undermine the ecological security on which all sides ultimately depend.

History shows that punitive settlements and incomplete victories can plant future wars. A battlefield may silence an opponent without creating justice, trust or workable institutions. Modern warfare is even more dangerous because nuclear weapons, cyberattacks and interconnected economies make escalation difficult to contain. No country can assume that violence will remain limited to its intended target.

Yet the statement requires qualification. A peaceful state may have to defend its people against invasion or mass violence. Refusal to resist aggression can reward the aggressor. International law therefore recognises self-defence. But even a necessary defensive war remains a tragic necessity, not a desirable solution. Force may stop an immediate attack; diplomacy, accountability, reconstruction and political accommodation must still build the peace.

Prevention is thus essential. Dialogue should begin before positions become rigid. Strong international institutions, arms control, economic cooperation and respect for sovereignty reduce incentives for conflict. Within societies, inclusive government and fair access to resources address the grievances from which violence grows. During conflict, humanitarian law must protect civilians and keep open the possibility of negotiation.

Peace is not passivity. It requires courage to compromise, institutions capable of enforcing agreements and justice that does not become revenge. War can change borders or governments, but it cannot by itself create mutual security. The real solution to conflict is a political order in which parties can live without recurring fear. War, even when unavoidable, marks the failure or exhaustion of better methods; its deepest lesson is the urgency of restoring them.

**Why this earns marks:** It sustains the proposition while acknowledging legitimate self-defence and distinguishing military necessity from durable peace.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Q3. Précis writing · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Compression logic:** Retain the two central recommendations, the distinction between professional and lay readers, the Thucydides example, history’s influence on later conduct, its universal lessons and its relevance to present and future. Remove descriptive repetition and incidental detail.

**Study title:** *Why We Read History*  
**Exam-format note:** The official instruction expressly forbids a title. The title above is supplied only to identify the central idea for study and should **not** be written in the examination answer.

### Model précis

When only one account of an event is available, certainty about what actually happened is limited. Yet establishing facts is not the sole purpose of reading history. A professional historian must rigorously compare every available source, while an ordinary reader should be neither equally exhaustive nor merely amused by the past.

Thucydides’ history of the Peloponnesian War illustrates the wider value of historical writing. Although the ancient combatants, their immediate aims and their cities have disappeared, his account remains important. Later leaders interpreted situations through his description of Athens and Sparta and used it to justify or guide their conduct. His representation of the past therefore helped shape subsequent events and still assists our understanding of the present.

Poetry is more universal than history because it is less tied to particular events, but good history also reveals recurring patterns of human behaviour. Thucydides intended future generations to avoid the errors he had witnessed, yet similar mistakes continue to be made. A reader concerned only with recovering exact past facts may consequently miss history’s chief lesson.

History explains how the present came into being; since the present partly determines the future, it also offers insight into what may follow. We should therefore, whenever possible, compare more than one account of an event. More importantly, we should read history not only to discover what occurred at a particular time and place, but to understand how people act across ages and, especially, how they act now.

**Approximate length:** 243 words  
**Why this earns marks:** It is close to one-third of 773 words, uses independent phrasing, retains the argument’s sequence and omits examples that do not advance the central logic.
