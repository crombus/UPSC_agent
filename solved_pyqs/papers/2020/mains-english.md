### Q1(a). “Indian Judicial System Needs Overhauling.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Judicial reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → access, delay, independence and reform · `qualifying-english/essay-indian-judicial-system-overhauling.md`

**Model essay**

A constitutional democracy depends on courts that are independent, accessible and able to deliver justice within a reasonable time. India’s judiciary has protected liberties, reviewed executive action and developed important principles of equality and dignity. Yet for many citizens justice remains slow, expensive and difficult to understand. The system therefore needs overhauling—not to weaken judicial independence, but to make constitutional justice effective.

Delay is the most visible problem. Cases remain pending because of vacancies, repeated adjournments, inadequate courtrooms, uneven case management and procedural complexity. An undertrial may lose years of liberty before acquittal, a victim may wait indefinitely for closure, and a small business may be unable to recover legitimate dues. Delay can thus become a denial of justice.

Access is equally important. Litigation demands money, time and familiarity with legal language. Rural citizens, women, persons with disabilities and economically weaker groups may find courts physically or socially distant. Legal aid exists, but its quality is uneven. Procedures and judgments should use clearer language, facilities must become accessible, and competent assistance should reach people before disputes become unmanageable.

Reform requires adequate capacity. Vacancies should be filled through a transparent and timely process. Subordinate courts need trained staff, modern infrastructure and reliable digital systems. Electronic filing and virtual appearances can save time in suitable matters, but technology must supplement rather than exclude citizens without devices or connectivity.

Case management also needs discipline. Courts should identify real issues early, discourage unnecessary adjournments and use realistic schedules. Routine matters need not consume the same time as complex constitutional or criminal cases. Special courts and tribunals help only when properly staffed; otherwise they merely transfer delay. Mediation and Lok Adalats can settle appropriate civil, family and neighbourhood disputes, but weaker parties must not be pressured into compromise.

Overhaul must protect quality and independence. Transparent appointments, ethical standards and reasoned accountability strengthen trust, whereas political control over judges would damage constitutional government. Greater diversity on the Bench can improve institutional understanding. Continuing education can prepare judges for new questions involving technology, environment and complex finance.

The police, prosecution, prisons and forensic services also shape outcomes. Courts cannot deliver timely criminal justice when investigations are poor, witnesses are intimidated or reports arrive late. Reform must therefore be systemic rather than confined to judges.

Citizens also need predictable enforcement. A judgment has little value if an authority ignores it or if execution proceedings begin another long contest. Better compliance systems, enforceable timelines and simpler execution of civil decrees would connect judicial declarations with practical relief. Reliable publication of court data can meanwhile reveal where delay arises and permit reform without reducing justice to crude disposal targets.

Public legal education would complete these institutional reforms. Citizens who understand basic rights, limitation periods and available remedies can seek help early and avoid exploitative intermediaries. Schools, legal-services authorities and local bodies can make law more intelligible without pretending that every dispute requires formal litigation. Prevention and informed settlement reduce pressure on courts while strengthening the rule of law.

India does not need a hurried judiciary that sacrifices fairness for disposal statistics. It needs a responsive system combining independence with accountability, speed with due process, and technology with inclusion. Reform should be measured by whether an ordinary person can obtain a reasoned, enforceable remedy without ruinous cost or endless waiting. That is the overhaul constitutional justice demands.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances institutional achievement with delay, access, capacity, process, independence and system-wide reform.

### Q1(b). “Not Rural or Urban, India Needs Rurban Growth.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Balanced regional development · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → rural–urban linkages · `qualifying-english/essay-rurban-growth.md`

**Model essay**

India is often described through a sharp contrast between village and city. The village represents agriculture and community; the city represents industry and opportunity. Reality is more connected. Villages supply food and labour to towns, while towns provide markets, hospitals, colleges, finance and technology. India therefore needs neither isolated rural development nor uncontrolled urbanisation, but “rurban” growth combining rural strengths with urban-quality opportunities.

Migration shows why the old division is inadequate. People leave villages because secure work, higher education and specialised healthcare are concentrated in cities. Rapid migration then creates congestion, costly housing and pressure on sanitation. If small towns and village clusters offered reliable services and varied livelihoods, migration would become a choice rather than a necessity.

Rurban growth begins with infrastructure. Roads, electricity, broadband, safe water, sanitation and public transport can connect villages to markets and institutions. Digital connectivity can support telemedicine, learning, payments and government services. Yet a cable alone creates no capability; residents also need devices, skills, local-language content and protection against fraud.

Economic diversification is central. Agriculture must remain productive, but families should not depend on uncertain cultivation alone. Food processing, repair services, crafts, tourism, renewable energy and small manufacturing can create local employment. Storage, cold chains and producer organisations can help cultivators retain more value. Training should correspond to real regional opportunities rather than offer certificates without jobs.

Small towns can serve as bridges. A planned town supports surrounding villages through hospitals, schools, warehouses and markets while drawing resources and talent from its hinterland. Planning should treat the town and neighbouring villages as one functional region. Otherwise development stops at municipal boundaries while nearby settlements remain excluded.

Rurbanisation must not copy the worst features of cities. Unplanned construction can consume fertile land, strain groundwater and produce unmanaged waste. Growth should preserve common lands, water bodies and ecological buffers, use compact settlement patterns and involve gram sabhas and urban local bodies in transparent decisions.

Social services require equal attention. A modern road is of limited value if the school lacks teachers or the health centre lacks staff. Women’s safety, childcare, disability access and affordable housing determine who can use new opportunities. Rural knowledge also matters: traditional water harvesting, local crops and crafts can be joined with scientific improvement.

Finance and governance must fit local needs. Small enterprises require affordable formal credit, while local bodies need dependable revenue and professional planning support. Development imposed from above may build assets that communities neither need nor maintain. Participatory planning can identify the right service centre, transport route or livelihood cluster and can also hold contractors and officials accountable for results.

Rurban regions can also strengthen national resilience. When employment, storage, healthcare and digital services are distributed across many smaller centres, a drought, flood, epidemic or industrial closure in one large city does not paralyse opportunity for an entire population. Balanced settlement is therefore not merely a welfare idea; it is sound economic risk management and a basis for more even political participation.

Rurban growth can reduce distress migration, spread employment and ease pressure on megacities. It will succeed only through empowered local government, regional planning and ecological care. India’s challenge is not to choose between village and city, but to build connected regions where rural residents enjoy urban-quality capabilities without losing valuable social and environmental assets.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the rural–urban continuum and combines infrastructure, livelihoods, planning, inclusion and ecology.

### Q1(c). “Relevance of Hindi as the National Language.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Multilingual national communication · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Argumentative essay → language and constitutional balance · `qualifying-english/essay-hindi-national-language.md`

**Model essay**

Language carries memory, identity and belonging. In a diverse country, the search for a common means of communication is understandable. Hindi is spoken or understood by a large number of Indians and has rich literature, vigorous media and wide administrative use. Yet the proposition that Hindi is the “national language” requires care, because India’s unity rests not on linguistic uniformity but on constitutional respect for many languages.

The Constitution does not declare any language the national language. It provides for Hindi in Devanagari as the official language of the Union while permitting continued use of English and recognising languages in the Eighth Schedule. States choose their official languages. This arrangement reflects experience: common administration is useful, but imposing one language can create fear of cultural and political domination.

Hindi remains highly relevant. It enables communication across several states, supports cinema and broadcasting, and increases mobility and access to employment. The Union also needs Indian-language channels through which public information can reach citizens rather than remain confined to English-speaking elites.

However, numerical strength alone does not create national ownership. Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Odia, Assamese, Punjabi, Urdu and other languages possess long traditions and millions of speakers. A mother tongue is not a regional inconvenience but the primary home of thought and culture. If Hindi is promoted by diminishing these languages, it will weaken national integration.

Education illustrates the balance required. Children often learn foundational concepts best in a familiar language. Strong mother-tongue education can coexist with learning Hindi and English where appropriate. Multilingual ability should be treated as an asset, not as a hierarchy in which one Indian language is patriotic and another merely local.

English also has a practical role. It connects speakers of different Indian languages, supports higher education and international exchange, and in some regions is viewed as more neutral. Dependence on English can perpetuate inequality, but replacing it abruptly with Hindi would transfer advantage rather than remove it. Translation and interpretation should make laws, courts and services available in languages citizens understand.

Technology makes pluralism more feasible. Digital dictionaries, translation, subtitling and local-language interfaces can circulate information across languages. Investment should support terminology and content in all major Indian languages, including endangered tribal languages.

Public employment and competitive examinations require similar sensitivity. A citizen should not be denied opportunity merely because crucial information or a test is unavailable in a language that the Constitution respects. Expanding accurate translation is difficult, especially for technical vocabulary, but this is a reason to build linguistic capacity rather than compel uniformity. Language policy should widen participation, not create a new elite.

The same principle should guide parliamentary and administrative communication: intelligibility should be expanded through translation, not secured by excluding citizens.

Hindi’s relevance should arise from attraction, usefulness and exchange, not compulsion. Non-Hindi speakers should have opportunities to learn it, while Hindi speakers should learn other Indian languages and appreciate their literature. Reciprocity turns language into a bridge rather than an instrument of dominance.

India needs common communication, not a single cultural voice. Hindi can play a major linking role alongside English and the many languages of the Union. The soundest policy is multilingual: promote Hindi, protect every mother tongue, widen translation and permit practical choice. India’s unity is strongest when citizens can meet through several languages without surrendering their own.

**Why this earns marks:** It corrects the constitutional premise while fairly assessing Hindi’s linking role and India’s multilingual reality.

### Q1(d). “COVID-19 Pandemic and the Indian Economy.”
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Essay Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/essay-writing.md) → Pandemic shock and recovery · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Marks/limit:** 100 · About 600 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Analytical essay → inequality, resilience and recovery · `qualifying-english/essay-covid19-indian-economy.md`

**Model essay**

The COVID-19 pandemic began as a health emergency, but containment quickly produced an economic crisis. Illness, uncertainty, restrictions on movement and temporary workplace closures disrupted production and consumption together. In India, the shock exposed both the adaptability of a large economy and the insecurity supporting millions of livelihoods.

Factories, construction sites, shops, transport services and small establishments could not operate normally. Firms lost revenue while still facing rent, interest and wage obligations. Supply chains broke because materials and workers could not move. Even permitted sectors faced weak demand and fear of infection.

The burden was unequal. Salaried employees often had savings or could work from home, but daily-wage workers, vendors, domestic workers and small producers lost income abruptly. The return of migrant workers to villages revealed how urban growth depended on labour lacking secure housing, portable welfare and social protection. Women carried increased unpaid care and were vulnerable to job loss in informal services.

Agriculture provided some support, yet faced transport, market and labour difficulties. Perishable produce could not always reach consumers. Rural employment and food distribution therefore became vital cushions. The crisis showed that resilience depends not only on large firms and finance but also on public health, food stocks, local administration and social security.

Government had to balance infection control with survival. Food, cash support, credit guarantees and employment measures could prevent temporary disruption becoming permanent destitution. Banks could offer limited relief to viable borrowers. Yet credit is not income when a business has no customers, and poorly targeted relief can exclude informal workers without registration.

The pandemic accelerated digital work, education, payments, commerce and telemedicine. This preserved some activity and encouraged innovation. It also exposed the digital divide: households without devices, internet or literacy could lose access to schooling, markets and services. Digital growth therefore required inclusion and cybersecurity.

Pharmaceuticals, medical equipment, logistics and digital services demonstrated capacity. Supply disruptions renewed interest in domestic manufacturing and diversified chains. Yet self-reliance should not become isolation. India benefits from trade, scientific cooperation and global inputs; resilience means reducing dangerous dependence while remaining connected.

Fiscal choices were necessarily difficult. Emergency spending increased when revenues were weak, while states carried major responsibilities for hospitals, local restrictions and welfare delivery. Cooperation between levels of government was therefore an economic necessity. Clear communication also mattered: abrupt or frequently changing rules could impose avoidable costs on transporters, employers and households even when restrictions served a legitimate health purpose.

The experience also strengthened the case for portable social protection. Workers who move across state boundaries should not lose access to food, health support or basic registration when employment disappears. Better worker databases must protect privacy, but portability can make labour mobility safer and reduce the human cost of future shocks.

Recovery cannot be judged by output alone. Closed small firms, interrupted education, malnutrition and delayed healthcare can damage future productivity after restrictions end. Policy must rebuild household demand, support viable enterprises, strengthen health systems and help workers acquire new skills. Transparent data and predictable rules restore confidence.

The pandemic proved that health and economy are not competing worlds. A weak health system prolongs disruption, while poverty makes public-health compliance difficult. Durable recovery must combine growth with protection, formalisation with worker security, and technology with universal access. An economy is resilient only when its institutions protect both productive capacity and human dignity.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers sectoral disruption, inequality, policy response, digitisation, resilience and an inclusive recovery thesis.

## Q3. Précis writing
**Primary route:** Qualifying English → [Précis Writing](../../topics/qualifying-english/precis-writing.md) → Compression and faithful restatement · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)  
**Official wording:** “Make a précis of the following passage in about one-third of its length. Do not give a title to it. The précis should be written in your own language:”  
**Marks:** 75  
**Source length:** 792 words · **Target:** about 264 words  
**Language skill / slug:** Faithful compression without title · `qualifying-english/precis-ashoka-dhamma.md`

**Compression logic**

- Retain Ashoka’s accessibility and knowledge of ordinary disputes.
- Preserve the social hierarchy described in the empire.
- Compress the Buddha’s teaching and Ashoka’s gradual conversion.
- Keep the Kalinga turning point, renunciation of conquest, missions of Dhamma and qualified legacy.
- Supply **no title**, because the paper expressly prohibits one.

**Model précis — intentionally untitled**

Ashoka remained constantly available for consultation and personally settled disputes over water, grazing and money-lending, thereby staying close to administration. His empire had a developing hierarchy: influential priests stood first; herdsmen, cultivators and craftsmen formed the large productive castes; soldiers, officials and councillors were fewer; and slaves and others remained outside the caste order.

Ashoka’s new imperial ideas grew from Buddhism. Although reliable knowledge of Gautam Buddha was limited and legends accumulated only later, his central teaching was clear: old age and death make righteous living urgent, and people attain serenity by rising above petty hopes, fears and hatreds. Buddhism gradually separated from Hinduism and spread, partly through Ashoka.

Ashoka’s acceptance of Buddhism was gradual rather than a sudden revelation. The Kalinga War became his decisive moral turning point. Unlike rulers intoxicated by victory, he was appalled by the conflict between its cruelty and Buddhist teaching. His rock inscriptions renounced conquest, fame and glory, urged descendants to live peacefully, and declared adherence to Dhamma the only true glory.

Instead of relying solely on envoys or armies, Ashoka sent missionaries to explain Dhamma across Asia. His influence as a wise ruler travelled farther than military conquest, while ideals of humility, serenity, goodness and reverence for life spread abroad. His political achievement proved temporary: his subjects later abandoned Buddhism and his empire fragmented within fifty years. Yet his deeper influence, strengthened after death, endured. His lasting lesson was that rulers’ power must be governed by kindness and wisdom if peace between countries is to be possible.
